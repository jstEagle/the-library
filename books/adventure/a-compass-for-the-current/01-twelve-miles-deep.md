## 1. Twelve Miles Deep

The fog came off the water at dawn the way it always did on this coast, thick as wet wool, and Jonah Hartwell stood at the rail of the *Corvus* and watched it swallow the horizon until there was nothing left in the world but gray water, gray sky, and the orange blur of her own dive buoy bobbing forty feet off the stern.

Twelve miles out. Twelve miles deep, or close enough — the navy's transport sat on a shelf at three hundred ten feet, which was past the line where most salvage divers stopped calling it work and started calling it a gamble. The navy called it a restricted recovery operation. The navy paid triple rate for restricted. Jonah had learned a long time ago that when someone pays you extra to stop asking questions, the questions are usually worth more than the money.

She hadn't asked any yet.

"Miss Hartwell." The voice came from behind her, smooth as poured concrete. Lieutenant Reyes. He'd introduced himself yesterday with a handshake that lasted exactly one second too long, and he'd been standing somewhere within eyeshot of her ever since, like she was cargo he'd been assigned to guard rather than a contractor he'd hired. "The fog's lifting. We should have a window by eight."

Jonah didn't turn around. "I dive when the viz says I can dive. Not when your window says."

"Of course." A pause, measured out like everything else he did. "Just noting the schedule."

"Noting away."

She heard him shift his weight, heard the creak of his boots on the deck plates, and then — mercifully — heard him walk back toward the wheelhouse, where he'd spent the previous afternoon talking quietly into a satellite phone with the door cracked open just enough that anyone passing could hear him not saying anything worth hearing.

Jonah breathed the fog and let it sit in her chest. Below her, under two hundred fathoms of cold green dark, lay the USS *Meridian Star*, a naval transport that had gone down twelve years ago with a crew of nine and a cargo manifest so redacted it might as well have been written in disappearing ink. The official story was engine failure. The unofficial story was nothing, because nobody official would say anything at all. The wreck had sat untouched for a decade while the paperwork fought its slow war through courts and insurance claims, and now, suddenly, the navy wanted three specific items recovered from the aft hold, and they wanted them recovered quiet, and they wanted Jonah Hartwell to do it because her reputation said she could work deep, cold, and alone without leaving fingerprints.

Her reputation was accurate. Her curiosity was worse.

She went below to check her gear.

* * *

The *Corvus* was a converted trawler, sixty feet of rust and stubbornness that Jonah had bought with five years of Gulf oil-rig money and kept alive the way you keep anything alive out here — by refusing to let it die. Her deckhand, a silent Salish man named Frank who'd worked with her four seasons and spoken maybe four hundred words total, had the compressor running and the mixed-gas racks staged by the time she came down the companionway.

Frank looked up when she entered. His eyes went to her face, then to the porthole, then back. He knew Reyes had been watching her. Everybody on the boat knew. Frank's opinion of navy liaisons was a single word he'd used exactly once, in year one, and Jonah had never needed him to repeat it.

"Bottom's three-ten," she said, checking her gauges though she'd checked them twice already. "Hang at fifty, thirty-minute bottom window if the current cooperates. You know the drill."

Frank nodded once and handed her the last item: the recovery manifest, printed on navy letterhead, listing the three items to be brought up from the aft hold. Sealed document case, serial number redacted. Navigation module, serial number redacted. Personal effects locker, contents redacted.

Three items. Every line of description redacted except what they were made of. It was less a manifest than a dare.

"You read this?" Frank asked. It was the longest sentence he'd offered in a week.

"I read it."

"And?"

"And I'm getting paid either way." She folded it into her drysuit pocket. "But I'm also getting curious, and curiosity doesn't show up on an invoice."

Something that might have been a smile moved across Frank's face and was gone. He clipped her lift bags to her harness and said, "Current turns at slack. Go."

* * *

The water took her the way it always did — a cold slap, then silence, then the long blue-gray fall into a world where sound traveled wrong and light came down in shafts that bent and broke before they reached the bottom. Jonah equalized her ears in increments, watching her depth gauge unwind, feeling the pressure close around her like a hand.

One hundred feet. One hundred fifty. The transport's superstructure rose out of the gloom at two hundred, a black silhouette resolving into detail as her lights cut through the particulate — the bridge windows starred inward, a forest of soft coral colonizing the railings, a school of rockfish hanging in the doorways like tenants who'd never gotten the eviction notice.

Two hundred eighty feet. The hull loomed, lying on its starboard side on the shelf, and Jonah swam the length of it the way she'd swum it twice before on recon dives, running her light along steel that was already starting to weep rust into the sea. Twelve years underwater. Long enough for the metal to start forgetting it was a ship. Long enough for most people to forget there'd been one.

She found the aft hold access on the first pass. The hatch had been cut — not blown, cut, cleanly, recently, by someone with plasma equipment who'd done it during the navy's preliminary survey six months ago. She slipped through into the hold and her lights swept across stacked crates, collapsed racking, the sediment of a decade settling over everything like gray snow.

The document case was where the survey photos said it would be, lashed to a rack frame near the bulkhead. Titanium, sealed, roughly the size of a footlocker. She ran her gloves over the surface, checking for damage, checking the latches, and that was when her light caught the stencil on the lid.

Not a serial number. Not a navy marking.

Initials. Hand-stenciled, in the old surveyor's style, the kind of field marking a man put on his personal kit so it wouldn't get lost in a government warehouse:

**R.A.H.**

Robert Almeida Hartwell.

Her father.

Jonah hung in the black water of the hold, three hundred feet down, breathing gas that cost more per breath than most people made in an hour, and felt the cold come through her suit in a way it hadn't a moment before. The water didn't change. Her body changed. Something surfaced in her chest the way bodies surface — slowly at first, then all at once, bloated and undeniable.

Her father had disappeared twelve years ago. Not died — disappeared. His survey vessel had gone missing upriver, inland, four hundred miles from this salt water, and the search had lasted eleven days before it became a file, and the file had lasted two years before it became a footnote, and the footnote had become the shape of a hole in Jonah's life that she'd spent twelve years diving around. She'd gone to sea because the sea held everything down. Miles of water between herself and the question. That was the whole architecture of her life: distance as ballast.

And here, at the bottom of the ocean, in a navy wreck that sank the same season her father vanished, was a case with his initials stenciled on it.

She checked the date stamp riveted to the latch. Manufacturing lot, shipping date. The case had been loaded aboard the *Meridian Star* three days before the ship went down. Three days before her father's last known transmission.

It wasn't possible. Upriver was inland. This was open ocean. Unless the case had traveled. Unless someone had carried it. Unless—

Her air integration alarm chimed softly, reminding her she was having a revelation at three hundred feet on a clock. Jonah closed her fist around the latch handle and made herself be a professional. She rigged the lift bag, ran the line, filled the bag, and watched the case rise off the deck in a cloud of silt, climbing toward a surface she no longer entirely trusted.

Then she went back for the other two items, because she was being paid either way, and because hands that are busy don't shake.

* * *

The navigation module came up clean. The personal effects locker came up dented, its lock corroded open, its contents — whatever they'd once been — apparently removed long before the navy ever sent anyone down. Empty locker. Redacted manifest. Somebody had gotten here first, years ago, and taken exactly what mattered.

Or somebody had gotten there first and left the one thing they thought didn't matter.

Jonah shed her gear on the deck and stood dripping in the gray morning while Frank and the deck crew secured the lifts. The fog was burning off in ragged patches. The case sat on the deck plates, seawater streaming off titanium, and Lieutenant Reyes came down the ladder from the wheelhouse with a towel over his arm and an expression arranged into pleasant neutrality.

"Successful dive," he said.

"Successful dive," Jonah agreed.

"The case. You marked its condition?"

"Sealed. Latches intact. No visible breach." She stripped her gloves. "Locker was empty, though. Contents gone. Corrosion pattern on the hasp says it's been open for years."

Something flickered behind Reyes's eyes — quick, controlled, gone. "Noted. I'll need the case secured in the hold. We'll take custody at port."

"We'll take custody at port," Jonah repeated. "Meaning I carry it four days and hand it off."

"Standard procedure."

"Sure." She looked at him. He looked at her. Somewhere out in the fog a gull cried once and quit. "Lieutenant, you want to tell me why a navy transport that sank in open water is carrying a surveyor's equipment case belonging to a man who disappeared four hundred miles upriver?"

Reyes smiled. It was a good smile. Practiced the way a rifle is practiced. "Is that whose case it is?"

"It's got his initials on it."

"Stenciling can mean many things." He picked up the manifest copy from the crate lid, squared its edges against the wood with two precise taps. "Common surname. Common initials. The navy doesn't discuss cargo manifests with contractors, Miss Hartwell. You know that going in."

"I know a lot of things going in." She wiped her face with the towel he'd offered and finally accepted, mostly so he'd stop holding it. "Here's what I know coming out. That case was loaded three days before my father vanished. Your survey team photographed this hold six months ago, which means the navy's known about it for six months, which means somebody upstairs has been sitting on a dead man's luggage for half a year before deciding to fish it out. And you've been watching me since I stepped aboard like I'm the part of this job most likely to go wrong."

Reyes set the manifest down. For a moment the pleasantness thinned, and underneath it Jonah saw something older and more tired — a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Your father's name came up in the pre-dive briefing," he said quietly. "Yes. I recognized it. I requested confirmation of your identity before you boarded, and command approved the contract anyway." He met her eyes. "That should tell you something about how much they're worried about you, Miss Hartwell. They're not. If they were, you'd never have gotten this close."

He turned and gestured to two of his own men, who'd appeared at the top of the ladder without her noticing — navy, armed, wearing civilian jackets that fooled nobody. "Secure the case in the forward hold. Double seal. Nobody touches it but me."

They carried it away. Jonah watched it go, watched the titanium disappear below decks, watched the hatch close over it, and felt the hole in her life change shape. For twelve years it had been a absence, a negative space, a question with no address. Now it had weight. Now it had a location. Now it sat in a hold forty feet from where she slept, stamped with her father's hands, and every mile of ocean she'd ever put between herself and that question suddenly felt like distance she'd run for nothing.

"Four days to port," Reyes said from the ladder. "Get some rest, Miss Hartwell. The weather's supposed to build."

He climbed. Jonah stood at the rail as the *Corvus* turned south, and the fog closed back in behind them like a curtain drawn over a stage, and she thought about locks, and seals, and how many hours there were in four days, and how many of those hours the forward hold would be unwatched.

Frank came and stood beside her. He didn't say anything. He never had to.

"Frank," she said, watching the gray water slide past, "how do you feel about breaking into government property?"

He considered it with the gravity he gave all things.

"Tools are in the second locker," he said.
