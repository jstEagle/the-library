## 2. The Wreck They Won't Name

The *Corvina* rode at anchor like something that had forgotten how to sink and hadn't forgiven the water for it. Jonah stood at the rail of the work barge and watched the navy transport's superstructure break the fog in gray slices, forty feet down and twelve years dead, and she thought about how a ship could be unnamed without being unmarked.

They'd given her a hull number on the work order. Not a name. Hull 7734, lost with all records sealed, salvage authorized under a contract number that had more digits than her bank balance would ever see. She'd been diving wrecks for eleven years and she knew what an unnamed ship meant. It meant somebody upstream was embarrassed.

"Ms. Hartwell."

Reyes. He always announced himself two steps before he arrived, which she'd decided was either courtesy or training, and she hadn't ruled out both. He came along the deck in his civilian jacket that fit him like a uniform he was pretending not to wear, carrying two cups of coffee from the galley thermos.

"You've been up since four," he said, handing her one.

"I've been up since four because the tide window doesn't care about my feelings."

"The tide window," he agreed, "is indifferent." He drank, watching the fog eat the headland. "You found something yesterday."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it. She sipped the coffee. It was terrible in the specific way navy-adjacent coffee was terrible, burnt twice and proud of it.

"A case," Reyes said. "Sealed. You brought it up yourself instead of sending the lift bag."

"Lift bags tear. Cases don't."

"Most contractors send everything up in bags. Faster."

"Most contractors haven't spent eleven years learning what tears." She turned to look at him. He was maybe forty, dark-haired going silver at the temples, with the kind of face designed to be forgotten by witnesses. "You want to tell me why you're counting my hands?"

He smiled. It reached his eyes about halfway and stopped, like a man wading into cold water. "I'm liaison. Counting is the job."

"You counted the case."

"I inventory recoveries. Standard procedure."

"Then you know I logged it. Sealed case, aluminum, marine growth consistent with immersion depth and salinity. Contents unknown — the seal's intact. It goes to your people when we make port Thursday."

"It does," Reyes said pleasantly. "Thursday."

Something in the way he said it made the word feel like a hand on her shoulder, guiding her gently toward a door she hadn't agreed to walk through. She finished the coffee and set the cup on the rail.

"I'm diving again at slack water," she said. "The cargo hold section hasn't been cleared."

"We have what we need from the hold."

"*We* haven't looked behind the collapsed bulkhead. There's a void back there — my sonar showed it Tuesday. Could be more compartments, could be bodies. Families tend to want the bodies."

"Families," Reyes said, "have been notified of everything they're entitled to know."

"And there's the sentence I'll be thinking about all day." She pulled her gloves back on. "Slack water's in ninety minutes."

She felt his eyes on her back the whole way to the dive locker, patient as current.

* * *

The case sat in her cabin's footlocker under a layer of foul-weather gear, and for six hours she did nothing but dive, work, eat, and pretend she wasn't thinking about it. That was its own kind of discipline. Her father had taught her that too — not the diving, though he'd taught her that first, in the cold rivers of her childhood, standing waist-deep behind her with his hands under her arms saying *the water tells you where it wants to go, Jonah-girl, your job is just to listen*. No, what he'd really taught her was patience underwater. Panic kills faster than pressure. Want kills faster than panic. You do the task in front of you and let the thing you want wait its turn.

Elias Hartwell had been a surveyor, the kind who worked alone upriver for months and came back smelling of woodsmoke and pencil shavings, with maps drawn in a hand so precise the county used them for twenty years. Then one spring he went up the Kestrel River with a government contract and never came down it. They found his truck at the trailhead. They found nothing else. Twelve years ago this month, and Jonah had spent every one of those years putting miles between herself and the question, because the question had no bottom and she'd learned early that some holes you don't free-dive.

And then yesterday, forty feet down in the belly of a ship the navy wouldn't name, wedged behind a pipe bracket in a compartment that should have flooded and somehow hadn't, she'd found a sealed aluminum case stamped with three initials she'd know anywhere, even crusted in twelve years of rust and silt.

**E.H.**

Her father's initials. On a wreck that sank — according to the fragmentary records Reyes had grudgingly shared — eight hundred miles from any river Elias Hartwell had ever surveyed.

Patience underwater. But she was on dry land now, and the sun had gone down, and the generator hum covered small sounds, and Reyes's cabin light had gone dark an hour ago.

She waited until the watch changed at midnight. She heard the new man's boots on the deck above, slower than the last one, a tired shuffle. She gave it another twenty minutes anyway. Then she knelt on the cabin floor, took the case out, and set it under her lamp.

The seal was a compression latch, military pattern, and it opened easier than it should have, as if someone had opened it recently and closed it carefully. That thought arrived and she made herself look at it squarely: someone had put this case in that compartment. Wrecks don't hide things behind pipe brackets. People do. Someone had stowed her father's property inside a sinking ship, or the ship had sunk around it, and either way the arithmetic only worked if Elias Hartwell had been aboard.

She lifted the lid.

Documents. A canvas document wallet, swollen with damp but sound. A brass survey compass she recognized before she fully saw it — her father's, the one with the dented bezel he'd refused to replace because *a dent is a memory, Jonah-girl*. And beneath the wallet, wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of survey sheets and a field journal with a cracked leather cover.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that with distant surprise. Eleven years of salvage work had trained her hands to be steady around other people's drowned belongings, and apparently they extended the courtesy to her own grief.

She got her phone, checked it was in airplane mode — no signals leaving this boat, not tonight — and began photographing.

The journal first. She photographed the cover, the spine, every page, working in order like a crime scene tech because that was the discipline that kept her from simply stopping at the first page and reading until dawn. His handwriting hit her like cold water anyway. The same slanted capitals, the same habit of pressing hard enough to emboss the next page. *Day 1. Put in above Kestrel Falls. Contract terms amended — client now designated only as OGA. Amended pay is triple. Triple means they expect trouble.*

She turned pages with a dive knife tip, never touching the paper. *Day 4. Found the seam in the canyon wall. Water flow doesn't match surface geology. There's a system behind the falls — big one. Surveying it proper will take a week.*

*Day 6. Took samples. What's in this rock shouldn't be in a watershed. Ran the assay twice. Somebody upstream knows. Somebody upstream has known.*

*Day 8.* The entry stopped mid-sentence. Three days later, by the date stamp of the next document, Elias Hartwell vanished from the face of the earth.

The survey sheets were worse. Mineral assays, watershed diagrams, and a map — hand-drawn, gorgeous, exact — of a river canyon with a waterfall marked and, behind it, in her father's careful hatching, a cave system with coordinates. Coordinates that matched, to the second decimal, the ones printed on the contract work order Reyes had handed her a week ago. The navy hadn't hired her to salvage a wreck. The wreck was a errand. The coordinates were the job, and they'd dressed it in a dead ship's clothes because nobody asks questions about a wreck.

The last document was a single typed sheet on letterhead she didn't recognize, stamped across its face in red: **CLASSIFIED — CUSTODY OF EVIDENCE PENDING DESTRUCTION AUTHORIZATION.**

Destruction authorization. She read it three times. It referenced *Hartwell materials, lot 2 of 2*, and a disposal protocol, and a signature block signed by someone whose name meant nothing to her and whose rank meant everything: Commander.

Lot 2 of 2. Which meant somewhere there was a lot 1, and her father's own government had catalogued his life's work for burning.

She sat back on her heels in the lamplight and let the fury come, because fighting it would take longer than feeling it. This was the hole she'd spent twelve years swimming away from, and it had a floor after all, and the floor was paved with paperwork. They hadn't lost him. They'd filed him.

A knock at the door.

One knock, soft, and then Reyes's voice, pitched exactly loud enough to carry and no louder: "Ms. Hartwell. May I come in?"

She looked at the case. Looked at the phone, screen dark, forty-one photographs stored behind a passcode. She closed the journal, replaced the oilcloth, shut the lid, and slid the case back into the footlocker under the foul-weather gear in nine seconds flat, a personal record she had no intention of ever celebrating.

"Come in," she said, and sat on the bunk with a repair manual open on her knees, a trick she'd prepared hours ago and hated herself for needing.

Reyes entered without waiting, which told her he'd already decided the answer didn't matter. He glanced at the manual. He glanced at the footlocker. He did not glance long at anything, which was its own confession.

"Can't sleep?" he asked.

"Salt air. Does it to everybody."

"Mm." He leaned against the bulkhead, arms folded, and regarded her with the mild expression of a man discussing weather. "Tomorrow we make port Thursday, as discussed. Your contract concludes. Payment clears within seventy-two hours. And then, Ms. Hartwell, I'd advise a long vacation somewhere without rivers."

"There it is," she said. "I was wondering when you'd get to the part where you threaten me politely."

"I'm not threatening you."

"No. You're advising me. Threats leave marks; advice leaves plausible deniability. I've worked enough navy contracts to know the difference." She closed the manual. "Here's my problem, Mr. Reyes. You put my father's initials in front of me and expected me to log it and move on. Either you didn't know whose case that was — in which case your inventory procedure is garbage — or you knew exactly, and you wanted to see what I'd do."

For the first time, something moved behind his eyes. Respect, maybe, or recalculation.

"Elias Hartwell," Reyes said slowly, "was a contractor who died in a wilderness accident twelve years ago. The navy regrets the loss. The navy also regrets that certain materials associated with his final survey cannot be released, because releasing them would compromise sources and methods that keep people alive. Including, potentially, you."

"That's not an answer. That's a eulogy with a leash on it."

"It's the truth as it's officially constituted." He pushed off the bulkhead. "Officially constituted truths are load-bearing structures, Ms. Hartwell. Pull one out and things fall on people. I've seen it happen. I'd rather it didn't happen to you."

"Was he on this ship?"

Reyes paused at the door. "The *Corvina* — " he caught himself, a half-second too late, and she watched him register the catch — "hull 7734 sank in a storm with all hands. Whatever you think you found down there, the sea keeps its own records, and the sea doesn't testify."

"The sea doesn't," Jonah agreed. "But it photographs."

His gaze flicked, very briefly, to her phone charging on the shelf. One beat. Two. Then he smiled his halfway smile.

"Port Thursday," he said. "Sleep well."

The door clicked shut behind him. Jonah sat motionless for a full minute, listening to his boots fade along the deck, doing the math on what he'd seen and what he'd report and to whom. He'd caught himself on the name — *Corvina* — which meant the ship had a name the navy pretended it didn't, which meant the wreck they wouldn't name had a name worth protecting, and names, unlike hull numbers, could be searched.

She picked up the phone and thumbed through the photographs one more time, slow, committing each page to a memory she'd already begun building — her father's craft applied to her father's words, redundancy against loss, the diver's oldest rule: never trust a single line to hold you.

Coordinates up the Kestrel. A cave behind a waterfall. Lot 1 of 2 still out there somewhere, unless the navy had already burned it — and if they had, then these photographs were the last testimony her father would ever give, and she was the only witness left alive.

Outside, the fog pressed against the porthole like something trying to get in. Jonah lay down without sleeping and listened to the anchor chain shift with the tide, and somewhere past three in the morning the radio on the bulkhead crackled awake with the coast guard relay traffic she'd half expected and fully dreaded: a survey vessel called *Meridian*, overdue upriver, last known position transmitted in the clear, search suspended pending weather.

Last known position: the Kestrel headwaters. The coordinates on her father's map.

She was already pulling on her boots.
