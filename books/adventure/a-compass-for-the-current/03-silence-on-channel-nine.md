## 3. Silence on Channel Nine

The harbor bar had a radio behind the counter, an old marine set bolted to the shelf above the liquor, and the bartender kept it on out of habit rather than interest. Jonah was three fingers into a plate of chowder she wasn't tasting when the voice came through the static — a woman's voice, clipped and professional, reading coordinates like a eulogy.

"—repeat, all stations, this is a distress relay originating from the Meridian Survey Expedition, party of six, last contact forty-eight hours ago at grid reference—" The pause. The rustle of paper. "Grid reference seven-one-four dash nine. Upriver of Kestrel Narrows. Any vessel or aircraft with range to assist, respond on channel nine."

Jonah's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

Seven-one-four dash nine.

She knew those numbers the way you know your own birthday. Not because anyone had taught them to her recently — because she'd found them scrawled in the margin of a document she'd photographed at two in the morning four days ago, in handwriting that still lived in her hands the way a dead language lives in the mouth. Her father's slanted fives. His sevens with the little crossbar he'd added so no one could forge them.

The bartender was wiping glasses, not listening. The radio crackled on with weather for the outer banks. Nobody else in the room had gone still.

Jonah set down the spoon. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Twelve years of salvage work had taught her body one lesson above all others: panic kills divers faster than pressure ever did. So her hands stayed flat on the scarred wood of the bar while something underneath them roared.

*Meridian Survey.* She'd seen the name too — on the charter manifest pinned to the board at the floatplane dock, six names listed under a company logo that looked like every other survey logo in the territory: a mountain, a river, a promise. She'd read it without reading it, the way you read everything when your whole trade is noticing what other people miss.

Six names. One of them, she remembered now, had been *Vasquez.*

"Hey." She kept her voice level. "That relay. When did it first go out?"

The bartender shrugged. "Hour ago, maybe. Coast guard's been trying to raise them since yesterday, far as I know. Nothing back." He said it the way people say things about drownings up here — with the particular flatness of folks who live where the water takes its tax regularly.

Forty-eight hours dark. In late season, with the snow line already creeping down the ridgelines like gray rot. Six people who'd been standing, three days before they vanished, on coordinates written in her father's hand.

Jonah paid for the chowder she hadn't eaten and walked out into the fog.

* * *

The fog hadn't lifted since the salvage job started, and it wouldn't lift now until the wind turned, which the forecast said would be Thursday, which might as well have been never. It lay over the harbor in wool layers, swallowing the masts, turning the floatplane dock into a series of suggestions. Jonah walked its length anyway, boots ringing on the aluminum floats, past a beached skiff, past a fuel drum someone had abandoned, to the last plane tied off at the end.

It was a de Havilland Beaver, forty years old if it was a day, yellow paint gone chalky, one strut wrapped in fresh safety wire. A man was crouched under the wing doing something to the fuel line with a wrench and a great deal of profanity delivered conversationally, as though the plane were an old friend who'd earned it.

"You're grounded," Jonah said. "Whole coast is."

"I'm *waiting,*" the man said without looking up. "Different thing. Wind turns Thursday, I fly Thursday. Until then I drink coffee and insult this aircraft, which has it coming." He backed out from under the wing and stood, unfolding — tall, sixty-odd, a face like a dried riverbed, eyes pale and quick. He looked at her the way pilots look at everyone: assessing weight, assessing sense. "You're the diver. Off the navy barge."

Word traveled fast in towns this size. "I'm done with the barge."

"So I heard. Heard you quit mid-contract, too, which the liaison took poorly." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being white sometime last decade. "Name's Okafor. Sol Okafor. I fly charter, freight, medevac, whatever pays, and right now what pays is sitting here because my next booking went dark."

"The Meridian Survey."

His eyebrows climbed a fraction. "You keep up."

"I keep up." She nodded back toward town, toward the bar, toward the radio. "They just put out a relay. Forty-eight hours dark, grid seven-one-four dash nine."

Something changed in his face. Not much. A tightening around the eyes, the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "That's a bad piece of country," he said. "Kestrel Narrows and above. No strips, no roads, fuel stop at Grayling Landing and then nothing but gorge for eighty miles. You'd need to read terrain like scripture to fly support in there, and even then—" He stopped. Looked at her again, longer this time. "Why do you care about a survey crew?"

Because my father wrote those numbers. Because the navy sealed a case with his initials and told me to forget him. Because every mile I've run for twelve years just ended at these coordinates and I don't know how to run anymore.

"They've got a cook named Vasquez," she said instead. "Teo Vasquez. He worked a season on my uncle's boat, years back. Good man. I owe the family."

It was a lie built on a true foundation, which was the only kind of lie she allowed herself. Okafor studied her with those quick pale eyes, and she watched him decide — visibly, the way old bush pilots decide things, in one motion, no committee — that the truth was bigger than the lie and he'd get to it eventually or he wouldn't.

"Coast guard's grounded," he said. "Weather. Navy's got helicopters but they're 'assessing,' which means they're waiting to see if this is a problem or a tragedy, and they won't move for either until it's both." He tossed the rag onto the strut. "I could be at Grayling Landing in ninety minutes if the ceiling holds at eight hundred feet. After that I'd need somebody riding shotgun who can read contour lines better than I can, because I've flown that gorge twice and both times it tried to keep me."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then you're either the best hire I never meant to make, or you're running toward something a smart woman would run from." He smiled without much humor. "Either way, wheels up at dawn if the fog breaks. Bring your own maps."

"I don't need maps," Jonah said. "I need paper and a pencil."

* * *

She drew them that night in her rented room above the hardware store, by lamplight, the way he'd taught her on the kitchen table of a house that didn't exist anymore.

*Terrain isn't lines, Jojo. Terrain is water deciding where it wants to go. Read the water and the land tells you its secrets for free.*

He'd sit with a topographic sheet spread between their elbows, her father with his coffee going cold, tracing valleys with one blunt finger. *See how the contours pinch here? That's a gorge. See how they spread like fingers? That's a floodplain, and floodplains mean soft ground, and soft ground means don't land there unless you hate your landing gear.* He'd quiz her until she dreamed in contour intervals. *What's this?* *Saddle.* *And this?* *Spur, dropping east.* *Good girl. Now find me water.*

Find me water. That had been the whole curriculum, really. Everything else was decoration.

So she drew the Kestrel drainage from memory — memory plus the photographs locked in the waterproof case beneath her bunk aboard the *Maren L*, plus twelve years of diving wrecks and reading currents, which is the same skill wearing different clothes. The river's course. The narrows where it pinched white. The tributaries feeding in from the west, each one a green vein on the map. And there, upstream of the second major fork, the coordinates her father had written three days before he vanished, sitting in a hanging valley below a ridge that ran unbroken for miles except—

Except for one place. One low shoulder in the rock, a saddle any pilot would clock as the only crossing within fifty miles, and a waterfall marked on no chart she'd ever seen, feeding the river from a canyon the surveys had always left blank. Blank like a censored letter. Blank like a page torn out.

Her father hadn't been exploring. She understood that now, pencil hovering over the paper. He'd been *documenting*. There was a difference, and the difference was why the navy had sunk a ship over it, and why a liaison named Reyes looked at her like a problem to be managed, and why six people were dark in a valley that officially contained nothing worth finding.

She finished the map at three in the morning, and then she sat with it a long time, because there was one more thing to do and she knew it, and knowing it didn't make it easier.

She wrote a note. Folded it. Addressed it to no one, because there was no one — that had been the arrangement of her life for twelve years, the whole point of the arrangement — and finally she addressed it to the harbormaster, who at least would wonder. *If I don't come back, the case on the Maren L goes to the provincial records office. Don't let the navy touch it.* It wasn't much. It was the first time in twelve years she'd left anything behind anywhere, and her hand shook slightly doing it, and she hated that, and she did it anyway.

* * *

The fog broke at dawn the way a fist opens — sudden, grudging, revealing a world rinsed gray and silver. Okafor was already at the dock, loading fuel drums, and he glanced at the tube of charts under her arm and the pack on her shoulders and said nothing, which was its own kind of contract.

"Grayling Landing," he said, firing the engine. "Last fuel, last phone, last anything. From there it's gorge country. You sure?"

She looked down the length of the harbor, at the navy barge riding at anchor with its lights still on, at the liaison office window where a curtain moved — Reyes, watching, or someone watching for Reyes. Somewhere upriver, six people had stopped answering their radio on coordinates written by a dead man. Somewhere behind her, an organization that sank its own ships was deciding what she was worth.

Every instinct she'd sharpened for twelve years said *run the other way.* New coast, new name, new miles. That was the creed. Miles can't die. Miles don't leave you in a valley with your lungs full of water.

But her father's handwriting was upriver. And so, probably, were the people who knew why.

"No," Jonah said, climbing into the seat. "But go anyway."

Okafor laughed once, short and real, and pushed the throttle forward, and the Beaver clawed off the gray water into the gray air, and the harbor fell away beneath her like something she was finally done measuring distance from.

Below them, the river turned north, toward the narrows, toward the blank space on every map.

Toward the answer that felt exactly like a trap, and smelled like her father, and was calling her home whether she wanted to come or not.
