## 4. The Last Fuel Stop

The town was called Meridian Flats, which told you everything about how it had been named: by people who expected the river to make them rich and then left when it didn't. A gas station with a dead sign, a general store whose window displayed one jar of pickles and a rack of bait that had frozen and thawed so many times it had become something new entirely. A church with a steeple leaning like a man listening for a train that stopped running decades ago.

The charter pilot — Doss, he'd said, no first name offered — set the Cessna down on a strip of gravel that passed for an airstrip and killed the engine like he was wringing its neck.

"Fuel's a maybe," he said. "Town's a maybe. Everything past this is a no."

"You said you'd take me upriver."

"I said I'd take you to the last fuel stop. I said if." He climbed out and stretched until his spine cracked. "I've got a bad feeling about the weather, and a worse feeling about whatever's got the navy flying circles over a town with four hundred people in it. You count the helicopters, you tell me what kind of survey operation needs that."

I counted. Three, in the last hour, wide lazy orbits to the north where the river bent into the gorge country. Not search patterns. Patrol patterns.

The general store smelled of diesel and old coffee. The man behind the counter was maybe seventy and had the look of someone who had watched every interesting thing leave. He pumped fuel for Doss at the speed of continental drift and answered my questions the way people answer questions in towns like this: by telling you something adjacent.

"Survey crew came through in September," he said, ringing up nothing. "Bought out half my stock. Good tips. The cook was a talker."

"Which way did they go?"

"Up." He nodded north, at the mountains just visible past the church steeple, white already above the treeline. "And then some of them came back down. Different ones. Quiet ones. Bought fuel and didn't tip."

"When?"

"Ten days back. Maybe twelve. Time moves funny up here after the first frost."

Doss was outside arguing with the fuel pump. I bought coffee I didn't want and asked the old man if anyone had stayed behind. He shrugged, which up here apparently meant yes.

"There's a fella been sleeping in the boat shed down by the landing," he said. "Came off the river maybe a week ago. Half-dead, I'd say. Won't take charity, but he'll trade work for food. Talks to himself in two languages."

* * *

The boat shed leaned over the water on pilings that had rotted to the consistency of biscotti. The door was ajar, and inside it smelled of bilge, creosote, and a wood stove barely holding on. A man sat on an upturned crate with a blanket around his shoulders like a poncho, feeding splints into the stove one at a time, as if fuel were a language he was rationing.

He was maybe thirty, dark-haired, hollow-cheeked, with the hands of someone who cooked for a living — burns and knife scars, fingers that moved even when the rest of him was still. He looked up when the light changed and I watched him decide in one motion whether I was a threat. He decided I wasn't. Then he decided again. Then he decided I might be worse than a threat: a question.

"You're not from the store," he said. His voice was rough from disuse.

"No."

"You're not navy."

"Do I look like I row my own boat?"

"Everyone looks like they row their own boat until the wake comes." He almost smiled. Almost. "Who are you?"

"Jonah Hartwell. I'm looking for the Meridian Survey Expedition."

The splint in his hand stopped halfway to the stove. For a long moment the only sound was the river slapping the pilings underneath us, patient as a metronome.

"You're looking for them," he repeated. "All of them? Or one of them?"

"Whoever's left."

He stood, and the blanket slid off one shoulder, and I saw the frost damage on his ear — a crescent of skin gone white and hard, the kind of wound you get when you stop caring whether you live and the cold takes advantage. He crossed to the shed's single window, a square of river-fogged glass, and looked out at the water for a long time before he turned around.

"Teo Vasquez," he said. "I cooked for the Meridian Survey Expedition. Nine of us went up. Three came back down. I'm the only one who kept going south." He fed the splint to the stove at last. "You'd better sit down. And you'd better tell me why a stranger comes off a plane asking after us, because I've spent a week deciding whether the navy sent you, and I've about run out of reasons to say no."

"I'm not navy. The navy—" I stopped. Twelve years of practice at not saying it, and here it was, rising anyway. "The navy is the reason I'm here."

He studied me the way I studied water. Looking for what moved underneath.

"Sit," he said again, and this time I did, on a coil of rope that smelled of tar and seasons.

* * *

He talked the way starving people eat — fast at first, then slower, then not at all, staring at the middle distance where the story lived.

The expedition had gone up the gorge in September. Nine people: Mara Chen running the show, a geologist named Okonkwo, two techs, a river man named Brandt, a navy liaison who "wasn't navy, just an observer," a student, Teo, and one more whose name he said and I've lost, which is a thing grief does, it drops details like a clumsy waiter. They were mapping mineral claims for a company that had bought the survey rights from the government at a price so low the old man at the store would have called it a gift.

"Third day up the gorge, we found the falls," Teo said. "Not on the charts. Not on the satellite imagery. A whole river going into a wall of rock and coming out the other side. Mara wanted to survey it. The liaison — Ives, his name was, quiet guy, helpful, always first to volunteer — Ives said the falls were a hazard, said we should log it and move on. Mara's not the kind of woman you tell to move on."

He fed the stove another splint. His hands were shaking now, and he looked at them with something like betrayal.

"Behind the falls there's a cave system. Big. And in the cave system there's equipment. Old equipment. Survey gear from twelve years ago, sealed in waterproof cases, cached like somebody planned to come back for it." He looked up. "You know anything about that?"

The river slapped the pilings. I made myself breathe.

"My father," I said. "Elias Hartwell. He was a surveyor. He disappeared twelve years ago working a government contract up this river. They told my mother it was a boating accident. They never found the boat."

Teo closed his eyes like a man hearing a diagnosis.

"There were initials on the cases," he said. "E.H. And there were journals. And there were mineral assays, and maps, and—" He stopped, and his voice went somewhere I recognized, the place you go when the thing you're carrying is heavier than your arms. "There was a report. A draft report. About the watershed. About what got dumped into it in '61 and '63, and what it did to the water table, and what the government knew, and what the government decided to do about it, which was nothing, which was worse than nothing, because the draft said the data should be published and the data was never published, and the man who wrote it—"

"Was my father."

"—was your father. And then the team found something else. In a side chamber. A section of hull plating, painted navy gray, with a hull number still legible. And a date stenciled on it from twelve years ago." Teo's eyes were wet and steady. "Your father's boat didn't sink in an accident, Jonah. It was sunk. And the navy has spent twelve years making sure nobody walked far enough up this river to find out."

I sat with that. Twelve miles deep, I'd have called it, in the old dive-log shorthand of my head. Twelve miles down and no light.

"What happened to the team," I said. It wasn't a question. It was a door I had to walk through.

"Mara wanted to bring everything out. All of it. Publish, or give it to a journalist, or bury it in a courthouse steps, she didn't care, she said the river had been lied about long enough. Ives argued for logging it as a hazard and sealing the cave. They fought. And then—" He rubbed the frostbitten ear, a gesture like penance. "Then Ives made a radio call he thought nobody heard, and Okonkwo heard it. Okonkwo told Mara. Mara told the team. And the team split. That's the part I can't—" He shook his head. "Some of them believed Ives was just doing his job. Some believed Mara. Brandt took the boat and half the gear and went downriver to get help, and the boat didn't make the gorge, or it did and he didn't, I don't know. Okonkwo and the student stayed at the cave to guard the cache. Mara went after Brandt. I—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, I could see him hating it. "I ran. I took the emergency pack and I ran downriver like a coward, and I've been hiding in this shed eating the store's charity for a week, waiting for either the navy or the winter to finish the job."

"You're not a coward."

"You don't know me."

"I know the math," I said. "Nine went up. You're one of three who came down. The other two went downriver quiet and didn't tip, which means they were running too. Cowards don't come back down a gorge alone in September. Cowards stay where it's warm and let other people do the dying." I stood up. My legs had gone stiff on the rope coil. "Where's the cave?"

"Behind the falls. Two days up the gorge by water, if the water lets you. Three if it doesn't." He looked at me with the first real fear I'd seen on him — not fear of the navy, not fear of the winter, but fear of me, of what I was about to say. "You're not thinking of going up there."

"I'm thinking of what my father wrote in a report nobody read: the data should be published. He cached it because he planned to come back. He never came back. So somebody has to." I pulled my gloves on. "And the navy is flying patrol patterns over a town of four hundred people, which means they know the team found it, which means they're going to go get it before the snows seal the pass, and then it's gone. Twelve years of gone. Permanently gone."

"Jonah." He said my name like a warning buoy. "They sank a boat. They'll sink whatever they have to."

"Then we'd better be hard to sink."

He stared at me. Outside, one of the helicopters came over the town low, low enough to rattle the fogged window in its frame, and we both went still, two people in a boat shed pretending to be furniture until the sound moved north again.

"I know a guy with a jetboat," Teo said finally. "He owes me for a season of cooking. He'll rent it to us and pretend he didn't." He stood, swaying a little, and pulled the blanket off his shoulders like a man shedding a skin. "The gorge gets ugly past the narrows. Can you read water?"

"My father taught me. Before he taught me to read."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, closer to recognition, the look of a man finding a piece of a map he thought was lost.

"Then maybe," Teo Vasquez said, "we don't drown."

* * *

We bought the store's last two tanks of stove fuel, all the coffee, and a map that was thirty years out of date, which the old man sold us without comment and then, at the door, added one thing for free.

"The quiet ones who came down," he said. "They asked after the falls. Asked which fork of the river. I told them I didn't know." His eyes were old and clear. "I didn't know."

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me. Just come back down. Both of you." He looked past us at the mountains. "River keeps what it's given. Don't give it anything you want back."

Doss was preflighting the Cessna and refusing to meet my eye. "I'm not flying upriver," he said. "Whatever you're doing, do it without me."

"I need one thing. Mail a letter if I wire you the postage. From here, today, postmarked here."

He frowned. "What letter?"

"The kind you don't read." I'd written it in the store, on the back of the out-of-date map, in the twenty minutes while Teo negotiated the boat: everything I knew, addressed to a journalist in the city whose name my father had once mentioned in a letter home, back when letters home were a thing we did. Insurance, I'd told myself. Divers carry backup air. "Postmark matters. Today's date. If I don't wire you in three weeks, it goes out anyway."

Doss looked at the envelope, then at me, then at the mountains, and took it without another word. Some men you can read like water. He was one.

Teo was waiting at the landing with a jetboat the color of a bruise and an engine that sounded like a bad decision. The river ran gray-green under the last of the light, fast and cold and full of opinion. Somewhere north, the helicopters were turning their slow circles, patient as vultures that had learned to fly on schedules.

"You sure?" Teo said. His frostbitten ear was bright red in the wind. His hands had stopped shaking.

"No," I said, and pushed off from the landing, and pointed the bow upriver, toward the falls, toward the cave, toward whatever my father had left me that the navy wanted buried deeper than any diver could reach.

Behind us, the first snow of the season started falling on the high passes, early, too early, like a door beginning to close.
