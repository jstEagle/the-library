## 5. What Wasn't on Any Map

The river had a sound it made only at night, and Teo Vasquez had learned it by the fourth week: a low swallowing noise, like the valley clearing its throat. He was scrubbing the cook pot with sand when he heard the shouting start upstream — Mara's voice first, then Okafor's, then something that wasn't words at all.

He set the pot down in the shallows and left it there.

* * *

Three days earlier, they had found the falls.

Mara Chen ran her expeditions the way she ran her life: on a schedule so tight it squeaked. The Meridian Survey had eleven days left on its permit, six sample sites to close out, and a fuel cache to recover before the charter plane came back for them. The falls weren't on the itinerary. They'd come around a bend of the gorge where the canyon pinched to nothing and the water went from green to white, and there it was — a curtain of falling water forty feet high, pouring off a shelf of black rock into a pool the color of bottle glass.

"Unmapped," Okafor said. He was their geophysicist, a big quiet man who wore his instrument harness even at meals. "There's no falls on the survey. No gorge this deep either." He tapped his GPS unit, frowned at it, tapped it again.

"Satellite shadow," said Ives.

Teo remembered thinking, later, how fast Ives had said it. How ready the answer was. But that evening he'd only been thinking about dinner — rice and beans and the last of the dried chilies, because tomorrow they'd be hauling gear up scree and nobody climbs well on an empty stomach.

It was the kid, Priya, who found the cave. Seventeen days in the field and she still moved through the bush like it was a game, ducking under deadfall, testing every ledge. She came sprinting back into camp soaking wet, eyes enormous.

"There's a gap behind the water," she said. "You can walk behind the falls. There's a whole — Mara, there's a whole *cave* back there."

Mara looked up from her maps. Teo saw her do the arithmetic she always did — time against daylight against risk — and watched the numbers lose for once.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

They went in at first light, all six of them: Mara, Okafor, Priya, the two field techs — Sorensen and Delacroix — and Ives, who volunteered before anyone asked him to. Teo stayed with the boats. That was his job. Cook, boatman, keeper of the satphone. Somebody had to stay.

The gap behind the waterfall was exactly what Priya said: a slot in the rock you could walk through sideways, the falls thundering past close enough to soak you to the bone in three steps. And then the sound cut off like a door closing, and they were inside a cavern that swallowed their headlamps whole.

Teo knew all this because they told him that night, all talking over each other, and because two days later he went in himself and saw it with his own eyes.

The main chamber was bigger than the lodge hall back in his hometown, bigger than any church. Stalactites hung down like the ribs of some drowned animal. And on the far wall, stenciled in fading yellow paint, was a mark Teo recognized without knowing why until his stomach turned over: a survey benchmark. Government issue. Numbered.

Somebody had been here before them. Decades before them.

Okafor found the crates on the second day.

* * *

Teo heard the story in pieces, the way you hear weather coming. First Okafor just stopped talking at dinner. Then Mara started sleeping four hours instead of six. Then Priya wouldn't go in the cave anymore, and wouldn't say why, and Sorensen and Delacroix took to speaking in low voices that shut off whenever anyone else came near the fire.

On the fifth day after the falls, Teo cornered Okafor at the water filter.

"What's in the crates?"

Okafor looked at him for a long moment. He was a man who believed data belonged to everyone; Teo had watched him spend an entire evening explaining isotope ratios to Priya using marshmallows. Whatever was fighting behind his face right now, it was bigger than marshmallows.

"Come see," he said finally. "But not tonight. Tomorrow. When Ives is on radio duty."

"Why does it matter if—"

"Tomorrow, Teo."

* * *

What was in the crates:

Six aluminum cases, military surplus, sealed with wax and wire. Inside them, wrapped in oilcloth that had gone stiff as bark: notebooks. Dozens of them. Handwritten surveys, mineral assays, water samples logged season after season across twenty years, all in the same small precise hand. A name signed inside the front cover of the earliest one.

**E. Hartwell.**

And beneath the notebooks, wrapped separately, photographs. Black and white, then color, then the ugly washed-out color of cheap film. Drums being loaded onto a barge. Drums being unloaded into a mine adit upriver. A trench dug beside the creek, drums in it, soil pushed over them. Men in coveralls with no insignia. And one photograph — Okafor held this one under the lamp for a long time before he let Teo look — of a navy transport at anchor in the lower river, its deck crammed with those same unmarked drums, and a date stamped on the border that meant nothing to Teo until Okafor said it quietly:

"That's the *Corvus*. She sank in this reach twelve years ago. Officially it was a navigation error. Six men died."

Teo's mouth had gone dry. "Officially."

"The last notebook has the assay results." Okafor's voice was flat, the way a man's voice goes when he's holding himself together with procedure. "Hartwell tested the water below the mine site for years. Heavy metals, off the scale. Solvents. He documented the plume moving downstream, season by season, toward the valley towns. Then he compiled everything and tried to get it out." Okafor closed the notebook with both hands, gently, like it was a bird with a broken wing. "The ship was supposed to carry his evidence to the capital. It never arrived. Three days later, Hartwell disappeared. The navy declared the wreck unrecoverable and fenced the reach."

"And nobody ever—"

"Somebody filed a complaint. It went nowhere. Hartwell's daughter spent years asking questions, from what I can tell. She got called a crank." Okafor finally looked up. "This isn't a shipwreck, Teo. This is a man who found out what the navy buried in this watershed, and the navy buried *him* too. Right along with it."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside, the river kept making its swallowing sound.

"Why are you telling me and not Mara?" Teo asked.

Okafor's jaw worked. "Because I don't know yet which side Mara's going to land on. And I needed one other person to know I'm not crazy."

* * *

The trouble came faster than either of them expected.

Ives had been with the expedition from the start — hired through a logistics contractor, quiet, competent, good with the radios and better with the generators. He'd been friendly enough. He'd taught Priya card games. But in the week since the falls, Teo had started noticing things the way you notice a draft in a warm room: Ives's satellite calls home, longer than they should be. Ives absent from camp twice at hours when nobody else was moving. Ives asking Okafor, casually, over breakfast, "How much material did you catalog yesterday? For the manifest."

For the manifest. Nobody had asked for a manifest.

On the eighth night, Teo woke to voices in Mara's tent. He lay in his bag in the cold and listened, and the shape of the argument came through the nylon clear enough: Okafor saying *we publish, we take copies out, we don't leave anything here*, and another voice — Ives, smooth as river ice — saying *chain of custody, national security, this goes through proper channels*, and then Mara, tired, torn, saying the sentence Teo would hear for the rest of his life:

"He's right, Emeka. If we run it ourselves, we're just two more cranks with photocopies. Through channels, it becomes real. It becomes *actionable*."

"It becomes *buried*," Okafor said. "You've read the file. Every channel it touches gets poisoned. That's the whole point of what Hartwell found."

A long silence. Then Mara again, and her voice had the finality of a rudder hard over:

"We go through channels. Ives makes the call in the morning. That's my decision as expedition lead."

Teo stared at the tent ceiling until the gray light came.

* * *

He didn't decide to run. That was the thing he could never explain afterward, to Jonah or to anyone — that he hadn't decided anything. His body decided. He was packing his dry bag by feel in the dark, taking the spare satphone battery and the emergency ration tin and nothing else, when Priya's voice came out of the dark beside the boats, small and awake:

"You're leaving."

He froze. She stood there in her parka, seventeen years old, hugging her elbows.

"Ives made a call already," she whispered. "Tonight. I couldn't sleep, I heard him on the ridge with the long antenna. He wasn't calling any contractor, Teo. He used a frequency band we're not licensed for. And this morning he asked me which side of the river I'd be sampling on tomorrow." Her breath fogged. "Like he wanted to know where everybody would be standing."

Teo's blood went cold in a way the mountain cold had never managed.

"Get your bag," he said. "Quietly."

She was back in four minutes. They didn't wake the others. Later, that failure would sit in Teo's chest like a stone — Sorensen and Delacroix asleep thirty feet away, Okafor asleep with the truth in his head, all of them left to whatever Ives's morning brought. But in that hour, with the river loud and the sky still black, there was only one thought with any weight to it: *get downstream, get to the last fuel stop, get to a phone that isn't ours.*

They took the small cataraft. Teo ran the first rapid half-blind by memory, Priya clipped in behind him, and when the gorge walls fell away and the first gray light showed the valley floor, he looked back once.

The falls were invisible from this distance. Just a white thread in black rock. Behind it, a dead man's testimony sat in aluminum boxes, waiting for whoever came next.

Behind it, too, was Okafor, who had trusted him. Who had said *tomorrow*, and whose tomorrow Teo had stolen.

Priya touched his arm. "Where are we going?"

"Downstream," Teo said, because it was the only true word he had.

* * *

They made sixty miles that day, running water Teo barely knew, camping in a log jam with no fire. On the second day the helicopters came — two of them, dark, running the gorge line slow, and Priya pressed flat against the raft tube while Teo held them in an eddy under cutbank willow until the rotor noise faded upstream.

They were looking for something. Or someone. Or making sure nobody came *up*.

On the third day, Priya's boots gave out — the stitching split, and she was walking on wet socks wrapped in tape — and the temperature dropped like a trapdoor, and Teo understood the math he'd been refusing to do: they were not going to make the fuel stop together before the storm. Not both of them. Not at this pace.

There was a trapper's cabin marked on the old timber map, eight miles off the river. He left her there with the stove, the ration tin, the satphone, and instructions so simple a tired man could follow them: *stay, dry out, call the charter number, tell them where you are.* She cried and argued and finally gripped his sleeve with both hands.

"Come back for me," she said.

"On my life," he said, and meant it, and hated how easily the words came compared to how hard they'd be to keep.

Then he went downstream alone, into the freezing rain, because somewhere ahead was a town with a phone, and somewhere behind him was a cave full of truth, and somewhere between those two points the navy was already moving, and the only person he could think of who might believe him — the only person with a reason to — was a salvage diver named Hartwell whom he'd never met, whose father's handwriting he'd seen in a lantern-lit cave, and whose family's ghost had just swallowed his whole expedition.

The river carried him toward her the way rivers carry everything: without caring whether you were ready.
