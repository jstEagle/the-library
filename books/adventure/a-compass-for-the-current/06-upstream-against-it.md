## 6. Upstream Against It

The man who owned the jetboat was named Dree, and he wanted three things: cash up front, his boat back by Thursday, and no questions about why a woman with navy eyes on her wanted to run the gorge in October.

Jonah gave him the first two and lied about the third.

"Salvage job," she said. "Lost equipment upstream. Client's impatient."

Dree looked at her the way river people look at anyone who lies about water — not offended, just cataloguing. He was sixty if he was a day, with hands like braided rope and a face the sun had been working on for decades. Behind him, tied to a rotting dock that had once been a fuel pier, sat the jetboat: a fifteen-foot aluminum hull scarred white along both gunwales, an intake grate clogged with last season's weeds, an outboard that looked like it had been rebuilt more times than most engines get run.

"She'll do forty on flat water," Dree said. "She'll do nothing you want in the gorge if you don't know what you're doing. You know what you're doing?"

"I know what I'm doing."

"Your friend doesn't look like he knows anything."

Teo stood at the end of the dock with his arms wrapped around himself, still wearing the expedition gear he'd walked out of the mountains in — a shell jacket gone gray with grime, boots that had been resoled twice and were due again. He'd slept twelve hours in the back room of Dree's bait shop and woken up looking worse, like rest had only given his body time to itemize everything it had been through.

"I can bail," Teo offered.

"You can bail," Jonah said. "And keep your head down when I say down."

Dree counted the money twice, then folded it into a plastic bag and zipped it into his coat. "Navy's been on the river since yesterday," he said, conversational, like commenting on weather. "Two inflatables below the narrows and a patrol boat sitting off the old mill. They stopped my cousin this morning. Asked if anybody'd bought fuel."

"What'd he tell them?"

"He told them the truth. Nobody'd bought fuel." Dree almost smiled. "You're buying diesel, not fuel. Different thing entirely." He spat over the rail into the brown current. "Gorge runs high for the season yet. Snowmelt's done but the fall rains haven't started, so she's mean instead of murderous. Mostly." He handed Jonah the key — an actual key, brass, ancient, for an ignition that probably hadn't needed one in twenty years. "There's a channel through the gorge that isn't on any chart. My father showed me. His father showed him."

"I know the channel," Jonah said.

Dree studied her for a long moment. "Whose girl are you?"

The question landed harder than he could have known. She thought of her father standing in the bow of their old skiff, twenty years ago, pointing his chin at a stretch of water that looked like every other stretch of water and saying, *See where it goes dark? That's depth without rock. See where it goes green? That's rock under shallow. The river writes itself down, Jonah. You just have to read the handwriting.*

"Hartwell," she said.

Something moved behind Dree's eyes — recognition, then something older, something like grief or respect or both wearing the same coat. "Hartwell," he repeated. "He ran this gorge faster than anybody before or since. People said he was showing off." He looked out at the water. "He wasn't showing off. He just saw more than the rest of us. River gives you what you can see." He stepped back from the dock edge. "Bring her back by Thursday."

* * *

They cleared the last of the dead town within ten minutes, running the wide brown reach below the mill, and that was where the patrol boat found them.

It came off the bank a half mile back — a gray rigid-hull inflatable with two figures in it, too far away to make out faces, close enough to make out intent. It didn't chase. It didn't hail them on any radio. It just fell into their wake, half a mile back, matching their speed, patient as a debt.

"They've been doing that since dawn," Teo said. He'd taken up a position in the bow where he could see both the water ahead and the water behind, which Jonah suspected was the position of a man who had already been ambushed once and intended never to be surprised again. "Since I came down off the ridge. Inflatables. A helicopter yesterday. They don't stop you. They just — follow."

"That's the message," Jonah said. "We see you. We can take you whenever we want. Run anyway." She throttled up. "So we run."

The river narrowed ahead, and the walls began.

The gorge didn't announce itself. The valley simply tightened around them over the course of a mile, the forested slopes steepening, then verticalizing, gray granite rising on both sides until the sky was a strip of pale blue directly overhead and the river filled the world from wall to wall. The water changed color as it deepened and quickened — brown to tea to a dark glassy green that moved with a smooth, muscular surface broken here and there by white seams where the current found rock.

Jonah slowed at the mouth of the gorge proper, letting the boat drift while she read.

This was the part nobody understood who hadn't grown up doing it. Reading water wasn't knowledge, exactly. It was closer to language. Her father had taught her the grammar before she could ride a bike: *V-strings pointing downstream mean rocks under the surface — the water piles against them and spreads. V-strings pointing upstream mean a channel between rocks, water funneled clean. Smooth green tongues mean depth. Broken white means trouble, but the shape of the trouble tells you whether it's a rock you go around or a hole you go through.*

"Talk to me," Teo said quietly. Not a demand. An offer — give me something to hold onto.

"Green tongue center-left, past the first bend," she said. "That's our line. Everything right of it is a boulder garden." She pointed. "See how the current stacks up against that far wall? There's an eddy behind the point. If we get pushed wide we tuck into it and reset."

"And if we miss?"

"We don't miss."

She said it with more certainty than she felt, and felt more certainty than she'd expected to. Something was happening in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet — a loosening, a rightness, like a joint going back into socket after years out of place. Twelve years she'd been diving other people's wrecks, reading other people's water, working seas that had nothing to say to her. This river knew her name. She'd known its handwriting since before she knew her own.

She opened the throttle and entered the gorge.

* * *

The first rapid took ninety seconds and rearranged something in her nervous system.

The green tongue was exactly where the river had promised it would be, and she threaded it at thirty miles an hour with spray coming over the bow in cold sheets, Teo whooping once — a single involuntary syllable of joy that seemed to surprise him more than her — and then they were through, into the eddy behind the point, the roar falling away behind them, the walls closing in again above.

Behind them, distantly, the gray inflatable appeared at the mouth of the gorge. It slowed at the entrance. Hesitated. The men in it had charts, Jonah guessed. Charts and training and a chain of command, and none of it would tell them where the green tongue ran.

"They'll lose us in the narrows," she said.

"You sound sure."

"My father used to say the river takes sides. It just takes a while to show you which side it picked."

The narrows came an hour in, and they were everything Dree had promised. The river compressed to less than a boat-length and a half of navigable water in places, dropping fast, the granite walls leaning overhead so far they shut out the sun. Jonah ran it by feel and by eye, throttle feathered between full and idle, the hull skating across standing waves, threading gaps that opened and closed in the white water like doors in a dream. Twice she saw the dark V-strings too late and corrected hard, the boat slewing, Teo's bail bucket slopping, her own heartbeat a fist against her ribs. Once they went through a hole she'd meant to go around — a breaking wave the size of a truck — and the boat buried its nose, threw a curtain of water the length of the hull, and punched out the far side streaming, both of them gasping, laughing, alive.

*Alive.* When had she last done something that made her feel that word in her body rather than think it?

The channel no chart showed came at the worst possible place: a section where the main flow slammed into an unbroken wall of whitewater, a quarter mile of churning chaos that no boat could survive. Every map — she'd checked, years ago, obsessively, after her father vanished — ended the navigable river here. Portage trail, the maps said. Five miles around.

But the river wrote itself down, and her father had taught her to read.

"There," she said, and cut the engine.

The inlet was invisible until you were nearly on top of it — a slot in the left bank barely wider than the boat, hidden behind a fallen spruce, where a side channel slid away from the main current into the rock. The water in it was dark and slow and deceptively deep. On the chart, this was solid land. In the river's own handwriting, it was a back door.

"Dree told you about this?" Teo asked.

"Dree's family has known about it for three generations. My father learned it from a trapper in seventy-nine and put it in a survey that got classified." She nosed the boat into the slot. "Which is how I know the navy doesn't know it exists. Their charts end at that wall."

The side channel ran quiet for two miles through a canyon within the canyon, walls so close she could have touched both at once, the engine idling, the only sounds water dripping from the stone and their own breathing. Then it dropped through a chute no wider than the hull — a ten-second plunge that screamed the whole way down — and spilled them back into the main river a quarter mile below the unrunnable rapids.

Jonah looked back upriver. The gray inflatable was nowhere. It wouldn't be anywhere for hours — its crew would be hauling their boat over a portage trail built for pack animals, cursing, losing time they couldn't afford.

She killed the engine and let them drift, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The gorge rose sheer on either side. Somewhere above, a raven said one flat note into the silence.

"My father ran this whole river," she said. "Before I was born. Before the survey. He mapped every channel, every eddy, every lie the water tells. And then the navy took all of it and locked it in a vault because it proved what they did to this watershed, and they sank his ship with his evidence in it, and they let everyone believe he drowned drunk." Her voice stayed level. Her hands didn't. "I spent twelve years diving as far from this river as salt water goes. I told myself it was work. It was running. And now I'm back on his water, reading his handwriting, and it fits my hands like it never left."

Teo was quiet a moment. "Mara used to say the expedition wasn't really looking for minerals," he said. "She said Hartwell's survey was a compass. Pointed at whatever the navy most wanted buried." He looked at her. "We just didn't know we were the ones being buried."

Ahead, the gorge bent west toward the high country, and the light coming down the canyon had begun to change — thinning, cooling, taking on the metallic quality that mountain people learn to read the way sailors read swells. Snow weather. Days away, maybe. Hours, maybe not.

Jonah restarted the engine.

* * *

The patrol boat caught them at dusk, and that was how she knew something had changed.

It didn't come from behind this time. It came around a bend ahead of them, sitting broadside in a slow reach below the second gorge, floodlit, deliberate — a gray hull with NAVY stenciled on the side and a mounted gunner whose weapon stayed pointed at the sky in a way that communicated everything a pointed weapon would have. A figure stood at the bow with a loudhailer.

Jonah throttled down. Drifted. Put her hand on Teo's arm before he could speak.

The loudhailer crackled. "Jetboat, this is a naval safety vessel. The gorge is closed to civilian traffic by order of—" The voice paused, and when it resumed, the bureaucratic varnish had thinned. "Ms. Hartwell. Commander Hale would like a word."

The name went through her like cold water through a wetsuit. She hadn't heard it in twelve years — had heard *of* it, once, from a lawyer who'd represented the family in the inquiry that went nowhere, a name attached to a signature on a report that called her father's death an accident caused by operator error. Operator error. Her father, who could read this river blindfolded by the sound of it.

She stood up in the boat.

"I'll save him the call," she shouted across the water. "Tell him I'm going upriver. Tell him I'm going to find what he sank a ship to hide. And tell him my father says hello."

Silence on the water. The floodlight held her in a white circle, and she stood in it and did not flinch, and some distant part of her noted that this, too, was inheritance — the same stubbornness that had gotten Elias Hartwell's ship sunk now stood in his daughter's spine.

The loudhailer clicked off. A low conversation on the patrol boat's deck, too far to hear. Then the figure at the bow raised a hand — not a wave, a dismissal, or maybe a promise — and the gray hull swung away into the darkening reach, its lights shrinking downstream.

Teo exhaled beside her. "That was either very brave or very stupid."

"Usually the same thing." She turned the bow upriver. The current strengthened against them, and the last light was dying on the water, and somewhere above the gorge the first snow of the season was already forming over peaks they couldn't see. "Get the running lights. We run the night stretch while they're regrouping."

"And after the night stretch?"

Jonah watched the black water slide past, reading it by memory now, by blood. "After the night stretch," she said, "there's a waterfall nobody's supposed to be able to find. And behind it, answers that cost my father everything."

She opened the throttle, and the river carried them up into the dark.
