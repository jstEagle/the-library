## 8. Cold Camp

The cabin appeared out of the snow like something surfacing from deep water — a dark rectangle of logs with one shutter hanging by a single nail, half-swallowed by alder brush. Jonah saw it first because she'd been taught to see it. Her father used to say the country always told you where its shelters were; you just had to stop talking long enough to listen.

"Left bank, above the cutbank," she said. "There'll be a spring under that overhang. Trappers always built near water they didn't have to drill through ice to reach."

Teo squinted through the flurries. "You're making that up."

"I'm reading it." She adjusted her grip on the pack straps and felt the journals shift against her spine, that small dense weight she'd stopped noticing and now couldn't stop noticing, like a second heartbeat. "My father's trick. He could look at a valley and tell you where a man would've built a cabin forty years before anyone surveyed it."

"He sounds like he was fun at parties."

"He never went to parties."

The storm had been chasing them for two days, a gray wall sliding down the valleys behind them, and by mid-afternoon it caught up. The snow came in sideways, dry and hissing, erasing the river, the ridge, the sky — everything but the ten feet in front of their boots. When Jonah shouldered open the cabin door, the hinges screamed, and a winter's worth of mouse nests and old leaves skittered across the floorboards.

Inside: a stone hearth blackened by decades of fires, a bunk frame with no mattress, a table scarred with knife marks and initials. Somebody's name carved into the doorframe — *E. Kowalski, 1974* — and beneath it, smaller, *still here?*

"Nobody's been through since fall," Teo said, running his hand along the mantel. "No tracks. Woodpile's half full."

Jonah was already working. Kindling from the split cedar stacked under the eave, fire laid in the hearth, match cupped against the wind that followed them inside. The stovepipe drew clean on the third try, and the cabin filled with the smell of woodsmoke and thawing dust, and something in her chest unclenched for the first time since the gorge.

They'd made thirty miles in two days. It wasn't enough. She did the math anyway, the way she did everything — in distance. Thirty miles behind them. Nineteen to the falls. Eleven more after that to the high pass, if Mara's map memory held. Forty-some miles between her and whatever her father had left her, and somewhere behind all of it, a navy patrol boat and whatever else Commander Hale could put in the field.

Outside, the storm thickened until the windows went white.

* * *

By full dark the temperature had dropped hard enough that the frost climbed the inside of the glass in ferns. They'd eaten cold beans and half a brick of cheese, and Teo had rigged the bunk frame with a tarp and spruce boughs so Mara had something soft to lie on while her ankle swelled and unswelled like something breathing.

Mara slept in fits, muttering coordinates. Jonah sat with her back against the hearthstone, boots off, socks steaming, and watched the fire the way other people watched television.

"You always this quiet?" Teo asked.

He'd wedged himself into the corner farthest from the draft, knees up, a tin cup of snowmelt cradled in both hands. He had the particular stillness of a man who'd learned to be quiet around other people's moods — kitchen work, she guessed. You didn't cook for an expedition crew without learning when to talk and when to just keep the coffee coming.

"You always this chatty?"

"Only when I'm scared." He said it easily, no self-pity in it, and that ease disarmed her more than any joke would have. "Quiet people scare me worse. Quiet people are doing math."

She almost smiled. "I'm doing math."

"Yeah. I figured." He nodded at the window, the white blindness beyond it. "How many miles tomorrow?"

"If the storm breaks by dawn, eighteen. If not, we sit."

"And if we sit too long?"

"Hale catches us."

Teo turned his cup slowly. The firelight moved across his face — young, younger than she'd first thought, maybe twenty-six, with the kind of tired that doesn't wash off. "Can I ask you something and you won't bite my head off?"

"That depends on the question."

"The case you pulled off that wreck. The one with your dad's initials." He said it carefully, like handling someone else's gear. "Was he a diver too?"

Jonah looked at the fire. This was the moment where she could give him nothing — the wall she'd built was load-bearing, she'd built it well, twelve years of maintenance. But the cabin was small and warm and the storm had erased the whole world outside it, and there is something about a room with only one door that makes walls feel temporary.

"He was a hydrologist," she said. "Water survey. He mapped rivers — flow rates, mineral content, contamination. Government contract work, mostly. We lived out of trailers and rented rooms from Terrace to Whitehorse to places that don't have names, just numbers." She turned her hands toward the heat. "He taught me to read water before he taught me to read books. Said a river tells you everything if you know its grammar. Where it speeds up, where it lies, where it hides things under the surface."

"Sounds like he was describing himself."

She looked at him.

"Sorry," Teo said. "Kitchen habit. You spend enough years watching people, you start saying what you see."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You're right. That's exactly what he was like. Told you everything about himself except the parts that mattered." She fed a split of cedar to the fire and watched it catch. "Twelve years ago he took a contract upriver. Same watershed. He flew out on a Tuesday and the Tuesday after that, a navy transport went down in the sound — the one I dove last month. Official story was engine failure. His body was never recovered. Neither were four others. The navy paid out death benefits to the families and sealed the file, and everyone acted like grief was a receipt you signed and filed away."

"And you didn't sign."

"I ran." The word came out flat, factual, stripped of anything. "I got my dive tickets and I worked wrecks. Deep ones. Cold ones. Places where nobody asks questions because nobody stays long enough to ask them. Twelve years, I measured my life in miles. Miles are honest. A mile is a mile whether you're happy or not. And—" she heard herself say it before she decided to, which was how the truest things always left her mouth— "miles can't die. People die. Miles just accumulate. You can put three thousand miles between yourself and a grave and the grave doesn't get closer, doesn't call you, doesn't look at you across a breakfast table with your mother's eyes and ask why you're not eating."

Teo didn't say anything for a while. The stove ticked. Wind pressed at the cabin like a large animal leaning its shoulder into it.

"My sister died when I was nineteen," he said finally. "Leukemia. Slow version, the cruel kind, where you get two years to watch it happen." He turned the cup again. "After, everybody kept telling me time heals. Time heals. And I remember thinking — no it doesn't. Time just gets farther away from it. It's not healing, it's distance. Same thing you're doing, except I did it with years instead of miles."

"Did it work?"

"No." He laughed, short and real. "I cooked for the Meridian expedition because Mara asked me at a diner in Prince George and I said yes before I thought about it, and halfway upriver I realized I'd done it because being needed by eight people at three meals a day is the closest thing to family I've found since she died." He looked over at Mara, asleep under the tarp, her breathing shallow but steady. "And then the navy man showed up in our crew and started pulling it apart, and I watched it happen the way you watch a pot boil over — seeing exactly what's coming and unable to move fast enough."

"How many are still out there?" Jonah asked. "Your crew. Unaccounted for."

Teo set the cup down. He counted on his fingers, quietly, like a man laying out tools.

"Mara, obviously. Me. Dev Okafor, our geologist — he split with me the night we ran. He was heading downstream on foot along the east bank. If he made the fuel stop, someone would've seen him." He paused. "Priya Raghavan, our tech specialist. She stayed at the cave with Mara. When I left, both of them were alive. After what Ives did—" his jaw tightened— "I don't know. And Sam Ellery, our boat pilot. He was moored below the falls. Last I saw of him he was waving me off, telling me to run." His voice thinned. "That was nine days ago."

"Nine days in this cold."

"I know what nine days means." He met her eyes. "I do the math too. I just do it in people instead of miles."

The wind gusted and the cabin shuddered, and snow found some gap in the eaves and sifted down in a fine white thread that the firelight turned gold. Jonah got up, found the gap, and stuffed it with a rag and a handful of moss from between the logs. Small work. Work for hands. She understood why trappers had built these cabins — not for shelter from weather, exactly, but for shelter from the size of the country. A room small enough to hold.

When she sat back down, Teo said, "Your dad. In the cave. Did you find—"

"Not yet." She stared into the coals. "We're two days out. Maybe less if the weather gives." She hesitated, then heard herself continue, which frightened her more than Hale's helicopters had. "There's a page missing from the journal I have. The last one. Whatever he wrote in his final days, somebody tore it out — or he hid it separate. I keep thinking it's in the cache. I keep thinking he wrote it to me."

"Do you want to know what I think?"

"You'll tell me either way."

"I think he knew the ship was going to go down." Teo said it gently, but he didn't soften the shape of it. "I think he mailed copies or hid copies because he knew what happens to men who find out what the navy buried in that watershed. And I think the last thing he did was make sure his daughter could read the same rivers he did. So that someday, if she ever went looking, she'd be able to follow him." He shrugged. "That's not a man who abandoned you. That's a man who left a trail."

Jonah's throat closed. She looked at the fire until it blurred, and let it blur, and didn't wipe her eyes because Teo had the decency to study his cup very intently while she did.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned to a red heart and the storm had settled into its long overnight roar, Mara surfaced from sleep just enough to drink water and ask, in a voice scraped thin, "Are we moving at first light?"

"If the sky clears," Jonah said.

"It'll clear." Mara's eyes closed again. "Fronts like this burn out fast once they hit the divide. My father chased storms for the weather service thirty years. Trust me." A pause, and then, softer, drifting back under: "Jonah. Your father's data. The third copy. It's real. Don't let anyone tell you different."

Then she was gone again, and the cabin held its two awake occupants and its one sleeping fever and the sound of the world trying to bury itself.

Teo banked the fire. Jonah checked the door bar, the window latches, the back of her mind already running tomorrow's route — the river bend, the portage line, the elevation gain past the falls where the going would turn vertical and Mara's ankle would become a problem that couldn't be solved with tarp and boughs.

She lay down on the floor beside the hearth with her jacket rolled under her head, and found Teo looking at her across the dying light.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. Just — you check doors twice. Before bed, I mean. Even here."

"Habit."

"From the trailer years?"

"From every year." She pulled her jacket higher around her shoulders. "You learn early that the world comes through doors."

Teo nodded slowly, like a man filing something away. Then: "For what it's worth — I'm glad it was your boat that picked me up on the bank. Out of everyone the river could've sent."

"The river didn't send me. I came for my father."

"Sure," Teo said, and there was a smile in it, tired and warm. "Whatever current carried you."

She didn't answer. The phrase snagged somewhere in her chest and stayed there, unfamiliar, the way a word in another language stays after you've heard it once.

Sleep came for her in pieces. Once, near the coldest hour, she woke to absolute silence — the storm pausing between pulses — and heard, or thought she heard, far off down the valley, a sound that didn't belong to wind or trees. A mechanical sound. Distant, rhythmic, patient.

An engine, running low, somewhere in the dark below them.

She lay still and listened until the wind rose again and swallowed it, and told herself it was the river, ice shifting, the country settling. But she was already doing the math, and the math had changed: whoever was down there in the night was between them and the fuel stop.

Which meant the only direction left was forward. Up. Toward the falls, and whatever her father had left waiting in the dark behind the water.
