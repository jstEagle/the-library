## 9. The Waterfall Cave

The falls announced themselves an hour before we saw them — a low thunder rolling up the gorge like something enormous turning over in its sleep. By midmorning the sound had filled the whole valley, and by the time we came around the last bend of the river it was the only sound left in the world.

Jonah stopped on the gravel bar and just looked.

The water came off the rock face in a single unbroken sheet, forty feet of it, wide as a barn door, white at the top and green where it gathered itself before the drop. Mist hung in the gorge like weather that had decided to stay. The cliffs on either side were black with wet, streaked with old mineral stains — rust and copper-green running down the stone in long weeping lines, like the mountain itself was bleeding slowly and had been for years.

Teo stood beside her, breathing hard from the portage. He didn't say anything for a long moment. Then: "It's bigger than I remembered."

"Everything is," Jonah said, "when you come back to it."

She hadn't come back to it. That was the thing. She'd never been here in her life. But her father had, twelve years ago, and standing on this bar she felt the strange vertigo of walking into a place she already knew — knew from his handwriting, from the coordinates she'd photographed off that sealed navy case, from the way he'd described the approach in the journal pages Mara carried. *Approach from the east bank after low water. The cave sits behind the fall itself. You'll know it when the spray parts.*

The spray parted now, in gusts, the wind pushing the mist aside in slow curtains. And behind the water, when the curtain lifted, there it was: a dark seam in the rock, man-high, half-hidden by the falling sheet.

"You'll know it," she said quietly. "Yeah, Dad. I know it."

* * *

They left the boat cached under alder brush above the high-water line and worked their way along the east bank, over slick cobbles and deadfall, Teo carrying the dry bag with the medical kit and what remained of their food. Jonah carried nothing but her father's compass and the pistol she'd taken off the jetboat, which she'd checked twice and still didn't trust herself to need.

The last hundred yards were the worst. The rock shelf ran right along the base of the falls, and the spray hit them sideways, cold enough to take the breath, loud enough that they couldn't have heard a shout from ten feet away. Which cut both ways, Jonah thought. If anyone was following — and someone was; she'd seen the patrol boat's wake two days ago, holding station downriver like a dog told to wait — they wouldn't hear anything either.

She went first. Of course she went first. Teo had learned by now not to argue about it.

The seam in the rock was exactly where the journal said. She put her shoulder into the gap, turned sideways, and pushed through cold stone that smelled of wet granite and old iron — and then the noise changed, dropped away all at once, and she was through.

She stood in the dark and listened to the waterfall become a distant heartbeat behind her.

"Teo." Her voice sounded strange in here, flattened, close. "Headlamp."

His light bloomed past her shoulder and swept the chamber, and Jonah felt her breath go out of her.

The cave opened into a room the size of a small church. The ceiling rose in smooth black folds, dripping somewhere back in the dark, and the floor was dry sand — dry, impossibly, this close to all that water, as if the mountain had built this one room deliberately apart from its own plumbing. And against the far wall, stacked with a surveyor's precision, sat the cache.

Aluminum cases. Three of them, olive drab, stenciled with faded markings. A wooden crate beside them, its lid pried loose and set back carefully in place. A coil of rope hung on a driven piton. A camp stove, wrapped in oilcloth. Everything squared, everything labeled, everything waiting.

Twelve years of waiting.

Jonah crossed the sand without deciding to cross it. Her knees felt wrong, too loose, like the floor was moving. She knelt at the nearest case and read the stencil in the headlamp glow:

**R. HARTWELL — SURVEY PARTY — PROPERTY OF**

And then, beneath it, in marker, in her father's blocky hand, the word he must have added himself, later, after whatever happened here made him stop trusting the government that had hired him:

**PROPERTY OF NO ONE.**

Her throat closed.

"Jonah." Teo's voice came from behind her, careful. "We should check the back chambers first. Make sure—"

"In a minute."

She got the latches open. The case exhaled a breath of cold, dry air, twelve years sealed, and inside, packed in wax paper like something precious — because it was — lay the journals.

Seven of them. Hardbound, cloth-covered, the spines numbered in her father's hand. She lifted the first one and it weighed exactly what a book weighs and infinitely more. She knew these hands. She'd grown up watching them. The same blunt pencil strokes that had annotated her homework margins, that had drawn her treasure maps on napkins in diners, that had taught her to read water on a hundred rivers before he taught her to read anything else.

She opened to the first page and the date hit her like cold water: eleven months before he vanished.

* * *

They made camp in the cave mouth, where the spray-mist drifted through in cold veils but the sand stayed dry. Teo got the stove going behind a windbreak of cases, and the small blue flame threw their shadows huge against the black walls. Neither of them suggested leaving. There was nowhere to leave to until the patrol boat moved, and besides — neither of them could have walked out of this room yet if their lives depended on it.

Jonah read.

She read by headlamp while Teo cooked and pretended not to watch her face change. Page by page, week by week, her father's voice came up out of the paper the way bodies come up out of deep water — slowly, terribly, unmistakably.

He'd been contracted by the Navy Corps of Engineers, officially, to do a hydrographic survey of the upper watershed. Unofficially — and he'd figured this out by the third journal — the contract was cover. What they actually wanted mapped was the geology. Specifically the ore body.

She found it in the second journal, dated spring, twelve years gone. His handwriting getting faster, tighter, the way it did when he was angry:

*Assays confirm it. Chalcopyrite and arsenopyrite in quantities nobody upstream has any idea about. The deposit runs under the whole eastern fork. Here's what nobody will say out loud: development means tailings, and tailings mean leachate, and every drop of it goes into the water that six towns drink. I've run the numbers. I've run them three times. There is no engineering solution that makes this clean. They know. Whitmore knows. He looked at my figures today and said "mitigation is a phase-two concern." Phase two. Like the drinking water is a problem for next quarter.*

Jonah's hands had gone cold around the journal. Teo set a tin cup of coffee near her knee and retreated without a word.

Third journal. Summer. The tone shifting from anger to something quieter and worse — methodical fear.

*I've started keeping duplicate records. One set stays here in the cache, one goes out with me, and I'm mailing copies of the assay sheets to a lawyer in Juneau under a retainer I can't really afford. If anything happens to me, the data survives. I keep writing that sentence like it's a plan and not a premonition.*

*Whitmore asked me today if I'd considered "the broader strategic value" of my discretion. I asked him what happens to the towns. He smiled at me the way you smile at a dog that's barked at the mailman. "The towns," he said, "are downstream of the decision."*

Downstream of the decision. Jonah read the phrase twice and felt it land in her chest like a hook. Six towns. Thousands of people. All of them living comfortably, trustingly, downstream of a decision made in a room they'd never see, about water they drank every day.

Fourth journal. Early autumn. The last full one.

*They're moving faster than I thought. Survey equipment arriving that has nothing to do with hydrography — core drills, blasting permits filed under a timber company name. I confronted Whitmore. Told him I'd file my report with the state environmental office regardless of my contract. He didn't argue. That scared me more than shouting would have. Men only get calm like that when the outcome's already been arranged.*

*The transport ship arrives next week to carry the advance equipment out before freeze-up. I'm putting everything that matters aboard it. Every assay, every map, every photograph of the drill sites, the whole chain of correspondence including Whitmore's own memos. It's the safest place for it — a navy vessel, official custody, no one dares touch naval evidence.*

*God. I actually wrote that. I actually believed it.*

Jonah stopped reading. She sat in the stove-light with the open journal in her lap and let the knowledge arrive, because she'd known it since the sealed case came up off that wreck, since Reyes watched her too closely across a fogbound deck — but knowing a thing and feeling it are different animals, and this one had teeth.

The ship. The transport she'd spent the last month diving. The wreck the navy wouldn't name, sitting in ninety feet of gray water off a coast they wouldn't discuss.

They hadn't lost it. They'd sunk it.

Her father had loaded his life's work — his evidence, his testimony, his insurance policy for six towns he'd never met — onto a navy transport, and the navy had scuttled it with everything aboard, and then they'd erased her father, and then they'd waited twelve years for everyone who might remember to forget.

Except he'd been smarter than they gave him credit for. He'd kept duplicates. He'd built this cache behind a waterfall no chart named, in a room the mountain kept dry, and he'd written it all down in seven cloth-bound journals in handwriting his daughter would know anywhere.

"He hid it from them," Teo said softly. He'd come to stand behind her, reading over her shoulder. "The ship was the decoy. Or he thought it was safe and they proved otherwise, and the cache was the backup. Either way—" He touched the stack of cases. "This is the only copy that survived."

"The only original," Jonah said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Mara has copies. And whoever finds what I mailed—"

She stopped. Teo looked at her.

"Mailed?" he said.

"Later." She wasn't ready to explain the last fuel stop, the post office with the hand-cranked scale, the padded envelope addressed to a journalist whose name she'd pulled off a wire service masthead. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Some things you did alone because trusting people was a skill she'd never finished learning.

She turned back to the fifth journal — the thin one, the last one, three entries and then blank pages. Dated the final week.

*They know about the cache. I don't know how — Ives, maybe, or someone watching the falls. Two men came up the east bank yesterday asking questions in town. So I'm doing the last thing I can do. I'm splitting the record. The originals stay here. Copies go out tomorrow on the transport, and I'll carry a third set myself to the pass and down the far side, out of the watershed entirely, and mail them from the coast.*

*If I make it, this ends in daylight. Hearings, headlines, whatever it takes. If I don't make it —*

The entry stopped there. The next line was a single sentence, set apart, the pencil pressed so hard it had nearly torn the paper:

*If I don't make it, Jonah. If you're reading this someday, baby girl, it means I ran out of miles. Don't you do the same. Come home by whatever current carries you.*

Jonah closed the journal.

For a long time there was only the stove hissing and the waterfall beating its slow drum beyond the cave mouth and Teo breathing beside her, patient as furniture. She held the closed book against her sternum with both hands, the way you hold a wound.

"He knew," she said finally. "He knew they'd kill him, and he wrote to me anyway. Twelve years ago. He's been talking to me this whole time and I—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and let it crack anyway. "I spent twelve years diving wrecks. Looking in the water. He was up here the entire time, waiting for me to come home."

Teo crouched down in front of her. In the stove-light his face was all hollows and patience.

"So we finish it," he said. "That's why we're here. That's why any of us are here. Mara bled for this. The others—" He stopped, jaw working. "Two of our people are still unaccounted for, Jonah. Whatever your father started, it's ours to carry out now. All of it. To the pass, down the far side, into daylight. Exactly like he wrote."

Jonah looked at the cache — the cases, the journals, the rope on its piton, the whole careful architecture of a dead man's defiance — and the awe she'd felt walking in curdled, slowly, completely, into something hotter. Fury, yes. But fury with a direction. Fury with a current.

Because this wasn't treasure. She understood that now, fully, in her bones. No gold in these cases, no salvage value, nothing she could sell or spend or run away with. Just paper. Testimony. The truth about poisoned water and a murdered ship and a man who died trying to put it all into daylight — the one inheritance her father had left her, and the only one that mattered.

"They sank their own ship," she said. "Their own crew, maybe. Their own people. For a mine." She stood, and picked up the fifth journal, and zipped it into the inner pocket of her jacket, against her chest, where she could feel it with every breath. "Then they sent divers to make sure. Then they hired me to do it for them."

Outside, the light through the falling water had begun to fail toward afternoon. And with the failing light, faint under the thunder of the falls, came another sound — one Jonah's ears, trained on rivers since before she could read, caught and catalogued before her mind even agreed to hear it.

An engine. Upstream. More than one.

Teo heard it too. Their eyes met in the stove-light, and neither of them said the obvious thing, because saying it would make it true.

But it was true anyway. Someone had found the falls.
