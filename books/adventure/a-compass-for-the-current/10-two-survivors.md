## 10. Two Survivors

Teo smelled her before he saw her.

Not blood — though there was that too, a copper thread under the cold — but woodsmoke, old and faint, the ghost of a fire someone had burned days ago and let die. He stopped at the fork where the main passage split around a shelf of limestone, and he stood very still in the dark with his headlamp off, because twelve years of kitchens had taught him that you learned more from what a room gave up on its own than from what you went looking for.

Jonah's light swept past him, probing deeper into the cache chamber they'd spent the morning cataloguing. "Teo?"

"Quiet," he said.

She killed her lamp. The dark came down like a lid.

In the silence he heard it again: not water, not wind through the falls behind them, but something smaller. A scrape. Then — so faint he might have invented it — a voice, cracked to almost nothing, saying a word that might have been *here* or might have been *help* or might have been nothing at all.

"It's coming from the side chamber," Jonah whispered. "The one past the survey stakes."

"The one Ives said was flooded."

"Yeah." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Yeah, it is."

They moved together, Teo in front because he was wider and the passage wanted shoulders, Jonah behind with one hand on his pack strap so they wouldn't lose each other if the floor dropped away. The ceiling lowered until he was walking bent, breath loud in his own ears, and then the passage opened without warning into a low chamber no bigger than his kitchen back in Anchorage, and his light found a shape against the far wall that made his knees go strange.

A person. Sitting propped against stone, wrapped in a silver emergency blanket gone gray with dust, a climbing helmet beside her with its lamp still clipped to it, dead for days.

"Mara," he said. It came out as air, not sound. He tried again. "*Mara.*"

The shape moved. A hand came out of the blanket, filthy, fingernails black, and pushed back a hood, and beneath it was a face he'd cooked three hundred meals for — thinner now, hollowed, lips split, one eye swollen nearly shut — but unmistakably, impossibly, Mara Chen.

"You're late," she rasped.

Then she fell forward, and Teo caught her before the stone could.

* * *

They got her back to the main chamber by stages — Teo carrying her across the bad ground, Jonah running ahead to clear space among the crates and lay out the sleeping pads they'd hauled up from the jetboat. Mara weighed half what she should have. When he lifted her he felt the architecture of her ribs under his hands like a cage built too small, and he thought: *nine days*. She'd been down here nine days, since the night the expedition tore itself apart, since Ives put a rifle barrel in Denali Okafor's chest and told everyone whose side history was on.

Nine days in the dark, on a broken leg, alone.

Except not alone, it turned out. Not entirely.

"She had the dog," Jonah said quietly, nodding toward the corner as Teo laid Mara down. A small heap of fur lay there — a sled-dog mix, one of the expedition's pack animals, ribs showing, eyes dull but tracking. It raised its head when Jonah approached, decided she wasn't a threat, and put its chin back on its paws with the dignity of the profoundly exhausted. "It must have stayed with her."

"Biscuit," Mara murmured. Her good eye found Teo and held on like he was a fixed point in current. "She wouldn't leave. Wouldn't hunt. Just — stayed." A breath that was almost a laugh. "Loyal idiot."

"What happened?" Teo kept his voice level the way you kept a pan level when it was full of something that would burn. "Mara. What happened that night? We only got your last broadcast. *Ives isn't ours, get out,* and then nothing."

Her eye closed. For a horrible moment he thought she'd slipped away, but her mouth kept moving, slow, rationing words like the last of a food store.

"Ives waited until we were inside the falls. Until we'd found the cache and loaded the copies. Then he sealed the deal — called it in on the sat phone, coordinates, inventory, everything. I heard him. He didn't know I was close enough to hear." Her throat worked. "He told them we'd been compromised. That *we* were the security risk. And then he took the third data set off me personally — took it out of my chest pocket like he was taking my keys — and put two rounds into the generator and walked out with Whitfield and Sorren. Told the others I'd ordered them downstream. Told them—" her voice snagged, "—told them I'd gone back for the journals and gotten pinned by rockfall. Gave them a story clean enough to swallow."

"And you crawled back here."

"There was nowhere else. Leg was broken above the knee — Ives's parting gift when I grabbed his arm. I dragged myself back inside before the light died." She looked around the chamber, at the crates stamped HARTWELL SURVEY, at the waterproof cases Jonah had opened and spread with something close to reverence. "Figured if anyone came looking, they'd come here."

"We weren't looking for you," Teo said, and hated how it sounded. "We were looking for the cache."

"I know." Something moved at the corner of her mouth that wasn't quite a smile. "That's fine, Teo. Nobody comes into a place like this for a person. People are just what you find on the way."

Jonah crouched at Mara's side with the med kit open, and Teo watched her work — quick, unsentimental, hands steady on the swollen knee, checking pulse, peeling back the blanket to look at the leg without flinching. She splinted what she could and dosed what she couldn't, and all the while Mara watched her with an expression Teo recognized because he'd seen it in mirrors during the worst weeks after the expedition died: the look of someone trying to place a stranger who felt familiar.

"You're Hartwell's daughter," Mara said.

Jonah's hands didn't stop moving. "News travels."

"He talked about you. In the field, nights, when the stove was going." Mara's voice was fraying at the edges; Teo could hear the fever under it, a thin harmonic. "Said you read water better than any hydrologist he'd ever trained. Said you'd left because staying hurt worse than leaving, and that he understood, because he'd done the same thing to his own father." A pause. "He was wrong about a lot of things. He wasn't wrong about you being able to read this river."

"Save your breath," Jonah said, but her voice had roughened, and Teo saw her fold the journal she'd been carrying — the dead man's handwriting, the testimony — a little closer to her body, unconsciously, the way you shield a flame.

* * *

Later, while Mara slept in shallow, rationed increments and Biscuit pressed herself against the injured woman's side like a hot water bottle with a heartbeat, Teo sat with Jonah between the crates and the cold blue mouth of the passage, splitting the last of the cheese with his knife.

"Three copies," he said.

Jonah nodded. "His protocol. Dad never trusted one copy of anything. One stays in the cache. One goes out with the expedition lead. One goes somewhere nobody would ever think to look." She turned the page of the journal toward him, to a line in Elias Hartwell's tight engineer's hand: *Redundancy is love, properly expressed.*

"So Ives has copy two."

"Ives has copy two. Which means Hale has copy two, or will within the week." She chewed, swallowed. "Which means the navy already knows exactly what's in these crates, and exactly how bad it looks, and exactly why it can't surface. Copy three was insurance against copy two falling into the wrong hands. Mara was the insurance."

"And now we're the insurance."

"Now we're the insurance." She looked at him sideways. "You say that like it's a problem."

"It is a problem. It's the whole problem." Teo wiped his knife and folded it away. "Jonah. Look at her. She's septic or close to it — that knee's hot all the way to the hip, and she's been rationing water for nine days. She can't walk out. She can barely be carried. And the window—" he tipped his head toward the passage, toward the mountain beyond it, where the sky they'd glimpsed at dawn had been the color of a bruise coming up, "—the window is closing. You said it yourself at the fuel stop. First real snow seals the high passes for the season. After that, nobody gets over the range. Not us. Not rescue. Not Hale."

"Not Hale," Jonah agreed quietly. "Hale doesn't need the pass. Hale needs us to come to him, or needs the weather to do his work for him." She was quiet a moment. "Either way, he wins unless we move tonight."

"Tonight." Teo laughed, short and disbelieving. "She weighs nothing and everything. The boat's four hours downstream through water I don't want to run in the dark. The pass is two days beyond that if the weather holds, which it won't."

"Then we'd better be fast and lucky."

"That's not a plan, that's a prayer."

Jonah looked at him, and in the lamplight her face was the one from the gorge — the one that had threaded channels no chart showed, calm as arithmetic. "My father prayed with a compass," she said. "Come on. Help me rig a litter."

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the storm gathered itself somewhere above them, patient as tide. Teo broke down two pack frames and lashed them into a travois; Jonah cut foam padding from crate lining and rewrapped Mara's leg with a compression wrap that made the woman hiss through her teeth and then, grudgingly, thank her. Biscuit supervised from the corner, tail giving one slow sweep whenever anyone passed close, as if signing off on the operation.

Mara surfaced from sleep once, mid-afternoon by Teo's watch, and beckoned him near with two fingers.

"Teo." Her voice was a husk. "Listen. If it comes down to speed — if it comes down to leaving weight behind—"

"Don't," he said.

"—you leave me. You hear me? You take the data and the girl and you go. I've been dead for nine days. Everything since is housekeeping."

Teo crouched so their eyes were level, and he thought about the galley on the *Meridian*, about the hundred mornings he'd set coffee in front of this woman while she plotted the day's lines, about how she'd always eaten standing up, always, like sitting down was a concession she refused to make to gravity. He thought about the night the team split, and how he'd run, and how every mile since had felt less like escape and more like abandonment wearing escape's coat.

"Mara Chen," he said, "I have watched you eat standing up for eleven months. I have never once seen you quit anything, including arguments with river guides and including that time you fought a moose over right-of-way. I am not going to be the man who leaves you in a hole in a mountain. So save your breath for the climb, boss. You're going to need it."

Her good eye glittered. For a second he thought she'd argue. Then she exhaled — long, slow, surrendering something heavy — and said, "Fine. But if I slow you down past the point of stupid, that changes."

"Deal," Teo lied, and knew she knew he was lying, and saw in her face that she'd decided to allow it.

* * *

They were loading the last of the journals into the waterproof duffel when Jonah went still.

Teo followed her gaze to the chamber wall — to the section of limestone beside the cache crates where the lamplight caught something scratched into the stone. Letters, shallow, made with a knife tip or a piton point, in a hand he didn't know.

*E.H. + J. 3 COPIES. TRUST THE RIVER, NOT THE MEN.*

Below it, smaller, almost hidden: a date. Three days before Elias Hartwell vanished.

Jonah stood in front of it for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was stripped down to studs and joists. "He wrote this for me. Twelve years ago. He knew someone from our family would come eventually, because he made sure the coordinates ended up somewhere only we could read them." Her fingers hovered over the letters without touching. "He knew the navy would come too. That's why three copies. That's why 'not the men.'"

"Your dad planned his own disappearance," Teo said slowly, "as a message in a bottle."

"No." Jonah shook her head once, decisive. "He planned his *evidence*'s survival. Those aren't the same thing. He just ran out of time to survive along with it." She turned from the wall, and her jaw was set in a way Teo was beginning to understand meant the decision had already been made somewhere below language. "Copy three. Where's copy three, Mara? You said Ives took yours. Where's the one nobody would think to look for?"

From the litter, Mara's laugh came out cracked and feverish and somehow still triumphant.

"Ask the river," she said. "Elias sank it. Sealed case, weighted, dropped in the deepest pool below the falls — the one he used to call the vault. He told me the coordinates the night before Ives turned. Told me if anything happened to him or to me, the river would keep it better than any safe." Her eye closed. "Twelve years, Teo. It'll keep another twelve. But we won't, unless we move *now*."

Outside, far above the falls, thunder rolled — or something that wasn't thunder, the first fat flakes of the season ticking against stone at the cave mouth like static before a broadcast.

Teo looked at Jonah. Jonah looked at the storm.

"Two survivors," she said, shouldering the duffel of testimony. "Let's go make it three, and beat the snow doing it."
