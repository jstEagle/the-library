## 11. The Embedded Man

The radio crackled at 0400, and Sergeant Dean Ives was already awake because he had never really slept.

He'd learned to sleep in thirds back at Bragg — a third on watch, a third half-awake listening for the sounds that didn't belong, a third actually down if the first two went clean. Twelve years of fieldwork had compressed that into something less healthy: he slept like a man holding a door shut from the inside. So when the satphone buzzed against his chest where he'd zipped it into an inner pocket for warmth, he was conscious before it finished its first ring.

He rolled out of the bivouac sack without letting the cold in behind him, which was a skill, and stepped over the sleeping forms of the two hired rivermen who thought they were here as backup, and walked thirty yards downstream to where the gorge wall cut off sightlines from the camp.

"Go for Ives."

Hale's voice came through flat and close, the way it always did on the encrypted net, as if the man were standing at his shoulder instead of a hundred miles south in a heated operations center. "Report."

"They're moving." Ives kept his voice low, breath fogging in the beam of his red-lens headlamp. "Broke camp before first light yesterday, took the north bank trail past the slide zone. Three of them. The diver, the cook, and Chen."

"Chen's alive."

It wasn't a question, but Ives answered it anyway. "Alive when they pulled her out of the side chamber. Leg wound, bad enough they're carrying her on a litter made from pack frames. She'll slow them down." He paused. "She's also got the third copy. The one we couldn't find."

A silence on the line. Hale's silences were famous in certain circles — not the silence of a man thinking, but the silence of a man deciding how much of what he was thinking you needed to hear.

"The cache," Hale said finally. "Describe what they have."

"Journals. Survey folios. Mineral assays with chain-of-custody documentation." Ives had seen them for exactly ninety seconds inside the waterfall cave, long enough to photograph three pages with his phone before Chen's absence started to look like a problem instead of a convenience. "Water chemistry data going back fourteen years. Contamination curves for every tributary below the confluence. And the shipping manifests from the *Corvus* — including the ones showing what she was carrying when she went down."

"You photographed them."

"I did."

"Send them."

Ives thumbed through the images, watching them crawl up the satellite link one bar at a time. While they climbed, he looked upriver, toward the black seam of the gorge where the water ran loud even at this hour, a sound like a crowd murmuring just out of earshot.

Three days ago he'd stood in that cave with Mara Chen and watched her understand, page by page, what her survey team had actually found. He'd watched her face do the arithmetic — her crew, her expedition, her name on the permits — and he'd watched her decide she was going to be a problem. He'd done what he'd been embedded to do. Cut the rope bridge while she was on the wrong side of it, sealed the chamber entrance with the rockfall charge he'd carried upriver in a crate marked SEISMIC EQUIPMENT, and told the others she'd gone ahead alone to check the upper route.

Two of them had believed him. The cook hadn't. Teo Vasquez had looked at Ives with those flat dark eyes and said nothing, which was worse than saying something, and then Vasquez had vanished into the trees the same night, and Ives had reported him dead in the rapids because a missing man gets searched for and a dead one doesn't.

Now Vasquez was walking out of the mountains next to Jonah Hartwell, and Hartwell was the whole reason any of this was happening again after twelve quiet years.

The upload finished. "You've got them," Ives said.

Another silence. Longer this time.

"Hartwell has her father's original journals," Hale said at last. "Not copies. The originals, with his marginalia. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means she can authenticate everything."

"It means she can make a jury feel it." For the first time, something moved under the flatness in Hale's voice — not anger, exactly. Something more like recognition. "A document in a dead man's handwriting is worth ten affidavits from living men. I've spent twelve years making sure Elias Hartwell stayed a rumor. His daughter is carrying the antidote up a mountain in a waterproof bag."

"So we take it."

"We intercept." A pause. "Before the snows seal the range. There's a front coming down off the divide — forty-eight hours, maybe sixty, and then that pass is closed until June. If they cross, they reach the highway, the towns, the press. If we seal them in, we control the timeline." Hale let that sit. "There's a helicopter on standby at the valley strip. It's grounded until this front moves or breaks apart. Which means for the next two days, Sergeant, you are the operation."

Ives felt the weight of that settle onto him the way weight always did — not suddenly, but completely. "My assets are two river guides with hunting rifles and a man who thinks he's here for security contracting."

"Then be creative. You've been creative before."

That landed harder than Hale probably intended, or exactly as hard as he intended; with Hale it was impossible to know, and Ives suspected the ambiguity was the point. Creative. That was the word they'd used twelve years ago too, in a briefing room that didn't officially exist, when Commander — then Lieutenant Commander — Marcus Hale had laid out the situation with the *Corvus* sitting at anchor and Elias Hartwell's evidence sitting in her hold, and asked for volunteers to solve a logistics problem.

Ives had volunteered. He'd been twenty-six and certain that men like Hale knew where the lines were, and that the lines existed for a reason, and that standing on the right side of one was what service meant.

The *Corvus* had gone down in ninety feet of water with four crew aboard who'd been told it was a routine night exercise. Two of them hadn't known anything. The other two had known too much, and Hale had made sure neither category survived the flooding compartments. Ives had handled the scuttling charges himself. He could still draw the hull layout from memory. He could still hear the way the water sounded coming in — not dramatic, not like the movies. Just patient. Like the sea doing paperwork.

Twelve years. He'd told himself the math worked. One ship, six men total across both categories, against a watershed that fed three hundred thousand people — no. Against a cleanup bill in the billions, against a weapons program that would've been exposed, against a chain of command that went higher than Hale and certainly higher than a sergeant from Fayetteville. The math worked. He'd checked it a thousand times the way you check a locked door, and it had always held.

But lately the lock had been sticking.

Because of the girl. Because Jonah Hartwell had surfaced out of the fog off that coast with her father's initials stamped on a case, and the universe — which Ives had always privately suspected of being a bureaucracy rather than a mystery — had opened a file everyone assumed was closed.

He broke camp at 0530 and pointed his team upriver.

The two rivermen — Dade and Kowalczyk, ex-loggers with the forearms and the silence of men who'd spent decades around machinery that could kill them — fell into trail rhythm behind him without questions. The third man, Pell, the one who thought this was a private security contract, asked questions constantly, and Ives had learned to answer exactly none of them in ways that sounded like answers.

"How far to the pass?" Pell wanted to know, huffing up the switchbacks above the slide zone.

"Two days if the weather holds."

"And these people we're intercepting?"

"Hikers. They've got property that belongs to our client."

Pell nodded with the satisfied nod of a man who preferred not to examine things. Ives had selected him for exactly that quality. Dade and Kowalczyk he'd selected for other qualities: they would follow instructions precisely and ask nothing afterward, and afterward was where operations lived or died.

By midmorning they'd gained the bench of land above the gorge where the trees thinned and the trail — such as it was, a game path widened by survey crews years ago — ran along a slope of broken granite. Ives stopped at the overlook and put binoculars on the valley floor a thousand feet below.

He found them within ten minutes. He'd been tracking people since before Pell was born, and a party moving with a litter leaves a signature: the rest stops too frequent, the spacing between the strong and the weak stretching and compressing like a heartbeat with arrhythmia. Three figures and a burden, small as ants on the north bank trail, angling up-valley toward the place where the terrain funneled everything — water, game, human traffic — toward the high pass.

The only pass.

Ives lowered the binoculars and did the arithmetic he'd been doing all morning. The trio was a half day ahead but moving at a wounded woman's pace. His team was faster, unburdened, and fresh. Catching them wasn't the problem. The problem was what happened after, and the problem was the weather, and the problem — though he'd have shot anyone who said it aloud — was the specific composition of the party.

Jonah Hartwell had read water the way her father had. Everyone on the operation knew the story by now: how she'd run the gorge rapids in a jetboat with no chart, threading channels that didn't exist on paper, with a navy patrol boat eating her wake. Hale had listened to that report twice, Ives heard, and gone very quiet, and then said, *She's better than Elias was,* in a tone that Ives had turned over for days without deciding whether it was admiration or appetite.

And Teo Vasquez had seen his face. Had seen him in the cave, or near it, or had simply looked at him one evening across a cook fire and understood something Ives had spent twelve years keeping off his own face. That was the thing about cooks. They watched people the way snipers watched doors.

If it came to shooting, it wouldn't come clean. It would come messy, at altitude, with witnesses who'd already survived one betrayal and would recognize the second one instantly. And survivors who recognized things talked, and talkers needed handling, and handling was the word that had started to taste different lately, like meat going off so gradually you couldn't name the day it turned.

Ives pushed the thought down where he kept the others and keyed the satphone.

"Visual on the package," he said when Hale picked up. "North bank trail, making maybe a mile an hour with the litter. We'll have them at the scree fields below the pass by tomorrow afternoon."

"Intercept there. Not before." Hale's voice had the clipped cadence of a man reading from an internal checklist. "Above the treeline there are no other trails, no other parties, no variables. Whatever happens on that slope stays on that slope."

"Understood."

"The journals come back intact. The copies burn. And Hartwell—" Hale paused, and Ives waited, and the pause stretched a beat too long, which meant Hale was choosing his words, which meant the words had been reviewed by someone above Hale. "Hartwell is a loose end. You know what that means."

Ives looked out at the mountains. The front Hale had mentioned was visible now, a bruise-colored wall stacked along the western divide, beautiful the way dangerous weather is beautiful, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been late.

"She's a civilian," Ives said. "Salvage license, no record, no affiliation. Killing a civilian on federal land—"

"Is what happens to civilians who carry state secrets through a war zone, which is what she's doing, whatever her permit says." Hale's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the most frightening thing about him — twelve years of orders given in the same level tone, as though he were confirming a dental appointment. "You've read the same math I have, Sergeant. You did the arithmetic once before and it held. It holds now."

Ives said nothing. On the line, Hale seemed to hear the nothing.

"Doubt is a luxury of men whose work survives them," Hale said quietly. "Ours doesn't. Get above them tonight. Take the high route through the col. I want you positioned on the scree before they reach it tomorrow." A pause. "This ends at the pass, one way or another. Either the snows seal it with us holding everything, or it seals with nothing left to hold. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"One more thing." The flat voice dropped half a register. "Vasquez. If the opportunity presents itself, handle him first. He's the one who sees people."

The line went dead.

Ives stood at the overlook a moment longer, the satphone warm against his palm, the cold working through his jacket seams, the storm wall crawling eastward with its cargo of white. Below him, almost invisible now in the distance haze, three figures and a burden moved along the river toward the only way out of the range.

He thought about the sound the water had made coming into the *Corvus*. Patient. Like paperwork.

Then he shouldered his pack, turned uphill, and began to climb — taking the high route through the col, moving fast over ground no map showed, a man positioning himself above the trail like a stone waiting a hundred years for one particular footstep.

Behind him, the first flakes of the front came down fine and dry, tasting the mountain.

Ahead, somewhere past the scree and the col and the closing weather, the pass waited — and Ives understood, with the clarity that comes only to men who have run the numbers too many times, that tomorrow afternoon one of two things would happen on that slope.

Either he would finish what the *Corvus* started.

Or the mountain would do what the sea had done, and open the file all the way.
