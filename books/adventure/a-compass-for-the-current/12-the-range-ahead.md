## 12. The Range Ahead

The storm showed itself before it made a sound. From the treeline where the valley finally gave up and the rock began, we could see it sitting on the western peaks like a bruise coming up under skin — purple-black, low, patient. Weather that had already decided.

"Twelve hours," I said. "Maybe ten."

Mara laughed, which scared me more than the sky did. It came out wrong, thin and wet at the edges, and she had to stop and put a hand on a boulder to finish it. "You sound like your father."

"You didn't know my father."

"I read his field notes for three days in a cave." She straightened up, waved off Teo's hand when he reached for her elbow. "He sounded exactly like that. Like the weather was a schedule he'd already memorized."

Teo adjusted the strap of the pack with the journals in it — my pack now, though we traded it back and forth by unspoken agreement, because the man who carried the truth was the man they'd shoot first and we all knew it. He squinted west, then north, where the pass cut the skyline in a shallow V maybe four hours' hard climbing above us. "So we climb fast or we don't climb at all."

"We climb fast," I said.

Above the treeline there was nowhere to hide and nothing to hide behind, and every step was a step we took in full view of whatever was behind us. I made myself not look back. Looking back was how people died on scree — you caught a heel, you went down, and loose rock doesn't care what's chasing you. But some animal part of my brain kept counting anyway, kept insisting on a glance every hundred yards, and every time I gave in, the valley below was empty. That was worse. Empty meant Ives hadn't caught up. Empty meant Hale's people were still somewhere out of sight, moving on their own schedule, and their schedule ended with us.

Mara lasted two miles before the fever won the argument.

She'd been holding her own all morning — stubborn, silent, rationing her breaths like a woman who understood exactly what her body cost to run. Then she sat down on a flat slab of granite without deciding to, the way you sit down when the ground asks politely, and Teo was beside her before I'd even registered she'd stopped.

"Five minutes," she said. "Just — five minutes."

Her lips were gray. The wound in her side had been cleaned and packed in the cave with half our gauze and most of our hope, but infection doesn't negotiate, and three days of climbing had given it everything it needed. Her skin was hot as sun-heated stone when I touched her forehead, and she flinched from my hand like I'd pressed ice there instead.

"I'm fine," she said.

"You're septic," I said. "Or close enough that the word doesn't matter."

"Then we shouldn't waste time sitting." She tried to stand. Her legs informed her otherwise. Teo caught her, eased her back down, and crouched in front of her with his forearms on his knees, the way he used to crouch by his cook fire back when he was feeding an expedition instead of burying one.

"Mara." His voice was gentle in a way I hadn't heard from him yet. "Jonah can carry the pack. I can carry you. But only if you let us."

"I led eleven people into that valley," she said, staring at the rock between her boots. "I'm bringing out two. Maybe. If you're lucky, three, and one of them is me, and I'm the least sure about that one." She looked up, and her eyes were too bright, fever-lit. "I'm not going to be carried off this mountain because I got careless with a dressing."

"You got shot," Teo said. "That's not carelessness. That's betrayal. Different thing entirely."

Something moved through her face then — not softening exactly, more like a door opening onto a room she'd been keeping locked since the waterfall. She looked at me.

"There's something you should have," she said. "Before I'm not in any shape to argue about it."

* * *

She'd sewn it into the lining of her jacket — I should have guessed; Mara Chen struck me as a woman who sewed things into linings the way other people left notes on refrigerators. Three stitches of dental floss, a flat plastic sleeve, and inside it a single page torn from a survey journal, folded twice, soft as cloth from handling.

I knew the handwriting before I finished unfolding it.

I'd spent twelve years knowing it. Grocery lists. Margin notes in navigation manuals. A birthday card when I turned fourteen, mailed from nowhere, postmarked nowhere, the last thing he ever sent me: *Sixteen next year. Learn to read water. It reads back.* And then nothing, for twelve years, until a sealed case came up out of a navy wreck with his initials stenciled on the lid and my whole life tilted on its axis.

The page was dated three days before he vanished. The same date as the coordinates in the case. His last entry, or close to it.

It wasn't survey data.

*J.,*

*If you're reading this, then either I got sloppy or somebody got curious, and either way you found the cache, which means you also found out what I found, which means I'm sorry. Not sorry for finding it. Sorry that finding it put your name anywhere near it.*

*I've been measuring things my whole life — depth, distance, tonnage, yield. It's a good trade. Numbers don't lie and they don't leave. But I did the math on this one and the numbers say the water is poisoned from the source down, and the numbers also say the men who know will sink a ship full of evidence rather than let it surface, and both of those numbers are true at once, and there's no chart for that.*

*Here's the thing nobody tells you about salvage work: you can't recover what you won't name. You can raise a hull, an engine, a body. But if the navy calls it debris, it stays debris. If they call it nothing, it stays nothing. So this — all of it, the surveys, the samples, the photographs — this isn't treasure, J. It's a name. I spent my last week up here writing down its name.*

*If I don't come home, don't come looking. That's the part I mean. Don't come looking. Live your life in miles if you have to. Just know that wherever you run, the current runs with you, and currents always find the sea.*

*Come home by whatever current carries you.*

*Dad*

I read it twice. The second time my eyes weren't much use. The wind came up the slope and flattened the page against my hands and I held on to it like it might go over the edge.

"He wrote it the night before he went down to the ship," Mara said quietly. "We found it tucked in the back of the main journal. There was an envelope with your name on it, sealed. Ives went through everything we recovered, but he never opened that one. I think he assumed it was data." Her mouth twisted. "Men like him assume everything important is data."

"Why didn't you give it to me at the cave?"

"Because at the cave you were still deciding whether to take the journals at all." She said it without apology. "And because it wasn't mine to give until you were the person who'd carry them this far. Your father waited twelve years to say something to you. I wasn't going to hand it over in the first five minutes."

Teo had turned away, giving us the fiction of privacy on an open mountainside, watching the bruise-colored west with his hands jammed in his pockets. When I folded the page along its old creases and put it in the inner pocket of my jacket, against my chest, where a compass would go if I still carried one, Mara watched me do it and nodded once, like a transaction completing.

"He says don't come looking," I said.

"He wrote that before he knew what the navy would do," she said. "Before the ship. Before any of it. People write instructions for the person they think is coming. You're not that person anymore, Jonah. You're the one who came anyway."

* * *

We climbed.

The grade steepened and the air thinned and Mara rode Teo's back in a fireman's carry he had no business sustaining, and I stopped suggesting rest stops because every stop was a debt we paid with daylight. The bruise on the horizon climbed faster than we did. By midafternoon it had swallowed two of the western peaks whole, and the light went strange and metallic, the way it does when the atmosphere starts clearing its throat.

I navigated. That was the inheritance too — not just the journals, not just the page against my chest, but this: reading terrain the way my father taught me to read water. Where the scree funneled, a chute followed. Where the snow patches clung in July shadows, a bench of stable ground ran beneath them. The mountain was a river upside down, and I could hear him saying it, that summer on the Klamath when I was fifteen and furious at him for being gone so much: *Everything flows, J. Rock, water, weather, money, men. Find the flow and you'll never be lost.*

We crossed a shoulder of exposed ridge with the wind hitting us sideways, and below us, far below, I saw it — a thread of dust on the valley trail, moving. Not animals. Animals don't move in a line.

"Hale," Teo said. He'd seen it too. He set Mara down behind a fin of rock and stood beside me at the edge, breathing hard, and we watched the line crawl.

"How many?"

"Hard to tell. Four. Five." He paused. "They're not hurrying."

"They don't have to hurry. They know where the pass is. There's one pass." I looked north. The V in the skyline was close now, close enough to see the snow in it, old snow, deep drifts banked against the lee side. Behind us the storm. Ahead of us the pass. And past the pass, another range, and beyond that the long descent toward roads, radios, witnesses.

One pass. One way through. They'd known that before we started climbing.

"So why aren't they running?" Teo asked.

Because they didn't need to beat us to the top. They needed to be there when we arrived. Because a man like Commander Hale didn't chase people up mountains; he arranged for them to arrive somewhere convenient. I didn't say any of it out loud. Some math you keep to yourself until you have to spend it.

Mara was shivering now, curled against the rock with her jacket zipped to her chin, and when I knelt by her her eyes tracked me sluggishly, a half-second behind.

"The page," she murmured. "Read it again later. Not now. Now you climb."

"I'm going to get you over this pass," I told her.

"No." She caught my wrist. Her grip surprised me — feverish and iron at the same time. "You're going to get yourself over this pass. And Teo. And if there's anything left over, you spend it on me. In that order. Promise me the order."

"I don't make deals with dying women."

"I'm not dying." A ghost of her real voice, the expedition leader, the woman who'd held eleven people together in a hole in the earth. "I'm expensive. There's a difference, and the difference is I intend to be worth it."

I promised her the order. I lied about meaning it. Then I got her up, got her arms over Teo's shoulders, got us all moving, because the first flakes came down while we were still on the shoulder — fat, slow, indifferent, the storm's advance scouts — and the light began to fail around the edges like a photograph developing in reverse.

The climb had stopped being a race somewhere in the last hour. I understood that now. It had become something else, something my father would have recognized and named without drama, because naming things was how he honored them. Pilgrimage. The difference was simple: a race you run to win. A pilgrimage you walk because turning around would be a different kind of death.

My father had walked up this same mountain carrying a name for a poison. I was walking up it carrying the name, and the page, and two people I had known for less than a week and would now dismantle the world to protect. Miles couldn't die. I'd built my whole life on that arithmetic. But people could die carrying miles for you, and Teo was bleeding sweat and effort into every switchback with Mara's weight on his spine, and I finally understood what the miles were for.

Not distance. Carrying.

The wind rose. The snow thickened. Somewhere ahead, in the white closing over the pass, five men were waiting, and behind us the storm ate our backtrail, and none of it mattered, because the only way out was through, and through was up.

"Jonah." Teo's voice, ragged, from behind me. "Talk to me. What's the flow?"

I looked at the mountain — the chutes, the benches, the drifts, the V — and read it the way he'd taught me, the way it reads back.

"Left of the drifts," I said. "Stay off the ridge. We go now, we go steady, and we don't stop until the top."

And below us, faint through the thickening white, a sound rolled up the valley that froze us all mid-step:

A helicopter, fighting the weather, and losing.
