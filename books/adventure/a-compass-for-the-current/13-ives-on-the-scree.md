## 13. Ives on the Scree

Teo smelled the man before he saw him. Cigarettes and gun oil, faint on the wind that came down off the ridge — a smell he'd catalogued without knowing it during those last weeks with the expedition, when Ives had sat apart from the fire every night, smoking downwind like a man who'd learned to keep his habits invisible.

He stopped so fast Jonah nearly walked into his pack.

"What," she said.

"Smoke." He lifted his chin toward the slope above them. "Not woodsmoke. Cigarette."

They were three hundred feet above the treeline now, the larches behind them stunted and bent like old men leaning into weather they'd been fighting for centuries. Ahead the mountain opened into a long field of scree — ten thousand tons of broken slate fanned across the face of the range, gray and loose and treacherous, the kind of ground where every step was a negotiation. The pass showed beyond it, a notch in the ridgeline still white with last winter's snow. Maybe two hours away. Maybe less.

Mara leaned against a boulder, her arm bound tight against her chest, her fever visible even in this cold — a flush across her cheekbones, eyes too bright. She'd stopped complaining about the pain an hour ago, which frightened Teo more than any complaint would have.

"Ives," she said quietly.

"You're sure?"

"He smoked the same brand as Hale's people. Virginia blend. Nobody up here smokes Virginia blend." Her voice was flat, clinical, the expedition leader surfacing through the fever like something rising through dark water. "He'll be above us. He won't come at us from below — he wants the high ground."

Jonah crouched, unslung her pack, and began redistributing weight with quick, economical movements: water forward, the journals wrapped in oilcloth and cinched against her spine where a fall couldn't crush them. Teo watched her hands and thought, not for the first time, that she moved like her father must have moved — like someone who'd learned early that hesitation was just another way of drowning.

"We could go around," Teo said. "West flank. It adds four hours."

"Four hours puts us on this face when the front hits." Jonah nodded at the horizon, where the bruise-colored cloud bank had thickened all morning, pressing down on the peaks like a thumb. "And it puts Mara in the open when it does. No."

"So we go straight through him."

"We go straight through him."

* * *

They crossed the scree the way her father's maps said to cross scree — in echelon, never in line, each of them stepping where no one else stepped, because slate fields carry sound the way water carries blood. Teo went first. He'd volunteered before he understood why, and by the time he understood it was already done: some part of him had decided, back in the waterfall cave or maybe back in the frozen cabin, that if anyone was going to bleed for Elena Chen's survey data, it wasn't going to be the daughter who'd crossed a country to claim it.

The rock talked under their boots. That was the thing about scree nobody told you — it wasn't silent, ever. Every step produced a small metallic whisper, plates of stone shifting against stone, and after twenty minutes Teo stopped trying to move quietly because quiet was impossible. You moved deliberately instead. You committed to each footfall fully, because half-steps were what sent slides running.

Halfway across, he heard the click.

Not stone. Metal. A safety, taken off, somewhere upslope and to the left, behind a fin of standing rock the color of old bone.

"Down!" he shouted, and threw himself sideways at Mara.

The shot cracked off the face of the mountain and came back twice, echoing, directionless. Stone spalled off the boulder where Jonah's head had been a half-second earlier. Then everything happened at once and nothing happened slowly enough to think about.

Jonah was moving — low, fast, zigzagging between boulders, not toward cover but *along* the slope, forcing Ives to track laterally across ground that didn't hold a sightline. Teo got Mara behind the biggest slab within reach and pressed her down into the lee of it, her breath coming hot and ragged against his neck.

"The packs," she gasped. "He doesn't want you dead yet. He wants the—"

The second shot took out the radio on Jonah's hip. Teo saw it happen — saw the plastic burst, saw Jonah stagger, roll, come up running without the radio and without breaking stride. A professional shot. A warning shaped like precision.

Then Ives stood up from behind the bone-colored fin, forty yards upslope, rifle shouldered, and Teo saw him clearly for the first time since the river camp: the same mild face, the same forgettable build, the man who'd cooked breakfast beside him for six weeks and passed coordinates to Hale the whole time. He looked exactly like what he was. Nothing at all.

"Jonah Hartwell!" His voice carried flat and clear in the thin air. "You can walk away! The woman stays, the documents stay, and you walk south and live!"

Jonah answered by putting a boulder between herself and him and starting to climb.

* * *

What Teo remembered afterward, in the years when he let himself remember it, was not the shooting. It was the silence between shots — how the mountain filled it, wind and ticking stone and Mara's breathing, and how in that silence he made the decision that would follow him longer than the bullet graze did.

Ives was traversing to cut Jonah off from the route to the pass. It was good tactics; the scree funneled there, a natural choke where the slate field narrowed between two ribs of exposed bedrock. Jonah would have to cross the funnel or turn back. Ives would be waiting at the narrow part with a rifle and patience.

But the traverse put Ives on the worst ground on the whole slope — a steep apron of loose plate rock overlying ice-glazed substrate, the kind of surface that held together right up until it didn't. Teo had grown up around mountains that lied like this one. He knew what he was looking at.

He also knew that Jonah didn't. She'd read rivers her whole life, but scree was a different grammar, and she was climbing toward the funnel with her eyes on Ives instead of her feet.

"Mara." He was already moving. "Stay down. Don't move unless the mountain moves first."

"Teo—"

"He can't shoot me and Jonah at the same time, and he needs the journals more than he needs either of us dead." It was mostly true. He climbed.

The slope shifted under him with every stride, plates sliding, his boots finding purchase and losing it in the same heartbeat. Forty feet below Ives's position, the ground steepened into the apron proper, and Teo did the only thing he could think of that a cook with no weapon and bad knees could do against a trained soldier.

He started throwing rocks.

Not at Ives — at the slope beside him, downslope, small ones first, then bigger, each impact sending a fresh rattle of slate running down the apron. Noise. Vibration. A message written in the mountain's own language: *this ground is alive.*

Ives turned toward him. Teo saw the muzzle swing, saw the man's expression flicker from professional calm to irritation to something colder, and understood with terrible clarity that the calculation had changed — that a cook throwing stones was now a problem to be solved rather than a variable to be managed.

The third shot hit him.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the lie of every movie he'd ever seen. It felt like being struck with a length of rebar, a white-hot line across the outside of his left arm just below the shoulder, spinning him half around and dropping him to one knee. The cold arrived instantly, then the wet, then — delayed, absurdly, like thunder after lightning — the pain.

He'd fallen across the pack with the journals. He didn't remember doing it. But his good hand was on the strap, and when Ives started down the apron toward him, boots deliberate, rifle leveled, Teo did the last thing his body had strength for.

He pulled.

Not himself. The slope. He hauled sideways on a slab the size of a door, the keystone of a little terrace of plate rock, and the terrace let go.

It went all at once, the way avalanches do — no warning, no gradual failure, just a sudden absence of coherence, hundreds of square feet of slate deciding simultaneously to become liquid. Teo rode it for a horrible sliding second, arm burning, world tilting, and then a rib of bedrock caught him like a catcher's mitt and he slammed into it and stopped while the mountain kept moving without him.

Ives didn't stop.

Teo saw it all, pinned against the rock with his ruined arm, unable to look away: the sergeant's boots skating on the accelerating slope, the rifle swinging wide, both arms pinwheeling for balance that the mountain had already revoked. For one instant their eyes met. There was no fear in Ives's face. That was the worst part. There was only surprise — the genuine, almost offended surprise of a man whose plan had accounted for everything except the ground itself.

Then the slide took him.

He went down the apron feet-first, riding the crest of the moving rock like a man surfing something black and hostile, and the slope steepened, and the slide accelerated, and near the bottom — where the scree field ended in a fifty-foot drop of clean granite to the talus below — the mass of him and the mass of stone separated, and Ives went over the edge alone.

The sound he made hitting the rocks below was small. That was the other thing nobody told you. From forty yards, a human body makes almost no sound at all.

* * *

Jonah reached him in under a minute, sliding the last stretch on her heels, and her hands were on his arm before he could speak — peeling back the shredded sleeve, reading the wound with a salvager's efficiency. Graze. Deep enough to bleed like a severed artery, shallow enough to miss everything that mattered. The furrow ran from mid-bicep almost to the elbow, a trench plowed through skin and fat, and Teo looked at it with distant fascination, the way you look at damage to a boat you're standing in.

"Lucky," Jonah said.

"Yeah." His voice came out strange, thinned. "Lucky."

She worked fast: pressure, the trauma pad from her kit, the compression wrap cinched with her teeth holding one end. Mara arrived slower, favoring her side, and stood over them both looking down the slope to where the slide had deposited its cargo.

Ives lay at the base of the drop, half-buried in slate, one arm flung out at an angle that told its own story. Even from here, even at this distance, the stillness was complete. Not unconscious-stillness. The other kind.

"Check him," Mara said quietly.

"He's gone." Jonah didn't move. "You know he's gone."

"Check him anyway. Because if he isn't, and we leave him, we're different people than if we do."

So Jonah went down, picking her way along the stable margin of the slide, and knelt over the man who had betrayed her father's memory and hunted her across a mountain range, and checked his throat, and stayed there a moment longer than checking required. When she climbed back her face was closed, unreadable, a hatch sealed against weather.

"Gone," she confirmed.

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind moved through the scree, making its endless dry whispering, and somewhere below them a raven commented once and fell silent.

Teo found that his hands were shaking. Not from the wound — the wound had a clean, manageable pain, almost abstract. The shaking was coming from somewhere deeper, some compartment of him that had finally processed what his eyes had recorded and his mind had refused: *I killed him.* Not with a bullet, not with a knife, but with a pull on a slab of rock, with intent, with the full knowledge of what the slope would do. He had aimed the mountain at a human being and the mountain had fired.

"He would have shot all three of us," Jonah said. She said it plainly, without comfort in it, and somehow that helped more than comfort would have. "He shot you. He was walking down to finish it. Whatever story you tell yourself tonight, start with that part."

"That's the part I'm telling myself." Teo laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "That's the problem. It's a true story and my hands won't stop shaking anyway."

Mara crouched beside him, careful of her bound arm, and put her good hand over his shaking one. Her fever-bright eyes were steady.

"In the cave," she said, "when Ives and Rourke voted to sell the survey to Hale's people, and I voted against, and it became two against one — do you know what I thought? I thought, *if I were braver, I'd have taken the third copy and walked out that night.* I thought my caution cost us everything." Her grip tightened. "Your hands are shaking because you're a man who just saved two lives and ended one and understands the difference matters. That's not weakness, Teo. That's the whole job of being human. The men who don't shake are the men like him."

Above them, the light changed.

Teo looked up and felt the words die in his throat. The bruise on the horizon had become a wall — a towering, churning front rolling over the western peaks like dirty foam, swallowing ridgeline after ridgeline, and the air had gone still in that particular way that meant the atmosphere was holding its breath before it screamed.

"How far to the pass?" Mara asked.

Jonah squinted at the notch in the ridgeline, then at the sky, doing arithmetic with weather the way her father must have done it.

"Ninety minutes, if we push. The front hits the high country inside two hours." She stood, shouldered the pack with the journals, and looked down once more at the slope that had swallowed Sergeant Ives — at the price tag hanging off the next mile of their lives. "We bury nothing. We mark nothing. We move."

Teo got to his feet with Jonah's help, his arm wrapped and screaming, and took one last look back at the place where a man had stopped existing. Then he turned uphill, toward the pass, toward the storm, and followed the Hartwells — father and daughter, map and compass — into the white.
