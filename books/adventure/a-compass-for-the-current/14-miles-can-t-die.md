## 14. Miles Can't Die

The crevasse opened in the glacier like a wound that had learned to be useful — a blue-lit slot forty feet deep, its upper chamber wide enough for three people and their misery. Jonah had spotted it the way her father would have: not by looking for shelter but by reading the shadow, the way the snow bridge sagged just slightly wrong against the wind-scoured ice around it. She'd probed it with an axe, found solid roof, and dropped them into it before the storm's leading edge could find them first.

Now they were inside it, and the storm was doing what storms did to this range — erasing the world.

Wind screamed across the surface above them. Snow came down the entrance shaft in fine white threads, hissing onto the floor of blue ice where Mara lay wrapped in both sleeping bags and every spare layer they owned. Her fever had climbed through the afternoon. She talked in fragments now — names Jonah didn't know, coordinates nobody needed anymore, once, terribly clearly, *Ives* — and then went quiet again with her breathing shallow and fast.

Teo melted snow over the little stove, feeding it fuel tablets one at a time like a man rationing hope. His graze had stopped seeping. He hadn't complained about it once, which worried Jonah more than complaining would have.

"We should be moving," she said.

Teo didn't look up from the stove. "We should be alive."

"The front's on us early. If we sit here—"

"If we go out there tonight, we die competently." He poured hot water over the last of the soup mix, stirred it, handed her the cup. "Drink. You've been navigating for fourteen hours."

She drank because refusing would cost energy she'd need later. The soup was mostly salt and memory of chicken. It was the best thing she'd tasted in days.

Above them, the wind changed pitch — rose, held, rose again. The sound of a mountain deciding something.

Jonah set down the cup and looked at the pack.

It sat against the crevasse wall, gray canvas, salt-stained from the river, one strap mended with fishing line from Teo's kit. Inside it: the journals. Her father's journals. Eleven years of survey notes, mineral assays, water samples logged in a hand she knew better than her own, and the final pages — the proof of what the navy had done to the watershed, what they'd sunk the *Meridian* transport to bury, what three people were now dying at altitude to carry over a pass no sane person would cross in October.

The pack weighed eleven pounds. She'd weighed it at the last fuel stop, out of habit, out of the old arithmetic. Eleven pounds. At altitude, in a storm, carrying a feverish woman down off a glacier, eleven pounds was the difference between making the pass and not making it. Between all three of them living and some fraction of them living. Every ounce had a price up here, and the price was paid in breath, in heat, in margin.

*Drop it,* said the voice that had kept her alive for twelve years. *You can testify from memory. You've read every page twice. Drop the weight, move fast, get Mara down the mountain.*

And underneath that voice, quieter, older, the other one — her father's voice, reading tide tables to her when she was nine, teaching her that a chart was a promise you made to whoever came after you. *A survey nobody finds is just paper, Jo. The work is getting it home.*

She stared at the pack until Teo noticed.

"What," he said.

"Nothing."

"Jonah."

"It's nothing." She stood, too fast, and the crevasse ceiling pressed close — blue ice overhead, lit faintly by the stove, like being inside a bottle of cold light. "I'm going to check the entrance shaft."

"You checked it an hour ago."

"I'm going to check it again."

She climbed the ice steps they'd cut into the wall, hooked herself to the anchor she'd placed, and put her head up into the weather. It was worse. Of course it was worse. The wind had gone from screaming to a flat white roar, and the temperature was falling in a way she could feel through her gloves, a slow theft. Somewhere south of them, Hale was climbing. Somewhere behind them, Ives was dead on a scree slope and his radio was silent or it wasn't, and either answer was bad.

She stayed up there longer than the check required, letting the wind hammer her, because it was easier than going back down and saying what she was thinking.

When she finally descended, Teo had laid out the contents of the pack without asking permission. The journals in their oilcloth wrap. The copied pages Mara had carried. Her father's last entry, the one Mara had given her on the ascent, the one she still hadn't been able to read twice.

"You're thinking about leaving them," Teo said. Not a question.

Jonah sat down across from him. The stove hissed. Above them the storm worked the glacier like a man working a grindstone.

"Eleven pounds," she said.

"I know what they weigh."

"At altitude? It's not eleven pounds. It's more. And Mara's getting worse, and if the storm breaks tomorrow we move fast or we don't move at all, and—" She heard her own voice go flat and efficient, the salvage-diver's voice, the voice that calculated air and distance and bottom time and never flinched, and she hated it, and she couldn't stop it. "I can carry the testimony in my head. That's enough. That's always been enough."

"For you."

"For anyone."

Teo fed another tablet to the stove. He was quiet long enough that she thought he might let it go. He didn't.

"My sister died in a bus accident outside Cusco," he said. "I wasn't there. I was on a river two hundred miles away, cooking for a geological survey, because the money was good and the survey kept moving and I liked that it kept moving. I found out nine days later because there was no phone where we were camped and nobody thought to send a runner." He turned the spoon over in his fingers. "Nine days. She was buried before I even knew."

"Teo—"

"After that I took jobs that kept me close to towns. Close to phones. I told myself it was practical." He looked up. "But it was the same thing you do. I was measuring my life in miles from the next place grief could reach me. And here's what I learned, Jonah, sitting in that cave behind the waterfall listening to Mara argue with a man who was going to sell us all: the miles don't work. Grief doesn't have legs. It doesn't need roads. It was already in the tent with me the whole time. All those miles bought me was nine days of not knowing."

Jonah's hands had gone still in her lap. On the ice between them, her father's journal lay open to a page of water-sample numbers, columns of them, patient as prayer.

"That's not the same," she said.

"No?"

"I left places. People. I left a man in Ketchikan who asked me to stay and I was on a boat north before the week was out. I left my mother's funeral early because the harbor was calling a window and I told myself the tide wouldn't wait." The words came out of her like water finding a crack, pressure she hadn't known was there. "Miles can't die, Teo. That was the whole point. A mile is the only thing I ever loved that couldn't leave me first. You put enough water between you and everyone who matters, and nothing that happens to them can happen to you."

"And has it worked?"

The wind answered for her, a long descending howl down the entrance shaft, snow sifting onto the blue floor like ash.

"Mara's dying eleven pounds lighter because of what's in that pack," Teo said gently. "Not heavier. Think about it. Everything bad that's happened since the falls — Ives, the bullet, the climb — none of it is because you carried the journals. And everything good that comes after — the hearings, the cleanup, her name cleared, your father's name cleared — none of it happens unless someone carries them over that pass. The weight isn't the problem, Jonah. The weight is the whole point. Some things are supposed to be heavy. That's how you know they matter."

"He'd have carried them," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "He carried them into the ground. He died for paper."

"He lived for it. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone, because you're the one who taught yourself to tell charts from treasure." Teo reached over and closed the journal, carefully, and pushed the pack toward her. "Your father spent eleven years building testimony so that someday somebody could stand in a hearing room and say *this happened, here are the numbers.* You're that somebody. You don't get to hand that job to your memory. Memory is what I had for my sister, and it wasn't enough. Memory is a story we tell ourselves about the dead. Paper is what the dead tell us."

Jonah picked up the pack. Held it. Eleven pounds, or whatever they weigh up here where pounds are paid for in breath.

"I keep waiting," she said, "to feel like running. That's the thing. My whole life, when it got hard, I felt the pull — north, seaward, anywhere — and I followed it, and it saved me, and I called that survival. And right now, sitting here, I should feel it. Storm coming. Hale behind us. Everything I've ever done says *go light, go fast, go alone.*" She looked at Mara, flushed and muttering under the sleeping bags, then at Teo, his graze crusted brown along his ribs, holding the stove together with his hands and his stubbornness. "And I don't feel it. I feel like staying. I feel like this — this freezing hole in a glacier with two half-dead people — is the first place I've ever actually been."

Teo smiled. It transformed his face, made him look briefly like the cook he'd been instead of the fugitive he'd become. "Welcome to having a family," he said. "Terrible real estate. Great company."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her — and it broke something loose in her chest that had been locked since the fog bank off the coast, since the case stamped with her father's initials came up dripping from twelve miles of dark water.

They repacked together. Journals in the oilcloth, centered against her back where the frame took the load. Copies split between Teo's jacket and hers, so no single fall, no single bullet, could take all of it. She divided the remaining food with the old precision and gave Mara's share the largest portion, and when Teo raised an eyebrow she said, "She'll need it if the fever breaks," and he said, "When," and she let him win that one.

Then she did the thing she'd been avoiding. She took out her father's final page — the one Mara had pressed into her hands above the treeline, fever-bright, whispering *he wrote it for you* — and she read it all the way through for the second time.

It wasn't survey data. It wasn't coordinates. It was four lines, written in a shaking hand, dated three days before the *Meridian* sailed and he vanished with it:

*If anyone finds this, find my daughter. Jonah reads water the way I never could — she sees the current under the current. Tell her the cache is real, the work is true, and she shouldn't come looking for me. Tell her to come home by whatever current carries her.*

Jonah folded it along its worn creases and put it in the inner pocket, against her chest, where her heartbeat could keep it.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. We wait out the worst of it here, six hours, maybe eight. Then we move, journals and all, all three of us, or none of us." She met Teo's eyes. "Nobody gets measured in miles anymore."

He nodded once, solemn as a vow, and went back to melting snow.

Jonah settled against the ice beside Mara, pulled the watch cap down over her ears, and let herself rest — really rest, for the first time since the river — while the storm ground over them and the glacier creaked its slow tectonic thoughts.

She woke to Teo's hand on her shoulder and his face wrong in the stove-light.

"Jonah." His voice was barely a breath. "Listen."

She listened. Under the wind, thin and rhythmic, rising and falling with the gusts:

Engines. More than one. Coming up the valley below the pass.

Headlights swept the underside of the storm clouds, far off to the south, like lightning that had learned to hunt.

"Hale," she said.

Teo killed the stove. In the sudden dark, the blue ice glowed faintly around them, and somewhere above, the storm was deciding whether to save them or bury them — and the lights in the valley said Hale wasn't waiting to find out.
