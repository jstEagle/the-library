## 15. Hale Closes In

The helicopter wouldn't start.

Hale stood on the gravel pad at the foot of the range and listened to the turbine spin down into that particular silence — the mechanical shrug of a machine deciding the weather was stronger than it was. The pilot climbed out and pulled his headset down around his neck.

"Wind shear off the ridge," the pilot said. "Front's moving faster than forecast. I'm not putting us on a rock in that."

"Forecast said we had until tomorrow night."

"Forecasts are written for people with options, sir."

Hale looked at him for a long moment. The pilot held it, which Hale noted and filed away. A man who would look back at you over a grounded aircraft was either stupid or honest, and this one didn't have the face for stupid.

"Then we go up on our feet," Hale said.

The pilot blinked. "The pass is two days' hard climb from here even in good conditions. The front—"

"I know where the pass is, Chief. I've been looking at it for twelve years." He turned to the four men waiting by the fuel drums, their breath steaming in the gray light. "Pack light. One day of rations each, one stove between two, ropes and cold kit. Everything else stays here."

One of them — Okafor, the big quiet one who'd carried a radio through three deployments before this — glanced at the pile of equipment as if weighing it. "And when we catch them?"

"We won't need to camp."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about orders that came wrapped in words like *at any cost*: they made argument feel like a luxury, and men who'd been fed on urgency learned to mistake it for purpose. Hale had spent a career learning how much weight a man would carry if you told him the mission couldn't afford hesitation. He'd never quite stopped being surprised at how far you could push before someone asked what the cargo actually was.

He took the lead himself. Not because he didn't trust his men to navigate — Okafor could read terrain better than most officers he'd served under — but because he needed to be at the front of this, close enough to the decision point that when it came, there would be no distance between him and it. Distance was how things went wrong. Distance was how a transport ended up on the bottom of a river with its evidence sealed in the hold, and how a surveyor ended up in the same water, and how a girl grew up measuring her life in miles because her father had taught her nothing else worth keeping.

The valley swallowed them within an hour. The trail — such as it was — followed the river's north bank through timber gone black and wet with the season, and above the treeline the ridge showed its teeth: scree fields, snow already lying in the couloirs like something poured there and left to set. The front sat behind all of it, a bruise-colored wall building along the crest, and Hale watched it the way he'd once watched tide tables. It would arrive when it arrived. The only variable he controlled was speed.

By midday they were moving well. He set a pace just short of breaking anyone and held it there, checking faces without turning his head, reading exhaustion the way other men read weather. Okafor on his left flank. Vasquez — no relation to the cook, though the coincidence had irritated him for days — trailing the heavy pack. Two others whose names he used correctly and felt nothing about. He had learned long ago that names were a currency you spent carefully; spend too many and you started owing people grief.

At the tree line they stopped to rope up, and Hale took out the satellite phone and called in.

The voice on the other end belonged to a man he'd spoken to perhaps six times in twelve years, always briefly, always in the flat administrative tone of someone confirming a delivery. This time the voice was different. This time it stayed on the line longer than it needed to.

"The situation has evolved, Commander. The survey materials are compromised. The woman — Hartwell — has demonstrated she won't be deterred by warning or obstruction."

"She's a salvage diver with a grudge," Hale said. "She can be deterred."

"You've been saying some version of that for twelve years."

The line crackled. Above them the wind had begun to talk to itself in the rocks, a low conversational mutter that would build into shouting by dark.

"What I'm telling you," the voice continued, "is that the calculus has changed. If those materials reach the pass and cross it, they're beyond recovery. If she reaches any population center with copies—" A pause, deliberate, letting the sentence die where it would do the most work. "You are authorized to take whatever measures the situation requires. Whatever measures. Is that understood?"

Hale stood with the phone against his ear and watched his breath drift sideways in the wind. Twelve years ago, the order had been softer. *Contain the situation. Ensure the evidence doesn't surface.* Words with room in them, room enough for a ship to be scuttled in deep water and a surveyor to be listed as lost in a training accident that had never happened. He had told himself then that the room was mercy — that soft language meant soft outcomes, that nobody upstairs wanted Hartwell dead, only silent.

Now the language had no room at all.

"Understood," he said.

"And Commander — the divers. The survivors. All of it ends on this mountain. Nothing comes down but your team."

The call ended. Hale put the phone away and looked up at the pass, a notch in the ridge still white with last winter's leavings, and felt something settle in his chest that wasn't quite resolve and wasn't quite dread. It was closer to arithmetic. Four people on the mountain, one of them wounded, moving slow. Five of his own, moving fast, knowing the ground. Storm coming in behind both parties like a door closing.

The math favored him. The math almost always favored him. That had never once made the math clean.

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon and into the first edge of the front, and Hale burned supplies the way he'd promised himself he would. At the abandoned trapper cabin — he recognized it from the aerials, knew the expedition had sheltered there two nights ago — he ordered the men to leave the spare stove, half the rope, and every ounce of food they could spare from the minimum. Weight was time. Time was the only weapon the storm hadn't taken from him yet.

Okafor watched him dump the provisions into a corner of the cabin with the expression of a man doing sums he didn't like.

"If the weather turns before we're down," Okafor said, "we come back through here with nothing."

"Then we don't turn back."

"That's not an answer, sir."

"It's the only one the mission supports." Hale shrugged his pack higher. "We go up, we finish it, we come down ahead of the worst of it. That's the plan. Plans don't require backup plans when the timeline holds."

"And if the timeline doesn't hold?"

Hale looked at him. Okafor was thirty-something, competent, married — Hale had read the file, everyone's file, because reading files was half his job and the half he was best at — and he was asking the question the way honest men ask it: not to challenge, but to hear it answered out loud so he could stop carrying it alone.

"Then we get wet and cold and miserable," Hale said, "and we finish it anyway. Move out."

They moved. The trail bent upward through krummholz that grabbed at their sleeves, then broke onto open slope where the wind hit them full in the chest and the temperature dropped ten degrees in fifty yards. Hale checked his watch against the light and did the arithmetic again. The expedition party had maybe eight hours' head start, but they were carrying a septic wound and a man who'd never worn crampons before today. Eight hours of head start against a burden like that was nothing. It was a rounding error.

What troubled him — what he turned over as he climbed, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth — was not the pursuit. Pursuit was simple. What troubled him was the order itself, and how little friction it had caused on the way down from wherever orders came from. Twelve years ago there had been meetings, hedging, the elaborate choreography of plausible deniability. Now there was a voice on a satellite phone saying *whatever measures* like a man approving a supply requisition.

That meant the stakes had grown. Or it meant the people involved had stopped believing anyone would ever ask questions. Both possibilities pointed the same direction: toward a mountain where five armed men were authorized to erase four civilians, and where the only person who might ever write down what happened was the woman they'd been sent to erase.

Hartwell. He'd read her file more times than any of the others. Father lost in the accident-that-wasn't; mother gone within the decade after, the way spouses of the disappeared sometimes went; daughter scattered across a decade of salvage contracts, always coastal, always transient, a life built deliberately out of departures. He understood her better than she would ever want to know. She was running the way he was climbing — not toward anything, but away from the shape of a loss neither of them had been permitted to name.

The difference was that she'd found something to name it with. And now she was dragging it up a mountain in a waterproof case, straight toward him.

* * *

Dusk came early, eaten by the front. They bivouacked in a shallow col below the second ridge, no tents, backs to the rock, stoves lit low behind a windbreak of packs. Hale ate half a ration and gave the rest to Vasquez, who was young enough to still burn calories like a furnace and proud enough not to ask.

From the col they could see the upper route — a traverse below a hanging snowfield, then the final push to the pass through a gap that the maps optimistically called a saddle. In clear weather it was a hard day's climb. In the weather now visibly spilling over the crest to the west, plume by plume, it was something else entirely.

"They'll hole up," Okafor said quietly, beside him. "Anyone sensible holes up in that."

"Anyone sensible isn't up there. A dying woman is up there. A cook. And a diver who's spent twelve years proving she doesn't know when to quit." Hale watched the plumes stream off the ridge, bending east, fattening. "They move tonight or they die in place. Either way, they're slower than us tomorrow."

"And if they've already crossed?"

"Then we follow them down the far side and this becomes a much longer conversation." He let that sit. "They haven't. Mara Chen needs evacuation, not a descent. Hartwell won't leave her. I've read her file, Sergeant. She leaves everything except people she's decided are hers."

Okafor was quiet a moment. "You sound like you admire her."

"I sound like I've studied her. Admiration is a luxury the same way backup plans are." But the word snagged somewhere on the way out, and Hale heard the snag, and hated that he heard it. Because it was true, in the way inconvenient things were true. There was something in the woman's record — the refusal, the return, the sheer stubborn mileage of her — that resembled a quality he'd once been praised for and had since spent like a depreciating asset. He had been the man who didn't quit, once. Before the river. Before the sealed hold and the sinking and the twelve years of managing a lie the way you manage erosion, one small repair at a time.

The radio crackled. Ives, from somewhere on the high route, signal barely holding.

"Base, Ives. Visual on the target party at last light — headlamp cluster, moving slow, maybe two klicks below the saddle. They've stopped. Looks like they're making camp." Static chewed the next words. "Storm's going to hit them before midnight. Confirm intercept authority."

Hale keyed the mic. The whole team was listening. He could feel them listening, the way you feel weather change.

"Negative on intercept in this visibility," he said. "Hold position, mark their location, report movement at first light. We converge at dawn. Nobody engages in the dark on loose ground. Am I clear?"

A pause too long to be static. "Clear, base."

He signed off and sat back against the rock. Around him the men settled into their bags, and the wind rose through the col in long sustained gusts that sounded, at intervals, almost like speech. Hale kept his watch on. He had never been able to sleep the night before an operation, and this one less than any, because this one had finally shed its language. For twelve years the mission had dressed itself in euphemism — containment, recovery, disposition of assets. Tonight the clothing was off. Somewhere above him in the storm-dark, a woman was keeping her father's testimony dry against her body, and his orders were to take it from her corpse if necessary, and the voice on the phone had said *whatever measures* without pausing to breathe.

He found that he was thinking about the transport. Not the sinking — he'd trained himself out of the sinking, mostly — but the moment before it, the hold sealed and the pumps rigged, the ship riding low and quiet in the fog while Hartwell's father stood on the dock arguing about chain of custody. Hale had watched him from the wheelhouse window. The man had looked, in the end, less angry than baffled — a scientist confronting the discovery that the world ran on something other than evidence. He had gone aboard because he believed the paperwork would protect him.

It hadn't. Paperwork protected whoever wrote it.

Above the col, the first snow came down — not the storm yet, just its outriders, fat flakes that melted on Hale's sleeve and left dark spots like small wounds. He watched them fall and did the final arithmetic of the day. Dawn convergence. Four hours of margin. A pass that would seal behind them all within thirty-six hours, sealing the story in with the bodies, if bodies there had to be.

He closed his eyes and let the wind say what it wanted.

At 0300, the radio woke him. Ives again, and this time the signal was strong, which meant Ives had moved, which meant Ives had disobeyed a direct order, and the first thing through Hale's sleep-fogged mind was not anger but a cold, precise recognition of what desperation sounded like when it wore his own uniform.

"Base, Ives." Breath ragged. Wind screaming behind the words. "They're moving. In this. Climbing for the saddle right now — I can see their lights. And sir—" a pause, filled with weather, "—I slipped. Lost my rifle down a chute. I'm continuing up. Requesting you confirm: whatever measures. Confirm."

Hale sat up in the dark, snow gathering on his shoulders, and looked up at the invisible pass where four lights crawled upward through the teeth of the storm, refusing to stop, refusing to die in place, doing exactly what he would have done in their position and hating them for it.

"Confirmed," he said. "Rouse the men. We move now."
