## 16. Whiteout

The storm came early, the way storms do when you've bet everything on beating them.

One minute we were picking our way through a saddle of gray rock with the sky still holding its shape above us, and the next the light went wrong — flat, sourceless, the kind of light that erases shadows and then erases the things that cast them. I stopped. Teo walked into my back.

"What," he said.

"Look at the snow."

There wasn't any snow yet. That was the problem. The air had gone granular, a fine dry hiss that wasn't falling so much as arriving, filling every gap between us and the mountain. Behind us the ridge we'd crossed an hour ago was already gone — not hidden, *gone*, as if the world had been rolled back to the morning of creation.

Mara made a sound against Teo's shoulder that might have been a word. Her fever had climbed through the night, and now she burned like a stove even through both our jackets. I put my hand on her forehead and felt heat radiating off her skin into air that was already below freezing. The wound in her side had gone from angry red to a bruised purple-black at the edges, and when I'd changed the dressing at dawn there'd been a smell I didn't let myself think about. A sweetish, rotten smell. My mother had died of something like that, in a hospital where they could pump her full of antibiotics for eleven days before it took her anyway. Mara had no hospital. Mara had me, and a first-aid kit meant for blisters, and whatever distance we could buy between here and help.

"Jonah." Teo's voice, tight. "How far to the crevasse field?"

I looked at the compass on its lanyard around my neck. It pointed north, steady as ever, indifferent as ever. Somewhere under that white sheet lay a glacier tongue my father had described in a journal entry dated nineteen years ago: *crevasse field, lower third, cross high right along the medial moraine, two hours if the visibility holds.*

Visibility did not hold.

"Two hours," I said. "If I can find the moraine."

"And if you can't?"

"Then we stand here until we freeze, or we walk until we fall in a hole."

He nodded like this was reasonable information. That was the thing about Teo I'd come to depend on without admitting it: he took bad news the way he took soup, without complaint, one spoonful at a time. He shifted Mara higher on his back. She'd insisted all morning she could walk, and all morning her legs had answered otherwise.

"Lead," he said. "I'll follow your tracks."

* * *

My father taught me dead reckoning when I was nine years old, on the deck of a charter boat in weather much milder than this but frightening enough to a child. He'd sat me down with the compass and a grease pencil and told me the trick of it, the whole secret, which is that navigation is not about knowing where you are. It's about knowing where you were, and being honest about how fast and how crooked you've moved since.

"You can be lost," he said, "or you can be found and not know it yet. Same spot. Different man."

We weren't men. We were three people staggering up a glacier in a white room with no walls, and I was keeping count in my head — pace after pace after pace, because I'd learned to count paces the way other people learn songs. Two hundred paces to the minute at my stride, adjusted for slope, adjusted for the wind shoving us leftward off our bearing. Every hundred paces I checked the compass against my wrist, corrected, held the line. Every thousand I called out the time to Teo so he could mark it, because a watch and a compass and a stubborn arithmetic are the only instruments that work when the world goes white.

The wind rose through the afternoon. By four o'clock it was screaming across the ice in long gusts that hit like hands, and the snow came for real now, horizontal, stinging, packing itself into every seam of my clothing. I couldn't feel my face. I couldn't feel my feet inside boots I'd trusted for six years. What I could feel was the compass needle under my thumb, and the count, and behind the count a voice I hadn't heard in twelve years saying *trust the numbers, Jonah-girl, the numbers don't lie even when your eyes do.*

The eyes lie worst in whiteout. That's the second lesson. Your brain, starved of horizon, starts inventing one. You'll see slopes that aren't there, ridges, figures walking beside you. Old mountaineers call them nulls. My father called them ghosts and said the only defense is to navigate like you're blind, because you are. So I navigated blind. I kept my eyes on the compass card and my mind on the count and I let the mountain stay imaginary.

Somewhere in the fifth hour — I think it was the fifth hour; time goes soft in the white — the ground changed under my boots. The rhythm broke. Instead of the hard glazed ice there was a crunching, tumbling instability, stones rolling under each step, and I knew what it was before I knew what it was: rock. Debris. The medial moraine, a river of stone riding down the glacier's spine exactly where my father's nineteen-year-old entry said it would be.

I stood in the shrieking dark-white and laughed. It came out of me like something escaping.

Teo caught up, hunched under Mara, ice crusted in his eyebrows. "What?"

"It's here." I grabbed his arm and pulled him close so he could hear me over the wind. "The moraine. Dad's line is good. Follow the crest — it runs northeast toward the crevasse field's upper edge. There should be a serac band, blue ice, maybe half a mile."

"How do you know it's northeast? You can't see anything!"

"Because I've counted!" I shouted it, and heard the madness in it, and didn't care. "Two hours at three miles an hour minus drift — northeast, Teo. Trust the numbers."

He looked at me for a long moment through the ice on his lashes. Then he turned his back to the wind and followed.

* * *

We found the serac band at dusk, or what I decided was dusk, because dusk in a whiteout is a decision you make rather than a thing you see. Below it, just where the journal promised, the glacier surface dropped away into a chaos of fissures — the crevasse field, invisible now, a graveyard wearing a bedsheet. And cut into the serac's base, half-blocked with drifted snow, a hollow. Not a cave. A scoop of shadow in the blue ice, deep enough to crawl into, sheltered from the worst of the wind.

My father had slept there. I knew it the way you know a handprint. When I dug out the entrance and got a lantern going, I found the wall blackened with old soot, and pressed into the soot, faint as breath, the ghost of letters: *J.H.* He'd marked it. Twelve years ago or more, he'd sat in this same cold pocket and burned fuel and written his initials in smoke, and some part of him must have known someone would come looking, someday, following his numbers home.

I got Mara inside and stripped her jacket and saw the wound properly by lantern light, and the sight of it closed a fist around my throat. The infection had spread past the dressings, red streaks climbing her flank like ivy. Her lips were gray. When I touched her she opened her eyes and they were bright and wrong, fever-bright, seeing something else entirely.

"Dad?" she said. "Dad, I found it. I found the whole—"

"She means the cache," Teo said quietly. "She's been talking to him since noon."

I fed her water and the last of the fever tablets and wrapped her in everything we owned, and then I crawled to the entrance and looked out at the storm and thought about Hale.

Because Hale was out there. I knew it in my bones the way I knew the moraine would be where the journal said. A man who burns his own ship doesn't stop at the waterline. He'd have pushed through the pass approach tonight, driving his team hard, because his orders were the same as ours: get over the range before the snows seal it. Except the snows hadn't waited. The storm had swallowed the mountain whole, and somewhere in that swallowing were armed men who couldn't see their own boots.

I hated how much comfort I took in that. Then I hated myself for hating it. Then I went back inside and did the math on our food, which was worse than the math on the weather.

* * *

They died in the night. We never heard it happen — no shots, no shouts, nothing carried through the wind except wind. We learned it the way you learn most terrible things: afterward, sideways, from evidence.

Near dawn the storm eased a fraction, the scream dropping to a moan, and I crawled out to check the world. The light was coming up gray and formless, the snow still sifting down, and the glacier below the seracs lay smooth and blank as fresh paper. Except it wasn't blank. Down-slope, perhaps three hundred meters out at the edge of the crevasse field, two shapes lay on the ice where the wind had stripped the snow thin. Dark shapes. Man-shaped.

I watched them for a long time. They didn't move. One of them had lost a glove and the bare hand was blue-black. Crevasses, I thought. Or cold. In this, cold does the work of bullets and nobody has to fire anything. Two of Hale's men, dead on the ice, and I felt — God help me — nothing. No triumph. No grief. Just a cold recognition, like reading a depth sounding: *here the bottom drops away.* They had come onto my father's glacier with orders to kill me, and the glacier had done what glaciers do, and the inheritance that was saving my life had spent theirs like small change.

That was the thing about my father's craft I was only beginning to understand. It didn't take sides. Dead reckoning would carry me through the whiteout and carry Hale's men into a hole in the world, and the compass would point north over all of it, steady, indifferent, honest. The numbers don't lie. They don't care, either.

Teo came out and stood beside me and looked at the shapes and crossed himself with a mittened hand.

"They were going to kill us," he said. Not asking. Reminding me. Maybe reminding himself.

"I know."

"You don't look glad."

"I'm not glad." I turned back toward the hollow, toward Mara's breathing, shallow and fast as a bird's. "I'm scared, Teo. Because if the mountain can spend their lives that easy, it can spend ours too. And Mara—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "Her wound's gone septic. I've seen it before. Without real antibiotics she's got maybe a day, maybe less. And the pass is still a full day beyond the crevasse field, and the storm isn't done, and Hale—"

"Hale is down two men and however many frostbitten," Teo said. "And he still has more guns than we do."

"Yes."

"So we walk."

"We walk."

Inside, Mara had surfaced from the fever enough to know us. I gave her water and she gripped my wrist with shocking strength and said, clear-eyed, almost gentle: "Your father's page. Did you read it again?"

"Last night."

"Then you know why we keep moving." She closed her eyes. "*Come home by whatever current carries you.* He wrote that to you, Jonah. Not to the navy. To you. Don't you dare die carrying it."

I packed the journals deep against my back, where my body heat would keep the pages from freezing brittle. I checked the compass. North-northeast, up the glacier's spine, toward the pass that would either save us or end us.

The storm was rebuilding behind the seracs — I could hear it coming back, a low pressure-roar like a freight train on an endless grade. We had a window, maybe two hours, maybe less, to cross the crevasse field while I could still read the ice.

I roped Teo and myself together, tied Mara between us, and stepped out onto the glacier with my father's numbers running in my head like a pulse.

Halfway across, the wind found us again.
