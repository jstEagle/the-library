## 17. The Pass

Teo smelled the pass before he saw it — cold iron and old snow, the thin mineral breath of rock that had never known a tree. He'd been breathing through his mouth for the last hour, saving his lungs, saving his legs, saving everything, because somewhere behind them in the dark Ives had died and somewhere ahead of them in the dark Hale was climbing, and the only currency they had left was distance.

They came over the last shoulder of scree as the sky went from black to bruise. The stars were still out above, hard and close, but east of them the horizon had turned the color of an old scar, and the light came up slow across the range like something being revealed against its will.

The pass itself was nothing. That was the joke of it — three weeks of river, cave, ambush, storm, and the summit was a saddle of wind-scoured gravel maybe forty meters wide, a cairn of stacked stones on one side, a view of two worlds on the other: the watershed falling away west into cloud, and beyond the ridge, the long eastern valleys where the rivers ran toward towns, toward roads, toward people.

Mara made a sound when she saw it. Not a word. A small wet sound, like something breaking underwater.

She'd stopped talking properly around midnight. She'd stopped walking an hour after that, and since then Jonah and Teo had carried her between them in a sling made from the tent fly and two pack straps, her boots dragging through the snow, her fever heat bleeding through the fabric even at twenty below. Every hundred meters they set her down and Teo pressed two fingers to her throat and counted, and every time the count was worse.

"Set her down," Jonah said now. "In the lee of the cairn."

Teo lowered his end too fast and went down on one knee beside her, and for a moment he just stayed there, forehead nearly touching the frozen gravel, breathing. His graze from the scree had stiffened overnight; his whole left side felt like a rope pulled tight. He looked at Mara's face — gray, cracked lips, eyes moving under closed lids like she was reading something urgent — and he thought about eggs. He thought about the galley of the *Meridian*, about the way he used to stand over the stove at five in the morning making breakfast for eleven people who complained about the coffee, and how he would give anything, anything, to hear someone complain about the coffee again.

"Teo." Jonah's hand on his shoulder. "Look."

He looked.

Down the eastern slope, where the switchbacks descended toward the tree line, there were lights. Not moving. Stationary. Four of them, arranged with the neat spacing of people who had been told where to stand, and above them, faint in the last of the starlight, the shape of a helicopter with its blades tied down.

"They beat us here," Teo said. His voice came out flat, factual, the voice he used for reporting a burned pot or a broken axle. It was the only voice he trusted right now. "How—"

"They pushed through the night." Jonah was standing very still, binoculars raised. "Same as us. They just didn't have to carry anyone." She lowered the glasses. "Six that I can count. Maybe more below the ridge line."

"Seven," said a voice behind them. "But who's counting."

* * *

Sergeant Ives had taught Teo one useful thing before the scree took him: that Hale liked to talk. Liked to walk out alone, hands visible, voice pitched reasonable, while his men held the geometry. So when the man came up the slope from the lights — parka unzipped, rifle slung, breath steaming, unhurried — Teo recognized the choreography immediately and hated how well it worked.

Commander Hale stopped ten meters out. Close enough to talk without shouting. Far enough that any sudden movement by anyone would be answered by seven rifles.

"Ms. Hartwell," he said. "Mr. Vasquez. And Dr. Chen." His eyes went to Mara and stayed there a moment longer than necessary, doing arithmetic. "You've done something remarkable. All of you. I want that on the record before anything else happens."

"Put it on your record," Jonah said. "I know you keep one."

Something moved at the corner of Hale's mouth. Not quite a smile. "May I?" He nodded at Mara without waiting for permission and crouched at a respectful distance, professional eyes cataloguing what anyone could see: the septic line climbing her forearm, the shallowness of her breathing, the smell. "She has hours. Maybe less. There's no aircraft that can reach this pass until this weather system clears — except mine, which is grounded at the base camp with the same ice on its rotors that kept you alive last night. But there is a snowcat road to the east, down the far side. Maintenance route for the relay station. We have a vehicle on it."

"Then use it," Teo said.

"I intend to." Hale stood, brushed gravel from his knee, and looked at Jonah. "Here is the situation as I understand it. You are carrying approximately nine kilograms of documents, film, and survey plates that do not officially exist, recovered from a site that does not officially exist, documenting events that did not officially happen. My orders regarding those materials are explicit. My orders regarding you—" he paused, and Teo watched him choose honesty like a man choosing which card to show, "—were also explicit. As of yesterday afternoon."

"And yet here we all are, talking," Jonah said.

"Because I have discretion in the field. Because Dr. Chen is dying. And because I have read your father's file, Ms. Hartwell, cover to cover, twice, and I believe he would have negotiated exactly the way you're about to." He spread his hands. "So let's not waste her hours on theater. What do you want?"

"The obvious," Jonah said. "Mara gets to that snowcat, then to a hospital, today. Teo and I walk away. Nobody follows. Nobody files reports. We were never on this mountain."

"And the cache?"

"Goes with you. All of it. Journals, plates, copies, everything in our packs. You burn it, bury it, put it in a drawer next to the ship — I don't care. I hand it over here, on this gravel, and I never see it again."

Teo's head snapped toward her. "Jonah—"

"It's done, Teo."

"It is *not*—" He caught himself, dropped his voice, though every man on that ridge could probably read lips. "We carried it. Mara carried it. People died carrying it. You're going to hand it to him because he asked nicely?"

"I'm going to hand it to him because Mara has hours," Jonah said, "and because you can't spend paper on a dead woman." She turned back to Hale. "And my silence. You get that too. I don't talk to journalists. I don't talk to hearings. I go back to diving wrecks nobody names, and this stays buried the way you wanted it buried twelve years ago."

Hale studied her for a long moment, and Teo, watching him, saw something he hadn't expected: relief. Real relief, arriving on the man's face like weather. Whatever Hale had been ordered to do if negotiation failed, he had not wanted to do it. That didn't make him good. It made him tired.

"Agreed," Hale said. "With one amendment. The medical evacuation happens regardless of what I find when I inspect those packs. If you've shorted me, Ms. Hartwell, I'll deal with that later — but Dr. Chen rides down either way. I'm not in the business of letting people die to make a point."

"That's almost decent," Jonah said.

"It's the part of the job I haven't lost yet." He turned and spoke two words into his radio, and down the slope the lights began to move.

* * *

The transfer took twenty minutes and was the quietest violence Teo had ever witnessed.

Two of Hale's men came up and unpacked their gear with gloved, methodical hands — the journals wrapped in oilcloth, the film canisters, the survey plates in their wooden case, the copied pages Jonah had made in the waterfall cave by headlamp. One of them, a young specialist with windburned cheeks, handled her father's handwriting with something close to reverence, and Teo hated him a little for it.

Mara woke during it. Came up out of the fever just enough to find Teo's hand and grip it with shocking strength.

"They're taking it," she rasped.

"I know."

"We carried it out. All three of us. It was—" Her eyes found Jonah across the gravel. "It was supposed to matter."

"It matters," Jonah said, kneeling so her face was level with Mara's. "Listen to me. It matters because it existed, and because we know, and because he wrote it down. What happens to the paper isn't the whole story. You got it off the mountain. That was the job. Now the job is staying alive, and you're going to do that too, because I didn't drag you across a range to lose you at the finish line. Understood?"

Mara looked at her for a long time. Then, faintly, she laughed — a dry crackle, half delirium, entirely real. "You sound like your father."

"I've been told."

The snowcat came up the maintenance road an hour after dawn, growling through the drifts, and they loaded Mara into it wrapped in every blanket Hale's men could spare. Before the hatch closed she caught Teo's sleeve.

"The third copy," she whispered. "The one I carried. It wasn't in the packs."

Teo froze. "What?"

"I cached it. Below the treeline, day before the ambush. In a hollow hemlock, wrapped in plastic, marked with a cairn of three stones." Her grip tightened. "Ives knew about the first two copies. He never knew about mine. I figured — insurance." Her eyes were burning now, fever-bright and utterly lucid. "It's still there, Teo. Everything they're taking up here is worthless if that copy walks off this mountain."

The hatch shut. The snowcat ground away down the eastern road, taillights shrinking into the white, and Teo stood there holding the knowledge like something hot, looking at Jonah, who was watching the taillights too, her face giving away nothing at all.

* * *

Hale walked them to the edge of the pass himself. No escort within earshot, which was either courtesy or a message.

"You understand the terms," he said. "You descend the western route. My men will watch you go as far as the tree line. After that, you're civilians on public land, and I have a helicopter to defrost and a report to write that says I recovered destroyed materials from a derelict site. Everyone lives inside that report. Stay inside it."

"And if I don't?" Jonah asked.

Hale looked at her for a moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him exhale slowly through his nose.

"Then I'd advise you to be faster than I am," he said, "and smarter than I was." He stuck out his hand — to Teo, surprisingly, not to Jonah — and Teo shook it because refusing seemed like the kind of thing that got remembered. "Your cook kept eleven people fed at fourteen thousand feet on freeze-dried and spite. That's the hardest job on any expedition. I want you to know someone noticed."

Then he turned and walked back toward his men, and the pass belonged to the wind again.

They stood at the cairn while the soldiers packed up below them, efficient as ants dismantling a nest. Jonah shouldered her pack — lighter now by nine kilograms, and somehow heavier. Teo watched her look west, at the country they'd crossed, and then east, down the maintenance road, and he saw her doing the math he was doing.

"She lied to Hale," Teo said quietly. "Or hid it from him. The third copy."

"I heard her."

"If it's still there — if the hemlock's still standing — that's everything. The real thing. The thing that actually matters." He swallowed. "Jonah. We could go back."

She was quiet for a long time. The wind came through the saddle and flattened the frost flowers on the gravel and carried away, eastward, the sound of Hale's snowcat fading down the mountain.

"No," she said finally. "We go down. We get warm. We get Mara's story straight, and we get ours straight, and we figure out who we trust — really trust — with what's in that hollow tree." She looked at him, and for the first time since the fog bank off the coast, since the case stamped with his initials, Teo saw her look tired in a way that wasn't about her body. "My father spent twelve years running from what he found. I just sold his life's work on a mountaintop to buy us a morning. I'm not deciding anything else today on two hours of sleep and a bullet graze."

She started down the western trail. Teo followed, and neither of them said the thing they were both thinking:

That Jonah had mailed a package from the last fuel stop, weeks ago, to a journalist whose name she'd never spoken aloud.

And that Hale, walking back down to his men, had watched her shake on the deal with the calm of a man who already knew exactly what was in that hollow tree — and had decided, for reasons of his own, to let them live anyway.
