## 18. What the Navy Buys

The deal took four minutes to make and would take the rest of her life to understand.

Hale stood at the head of the pass with six men behind him and the dawn coming up gray behind that, and he listened to Jonah's terms without interrupting once. That was the first thing she noticed about him up close — the patience. She'd spent two weeks imagining him as a man who shouted orders into radios, who sank ships and burned expeditions with a wave of his hand. Instead he was compact and gray-templed and had the stillness of someone who'd learned long ago that urgency was a tell.

"Journals, copies of the survey data, and your silence," he said. "In exchange for safe passage down the east side and a medevac for Chen."

"And no one follows us," Jonah said. "No one ever finds us again."

Hale's eyes went to Mara, laid out on Teo's parka with her leg splinted from a ski pole and her fever burning through three days of infection. Then back to Jonah. "She needs a hospital in the next twelve hours or she loses the leg. Possibly more than the leg. You understand I'm not negotiating from weakness."

"I understand you're negotiating," Jonah said. "That's new for you."

Something moved behind his face — not quite a smile. "You have your father's mouth, I'm told. I never met him. I've read enough of his reports to feel like I did." He held out his hand. "The materials, Ms. Hartwell. All of them."

She gave him the dry bag. Inside it: her father's journals, eleven of them, water-stained and soft as old skin. The mineral surveys rolled in oilcloth. The photographs of the navy's own correspondence — the memos proving they'd known what the mine tailings were doing to the watershed three years before the transport *Corvus* went down with every page of evidence aboard, sunk in ninety feet of cold black water off a fogbound coast, where twelve years later a salvage diver with steady hands and her father's training would be hired to put a line on it.

All of it real. All of it surrendered.

Hale's sergeant inventoried the bag while Hale watched the ridgeline out of habit, and Jonah stood in the wind with her empty hands and felt the strangest sensation, which was lightness. Twelve years of carrying this. Her father's initials stamped on a sealed case in a navy wreck. His handwriting on coordinates dated three days before he vanished. Every mile she'd run since — south coast, gulf ports, two years in Alaska, one in a town so small the chart didn't name it — all of it had been distance bought against this exact moment, and now the moment was here and she'd paid with everything and she was still standing.

"You did the right thing," Hale said quietly, when the sergeant zipped the bag closed.

"No," Jonah said. "I did the survivable thing. Don't flatter either of us."

He almost smiled again. Then he turned and gave the order, and two of his men came forward with a litter frame for Mara, and the deal became a fact instead of a conversation.

* * *

They descended the east side through the morning, the storm spent, the sky scoured blue and hard above them. Hale's men carried the litter in relays, competent and silent, and Jonah walked beside it because Mara's eyes kept finding hers through the fever-haze and she needed something familiar to hold onto. Teo walked behind with his graze-dressed arm tucked against his ribs and watched the navy men the way a man watches water he doesn't trust.

It was near noon, stopped at a stream crossing while the litter bearers rotated, that Jonah understood.

Hale crouched across the stream filling a bottle, close enough to talk without raising his voice. He did it deliberately, she realized later. He wanted privacy for what he was about to say, which meant he'd been waiting for the right stretch of trail.

"Do you know why I honored the terms?" he asked.

"Because you're a man who keeps deals."

"Because I'm a man who reads situations." He screwed the cap on the bottle. "Twelve years ago I made a different calculation. A ship, some documents, a surveyor who wouldn't let go. Clean solution. Contained. And do you know what it bought? Nothing. Twelve years of nothing, because the problem was never the paper. The problem was that people knew. Your father told people before he went upriver. He was smarter than we gave him credit for — he mailed a set of everything to a lawyer in Vancouver with instructions to open it if he stopped checking in. We found that out too late, and we've been managing the consequence ever since."

Jonah's blood went very slow and very cold. "Go on."

"So when you come down my mountain holding a copy of everything — and you are holding a copy, Ms. Hartwell, don't insult us both — I have exactly two options." He stood, unhurried. "Option one, I erase you the way we erased the expedition's loose ends. Three bodies in the high country, an accident report, weather blamed. And then what? The story exists anyway. A diver who worked the Corvus wreck disappears the same month a survey expedition goes dark. A journalist starts asking questions I can't drown. Killing you doesn't bury the story anymore. Killing you *is* the story."

The stream ran loud between them. Somewhere below, a ptarmigan clattered out of the willows.

"Option two," Jonah said.

"Option two, I give you your medevac and your passage, and I pray — and I am not a praying man — that you're smart enough to understand what you're holding. Because here is what you need to hear, and I need you to actually hear it." He looked at her straight on, and there was nothing cold in his face now, which was worse than cold. "If you publish, people will go to prison. Maybe me first. And the watershed gets cleaned up, and the hearings run eighteen months, and the story burns bright for a season and then the country moves on, and somewhere in year three some committee decides the cleanup budget is better spent elsewhere, and the river runs orange again by the time your daughter is old enough to ask you what you did with your inheritance. That's what the truth buys. It buys a fight, not an ending."

"You sank a ship," Jonah said. "You killed my father."

"I gave an order in a room full of men who nodded," Hale said. "That's not a defense. It's just what happened. And I have spent twelve years being the man who keeps the lid on, and I am tired, Ms. Hartwell, in a way you can't fix with altitude." He shouldered his pack. "Your medevac is on the strip at Mile Forty by sixteen hundred. Chen rides first. You and Vasquez ride second, or walk, your choice — the terms say safe passage, and I keep my terms. It's the only currency I have left that's worth anything."

He turned to go, then stopped.

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — your father's last journal entry, the one addressed to you. I've read it. Everyone who's handled this file has read it." His voice didn't change at all. "*Come home by whatever current carries you.* He wrote that to a girl who was already gone by then. He knew it too. He wrote it anyway." Hale looked at the ridgeline, at the clean blue sky, at anything but her. "People keep things like that. It's how they lose."

Then he walked back up the trail toward his men, and Jonah stood at the stream crossing with the water running past her boots and her heart going like a fist on a door.

* * *

She didn't tell Teo until they were alone in the lee of a rock bluff while the litter went ahead with Mara, out of earshot of the escort. He sat with his bad arm cradled and listened all the way through, and when she finished he was quiet for a long time.

"He knows," Teo said finally. "About the mailing."

"He knows there's a copy. He guessed it was mailed — he said *you're holding a copy* like he could smell the weight missing from the bag." Jonah pulled her knees up. The sun was warm on the rock, absurdly warm, the first kindness the range had shown them. "Which means he knows about the last fuel stop. Which means he's known since before the pass. Since before Ives. He could have taken the mail sack off the float plane with one phone call and he didn't."

"Why?"

"That's the part I keep turning over." She stared at the creek. "At first I thought it was arrogance. That he figured one envelope to one journalist wasn't a threat worth burning his own deniability over. But that's not it. I watched his face. He *wanted* the story out. Not the way we want it — he wants it contained, managed, published on terms he can survive. If it comes out as a disappearance, as bodies in the snow, it's a scandal that swallows everyone around it. If it comes out as testimony — documents, hearings, a commander who cooperated with the investigation — then it's a process. Processes have survivors."

Teo absorbed that. "So he let us live because dead witnesses prove murder and living ones only prove negligence."

"Dead witnesses prove the story," Jonah said. "Living ones can be negotiated with. He traded me the pass because killing us costs him more than the journals were worth — and because he thinks he's already won, since he believes the only copy is the one in the mail." She heard her own voice go flat and strange. "And the worst part, Teo. The part I can't get past."

"What."

"It worked. Whatever his reasons — however much of it was him covering himself — Mara gets a hospital. You get to keep both arms. I'm standing in the sun instead of in a crevasse with a bullet in me." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I sold everything my father died carrying, and it bought exactly what I traded it for, and I'd do it again in a heartbeat, and I don't know what that makes me."

Teo reached over with his good hand and gripped her shoulder, hard, the way you grip someone on a boat in rough water. "It makes you someone who's still here. That's the whole job right now. Everything else is later."

* * *

The strip at Mile Forty was a gravel bar masquerading as an aviation facility, and the medevac bird came in at 1552 with its rotors flattening the grass along the banks. They loaded Mara first; the flight medic took one look at her leg and her vitals and started talking fast into his headset, and then the bird lifted and shrank and was gone north over the trees, and Jonah stood in the rotor wash watching it go and feeling the specific vertigo of having handed the most important person in her new, small world to strangers with a radio.

The second flight was a charter twin otter, civilian, a pilot who asked no questions and took no eye contact. Hale's sergeant walked them to the door with the dry bag — empty now, Jonah noted, returned to her like a receipt — and said only, "Commander says the east side of the range stays closed to salvage for the foreseeable future. Officially, environmental review." A pause. "Unofficially, he says you read water better than anyone he's seen, and he'd rather you were on a boat than on a mountain."

"Tell him boats can be followed too."

The sergeant almost smiled. "He knows. That's sort of the point."

The otter climbed away from the valley and the whole watershed opened beneath them — the river unspooling silver from the pass to the coast, the falls a white thread in its throat, the old mine road a scar nobody had bothered to heal. Jonah pressed her forehead to the window. Somewhere under that water was the Corvus, and inside the Corvus was the original case with her father's initials, and the navy would seal the site or flood it or lose the paperwork, and none of it mattered anymore, because the truth was already in a mail sack two hundred miles south, riding toward a journalist named Okafor who answered her letters from a payphone and never used the same one twice.

Teo slept against the window, exhausted, his bandaged arm propped on a life vest. Jonah watched the river and did the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing.

Her father had copied everything and mailed it ahead, and it hadn't saved him. She had copied everything and mailed it ahead, and it had saved all of them — barely, ugly, at the price of the originals and the site and any claim she might have made on the truth itself. Leverage, she thought. Hale had taught her the word for it without meaning to. You didn't win by holding the documents. You won by making sure that hurting you cost more than leaving you alone. Her father had never learned that lesson; he'd believed the truth was armor. It wasn't. It was cargo, and cargo was only worth something if the other side knew you'd already sent it somewhere they couldn't reach.

She had discovered the leverage in the act of losing everything else. That was the shape of it. That was the trade.

The pilot's voice crackled over the intercom: fuel stop at the river town, twenty minutes on the ground. The last fuel stop. The place where she'd found Teo half-frozen beside a wood stove, where she'd mailed the envelope from the post office that was also the liquor store, where the whole turn of the wheel had begun.

When they landed, she was the first one out. The post office was open. She bought a stamp.

Not for Okafor — that letter was gone, in the system, unstoppable. This one was smaller. She wrote the address from memory, her hand steady, and it was the address of a marina office on the south coast where a harbor master named Denny owed her twelve years of unanswered voicemails, and the note inside said only:

*Coming home by whatever current carries me. Tell the yard to warm up the boat.*

She dropped it in the slot and heard it land, and outside the otter was refueling and Teo was watching her through the glass with an expression she couldn't read yet, and somewhere north of them a man in a gray jacket was drafting a resignation letter he hadn't admitted to anyone he was writing.

Jonah stood in the doorway of the post office in the last fuel stop, in the place where the running had started, and understood that she had just done the one thing she'd sworn she'd never do.

She'd told someone where she was going to be.
