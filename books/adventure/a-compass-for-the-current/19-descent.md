## 19. Descent

The storm let go of the range like a fist unclenching.

One hour we were blind, roped together in the white roar, and then the wind dropped as if someone had shut a door, and the clouds tore open in ragged strips, and there it all was: the valley, blue and enormous, the river a bright thread stitched down its middle, the world we'd been climbing toward for nine days laid out below us like something we'd earned.

Mara rode my back in a rig Teo built from pack straps and a foam pad and forty minutes of profanity. She'd gone quiet in the way that scared me more than screaming — fever burning through her at a hundred and three, the wound in her side angry red to the edges, her head lolling against my shoulder blade with every step. She weighed nothing. That was the worst part. A woman who'd led six people into a gorge and out the other side of a betrayal, and she weighed nothing at all.

"Talk to her," Teo said, falling in beside me. "Fever patients do better when you talk to them."

So I talked. I told her about the water below us, the way the river braided out in the flats and came back together, the way it always came back together, because that's what currents do — they lose each other and find each other and lose each other again, and the finding is the whole point. I told her my father used to say that. I didn't tell her he'd written it to me in a journal I'd carried over a mountain while men died trying to take it.

Teo walked point. He'd cleaned and dressed his graze that morning without being asked, without flinching, and when I'd offered to look at it he'd said, "You've got enough weight," and something in the way he said it had landed in me like a stone in still water.

We came down off the glacier tongue onto the moraine as the sun broke through whole, and the snow squeaked under our boots, and for the first time since the pass I could see more than twenty feet in any direction.

Which meant, for the first time since the pass, I could see that nobody was following us.

I stopped. Teo stopped. We stood there in the enormous silence, listening.

"Helicopter would've come by now," Teo said. "If he was coming."

"He's not coming." I adjusted Mara's weight. "He let us go."

Teo looked at me. "You said the deal—"

"The deal was real. The deal was honored." I started walking again, because standing still felt like sinking. "But Hale doesn't honor deals. Hale honors arithmetic. And the arithmetic of killing three people on a mountain, after shaking their hands on a deal—" I shook my head. "It proves the story. It makes us martyrs. It makes the journals true. He knows that. He's known it since the pass."

Teo was quiet for a hundred yards. The moraine crunched. Somewhere below, meltwater ticked and ran under the rocks, the mountain's own plumbing, going about its business.

"Then why does it feel like we won?" he said.

I didn't have an answer for that. I had a journal in my pack and a copy in a mailbox nine hundred miles south and a woman dying on my back, and none of it felt like winning. It felt like a bill coming due.

* * *

We camped that night at treeline, in the last stand of stunted spruce, and I got Mara's fever down half a degree with wet cloth and the antibiotics from the navy's own med kit — Hale's team had left a cache at the pass, part of the deal, and there was a bitter joke in that I didn't have the breath to tell.

Teo built a fire small enough to hide and big enough to matter, and we sat on either side of it while Mara slept, actually slept, her breathing ragged but even.

"You should eat," he said, and handed me a foil pouch, and I ate, because he was right, and because arguing with Teo Vasquez about food was like arguing with the river about downhill.

"Teo." I turned the pouch over in my hands. "The mailing. From the fuel stop. You said you sent it."

He looked into the fire. The flames put orange light on the hollows of his face.

"I didn't send anything," he said.

The words took a second to arrive. "What?"

"The morning we ran the gorge — you went down to the boat early. You were gone twenty minutes." He still wouldn't look at me. "I thought you were checking the fuel line. I always thought you were checking the fuel line. Then on the mountain, when you told me the journals were already copied, already mailed — Jonah, I never mailed anything. I never even knew there was anything to mail. I said I had, because—" He finally turned, and his eyes were wet in the firelight, and he looked ashamed in a way that made my chest hurt. "Because I wanted you to not be carrying it alone. That's all. I wanted you to think someone had your back on it. It was a stupid lie and I've been chewing on it since the scree."

I sat with it. The fire popped. Mara breathed.

"You lied to me," I said, "to make me feel less alone."

"Yeah. And it was wrong. And I'd probably do it again." He almost smiled. "Ives was coming down that slope at us with a rifle, and I thought, whatever happens, she shouldn't think she's out here by herself. So."

I looked at my hands. They were steady. They were always steady; that was the thing about them. Twelve years of salvage work, twelve years of one contract after another, one coast after another, and my hands never shook, because there was never anything in them worth dropping.

"Nobody mailed it," I said. "I mailed it. Alone. I copied the journals at the fuel stop at four in the morning while you slept, walked to the post office, and mailed it to a journalist whose name I got off a wire service three years ago and never contacted since. I didn't tell you because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because I don't tell people things. That's the whole of it. That's the entire architecture of my life. I measure in miles because miles don't need to be told anything. You put distance between you and a person, you never have to find out what they'd do with the truth."

"Jonah—"

"I had it in my head the whole climb," I said, "that we were doing this together. And the whole time, the one thing that mattered most, I did it in the dark, by myself, because that's what I do. Ives didn't betray this expedition, Teo. I did. Just quieter."

"That's not—"

"It is, though." I looked at him across the fire. "You lied to carry weight that wasn't yours. I hid weight that was mine so nobody could offer to carry it. One of those is a mistake. The other one's a way of living. And I've been doing it so long I didn't even clock it as a choice."

Teo was quiet. He fed a stick to the fire, careful, like it was a small animal.

"My abuela used to say," he said finally, "that trust is the harder climb. Anybody can go up a mountain. Going up a mountain with somebody — that's the one that kills you." He shrugged. "She was talking about my grandfather and a card game, but it holds."

I laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, the first laugh in nine days, maybe the first in twelve years, and it hurt my ribs, and I let it.

"So here's the thing," I said. "The journalist. The copy. I did it alone, and it worked, and Hale let us walk because of it. But if it hadn't worked — if that envelope got lost, or the journalist sat on it, or Hale's people intercepted it — then everything we bled for on that mountain is sitting in a dead-letter office somewhere, and I'm the only one who knows, and I would have told you. Eventually. Maybe." I turned the foil pouch over again. "I'm telling you now. Everything. From here down. That's the deal I'm making, with you and with her." I nodded at Mara. "No more solo runs. No more four-in-the-morning mailboxes. If there's a next time, we do it in daylight, together, where everybody can see."

Teo studied me for a long moment. Then he reached across the fire and held out his hand, and I took it, and his grip was warm and careful around my rope-burned fingers.

"Deal," he said. "But I'm holding you to the daylight part. I hate secrets at altitude. They give me heartburn."

"Noted."

He settled back against his pack. "For what it's worth — the four a.m. version of you got us off that mountain alive. The trusting version better be at least as good."

"I guess we'll find out."

Mara stirred, murmured something, went quiet again. Above the trees, the sky was doing what it does after a storm in high country — scrubbed clean, salted with stars, the kind of sky my father used to navigate by when the electronics failed and all you had left was what you knew and who you trusted to know it with you.

I lay back and looked at it and understood, finally, the thing he'd been trying to tell me for twelve years from the bottom of a lake: the compass was never the point. The compass was just how you found the people.

* * *

We made the river on the third day down.

It came up on us the way it always does in that country — you hear it before you see it, a low conversation under the trees, and then the trees open and there it is, wide and brown-green and running hard with snowmelt, and something in my chest unlocked that I hadn't known was locked.

There was a ranger station two miles downstream, the charts said. A radio. A medevac flight if the weather held, which, for once, it looked like it would.

Teo went in the water to the knees to test the current at the crossing, and I watched him do it, watched him read the surface the way I'd taught him on the gorge — the V's pointing downstream, the smooth tongue where the water runs deep and kind — and he read it right, first try, and waved us across.

I carried Mara through on the last of my legs, the current pushing at my hips like a living thing, insistent, and I thought: this is what it wants. Not to take you. To carry you. You just have to stop fighting the direction.

On the far bank I set her down in the grass and Teo checked her pulse and her eyes and said, "She's still in there. She's fighting." And Mara's eyes opened, fever-bright, and found my face, and she said, in a voice like gravel underwater:

"The third copy. In my jacket. Don't—"

"We know," I said. "It's going with us. All of it. Together."

She almost smiled. "Together," she repeated, like she was testing the weight of the word, and then she was out again, and Teo and I looked at each other over her body, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: that together was a skill, and we were beginners, and the exam wasn't over.

We rigged her into the carry and started down the river path, the water talking beside us the whole way, and around the second bend I saw the smoke.

A thin gray thread rising off the far bank, maybe a mile down. A camp. And tied at the bank below it, riding the current against its will, a navy inflatable — hull number freshly painted over, but not fresh enough.

Teo saw it a half-second after I did. His hand found my arm.

"Jonah," he said. "Is that—"

"Yeah," I said, and shifted Mara's weight higher on my shoulders, and kept walking, because there was no other direction. "It's a navy camp. On our river. Between us and the radio."

The smoke rose straight up in the windless air, patient as a question, and we walked toward it.
