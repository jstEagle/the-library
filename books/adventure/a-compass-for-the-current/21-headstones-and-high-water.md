## 21. Headstones and High Water

The cemetery sat on a bench of land above the river valley, close enough to the water that on quiet mornings you could hear it working the gravel bars, that low continuous conversation of current against stone. Jonah had not been back in twelve years. She had told herself it was distance — too many miles, wrong coast, work that didn't let go — but standing now at the gate with the original journal in a dry bag slung across her chest, she admitted what she'd always known: distance was just grief wearing boots.

She climbed the hill slowly. Her knee still ached from the pass, a deep cold ache that flared on the switchbacks and settled into something almost companionable by the top. Teo's graze had healed to a pink seam along his ribs. Mara was three weeks out of surgery in Whitehorse, down a section of intestine and up one hell of a story. And Jonah — Jonah had spent those three weeks doing the thing she was worst at, which was staying in one place long enough for people to learn her face.

The stone was smaller than she remembered. Granite, gray-flecked, flush with the ground like it was trying not to be noticed. ELIAS HARTWELL, 1961–LOST AT SEA. No body, no date. The navy had given her mother a folded flag and a line about a training accident aboard a transport lost in heavy weather, and her mother had lived eleven more years carrying that lie like a stone in her boot before cancer took her, and Jonah had carried both of them ever since, or rather had run from carrying them, which amounts to the same weight distributed differently.

*Lost at sea.* She crouched, brushed pine needles off the letters with her thumb.

"You were never lost," she said quietly. "You knew exactly where you were. It was everybody else who couldn't find you."

A crow worked the treeline behind her, patient as an auditor. Below, the river ran high with early snowmelt, brown-green and muscular, hauling whole trees downstream like it was clearing furniture out of a house. High water season. Her father had loved this time of year — *the river tells you everything it knows,* he used to say, *you just have to be willing to hear bad news.*

Jonah unslung the dry bag and took out the journal.

It was the original — not the copies she'd mailed from the last fuel stop, not the set Hale had confiscated at the pass with such scrupulous, insulting courtesy, counting each notebook into evidence bags while his men pretended not to watch Mara bleed through her jacket. This one she had kept against her skin since the cave, wrapped in plastic, warmed by her own body through two nights of whiteout and one night of negotiation. Her father's handwriting covered every page: soundings, mineral assays, water samples annotated in that cramped left-leaning script she'd know anywhere. And threaded through the data, dated entries that read less like science than testimony. *Navy sampling team on site again. They are not testing the water. They are testing whether anyone else has.*

And the last page. She hadn't been able to read it more than twice without having to put the book down and look at sky.

She opened to it now, kneeling in the wet grass, and let herself read it a third time.

It wasn't addressed to her by name. He hadn't known who would find it — maybe he'd hoped no one would, maybe he'd known someone must. But the last entry, written three days before the Meridian went dark and the transport went down and his life got compressed into four words on a slab of granite, ended with a paragraph that had nothing to do with watershed contamination or chain of custody:

*If my girl ever reads this — and she'll be the one who reads it, because I taught her to read water and she taught herself to read everything else — tell her the miles were never the point. I ran rivers because they go somewhere. Every current ends where it's supposed to end if you stop fighting it long enough to steer. Come home by whatever current carries you. Don't measure the trip. Just make it home.*

Jonah sat back on her heels and breathed.

Below her the river kept talking. Somewhere upstream, past the gorge, past the falls, there was a cave behind a waterfall where the government had spent twelve years pretending nothing existed, and crews would go in this summer with pumps and filters and court orders, and the water would come out cleaner than it had been since before she was born. That was the story breaking across the country in slow motion — hearings, resignations, a cleanup order signed by a minister who'd never seen the river. None of it felt like fireworks. It felt like this: a woman on a hill, holding a dead man's notebook, listening to water do what water does.

She had thought vindication would feel bigger. She'd made peace with that already, mostly. What she hadn't expected was how much this moment would feel like the whiteout — blind, hands out, trusting instruments she couldn't see.

Because here was the thing she'd figured out somewhere around mile nine hundred of the descent, carrying Mara on a litter made of paddles and pack straps while Teo broke trail: she had spent her whole adult life measuring life in miles because miles were honest. A mile was a mile. It didn't get sick, didn't leave, didn't die in a helicopter crash that wasn't a crash. You logged it and moved on and the number only ever went up, and going up felt like progress even when it was just going.

But miles couldn't die because miles were never alive. And neither, it turned out, was she, most of those years. She'd been a logbook with legs.

"I kept everything," she told the stone. "That's the joke, right? Twelve years running from this, and the first thing I did in that cave was hold your journals like they were going to evaporate." She laughed, and it came out rough. "Teo wanted to split the weight. I wouldn't give up a single page. Nearly killed us on the scree, all that paper. Worth it."

The crow dropped down onto a neighboring marker and regarded her with professional interest.

She'd brought things. Of course she'd brought things — she was a salvage diver, keeping things was the whole trade. A tin of her father's pipe tobacco, found in a box of her mother's effects, too old to smoke, perfect to burn. A river stone from below the falls, smooth and green-gray, taken from the exact spot where the cave mouth opened behind the falling water. A photograph of the three of them — her, Teo, Mara — taken by a nurse in Whitehorse, all of them hollow-eyed and grinning like survivors of something, which they were.

But she scattered none of it.

Her mother had asked for ashes spread on the river, and Jonah had done it, eighteen years old and furious, hurling the urn's contents into the current so hard she'd nearly gone in after them. She understood now what that had been. Not a goodbye. A launch. She'd treated grief like cargo to be jettisoned before running whitewater, and she'd been jettisoning it ever since, lightening the boat, gaining speed, arriving nowhere.

Not this time. She kept the tobacco. She kept the stone. She kept the photograph, and the journal, and the ache, and the name.

"Everything stays," she said. "I'm done throwing things overboard."

What she left instead was the journal itself — open, weighted with the river stone, propped against the granite so the last page faced the sky. Anyone walking this hill could read it. *Come home by whatever current carries you.* Let the message sit out here in the weather, doing its work on strangers the way it had done its work on her. His words belonged to the open air more than to a dry bag against her ribs. She'd copied every page anyway. Keeping and letting go, she was learning, weren't opposites. They were eddies in the same current — you could rest in either one, as long as you knew which way the water ran.

She stayed until the light went long and gold.

She talked. That surprised her — she'd braced for silence, for the awful formality of addressing a marker, and instead the words came easy, the way they came easy with Teo over a camp stove. She told him about the jetboat run through the gorge, how she'd read the water exactly the way he'd taught her, V's pointing downstream, green water deep, and how for six minutes she'd been so fully his daughter that death itself seemed to stand aside and watch. She told him about Mara's fever breaking on the third morning after surgery, about Teo learning to cook again in the hospital family kitchen because, he said, feeding people was how he prayed. She told him about Hale — not with hatred, which had burned down to ash weeks ago, but with a kind of tired clarity. A man who measured the world in security the way she used to measure it in miles, and both of them alone at the top of their own arithmetic.

"He let us go," she said. "Did you know that? He knew about the mailing and he let us walk. Because killing us would've proved your pages true better than any hearing." She shook her head. "Everybody in this story finally stopped lying at the same time. Took a mountain to arrange it."

The river rose a little. She could hear it change pitch, the way a diver hears depth.

When the sun touched the ridgeline she stood, knees cracking, and pressed her palm flat to the granite one time. Cold stone, warm hand. Twelve years of distance ending in five inches of contact.

"I'm staying," she said. "For a while, anyway. There's a stretch of river below the gorge nobody runs anymore because the charts are wrong, and somebody should fix the charts." She smiled. "Somebody with the right survey notes."

She walked down the hill without looking back, which was different now — not refusal, just trust that the stone would keep.

* * *

Teo was waiting at the truck, sitting on the tailgate with two thermoses and the expression of a man who had learned exactly how much space to give her and not one inch more.

"Well?" he said.

"He heard me."

"They always do." He handed her a thermos. Coffee, cut with the condensed milk he bought in quantities that suggested a bunker mentality. "Mara called. Doctor cleared her for light duty. She wants to know if we're actually doing this thing or if we're just talking about it in diners like normal traumatized people."

Jonah drank. The coffee was terrible and hot and tasted like the last fuel stop, like cold camps, like every good bad decision of the past two months.

"The charts are wrong below the gorge," she said. "Somebody should fix them."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I've got left." She looked at him — really looked, the way she'd learned to on the mountain, when reading a person's face mattered as much as reading water. "Yeah, Teo. We're doing it. Boat, guide outfit, the whole ridiculous thing. Kids from the valley, teach them the water before the water teaches them."

He grinned, and something in her chest that had been clenched since she was twenty-six loosened another turn.

They drove down out of the hills as the light failed, the river flashing silver through the trees beside the road, running high, running fast, going somewhere. Jonah's compass rode on the dash where she'd mounted it that morning — her father's compass, brass-cased, the glass scratched from forty years of pockets. It didn't point north anymore. She'd adjusted it herself, carefully, the way you adjust anything you intend to keep.

It pointed downriver.

Toward the gorge. Toward the falls. Toward spring, and a boat with a name she hadn't chosen yet, and two people in the cab with her who knew every bad camp and worse decision and had stayed anyway.

"Mara says if we call it anything nautical and pretentious she's staging a mutiny," Teo said.

"Tell her the current gets a vote too."

The road curved, the river widened, and the compass held steady on home.
