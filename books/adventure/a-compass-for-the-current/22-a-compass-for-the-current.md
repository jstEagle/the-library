## 22. A Compass for the Current

The river broke up on a Tuesday, and by Friday the whole valley smelled of it — cold water and rotting ice and the green insistence of things deciding to grow again. Jonah stood on the new dock at Miller's Bend with a mug of coffee going lukewarm in her hands and watched the current carry the last of the winter downstream, whole shelves of ice riding the surface like pale ships, tilting, groaning, surrendering.

Twelve years she'd measured her life in miles. She'd counted them the way other people counted birthdays: two hundred miles from her father's last known position. Four hundred from the memorial stone she couldn't look at. A thousand from the coast where she'd pulled his initials out of a navy wreck and felt the past grab her ankle like a snagged line. Miles were clean. Miles didn't die on you in a side chamber with a septic wound, didn't fall off a scree slope chasing you, didn't sit across a diner table from you with their hands shaking around a coffee cup because they'd carried a bullet graze through a whiteout for your sake.

Miles just accumulated. You could always put more between yourself and whatever hurt.

She'd stopped counting in October, somewhere above the treeline, with Mara's fever burning through her sleeping bag and Teo's voice steady as a metronome telling her that the journals weighed four pounds and her father had carried them uphill once already and she could damn well carry them home. She hadn't cried at the pass. She hadn't cried when Hale honored the deal, or when the helicopter lifted Mara out, or when the story broke in November and the hearings started and a man named Commander Hale resigned rather than testify. She'd cried in March, alone, reading her father's last page by the woodstove — *Come home by whatever current carries you* — and understood finally that he hadn't written it to a daughter he expected to find it someday. He'd written it to whoever found it. He'd just hoped, the way drowning men hope, that it would be her.

The ice shelf went past the dock, tipped, and folded under with a sound like a door closing.

"Dock's holding," Teo said behind her. "Mostly."

"Mostly is a strong word for that pilon on the end."

"That pilon has character." He came up beside her, steaming mug in hand, hair still doing what it did every morning — defying gravity, weather, and Jonah's repeated suggestions about hats. The graze on his arm had healed into a thin pink seam he touched sometimes without noticing, the way she touched the compass in her pocket. "Mara says if we don't get the sign up before the tourist flyers go out, we're dead in the water. Her words. She's very proud of them."

"How's she walking?"

"Like a woman who intends to keep doing it." He sipped. "Doc cleared her for dock duty. Not dive duty. She's furious about it, which means she's healing right."

Jonah looked downriver, toward the bend where the water went smooth and deep over the old channel — the spot she'd already mapped twice with the fish finder, where something man-made sat in forty feet of current-shadow. A steamboat boiler, maybe. Maybe better. First job on the books, whenever the water warmed enough to dive. Legitimate salvage. Permitted, insured, boring in all the ways she used to run from.

Miller's Bend Salvage and Guide. The sign was leaning against the boathouse behind them, hand-painted by a kid from town who charged them twenty dollars and a ride on the jetboat and talked the entire time about becoming a diver himself someday. Jonah had looked at that kid's face — hungry for the water, unafraid of it, the way she'd been at twelve before the miles started — and thought: *this*. This is what he left me. Not the cache. Not the testimony, not the hearings, not the name in the newspaper. This. The reading of water, handed down like a compass, meant to be passed on again.

"You're doing the thing," Teo said.

"What thing."

"The staring-at-the-river-like-it-owes-you-money thing."

"It does owe me money. I have expenses now. Insurance premiums." She smiled despite herself. Teo had a gift for that — pulling her back to the surface when she went twelve miles deep. He'd been doing it since a frozen trapper cabin, since stories traded across a storm, since he told her about every member of the Meridian crew who hadn't come home and watched her decide, visibly, that she would not be one more person who vanished.

* * *

They opened on the first Saturday of May, which in the valley meant mud, optimism, and eleven kids from the middle school whose science teacher had read about them in the paper and shown up unannounced in a yellow bus.

Mara handled the paperwork from a folding table on the dock, leg propped on a cooler, radiating the particular authority of a woman who had led an expedition into a waterfall cave and intended everyone to remember it. Teo ran the safety briefing with the same calm he'd used cooking for nine people in country with no resupply — unhurried, specific, funny in a dry way that made the kids lean in. And Jonah took them to the water's edge and taught them the only inheritance worth anything.

"Tell me what the river's saying," she said, crouched at the bank with the current sliding past them, brown-green and muscular with snowmelt.

A girl named Priya squinted at it. "It's moving fast?"

"It's always moving fast. Look closer. Where's it smooth?"

They found it together — the glassy tongue of water over the deep channel, the V of ripples pointing upstream over the shallows, the eddy line stitching itself along the far bank where the current turned back on itself. Jonah showed them how a downed tree bent the flow, how a rock showed its shoulders as a boil, how the water wrote its own chart for anyone patient enough to read it.

"My dad taught me that," she heard herself say, and the words came out easier than she expected. "He said the river never lies to you. It just doesn't repeat itself."

Priya looked up at her with that same hunger Jonah remembered wearing. "Can we go out in the boats?"

"Today you learn to read. Reading comes first." She tapped her own temple. "The boats are just transportation. The reading is the skill. You can lose everything else and if you can read water, you're never actually lost."

She caught Teo watching her from the dock with an expression she pretended not to see, and Mara pretending harder, and let it go. Some things you let people see even when it costs you. That was the whole lesson, really. It had taken her thirty-one years and one mountain range to learn it, but there it was.

* * *

The first real job came in June: the steamboat boiler, confirmed, plus a cargo hold full of century-old bottles that the county historical society got very excited about. Jonah dove it herself on a bright cold morning, hanging in the green light over the wreck while her bubbles climbed toward a sky she no longer needed to put distance between herself and.

Her father had taught her to dive in water like this. She could almost hear him — check your gauge, check your buddy, the river keeps what it keeps and gives back what it gives — and for the first time in twelve years the hearing of him didn't hurt. It steadied. He wasn't a wound anymore. He was a craft. He was a set of hands over hers on a tiller, a voice saying *read it again*, a final page in a journal that had traveled farther than any mile she'd ever logged and come home anyway.

She surfaced into sunlight and Teo's hand hauling her up the ladder, and Mara shouting something about the historical society wanting to know if they'd found the *manifest*, and the ordinary beautiful noise of a working day.

That evening they ate on the dock — Teo's chili, Mara's terrible guitar playing, the river running gold with the last of the light. The compass sat on the boat dash where it always sat now, her father's brass compass, needle settling after its long years of pointing wherever Jonah had been running. It didn't point north. It hadn't for months. It pointed downriver, toward the bend, toward the town lights coming on one by one, toward the memorial stone she visited now not as penance but as conversation — *told the kids about the eddy lines today, Dad. They got it faster than I did.*

Teo followed her gaze to the compass. "Still broken?"

"Never was." She leaned into his shoulder. "It was just waiting for me to stop moving long enough to settle."

Downriver, the current carried on — patient, honest, repeating nothing, keeping everything — and Jonah Hartwell, who had once measured her life in miles, sat still on her own dock and let it measure her instead: in seasons, in students, in the sound of Mara laughing at her own bad chords, in the weight of a shoulder against hers that she trusted completely, in the long slow current of a home she had finally, finally come to by whatever current carried her.
