## 1. The Weight of Nothing

The basin held heat the way a grudge holds a name.

August Nystrom had been walking since before dawn, and by midmorning the salt flats had gone white and blind, the horizon dissolved into a shimmer that made the world seem unfinished—a sketch someone had abandoned halfway through. He moved through it without hurry, reading the ground the way other men read letters. A scuffed crescent where a boot had pivoted. A thread of sagebrush broken at knee height, still weeping its green into the dry air. The thief was three hours ahead of him, maybe four, and she was tired, and she was careless in the way that only frightened people were careless.

He did not hurry. Hurrying was how men died out here—twisted ankles, emptied canteens, bad decisions made in the glare. He walked with the flat, patient rhythm of a man who had learned that the desert always won eventually, and that the only dignity available was to lose slowly.

Her name was Cass Merrow. He knew it because he'd heard it shouted across the trading post at Dry Fork two nights ago, a storekeeper bellowing it after her like a curse flung at a departing cart. *Cass Merrow, you gutter-hearted little magpie!* She'd taken a tin of tea, a spool of wire, and—this was the part August cared about, though he'd have denied caring under oath—his rations. His whole pack of them, lifted off the porch rail while he stood inside paying for lamp oil with coins he'd earned breaking rocks at the quarry outside Vell's Crossing under a name that wasn't his.

That was the trouble with rations. They were the one thing in the world that carried his smell, his habits, his fingerprints in the grease of the wrapping paper. He could buy new ones anywhere, but these—he'd packed them himself, wrapped them himself, and somewhere on that paper his hand had smudged the ink of a supply stamp, and stamps could be traced, and traced stamps became reports, and reports required names.

So here he was, walking into the white heart of the Empty Basin after a girl who'd stolen eleven days of food, because the alternative was filing a report, and August Nystrom had not put his name on paper in nine years.

* * *

He caught her scent of her, almost literally, by late afternoon: woodsmoke from a fire she'd been too green to bury properly, drifting on the thermal wind that came up the basin's throat each evening. He followed it to a shelf of rust-colored rock where the flats began their slow climb toward the mesa country, and there she was—a slight figure crouched over a cookfire no bigger than a cupped hand, feeding it slivers of greasewood.

She heard him the moment he wanted her to. That was deliberate; surprising her would get someone hurt, probably him. He stopped ten paces out and let her turn.

She was younger than he'd expected. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and wariness, hair the color of dust cut short and ragged as if she'd done it herself with a knife. His ration pack sat behind her like a hostage. She had a skinning knife in her right hand and she held it low and competent, which told him she'd used it on something other than bread.

"You followed me," she said. Not a question. Her voice was rough-edged, unimpressed.

"Three days," August said. "You're slower than you think."

"I'm faster than you are."

"Probably." He crouched, unhurried, and turned a stone over with one finger, studying the ants beneath it as though they were more interesting than she was. "But I don't have to be fast. I just have to keep walking. You have to sleep sometime, and when you do, I'll be there. That's the whole arithmetic of it."

The girl—Cass—shifted her weight. He watched her eyes flick past his shoulder, calculating distances, escape lines. She was good. She'd made ninety miles across open basin ahead of a man who tracked for a living, and she was what, seventeen? In another life he might have admired her.

"Take the tea back," she said. "And the wire. Keep the food."

"It's not about the food."

"Then what is it about?"

August considered the question honestly, which surprised him. The truth was that it wasn't about anything—not the rations, not the storekeeper's outrage, not even the principle of theft. It was about the report. About the clerk at the ranger station in Vell's Crossing who would need a name to enter in the ledger, and the reward notice that would go up on the board with a description attached, and the way descriptions became wanted bills and wanted bills became questions asked by men with badges in towns he hadn't run to yet. Nine years ago he had burned his old life down to the waterline, and everything he'd built since rested on one principle: *be nothing, owe nothing, leave nothing behind.*

Except now there was a girl holding a knife between him and eleven days of food, and the desert between him and anywhere, and the arithmetic had changed.

"The food's mine," he said. "That's all it is. Give it back and walk away, and I never saw you."

"You'll report me anyway."

"No." And he meant it, and something in his voice must have shown it, because her knife-hand dropped an inch.

"You're a ranger," she said. "Rangers report things. It's the whole job."

"I'm not much of a ranger."

"That," said Cass Merrow, "I believe."

What happened next happened fast, and August would spend the walk back replaying it and finding himself, each time, a half-step slower than he remembered being. She kicked the cookfire—not at him, but sideways, scattering burning greasewood across the dry duff at his feet—and while he stepped left, away from the flame, she came right, snatched the ration pack, and went over the rock shelf in one motion, quick and fluid as water finding a crack. By the time he'd stamped out the fire and gained the top of the shelf, she was a hundred yards out and opening distance, the pack bouncing against her spine, her ragged hair streaming.

He could have shot at her. He carried a rifle, and he was a fair shot even in failing light. But a rifle shot was a sound, and sounds drew people, and people drew questions, and questions drew paper. So he stood on the shelf with the heat dying out of the stone under his boots and watched her dwindle to a speck, then a rumor, then nothing, and felt the strange lightness that always came afterward—the weight of nothing settling back onto his shoulders like an old coat.

Eleven days of food. Gone. Three days' walk back to Vell's Crossing on an empty stomach, or four days onward to the wells at Sorrow Bend if his legs held. He turned south, toward Sorrow Bend, because south was away from ledgers.

* * *

He found the well at dusk on the second day, and he found the woman at the well.

At first he took her for a heap of discarded clothing, a prospector's slicker crumpled against the stones. Then the heap coughed, and the cough had a person inside it.

She was old—old in the way the basin was old, worn down to essentials—and she lay with one arm hooked over the well's lip as though she'd been trying to climb in and had changed her mind halfway. Her lips were cracked black. Her eyes, when they found him, were the clearest thing in a hundred miles: gray, level, and burning with a purpose that had clearly kept her alive past every reasonable deadline.

"Water," she said. It wasn't a plea. It was an instruction.

August knelt and gave her his canteen, watching her drink with the disciplined smallness of someone who knew exactly how much a body could take and refused to waste a drop. When she finished, she looked at him for a long time.

"Nystrom," she said.

His blood went cold and quiet. "You don't know my name."

"No," she agreed. "But you signed the quarry ledger at Vell's Crossing last spring. 'A. Nystrom.' First name abbreviated. A man who abbreviates his first name is hiding the rest of it." She smiled, and the smile cost her something. "Odile Vane. Prospector. Dying, in case the arm-hooked-over-the-well didn't tell you."

"How long have you been here?"

"Two days. Waiting for whoever came along." Her eyes glittered. "I hoped it would be someone like you. Someone with nothing. Nothing men carry best—they've got room."

August should have stood up then. Should have given her his second canteen and walked on toward Sorrow Bend and his own empty belly, because everything in him that had survived nine years screamed that this woman was a door, and doors opened onto rooms, and rooms had people in them, and people wrote things down.

Instead he heard himself say: "Room for what?"

"For a delivery." From inside her slicker she drew a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, and pressed it against his chest with a hand that weighed almost nothing and would not be refused. "There's a wreck on the coast. The *Meridian*. This map finds her. I can't make the coast—I've got maybe a day in me, and I'd rather spend it talking than walking. So you'll take it where it needs to go."

"I'm not a courier."

"Everyone's a courier," Odile Vane said. "Some just don't know what they're carrying yet."

The stars were coming out over the basin, hard and white and indifferent. August sat on the cold stones of the well with a dying stranger's map in his hands and eleven days of hunger ahead of him, and understood, with the calm clarity of a man watching a flood come up the dry wash, that the weight of nothing he had carried so carefully for nine years had just gotten heavier.

"One promise," Odile said, gripping his wrist. Her fingers were bird-light and would not let go. "Swear someone finishes what I started. Swear it, Nystrom, and mean it, and I'll show you what's in the oilcloth that makes it worth swearing."

He swore it—to end the conversation, he told himself. To get the old woman's hand off his wrist so he could stand and walk away into the dark.

Dawn found her dead at the well's lip, peaceful as a woman who had finally set down a load, and August Nystrom sitting beside her, unwrapping the oilcloth by first light, looking at a map drawn in a hand that shook and did not shake at all—lines running west, toward a coast he had spent nine years making sure he'd never see.
