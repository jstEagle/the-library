## 3. Six Tides

The map smelled of tallow and iron.

August spread it across the flat stone of the abandoned waystation's table, weighting the corners with four river pebbles he'd carried in his pockets for no reason he could name. Habit, maybe. A man who owned nothing learned to keep his hands full of small weights so the wind couldn't accuse him of emptiness. Outside, the basin breathed its evening heat against the shuttered windows, and somewhere a jackal made its one dry argument for the night.

Odile Vane had drawn this with a steadier hand than a dying woman should have possessed. The parchment was sailcloth, actually—salt-stiffened, patched at one corner with a scrap of linen stitched in thread the color of old blood. He turned it toward the lamp. The ink had been mixed with something that caught the light strangely, a faint metallic shimmer, as if she'd ground rust into it deliberately. Perhaps she had. Prospectors were superstitious in ways rangers pretended not to be.

The coast lay at the map's western edge, rendered in lines so fine they might have been etched with a whisker. He recognized the geography from charts he'd memorized years ago and then worked to forget: the drowned marshes below Cape Verren, the tidal channel the locals called the Throat, the scatter of sandbars like spilled teeth along the shallows. And there, in the deepest part of the channel where the silt ran thirty feet to bedrock, a small X drawn not in ink but in something darker—a burn, maybe, or a drop of her own blood pressed into the cloth.

*Meridian*, she'd written beneath it. The letters slanted hard, hurried, as though the word itself were trying to escape the page.

Around the X, Odile had crowded her margins with annotations in a cipher of her own devising—numbers paired with symbols August didn't recognize, tide-marks, he guessed, or soundings. But three things he could read plainly, because she'd written them in plain script, large enough to survive her own trembling hands:

*SIX TIDES.*

*THE THROAT CLOSES AT THE DRY MOON.*

*BRING IT TO THE ONE WHO KNOWS THE COAST.*

He sat back. The lamp guttered, righted itself. Six tides. He did the arithmetic the way he did everything—silently, without moving his lips, without leaving any trace of the working. Tides came twice a day in the Throat; he'd heard fishermen curse them often enough in another life. Twelve days, then, before the channel dried to mud and the Meridian became unreachable until the wet season returned—if it returned kind, which on this coast it never did.

Twelve days. And between him and the coast lay ninety miles of basin, salt flat, and the first ridges of the Verren hills.

He should burn it. The thought arrived with the calm of an old acquaintance. Burn the map, scatter the ashes, walk north to the high desert where nobody asked questions because nobody lived long enough to ask them. That had been the arrangement for nine years now—the arrangement he'd built plank by plank, tearing down every bridge behind him until there was nothing left to cross and nothing left to chase him across. No name on any ledger. No face in any memory worth keeping. He'd been so careful. He'd been so *good* at it.

But promises, he was learning, were a species of debt that collected interest after death. Odile Vane had died at dawn with his hand in hers, and her grip had transferred something that couldn't be burned, because burning it would only mean carrying the ashes.

He folded the map along its old creases, careful of the brittle patches, and tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

Outside, the stars came out in their merciless thousands. August stood in the doorway of the waystation and looked west, toward a coast he'd spent nine years pretending didn't exist, and understood—with the particular clarity that comes only when a man realizes the trap has already closed—that the map had made him visible the moment Odile pressed it into his hands. Somewhere, someone else knew what she'd known. Someone else always did. Secrets on this scale were never kept; they were only *lent*, and the lending always came due.

He went back inside, banked the lamp, and began counting supplies with the cold efficiency of a man planning a march he already resented.

* * *

Captain Renn Halloway ate last, and alone, and never enough.

This was policy, not poverty. His crew watched him take the smallest portion from the smallest pot, and they read it as discipline, and discipline was cheaper than gold when it came to holding a crew together through the doldrums off the Verren coast. What they didn't see—what no one aboard the *Greed's Mercy* was permitted to see—was that Halloway genuinely preferred it this way. Hunger kept a blade's edge on a man. Fullness made men soft, and soft men ended up like the last captain he'd served under: fat, trusting, and rolled overboard by his own quartermaster somewhere off the Sable Reefs.

He sat in his cabin with the stern lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light and studied the papers spread before him. Not charts. Papers. A letter, bought weeks ago from a dying prospector's nervous factor in the port town of Graywater, for a sum that would have embarrassed a smaller man. The letter spoke of a wreck in the Throat. It spoke of a map. It spoke, in language the factor probably hadn't understood, of *cargo beyond price*.

Halloway believed exactly half of it. Wrecks in the Throat were common enough—every storm season fed the channel another hull—but cargo beyond price was the language of swindlers, and he'd hanged two swindlers personally, off his own yardarm, as a courtesy to the trade. Still. The factor had wept real tears, and men wept real tears only over debts, and debts meant the seller had seen something true and needed money fast enough to sell it cheap.

Cheap was the whole religion of salvage. You bought other people's desperation and sold it back to them by the pound.

"Captain." Brannock's voice through the door, muffled, respectful in the way of a first mate who'd learned which questions shortened his life. "Lookout says smoke ashore. Waystation trail, heading west. One rider."

"One?"

"One. Small party. Moving fast."

Halloway considered the slit of lamplight on the desk. One rider, heading west, from the direction of the dead woman's territory. The timing was either coincidence—and he'd stopped believing in coincidence around the time he started believing in ledgers—or it was a courier, and couriers meant maps, and maps meant the Throat held something worth racing for.

"Log the sighting," he said. "Course holds. We make the outer shoals by tomorrow's dusk tide." He paused, and let the silence do its work; Brannock had served him six years and knew that pauses were where the real orders lived. "And Brannock. If anyone asks why we're running so heavy through the shallows, tell them we're chasing smugglers. Smugglers make sense to sailors. Everything else makes them think."

"Aye, Captain."

Footsteps receded. Halloway turned back to the letter, to the phrase he'd read forty times and would read forty more: *the one who knows the coast.* Odile Vane had written those words herself—he recognized the hand from the sample the factor had included, a shaky signature witnessed by a harbor clerk who was probably paid in drink. *The one who knows the coast.* Which meant the map wasn't meant for him. It was meant for someone else, some local, some chart-reader, and that someone was out there right now, one rider small in the dark, carrying Halloway's fortune toward a coastline full of hiding places.

He smiled without warmth, the expression of a man practicing for a negotiation. Let the courier run. Couriers were slow, cautious creatures, burdened by caution the way mules were burdened by packs. The *Greed's Mercy* drew twelve feet loaded, carried forty men, and answered to no tide but the ones Halloway chose. Whatever the Throat gave up, it would give up to him. He'd spent twenty years learning that the sea kept no secrets—it only charged admission.

He pinched out the lantern and sat in the dark, listening to the hull breathe against the swells, hungry on purpose, patient on purpose, while somewhere ahead the dry moon climbed toward its appointment with the channel.

* * *

Ninety miles took him four days, and the map rode against his chest the whole way like a coal that wouldn't cool.

August traveled by night and holed up by day, and in the daylight hours, in whatever shade he could find, he worked the cipher. Odile's symbols resolved slowly, grudgingly, the way locks open for men who've picked too many of them. Numbers first—they were always numbers first. Depths, he decided. Soundings, matched to tide-heights, keyed to the phases scratched in the margin. She hadn't just marked the wreck. She'd written instructions for reaching it, precise as a surgeon's notes, and hidden them in a code simple enough for a child but obscure enough to stop a greedy man skimming.

Which told him she'd expected greed. Expected pursuit. Expected, perhaps, exactly the silhouette now trailing dust on the horizon behind him.

He saw the ship's topmast on the third night, far out past the shoals where no honest trader would loiter, and felt the arithmetic shift beneath him like sand. They weren't searching. They were *waiting*. Holding position off the coast, patient as a spider that has already felt the first tremor in the web. Which meant word had moved faster than he had. Which meant the race he'd imagined was already half run, and he was losing.

On the fourth evening he crested the last ridge above the Verren hills and saw the coast for the first time in nine years: the marshes silvered with evening, the Throat a dark seam of water cutting inland, the drowned forest standing hip-deep in the shallows like a congregation of penitents. The air tasted of salt and rot and, underneath both, something almost sweet—the smell of a place that had once been loved and wasn't anymore.

Six tides. He counted them against the moon, against the almanac in his head, against the fading light. Two gone already since Odile died. Four remaining before the Throat closed for the season, and one of those four belonged to whoever reached the wreck first.

Behind him, faint as a scratch on glass, the salvage fleet's lanterns pricked the dark water.

Ahead, somewhere in the marsh country, lived the one who knew the coast.

August pulled his collar up, put his head down, and started walking—visible now whether he liked it or not, a man with a name again whether he'd signed it or not, carrying a dead woman's promise toward a tide that would not wait.
