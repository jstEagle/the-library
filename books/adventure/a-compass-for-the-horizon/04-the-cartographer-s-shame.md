## 4. The Cartographer's Shame

The town of Brackwater smelled of tar and old fish and the particular sourness of a place that had given up pretending to be more than it was. August came down off the ridge at first light, the map pressed flat against his chest beneath his coat where it had ridden for three days like a second heartbeat. He had not slept properly since Odile Vane died with her hand still curled around his wrist. Sleep brought dreams of dry wells and promises, and he had learned long ago that the only reliable cure for dreams was motion.

Brackwater was motion of a kind. Its single street ran crooked between leaning buildings, its harbor held eleven boats and half of those were hulls with opinions. Gulls screamed over the fish racks. A dog with one ear watched him pass with the professional indifference of a creature that had seen every kind of stranger and been impressed by none.

He asked after Edda Lune at the chandlery, and the chandler spat into a bucket before answering.

"Third house past the drying sheds. The one with the shutters always closed." The man's mouth twisted. "She still buys ink. Nobody who buys that much ink is drawing anything worth looking at."

August bought a coil of rope he didn't need, because buying nothing would have meant conversation, and walked on.

* * *

The third house past the drying sheds was smaller than its neighbors and better kept, which surprised him. The shutters were closed as promised, but the doorframe bore scratches—fine, precise, deliberate scratches—and when August looked closer he saw they were not scratches at all but survey marks, cut in miniature: bearing lines, depth notations, the shorthand of someone who could not stop measuring the world even when the world had stopped wanting her to.

He knocked.

Nothing. He knocked again, harder, and heard movement inside, slow and deliberate, like something large deciding whether to be bothered.

"I'm not buying," said a voice through the wood. It was a woman's voice, low and roughened, the voice of someone who had spent years talking mostly to herself. "I'm not teaching. I'm not signing anything. And if you're from the Guild, you can tell them my eyes went bad and my hands shake and their money can rot."

"My name is Nystrom," August said. "I have a map I can't read."

Silence. Then, after a long moment: "Everyone has a map they can't read. That's what maps are *for*."

"This one belonged to Odile Vane."

The silence changed quality. It went from dismissive to attentive the way weather changes—the same air, suddenly charged. Bolts slid. The door opened six inches, then all the way, and Edda Lune stood in the gap with her sleeves rolled and her forearms stained black to the elbow.

She was perhaps fifty-five, built like a mast—tall, straight-backed, weathered gray at the temples—and her eyes were the pale, unblinking gray of a winter sea. She looked at August the way he imagined she once looked at coastlines: taking his measure, noting his defects, deciding whether he was worth charting.

"Odile Vane," she repeated. "Dead?"

"Three days ago. At the dry well past Sorrow Flats."

Edda's jaw worked. "I told her not to go out this season. I told her the channels were closing." She stepped back from the door. "Well. Come in, then, man with an unreadable map. But wipe your boots. I have enough dirt in my life."

* * *

Inside, the house was a single room surrendered entirely to paper. Charts hung from every wall, layered over older charts, over sketches, over notes pinned in drifts. A drafting table dominated the center beneath a lamp hung low on a pulley, and the table's surface was scarred with decades of compass points. The air smelled of ink and linseed and, faintly, of salt—though the sea was a quarter mile away. It had soaked into everything, August supposed. Into the paper, into the woman.

Edda took the map from him without asking and spread it under the lamp, and August watched her face as she read it.

He had spent three days trying to make sense of the thing himself. It was drawn on oiled linen, dense with lines that weren't quite contour lines and numbers that weren't quite soundings. He'd recognized fragments—a coastline here, a channel there—but the whole refused to resolve, like a face seen through warped glass.

Edda's face went through recognition, then disbelief, then something rawer that she turned away from him by turning toward the lamp.

"Where did she get this?" Edda asked quietly.

"She drew it. She was a prospector. She'd been looking for something for—" He hesitated. "For most of her life, I think. She called it finishing what she started."

"And she gave it to you." Edda looked up. "A stranger. A ranger who won't give his first name."

"Nystrom is my name."

"It's half a name. Everyone has a surname; only people hiding from something lead with the last part." She said it without malice, the way you'd note a reef on a chart. She bent back over the map. "These aren't depth markings. They're tide intervals. She was recording how many tides it takes for the silt to shift off the wreck each season. See here—" her stained finger traced a cluster of numerals, "—six tides at the end of dry season. That's her window. After that, the channel silts shut until spring."

"The Meridian," August said.

"You know the story?"

"Salvage fleet thinks so. There's a captain named Halloway sailing for it now."

"Halloway." Edda's mouth flattened. "Renn Halloway. Yes. He'll strip it to the ribs and call it salvage law." She straightened, and August saw her hands were trembling—not with age, he realized, but with something held down hard. "Do you know why I buy so much ink, Ranger Nystrom?"

"No."

"Because I charted these coasts twenty years ago, and I charted them *wrong*. Not by accident." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "The Guild paid me to leave certain features off. A settlement that didn't suit the land registries. Families whose claims were inconvenient. I drew the map they wanted and I was paid well and I told myself a map is just a map, it isn't a verdict." She touched the edge of Odile's linen almost tenderly. "And then Solmere drowned—or was allowed to drown, depending on which records you believe—and the people scattered, and my beautiful wrong maps helped everyone forget they'd ever existed. So now I draw. Everything. Every rock, every channel, every truth the Guild doesn't want on paper. I will die in this room with ink under my nails, and it will not be enough, but it is what I have."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a gull argued with the wind.

August found, to his surprise, that he wanted to say something. He was not a man who wanted to say things; saying things created records, and records created obligations. But he heard himself speak anyway.

"She made me promise," he said. "To deliver the map where it needed to go. I don't know where that is. I barely knew the woman. She was dying and I said yes because it was easier than watching her beg."

"That," said Edda, "is the most honest thing anyone's said to me in ten years." She began rolling the map with quick, practiced motions. "I'll come with you. Not for you—for me. For the thing I left off my charts. If Odile Vane found proof of Solmere, then I want to be standing there when it comes up out of the silt, and I want my name nowhere near Halloway's version of events."

"I work alone."

"So did she, apparently, and look where it got her." Edda snorted, already pulling a satchel from under the drafting table and filling it with instruments: dividers, a brass alidade, rolls of blank linen. "You need me. Those markings are tide-math; without me you'll read them as depths and drown yourself politely. Besides—" she paused at the door, lamp light catching the gray of her eyes, "—you didn't correct me when I said you were hiding from something. So we're both carrying shame, Ranger. Mine's just heavier, because I know exactly what it weighs."

* * *

They left Brackwater by the north path along the tideline, walking the wet sand where the going was firm and no one asked questions. Edda set a pace that shamed her age, her satchel bouncing against her hip, and talked the way lonely people talk when they've finally got an audience—about currents, about the Guild's cartographic crimes, about Odile Vane coming to her door four winters ago with sand in her boots and a question about a symbol.

"What symbol?" August asked.

"A circle quartered by a line. She'd found it carved somewhere, she wouldn't say where. I told her I'd seen it in old parish records from the coast settlements. Nothing more." Edda squinted at the horizon. "Why?"

"Nothing. Probably nothing."

It wasn't nothing. It was the small cold weight of coincidence, and August had learned to distrust coincidences the way he distrusted rivers at flood. But he filed it away and kept walking.

They made camp that night in the lee of a dune, a fire small enough to hide with cupped hands. Edda slept almost immediately, exhausted by company, and August sat up keeping watch out of habit more than necessity.

That was when he saw the figure slip along the base of the dune toward their packs.

He was moving before thought caught up—years of chasing thieves across empty basins had worn a groove in him, and Cass Merrow fit that groove exactly. He came around the dune's shoulder silent as shadow and caught her wrist mid-reach for Edda's satchel.

She didn't even flinch. She just looked up at him in the firelight with that same insolent calm she'd worn in the basin, when she'd lifted his rations and vanished into the night and left him stranded with an empty stomach and a full temper.

"You again," he said.

"You again," she agreed. "You walk loud for a ranger."

"I'm not a ranger anymore. I quit." He hadn't quit. He'd simply stopped reporting in, stopped filing, stopped existing on paper—but the distinction felt important tonight. "Give me one reason not to tie you up and carry you to the nearest magistrate."

"Because your friend's satchel has nothing worth stealing and we both know it. Because you're headed somewhere interesting, and interesting places are dangerous, and dangerous places go better with two sets of hands." She tilted her head, and firelight moved in her eyes. "And because you didn't turn me in last time, either. You had me dead to rights in the basin and you let me walk. Don't pretend you didn't. I counted your footsteps following me for an hour and then they stopped."

August's grip tightened, then loosened. He should turn her in. Every sensible instinct said so: thieves were liabilities, liabilities were anchors, anchors drowned men. He had spent nine years ensuring that no one depended on him and he depended on no one, and here was this girl offering herself up as a dependency with a grin.

But he thought of Odile Vane's hand around his wrist. Of debts that outlive the debtor. Of a dying woman trusting a stranger because strangers were all she had left.

"Third chance," he said. "You steal from us again—from *us*, do you understand, from the crew—you're done. Magistrate, chains, whatever Brackwater's got. Agreed?"

Cass's grin widened into something that was almost, briefly, genuine. "Agreed, Ranger."

"Don't call me that."

"What should I call you? You only gave me half a name."

He let go of her wrist and turned back toward the fire, and behind him he heard her settle against the dune with the untroubled ease of someone who had never once slept badly outdoors.

Edda Lune stirred in her bedroll and said, without opening her eyes, "I heard all of that. We're collecting strays now?"

"We're not collecting anything."

"Mm." The old woman rolled over. "That's what Odile said too, right before she handed you the map."

August stared into the coals and did not answer. Three of them now, bound by nothing but failure—his, hers, the girl's—and the tide-clock ticking somewhere out beyond the dark water, six beats and counting down.

In the morning, they would find the small footprints in the sand beside Cass's bedroll. Tiny ones. Bare ones. Leading away north, toward the marshes, as if someone had been traveling with them all along—and waiting.
