## 5. What the Girl Carried

The road to the coast was not a road at all, only the memory of one—two ruts drowned in bunchgrass, a fence line that wandered off and never came back. August walked it with Odile's map folded against his chest like a second heartbeat, and he told himself the ache in his shoulders came from the pack straps and nothing else.

Three days out of Edda Lune's cluttered workshop, they had found their rhythm. Edda walked in front because she claimed to know the way and August let her believe it; her pride needed the fiction more than his did. Cass drifted at the edges of the group like smoke, always half a field away, always within lunging distance of anything left unattended. And August brought up the rear, counting supplies each morning with the grim arithmetic of a man who had already been robbed once by the same hands.

Which was why, on the fourth morning, the numbers refused to come out right.

He crouched over the packs in the gray light before dawn, turning a bag of dried apricots over in his palms. Lighter than yesterday. Not empty—he'd have noticed empty—but lighter, the way a purse is lighter after a pickpocket has been careful rather than greedy. He weighed it again. Then the flour. Then the water skins.

Someone was eating.

"Cass," he said to the dark.

A pause from the far side of the cold fire ring. "I haven't touched your precious rations."

"I didn't say you had." He turned the apricot bag toward where he judged her face to be. "But you know who has."

Silence. The particular quality of it told him more than an answer would have—the silence of someone deciding whether to lie, then deciding the truth would cost less. When she finally spoke, her voice had lost its usual blade-edge.

"She doesn't eat much. I've been stretching it."

August stood slowly. "She."

Cass swore under her breath, low and inventive, and for a moment he thought she might run. Her whole body seemed to consider it—weight shifting to the balls of her feet, eyes flicking to the treeline. But the girl, whoever she was, was somewhere behind that silence, and running would mean leaving her, and whatever else Cass Merrow was, August had begun to suspect she was not that.

"You're going to be angry," Cass said.

"I'm usually angry. Get on with it."

* * *

The stowaway had been living in the bottom of the supply cart's false floor since before they'd left Edda's town—a space Cass had built herself, back when the cart belonged to her and theft was still her only trade. She emerged now blinking into the morning light: a child of perhaps eight or nine, small enough that three days of hiding had left no mark on her beyond grass-stains on her knees. Dark hair cut raggedly, as if by someone in a hurry. Eyes the color of shallow water over sand.

She looked at August the way wild things look at a snare—not afraid exactly, but measuring the distance to the treeline.

"Her name's Wren," Cass said. "Or that's what we call her. She doesn't talk. Hasn't said a word since I found her in Saltmarsh, months ago, picking through the fish market scraps. Somebody dumped her there. Or lost her. She wouldn't say even if she could."

"Why hide her?" August kept his voice flat. It took effort. "Why not just tell us?"

"Because you'd have made me leave her behind." Cass lifted her chin, defiant and honest at once. "And because—" she hesitated, and the hesitation cost her visibly, "—because I know what it is to be nobody's. You don't get to decide she's cargo, ranger. Nobody decided that about me either."

Edda had come forward during all this, drawn by the commotion, and now she crouched down to the child's level with the unhurried grace of a woman who had spent years bent over drafting tables. "Hello, Wren," she said gently. "I'm Edda. I make maps."

At the word *maps*, something changed in the child's face. Not fear. Recognition, almost—like hearing a word in a language she'd thought forgotten.

Wren reached into the collar of her shirt and drew out a cord. On the cord hung a token: carved bone or antler, worn smooth by handling, no bigger than a thumb. A spiral, coiling inward around a single point, with a line struck through it like a horizon crossing a sun.

August felt the ground tilt beneath him, though his feet hadn't moved.

He knew that symbol. He had spent two nights hunched over Odile's map by candlelight, tracing its margins, and there—in the lower corner, beside the notation *Solmere* in the dead woman's cramped hand—that same spiral, that same crossed line. He had assumed it was a surveyor's mark, a compass rose variant, nothing.

"Wren," Edda said softly. "May I see?"

The child hesitated, then placed the token in the cartographer's palm with the solemnity of a queen surrendering a crown jewel. Edda turned it over. Her breath caught audibly.

"This isn't a charm," she murmured. "This is a seal. A house-mark." She looked up at August, and he saw something in her face he had never seen there before—not shame, not calculation, but wonder, raw and unguarded. "I charted these coasts when I was young. Before my disgrace. There were stories among the old fishermen about a settlement north of the Meridian channel. Solmere. Drowned, they said—taken by the sea in a single night, generations ago. No charts ever confirmed it. No wreckage ever surfaced. The Admiralty struck it from the records as superstition." Her fingers tightened around the token. "But this mark appears in one place. One. In the oldest tide-tables, in the marginalia of charts so old they were drawn on vellum. The mark of Solmere. I thought it was a guild sigil. I thought—" She stopped, laughed shakily. "I thought a great many wrong things."

August took the map from his coat. His hands were steady; his pulse was not. He unfolded it to the lower corner and held it beside the token in Edda's palm.

The match was exact. Not similar. Exact—the same spiral, the same strike-line, down to the slight asymmetry where the carver's tool had slipped. Whoever had carved Wren's token had learned the mark from the same source that had taught Odile Vane.

"The map isn't leading us to treasure," Edda said quietly. "It's leading us to proof. To people. If Solmere existed—if it was drowned rather than destroyed—then there were survivors. Descendants. Records. Names." She looked at Wren, and her voice roughened. "This child may be carrying the last of them."

* * *

They walked on, and the day grew hot, and August found himself unable to stop glancing back at the girl.

Wren rode in the cart now, openly, sitting atop the supplies with her legs swinging, watching the country roll past with those pale, patient eyes. Cass had given up any pretense of apology and instead hovered nearby with the fierce, defensive air of a dog guarding a foundling. Every time August looked at them, some old stone in his chest shifted against another.

He had spent nine years making himself weightless. No name on paper, no debts, no ties. He had watched Odile Vane die holding his hand and had told himself the promise was a burden, a debt, a thing to be discharged and forgotten. He had told himself the same about the map, about Edda, about Cass.

But the girl was not a debt. The girl was a question—one he had no armor for.

Near midday they stopped at a stream, and while Edda pored over the map and Cass filled the waterskins, Wren slid off the cart and came to stand beside August. Close. Closer than anyone had voluntarily stood to him in years. She held up her token, then pointed at the map in Edda's hands, then looked up at him with an expression of such grave expectation that he felt it physically, like pressure behind the ribs.

"You want to know if it's true," he said.

She nodded once.

He crouched, bringing himself to her level, and studied the little carved spiral. Smooth from years of handling—years, plural, which meant someone had carried it before her. Someone had pressed it into her hand and taught her what it meant, or failed to teach her, or died trying.

"My grandmother wore one like it," Edda said suddenly, from behind them. August turned. The cartographer stood with the map forgotten in her hands, staring at the token as though seeing a ghost. "I never knew what it was. I asked her once, when I was small, and she hid it in her fist and changed the subject. She came from the coast. From the marsh country." Edda's voice dropped to almost nothing. "I always assumed she was born there. What if she wasn't? What if she was born *before*—"

She couldn't finish. She didn't need to. The mathematics hung in the air between them, terrible and luminous: if Solmere's survivors had scattered, if they had married and multiplied and forgotten, then the drowned settlement was not history. It was bloodline. It was hundreds of living people who did not know what they were.

Wren tugged August's sleeve. He looked down. She held out the token to him—not offering it, exactly. Showing him. And then, with her other hand, she pointed south, toward the coast, toward the channel, toward the wreck of the Meridian sleeping in its silt.

Then she pointed at herself.

August understood, and the understanding went through him like cold water. *That is mine. That is where I am from.*

Odile's dying words came back to him: *Deliver it where it needs to go.* He had imagined a place, a person, a destination. He had not imagined that the destination might be standing in front of him, eight years old and mute, wearing the answer on a cord around her neck.

"We should move," Cass said, breaking the silence. She was looking at the southern horizon with narrowed eyes, and August followed her gaze—and felt his stomach drop.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, a signal-smoke rising from the ridgeline five miles south, straight between them and the coast. Someone was marking position. Someone was talking to ships they could not yet see.

"Halloway's scouts," Cass said quietly. "They're ahead of us."

August rose, folding the map with hands that were no longer entirely steady. Above him, Wren climbed back into the cart and settled the token beneath her shirt, over her heart, where it had clearly lived a long time.

Six tides, Odile had calculated. They had already burned four.

"Walk fast," August said. "And pray the fifth tide waits for no one but us."
