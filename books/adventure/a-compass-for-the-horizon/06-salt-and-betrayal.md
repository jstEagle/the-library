## 6. Salt and Betrayal

The cave announced itself by smell before sight—a reek of kelp and old brine, the breath of something tidal and patient. August smelled it a quarter mile out and raised his hand without turning, the way he'd learned to in years of reading country that never spoke plainly. Behind him, Edda stopped. Cass stopped. Somewhere in the sling against Edda's chest, Wren went very still, as if the child understood silence was a language too.

"High water mark's wrong," Edda said quietly. She had come up beside him, her satchel of instruments clutched like a shield. "See the wrack line? It runs too high for this season. Something's been damming the outflow."

August crouched, pressed two fingers into the wet sand above the tideline, and read it the way other men read letters. Boat tar. A boot heel, deep-set, recently made. And there—half buried at the cave mouth—a fishhook of hammered iron, the kind the fleet men used, barbed for hauling salvage nets.

"They're already here," he said.

The realization settled over him with a terrible calm. He had counted tides, measured distances, rationed their water down to the swallow—and all the while Halloway's scouts had been walking the same shore, following the same map he carried, or perhaps simply waiting, patient as the sea itself, for the people who would inevitably come to them.

"We go around," Cass said. "There's a bluff path—"

"There's no time." August looked at the sky, where the sun hung low and fat over the water, bleeding orange into the haze. "Fifth tide comes tonight. If we're not through the marsh channel before it turns—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. They had all become fluent in the grammar of the countdown.

They might have made it anyway. August believed that afterward, turning it over in his mind like a stone with a flaw running through it. If Wren hadn't slipped on the wet rock at the cave's edge—if the child hadn't cried out, one small sharp sound swallowed almost instantly by the crash of surf—

Almost instantly. Not quite.

The first crossbow bolt buried itself in the driftwood an inch from Cass's hip. The second took Edda's shoulder.

She made no sound at first. That was the worst of it—the way she simply folded, the way her instruments scattered across the stones, brass calipers ringing off rock like small bells tolling for something. Then the sound came, low and ragged, and August was moving before thought caught up, dragging her by the collar into the shadow of the cave while Cass swept Wren up and ran bent-double behind them.

Inside, the cave opened into a throat of black stone, slick with spray, lit only by the gray light leaking from the entrance. August pressed Edda against the wall and put his hand over the wound. Blood welled between his fingers, hot and shocking in the cold air.

"Bolt's still in," she said through her teeth. Her face had gone the color of old candle wax. "Don't you dare pull it here. You'll bleed me dry before the tide does."

Boots on stone outside. Voices—two, maybe three, speaking in the clipped shorthand of fleet men. August drew his knife and did the arithmetic he hated: three scouts, one wounded woman, a thief, a mute child, and himself. The numbers were not kind.

Then Cass moved.

He saw it happen almost too slowly to stop—her snatching the oilcloth bundle from Edda's fallen satchel, the decoy they'd wrapped around a spare compass case to look like a map roll, and hurling it out through the cave mouth into the open light. It landed on the sand with a slap. One of the scouts shouted. Footsteps pounded toward it, away from them, and in that stolen breath August hauled Edda upright, got her arm over his shoulders, and ran.

They ran along the base of the bluff with the tide pool water sloshing at their knees, Cass ahead with Wren strapped now to her own back, the girl's arms locked around her neck. Behind them a shout went up—not alarm but triumph, a man's voice crying that he had it, he had the map—and August allowed himself one grim flicker of respect for Cass Merrow, who could steal anything, even an enemy's certainty.

* * *

They made two miles before Edda collapsed.

It happened at the edge of the salt marsh, where the ground turned soft and black and the smell of sulfur rose from the mud like the breath of something buried alive. She simply sat down mid-stride, as if her legs had resigned their commission, and August caught her before her head hit the muck.

The bolt came out then, because it had to come out somewhere, and this was nowhere, which was as good a place as any. She bit down on the strap of her own satchel while he worked it free, and she did not scream, though tears cut clean lines through the salt grime on her face. Cass heated the last of their spirits over a scrap fire and poured it into the wound while August bound it with strips of his own shirt. Wren watched everything with those enormous dark eyes, memorizing, cataloguing, saying nothing.

"She needs rest," Cass said. "Real rest. Days of it."

"The tide doesn't give us days." August tied off the bandage. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was the rest of him that felt like it was coming apart at the seams. "We move at slack water or we don't move at all."

"You can't carry her through sinking marsh."

"I've carried worse."

"Have you?" Cass's voice cracked on it, and something in the crack made him look at her properly for the first time since the cave. She was kneeling in the mud with Wren's weight still against her back, and her face was doing something complicated—guilt and fear and something older than either, something that had been waiting in her for weeks like a splinter working toward the skin.

"Cass."

"I sold it." The words came out flat, fast, like coins spilled from a torn purse. "The map. Not the map itself—I never saw it—but word of it. Odile Vane, dying at Dry Well, carrying a chart to the Meridian. I sold that to one of Halloway's runners three weeks ago. For forty crowns and passage east." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "I didn't know what it meant. I didn't know any of it. I just knew I was hungry and the fleet pays for rumors."

The marsh went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

August stood up slowly. He looked at her—at the thief who had escaped him across the basin, who had led him to Edda, who carried a child not her own on her back through hostile country—and he felt the promise he'd made to a dead woman turn over inside him like a body in shallow water.

"Three weeks," he said.

"Yes."

"Before Odile died. Before any of this."

"Yes."

"So every mile we've walked, every tide we've raced—" He heard his own voice go cold and distant, the ranger's voice, the voice he used on strangers and corpses. "Every drop of blood in that bandage is downstream of what you sold."

Cass didn't flinch. He almost wished she would. "Yes," she said again. "And if you leave me here, I'll understand. But take Wren. Take Edda. Finish it. Just—" For the first time her composure broke, and what was underneath was young and terrified and terribly human. "Just don't make the child pay for me."

August turned away.

He walked ten paces toward the marsh and stood looking at the black water and the drowned grass and the far gray line where the channel waited, six hours' walk and a lifetime away. Behind him he could hear Edda breathing in shallow, careful pulls. He could hear Wren's small fidgeting, the child's hands patting Cass's shoulders in a rhythm that might have been comfort given or sought.

He could walk away. That was the truth of it, the clean and simple truth he had built nine years of invisibility upon: he could walk into the marsh alone, follow the channel, reach the Meridian, keep the letter of the promise, and never see any of them again. No more debts. No more names. No more people whose wounds became his wounds, whose betrayals became his betrayals.

No one to lose.

His hand found the map tube at his belt—Odile's map, Odile's dying grip still somehow in the leather—and he thought of her voice at the dry well: *Someone has to finish what I started.* He had said yes mostly to end the conversation. He had told himself that ever since. But promises were debts that outlived the debtor, and debts, he was beginning to understand, were just another word for the ties that held a man to the world.

"Get up," he said, without turning around.

Silence. Then Cass's voice, small: "What?"

"Both of you. Get up. We're losing the light." He turned. Cass was staring at him with an expression he couldn't read, and Edda had lifted her head from the mud, watching him with the weary shrewdness of a woman who had already known how this would end. "You sold a rumor, Cass. Halloway's going to sell these people's whole history, if he gets the chance. You want to square it? Walk."

She scrambled up. Wren tightened her arms around her neck, and for a moment the two of them made a single shape against the graying sky—one creature, four eyes, bound together by whatever silent contract children and thieves negotiate among themselves.

"Edda can't march," Cass said.

"Edda doesn't have to." August knelt and pulled the driftwood from their abandoned fire, lashing it with cordage into a rough litter, working fast, his hands finally admitting what his pride wouldn't: that he had never once considered leaving, and that the anger was only grief wearing armor, and that the armor had a hole in it now shaped exactly like three people he had known for less than a month. "We drag her. Slow and low, through the channels. Halloway's scouts will be chasing a decoy map until dawn, and by then we'll be in the marsh where their boats can't follow."

Edda managed a ghost of her old dry smile. "The cartographer, carried through her own uncharted marsh. There's a humiliation I'll want recorded accurately someday."

"Hold your instruments together," August said, "and I'll record whatever you like."

They moved out as the sun went down, a strange procession: a ranger dragging a litter, a thief carrying a child, a wounded woman navigating by memory and pain. The marsh took them in by degrees, the black water rising past their ankles, their knees, the drowned grass whispering around them like a congregation murmuring warnings.

It was Wren who stopped them.

She stiffened against Cass's back and made her sound—the small urgent click of her tongue that meant *look*—and pointed with one thin arm back the way they'd come, toward the darkening coast.

Far off, at the edge of the world, lights were moving. Lanterns, many of them, strung out along the bluff in a slow descending line. Not scouts. Not a patrol.

The fleet had found the decoy. And instead of chasing it, they had simply brought everything.

Behind them, faint across the water, came the groan of ship's timber and the rattle of anchor chain—Halloway's main vessel standing into the shallows, close enough now that August could count the lanterns on her deck.

Six hours to slack water. Five miles of drowning marsh. And the sixth tide, when it came, would bring the sea—and Captain Renn Halloway—straight to the wreck of the Meridian.

"Walk faster," August said.
