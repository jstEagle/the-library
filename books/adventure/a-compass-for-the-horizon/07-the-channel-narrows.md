## 7. The Channel Narrows

The marsh began to drink itself an hour before dawn.

August felt it before he saw it—the ground going soft under his boots, the smell of the air changing from dust to brine, a low wet breathing in the reeds as the tide turned and came looking for them. Behind him, Edda rode slung across Wren's small back like a sack of grain, her bandaged shoulder seeping through the linen in slow brown blooms. Cass brought up the rear, driving them forward with a flat, urgent whisper whenever anyone slowed.

"Channel narrows past the dead mangroves," August said. "If we're not through by the fifth tide, we walk out of here on the bottom of the sea."

"Then stop talking and walk."

He almost smiled. It was a strange thing, being ordered about by a thief he'd once had in irons, stranger still that he obeyed. Nine years of answering to no one, and now he was measuring his life against the rhythm of water because a dying woman's map said he must.

The marsh opened around them in gray tiers—mudflats stitched with creeks, hummocks of salt grass, the drowned skeletons of trees standing knee-deep in the shallows like a congregation waiting for a sermon that never came. The sky was the color of old pewter. Somewhere behind, beyond the sandbar at the mouth of the estuary, Halloway's ship *Kestrel* sat at anchor, too deep of draft to follow them into the shallows—but not too proud to send her boats.

August counted heads without meaning to. Four. He'd spent nine years making sure the number was one. Now he caught himself doing arithmetic on other people's survival as naturally as breathing, and he didn't know when that had started, or how to make it stop.

Edda groaned against Wren's back. The girl adjusted her grip without breaking stride, staggering under the cartographer's weight, her bare feet finding purchase on mud that would have swallowed a grown man to the shin. She hadn't made a sound since they'd found her—not a whimper, not a cry, even when the scouts' crossbow bolt had torn through Edda's shoulder and the cave had filled with shouting and spray. She just carried what she carried, silent as the tide itself.

"Hold," August said, and dropped to one knee at the edge of the first creek.

The water was coming in fast—a finger of silver threading between the mudbanks, thickening as he watched. On the far side, maybe two hundred yards of open flat, then the dead mangroves, then the channel proper: a winding throat of deeper water that would carry them, if they timed it right, all the way to the Meridian's grave.

And above the mangroves, faint but unmistakable, the glint of lantern light. A boat. Halloway's men, poling along the outer channel, cutting off the long way around.

"They're ahead of us," Cass breathed.

"They think they are." August studied the creek, the current, the angle of the lightening sky. "This crossing fills first and drains last. We go now, wade the middle where the current's weakest, and we're in the mangroves before their boat rounds the point. After that—" he glanced back at Edda, at the blood, at Wren's set little jaw, "—after that we let the marsh do our fighting."

"And if the marsh decides it likes *them* better?"

"Then we'll have bigger problems than a thief with a guilty conscience."

Something flickered across Cass's face—hurt, maybe, or its close cousin, shame—and August regretted the words even as they left his mouth. Not enough to take them back. But he noted the regret, filed it away with the other unfamiliar things this journey kept handing him.

They crossed. The water took them to the waist, cold enough to steal breath, and Wren bore Edda high against her chest like something rescued from a flood, her eyes fixed on August the whole way over. Watching him. Trusting him, or weighing him—he couldn't tell which frightened him more.

* * *

Bram Tull had been a salvage man for eleven years, and he knew the smell of a bad voyage the way sailors know weather.

It was in the way Captain Halloway stood at the *Kestrel*'s rail, glass raised toward the distant marsh, saying nothing. It was in the silence among the crew—the particular quiet of men who had signed on for a wreck full of gold and were beginning to suspect they'd signed on for something else entirely. And it was in the girl's token, hanging now in a leather pouch on the captain's belt, fished from the pocket of a scout who'd come back empty-handed and bleeding.

A carved bird. A child's toy, Bram would have called it, except the captain wouldn't put it down.

"Marsh runs six feet at flood," Bram offered, when the silence had gone on too long. "Our boats can't follow them into the mangrove tangle. We should run up the outer channel tonight and be at the wreck site before—"

"We wait." Halloway didn't lower the glass. "They'll lead us to it. Six days of hard country with a wounded woman and a mute child—they'll make mistakes. Mistakes leave marks."

Bram chewed on that. Eleven years, and he'd learned that the marks a man left told you more than the words out of his mouth. Halloway's mark was a fleet of four ships and forty men who ate because he fed them. But lately the marks were different. Scouts sent into tidal caves without backup. A chart of the coast burned rather than shared. And the captain's own eyes, when he looked at that carved bird, held something Bram had only seen once before—in a man watching a debt collector come up the gangway.

"Captain," Bram said carefully, "the men are asking what's in the wreck. Gold, they figure. Salvage rights say—"

"Salvage rights." Halloway finally lowered the glass, and his smile was the kind that made green hands volunteer for night watch. "There's no salvage law out here, Tull. There's the tide, and there's me. Get your boat ready. If they reach the wreck before us, I want to know why."

Bram went. But at the top of the ladder he paused, because below him the captain had taken out the carved bird again, turning it in his thick fingers, and Bram would have sworn on his mother's grave that Renn Halloway looked afraid of it.

* * *

The dead mangroves received them like conspirators.

August led them single file along a root-ridge above black water, the trees' gnarled knees rising around them in the half-light, every branch hung with drying weed that whispered as they passed. Edda had slipped into fevered half-sleep; Wren carried her still, refusing all help, though her legs shook. Cass scouted ahead, moving through the tangle with the same boneless ease she'd used stealing his rations a lifetime ago.

At midmorning they reached the channel proper—and August's stomach dropped.

The water was already thigh-deep in the cut, and rising. The fifth tide had turned early, or the wind had backed around, or the sea simply meant to have them. Ahead, the channel wound three miles through sinking marsh to the high ground near the wreck site. Three miles of water climbing around their ankles, their knees, their waists.

Behind, through the trees, came the creak of oarlocks. Two boats. Closing.

Wren stopped. Set Edda down gently against a root. Looked at August with those dark, patient eyes, then at Cass, then back at him—and pointed, small hand steady, at a thread of higher ground snaking off to the north that August had already dismissed as too exposed.

"No," he said. "Open flat. They'd see us."

She pointed again. Then, slowly, deliberately, she cupped her hands and struck them together—a dry, hollow clap, like fire catching.

Cass went very still. Then she grinned, fierce and sudden, and August watched understanding move across her face like sun across water.

"A fire," Cass said. "Out on the flat. Big and wrong and *visible*. They'll think we've stopped to camp—wounded woman, can't travel. They'll hold position till dark, send a boat to confirm, lose the tide window." She was already unshouldering her pack. "I can build one. I've built a hundred decoy fires. It's the first thing you learn, running."

"It won't fool them past dusk."

"It doesn't need to fool them past dusk. It needs to fool them for three hours." She met his eyes, and there was no guile in her face now, none of the quick bright trickery—just a raw, exhausted honesty. "Let me do this. Please. I sold you all once. Let me buy it back."

August looked at her a long moment. Every careful ledger in his head said *no*—said trust was a debt that always got called in, said the thief who betrayed you once had simply established precedent. But the ledger was getting heavy. It had been getting heavier since Odile Vane died with his promise in her mouth.

"Take Wren," he said. "Build it fast, burn it hot, and be back in the water before it catches. If you're not back in an hour, we don't wait."

"I know." Something eased in her shoulders. "You'd leave me. That's fair."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Come back anyway."

* * *

From the *Kestrel*'s lead boat, Bram Tull saw the smoke rise off the northern flat an hour past noon—a dirty black column, too much green wood in it, burning wrong.

"Campfire," said the young poleman beside him.

"Burning wrong," Bram said again. He'd built decoy fires himself, back in his poaching years, and he knew the signature: fuel stacked to smoke, not flame, placed where eyes would find it. This was textbook. Which meant either their quarry were amateurs—which the last five days argued against—or someone aboard that wreck of a party knew exactly what a hunting boat looked like.

He signaled the second boat to hold. Held his own position. Watched the smoke stand up straight in the windless air, lying to them with every curl of it.

Three hours, he figured. Maybe four. Enough time for four exhausted people to move three miles of flooding channel, if they moved like their lives depended on it.

Which, Bram thought, watching the smoke with something uncomfortably like respect, theirs did.

He didn't report the fire's oddity to the captain. He couldn't have said why. Only that some part of him—the part that remembered being hungry and hunted and young—was quietly glad to see them slip the net.

* * *

They came out of the marsh at dusk, wrung out and salt-crusted, onto a shelf of shell and clay above the drowning flats. Behind them the channel had become a sea; somewhere in that gray expanse the decoy fire had burned down to embers, and somewhere beyond it Halloway's boats sat anchored, waiting for a camp that wasn't there.

Edda was delirious, murmuring fragments of old charts and older regrets. Wren knelt over her, feeding her water from Cupped hands, tireless as the tide she'd outwitted. Cass stood dripping at the waterline, soaked to the ribs, smelling of smoke and mud, alive.

"You came back," August said. It came out more like accusation than welcome.

"Told you I would." She pushed wet hair from her face. "You owe me nothing now. We're square."

And August realized, with a sensation like setting down a pack he'd forgotten he wore, that he'd stopped counting. Somewhere in the black water, in the smoke, in Wren's silent certainty—he'd stopped keeping the ledger. Not because the debts were paid, but because they'd stopped being debts. What bound them now was something else, something without columns or terms, and he had no name for it except the simple fact that when Cass had been late returning, the minute after the hour had been the longest of his nine invisible years.

"Square," he agreed quietly.

Below them, as the last light failed, the tide drew back from the flats with a long retreating sigh—and there, far out where the water peeled away from the mud, something vast and dark began to emerge from the silt. A shape. A hull. Ribs of black timber rising like the bones of a leviathan exhaling after thirty years underwater.

The Meridian, showing herself at last.

And on the horizon behind her, riding the last of the flood, the masts of the *Kestrel* came into view.
