## 8. The Meridian

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

August stood on the mudflats with the others and watched the sea withdraw, peeling back in long silver sheets until the horizon seemed to retreat from them, ashamed. The marsh behind them steamed in the early light. Gulls wheeled and screamed over ground no gull had walked in a generation. And there, at the center of the exposed basin, dark as a rib cage picked clean by decades of salt—

The Meridian.

She was smaller than August had imagined and larger than he'd feared. Her hull lay canted to starboard, half-swallowed by silt, but her spine still stood: ribs of blackened timber arcing up out of the mud like the bones of something that had once been magnificent. Barnacles crusted her flanks in gray constellations. Kelp hung from her rails in ropes gone stiff as wire. Where her name had been painted—*Meridian*, Edda said, in gold leaf that some owner had once been proud of—only a ghost of the letters remained, readable now more by faith than by sight.

"She's been down forty years," Edda murmured. She had come down from the dune on Cass's arm, her bandaged side slowing her, her eyes doing what her legs could not—moving fast, hungry, everywhere at once. "Maybe fifty. The charts called this deep water. Whatever drowned Solmere drowned it well."

Cass said nothing. She hadn't said much since the cave, since the confession August still carried in his chest like a swallowed stone. But she stood close, closer than she needed to, and when Wren tugged at her sleeve and pointed at the wreck with an urgency that needed no words, Cass knelt and lifted the girl onto her hip without being asked.

They crossed the flats while the tide was still out, boots sucking in the mud, the smell of it thick and mineral and old—the smell of things the sea had kept and was briefly surrendering. Fish flopped in the shrinking pools. A crab the size of August's fist raised its claws at him and thought better of it. Wren wriggled down and ran ahead, fearless, her bare feet finding paths through the muck that none of the adults could see, as though the ground itself remembered her.

At the hull, they found the breach.

It was low on the starboard side, where the timbers had split—a gap wide enough for a person, its edges worn smooth by water and time. Odile's map had marked it with a small cross and a single word in her cramped hand: *enter*. August went first, knife drawn, lamp lit, telling himself the tightness in his throat was the smell.

Inside, the dark was total and breathing.

His lamp found a sloping deck furred with sediment, beams overhead dripping condensation, the whole interior echoing with the small sounds of a place adjusting to visitors after long solitude. He moved carefully. Forty years of rot made every step a negotiation.

"Steady," Edda called from the breach, her voice flattened by the wood. "The cargo hold should be aft. If Odile's markings are true—and they were always true, that woman couldn't draw a lie if she tried—it'll be under the main mast step. Sealed."

It took them an hour. An hour of hauling themselves along tilted decks, of Cass prying open hatches that shrieked like living things, of Wren leading them through turns that seemed impossible for a child to know. August kept telling himself the girl was lucky. Kept telling himself that. But she moved through the wreck the way he moved through country he'd charted himself, and the telling didn't take.

Beneath the mast step, exactly where the map promised, they found the seal: a sheet of copper nailed flat over a hatch, green with age, stamped with a symbol.

Wren touched it before anyone could stop her.

A circle bisected by a line, with three points rising from its crown like reeds.

Edda made a sound August had never heard from her—not grief, not quite awe, something between a prayer and a wound reopening. "Solmere," she whispered. "That's the town mark. That's—" She pressed her palm flat against the copper. "I mapped these coasts for six years and I never knew why the old surveyors left this bay blank. They weren't leaving it blank. They were erasing it. Someone paid for this whole coastline to be forgotten."

"And Odile spent her life un-forgetting it," August said.

He worked the nails loose with the pry bar while Cass held the lamp. The copper came away with a groan, and beneath it was a hatch, and beneath the hatch—

Not gold.

August had braced for gold. He had braced for Halloway's kind of treasure, chests and coin and the ugly arithmetic of men who killed for weight they could carry. What the lamplight found instead was paper. Hundreds upon hundreds of pages, wrapped in oilcloth and wax, packed into lead-lined boxes that had kept the sea out for four decades. Ledgers. Deeds. Letters bound with cord gone brittle. Charts covered in the same cramped hand that had drawn the map in August's pack. And photographs—or something near them, tintypes sealed in glass, faces looking up out of the dark.

Faces. Dozens of them. Families, standing stiffly before buildings that no longer existed anywhere but here.

"Open one," Edda said hoarsely. "Open one, August."

He cut the cord on the nearest bundle. The oilcloth parted, and inside were documents in a formal hand, dated the year of the flood: affidavits. Testimony, sworn and signed, name after name after name—*Maren Ostergaard, fisherwoman; Tobias Vane, shipwright; Ilse Vane, midwife*—each attesting to what had happened when the company men came to Solmere after the waters rose. How the survivors had been declared dead on paper while still breathing. How their land titles had been transferred, their names struck from the registries, their claims dissolved by men who had never seen the town and never would.

"They didn't drown," Cass said slowly. She had taken a page without asking and was reading by the lamp's edge, her lips moving. "The flood took the town, but the people lived. And the company—they just—" She looked up, and for once there was no cleverness in her face at all. "They wrote them out. All of them. Like August writes himself out, except—" She stopped. Went pale. "Except someone did it *to* them."

"To hundreds of them," Edda said. "And this—" she lifted a ledger, heavy as a brick, "—this is proof. Signatures. Witnesses. Property lines. Enough to reopen every claim. Enough to give back what was stolen." Her voice cracked. "This isn't treasure. This is a whole town's right to exist."

Wren stood among the boxes with her hands full of tintype glass, turning the faces toward the light one by one. Then she stopped. Held one image very close to her eyes. Her small finger traced the edge of the frame, and then she looked up—at August, at Edda, at Cass—and pointed to the woman in the photograph, young, unbending, standing with her hand on a child's shoulder.

Odile Vane. Fifty years younger. Alive.

And the child beside her—Wren touched her own chest. Touched the carved token at her throat. Made a sound, small and urgent, the closest thing to speech any of them had heard from her.

"She's kin," Edda breathed. "Odile's kin. Or the child in the picture is her mother, or—" She shook her head. "It doesn't matter. It matters that she's *heir*. August, do you understand? If the claims can be restored, this girl has a name waiting for her. A house. A history. Everything that was taken."

August looked at the child holding her own family in her two hands, and felt something shift in him, vast and slow, like the tide itself changing its mind.

For nine years he had believed a name was a hook, a handle by which the world could drag you where you didn't want to go. He had filed himself down to nothing to escape that hook. And here was a girl who had nothing—no name, no voice, no past anyone would write down—and the whole difference between her and the mud outside was sitting in these boxes. Paper. Ink. Names on a line.

The true cargo was identity itself. And it was worth more than all the gold Halloway could dream of, which meant Halloway could never be allowed to learn what it was.

"We move everything," August said. "Tonight. Every box, up the channel, before the sixth tide. We don't leave a page."

"It's hundreds of pounds," Cass said. "We're four people and one of us is gutted."

"Then we start now."

But even as he said it, August heard it—the sound he had been listening for since dawn without admitting it. Far off across the flats, beyond the retreating pools, a bell. Ship's bell. Then another. And beneath them, rolling in over the wet mud like weather, the creak of capstans and the carrying voices of men launching boats.

Edda turned toward the breach, her face draining. "The tide's coming back."

"No," August said quietly, moving to the gap in the hull, the knife already in his hand again. "That's not the tide."

Out on the brightening water, six longboats were pulling for the wreck, oars flashing in rhythm, and at the prow of the first boat a man stood with his coat open to the wind, watching the Meridian emerge from the silt like a man watching a debt surface.

Renn Halloway had found his treasure.

He just didn't yet know it wasn't the one he'd come for.
