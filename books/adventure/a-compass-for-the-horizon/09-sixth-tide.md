## 9. Sixth Tide

The water came back the way a debt comes due—slowly at first, then all at once.

August felt it before he saw it: a cold breath rising through the wreck's broken seams, threading between his boots where he crouched in the Meridian's flooded hold. The silt outside had begun to stir, gray ghosts of it lifting off the seabed, and through the gash in the hull he could see the channel brightening from green to silver as the sixth tide turned.

They had one hour. Maybe less.

"Again," he said, and passed another armful of ledgers up through the hatch to Edda's waiting hands.

She was above him on the tilted deck, her injured arm bound tight against her chest, using the other to stack oilcloth-wrapped bundles into the skiff that Cass held against the wreck's flank. Every swell knocked the little boat sideways; every knock made August's teeth ache. But Edda worked with the fury of a woman who had spent twenty years being told her maps were lies, and now held proof they weren't.

"There are crates under the ballast," she called down, voice raw. "Odile marked them on her chart—sealed wax, official seals. Those are the ones that matter."

August waded deeper into the hold. The water was at his knees now, black and cold as a well. His lantern swung shadows across rows of drowned bunks, a captain's chair fused to the deck by rust, and everywhere the soft drifting lace of weed that had grown over the bones of the ship like moss over a grave.

He found the crates wedged beneath a collapsed beam. Three of them, tarred oak, the wax seals intact after all these years—Solmere's town seal, a sun split by a horizon line. He'd seen it carved into Wren's token. He'd seen it burned into Odile's map. Now he understood what it meant: not a treasure mark. A signature. Hundreds of them.

The first crate came up easy. The second shifted the beam, and something below it groaned—a long, structural complaint that traveled through the whole hull.

Above him, faintly, came the sound he had been dreading all morning.

Oars.

* * *

Cass heard them too. She went still in the skiff, one hand flat on the gunwale, head cocked like a bird dog's. Through the sea fog rolling off the marsh, shapes were resolving—two longboats, then three, packed with Halloway's men, blades wrapped in cloth so they wouldn't flash.

"They're early," she whispered. "Tide's barely turned. Nobody runs a boarding at slack water unless they don't care about losing boats."

"Because they don't plan to leave," Edda said quietly. She looked at August as he surfaced through the hatch, crate in his arms, seawater streaming off him. Something passed between them—not an apology, not yet, but the shape of one. "He misjudged the channel. Or he doesn't care."

August set the crate in the skiff and looked out at the fog. Six tides. He'd counted them like a miser counts coins, and here was the last one spending itself beneath him while thirty armed men rowed toward everything Odile Vane had died to protect.

Wren was huddled in the bow among the documents, small and silent, clutching her carved token. When August met her eyes, she held the token out to him—not offering it, he realized. Showing him. *Remember whose this is.*

"I'll hold them here," he said.

Edda's head snapped around. "You'll *what*—"

"The skiff draws two feet. This wreck draws none, and the channel behind you is already shin-deep. They can't bring the longboats past the sandbar for another hour." He was pulling off his coat as he spoke, folding it around the lantern. "Take the first load out. Come back for the rest if you can. If you can't—" he almost smiled, "—then I was never here anyway."

"That's not funny," Cass said.

"It wasn't a joke." He stepped past her into the shallows, boots sinking into the cold mud, and took the boat hook from the skiff's floor. It was a poor weapon. It would have to do. "Go. Both of you. That's not a request."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Cass did something he didn't expect: she grabbed the front of his shirt and kissed him, hard and quick, salt-stung and furious, and shoved him away before he could decide how he felt about it. "Come back," she said. "Or I'm putting your name on every document in that crate. August Nystrom, hero, died being stupid."

Then she was rowing, Edda beside her, Wren's small shape vanishing into the fog—and August Nystrom stood alone in the rising tide, holding a boat hook, waiting for men he intended to disappoint.

* * *

Halloway came up the wreck's seaward side himself. Of course he did. August watched him climb, broad and deliberate, a man who moved like he owned whatever he stood on. The fog thinned around the boarding party as they swarmed the deck, and there were more than thirty—closer to fifty, some of them boys with borrowed knives.

Renn Halloway found August standing at the main hatch with the boat hook grounded like a spear.

"Ranger," Halloway said. He said it the way other men said *thief*. "I wondered when you'd stop running. You have a talent for being exactly where things end up."

"You have a talent for arriving after the work is done."

Halloway laughed, short and genuine, and August hated that it was genuine. "Where is it? The cargo. My scouts say Vane's map leads here, and my scouts say you've been hauling crates since dawn. Gold? Specie? What does a dead prospector hide in a wreck for forty years?"

"Paper," August said.

Something flickered across Halloway's face—there and gone, fast, but August caught it. Recognition. Fear, even.

"Paper," Halloway repeated.

"Deeds. Testimony. The records of a town called Solmere, and everyone who was driven off its land by men who wanted it cheap." August let that sit. "Men like the ones who financed your fleet, Captain. I'd guess you already know that. I'd guess that's why you're really here."

The silence stretched. Around them, Halloway's crew had gone quiet, listening the way sailors listen—badly, but completely.

"I'm a salvage man," Halloway said at last. "I don't care about paper. I care about what paper buys. Give me the cargo, ranger, and you can walk. Take the girl and the cripple with you. I'm generous when I'm paid."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take it, and the tide takes you. Look down, ranger." Halloway nodded at August's knees, where the water swirled gray and hungry. "You're standing in my argument."

August looked down. Then he looked up, and did the thing he had spent nine years training himself never to do: he told the truth about who he was.

"My name is August Nystrom. Desert ranger, fourth basin district. And I gave my word to Odile Vane that this archive reaches Solmere's descendants, and I am going to keep that word if the sea has to close over both of us to do it."

He swung the boat hook.

It was a poor weapon, but desperation makes even poor weapons honest. He caught Halloway across the wrist, spun, drove the butt-end into a boy's chest, and went over the hatch coaming backward into the hold as the deck erupted around him. Boots thundered overhead. Someone shouted about the water level. And August, waist-deep now in the dark, put his shoulder to the third crate and *pushed*, because the promise was heavier than the fear and always had been.

* * *

They came at him in the hold in ones and twos, because the flooding wreck frightened them more than he did. He fought the way he tracked: patiently, economically, letting the dark and the water do half the work. A man slipped on weed and went under; August hauled him out by the collar and shoved him toward the ladder, because drowning boys wasn't part of any promise he'd made. Another got a forearm across his throat until August's vision sparked—and then the man was simply gone, yanked upward, and Cass's voice came down the hatch, ragged and triumphant: "Skiff's empty! We came back, you idiot!"

Edda appeared at the hatch mouth, silhouetted against gray light, throwing down a line. "Last crate! Move!"

He moved. The hull groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a dying animal, and the water jumped a foot in the space of ten heartbeats. August got the crate to the line, got it lashed, got it hauled up—and then the deck tilted beneath him as the Meridian finally began to settle, stern-first, into the channel's throat.

On the tilting deck, in the chaos of retreating sailors and rising water, August found himself face to face with Renn Halloway one last time. The captain had lost his hat. He looked older without it, and suddenly very far from home.

"Help me," Halloway said. Not a command. A request, hoarse and human. His longboat was gone—swamped at the sandbar, just as August had known it would be—and the channel around the wreck was a churning brown torrent, deepening by the minute. His crew scrambled for the skiff Cass held steady against the rail, and there wasn't room, and everyone knew it.

August looked at the man. At the water. At the skiff, where Wren sat watching with those enormous, patient eyes.

He held out his hand.

Halloway stared at it. And August saw the exact moment the captain chose—the moment pride closed over his face like ice over a pond. "I don't take rescue from clerks," Halloway said.

"Suit yourself." August stepped into the skiff. "There's high ground east of the bar. You might make it swimming. The tide won't wait to find out."

They pulled away through the rain of spray as the Meridian went down for the second time in a century, taking nothing with her but her own rotten bones. Behind them, Halloway stood on the settling deck with the flood climbing his thighs, his fleet scattered, his fortune revealed as paper he'd refused to touch—and August did not look back again, because looking back was a luxury for men with nothing ahead.

Ahead, through the thinning fog, the marsh opened onto open water, and open water onto a coastline where, somewhere, a town waited to learn its own name.

Cass rowed. Edda held the crate. Wren, wedged between them, reached out and gripped August's sleeve—just gripped it, saying nothing, saying everything.

The sixth tide carried them in.
