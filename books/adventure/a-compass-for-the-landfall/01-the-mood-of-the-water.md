## 1. The Mood of the Water

The river told Silas Thornbury things it told no one else.

It spoke in the color of the water over a sandbar — tea-brown where it ran deep, the pale green of bottle glass where it shallowed, and that particular milky swirl, like cream stirred into coffee, that meant a bar was building where no chart said one should be. It spoke in the way driftwood rode the current, corkscrewing left or right to show her where the channel bent. It spoke in smells: mud and rot when the level dropped, pine resin and cold stone when it rose. Most captains read the river. Silas had learned, over nineteen years, to *listen* to it, and the Kestrel rewarded listening the way a wild animal rewards patience — grudgingly, and only so far.

She stood at the wheel now with one boot hooked on the brass rail, watching the morning fog burn off the flooded flats above Millbrook Landing. The *Magpie* moved beneath her like a living thing, sixty feet of patched hull and stubborn heart, her paddle wheel turning with the sound of a slow heartbeat — *whump, whump, whump* — steady as a clock in a house where nobody waited up for anyone.

"Bar's shifted," she murmured to the boat, because after all these years the habit of talking to the *Magpie* had outlived every other conversation she'd ever had. "Feel it? She's moved us east again."

The *Magpie*, being wood and iron and forty years of other people's bad decisions, said nothing. But Silas adjusted the throttle anyway, easing her a cable's length starboard, and felt through the deck plates the moment the current took hold properly — that smooth, muscular grip, like a hand closing around the keel. The river approved. For now.

Below decks, cargo: flour in sealed tins, lamp oil, nails, a crate of schoolbooks for the mission at Two Rivers, and — because Mrs. Okonkwo at the landing had pressed it on her with the fierce generosity of women who refuse to let a person live entirely on beans — a wheel of cheese wrapped in cloth, going soft at the edges. Above decks, Silas Thornbury, thirty-eight years old, five foot six of weathered sinew and salt-and-pepper hair cut short enough to shame a convict, wearing the same canvas coat she'd worn for six seasons and would wear for six more if the elbows held.

And below decks, in the ledger she kept locked in the chart drawer, debts. That was the third voice in her life, after the river and the boat. The bank at Corvane held the mortgage on the *Magpie* — two hundred and forty pounds remaining, renewed each spring like a wound reopening. The engine parts from Halloran & Sons sat unpaid since March. And there was the matter of young Piet Voss, who worked her deck for half wages and the promise of learning the river, and who deserved better than half wages, and knew it, and stayed anyway, which made it worse somehow.

"Captain." Piet came up the ladder with the telegram flapping in his fist, his face doing something complicated. "Fog station flagged us down. Wire came through Corvane this morning."

"Cost me?"

"What?"

"The wire. Did it cost me?" Everything cost her. That was the arithmetic of owning a boat on credit: every message, every repair, every mouth was a notch carved into the same stick.

"It's marked paid," Piet said, and held it out. "Paid by the sender."

That woke something in her, low and cold. Nobody paid for wires to Silas Thornbury unless they wanted something, and nobody wanted anything from her except what she could carry.

She took the slip. The paper was thin as a moth's wing, the type punched hard enough to nearly tear through:

> HARROW EXPEDITION UPRIVER MISSED TWO SCHEDULED CHECK-INS STOP FUNDS REQUIRE VERIFICATION OF STATUS AND DELIVERY OF PROVISIONS LIST ATTACHED STOP YOU ARE RECOMMENDED BY HARROW HIMSELF STOP RESPOND CORVANE OFFICE MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED STOP TRAVEL EXPENSES GUARANTEED STOP

Silas read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting the words sit in her mouth like stones.

Harrow. Professor Edmund Harrow, who'd come down to Corvane last autumn with a beard full of river grass and a head full of mountains, buying charts off her at the dockside while his assistants loaded survey gear. He'd asked her questions no one asked: about the upper reaches above Kestrel Bend, where the river spread into drowned forest and shifting channels that ate propellers and drowned the confident. About whether the flood season ever opened a passage north. He'd been the only man in twenty years to look at her sandbar charts and understand them on first reading, and he'd said — laughing, slapping the rolled charts against his knee — *"Thornbury, you're wasted here. You're a compass pointing at a country nobody's mapped."*

She'd thought about that sentence for most of the winter, in the way you think about a splinter too deep to dig out.

Now Harrow was silent. Two check-ins missed. And Meridian Consolidated — she turned the name over and found nothing under it but money. Mining concerns, timber syndicates, men whose telegrams were always paid by the sender because their attention was worth more than the postage.

"Piet," she said. "Get the provisions list."

He handed over a second sheet, and she scanned it down: rice, salt pork, medical stores, cartridges, cold-weather canvas. Cold-weather canvas. In high summer. Whoever wrote that list expected the expedition to be somewhere the seasons didn't behave.

"They're offering good money," Piet said carefully. He was nineteen and wore his ambitions on him like wet clothes. "Guaranteed expenses. Captain, we could clear the Halloran bill in one run."

"We could," Silas agreed. "If we come back."

Piet went quiet. He knew the upper reaches the way children know a dog that has bitten them once — with respect at a distance. Every pilot on the lower Kestrel had a story about the drowned forest above Kestrel Bend, and every story ended the same way: *and they never found the boat.* The channels up there weren't channels; they were rumors. The water didn't flow so much as *hesitate*, pooling among drowned pines, sliding sideways when the wind pushed it, hiding bars that formed overnight and dissolved by noon. No charts. No soundings worth trusting. Nineteen years, and Silas herself had only pushed above the Bend three times, and each time had come back with her hull scarred and her pride dented and a new page of notes in her own hand, which was the only chart of those waters that existed.

Which was, she understood now, exactly why the wire had come to her. Not because she was cheap. Because she was the only one who would say yes.

She looked out over the water. The fog was gone now, burned away, and the river lay wide and glittering and deceptively honest, running south toward the sea with all its cargo of other people's fortunes. Downstream was safety. Downstream was the mortgage and the flour runs and the schoolbooks and Mrs. Okonkwo's cheese and another year exactly like the last one, laid end to end until they ran out of years.

Nineteen years of competence. That was the word people used — *competent*, said with respect, said with relief, said the way you'd praise a bridge. Reliable Silas Thornbury, who never lost cargo, never lost a passenger, never lost her temper on the wheel. And underneath the word, if anyone had cared to dig, which no one had: a hunger with no name. A feeling, on flat grey mornings like this one, that she was a key that fit a lock somewhere far upstream, and had spent her whole life turning slowly in the wrong door.

Edmund Harrow had seen it. *A compass pointing at a country nobody's mapped.*

"Captain?" Piet said.

Silas folded the telegram along its creases, once, twice, precise as a chart fold, and put it in her coat pocket over her heart, which was where she kept things she hadn't decided about yet.

"Turn her around," she said. "We'll take on water at Millbrook and wire Corvane from the station. Tell them the *Magpie* accepts."

Piet grinned and went for the lines, and she heard him already rehearsing how he'd tell it in the taverns — *and the captain says, turn her around* — and she let him have it. Let him have his story. She had hers, forming now in the back of her mind like weather coming down a valley: black water between drowned trees, a silence two check-ins deep, and cold-weather canvas bought in July.

She brought the *Magpie* about with the easy authority of long habit, and the boat leaned into the turn as if she'd been waiting all morning for exactly that order.

But as the landing fell astern and the river narrowed ahead, green walls rising on either side, Silas caught herself looking north — upriver, past the last of the traffic, past the last of everything mapped — and the hunger in her chest turned over like a fish rising, and she could not have said, even to herself, whether what she felt was dread or appetite.

The river, which told her everything, told her nothing at all.

It just carried her north.

* * *

They wired Corvane from the Millbrook fog station at dusk. The reply came back inside the hour, which told Silas everything about how much Meridian Consolidated wanted her moving:

> TERMS ACCEPTED STOP PROCEED WITH ALL DESPATCH STOP FURTHER INSTRUCTIONS AT KESTREL BEND AGENT STOP BE ADVISED PARTY MAY BE DISORIENTED RATHER THAN LOST STOP EXERCISE DISCRETION STOP

She read it by lamplight in the wheelhouse, Piet snoring in the deckhouse below, the *Magpie* tied snug against the pilings with the current combing past her hull all night like a hand smoothing hair.

*Disoriented rather than lost. Exercise discretion.*

Discretion. In the language of men who paid for telegrams, that meant: don't ask questions, don't talk to strangers, and don't be surprised by what you find.

Silas set the wire down on the chart table, next to her own hand-drawn map of the upper reaches — the only one in existence, ink fading at the folds — and looked at it for a long time. Somewhere up there, past the top edge of her own knowledge, Edmund Harrow was either disoriented or dead, and a company with money to burn wanted her to go find out which, quietly.

Outside, an owl called across the water, once, and the river answered with its endless patient whisper against the hull.

"Discretion," Silas said aloud to the empty wheelhouse, and almost laughed. "You've wired the wrong woman."

She banked the lamp, and lay in her bunk listening to the current, and did not sleep so much as wait for morning — the way she'd waited, without knowing it, for nineteen years.
