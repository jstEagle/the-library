## 2. Dead Air at Kestrel Bend

The Kestrel above the Bend was not a river so much as a rumor of one — a brown thread gone fat and drunken with snowmelt, wandering through a valley it had drowned years ago and never apologized for. Silas Thornbury stood at the Magpie's wheel with her boots planted wide on the wet planking and read the water the way other people read letters: greedily, line by line.

There. A boil of foam where no boil should be, a quarter mile down, just left of the drowned church spire that leaned out of the flood like a man too tired to stand. Snag under the surface, big as a hay wagon. She eased the wheel two spokes to starboard, felt the current take the hull's hip and shove, corrected, and threaded the gap between the spire and the roofline of what had once been somebody's granary, now nothing but a shingled suggestion beneath six feet of green water.

"Nobody runs this stretch," the harbormaster at Two Forks had told her, not quite hiding his pleasure at saying it. "Not since the flood year. Not for any money."

Silas had smiled her river smile — thin as a fish knife — and signed the manifest anyway. Money wasn't why she'd said yes, though God knew she needed it. The Magpie carried a lien like a millstone carries a rope, and three seasons of thin cargo runs hadn't loosened the knot. But when the telegram came through — HARROW EXPEDITION SILENT TWO CHECK-INS STOP SPONSORS REQUIRE INVESTIGATION AND RESUPPLY STOP GENEROUS TERMS — she had read it twice and felt something in her chest turn over like an engine catching.

Generous terms. Investigation. Upriver.

*Something more than cargo,* whispered the part of her she didn't listen to at night.

She listened to it now, in daylight, with both hands on the wheel.

* * *

By midafternoon the valley narrowed and the drowned country gave way to living forest, black spruce crowding down to the waterline on both banks, so dense the light came down green and underwater even though the sky was clean blue overhead. The river here ran fast and shallow-braided, splitting into channels that rearranged themselves every season like a card sharp's deck. This was the upper reach. This was where charts gave up and became drawings with the word *unsurveyed* written across them in apologetic italics.

Silas ran it by memory and nerve. Six years ago she'd taken a fur buyer's boat up this far, and the river had shown her its teeth: a gravel bar that appeared in an afternoon, a cutbank that calved into the current like a glacier calving, whole islands that migrated. The Kestrel up here did not forgive. It barely remembered you were on it.

She sounded the channel with the lead pole, calling depth to herself out loud because the silence needed breaking — "four feet, hard bottom, four and a half" — and worked the Magpie upstream in long patient slaloms, engine at half throttle, the old boat grumbling but game. The Magpie was forty years of patched oak and stubbornness, and Silas loved her the way you love anything that has kept you alive without ever once being kind about it.

Toward evening she smelled smoke before she saw anything else. Woodsmoke, cold and stale, hanging in the spruce like a ghost that hadn't gotten the news.

She brought the boat alongside a gravel shelf where the bank firmed up, tied off, and went ashore with the lantern and the axe she wore on her hip the way other women wore hats.

The camp sat fifty yards back from the water in a stand of birch. Three tents, or what remained of them — canvas stripped from the frames, folded, *gone*. Fire rings cold and raked flat. Latrine trench filled in and marked with crossed sticks, done properly, done by people who expected someone to come looking and wanted to leave a good impression.

Silas crouched at the largest fire ring and pressed her palm against the stones. Cold, but not winter-cold. Days cold, not weeks. She turned over a scrap of paper near the ring: the corner of a supply manifest, water-stained, listing tinned beef and lamp oil in a clerk's careful hand. Harrow Expedition stores.

No blood. No scattered gear. No sign of panic at all.

That was the thing that put the first cold finger on the back of her neck. Wrecks were messy. Disasters were messy. This camp had been tidied away like a dinner table after guests who meant to come back — except they'd taken their tents, their cook kit, everything portable, and walked off into country with no trail, no road, and no reason.

People didn't scatter deliberately unless they were running from something. Or toward it.

She stood there a long moment, lantern hissing, the birches ticking their branches together overhead, and felt the wilderness look back at her. It was a feeling she knew from years alone on the water — the sense of being *regarded* — but up here it had weight and attention, like something leaning closer.

Back aboard, she cooked rice and salt pork without tasting it and slept with the axe by the wheel instead of below.

* * *

The second day she found the mast.

It stood on a granite knoll above a wide pool where the river slowed to gather itself, a radio mast guyed with wire, sixty feet of galvanized steel that should have been humming with the expedition's scheduled check-ins. Instead it lay broken at the third section, snapped clean, the top half dragged off into the brush and dropped. Not storm damage — the break was too deliberate, the fall too controlled. Someone had climbed it or rigged it and brought it down with care, then hauled the wreckage away from the camp site so it wouldn't be found easily.

Silas beached the Magpie on the pool's sandy margin and climbed the knoll. The base of the mast was littered: packing crates pried open, straw padding strewn, and one crate still intact, stenciled HARRISON & VANCE SCIENTIFIC SUPPLY, LONDON. Inside, nested in straw, a field radio missing its key. The sending key — the one piece small enough and valuable enough to carry — was gone.

They hadn't been silenced. They'd silenced *themselves*.

She sat down on a crate and let that settle over her like river fog. An expedition that missed its check-ins was a tragedy. An expedition that broke its own radio, struck its own camps, and vanished into the high country leaving tidy graves of empty fire rings — that was a plan. And plans like that meant someone downriver was expected to come looking, someone who would find exactly this: signs of disaster, signs of death, a reason to turn around and go home.

Except whoever planned it hadn't counted on the only captain fool enough to run the flooded reach.

Or maybe they had counted on exactly that.

The thought arrived uninvited and stayed. Silas looked at the wrecked mast, at the missing key, at the tidy emptiness of it all, and understood that she might not be investigating anything. She might be *delivering* something — supplies, or herself, or whatever it was the sponsors actually wanted up here that a scientific expedition had stopped reporting on.

The sponsors' telegram had used the word *investigation*. It had also used the words *generous terms*, and had offered them without negotiation, which in Silas's experience meant either desperation or design, and often both.

She went back down to the boat and cast off without lingering. The pool watched her go. So, she realized an hour later, did something else.

* * *

It happened at the bend the maps called Kestrel Bend, though no map agreed on why. The river swung hard north against a bluff of gray limestone, and the current piled up along the outside of the curve, deep and fast, and Silas hugged the inside where the water slid smooth as poured glass. The bluff rose sheer on her right, crowned with spruce, and the late sun laid long shadows down the rock face so that the trees at the top stood black against a sky going copper.

She saw the flicker between one shadow and the next.

A man-shaped absence of light, thirty yards back from the rim, moving parallel to the boat. There and then not there. If she'd blinked at the wrong moment she'd have called it a bird, a branch swaying, her own tired eyes making weather out of nothing.

But she'd spent twenty years reading moving things on moving water, and her eyes didn't lie about motion. Something had walked the bluff top at the exact speed of the Magpie, keeping pace, keeping cover, and had stopped when the trees thickened.

Silas did not change course. She did not look up again. She rang the engine bell twice — her own private signal, meaningless to anyone else — and let the boat carry her around the bend at a captain's unhurried pace while every hair on her arms stood at attention.

Whoever it was had let her see. That was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, turning it over as the dusk came down and she tied up for the night in a cutbank willow screen, dousing her lantern early, cooking cold. A scout who didn't want to be seen was never seen. This one had stepped into a gap in the shadows at the precise moment her eyes were on that stretch of bluff. Either the finest luck in the territory or an invitation.

*You're being watched,* she told herself, lying flat on the deck boards with the blanket to her chin, listening to the river talk to itself in the dark. *And you're going on anyway.*

That was the part that frightened her, when she was honest — and she made a practice of honesty in the dark, since she could afford it nowhere else. Not the watcher. Watchers she could handle; she'd run contraband seasons through worse country than this. What frightened her was the small hot coal of gladness under the dread. Three days out from Two Forks and already the trip had teeth, already it meant something, already she was choosing danger over the safe gray grind of cargo runs and debt payments and eating supper alone to the sound of her own engine.

The wilderness leaned close around the Magpie, spruce and stone and starlight, patient as a creditor.

*Come on, then,* Silas thought at it, at the watcher, at whatever waited upriver where the expedition had erased itself. *I'm listening.*

Somewhere ahead, past the last of the navigable water, a woman named Vale — if the manifests were honest, and Silas was learning not to bet on that — had stopped answering questions. Tomorrow Silas would start asking some of her own.

She slept in shifts after that, hand on the axe, and dreamed of a compass needle swinging without finding north.
