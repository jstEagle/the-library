## 3. What Vale Left Behind

The base camp sat in a bowl of the valley like a thing that had died mid-scream.

Silas brought the *Magpie* in against the current with her teeth set and her hands doing what they always did — reading the water through the wheel, feeling for the lies beneath it. The river here ran green and glassy over drowned timber, deceptively calm, the kind of reach that swallowed propellers whole. She nosed the boat into a backwater below a gravel shelf, tied off to a spruce that leaned out over the bank like a man craning to look at something he wished he hadn't seen.

Then she stood on the foredeck and looked up at what remained of the Harrow Expedition.

The tents were ash. Not burned down — burned *out*, from the inside, by someone who had known how fire behaves in wet country and had fed it patiently until it took. The radio mast lay across the clearing like a felled tree, its guy-wires cut clean, its insulator caps smashed under boot heels. A crate of survey instruments stood open in the rain, its contents arranged with terrible neatness: theodolite, ranging rods, brass fittings, all of it soaked and rusting, none of it taken.

Nobody loots a camp and leaves the instruments. Nobody burns their own mast unless they don't want to be found — or don't want anyone listening while they go somewhere else.

"Scattered," Silas said aloud, to hear a human voice against the hiss of the rain. "They scattered."

She went up the bank with her rifle slung and her skin crawling. The wilderness here had a quality she'd felt only twice before in thirty years on the Kestrel — once above the falls in flood season, once in the winter she'd spent alone at Coldharbour Light after her father died. The feeling of being watched by something that was not an animal. Animals watched you because you might be food or danger. This was different. This was attention.

Boot prints in the mud, days old, half-drowned. At least six sets, moving away from camp in three directions. And one set — smaller, lighter, worn at the outside edge of the heel — that moved *toward* the treeline and never came back.

She followed it.

* * *

The journal was in a tin box under a cairn of river stones, forty yards into the trees, exactly where a person who wanted to be found by one specific stranger would leave it. The cairn was too deliberate to be natural and too fresh to be old. Beside it, scratched into birch bark with a knife point: **H.V.** — no, not H. The knife had slipped, been corrected. **I.V.**

Silas pried the tin open with her belt knife. Inside: a oilcloth-wrapped notebook, a stub of pencil, and a folded map so dense with annotation it looked like a moth's wing. She read the first page standing in the rain, and then she read the rest sitting under the spruce with her collar up, forgetting the cold entirely.

---

*14th of the Harvest Month.*

*Harrow is dead. I want to write that plainly so that some future reader does not mistake me for a woman given to drama. He is dead because he sent a telegram. Four words of position report, and within two days Reeve's men came up the valley with rifles and a bill of lading that listed our supplies as company property and our persons as, I quote, "assets under retention."*

*I have worked for institutions my whole life. Universities, societies, boards of trustees. I told myself they were clumsy rather than cruel. I am done telling myself things.*

*The compass does not point north. It points along the ridge to the pass the old maps call only the Seam — a crossing the colonial surveys never charted because the people who lived here did not draw maps for men who burn what they cannot own. When the needle settles, it settles toward that gap in the mountains, and it has not wavered once, not when I turn it, not when I hold it near iron, not when I beg it to be ordinary.*

*We voted last night. Six for scattering and silence. Two for giving them what they wanted. Harrow would have sided with the six if he had lived to vote. I think that is why he was made to stop living.*

---

*17th.*

*Ruth took the eastern route with the mule and most of the food, God keep her. Okafor and the brothers went south to raise alarm at the trading post. I stayed because someone must stay with the artifact, and because I am the one they will hunt least — a doctor, a woman, a footnote. Let them chase porters and guides. Let them waste a week learning I am not where a sensible thief would be.*

*I buried the compass in the cairn with my journal because I do not trust myself to carry it. That is a strange sentence to write. But I have watched what wanting does to faces these past weeks, including mine in the polished bottom of a pan. If I dig it up tomorrow and walk toward the pass alone, I will become exactly the kind of person this object makes of everyone. Better it wait in stones for someone whose hunger I can watch before I trust her.*

---

*21st.*

*Rain. Ate the last of the dried fish. A boat came up the gorge today — I saw her smoke against the cliffs, and then I saw her running the Narrows Reach in the dark, which no sane pilot attempts, which means either madness or Thornbury. The sponsors' advance team is camped at the bend below; I counted nine fires. If it is Thornbury, she will run straight into them unless she reads the water better than they read their orders.*

*I have decided to trust her. I have also decided to keep the pistol where she can see it. Both decisions can be true.*

---

Silas lowered the journal. Her heart was going hard, and not from the climb.

Thornbury. So the doctor knew her name before ever seeing her face — which meant Imogen Vale had chosen her the way a surgeon chooses an instrument. It should have angered her. Instead it settled over her like a coat that fit, and that frightened her more. Thirty years of trusting no one but the river, and here was a stranger's handwriting saying *I have decided to trust her*, and some starved part of Silas leaned into it like warmth.

She checked the pistol on her hip. Then she stood, and followed the small bootprints deeper into the trees.

* * *

The cave was behind a curtain of alder, high on the valley wall where the river noise covered everything. Silas smelled woodsmoke first, faint and old, then heard the click.

"Do not come closer."

The voice was wrecked — dry as paper, cracked at the edges — but steady. Silas stopped, hands loose and visible, rain dripping off her hat brim.

"Dr. Vale," she said. "Captain Thornbury. *Magpie*. Your note said—"

"I know what my note said." A pause. "Show me your hands again. Slowly."

Silas turned her palms to the grey light. From the dark of the cave, a woman emerged by inches, and Silas had to revise every picture she'd built on the journey upriver. She'd imagined a field scientist, weathered and competent. What stood there was a skeleton wearing a woman's face — cheekbones like shipwreck timbers, lips split, eyes enormous and burning in the hollows around them. She held a revolver in both hands, and both hands shook, and the muzzle stayed level anyway, which told Silas more about her than the journals had.

"You're half-dead," Silas said.

"I'm alive, which is the part they were trying to prevent." Vale's gaze went past her, down the valley, quick and practiced. "You came alone?"

"Me and a boat that hates me."

"The *Magpie*." Something flickered in the ruined face — not quite a smile. "I watched you take the Reach at night. I thought I was hallucinating. Starvation does that." The revolver dipped an inch. "Why did you come? The telegram asked for supplies. You brought yourself."

"The telegram asked questions nobody upstream was answering. I don't like unanswered questions on my river." Silas kept her voice even. "Your colleagues burned your own mast and walked into the bush in three directions, and your sponsors' men are camped at the bend below with nine cookfires and enough rifles to outfit a small war. So let's not pretend we're discussing cargo."

Vale laughed — a short, awful sound, more cough than laugh. "No. No, we're not." She stepped fully out of the cave, and now Silas saw what hung at her breast on a leather cord, tucked inside her shirt, and saw the shape of it pressing through the wet cloth. Round. Heavy. Old.

"Show me," Silas said.

"Why? So you can want it too?" Vale's eyes were terrible and clear. "Everyone wants it, Captain. Harrow wanted it. Reeve wants it. Half my colleagues wanted it by the end, and they were good people in June. It's a compass. It's a lump of bronze. And it points at a pass through these mountains that closes with the first snow and opens onto country no chartered map has ever carried — and the men who paid for this expedition intend to own that door, and everyone standing in front of it, before winter."

"I didn't ask what it does. I asked to see it."

A long moment. Rain on alder leaves. Somewhere downslope, distant, a sound that might have been thunder and might have been a rifle shot, and both women's heads turned toward it and turned back.

Vale drew the cord over her head.

The compass was smaller than Silas expected — a disc of green-bronze the size of a saucer, its casing worked with lines like watercourses, its glass clouded with age. Inside, the needle swung, hesitated, and settled. Not north. North was behind them, downriver, home. The needle pointed up-valley, into the white shoulders of the mountains, unwavering, patient as a dog at a door.

"It hasn't moved in three weeks," Vale said quietly. "Whatever it's pointing at, it isn't going anywhere. Unlike us." She wrapped the cord around her fist. "Reeve's men are between me and the pass. They're between you and downriver, if they've moved their picket since yesterday. I have a pistol, four rounds, and perhaps a week of strength left in this body. You have a boat and, judging by the Reach, more nerve than sense."

"That's the general report."

"Then here is my offer, Captain, and I'll make it only once, because I'm too tired to lie well." Vale stepped closer, and up close Silas could see the tremor in her that she was holding down through sheer will, like a man holding a hatch shut against a sea. "Take me upriver. Past their picket, to the last navigable pool. After that I walk and you're free of me — I'll ask nothing further. In exchange, when this is over, you get the truth. All of it. Including the parts that will make you wish you'd left me in that cave."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I start walking tonight and die somewhere interesting, and you go home and tell the magistrate a story about a burned camp." Vale's mouth twisted. "They'll believe you. Nobody believes anything about this valley."

Silas looked at the compass. The needle pointed at the mountains, at the closing door, at whatever waited beyond it — and she felt, distinctly, the pull of it in her own chest, the same hunger she'd sworn off years ago when she chose the river over every road that led away from it. She looked at the woman holding it instead. Half-starved. Armed. Honest the way a blade is honest.

"I don't trust you," Silas said.

"Good," said Imogen Vale. "Neither do I. We'll get along fine."

Down the valley, the sound came again — closer now, unmistakable. A rifle shot, then two more, spaced like signals. Like men talking to each other across the dark, saying *she's here, she's here, she's here.*

Silas was already turning downhill toward the river, toward the *Magpie*, toward the black water and the picket fires at the bend. "Can you run?" she said.

"I can run," said Vale, and followed her into the trees.
