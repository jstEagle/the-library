## 4. The Sponsors' Men

I knew they had come before I saw them — the way you know a storm is coming by the way the birds go quiet.

Imogen Vale crouched at the mouth of the burned camp, her fingers buried in cold ash, and listened to the valley change its mind about her. For six days the silence up here had been hers alone: the river's mutter, the wind combing the spruce, the small honest noises of a place that had forgotten men existed. Now there was something else underneath it. A rhythm too regular to be water. Boots, maybe. Or mules. Something with a schedule.

She rose slowly, the bronze compass heavy against her sternum where it hung on its cord beneath her coat. It did not point north. It never had. It pointed, with a patience that frightened her more than any gun could, toward the pass — the high notch in the western wall of the mountains that every map in Harrow's expedition had marked as impassable, and which this thing in her hands insisted was not only passable but *meant*, as though some pre-colonial hand had drawn a road through stone and then built an instrument to remember it.

She had stolen it. She was done pretending otherwise, even to herself. The word *recovered* belonged to other people's mouths.

The sound came again, clearer now, drifting down the valley's throat: the flat crack of a rifle being fired, once, deliberately — not at anything, but *for* something. A signal. And then voices, faint and human and wrong, the way any voice would be wrong here now.

They had found the wrecked mast at Kestrel Bend. They had read the scattered camps the way she'd meant them to misread. And they had come anyway, because Callum Reeve did not require permission from geography.

Imogen turned and ran for the river.

* * *

Silas Thornbury was waiting where she'd said she'd be, in the willow screen below the last navigable pool, and when Imogen crashed out of the brush the captain didn't startle — just looked up from the tiller with those grey eyes that seemed to hold the whole river's weather behind them, and said, "You're early."

"They're here." Imogen waded the last yards, boots filling with icy water, and hauled herself over the gunwale of the *Magpie*. "Downstream side of the valley. At least eight. Mules. They fired a signal shot."

"Reeve?"

"I didn't see him. But it's his shape of man." She wrung out her sleeve, hating how her hands wanted to shake. "He'll have pickets on the water. He knows we can't cross the pass without going downriver first — no, wait." She stopped. Corrected herself. "He knows *we know* the compass points to the pass. He doesn't need us to go downriver. He needs us to stop existing."

Silas considered this with the unhurried attention she gave everything, as if even death were a sandbar to be read. "Then we don't stop existing tonight," she said. "We run the Narrows Reach in the dark."

Imogen stared at her. "In the dark. Those rapids."

"You've seen them in daylight?"

"From above. From a ridge. They looked like the end of the world."

"They look better from inside," Silas said, and began unshipping the lamp glass so the flame wouldn't show. "Get below. Tie down everything you love."

* * *

Later — much later, when her knuckles had memorized every seam of the cabin bulkhead — Imogen would try to reconstruct those hours and find that memory refused to arrange itself into sequence. There were only fragments, each one lit like a struck match.

The *Magpie* sliding out of the pool with her engine muffled to a heartbeat, running black water under a sky the color of wet slate.

Silas at the wheel, bareheaded, hair whipping, reading the river not by sight but by some faculty Imogen couldn't name — the set of the current against rocks she could not possibly see, the smell of spray off a ledge, the *sound* of the channel changing its mind fifty feet ahead.

The first rapid taking them like a dog takes a rag. Water over the bow in a single green wall. Imogen thrown against the bulkhead, hearing timber somewhere aft complain in a voice like a dying man's.

And through all of it, Silas's face — lit once, briefly, by lightning that walked along the far ridge — wearing an expression Imogen had never seen on another living person. Not fear. Not thrill. Something older than both. Recognition, almost. As if the river were speaking a language Silas had been born knowing and had spent her whole life grateful to be fluent in.

They hit the picket's lantern light at the bottom of the second drop.

It happened fast. A glow through the trees on the left bank — a campfire where no campfire should be, and beside it, silhouetted, a man raising a rifle. Imogen saw him open his mouth to shout and heard nothing over the water, and then Silas did something with the wheel that should not have been possible, throwing the *Magpie* hard starboard into what looked like certain destruction, a rooster-tail of standing waves directly ahead —

— and the boat threaded it. Threaded a gap Imogen would have sworn was narrower than the hull, because Silas had known it was there the way you know your own doorway in the dark. The rifle cracked twice behind them, wild, the shots swallowed by the gorge. Then the river bent, the firelight vanished, and they were alone again in the roaring dark.

Only alone wasn't quite true. Something had gone wrong below decks. Imogen felt it before she understood it — a new note in the engine's voice, a shudder in the deck plates — and then Silas shouted down the companionway, "We're shipping water! Find me the leak!"

Imogen found it in the forward bilge: a seam opened where the hull had kissed a rock no one would ever name, a finger-thick wound pulsing river water into the boat with every wave. She stuffed it with her spare shirt, then her jacket, then held the wad in place with both arms while the *Magpie* limped the last miles downstream, and Silas brought her into a hidden slough — a backwater behind a willow island that no chart would show — and killed the engine, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

The boat sat low in the water. Not dangerously. But honestly. Like a creature that had been hurt and wasn't hiding it.

"She'll need caulking and a day out of the water," Silas said at last. Her voice was level, but Imogen had learned to hear the load-bearing timbers in it. "If I'd run that reach in daylight, clean, I'd not have touched. In the dark, past armed men—" She exhaled through her nose. "That's the price. That's always the price."

"You saved our lives."

"I nearly drowned them. Both are true. Get used to holding two truths; it's most of what this country offers." Silas looked at her then, really looked, and something in her expression shifted — softened by a degree, the way ice softens at exactly freezing. "You held the leak with your body for four miles. Most people would've been screaming."

"I've held worse things with my body," Imogen said, and touched the compass through her soaked coat, and watched Silas understand.

* * *

They dried out in the cabin by a stove fed with broken crate-wood, wrapped in blankets that smelled of engine oil and river, and it was there — warm for the first time in a week, alive in a way that felt borrowed — that Imogen finally told her everything. Not the version she'd rehearsed. The true one, or as close to it as she could bear.

"The sponsors sent Reeve because the expedition went quiet and quiet looks like theft. But that's not why he'll hunt us past reason. He has written orders. I saw the courier's satchel at base camp, before—" She stopped. Started again. "Before I burned my own trail. The orders say retrieve the artifact and the route survey *by any means*. Any means, Captain. That phrase has a grammar. It means the men with him can do whatever they decide not to report."

"And the pass?" Silas asked quietly. "Why does a compass matter enough to kill for? Routes get surveyed every year."

"Because this pass closes with the first snows and opens for no one after — but while it's open, it cuts three months off the crossing to the interior. Three months. Do you understand what that's worth? A road through the wall everyone else calls impossible. Whoever holds it holds the interior — trade, claims, all of it. And this—" she drew the compass out, let the stove-light move across the bronze, the needle swinging with slow certainty toward the northwest, toward stone and snow and a door no colonial map admitted existed — "this is the key that says the door is real. My sponsors want it. Someone else's sponsors want it. And I—" Her voice caught on the honesty coming up through her like floodwater. "I took it because I thought if it stayed in their hands it would become a weapon. And instead I've led it straight to the man with the guns."

Silas was silent a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the river kept its own counsel.

"So we're not delivering cargo," she said finally. "We're outrunning a claim."

"We're outrunning *men*, Silas. Men who've been told yes."

Something moved across the captain's face then — not fear. Imogen was beginning to learn her vocabulary, and fear lived elsewhere in it. This was anger, banked and structural, the kind that gets built into a house rather than lit as a fire.

"Then here's what I know," Silas said. "The Magpie needs a day I'm not sure we have. Reeve's party is provisioned for weeks and ours is provisioned for days. And between us and that pass lies forty miles of country where a woman with a rifle can watch every path." She fed the stove another piece of crate-wood and watched it catch. "So we take the high portage route. The mule track the trappers abandoned. It's slower, crueler, and invisible from the water — which is the only mercy on offer."

"And if he follows us onto the mountain?"

"Then he learns what winter teaches everyone eventually." Silas's eyes came up, grey and cold and absolutely clear. "But Imogen. Hear me. If it comes to choosing between that compass and you, I already know my answer. I'd just like you to know yours before the mountain asks."

Imogen held the instrument against her chest and felt its needle straining northward through her ribs, patient as gravity, and realized with a lurch that she could not answer — that she had never once, in all her careful double life, decided what *she* was willing to lose.

Far off, upstream, a rifle cracked again. Once. Deliberately. Closer than before.

Silas stood, and began, without hurry, to break down the stove.
