## 5. Portage

The last navigable pool lay at the mouth of a valley the maps called only *The Elbow*, a wide green eye of water where the Kestrel gathered itself before tearing north through country no boat could follow. Silas brought the *Magpie* in on the last of her headway, backing her down against the current with the delicacy of a woman setting down a sleeping child, and let the bow kiss the shingle at the pool's edge.

She stood at the wheel a long moment after the engine died. The silence was enormous. For eleven years the *Magpie*'s heart had beaten beneath her feet — the diesel's rough pulse, the tick of the cooling pipes, the groan of the hull in fast water. Now there was only the river moving past without her, and a mule named Bessie cropping grass on the bank as if she owned the place.

"We'll come back for her," Imogen said. She stood at the rail with her pack already shouldered, the compass wrapped in oilcloth and strapped inside it, and she said it the way people say things they need to believe.

"River'll keep her honest." Silas killed the lamp, ran her hand once along the wheel's worn spokes, and went down to tie off. She made the lines fast with more care than the situation required. Then she climbed the bank and did not look back, because looking back was a habit she had trained out of herself years ago, and she was not going to let a boat undo it.

They made six miles the first day, which Silas counted a triumph. The trail — if the deer track they followed deserved the word — climbed away from the river through timber so dense the light came down green and broken, like the bottom of the sea. Bessie picked her way over deadfall with the resigned patience of her kind, and Imogen walked behind her, and Silas walked in front, and for the first hours nobody said anything at all, because the country demanded all their breath.

It was Imogen who broke the silence, on the second morning, at a creek crossing gone loud with meltwater.

"Her name was Adhira," she said, without preamble. "Osei. The Kessels — Marta and her brother, they were inseparable, finished each other's survey notes. You'd have liked them, Silas. Marta hummed when she worked. Badly." She was watching the water instead of Silas, her voice level in the way of a woman who had rehearsed this crossing many times in the dark. "I keep setting places for them at meals that don't exist. I'll catch myself dividing rations five ways and then I remember there's no one left to divide for, and I—" She stopped. The creek roared. "I don't know what one does with that."

Silas let the quiet sit until it stopped hurting to hold. "You carry it," she said. "Same as anything else. It gets lighter. Not less. Lighter."

"Have you carried something?"

"Everyone on the river has." She held out a hand across the current and did not explain further, and Imogen took it, and they crossed.

* * *

The trail steepened. By the third day the valley walls had narrowed to a throat of granite and the timber thinned to scrub pine clinging to the scree, and the air took on the thin mineral edge of altitude. Silas talked more now — not because she had grown talkative, but because she had learned on the river that a voice in the darkness keeps the mind from wandering to bad places, and the nights up here were cold enough to make bad places easy to find.

"That sandbar below Kestrel Bend," she said, as they ate a supper of beans and hard bread, "moves a hundred yards some springs. Whole river rearranges itself like a drunk rearranging furniture. Old man Ferro ran that stretch forty years and swore he could feel the bar shift under the hull before his soundings told him. Called it *reading the water's mood*. Everyone thought he was touched." She turned her cup in her hands. "He was. But he was also right."

"And you? Do you feel it too?"

Silas considered the question with the seriousness she gave all questions about the river. "I feel it in my back teeth," she said. "Like a storm coming. The water tells you what it's going to do a half-second before it does it, if you're listening. Most men aren't. Most men are listening to themselves."

Imogen laughed — a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth, and then, finding that the sky did not fall, laughed again, properly this time, until she had to put her cup down. "Your back teeth. You pilot forty tons of other people's cargo by the state of your molars."

"Forty tons and their secrets," Silas said, and found, to her own surprise, that she was smiling. It felt strange on her face, like a boot worn on the wrong foot. It also felt good.

* * *

On the fifth day they crossed the tree line.

It came without ceremony — one stride among stunted pines, the next among bare rock and lichen and a silence vaster than the forest's. The pass showed itself to the north: a pale notch in the black ridge, still holding winter in its throat. Silas measured it with a look the way she measured rapids, and did not like the arithmetic.

"Four days," she said. "Five, if the weather turns."

"And if it turns badly?"

"Then it turns, and we climb anyway." She checked Bessie's pack straps, tightening a girth that didn't need it. "Snow up here doesn't fall, Doctor. It *arrives*. You'll be walking in sunlight and then you won't be walking in anything at all."

Imogen was quiet for a while, watching the notch. "You could still turn back," she said at last. "Take Bessie, take the boat money, go downriver and tell them you never found me. Reeve would believe it. He has no reason not to."

"He has every reason not to. I ran his picket at midnight and burned his picket boat's fuel cache doing it." Silas shouldered her pack. "Besides. I've delivered a lot of things up and down that river. Crates, rifles, men who didn't want to be found. Never once delivered anything that mattered." She started up the slope. "You matter. Don't make it strange."

Imogen stood a moment longer among the last of the pines, and something in her face — Silas caught it only in profile, and only for a moment — was not gratitude but a kind of pain, as if she had been handed a debt she did not know how to repay and could not confess. Then she followed.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, out of the wind, with Bessie picketed close and the small stove burning low. The stars up here were brutal in their clarity, a spill of cold fire from ridge to ridge, and neither woman slept much, and neither pretended to.

"I used to think I'd die on the river," Silas said, somewhere past midnight. "Not tragically. Just — eventually. The way a tool wears out. I'd made a kind of peace with it. A boat, a debt, a stretch of water, and then nothing." She fed a stick to the stove. "Then a telegram comes, and a doctor with a stolen compass and a face like a closed book, and now I'm climbing a mountain I can't read, for a woman I've known nine days." She glanced over. "I'm not complaining. I'm just noting the current changed."

"Is that what I am? A current?"

"You're a flood, Doctor Vale." Silas said it dryly, but her voice was lower than she intended. "You come through and rearrange the furniture."

Imogen reached across the space between them — not far, in that hollow, with the stove between their bedrolls — and took Silas's hand. Her fingers were cold and cracked from the scree and she held on like a woman holding a line in fast water.

"Adhira used to say the mountains don't forgive," Imogen said. "She was wrong. They don't even notice. It's people who forgive, when they bother. I don't expect it. But I wanted you to know I'd ask for it, if I were braver."

"Ask now."

"Not yet." Her grip tightened. "Soon. Before the pass. I promise you that much, Silas — whatever I am to you, I won't let you walk into the snow not knowing."

Silas looked at her a long moment — the firelight, the exhausted face, the compass-shaped bulge in the pack by her head — and did not ask the question rising in her, because the mountain would ask it soon enough, and mountains asked better.

"Sleep," she said instead. "We've got a notch to climb."

* * *

They rose before dawn into a sky the color of unpolished tin.

Silas was up first, as always, and stood at the mouth of the hollow reading the weather the way she read water — the pressure in her back teeth, the stillness of the air, the way the light lay flat and sourceless on the western peaks. It was the same feeling she'd had before the worst rapid of her life. The river's mood, translated into sky.

"Imogen," she said quietly. "We need to make the notch today. Whatever it costs."

But it was Imogen, scanning the trail below them with the field glasses, who went rigid first.

"Silas." Her voice had gone flat and careful. "There's a fire down in the timber line. Last night's. Still smoking."

Silas took the glasses. Far below, a thread of grey rose from the black-green sea of the forest — one fire, small, disciplined, banked by someone who knew how to travel light and move fast and leave nothing behind.

Reeve had made better time than the mountain. And he was no longer behind them.

He was below them, on the only trail, and the snow was already beginning to fall.
