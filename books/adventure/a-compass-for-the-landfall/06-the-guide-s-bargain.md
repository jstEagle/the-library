## 6. The Guide's Bargain

The rain came in off the glacier at dusk, a fine grey hiss that turned the world to wet wool, and Silas stood under the tarp with her hands cupped around a tin mug of tea gone bitter from over-steeping, watching Tomas check the mule's feet for the third time that evening.

He'd been checking them all day. Every halt, every rest, he was down on one knee in the mud, lifting the near hind hoof, running his thumb along the frog of it, murmuring to the animal in the low coaxing voice he used when he thought no one was listening. A careful man. A kind man, she'd have said a week ago. He carried the heaviest pack without complaint, cut firewood before anyone asked, and knew the high country the way she knew the river — by feel, by the lie of the land against the soles of his boots.

She had trusted him because of that. Because competence wore an honest face.

Now she watched him work and felt something cold settle behind her breastbone, the way fog settles on still water, and she could not have said why. Only that he hadn't looked at Imogen all day. Not once. And a man who avoided looking at something was a man keeping track of where it was.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder, a shallow overhang that kept off the worst of the rain, and ate half-rations of hardtack and smoked fish while the light died out of the valley below them. The pass showed itself in glimpses through the cloud — a white notch in the black spine of the range, impossibly far and impossibly close, the way mountains are.

"Two days," Tomas said, poking the fire flat so it wouldn't show. "Three if the weather holds like this."

"And Reeve?" Imogen asked. She sat with the compass wrapped in oilcloth in her lap, her arms around it the way a mother holds a child she means to give away but hasn't yet.

Tomas shrugged. "Behind us. Or ahead of us. In this country there's no telling." He smiled his easy smile. "But nobody crosses that pass after first snow, and the snow's a week out if it's a day. We're fine."

Silas said nothing. She banked the fire, spread her bedroll, and lay listening to the rain drum the tarp and the mule stamping softly and Imogen's breathing slow into sleep on her left, and Tomas — Tomas breathing too evenly on her right, the way a man breathes when he isn't asleep and wants to sound like he is.

She slept anyway. That was the mistake. You can't keep watch forever, and the body takes what it needs, and somewhere past midnight she went down into dreamless dark, and when she woke the sky was the color of dishwater and the space where Tomas had lain was empty except for a pressed-flat bedroll and the mule's picket line tied off neat as a church collection.

And the compass was gone.

* * *

She didn't wake Imogen at first. She stood in the rain with her hat dripping and read the ground the way she read water — not looking at things but at what things displaced. The mule's tracks led north along the slope, unhurried, a man leading an animal he expected to keep. Beside them, boot prints, deep at the toe. A man walking fast but not running. Running men leave a mess. This was a man with a destination.

There was a game trail cutting up toward a saddle between two ridges, and Silas knew it, because she'd marked it two days ago as their own route east. It would bring him down onto the old survey road on the far side, and the survey road ran south, and south was where Reeve would be.

Loyalty bought is loyalty sold. Her father had said that once, about a deckhand who'd jumped ship at Landfall with the pay chest. She'd been eleven. She'd thought it was just something old men said.

She woke Imogen with a hand on her shoulder, gentle, and watched the knowledge land in the woman's face like a stone dropped in still water — the widening rings of it, disbelief first, then hurt, then something harder underneath.

"He took it," Imogen said. Not a question.

"He took it."

"Why? He ate with us. He—" Imogen stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. When she took them away her face was composed again, the scholar's face, the mask she wore like Silas wore her captain's hat. "A pardon. Reeve will have offered him a pardon. Maybe passage home. Maybe money."

"Maybe all three." Silas shouldered her pack, checked the rifle's action out of habit, found it dry, swore softly, and wiped it. "Stay here or come. Your choice. But if you come, you walk quiet and you do exactly what I say."

Imogen was already lacing her boots.

* * *

They tracked him through four hours of cold rain, up the game trail and over the saddle, and the rain did half their work for them, filling his prints, yes, but also erasing theirs, and keeping him moving slow because a mule hates slick rock worse than it hates anything. Twice they saw him below them through the trees, a grey shape in the grey morning, and twice Silas held Imogen back in the cover of a spruce stand and let the distance open again.

She wasn't sure why she waited. Partly tactics — a man surprised does stupid things, and stupid things near a cliff edge get people killed, and she needed Tomas alive enough to talk. But partly, if she was honest, and the rain makes a person honest, she wanted to watch him a while longer. Wanted to catch some sign that she'd been wrong. That the trail would bend back toward them, that he'd remembered something, that there was a version of this where the man who'd shared their fire hadn't sold them for a boat ticket.

The trail never bent back.

Near midday they came down onto the survey road and found where he'd met them: fresh hoofprints of horses among the mule tracks, six or eight riders, cigarette ends ground into the mud, the trampled sign of men who'd been waiting. Waiting. They'd known where he'd come down. Which meant he'd arranged it days ago, which meant the checking of the mule's feet and the flat jokes and the extra firewood had all been performance, every hour of it, laid over the transaction like paint over rot.

"There," Imogen whispered, and pointed.

Below the road, in a fold of ground near a stream, the riders were mounting up. And a man on foot was standing apart from them, holding the lead rope of a horse someone had brought him, and even at three hundred yards Silas could see the set of his shoulders — the particular hunch of a man who has done a thing and is already beginning to discover its weight.

The compass was in a saddlebag on the nearest rider's horse. She could see the shape of it through the wet canvas, square-cornered, unmistakable.

"Reeve?" Imogen breathed.

"No. Advance team. Reeve doesn't waste himself on pickups." Silas counted riders again. Seven. Rifles slung, rain hoods up, sloppy — men who believed the job was done. "They'll ride south to meet Reeve on the road. There's a bridge over the Kestrel fork, maybe six miles down. Timber trestle, single span."

"You know it?"

"I know every crossing on this river system better than any living soul." She felt the old certainty rise in her, the river-sense, cold and clean. "Six miles of road, and they'll push the pace because the weather's turning. Horses on wet trestle timber, single file, no room to turn around." She looked at Imogen. "I don't need to beat seven men. I need to beat seven horses on a narrow bridge in the rain."

"That's insane."

"It's Tuesday," Silas said, and started downhill.

* * *

The bridge was older than the survey, a logging-era trestle gone silver-grey, its planks furred with moss, the fork of the Kestrel running brown and fast beneath it with the rain in its veins. Silas reached it ahead of the riders by cutting the streambed, which no horseman would use, and she crouched in the alder scrub at the north end with rain running off her hat brim and listened to them coming — hooves on wet road, jingle of tack, a man laughing about something.

Seven riders. The compass horse was fourth in line.

She'd told herself on the way down that she'd decide when she got there. Decide what, exactly, she hadn't let herself finish thinking. Whether she was a woman who'd shoot at men over an object, or a woman who'd let the object go and take Imogen south another way and be done with the whole cursed business. Both versions of herself stood in the alder scrub, and both had arguments, and the rain fell on both equally.

Then the lead rider put his boots to his horse and started across the trestle, and the timbers spoke under him — a groan, a shudder, the long complaint of old wood carrying more than it liked — and Silas heard, in that groan, the river's own voice, and the decision made itself the way water finds the low ground.

She shot the lead horse. Not the man — the horse, low in the shoulder, a mercy shot at that range, and the animal went down on the second span with a scream that scattered the rest of them backward off the bridge in a clatter of hooves and shouting. The fourth horse reared, screaming too, and the saddlebag tore loose on the pivot and dropped, and Silas was already moving, out of the scrub and onto the span with the wounded horse thrashing between her and the riders, and she got her hand on the bag's strap and hauled, and the canvas ripped and the bronze compass rolled free against her boot and she snatched it up and turned and ran.

A rifle cracked. The plank beside her foot jumped into splinters. She didn't stop. She went off the north end at a dead sprint and into the alders and down the streambed with the compass jammed inside her coat against her heart, and behind her the shouting faded into the rain, and no pursuit followed, because seven men with a dead horse and a lame bridge do not chase a woman into floodwater country they don't understand.

* * *

It cost them two days. Of course it did. The streambed route added miles; the detour around the advance team's camp added more; and they came back to their high camp on the second evening to find the mule gone — Tomas had taken it, naturally, the practical thief — along with most of the food, because he'd packed for a new life and hadn't been choosy whose life he packed.

They had hardtack enough for four days. Maybe five, hungry. The pass was three days' walk in fair weather, and the weather was not fair, and behind them on the southern road Reeve was meeting his advance team and hearing the story of the bridge, and a man like Callum Reeve would hear in that story exactly one thing: that the compass was still in play, and that the woman who held it was willing to kill for it.

Which meant he would stop being careful.

Silas sat under the tarp that night with the compass unwrapped in her lap, bronze and strange, its needle swinging lazy circles that had nothing to do with north, and Imogen sat close against her side for the warmth, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

"I liked him," Imogen said finally. "That's the worst part. I liked him, and I don't know anymore whether I liked him or the version of him he built for us."

"The version was real while it lasted," Silas said. "That's the trick of it. A bought man gives you true coin right up until the moment he spends you."

"And now we can't trust anyone we hire. Anyone we meet. Anyone—"

"Anyone," Silas agreed. She turned the compass over in her hands. Its casing was warm from her body, absurdly, as if the thing had opinions. "Including me. You should know that. I've spent my whole life being paid to carry things for other people and not asking what they were. If the price gets high enough, I don't know what I'd do either. Neither do you. Neither does anyone, until the moment comes."

Imogen was quiet a while. Then she reached over and closed Silas's fingers around the compass, both her hands over Silas's, and her palms were freezing and her grip was certain.

"Then we hold each other to the moment," she said. "Not to promises. Promises are just prices written down."

Above them, through a tear in the cloud, the notch of the pass caught the last grey light — and on its flank, thin and white and wrong for the season, the first snow of the year had already begun to fall.
