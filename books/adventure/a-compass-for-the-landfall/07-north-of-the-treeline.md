## 7. North of the Treeline

The trees gave up on us at noon, and I understood why.

They stopped all at once — not thinning, not dwindling, but surrendering in a single ragged line, as if some authority had drawn a border across the mountain and the forest had obeyed. Beyond it there was only stone and sky and a light that came down sideways through the storm-clouds, white and sourceless, with no shadows to soften it. Above the treeline you could hide from nothing. Not the wind. Not the cold. Not yourself.

I had been walking for six days on legs that no longer belonged to me, breathing air so thin that every step felt like a small theft, and I was carrying a lie that had grown heavier than my pack.

Silas walked ahead of me. She always did now — she'd taken point somewhere past the burned camp without discussing it, the way captains take the helm, and I had let her because watching her read the ground was like watching someone read water. She moved through scree fields the way she moved through rapids: testing, committing, never hesitating long enough for doubt to take root. The mule plodded behind her with our shrinking world on its back. And I followed, as I had followed the compass needle for two years, telling myself that following was not the same as belonging.

The storm-light made everything honest. That was the trouble with altitude. Down in the valleys a person could carry their secrets in the green shade, tucked under fern and shadow. Up here the light found every crack. It found the frostbite blooming on Silas's left ear. It found the hollows the hunger had carved into both of us since Tomas took our food and our trust and left us two days behind schedule with half a sack of oats. And it found me — Imogen Vale, doctor of antiquities, keeper of a bronze instrument that didn't point north — standing in a landscape stripped bare of everything except what I actually was.

"Rest here." Silas set her pack against a boulder split by frost, a black seam running through it like a healed wound. "We make the col before dark or we don't make it."

"We'll make it." My voice came out thinner than I intended.

She looked at me then — that long, level look she used on sandbars and on people, the one that measured depth before trusting it with weight. Wind pulled at her grey hair where it had escaped its knot. Her hands, wrapped in fingerless gloves gone stiff with ice, were steady.

"You've been composing something for three days," she said. "I can hear it. You talk in your sleep, Doctor, and you argue with whoever's listening."

My chest went tight around the thin air. "What do I say?"

"Nothing useful. Names mostly. A man called Aldous. And *not yet*, over and over, like a prayer said backwards."

I sat down on the stone because my knees decided it for me. Below us the valley we'd climbed out of lay drowned in cloud, the river invisible, the whole green world we'd come from erased — as if it had never existed, as if everything that happened below this line were a story someone else had told about a woman who no longer lived here. The compass rode against my breastbone inside its oilcloth wrapping, and I could feel its weight the way you feel a debt.

"Aldous Wren," I said.

Silas waited. She was good at waiting. It was, I think, the first thing I'd loved in her — back when love was still a word I permitted myself only in the privacy of my own skull — that she never pried a confession open like a crate. She just stood beside it until you handed it over yourself.

"He runs the Antiquarian Trust," I said. "In Meridian. The people who funded the Harrow Expedition — the Harrow Consortium — they're not scholars. They're brokers. They wanted the pass mapped so they could control it, and they wanted anything pre-colonial catalogued, valued, and shipped home to sit in a vault. I told myself I was going upriver to keep the artifacts honest. To document them properly before the brokers got there."

"And?"

"And I was lying." The words came out of me like splinters, one at a time, each one leaving a hole. "Wren planted me. Two years ago. He recruited me out of the university, paid my debts, trained me in field method, and put me on that expedition as his eyes. My job was to find whatever the mountains held and signal him. There was a cipher. Radio codes. I was supposed to transmit the moment I found something significant."

The wind came across the plateau in a long white gust and I pulled my collar up and kept talking, because if I stopped I would never start again.

"The compass was buried in a shrine above the lake. Bronze, older than any map of these ranges, and the needle doesn't point north — it points along an old trade road, a pass the colonials never found, a route through the mountains that closes with the snows. I found it. And instead of transmitting, I burned the codebook. I burned the base camp and scattered the expedition and went silent, because I thought — I *told* myself — that silence would protect it. That if nobody knew where it was, nobody could take it." I laughed, and the laugh cracked in the cold. "But that's not what happened, is it? What happened is I stole it. From them. For myself. I've been running with stolen goods for eleven days and letting you believe you were rescuing a scholar."

Silas hadn't moved. The storm-light lay on her face without mercy, and I couldn't read it, and the not-reading was worse than anger.

"Reeve's sponsors," she said at last. "The ones who want it by any means. Are they Wren's people?"

"No. Worse. Wren's rivals. Another faction of the same beast. When I went silent, Wren assumed I'd been turned or robbed, and he sold the location of the expedition to men who'd pay more to retrieve it than he'd pay to bury it. Reeve isn't working for the Trust. He's working for the people the Trust fears." I looked at my own hands, cracked and filthy, a stranger's hands. "Every terrible thing chasing us is chasing something I took. Not something I saved. Something I *took*."

The mule shifted and stamped. Somewhere below, a ptarmigan startled out of the rocks with a sound like tearing cloth. Silas crouched down slowly, forearms on her knees, and studied me the way she'd study a stretch of water she meant to run.

"Say the rest," she said.

"There isn't a rest."

"There's always a rest. There's the part where you tell me why you didn't tell me at Kestrel Bend, when you were half-starved and I had a boat and a choice. There's the part where you explain why a woman who stole a compass to free it has been steering us toward a pass instead of away from everyone who wants it." Her voice wasn't cruel. It was worse — it was careful, the voice of a captain reading a bar that might be shoal or might be safe passage. "You're a guide too, Imogen. So tell me what you're guiding me toward, and what it pays."

The question went through me like cold water through a hull. Because she was right, and I had known she was right since the night we portaged, since the laughter around the fire that I had treasured precisely because it was built on ground I knew would give way.

"I don't know anymore," I said, and it was the truest thing I'd said in two years. "At first — yes. The pass. I needed the pass because the compass needs the pass; the needle follows the old road and I thought — I told myself the artifact should go home the way it came, over the mountains, out of reach. But somewhere on the river I stopped navigating and started just — being with you. And I kept not telling you because every day I didn't tell you, the truth got more expensive, and I kept hoping we'd cross the pass before the bill came due." My throat closed. "You asked once if I trusted anyone. I said no. That wasn't modesty, Silas. That was a confession. I am not trustworthy. I have been bought once already, and the man who bought me is still out there thinking I'm his."

Silas was quiet for a long time. The wind filled it. Above us the col stood against the sky, a notch of darker grey in the grey, and beyond it — though neither of us said it — the descent gorges, the narrows, the country where Reeve's party was climbing toward us even now, better fed, better armed, and angry.

When she finally spoke, she didn't look at me. She looked at the horizon, at the wall of weather building in the north like a second mountain range.

"My father sold cargo twice," she said. "Took payment from two houses for the same shipment, ran it past both of them, and vanished upriver for a season. When he came back he told me: *a lie keeps only as long as the current does*. I hated him for it. Then I spent thirty years being the most honest captain on the Kestrel because I was so afraid of becoming him." Now she turned, and her eyes were the colour of deep water, and I could not look away. "And then I find the one person on this river I'd break that rule for, and she's been lying to me since the day I pulled her out of the ashes."

"Silas—"

"I'm not finished." She held up one gloved hand. "Here's what I know. You burned your own expedition rather than hand that thing to either pack of wolves. You starved eleven days rather than trade it. You could have given it to Reeve's men at any point and bought yourself warmth and food and forgiveness, and you didn't. Whatever you are, Imogen Vale, you're not for sale anymore. Maybe you never were. Maybe you were just for hire, and you fired yourself." She rose, stiffly, joints cracking in the cold, and shouldered her pack. "So here's my price. No more guides. No more factions. From this ridge forward, you tell me everything the moment you know it — including, especially, the things that make you look bad. And when we get down off this mountain, that compass goes somewhere it can't be owned. Not by Wren. Not by Reeve's masters. Not by you. Not by me. Can you live with that?"

"Yes," I said. And then, because the storm-light allowed nothing less: "It's the only thing I've ever wanted to live with."

Something moved through her face then — not forgiveness exactly, forgiveness implies a debt cancelled, and I think she understood that mine would take longer — but a decision, made the way she made decisions on the water: fully, without looking back. She reached down and pulled me to my feet, and her grip stayed on my arm a moment longer than balance required.

"Then walk," she said. "Storm's coming down the north face, and we've got a col to cross before it eats the trail."

We climbed. The air thinned until each breath was a negotiation. And near the top of the col, where the old road's paving stones still showed through the frost-shattered rock — stones worn concave by feet centuries dead, the compass needle straining against its housing toward them like a dog toward home — Silas stopped so suddenly I walked into her.

She was looking north, past the col, down the far side.

Through a tear in the clouds I saw it too. Fires. Small, disciplined, evenly spaced, descending the switchbacks of the far gorge in a line as neat as beads on a string.

"They're ahead of us," Silas said quietly. "Somehow, they're already ahead of us."

* * *

The wind changed while we stood there, swinging north with a smell in it like iron and old snow, and I watched Silas read the sky the way other people read letters. Her jaw tightened.

"That's not a squall," she said. "That's the season turning. First snows, early." She glanced at me, and there was no blame in it, only arithmetic. "Your pass closes in three days, Doctor. And between us and it stands Callum Reeve."
