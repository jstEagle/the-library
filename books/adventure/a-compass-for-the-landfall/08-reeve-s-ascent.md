## 8. Reeve's Ascent

The mule died on the third morning above the treeline, and Silas took it as a message from the country itself: *nothing up here is meant to carry a burden.*

They'd named the animal Biscuit, which had been Imogen's doing and which made it worse. She knelt in the scree beside the creature while Silas unloaded the panniers, her breath coming in white rags, one gloved hand flat against its shivering neck.

"It was old," Silas said. "It was old when we bought it."

"I know." Imogen didn't move for a long moment. Then she stood, wiped her face with the back of her wrist before the tears could freeze there, and shouldered her pack without another word. That was the thing about grief at altitude — there wasn't enough air to sob with. You carried it or you left it behind, and Imogen Vale had never once left anything behind that mattered to her.

Silas looked back down the valley they'd climbed out of, the way they'd come, and read it the way she read water. The cloud was wrong. It sat too low on the southern peaks and moved too fast beneath them, like smoke under a door. Somewhere below, in that grey murk, Callum Reeve was climbing with thirty men who ate better than they did, slept warmer than they did, and wanted what Imogen carried more than they wanted their own breath.

"They'll close the gap by tomorrow," she said.

Imogen followed her gaze. "Then we don't give them a tomorrow."

* * *

The glacier field began where the last stunted pine gave up, a vast white shoulder of ice slumping between two black ridgelines, crevassed like old porcelain. In clear weather it would have been a road. In the weather that was coming, it would be a grave.

Silas crouched at its edge and did what she always did when the river frightened other people: she stopped being afraid and started being interested. The ice had a grain to it, a set of seams running down-slope the way sandbars ran down a channel. There were safe lines through it. There were even, if you squinted, rapids — places where meltwater roared under the surface and the whole field groaned like a hull in a storm.

"You're smiling," Imogen said. "Why are you smiling?"

"Because it's just a river that forgot how to move."

"That is not comforting."

"No," Silas agreed, standing. "But it's true, and true things are all we've got."

They crossed by daylight because they had no choice, roped together with the mule's lead line, Silas in front reading the ice the way she read foam and current. Twice she called a halt and walked a wide arc around ground that looked perfectly solid, and twice, probing with the ice axe afterward, she found the blue-lipped mouths of crevasses hidden under snow bridges thin as communion wafers. By late afternoon they stood on the far side, on bare rock, and Silas let herself believe — the way she let herself believe every evening of her life, knowing better — that they'd bought enough distance.

That night the wind changed.

She woke with it in her teeth, salt-and-iron, the smell of a storm still hours off but already decided. Above them the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the light was wrong — flat, sourceless, the light of a world with no edges. A whiteout building. She'd seen them on the coast; she knew what they did to men who kept moving.

"We camp here," she said. "We sit it out."

"And Reeve?"

"Reeve sits it out too, or he loses men into holes in the ice."

Imogen looked north, toward the pass, then back at her. "You're sure?"

No. But she said yes, because a captain says yes, and they made camp in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, and the storm came down on them like a held breath finally released.

* * *

The compass was gone before dawn.

Silas knew it the way she knew a stolen boat — some absence in the rhythm of things, a wrongness in the weight of the camp. She rolled over and Imogen's pack lay open, its flap thrown back, and the oilcloth wrapping that had ridden against Imogen's spine for nine days was simply not there.

"Imogen." Her voice came out flat and cold. "Wake up."

Imogen woke. Saw. And Silas watched something cross her face that she had no name for yet — not guilt exactly, and not surprise, but the look of a woman finding a bill come due.

"There were tracks," Imogen said. "Small ones. I heard nothing."

"Tomas wears moccasins over his boots. I taught him that." The betrayal had a familiar taste, which was the worst part. "He circled wide in the storm. He's been following our camp-smoke since the treeline."

"He can't have gone far in this."

"He doesn't need to go far. He needs to go down. Reeve will be waiting at the glacier's foot, because Tomas told him where we'd come out." Silas was already dressing, hands moving without instruction from the part of her that was screaming. "Which means we've been sitting in a trap with a warm fire."

They broke camp blind, into the whiteout, because staying was death and moving was only probably death. Silas navigated by counting steps and feeling the fall of the ground through her boots, by the way wind sang off ice versus rock, by everything a river teaches you about going forward when you cannot see. They reached the glacier's edge as the light failed and found the tracks they expected: a broad churn of boot-prints, many men, freshly arrived, and cutting away southward, small neat moccasin prints with a mule-driver's rolling gait.

Tomas had left them a gift, though he couldn't have known it. His prints led along the glacier's margin, skirting the worst of the crevasse fields — the safe line, the line only someone who'd crossed with them would know. Which meant Reeve's party hadn't taken it. Reeve's party had come straight up the middle, confident in numbers and provisions, and the storm had caught them on the ice.

Silas stood at the boundary between rock and white and listened. Under the wind, faint, there was shouting. Men's voices, ragged with fear, and once — unmistakable — the crack of a snow bridge letting go, followed by silence and more shouting.

"Callum Reeve is losing his expedition to the same river I read," she murmured.

"Then we go around, south, and we're free."

And that was the moment the whiteout opened.

It happened the way it happens on water — a rift in the fog, ten seconds of terrible clarity — and through it Silas saw them: six figures strung along a rope maybe three hundred yards out on the ice, hauling a sledge, one man down and being dragged, and at the head of the line a big shape in a grey coat turning toward the gap in the fog as if he'd felt her eyes.

Reeve raised one arm. Pointed. Not at the pass. At them.

The rift closed. But it was done.

* * *

They ran. There was nowhere to run to — the glacier's margin was cliff on one side and crevasse on the other, and the storm had eaten the world down to a circle twenty feet across — but they ran anyway, because running is what the body does when the mind has run out of answers. Silas counted steps. Lost count. Found it again. Behind them, muffled by wind, came the sound of men following the rope-line ashore, spreading out, closing like a net.

The ground dropped away without warning.

Silas went down hard on scree, rolled, came up with the ice axe in her hand and found herself in a shallow bowl of rock with grey shapes pouring over the rim on three sides. Four of them. Five. Rifles slung, which was almost funny — nobody shoots anyone in a whiteout, you just take what you want — and then Callum Reeve himself, filling the sixth of the world, snow crusted in his beard, breathing like a bellows, eyes bright with the particular joy of a man whose quarry has stopped running.

"Captain Thornbury." He said it like a toast.

She swung the axe. It was a good swing; it would have opened him hip to ribs. One of his men caught her arm and the axe went spinning off into the white, and after that it was knees and fists and someone's boot, and then her arms were pinned and Imogen was screaming her name somewhere very close and very far away, and Reeve crouched in front of her, unhurried, and took her chin in one mittened hand like a man inspecting livestock.

"The doctor," he said. "Where is she?"

"Gone," Silas said, and spat blood into the snow between them.

Something flickered in his face — not doubt. Calculation. He stood, turned, and shouted into the storm, and his men fanned out, and Silas understood with a cold drop in her stomach that Imogen had doubled back at the first shout, gone to ground somewhere in the rocks, and that the whiteout that had doomed Silas was now hiding the woman she —

She didn't finish the thought. She filed it, the way she filed everything, in the place where captains keep the things they'll feel later if they live.

Reeve's men dragged the sledge into the bowl. On it, lashed under canvas: crates, a stove, coils of rope, and — her chest tightened — the oilcloth bundle, plucked from Tomas or taken from him, she'd never know which. Reeve unwrapped it right there in the wind, careless as a man opening his lunch, and held up the bronze compass, and its needle swung lazily, pointing at nothing, pointing at everything, and his men crowded around it like sailors around a lucky coin.

"She'll want this," one of them said, meaning Imogen, and Reeve said nothing, which told Silas everything about what *wanting* meant in Callum Reeve's grammar.

Then he crouched again, level with her, and spoke quietly, just for her.

"Here's your position, Captain. My guide is dead — fell down a hole in the world an hour ago. My map ends at this glacier. Beyond the pass is a descent gorge my sponsors' maps show as a dotted line and a question mark, and the snows are four days out, maybe three." He turned the compass over in his big hands. "You are the only person alive who reads country the way you do. So you're going to get my men down the far side of these mountains, and when we reach the river you're going to pilot us down it, and if you do both those things well, I may let Dr. Vale live when we find her. Because we will find her. Storms end."

Silas looked at him. Looked past him, at the wall of white where Imogen had vanished, and let despair come and sit with her for exactly as long as it takes to read a rapid — one glance, one breath, one decision.

Because here was the thing Reeve couldn't know. Here was the thing no landsman could know.

She'd felt the wind shift an hour before the storm broke, and she'd felt it again just now, and it had shifted again — backing, strengthening, carrying the dry iron smell of the high cold. This wasn't a storm passing over. It was winter arriving, early and all at once, the front that closes the pass and buries the gorges and turns the far descent into something no map has a dotted line for.

Three days, she'd have said yesterday. Looking at the sky's dead pewter light and tasting that wind, she amended it.

One day. Maybe less.

"Get me up," she said.

Reeve smiled, thinking he'd won. He untied her arms himself.

Above them, unseen, unheard, the snow began to fall in earnest — fat, silent, endless — and somewhere out in that drowning white, Imogen Vale was watching, and waiting, and alive.
