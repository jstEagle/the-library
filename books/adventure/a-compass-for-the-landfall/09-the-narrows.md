## 9. The Narrows

**Silas**

Reeve's men moved like a funeral procession that hadn't been told it was one. Nine of them left, counting the guide they'd taken off the glacier with frostbitten fingers and a rifle he no longer knew how to hold. They had the compass in an oilskin wrap inside Reeve's own pack, and they had me at the front of the line because Callum Reeve was not a stupid man, and stupid men die in gorges.

"You walk where I tell you," I said, for the fourth time since dawn. "You stop when I stop. You don't speak above a whisper under overhangs. And if any of you so much as looks at the slope on your right without my saying so, I sit down in the snow and you can carry me the rest of the way."

Reeve smiled his thin smile. He had a way of smiling that made you understand the smile cost him something. "You're enjoying this too much, Captain."

"I'm enjoying nothing." That much was true. My hands were still bound in front of me with a length of climbing line — loose enough to work, tight enough to remind me — and Imogen was somewhere behind us in the whiteout, or ahead of us, or dead in a crevasse, and every hour I spent leading these men down toward the descent gorges was an hour she spent alone in weather that killed people who knew what they were doing. I tried not to do that arithmetic. It came out wrong every time.

The pass began to fall away by midmorning. The wind dropped into the gorge like water down a drain, and suddenly we were walking in silence, the storm muffled above us, the rock walls rising sheer and gray-green on either side. This was country I knew only from maps and from Imogen's voice describing it across a fire — the descent gorges, three of them, stair-stepping down toward the treeline and the river country below. The maps called the last one the Narrows, which was mapmaker's humor: a slot barely wide enough for the meltwater stream, walled in stone that the freeze-thaw had been chewing at for ten thousand years.

I read terrain the way other people read faces. And what I read, as we dropped into the first gorge, was this: the whole eastern wall was rotten. Old slide scars striped it like old scars on a workingman's hands. The talus at its base lay in fresh fans, sharp-edged, unweathered. Somewhere up there, held together by ice and habit, was enough stone to close this passage until spring.

I didn't let my eyes linger. I gave them the western wall instead, the stable side, and made them climb down its ledges while I pointed out handholds, and all the while some cold quiet part of me was measuring the eastern slope the way I'd measure a sandbar before running it — depth, current, load-bearing capacity.

"Captain." Reeve fell in beside me on a ledge barely two men wide. Below us the stream ran white over black stone. "You've gone somewhere. I'd like to know where."

"Nowhere you can follow," I said.

He laughed — genuinely, briefly — and I hated him a little less, which frightened me more than the drop did.

* * *

**Imogen**

The whiteout did not care about my plan. That was the first lesson of the day, learned within twenty minutes of doubling back, when the world erased itself so completely that I navigated by the slope of the ground under my boots and the direction of the wind against my left cheek and nothing else. No compass. The compass rode in Callum Reeve's pack, and Silas rode in Reeve's custody, and I had a stolen knife, half a bar of chocolate gone soft as tallow, and a fury that burned hotter than any fire I'd ever built.

They think I'm dead, I told myself. Or lost. Either way, they aren't looking up-slope.

I climbed. Not along their route but above it, traversing the shoulder of the mountain where the storm thinned to blowing spindrift, until I could look down into the first gorge and see them — nine dark figures stitched along the western wall, moving with the careful, hating slowness of men who trusted neither the mountain nor their prisoner. I found Silas by her walk. Everyone else picked their footing. She owned hers, even bound, even guarded, even descending into a trap of her own design — because I saw her look east. Once. Twice. A captain checking her soundings.

She's going to bring the wall down.

I understood it the way you understand a sentence in a language you thought you'd forgotten. She wasn't leading them to safety. She was leading them to the Narrows, and at the Narrows she meant to bury the pass — the compass, the route, the whole rotten treasure of it — under a hundred tons of mountain, and I knew, with a certainty that arrived like nausea, that she did not mean to be standing clear when it went.

No. Absolutely not. I have lost an expedition, a career, a version of myself I can never get back. I am not losing her.

So I did the only thing available to a woman with a knife and a grudge and no rope: I began to run the traverse, cutting above and ahead of them, racing the party down-gorge through snow to my thighs, falling twice, getting up twice, telling my body stories it didn't believe. If I couldn't stop the rockslide, I could reach the narrows mouth first. I could be standing there when she made her choice — and make her make a different one.

* * *

**Silas**

By late afternoon we reached the third gorge, and the sky closed like a book. The storm came back with intent. Wind funneled down the slot hard enough to stagger the big man behind me, and the stream below had risen to a brown roar, and I stood at the head of the Narrows with the eastern wall looming over me like a debt come due.

There was a shelf. I'd seen it from above an hour earlier — a ledge of fractured rock jutting from the eastern face, maybe forty feet up, holding a wedge of broken stone the size of a chapel. All day the freeze had been working at the seam beneath it. All day I'd been choosing our route to pass directly beneath it, at the narrowest point, where the walls pinched and the only footing was the wet shelf beside the stream.

"Rest here," I said. "Five minutes. Then the worst of it."

Reeve studied me. Snow crusted his eyebrows; his lips were cracked and gray, and for the first time since the glacier he looked mortal. "Why do I believe the worst of it is exactly what you've planned?"

"Because you're smart," I said. "And because you need me for another mile yet."

He gestured, and two men flanked me while the rest drank melted snow and stamped feeling back into their feet. The compass sat in his pack between us. Forty feet up, the wedge of stone waited on its seam of ice.

I had done the arithmetic all day, and here is what it said: if I brought the wall down now, at the pinch, the slide would seal the gorge behind us and ahead of us both. Reeve's party stranded above the treeline until spring — nine men, most of whom would not see April. And me with them, unless I timed it perfectly and ran the fifty yards of open ground past the pinch before the mountain finished falling. Fifty yards, in deep snow, with bound hands, against falling stone.

Imogen would call it suicide wearing arithmetic as a coat. Maybe she'd be right. But the compass had already cost an expedition, a guide's honor, my boat, my freedom, and very nearly the woman I — 

I stopped that sentence where it stood. There'd be time for finishing sentences if there was a spring.

"All right," I called. "Up. Single file. Stay on the stream shelf, touch nothing on the right."

They formed up. Reeve took the rear, where he could watch everyone including me. Of course he did.

We entered the Narrows.

* * *

**Imogen**

I came down out of the storm onto the shelf above the narrows mouth just as the last of them entered the slot, and I saw the whole picture at once: Silas at the front, hands roped, walking beneath the hanging wedge; Reeve at the tail; the seam of ice above glowing faintly blue-white in the failing light. And I saw what she intended, because her stride had changed — shortened, measured, a woman pacing herself for a sprint.

She means to bring it down on top of herself.

I screamed her name and the wind ate it. So I did the thing I'd sworn on the glacier I'd never do again, the thing that had gotten colleagues killed and sponsors rich: I used my voice as an instrument of the expedition. I stood on the shelf, forty feet above the trail, and shouted down into the gorge with every gram of authority I had ever borrowed from institutions that didn't deserve me:

"REEVE! THE WALL'S UNSTABLE! SHE'S LED YOU UNDER A HANGING SLIDE — GET OUT OF THE SLOT!"

Chaos, then. Torches fumbled alight. Men shouting. Reeve's voice cutting through it, flat and furious: "Nobody moves. NOBODY MOVES."

And Silas turned and looked up at me through the snow, and even at that distance, even in that light, I saw her understand everything — that I was alive, that I'd read her play, that I'd just spent her surprise to save her life — and I watched her decide, in the space of a heartbeat, to spend it anyway.

* * *

**Silas**

She was alive. She was alive and shouting and ruining everything, and I have never loved anyone more in my life.

Because here is what she'd changed: Reeve's men were bunched at the pinch instead of strung along it. The wedge above them hung over solid mass. If it fell now, it buried them entire — and me with them, unless I moved this instant.

I moved.

The binding on my wrists had been my own work, hours ago, worked loose strand by strand whenever the man behind me looked at his feet. It parted as I spun. I drove my elbow into the throat of the nearest guard, shoved the second into the third, and ran — not away from the wall, but at its base, at the seam, where the ice held its thousand-year argument with gravity. I hit the slope below the hanging wedge and drove my boot-heel into the seam, once, twice, and felt the mountain shift its weight like a ship coming about.

Then Reeve's hand closed on my collar and hauled me backward off my feet.

"No," he said, and there was nothing thin about his smile now, nothing left of it at all. He dragged me toward the slot's mouth, shouting for his men to run, and above us the seam cracked — a sound like the river ice going out in April, long and groaning and final.

I got one arm free. One. And I swung it up and back and caught him across the ear with the heel of my hand, and he staggered, and I threw myself sideways off the trail, down the bank, into the shallows of the flooded stream, because the water was the only place the stone wouldn't reach.

The wedge let go.

It did not fall so much as pour — a hundred tons of the eastern wall unzipping down into the narrows, taking the trail, the torches, the screaming, the compass in Callum Reeve's pack, all of it down into white chaos and thunder. The blast of displaced air hit the water like a door slamming. Stone rained past me, hissing into the flood. Something struck my shoulder and the world went white at the edges, and the current took me, tumbling, and the last thing I saw before the water closed over my head was the pass sealed end to end — a new cliff face where a road had been, raw and smoking, with nine men on the wrong side of it and winter coming down behind them like a lid.

The river pulled me under. I thought, with strange calm: *the Magpie would have handled this better.*

Then the dark took the thought, and the thought took me.

* * *

**Imogen**

I came down the bank after the thunder stopped, calling her name into the roar of the stream, and the storm answered with silence. Where the narrows had been there was now a wall of fresh-fallen stone taller than a church, still settling, still breathing dust. Somewhere beyond it, muffled and furious, men were shouting. Somewhere in the floodwater below the slide, the current ran fast and brown and carried nothing visible at all.

I walked the bank with my knife in my fist and my heart in my throat, scanning the eddies, the willow scrub, the stones — until the storm-light shifted, and I saw her.

Silas Thornbury lay face-down on a gravel bar thirty yards downstream, one arm hooked through a root of driftwood, snow gathering on her shoulders like the mountain already claiming her.

I went to her through the water without feeling the cold, and I turned her over, and I put my ear against her lips, and I waited — one second, two, five, a lifetime — for the river to give her back.

Her chest moved.

Downstream, the valley opened toward the treeline, toward the river country, toward home — and behind us, sealed under a hundred tons of stone, the compass slept with everything it had ever promised.
