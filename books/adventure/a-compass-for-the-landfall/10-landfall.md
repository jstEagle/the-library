## 10. Landfall

**Silas**

The last ridge was a lie told in white.

Silas had climbed ridges before — she'd grown up on the bluffs above Kestrel Bend, where the river cut its first gorge and the wind came off the water like a blade — but nothing had prepared her for this: a spine of black rock rising out of cloud, the air so thin it felt borrowed, her fingers so frostbitten they no longer belonged to her. She climbed by feel and by stubbornness, one handhold at a time, and behind her she could hear Imogen breathing in short, deliberate pulls, the way a person breathes when they have decided not to stop.

They had walked out of the narrows with nothing. No compass — that lay under a hundred tons of collapsed stone along with Callum Reeve's ambitions, and Silas did not regret it even in her coldest hours, which were many. No boat. The *Magpie* sat at the bottom of a pool two hundred miles south of here, holed at the waterline where Silas herself had driven the spike, and the thought of her was a grief Silas carried like a stone in her boot: always present, always sharp, hers by choice.

No food to speak of. Two days' worth of pemmican stretched into five. A mule named Bess who had carried them faithfully as far as the treeline and then been turned loose with a slap on her neck and a word of thanks, because there was no grass up here and no mercy either.

But they had each other, and they had down.

"Look," Imogen said, and her voice was wrecked from the cold, a whisper dragged over gravel.

Silas looked. Below them, past the shoulder of the ridge, the clouds had torn open, and through the tear she saw it: a gray thread of water widening toward a gray seam of sea, and on the near shore, small as toys, the rooftops of Port Meridian. Smoke stood up from chimneys in straight, unhurried columns. Somewhere down there a bell was ringing, though whether for a shift change or a ship or just the pleasure of noise, she couldn't tell.

Landfall.

She wanted to weep and found she had no water left for it. Instead she laughed — a dry, cracked sound — and Imogen laughed too, and the two of them stood on the top of the world holding hands through ruined mittens while the first true snow began to fall. Not the storm's spiteful horizontal sleet but real snow, fat and slow and almost gentle, settling on their shoulders like a benediction or a burial, depending on how fast they moved.

They moved.

* * *

**Imogen**

Port Meridian smelled of fish oil, tar, and woodsmoke, and Imogen thought she had never smelled anything so beautiful in her life.

They came down off the headland at dusk, two figures staggering out of the white like things the mountain had spat back, and the town took notice the way small towns do — doors opening, faces appearing, a boy running ahead of them shouting that there were people coming off the pass, live ones, women, frostbitten half to death. Someone brought blankets. Someone else brought a kettle. An old woman with hands like tree bark chafed Imogen's fingers without speaking, and Imogen let her, because after eleven days above the treeline being touched by another human being felt like a sacrament.

The magistrate's office was a room above the assay house, lit by a single lamp, presided over by a magistrate named Corliss who had the look of a man who had hoped to retire quietly and resented every complication that washed up on his shore. He listened. That much could be said of him. He poured tea neither of them drank because their hands wouldn't close around the cups, and he listened while Silas told it all.

And Silas told it all. That was the thing Imogen kept turning over later, the thing that undid her a little every time she thought of it. Silas didn't shape the story. She didn't leave herself out of it or soften her part in it. She said: I scuttled my own vessel to block the narrows. She said: I led armed men onto unstable ground knowing what would happen, and the ground did what ground does, and men died under it, and I would do it again. She said: the Harrow Expedition's sponsors sent killers after a piece of bronze, and Dr. Vale and I buried that bronze rather than let them have it, and if there's a charge in that, magistrate, you'd best write it down.

Corliss wrote some of it down. Most of it, actually, in a slow round hand, pausing now and then to ask a flat question — *How many men? Were any recovered? Do you have proof of these orders?* — and Imogen watched him and understood that none of it would go anywhere. Not because it wasn't true, but because truth required someone downstream to care, and the men who cared about such things were the same men whose money had started all of it. Reeve's employers had names that appeared on hospital wings and railway bonds. A magistrate in a fishing town was not going to hang his career on their necks.

"You acted in extremity," Corliss said at last, capping his pen with the finality of a man closing a drawer. "The expedition's sponsors will be notified of the loss of their personnel. Whether they pursue the matter is—" a small shrug, eloquent in its indifference— "beyond my jurisdiction."

"And the compass?" Silas asked.

"The compass, Captain Thornbury, is under a rockslide in territory no surveyor will reach before spring, if ever." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to poetry the room contained. "As far as this office is concerned, it never existed."

Imogen felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched. Never existed. After everything — the dig, the theft, the flight, the glacier, the narrows — the artifact she had betrayed colleagues and crossed a country to possess was now officially a rumor, and rumors, unlike artifacts, could not be owned.

Outside, the snow was still falling, soft and businesslike, already erasing their footprints from the street.

* * *

**Shared**

Winter closed over Port Meridian like a hand over a candle, and they stayed.

It was meant to be a week. Then it was meant to be until Silas's hands healed enough to hold a tiller. Then it was simply winter, and they rented a room above the chandlery with one bed and one window, and Imogen wrote letters to the families of the dead — seven letters, written slowly, honestly, omitting only what omission could protect — and Silas found work hauling freight on other men's boats and hated every hour of it, and came home each evening smelling of diesel and other people's cargo, and each evening Imogen met her at the door, and that was enough. More than enough. Neither of them said the word *love*, not at first; they were both too well acquainted with the price of naming things too soon. But Silas learned the exact weight of Imogen's head against her shoulder, and Imogen learned that Silas hummed old river songs when she was happy and didn't know she was doing it, and between those two learnings a life assembled itself, plank by plank, the way a boat does.

In March a letter came from a survey crew working the far side of the range. They'd reached the narrows. They described a rockfall of recent origin, total, impassable, burying the descent gorge under an estimated hundred thousand tons of stone. They made no mention of anything metallic. They made no mention of bodies either, though everyone understood what the arithmetic implied, and Silas read the letter twice and set it in the stove and watched it curl.

"They'll say he vanished," Imogen said.

"He did vanish." Silas fed the fire another corner of the page. "That's just the honest version."

* * *

The *Magpie* came up in April, on the third attempt, hauled off the pool bottom by a salvage crew Silas paid out of the last of her money and Imogen's considerably better-organized savings. She rose dripping and stinking of river mud, listing hard to port, and Silas swam out to her in the freezing water before the crew could stop her and put her palm flat against the hull as if greeting a horse.

"She'll need new planking below the waterline," the salvage foreman said. "New shaft, probably. You're looking at more than she's worth."

"She's worth exactly what she costs," Silas said, "and I'm paying it."

They refitted her through the spring — Imogen caulking seams beside hired carpenters with ink-stained fingers and a scholar's surprising competence, Silas overseeing every board as though supervising surgery — and by the first week of June the *Magpie* sat at the Meridian dock painted fresh white and green, licensed for mail and passengers, her name regilded, her wheel repacked, her engine turning over with a sound Silas described as *smug*.

Their first run was mail and four passengers upcoast: a schoolmistress, a pair of geologists, a merchant with a sample case. Imogen handled the manifest and the fares and discovered she liked the arithmetic of it, the clean little economy of a small boat well run. Silas handled the water. And in the evenings, when the passengers asked — passengers always asked — about the story they'd heard secondhand, the captain and the doctor and the lost expedition and the treasure in the mountains, the two of them told it badly on purpose.

"That?" Silas would say, splicing a line, not looking up. "Mostly exaggeration. There was a rockslide. Rockslides happen."

"There was no treasure," Imogen would add, stamping a ticket. "There was a survey instrument of no particular value, and it was lost. Everything else you've heard is embroidery."

"But the woman who went off alone into the whiteout—" a passenger would begin.

"Frostbite makes people do strange things," Imogen said.

"And the boat sunk in the narrows—"

"Insurance fraud," Silas said solemnly, and Imogen would choke on her tea, and the passengers would laugh, uncertain, and the story would pass down the coast a little more wrong than before, which was precisely the point. A story told badly enough becomes worthless. A worthless story keeps what it loves.

On the evening of their fourth run, running home before a building breeze with the sun going copper behind them, Imogen came up to the wheelhouse carrying two cups of coffee and stood beside Silas at the wheel, hip against hip, watching the water.

"Do you ever regret it?" she asked. "The boat you sank. The road you closed. All of it."

Silas considered the question the way she considered water — seriously, and with respect for what lay underneath.

"I regret the mule," she said. "I hope Bess found good grass." She turned the wheel a spoke, easing the *Magpie* around a bend she knew by heart, and glanced sideways, and her mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling, which Imogen had long since learned to read fluently. "The rest of it bought me the only cargo I ever wanted to carry. No. I don't regret it."

Ahead, the channel opened wide and gold, and the first stars were coming out over the water, and somewhere downriver a bell buoy rang its slow, patient note — the sound, Silas had once told her, that meant safe passage, that meant *this way home*.
