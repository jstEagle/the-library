## 1. The Weight of North

The engine died on a Tuesday, which Edmund Villalobos considered typical, because Tuesdays were when the world had always chosen to remind her who was in charge.

She'd been airborne for eleven minutes, ferrying a crate of veterinary antibiotics to the mission at San Isidro, when the Cessna's engine coughed twice, thought about it, and quit like a man walking out of a meeting. She put it down on the tidal flat at Puerto Hondo with more luck than skill, and by the time she'd climbed out to look at the cowling, she already knew what she'd find: a cracked cylinder head, and beneath it, the particular silence of an aircraft that would not fly again until someone with money said so.

Edmund did not have money. Edmund had debts — three of them, itemized in a ledger she kept in the plane's map case, written in her own hand because she refused to let anyone else hold the pen. The largest belonged to a man named Corvus Dain, who owned half the fuel docks between here and the strait and all of the patience of a landmine.

So: grounded. Grounded with a busted engine, a deadline that arrived Friday, and a boat.

The boat was borrowed from Father Anselm, who lent it the way he did everything — cheerfully, and with the understanding that God would sort out the insurance. It was a flat-bottomed river skiff with an outboard that started on the third prayer, and into it, over the course of one long gray morning, Edmund loaded forty kilos of medicine bound upriver to the clinic at Alto Verde. Quinine. Penicillin. Rehydration salts in foil packets that whispered when you touched them. A wooden crate marked FRAGILE that contained, according to the manifest, surgical instruments, and according to Edmund's better judgment, probably also contraband cigarettes, because nothing shipped to Alto Verde was ever only what it claimed to be.

"You could wait out the storm season," Father Anselm offered, watching her lash down the last crate. He was a small round man with forearms like dock rope and the serene expression of someone who had never once been on schedule. "The monsoon's turning. Old Mateo says his knee is singing."

"Old Mateo's knee has been singing since 1974," Edmund said. "If it starts harmonizing, I'll turn back."

"And your creditors?"

"They can sing too."

He laughed, then didn't, then pressed a brown-paper parcel into her hands with the sudden gravity of a man handing over something heavier than paper. "This came for you yesterday. From the lawyer. Serrano's office."

Serrano. Her father's lawyer. Edmund looked at the parcel the way one looks at a snake one suspects is asleep.

Her father, Tomás Villalobos, had been dead eleven months now, drowned off a chartless island in a squall that the coast guard politely called "an act of weather" and everyone else called what it was. He had left behind a seaplane, a reputation, a scatter of charts annotated in pencil, and no money whatsoever. What he had not left behind, apparently, was finished leaving things.

"He said you'd want it before you traveled," Anselm said. "And he said — I'm quoting, Edmund, because the man made me write it down — *tell her it doesn't point where you'd want.*"

"That's a strange thing to say about anything."

"It's a strange thing to inherit." Anselm shrugged, palms up. "Open it or don't. But open it before dark. Things given at dusk have a way of sitting wrong overnight."

* * *

She opened it on the boat, moored at the fuel dock with the medicine lashed under canvas, while the afternoon went copper around her.

Inside the paper: a brass compass, old, heavy as a knuckle, its glass clouded at the edges like breath on a window. Her father's initials were engraved on the case — T.V. — and beneath them, worn nearly smooth by a thumb that had worried it for thirty years, a single word in Spanish: *NORTE.*

Edmund held it flat on her palm and watched the needle.

It swung. It settled. And then it did something needles are not supposed to do, which is lie. It pointed confidently, steadily, unhelpfully off north by a good twenty degrees, toward open water and the scatter of islands that appeared on no chart she owned — the ones her father used to call, in the tone he reserved for women and weather, *the ones that keep their own counsel.*

She turned the compass. The needle stayed where it was, stubborn as a mule, aimed at nothing.

"Doesn't point where you'd want," she said aloud, to the compass, to her father, to the gull perched on the piling who had opinions but kept them. "Well. Neither do I, lately. At least we have that in common."

The truth was that Edmund wanted very little, these days. She wanted her engine fixed. She wanted the sky back, because the sky was the only place she'd ever felt the correct size. And she wanted — though she'd have bitten her tongue bloody before admitting it — to be free of the ledger, free of Dain, free of the particular exhaustion of owing money she'd never spent to men she'd never met, debts signed in her name by a father who had loved her extravagantly and accounted for everything else negligently.

The debt was the inheritance. The compass was just the wrapping paper.

Except.

Except that when she turned the compass toward the channel, the needle trembled — actually trembled, a shiver like a struck string — and swung a few degrees further off true, pointing hard now, deliberate, at a low green smear on the horizon she knew for a fact was not on any map. An island without a name. An island her father's penciled charts circled, once, in ink so faint she'd always assumed it was a stain.

Edmund sat very still on the gunwale and felt the tide go out beneath her, the skiff settling lower, the water sliding away from the mangrove roots with a sound like a long exhale.

* * *

Low tide at Puerto Hondo was a revelation and an embarrassment. It exposed everything the town preferred hidden: rusted engines, a bicycle, the ribs of some vessel dead so long it had become geology. The locals called the flats *la vergüenza del mar* — the sea's shame — and walked out across them at dusk to gather shellfish and gossip in equal measure.

Edmund walked out too, compass in hand, telling herself it was exercise. Telling herself a great many things.

From the end of the sandspit, with the water withdrawn almost a mile, she could see the far island clearly for the first time — the chartless one, the green smear, close enough now to count its features. Volcanic bone showing through jungle. A beach like a comma. And at its seaward base, where the rock dropped into the channel, a darkness.

A cave mouth. Arched, tall as a church door, black as a missing tooth — visible only now, at this hour, at this tide, because the sea that guarded it had stepped aside to let her look.

The needle in her hand strained toward it like a dog at the end of a leash.

Edmund stood in the dying light with wet sand sucking at her boots and understood two things with total clarity. First: her father had known about this cave, had come looking for it, and had died within swimming distance of it. Second: whatever was in there — wreck, treasure, smuggler's fleet, answer — it was the only currency on this coast that could buy her out of a ledger by Friday.

The tide had begun to turn. She could feel it in the water's voice, that low gathering note, the sea drawing its first breath back in.

Behind her, at the dock, a lantern came alight, and a figure stood silhouetted against it — broad-shouldered, still, watching her with the patience of a man who had been watching for some time. A stranger. A salvager, by the salt-crusted gear at his feet, though she hadn't seen a salvage boat come in.

He raised one hand. Not a wave, exactly. More like a man signaling that he'd found what he was looking for.

Edmund closed her fist around the compass, felt its brass teeth bite her palm, and started walking back — toward the light, toward the stranger, toward the rising tide and everything it intended to keep.
