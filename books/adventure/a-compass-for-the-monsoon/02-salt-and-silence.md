## 2. Salt and Silence

The boat was named *Perseverance*, which Edmund took as a bad omen, since nothing in her life that required perseverance had ever gone smoothly. She'd borrowed it from a fisherman who charged her in cigarettes and warnings — "Don't trust the channel past the second marker, the sand moves at night like it's got somewhere better to be" — and by the time she cleared the harbor mouth, the sky had begun assembling something with intent.

The medicine sat in a crate lashed under the tarp: insulin, antibiotics, a box of sutures marked FRAGILE in three languages. The clinic upriver needed it before the monsoon closed the coast for good, and the creditors needed her back in nine days, which meant she had exactly enough time to do everything wrong.

Her father's compass sat on the chart table where the lawyer had left it, brass worn to gold at the edges, its glass clouded like an eye going blind. She'd been raised to believe a compass was a promise. This one felt more like a dare. The needle didn't point north. It pointed northeast, steady and smug, toward water no chart bothered to name except as a smudge of hatching labeled, unhelpfully, *shoal*.

"Fine," she told it. "But I'm not talking to you."

She talked to it for most of the morning anyway.

The first sign of trouble came as a sound — a soft, rhythmic *gulp* beneath the engine's drone, like the boat swallowing. Edmund cut the throttle and lifted the floor panel. Water stood two inches deep over the bilge, cold and clear and utterly indifferent to her schedule. She found the leak near the garboard seam, a weeping split no wider than a fingernail, and did the arithmetic every bush pilot knows by heart: how long until small becomes fatal.

Not long enough. Not alone.

She bailed with a bucket and scanned the horizon, and that was when she saw him — a low gray workboat anchored off a spit of rock, a man standing in it as if he'd been waiting there all his life and possibly several others. He raised one hand. Not waving, exactly. Acknowledging. The way you acknowledge weather.

Edmund weighed her pride against the Pacific and, for once, chose correctly.

"You're taking on water," he said when she came alongside. He said it the way other people say *good morning*. He was tall in the way of things built for endurance rather than display, with salt-bleached hair and hands that looked like they'd been repaired as many times as any hull. A salvager, by the gear stowed in his boat — lift bags, grappling line, a dive mask scarred opaque.

"I'm aware," Edmund said. "I'm managing."

"You're bailing into a rising tide." He nodded at the bucket. "That's not managing. That's negotiating with the ocean from a position of weakness."

She hated him immediately and completely, in the way one hates anyone who is correct. "Edmund Villalobos," she said, because her mother had raised her to be polite even while drowning.

"Ilya." He didn't offer a surname, and she noticed that the way she noticed everything now — filing it under a growing folder labeled *things people don't say*. "Come alongside my boat. We'll patch your seam before the squall makes it academic."

"The squall?"

He pointed east, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. "That one. It'll reach us in an hour. It always reaches us in an hour. That's the arrangement."

* * *

They ran the patch together, his hands and hers working the epoxy and canvas while the wind arrived in advance parties — gusts that shoved the boats around like drunks. He worked without wasted motion, without instruction, and twice handed her exactly the tool she was about to ask for, which unsettled her more than the leak had.

"You've done this before," she said.

"Once or twice." He set a clamp. "Your father taught me the epoxy trick. Canvas wetted first, laid diagonal."

Edmund went still, a wrench halfway to her palm. "You knew my father."

"Everyone on this coast knew Anton Villalobos." He said it lightly, but not lightly enough. "Best pilot and worst gambler these islands ever produced. Sometimes in the same afternoon."

"That's one version of him."

"It's the only version anyone tells." He looked at her then, directly, and there was something in his face she couldn't file anywhere — not pity, not curiosity. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a story he already knew the ending to. "The storm's here. Get your tarp sorted. We'll run for the lee of that island."

"Which island? There's nothing on my chart."

"No," he agreed. "There isn't."

* * *

The island had no name on any map Edmund owned, just a spine of black rock and a crescent beach, and behind the beach a fisherman's hut that Ilya unlocked with the ease of a man returning rather than arriving. Inside: dry blankets, a stove, a shelf of canned goods arranged by size with a precision that bordered on devotion.

"You keep a shelter stocked here," Edmund said, wringing out her hair, "on an island that doesn't exist."

"Salvagers keep shelters everywhere. It's cheaper than optimism." He lit the stove. "Squall will pass by midnight. Then the real weather comes — the monsoon's turning early this year. You shouldn't be on the water at all."

"I have medicine due upriver and creditors due sooner."

"Ah." He handed her a tin mug of tea that tasted of smoke and cloves. "The famous Villalobos debts."

"Famous," she repeated. "Interesting word choice."

"Small coast." He shrugged, and the shrug was very good, practiced, almost convincing. "People talk."

She watched him over the rim of the mug. Outside, the rain began in earnest, hammering the tin roof like applause from an unfriendly audience. "You were heading northeast," she said. "Same heading as me. Same stretch of chartless water. Before you ever saw me leaking."

"Currents drift boats."

"Not against a flood tide they don't."

He smiled — the first time, and it changed his whole face, made him look younger and more dangerous simultaneously. "You fly, don't you? You have that way of asking questions. Like you're on approach and won't be talked off."

"What are you salvaging, Ilya?"

"Whatever the sea gives back." He rose to check the stove, giving his back to her, which she understood perfectly well was also an answer. "And you? What does a grounded pilot want with the northeast shoals?"

"I'm delivering medicine."

"Mm." He fed the fire. "And the compass on your chart table points northeast too. Funny thing about old brass. They get opinions."

Her skin prickled. "You looked through my boat."

"I patched your hull. One looks at everything while patching." He turned, and his expression had gone careful, the smile packed away somewhere. "Anton had one like it. Same stubborn needle. He used to say it pointed at the only honest thing he ever owned."

"He said that?" Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"So I'm told." But something flickered across his face — the particular stillness of a man who has just said slightly more than he was paid to say — and Edmund, who had spent thirty years reading weather and men and the exact moment each turned dangerous, caught it and held it.

The storm thickened. They ate canned sardines and argued mildly about whether the stove smoked too much (it did; he defended it like family). And when the wind dropped to a moan and the rain softened to a whisper, they sat in the stove-light, two strangers pretending at ease, each waiting for the other to lie again.

"I should sleep," Edmund said finally, not moving.

"You should."

She didn't. Neither did he. Instead she took out her father's compass and set it on the floorboards between them, and they both watched the needle tremble — not north, never north — straining northeast through the hut wall, through the rock, through the dark, toward whatever the tide kept.

"It's stronger tonight," she murmured.

"They say the wreck pulls at low water," Ilya said quietly. Then, catching himself: "So the stories go."

"What wreck, Ilya?"

He looked at her across the compass, firelight moving on his face, and for a long moment she thought he might actually answer. Instead he said, "Sleep, Edmund. Tomorrow the tide goes out far enough to show us both what we came for."

And she lay down beneath the blanket he gave her, listening to the sea breathe against the rocks, understanding two things with perfect clarity: that she trusted this man more than she had trusted anyone in years —

— and that he had known about the cave before she ever told him it existed.
