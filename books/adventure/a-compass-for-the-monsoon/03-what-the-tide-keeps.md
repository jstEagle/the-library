## 3. What the Tide Keeps

The cave swallowed the boat whole.

Edmund had expected a mouth, a throat, maybe a chamber. What she got was a cathedral. The tide had pulled back like a held breath, and the water inside lay flat and green as bottle glass, lit from some fault in the rock above where the afternoon came through in a single trembling column. Her father's compass sat open in her palm, and the needle didn't waver. It pointed straight down the throat of the dark, toward whatever slept at the bottom of the channel.

"You knew," she said. Not a question. She'd been circling it for two days the way the needle circled its cage.

Ilya shipped the oars and said nothing, which was his way of saying everything.

"Ilya." She let the name sit there between them, salt-heavy. "You knew."

"I suspected." He nodded toward the dark. "The charts stop here. Charts only stop for two reasons. Nobody's bothered, or somebody paid to make sure nobody bothers." He began to row again, slow, the oarlocks creaking like old floorboards. "I came to find out which."

"And you didn't think to mention it to the woman whose compass you've been staring at since Tuesday?"

"I was enjoying the staring," he said, and almost smiled, and she almost forgave him, and then the channel turned and she forgot the argument entirely.

The wreck came out of the dark the way wrecks do — not all at once but in pieces, a rib of black oak, then a spar furred with barnacles, then the whole fleet of them, three hulls wedged into the cavern like spoons in a drawer, their timbers gone soft as bread. Cargo netting hung from the ceiling of one hold, still knotted, still holding the shape of what it had carried a century ago. Coins had sifted down through the netting and lay across the cave floor in drifts, dull with age, catching the light column and throwing it back broken.

Edmund stood in the bow and did not move for a long time.

There it was. The number that would zero out her ledger. The number with a face and a deadline and a man named Ostrander attached to it. Enough to buy back her engine, her wings, her name off the debt rolls. Enough to stop owing.

She looked down at the compass in her hand. The needle pointed at the treasure, steady as a heartbeat.

Her father had flown forty years by this compass. He had trusted it through whiteouts and monsoons and one memorable incident involving a herd of water buffalo. And at the end, dying, he'd left it to a lawyer with instructions to tell her it didn't point where she'd want.

"Edmund." Ilya's voice, quiet. "The tide's turning. We should look while we can."

She looked. And there, wedged in the crook of the second hull, a shape that was not a chest and not a coin — a metal document case, green with corrosion, its latch seized. Her father's initials were scratched into the lid, young and cocky, the handwriting of a man who hadn't yet learned what the world cost.

* * *

Ilya found the papers while Edmund fought the latch.

He hadn't meant to. That was the truth of it, and he held onto the truth of it the way a drowning man holds a plank. He'd gone to check the water level at the cave mouth — professional habit, the habit that had kept him alive through eleven seasons of salvage — and wedged behind a fallen spar, wrapped in oilcloth, was a satchel. Modern oilcloth. Modern satchel. Someone had been here within the year.

He knew before he opened it what he'd find. He opened it anyway.

Contract papers. A salvage claim, notarized, bearing the letterhead of Ostrander & Voss. His own name signed at the bottom in his own careful hand, and beneath it the terms, plain as a confession: *exclusive recovery rights, contingent upon location of the Villalobos wreck site, compensation to be rendered upon delivery of coordinates.*

And a second page. A debt instrument. Edmund Villalobos, debtor. The signature at the bottom was her father's — and Ilya had spent three nights watching her sign the boat's logbook, watching the particular way she crossed her sevens, and the signature on the debt was wrong. Forged by someone who'd only ever seen her father's name printed, never written.

He heard her behind him, breathing hard, the latch finally giving.

"Got it—" she started, and then she saw the papers in his hands, and the cave went very quiet, the way a room goes quiet before glass breaks.

* * *

Edmund read the contract twice. She read her own name in it. She read Ostrander's letterhead, and the figure at the bottom of the debt instrument, and the forged flourish of her father's signature, and somewhere in the middle of all that reading the world tilted and righted itself into a different shape.

"You were paid," she said. "To find this. To bring them the coordinates."

"Yes."

"And the compass. The way it points here. You knew that's what they wanted. You knew that's why you needed me."

"I needed the compass. You happened to be attached to it." He said it flatly, and she understood — he was handing her the worst version on purpose. Let her have it clean. "The debt's forged, Edmund. Look at the signature. Your father didn't owe them anything. They invented the debt to flush out this cave, and you were the bloodhound they pointed at it."

She threw the document case at him. It caught him on the shoulder and went into the water with a slap, and he didn't flinch, didn't reach for it, just stood there holding the contract like a man holding a live wire he refused to drop.

"Two days," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "Two days I told you things. About my father. About the engine, the deadline, the—" She stopped. Outside, the light in the fault above had gone from gold to a bruised, boiling gray, and the first thunder came down the channel like something rolling over in its sleep. "The monsoon."

"It's early," Ilya said. "It shouldn't be here for—"

The cave answered for him. The water at the mouth shivered, then rose, a brown wall of it, and the sound came next — not thunder now but the sea itself, the whole monsoon making landfall at once, wind screaming through the fault above, and the tide that had held its breath all afternoon let it out.

* * *

The water came up the channel like a hand closing.

Ilya got the boat loose and Edmund got the case — her father's case, retrieved from the shallows, the latch broken now, the contents spilling — and then the boat was gone, driven sideways into the wreck timbers, and they were both in the water, and the water was not water anymore but a churning brown animal with a hundred fists.

She went under. Came up. The light column had become a gray rope whipping in the wind. Somewhere Ilya was shouting her name, and somewhere her ledger, her deadline, Ostrander's whole arithmetic was dissolving into the flood, and none of it mattered, because the case was drifting away from her, turning end over end into the dark between the hulls.

*The logbook.* She'd seen it when the latch broke — her father's flying logbook, forty years of coordinates and crossings, and tucked in its spine the folded page she'd glimpsed, the one with figures in her father's hand, the real debt, the real ledger, the proof that Ostrander's paper was a lie.

She dove.

The water was a solid thing. It pressed her down and turned her and she couldn't tell up from down, and her shoulder hit a wreck rib hard enough to ring her skull like a bell, and still she went deeper, because the case was there, a pale ghost in the murk, snagged on netting a century old. Her lungs were a fist closing. Her hands found the case. The netting held it, held her, and the monsoon pressed down from above like a judgment, and she thought, with a strange floating clarity: *this is the first thing I've ever chosen that wasn't chosen for me.*

She tore the case free and the netting took a handful of her hair in trade and she kicked for the light, for the gray rope of it, and the light got farther away, or she got weaker, and the fist around her lungs became a scream—

A hand closed on her wrist. Hard, sure, familiar. Ilya hauled her up through the brown and the dark and the noise, up into the roaring gray, and they broke the surface together in the flooded chamber, gasping, clinging to a spar of the smuggler's fleet, the water still rising, the mouth of the cave sealed under ten feet of monsoon sea.

The case was in her other hand. She'd never let go of it.

Ilya looked at her — drowned-rat, bleeding at the temple, clutching a corroded box like scripture — and shouted over the wind, "You went back for *paper*!"

"It's not paper!" she shouted back, and started to laugh, or cry, or both, which in a monsoon sounds the same. "It's the *truth*, Ilya! It's the only thing in this cave worth anything!"

He looked at the case. Then at her. Then at the sealed mouth of the cave, and the rising water, and the long dark hours ahead of them with no boat and no exit and no lie left between them.

"Tell me," he said, quieter, hauling her onto the spar, shoulder to shoulder, the storm howling through the fault above like a thing trying to get in. "All of it. Start with your father."

Edmund opened the case in her lap, water streaming off her chin, and lifted out the logbook — and stopped.

Because there, tucked in the spine where her father had hidden it forty years ago, was a second document. Newer. Folded once. And written across its face, in ink that had not yet faded, was a name she knew.
