## 4. Landfall

The storm did not end so much as change its mind. Somewhere in the small hours the wind stopped trying to peel the island off the map and settled into a long, gray grumble, like a dog that had barked itself hoarse. Edmund woke on a shelf of dry rock with her father's compass digging into her hip and Ilya already awake, feeding a stubborn fire with driftwood that hissed its objections.

"You snore," she said.

"I don't."

"You do. Like an outboard with a fouled plug."

"Then it's a good thing," he said, without looking up, "that you're not flying today."

She almost laughed — almost — and then remembered everything: the contract papers, wet and curling on the rock between them like something they'd dredged up together, which, in fairness, they had. Her creditors' names. His signature. The price they'd put on a cave.

The tide was coming back in. Through the cave mouth she could watch it happen, the sea sliding over the smugglers' fleet one timber at a time, patient as a lawyer. The wreck that had glittered gold-green in their lantern light last night was already going dark again, going back to being nobody's business.

Ilya followed her gaze. "You could still take it," he said quietly. "The debt's real enough to them. The gold would cover it twice over."

"And then what? Spend the rest of my life knowing every compass in my cockpit points at a lie?"

"That's very dramatic for someone who snores."

"I *don't*—"

He held up the contract papers, water-stained, half-legible. "Here's what I know about forged debts, Edmund Villalobos. I've salvaged a lot of things off a lot of floors, and paper is the easiest to fake and the hardest to argue with — unless you have the original." He nodded at the oilskin bundle she'd nearly drowned for. "Your father's logbook. If your debt was written after his death, dated against cargo he never carried, this proves it. Not to me. To whoever has the authority to burn the ledger."

Edmund turned the logbook over in her hands. Her father's handwriting marched across the pages, steady as runway lights. He'd kept records of everything — fuel, freight, favors owed and paid. Favors. That was the word he'd used. Never debts. She hadn't understood the difference until now.

"So we go back," she said slowly, "and instead of paying them in gold, we pay them in embarrassment."

"The most valuable currency there is," Ilya said. "It never devalues."

* * *

They struck the bargain properly, sitting cross-legged on the rock like children dividing marbles. Edmund would take the logbook to Port Amara herself, to the harbormaster's office, to the notary who'd witnessed her father's signatures for twenty years. Ilya would come with her — not because she needed him to, but because a salvager's testimony about who hired him to find the cave, and when, and how much the dates didn't add up, was worth more than any single document.

"And the cave?" she asked.

He looked out at the water, which by now had climbed halfway up the cave wall, swallowing the last visible rib of the wreck. "What cave?" he said.

"What cave," she agreed.

It wasn't quite burning the ledger. It was better. It was letting the monsoon do the paperwork. By tomorrow's high tide the entrance would be under six feet of green water, and by next month the silt the storm had shaken loose from the headland would settle over it like a blanket pulled up to the chin. Somewhere down there, a smuggler's fortune would keep its secrets, and somewhere up here, Edmund Villalobos would owe exactly nothing to anybody.

Her father's compass sat in her palm. The needle no longer strained toward the cave — the cave was gone now, or hidden, which amounted to the same religion. The needle just pointed north, ordinary and honest, like a dog waiting by the door.

"Huh," she said.

"What?"

"It points where you'd want it to now."

Ilya considered this. "Maybe it always did," he said. "Maybe you wanted the wrong things before."

She decided not to throw him into the sea. It was, she reflected, a sign of growth.

* * *

Port Amara smelled of diesel and rain-washed fruit, and the harbormaster's office smelled of both plus forty years of pipe smoke. The notary, a tiny woman named Mrs. Okonkwo with reading glasses on a chain of actual pearls, went through the logbook page by page while Edmund's creditors' representative — a damp man named Ferris with the posture of a question mark — grew progressively less comfortable in his chair.

"Cargo manifest dated the fourteenth of March," Mrs. Okonkwo read aloud, "for a flight your client claims was made on the second of April. And Mr. Villalobos died on the twentieth of March." She looked up over her pearls. "So either your client's paperwork is creative, or your pilot flew posthumously, in which case I have questions for the aviation board."

Ferris opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "There may have been a clerical—"

"There may have been," Mrs. Okonkwo agreed pleasantly, "a great many clerical errors, all flowing in one direction, all worth money to your employers. I'll be writing to them. I'll also be copying these pages for the maritime court, since Mr. Ilyushin here has sworn testimony regarding a salvage contract built on the same creative foundation." She smiled at Ilya, who had the grace to look briefly uncomfortable. "Salvagers make excellent witnesses. You people notice everything."

"It's the job," Ilya said.

By evening the ledger was ash in the harbor office's iron stove — Mrs. Okonkwo insisted on doing it herself, with ceremony, like a woman who had waited her whole career for the opportunity — and Edmund walked out into the street owing nothing but the borrowed boat, which she returned, scrubbed, with a case of the medicine delivered intact to the mission upriver. Sister Agnes had pressed a bag of mangoes on her and told her God watched over fools and bush pilots, "which in your family is one category."

* * *

The plane was where Marco's mechanics had promised: engine rebuilt, propeller trued, fuselage patched where the storm-scouring gravel had chewed her belly. Edmund ran her hands along the cowling the way other people touched the shoulders of old friends.

"She'll fly," said Marco, wiping grease on a rag that was already more grease than rag. "Better than before. Don't tell her I said sentimental things about her, she'll get ideas."

Edmund loaded the mangoes, the logbook, the compass. Ilya stood at the edge of the strip with his duffel over one shoulder, wearing the expression of a man rehearsing a goodbye he hoped wouldn't stick.

"Where will you go?" she asked. "Back to salvaging?"

"Someone has to notice everything."

"Mm." She checked the magneto switches, purely for something to do with her hands. "You know, the eastern routes are badly charted. Rivers move every season. Sandbars appear overnight. A person could spend years mapping them and getting paid almost nothing and loving every minute of it."

"A person could," Ilya agreed.

"It's a two-seat plane."

"I noticed," he said. "I notice everything."

She laughed then, really laughed, and swung up into the pilot's seat, and patted the seat beside her once, briskly, like a woman who had made a decision and refused to be sentimental about it. Ilya climbed in, stowed his duffel at his feet, and buckled the harness with the careful competence of a man who intended to be around for landings.

The engine caught on the first try. Of course it did. The whole world, it seemed, had finally stopped working against her.

She taxied out past the harbor, past the stove where her debt had gone to ash, past the mission and the market and the low green hills, and pushed the throttle forward. The wheels left the earth the way they always did — all at once, the way decisions do — and Port Amara fell away beneath her, small and shining and done with her.

Beside her, Ilya unfolded a chart across both their knees, an old one, ink faded, coastlines guessed at. "Where first?" he asked.

Edmund took out her father's compass and set it on the panel, where it belonged, needle swinging lazily until it settled. North. Just north. Anywhere north. Everywhere.

"Somewhere uncharted," she said, and banked east into the morning.
