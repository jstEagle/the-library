## 1. The Thief Who Stole Nothing

The thief had made three mistakes by noon of the second day, and Sable Coldharbour had catalogued every one of them the way other men catalogued grievances — carefully, and with a certain grim pleasure.

The first mistake had been stealing from a ranger. Not from a merchant, not from a fat landowner who would post a reward and then forget about it, but from Sable specifically, lifting his water-satchel off the pack-mule while Sable stood three feet away buying dried apricots in the Last Chance market. That took either extraordinary courage or an extraordinary failure to look up, and Sable suspected the latter.

The second mistake was running east. Everyone ran west, toward the coast towns where a man could vanish into the fish-smoke and the harbor crowds. East lay the hardpan — forty miles of cracked clay that drank sweat and gave back nothing — and beyond it, the drowned lowlands, where the river spread out into a hundred dying channels and the mosquitoes were large enough to carry off small children.

The third mistake, Sable decided as he crouched over the thief's campsite on the morning of the second day, was stopping to make tea.

"He stopped," Sable said aloud, to nobody, because talking to yourself was one of the few luxuries the desert allowed free of charge. "He stole my water so he could stop and make *tea*."

There was a ring of pale ash among the stones, still warm when he pressed two fingers into it. Beside it, a twist of leaves — desert sage, the cheap kind, the kind that tasted like drinking a campfire. The man had my satchel, Sable thought, my good copper kettle, and my last of the decent black tea from the coast, and he brewed the sage instead. Either he didn't know what he'd stolen, or he was the kind of man who preferred punishment in his cup.

Sable touched nothing else. He rose, brushed the dust from his knees, and looked east, where the hardpan shimmered like the lid of a pot just beginning to boil.

The wilderness had given him his quarry's trail as clean as a signature. Now it only remained to see what kind of man came at the end of it.

* * *

He caught sight of the thief at dusk, a mile out, a dark smudge moving against the smudge of the horizon, and for a long moment Sable simply stood and watched him, the way you might watch weather come in.

The man walked badly. That told Sable more than a week of tracking would have. He favored his left leg, stopped often, and once — Sable squinted through the heat-shimmer to be sure — sat down without appearing to decide to. Dehydration, probably. The hardpan did that. It reached into a man through his mouth and his eyes and turned his knees to rope.

Which raised the question Sable had been chewing since the market: why run east at all? A man who couldn't walk well, carrying stolen water, crossing the worst ground in the territory, away from every town and well and living soul. People didn't do that unless something behind them frightened them worse than the desert ahead.

Or unless something ahead of them called louder than sense.

Sable drank — carefully, from his reserve, because a ranger who chased thieves across hardpan learned early that thirst was a debt collector and it always found you eventually — and kept walking. He didn't hurry. Hurrying on the hardpan was how you became the story other rangers told over cook-fires, the one that ended with *and they never found so much as his boot-buckles*. He let the dusk do his work for him. A dying man slows. A walking man does not.

By full dark the gap between them had closed to a quarter mile, and Sable could hear him now, or rather hear the absence of him — no fire, no cough, no muttering. Just the faint scrape of boots on clay, moving with the stubborn idiocy of a man whose body had stopped voting but hadn't yet been informed.

Then the ground changed under Sable's own boots. The clay went soft. The air, which all day had tasted of dust and hot stone, suddenly tasted green.

The river.

Out here the river wasn't a river so much as a rumor — a sprawl of channels and oxbows and drowned groves left over from some ancient flood, half-lost to silt, home to catfish the size of dogs and reeds tall enough to hide a hanging. The locals called it the lowlands and spoke of it the way you speak of a neighbor with too many dogs: with respect, and from a distance.

Sable heard the water before he saw it, a soft insistent working against mud, and then the moon came out from behind its cloud and showed him the thief.

The man stood at the edge of the nearest channel, waist-deep already, and he was not wading across. He was standing still, both arms wrapped around something at his chest, and he was looking back the way he'd come — looking, Sable realized with a cold prickle between his shoulder blades, directly at him.

"Ranger!" the man called. His voice was wrecked, sandpaper dragged over sandpaper. "You want it? Come take it!"

"Come out of the water," Sable said, keeping his voice level and unhurried, the voice he used on spooked horses and spooked men. "Whatever you've got, it isn't worth drowning for."

"You don't know what I've got." And there it was — not defiance, exactly. Something worse. Certainty. The man laughed, a dry cracked sound with no floor under it. "Nobody knows. That's the whole point of it."

"I'm not going to shoot you," Sable said, which was true; he hadn't even drawn. "I'm going to stand here while you walk back to shore, and then we're going to have a conversation about property, and then I'm going to give you some water, because you're about two hours from dead and we both know it."

The thief looked down at whatever he held. Moonlight silvered his face, and Sable saw that he was younger than the walk had made him sound — thirty, maybe, with a week's beard and the hollowed cheeks of a man who had been afraid for a very long time.

"They said it was buried deep enough," the man said, mostly to himself. "They said nobody would ever—"

The bank gave way.

It happened without drama, which is the way the river does everything important. One moment the man was standing on a shelf of mud that had been pretending to be ground; the next, the shelf folded like wet paper and the channel took him. No scream. Just a slap of water, a swirl of moonlit ripples, and then his head came up once, gasping, arms still locked around his chest, still clutching —

Clutching nothing. Sable saw it clearly, in that one silver instant: the man's fists were closed around empty air, around his own knuckles, around whatever idea had driven him sixty miles across the hardpan. There was no bundle, no sack, no prize. He had carried nothing out of the desert but himself.

"Drop it and swim!" Sable was already moving along the bank, boots sucking in the soft ground, looking for a branch, a root, anything. "Let go and swim, you fool, the current's slow here —"

The man went under again. The reeds shivered. A night bird complained somewhere upstream, indignant at the disturbance.

Sable found a fallen willow limb, dropped flat on the bank, and thrust it out over the black water. He held it there. He held it a long time.

The river worked softly against the mud, patient as arithmetic. After a while, Sable pulled the branch back in and sat on the bank with his elbows on his knees, and let the silence be what it was.

That was the thing about the wilderness that no ballad ever got right. It didn't care whether you caught your quarry. It gave and it took with the same blank face, and tonight it had given Sable his thief and taken the answer in the same motion, trading them across the water like a merchant who wouldn't explain the exchange rate.

He sat until the moon moved, because a drowned man deserved at least that much company, and because Sable had learned that grief — even a stranger's grief, even a thief's — needed a witness or it curdled into something else.

* * *

He recovered the body at first light, when the channel ran shallow and honest.

It took most of the morning. The river had lodged the man in a snag of roots fifty yards downstream, face-down with his arms finally open, and Sable hauled him onto the mud with the efficient tenderness of a man who had done this before and hoped, without much conviction, to stop doing it someday.

He searched the body because that was the job, and because the man had died saying *nobody knows*, and Sable had never in his life met a sentence he could leave alone.

What the thief owned fit in two hands: a cheap knife, a bent tin locket with no picture in it, eleven coins, and a scrap of oilcloth folded into eighths and sewn — actually *sewn*, with thread — into the lining of his shirt.

Sable unfolded it on his knee.

It was a chart fragment, the heavy waxed paper salvage fleets used, salt-stained and old. Someone had drawn part of a coastline on it in faded ink, a stretch of the lowlands with depth-soundings marked in a neat antique hand, and one channel marked with an X so small you'd miss it if you weren't looking. But it wasn't the X that stopped Sable's breath.

It was the seal stamped in the corner, half worn away: a circle crossed by a single straight line, with a star set above it.

Sable knew that mark. Not from any book — the rangers' library had exactly four books and none of them were old enough. He knew it from before, from a kitchen in a village he didn't let himself think about, from a woman's voice telling stories over the stove while bread baked. Stories she'd told quietly, always glancing at the door first. Stories about a kingdom across the water that the maps forgot on purpose.

*Vessia*, she'd called it. *A country so beautiful they had to drown it.*

He'd been six. He'd assumed he'd invented it later, the way children invent things, the way adults assume children invent things.

Sable sat on the riverbank for a long time with the scrap in his hands, while the reeds whispered their endless gossip and the water slid past toward the sea, carrying whatever else it knew.

The wilderness had given him his quarry. It had even, in its indifferent way, given him a grave to dig and a name he'd never learn to put on it. But the answer — the answer was upstream, marked with a little X on a dead man's secret, in a country that officially did not exist.

"Well," Sable said to the river, tucking the scrap inside his coat. "That's inconvenient."

The river, being a river, declined to comment.
