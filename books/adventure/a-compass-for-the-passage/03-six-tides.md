## 3. Six Tides

The harbor at Graymoss smelled of tar, old fish, and the particular despair of men who had bet against the river and lost. Sable came down the plank road with the compass wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, feeling every eye on him the way a man feels sunburn — not yet painful, but certain to be.

He had told himself he would be careful. He had told himself this in the desert, too, right before following a dying thief's chart scrap into a drowned lowland and prying open a chest that should have stayed buried. Careful was a word Sable used the way other men used prayers: often, and without much expectation of results.

The informer he wanted kept a shack at the end of Fishgut Row, wedged between a net-mender's and a chandler who sold candles that smoked like they were apologizing for burning. The door was already open, because it had never once been closed in living memory. Inside, a small round woman named Odji sat behind a table stacked with ledgers she could not read and did not need to, because everything worth knowing lived in her head, filed under *people who owe me*.

"Sable Coldharbour," she said, without looking up from her tea. "You've got river mud on your boots and a look on your face like a man carrying something heavy that isn't lead."

"Good morning to you too, Odji."

"Morning's over. It's nearly noon." She finally raised her eyes. They were pale as riverwater and missed nothing, which was the whole of her profession. "Sit down before you fall down. You look like you walked out of the flood plain. Nobody walks *out* of the flood plain. They get carried, or they don't come."

"I walked."

"Mm." She poured a second cup and pushed it across the table, an act of hospitality so unprecedented that Sable checked the tea for poison out of habit. It was just terrible tea. "So. What did the river give you?"

Sable turned the cup slowly on its saucer. Trusting Odji was like trusting a knife — reasonable, if you respected what it was. "A question first. Has anyone been asking about salvage? Iron chests, old seals, anything pre-Erasure?"

Odji's eyebrows climbed a fraction, which for her was the equivalent of another woman fainting. "Pre-Erasure." She said the word the way people said *plague*. "Vessia, you mean. Though nobody says Vessia. There's no such place. Never was. Ask anyone."

"Ask anyone, and they'll say exactly that, in exactly those words. I know. I grew up on it."

"Then why are you asking?"

"Because yesterday I pulled a chest out of the silt with a seal on it I've only ever seen in my mother's stories. And inside was a compass that doesn't point north."

Odji set down her tea. That was twice in one morning; the day was full of miracles. "Doesn't point north," she repeated. "Points where, then?"

"That's the part I haven't worked out. Upstream. Something upstream."

For a long moment the little informer studied him, and Sable had the uncomfortable sensation of being read like one of her unreadable ledgers. Then she leaned forward, dropped her voice, and said the thing that turned his blood to cold sand.

"Three days ago, a crier came through from Meridian waters. Fleet posting. A thousand crowns for recovery of an iron strongbox bearing a certain seal, contents intact. Double that for the contents alone." She tapped the table once. "They described the seal, Coldharbour. In detail. On paper. Which means somewhere upriver, someone drew that seal from memory — or from a record everyone swears doesn't exist."

Sable's mouth had gone dry. "The Meridian fleet. The salvage fleet."

"The same. Forty years they've ruled this river, and in forty years they have never once paid a bounty for anything. They *take*; paying is for people who can't just take. So either they're feeling generous—" She snorted. "—or whatever's in your chest is worth more than a thousand crowns, and they want it quiet."

"Six tides," Sable said. "How long until they reach the wreck site?"

Odji smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "You assume they don't already know where it is. The storm that uncovered your chest uncovered it for everyone watching the water. But the fleet works the lower reaches first — customs, tolls, shaking down the fisherfolk. My guess?" She held up one hand, fingers spread. "Six tides. Maybe five, if their admiral is impatient. And Corvane Ash is always impatient."

The name landed in Sable's chest like a dropped anchor. He knew it — not from the river, but from a child's half-memory, a voice gone thirty years, telling stories by a fire that no longer existed. *Corvane Ash*, his mother had said once, spitting the syllables like seeds. A name from the erasure. A name from before she disappeared.

"You know her," Odji observed, watching his face with professional interest.

"No," Sable said, which was true in the way that mattered. "Someone knew her. Someone who's dead."

"Well." Odji refilled his cup, unasked. "Here's my advice, free of charge, which should tell you how worried I am. Take that compass, walk into the deep desert, and bury it where even you can't find it again. The fleet has forty ships and ten thousand men. You have a skiff you haven't bought yet and a face everyone in this harbor will sell for a bottle of rotgut."

"And if I do that, the truth stays buried with it."

"What truth? You don't even know what the compass *is*."

"No," Sable agreed. "But somebody killed a whole nation to keep it secret, and then paid a bounty to keep a box shut. Whatever's under that water, Odji, it's worth drowning for. Somebody already did."

He stood, left a coin on the table — Odji charged for advice, but the tea had been free, and that frightened him more than the fleet — and paused at the door.

"One more thing. Where do I find a cartographer? A good one. Disgraced preferred."

Odji laughed, a sound like a hatch closing. "Disgraced is the only kind that comes cheap. Try the Broken Sextant, two streets up. Ask for Wren. And Coldharbour—" Her face went serious, all the humor draining out of it like water through a cracked hull. "Five tides. Don't waste one sleeping."

* * *

He wasted most of one anyway.

Not sleeping — Sable had learned long ago to run on scraps of rest the way his camel ran on thorns — but on the slow, maddening business of finding a boat that could float and a crew that could sail it, when both had to be acquired in a harbor where the fleet's eyes were everywhere and his coin purse contained eleven crowns and a button.

The skiff he found at the end of the second day: a flat-bottomed river boat called the *Patient Answer*, which its owner swore was seaworthy and which Sable suspected was neither patient nor an answer. It leaked, but philosophically, at the stern, as though it had considered sinking and decided against it for now.

The crew was harder.

Wren he found hunched over a corner table at the Broken Sextant, redrawing charts from memory because no one would buy hers anymore. She took one look at the compass, went white, and said, "Where did you get this," in a voice that made three sailors look over, and Sable decided on the spot that he trusted her, because fear like that couldn't be faked.

Brannoc he found in the brig-adjacent tavern where ex-fleet men drank themselves stupid, a huge gray-bearded diver with fleet tattoos burned off his forearms in ugly scars. He listened to Sable's offer, looked at the eleven crowns, and laughed for a full minute. Then he stopped laughing and said, "Ash is going upriver," and that was the whole negotiation.

Pip he did not find at all. Pip found them — a scrap of an orphan, maybe twelve, who had stowed away among the water barrels before the lines were cast and emerged mid-river with the cheerful announcement that leaving was not on offer, thank you very much, and throwing him back would be murder, and he'd heard Sable needed someone small enough to crawl into the hold.

"We'll discuss it at the next port," Sable said.

"There isn't time for a next port," Pip said, with the merciless logic of children and admirals. "Five tides, Brannoc says."

And so it was four of them — a ranger, a disgraced mapmaker, a defector, and a stowaway — poling the *Patient Answer* upriver through the brown evening water while behind them the lamps of Graymoss shrank to a smudge, and ahead of them the needle swung steady toward something none of them could name.

* * *

Forty miles upriver, aboard the flagship *Terminus*, Admiral Corvane Ash received the report standing at her window, because sitting made her knees ache and she refused to ache in front of subordinates.

"A chest surfaced after the flood," said the messenger, a lieutenant who had learned to deliver bad news quickly, like pulling off a bandage. "Salvage chart fragment recovered from a dead thief. And a ranger out of Graymoss — name of Coldharbour — pulled the box itself two days ago. He's gone upriver. Bought a skiff. Took on crew."

"Coldharbour." Ash said the name once, tasting it. It was a common enough name on the river. It was also, she thought, with the cold certainty of forty years' guilt, not common at all. Not tonight. Not with that seal surfacing after four decades of silence, like a body rising when it ought to have stayed down.

"Admiral? Your orders?"

She watched the dark water slide past. Somewhere beneath it lay a city she had helped drown twice — once with fire, once with silence — and a compass built by clever, doomed people so that the truth could always crawl back out of its grave. She had spent her whole life since making sure graves stayed graves.

"Signal the squadron," she said. "Full speed. We reach the site in six tides."

"And the ranger, Admiral? The bounty—"

"There is no bounty," Ash said quietly. "There never was. When we find them—" She turned from the window, and the lieutenant, veteran that he was, took an involuntary step backward. "Leave no witnesses. No one who has seen the chest, the compass, or the seal. Burn the skiff. Salt the water if you have to."

The lieutenant saluted and fled.

Alone, Ash opened a drawer she had not opened in years and looked at a small, faded sketch: two young women in cadet uniforms, arms slung around each other's shoulders, laughing at some joke forty years dead. One of them wore her own young face. The other—

The other had eyes that Sable Coldharbour would have recognized, if he had ever seen them outside of dreams.

"Six tides," Ash murmured to the empty cabin, and closed the drawer on the past. "Let's see whose grief is heavier."
