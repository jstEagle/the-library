## 4. A Crew of Castoffs

Sable Coldharbour had spent eleven years as a desert ranger learning one lesson above all others: trust no one farther than you can throw them, and only throw people in emergencies.

It was a good philosophy. It had kept him alive through sandstorms and bandit seasons and one memorable encounter with a nest of scorpions in his bedroll. But it had a flaw, and the flaw was currently staring at him from the harbor master's board at Greywater Reach, where the Meridian fleet's bounty notice hung nailed through the chest of a crude drawing that looked nothing like him.

*One hundred crowns. Chest of Vessian make. Compass within. Report all bearers.*

He couldn't outrun the fleet alone. He knew it the way you know weather coming — in his knees, in the set of his jaw, in the way the harbor informer had taken his coin and then looked at him a half-second too long before walking away.

So he did the thing he hated most in the world.

He went looking for help.

* * *

The disgraced cartographer lived above a chandlery that smelled of tar and old fish, in a room so crowded with maps that Sable had to enter sideways, like a crab.

"You're standing on the Serpent Coast," said a voice from beneath a table.

Sable stepped left. "Sorry."

"No, no, that's the Ashfall Delta now. You've ruined everything." A woman unfolded herself from under the furniture — mid-forties, ink-stained to the elbow, hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual drafting compass. She squinted at him. "You're the ranger. The one who pulled the chest out of the silt."

Word traveled fast in river towns. Word traveled faster when there were a hundred crowns attached to it.

"I'm looking for someone who knows Vessian work," Sable said. "Craftsmanship. Instruments."

The woman — Wren, according to the faded name painted on her old survey kit — went very still. Then she held out her hand, palm up, imperious as a duchess. "Show me."

He almost didn't. Every instinct he owned told him not to hand a stranger the only thing keeping him ahead of a firing line. But her eyes had already gone hungry, and he understood, suddenly, that she would take the compass out of his hands if he didn't choose to give it.

He gave it.

Wren turned it over once, twice. Her thumb found the seam along the bezel, the tiny interlocking waves stamped into the brass, and something in her face cracked open like ice on a spring river.

"Double-struck brass," she whispered. "Salt-quenched. This is guildwork — first-rank guildwork, the kind they only made in the capital." She looked up at him, and her voice dropped to something fierce and quiet. "I wrote my thesis on Vessia. They stripped my certification for it. Called it 'the pursuit of cartographic fantasy.' There is no such nation, they told me. There has never been such a nation." She laughed, a short, dangerous sound. "And here is its heartbeat sitting in your hand."

"The fleet wants it."

"The fleet wants a great many things buried." Wren was already clearing a space on her table, sweeping aside forty years of other people's lies. "You'll need a navigator. Someone who can read water this needle doesn't point at. I assume that's why you're here, because you have the survival instincts of a damp match if you think you're sailing upstream alone."

"I hadn't actually invited you yet."

"You walked into my room sideways and put a dead nation in my hands. That's an invitation." She was buckling on her kit. "Terms: I get to chart whatever we find. Everything we find. And when this is over, I publish."

"That second part might get us killed."

"Then I'll die accurately." She shouldered past him, then paused at the door. "Bring the compass. And don't let anyone else touch it. Some instruments remember their makers, and this one looks like it remembers being buried."

* * *

Brannoc was easier to find and harder to convince, mostly because he was drinking.

Sable located him at the Drowned Gull, a riverside tavern where the floor tilted gently with the tide and nobody minded. The man sat alone at the end of the bar, enormous, scarred from jaw to knuckle in the pale lattice patterns that marked a deep-water diver — pressure scars, the kind you earned below thirty fathoms. His fleet tattoo had been burned off his forearm. Badly. By himself, by the look of it.

"I know what you are," Sable said, taking the stool beside him.

"A handsome stranger with excellent taste in taverns?"

"A fleet diver. Defected."

Brannoc took a long pull of his drink. "That's a hanging word, friend. Use it quieter or use it never."

"The fleet reaches the wreck site in five tides. I need someone who knows their signals, their patrol patterns, their—" 

"No."

"—their diving protocols, their—"

"No." He set the mug down. "Listen, ranger. I gave that fleet nine years. Nine years pulling bodies and treasure out of the dark for men who counted the treasure and forgot the bodies. When I asked too many questions about a certain sealed order, they answered by putting my partner on a dive without a safety line." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I don't do fleet business anymore. Fleet business does *me*, and it always ends the same way."

Sable slid the compass onto the bar between them.

Brannoc stared at it. The tavern noise seemed to lean away from the two of them, leaving a pocket of silence. When the big man finally spoke, his voice had changed — gone rough, gone somewhere young.

"That's Vessian."

"You recognize it?"

"My grandmother sang songs about Vessia." He turned the mug in his scarred hands. "Lullabies. About a city with white spires where the river met the sea. I thought they were just songs." He looked up, and Sable saw the decision happen behind his eyes like a door swinging open. "Where's it pointing?"

"Toward something upstream. Something lost."

Brannoc grunted. Downed the rest of his drink. Stood — he stood like a mast going up, all at once and taking up the whole sky.

"One condition. If it comes to divers in the dark, I go down first. Nobody else. I've buried enough partners."

"Done."

"And ranger?" He fixed Sable with a look that could hold a hull together. "If this ends with me hanging, I want it noted I complained the entire time."

* * *

Pip was not recruited. Pip was discovered.

Sable found the stowaway two tides later, halfway upriver, curled in the skiff's anchor locker among the spare line — a scrap of a child, maybe ten years old, all elbows and defiance, eating Sable's emergency jerky with the unbothered calm of a cat in a sunbeam.

"How long have you been aboard?"

"Since Greywater." Pip swallowed. "You chew loud. Both of you. Like camels."

"*Both?*" Sable turned. Brannoc stood at the tiller wearing the expression of a man who had known for two days and found it funny. "You knew?"

"He brings in fish," Brannoc said. "Good ones. Wasn't worth the conversation."

"We are being hunted by an admiral with a flagship and a kill order, and you let a *child*—"

"I'm not a child," Pip said with tremendous dignity, "I'm crew."

"You're a stowaway."

"Stowaway *who's crew.*" The kid scrambled up onto the locker lid, chin raised. "I'm not going back to Greywater. There's nothing in Greywater. There's nothing anywhere for kids like me except the gutting sheds, and I'd rather drown, thank you very much, and also I can swim better than both of you combined, and also I found your leak."

Sable pinched the bridge of his nose. "What leak?"

"The one in the stern. It's small. I've been bailing." Pip gestured at a battered kettle serving as a bail bucket, sitting beside a suspiciously neat pile of dried fish. "See? Crew."

"Wren will have opinions about this."

"Wren already taught me to read the depth marks," Pip said sweetly. "She says I have good eyes."

Sable looked at Brannoc. Brannoc looked at the river. Somewhere downstream, five tides away and closing, an admiral was sharpening her intentions against them, and here he was, negotiating with a ten-year-old over a kettle.

"One week," Sable said finally. "First safe harbor, we find you—"

"You won't," said Pip, contentedly, and went back to the jerky.

* * *

They made strange music together, the four of them, on a skiff named — according to the flaking letters on her stern — the *Honest Answer*, though Wren insisted the original name had been longer and someone had sawed off the disappointing part.

Wren navigated from the bow with charts spread across her knees, arguing with the compass constantly, the way scholars argue with things they love. "North-northwest by the stars, but the needle says due north by the current, which means either the needle's wrong or the river's lying, and I've never trusted a river." Brannoc handled the rigging and the cooking, both with the same heavy competence, seasoning everything as though flavor were a personal debt he intended to pay in full. Pip bailed, fished, eavesdropped shamelessly, and asked questions in threes, always in threes, each one sharper than the last.

And Sable — Sable stood at the tiller and discovered, with mounting alarm, that he was talking.

Not much. Not well. But somewhere between Wren's third lecture on double-struck brass and Brannoc's fourth story about a salvage job gone wrong ("and that's when the eel got involved"), he heard himself explaining how rangers read a dust horizon, and Pip was listening with those quick bright eyes, and nobody was asking him for anything except his voice.

It was unbearable. It was — he searched for the word and came up empty, so he filed the feeling away for later examination, the way he filed everything.

On the evening of the third day, the needle swung.

They all saw it. The compass lay in its box on the thwart, and the needle, which had held steady north for days, trembled once — twice — and settled on a new bearing, hard to starboard, quivering like a struck string.

Wren went silent. Brannoc stopped rowing. Even Pip froze mid-bail.

"It's never moved like that," Sable said quietly.

"It's not moving toward a place," Wren murmured, leaning close, her breath fogging the glass. "Look at it. It's not settling. It's *reaching*."

Upstream, the river bent around a headland dark with old forest, and beyond it, unseen, something waited that the compass wanted badly enough to shake.

Brannoc spat over the side, unhurried. "Fleet's a day behind us," he said. "Whatever that needle's found — we'd best hope we find it first."

Sable turned the tiller and followed the shaking needle into the dark.
