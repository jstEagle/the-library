## 5. The Needle Turns

Wren had been thrown out of three cartographers' guilds, one university, and a single very memorable dinner party, and in all that time she had never once been accused of patience. So it said something about the compass that she spent two full days just looking at it before she touched it again.

She sat in the skiff's cramped cabin — a cabin being a generous word for the space under the tiller bench where the leak had been demoted from "catastrophic" to "character-building" — with the instrument open on her knees and a stub of candle burning beside her. The brass was Vessian work. She was certain of it now. The chasing on the bezel, the way the hinge was cut so the lid swung past flat and rested against the case like a bird folding its wing — she'd seen that craft once before, in a locked drawer of the Guildhall, in an atlas of a country that officially had never existed.

The needle did not point north. She had established that on the first night, to the tune of Sable standing over her shoulder saying *are you sure* eleven times. North was aft and slightly to port, toward the hardpan they'd fled. The needle disagreed. It held steady toward the bow, toward the drowned reaches upstream, and it did not waver when she turned the compass, or turned herself, or turned the entire skiff in a slow, dizzying circle while Pip laughed hard enough to fall in the river.

Which meant it wasn't pointing at a place.

Wren had spent her disgrace learning things the guilds said weren't worth learning — dead scripts, sealed instruments, the mathematics of maps that lied. And she knew, in the way one knows a word on the tip of the tongue, that she had read about a needle like this. She'd read about it in a margin, in a hand that had been hurried and afraid, in a book the guild had burned while she watched and said nothing. That silence was half of why she was here, leaking on a river with strangers.

*It does not show you where to go,* the margin had said. *It shows you what you cannot stop looking for.*

She needed a second holder. An instrument that answered to its bearer had to be tested against more than one bearer, or she was just charting her own longing and calling it science. And there was exactly one other person aboard whose hands the compass tolerated. Sable held it and the needle held still. Wren held it and it did the same. Either it was broken, or—

Or.

She turned it over in her fingers and thought about what she had lost. Her name on the guild rolls, struck through in red ink. Her teacher, who had denied her in front of the tribunal with his hat in his hands. Her city, which she could no longer walk into without an escort and a fine.

The needle trembled.

It was a small thing, a shiver, like a fish brushing the hull. But Wren had spent her life reading lines finer than hair, and she saw it: the needle had leaned a degree, maybe two, off its heading toward Sable — no. Not toward Sable. Toward Sable's side of the skiff. Toward the man himself.

She set it down on the bench as if it had bitten her.

"Oh," she said, to the empty cabin. "Oh, you clever, cruel thing."

* * *

She waited until evening, when the river went gold and the midges came out in congregations, and Sable was mending the jib with the grim competence of a man who expressed affection exclusively through rope work. Pip was asleep in a coil of netting. Brannoc was somewhere below, oiling the bilge and pretending not to listen, which was Brannoc's native language.

"Your compass," Wren said, "is a liar."

Sable didn't look up from his knots. "It pointed us true so far."

"It pointed you true. That's different." She sat down across from him, the compass between them on the thwart like a card player's hand not yet shown. "I tested it. It doesn't point at a place. It points at a *loss*. Whatever its holder has lost — really lost, down in the bone — the needle bends toward it."

Sable's hands went still on the sailcloth. Just for a moment. Just long enough.

"That's a story," he said. "Rangers tell stories about instruments. Keeps green rangers from selling their gear."

"I read about it in a burned book, in a margin, in a dead language, written by someone terrified." She kept her voice gentle, because she had learned — late, expensively — that the truth lands softer that way. "And I tested it on myself. It turned toward me, Sable. Toward what *I* lost. Then I watched it while you held it."

Now he looked up.

"It doesn't point upstream," she said. "It points at you. Or through you. Somewhere behind you, back the way you came before I ever met you. Somewhere you lost something."

The river made its small noises. Pip snored with the commitment only children and old sailors manage. Sable looked at the compass for a long time, and Wren watched him do the arithmetic that desert people do — the slow, careful weighing of whether a thing can be survived.

"My mother," he said at last, "was from a country that didn't exist."

* * *

He talked the way he did everything — sparingly, facing slightly away, as if the words might spook. Wren didn't interrupt. She had learned that too.

He'd grown up on the hardpan edge, in a river town called Marrow's Bend, with a mother who sang songs in a language the neighbors called *river-talk* and wouldn't let him repeat at school. She cooked things with too much dill. She kept a brass hairpin with a bird folded into it, the way the compass lid folded, and when he asked where she was from she'd smile and say *from before the maps changed, little wren* — no. Not wren. He stopped, frowning, trying to remember the endearment, and couldn't, and Wren saw that loss land on him fresh, forty years late.

"When I was nine," he said, "the fleet came through. Not raiding. *Correcting.* They went door to door with lists. My mother put me on a caravan heading east with an aunt I barely knew and said she'd follow on the next barge." His hands resumed the knots, too fast now. "There was no next barge. There were no barges from Marrow's Bend again. The town's on no chart. I've looked. I've looked my whole life, Wren. The maps say there's a marsh there. The maps say nobody ever lived there."

"The maps were paid for," Wren said quietly. "I've seen the receipts. Not for Marrow's Bend specifically. For the whole erasure. The guilds redrew Vessia out of existence in a single season, and everyone who signed got a pension."

Sable stared at her. "You *knew*?"

"I knew something was bought. I didn't know what. That's the other half of why I'm here." She turned the compass toward him. "The needle's been pointing at Marrow's Bend since the day you picked it up. Maybe since the day it was buried. Sable — I don't think it was buried to be hidden. I think it was buried to be *found*. By someone it could lead home."

He didn't touch it. That was the thing Wren would remember afterward — he looked at the compass the way a man looks at a well he's been told holds both water and drowning, and he did not reach for it.

"She taught me a song," he said. "I can't remember the words. I can remember the *tune*. What kind of man remembers a tune and not his mother's voice?"

"The kind the desert raised," Wren said. "Grief keeps badly out there. You buried her deep. The compass isn't pointing at a place, Sable. It's pointing at the grave you made and never marked."

He stood, abruptly, and went to the rail, and Wren let him stand there. The stars came out over the drowned country, thick and indifferent. After a while Brannoc surfaced from below, glanced between them, and with the delicacy of a man who'd once defected from a fleet rather than attend another meeting, said nothing and handed Sable a cup of tea.

Pip woke at some point, padded over, and looked at the compass on the thwart with the frank curiosity of the young.

"Who's it pointing at now?" Pip asked.

"No one," Sable said.

"Then why are you all sad?"

"Go back to sleep, tadpole."

But Pip didn't move. Pip looked at the needle, and the needle — Wren saw it, and felt the hair rise on her arms — the needle *twitched*. A small, hungry swing, toward the child, toward whatever a stowaway orphan had lost and carried like a stone. And then it settled back toward Sable, patient as a dog that has chosen its person.

"It's pointing at you," Pip said to Sable, with the terrible accuracy of children. "It's been pointing at you the whole time."

* * *

Wren stayed up with the chart that night, and it was while scratching out a correction — she could not help herself, the marsh where Marrow's Bend should be practically itched — that she heard Sable on deck, very quietly, singing.

The tune was the one he'd said he remembered. His voice was rough and low and it cracked in the middle, and he sang it through twice, and the second time a few words came back to him — three words, in river-talk, in Vessian — and Wren did not know their meaning but she wrote them down phonetically in the margin of her chart, because that was what she did. She kept what the maps threw away.

At dawn the wind came around out of the east, fair for the drowned reaches, and Brannoc came up squinting at the water and then squinting harder at the horizon, where the morning haze was arranging itself into a shape Wren didn't like.

"That's a reach buoy," Brannoc said. "Fleet water. We're inside their lines."

Sable picked up the compass. The needle held steady — through him, past him, toward the sunken town that was not on any map, toward a woman forty years drowned in someone else's paperwork.

"Then we sail faster," he said.
