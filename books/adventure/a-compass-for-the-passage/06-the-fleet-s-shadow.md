## 6. The Fleet's Shadow

Brannoc had spent eleven years learning the Meridian fleet's language — the flag-hoists, the lantern codes, the particular way a salvage tender sat in the water when she was hunting rather than working. He had spent one afternoon trying to forget it. Neither effort had taken.

So when he saw the signal lamp stuttering from the customs tower at Tarrow's Reach — three long, two short, a pause, three long again — his stomach dropped through the skiff's rotten planking and into the river.

"That's not weather talk," he said.

Sable looked up from the tiller. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning *Meridian to all stations: quarry sighted, hold position.* They're not chasing us yet. They're waiting for us." Brannoc wiped his palms on his trousers, which helped nothing. "I told you I'd know their signals. I didn't tell you I'd hate being right."

Wren, curled over her charts at the bow, didn't look up. "How long?"

"Reach is a bottleneck. One channel past the customs station, and they'll have a picket boat at each end if they're smart."

"Are they smart?"

"I was fleet," Brannoc said. "Draw your own conclusions."

Pip appeared from belowdecks — there wasn't really a below, more of a below-ish, a crawl space under the cargo boards where the boy slept and hoarded things — carrying a biscuit in each hand and eating neither, which for Pip constituted an emergency.

"Is it the fleet? It's the fleet, isn't it. I can tell because Brannoc's gone the color of old porridge."

"I am a normal color," Brannoc said.

"You're porridge," Pip confirmed, and handed him a biscuit anyway, which was the boy's way of being kind: insult first, rations second.

Sable brought them about into the reed shallows and studied the Reach through Wren's cracked spyglass. The customs station squatted on its pilings mid-channel, a fat stone toad of a building, and beyond it the masts of two fleet tenders stood against the morning haze like needles waiting for thread.

"There," Sable said. "The station itself. If we come through fast, hugging the pilings—"

"They'll burn the skiff out from under you," Brannoc said. "Unless." He hated the unless. The unless had been forming since he saw the lamp code, and every version of it ended the same way. "The station keeps a signal oil store. Lamp oil, pitch, that kind of thing. Fleet regulations say it's kept in a stone annex on the landward side. Fleet regulations also say the annex door is kept locked, and fleet locks are all made by the same man in Meridian City, and I could pick them in my sleep, because I did, once, actually asleep, as a dare."

Wren lowered her chart. "You want to set fire to a customs station."

"I want to set fire to the *oil store adjacent to* a customs station," Brannoc said with dignity. "The smoke goes across the channel, the tenders can't see us, we run the Reach blind at speed, and by the time they sort themselves out we're downstream and laughing."

"Will we be laughing?" Pip asked.

"Someone will be," Brannoc said. "Probably not me."

Sable was quiet a moment, watching the water. Then: "Can you get to the annex without being seen?"

"No," Brannoc said honestly. "But I can get there without being *recognized*, which is different, and better."

* * *

They put him ashore in the reeds a half-mile upstream, and Brannoc walked toward the customs station wearing the borrowed coat of a fleet quartermaster third class, a rank he had held briefly and dishonorably, and found the coat still fit, which felt like a personal accusation.

The annex was exactly where regulations said it would be. That was the thing about the fleet people never understood: it was not cruel because it was chaotic. It was cruel because it was orderly. Every horror had a form number.

He picked the lock in nine seconds, went inside, and stood among the barrels of lamp oil breathing the sweet tar smell of them, and thought, as he always thought, of the day he'd swum out of the fleet's shadow for good — the diver's exam he'd refused to finish, the wreck he'd been ordered to strip that still had bodies in it, the lieutenant who'd said *the dead don't file complaints.* Brannoc had filed one anyway. It had cost him everything except his life, and some days he wasn't sure he'd gotten the better end of the trade.

He was laying the fuse trail — a rope soaked in oil, running from the barrels to the reed bank — when the shouting started.

Not at him. At the skiff.

He came out of the annex in time to see it: a fleet picket boat sliding out of the smoke-haze ahead of schedule, grapples already thrown, and the skiff pinned against the customs pilings like a moth on a board. And on the deck of the skiff, standing on a crate with a boat-hook in his hands like it was a lance, was Pip.

"*Get down,*" Brannoc breathed, uselessly, a quarter mile away.

What happened next happened fast and badly, which was how most things happened. A fleet sailor swung aboard. Sable hit him with a boathook oar — Brannoc heard the crack of it even at distance, first blood, real blood, the sound changing something in the air. Wren threw a chart weight. And then a second sailor simply reached down and took Pip off the crate the way you'd take a kitten off a table, and the picket boat backed water with the boy screaming curses that would have gotten his mouth washed out in any decent port.

Brannoc ran. Not toward the skiff — toward the fuse rope. Because the arithmetic was brutal and simple: he could save Pip or he could save the escape, and if he saved the escape, someone else might save Pip, and if nobody saved the escape, nobody saved anybody.

He lit the rope and ran the other way.

* * *

The oil store went up with a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

Fire climbed the annex wall in a single orange sheet, and the smoke came across the channel thick and black and greasy, and through it the skiff tore free of the pilings with Sable at the tiller and Wren bailing and the whole rotten vessel groaning like an old man asked to dance.

But no Pip.

They ran the Reach blind, true to plan, threading between the tenders whose crews were coughing and cursing in the smoke, and Brannoc crouched at the bow doing his job — reading the water, calling the channel — and hating every second of it, because his eyes kept going back to the empty crate where the boy had been standing.

"He'll be alive," Wren said quietly, beside him. "Ash doesn't kill children. She makes examples of them. There's a difference, and it's worse, but it means time."

"How do you know what Ash does?"

"Everyone in cartography knows what Ash does," Wren said. "She burned my guildhall. You don't burn a guildhall and stay anonymous."

The smoke fell behind them. The river widened. An hour passed, gray and silent, nobody rowing so much as necessary, everybody listening to the water as if it might hand the boy back.

It was near dusk when the sail appeared — a scrap of canvas, ragged, absurd — and behind it a small boat, and in the small boat, sitting with his arms crossed and his chin up like a returning admiral, was Pip.

They pulled him over the gunwale and Brannoc grabbed him by the shoulders and shook him once, hard, before he could stop himself, and then just held on, which was worse, and Pip let him, which was worst of all.

"I'm fine," Pip said. "Mostly. They gave me soup."

"They gave you *soup*."

"Fleet soup. It had a button in it." Pip's chin wobbled, just once, and he mastered it. "There was a lady. On the flagship. She talked to me like I was a person, which was creepy. She said—" he swallowed, and recited it carefully, the way a child recites words too heavy for him: "*Tell the ranger: surrender the compass and live. All of you live. Keep it, and I will take it from whatever's left floating.*" Pip looked up. "She said you'd know who she meant. She said she knew your mother."

The river went very quiet around them.

Sable stood at the tiller with the compass in his hand, though Brannoc hadn't seen him take it out, and the needle lay steady, pointing upstream, pointing home to something, patient as grief.

"She doesn't make offers," Sable said finally. "She makes deadlines dressed as offers." He looked at each of them in turn — Wren, Brannoc, Pip — and Brannoc braced for the speech, the noble self-sacrifice, *you should leave while you can.*

Instead Sable said: "Pip, are you hurt?"

"No."

"Wren, can you still read the needle?"

"Yes."

"Brannoc. Can you get us past anything else they put in the water?"

Brannoc thought about the burning station dwindling behind them, about the lieutenant and the drowned wreck and eleven years of swimming away. He thought about the boy with the button in his soup, alive because four strangers had been stupid enough to come back for him.

"Yeah," he said. "I can get us past anything. Ask me tomorrow, though. Tonight I'm having a lie-down."

That got a laugh — small, cracked, but real, the first laugh any of them had shared, and it changed the shape of the skiff somehow, changed it from a boat carrying four separate disasters into something with a name it hadn't earned yet.

Sable turned the compass over in his palm. "Then we don't surrender," he said. "And we don't hide. We race them. Five tides to the capital, if Wren's right about the current."

"And if she's wrong?" Pip asked.

"Then we're lost together," Sable said, "which is still better than being found alone."

Brannoc took up his oar. Behind them, far downstream, the fleet's signal lamps were talking to each other in their tidy, murderous grammar — and for the first time since he'd left, Brannoc understood every word, and none of it was aimed at him anymore.

It was aimed at all of them.

He rowed harder.
