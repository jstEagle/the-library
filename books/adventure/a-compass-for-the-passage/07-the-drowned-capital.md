## 7. The Drowned Capital

Pip had counted the tides the way other children counted sleeps before a festival. Four down. One to go.

On the morning of the fifth, she climbed into the rigging of the *Kindly Liar* — which was what they'd finally named the leaky skiff, after Wren pointed out that a ship should be named for its most honest feature — and saw the water change.

It happened gradually, then all at once. The brown river-water thinned to green, and the green deepened to something like glass, and beneath the glass there were shapes.

"Pip," Brannoc called from the deck, "if you fall out of my rigging again I'm not diving after you."

"You'd dive after me."

"I'd think about it first. There's a difference, and it's an important one for a diver to understand."

But he came up the shrouds anyway, because Brannoc always did, grumbling like a man twice his size instead of half his heart. Pip pointed, and watched his face do the thing it did when he forgot to be gruff: go quiet and open, like a door left unlatched.

Spires. Dozens of them, rising out of the flooded plain like the fingers of drowned giants reaching up to check the weather. Some broke the surface, crusted white with salt-bloom and birdlime. Others stood just beneath, bent and wavering in the current, so that the whole city seemed to be waving at them — or drowning slowly, politely, without wanting to make a fuss about it.

"That's not a reef," Pip said.

"No," Brannoc said. "That's Vessia."

* * *

They anchored in the shadow of what Wren determined, squinting through her cracked spyglass, had once been a customs house. She said this with the particular satisfaction of a woman who had spent thirty years mapping places that didn't want to be mapped and had finally found one that couldn't argue back.

"It's all still here," she kept saying, turning in a slow circle on deck. "The flood lines, the bell towers, the — look, you can see the canal gates. Sable, the canal gates are *labeled*. Someone maintained these charts. Someone wanted them found."

"Someone also buried a compass in iron and paid a fleet to keep it hidden," Sable said. He stood at the bow with the compass open in his palm, and Pip noticed he hadn't looked away from it since dawn. The needle didn't waver. It pointed straight down and slightly ahead, toward the tallest spire, the one with the broken crown.

"What's it pointing at?" Pip asked.

Sable was quiet long enough that Wren answered for him. "Whatever he lost. That's how it works." She said it gently, but gently the way a surgeon is gentle.

Pip had learned the crew's griefs the way she'd learned their knots — by watching hands. Sable's hands were steady on the compass, but his jaw was set like a man walking into weather. She thought about asking. She decided against it. Some doors you let people open themselves; slamming them open only breaks the hinges.

Instead she said, "I want to dive."

Everyone turned to look at her.

"You're eleven," Wren said.

"Ten and three-quarters."

"You're ten and three-quarters, and the last time you went in the water past your knees I had to fish you out of the bilge."

"That was *on purpose*, I was checking the hull—"

"She dives with me or not at all," Brannoc said, and the deck went quiet, because Brannoc almost never spent words defending anyone, and when he did, he spent them like a man paying a debt. "She stays on the line. She keeps the rope count. If I tug twice, she hauls. Can you do that?"

Pip lifted her chin. "I've been keeping count since the trading reach. Nobody asked me to. I just did."

"I know," Brannoc said. "That's why I'm asking now."

* * *

The water took her breath the way cold always did — one sharp theft, then a bargain. Pip hung on the anchor line in the green half-light while Brannoc sank below her, a dark shape trailing bubbles, and she counted.

One. Two. Three.

The city opened beneath her like a book held underwater. Streets ran where streets had run, cobbled and furred with weed, and through every doorway swam schools of silver fish, hundreds upon hundreds, pouring through windows and archways as if the whole population had simply changed shape one day and decided to stay. A bell tower leaned overhead, its bell furred green, silent for forty years and somehow still waiting to be rung. A fountain stood in a square, dry as bone even down here, and around it stone benches where stone citizens might have sat.

It was beautiful. That was the thing nobody warned you about drowned things — that they got more beautiful, not less. Grief with water in it turned to glass.

Brannoc tugged once: *found something.* Then, after a moment, twice: *haul.*

She hauled, hand over hand, her arms burning, until his head broke the surface beside her and he spat river and said, in a voice carefully stripped of everything he felt, which meant it was full of it:

"There's a door. Under the spire. Bronze, sealed, marked with the seal from the chest. And Pip—" he paused, and she saw him deciding whether to tell her, and telling her, because that was new too, that was three weeks old, the telling-her, "—there's a mooring ring beside it. Fleet-forged. Old, but fleet-forged."

"So they've been here."

"Somebody has. Forty years ago, maybe. Maybe last month." He wiped his face. "We go down together, careful, no touching anything that looks like it was put there on purpose. Understood?"

"Understood," Pip said, and meant it, mostly, right up until she didn't.

* * *

The archive door was exactly as Brannoc had described: bronze, tall as two men, worked over with the same seven-pointed star that was stamped on the compass's case. Kelp hung from its lintel like funeral bunting. When Pip put her bare hand against it — just to feel it, just to say she'd touched a nation — the metal was warm.

Warm. Forty years underwater, and warm.

"Brannoc," she started to say.

He was already looking past her shoulder, up along the wall, and his eyes had gone flat and hard in the way she'd learned meant *danger, old danger, the kind he'd sailed away from.* She followed his gaze.

A cable. Thin, tarred, running from the mooring ring up through the green murk toward the surface, taut as a harp string. And looped around it, every few fathoms, small brass bells — signal bells, the kind the fleet used to mark salvage claims, the kind Brannoc had spent six years ringing and two years trying to forget.

"It's a trip-line," he breathed, bubbles streaming. "Old claim-line. They strung the whole site. Don't—"

Pip's other hand, braced against the wall for balance, found a second cable.

The bells rang.

Even underwater it carried — a bright, silvery shivering that ran down the line and out across the drowned city, bell to bell to bell, door to window to leaning tower, until the whole capital seemed to be laughing at them. Fish scattered from the doorways in silver explosions. Somewhere above, faint as memory, Wren's voice shouted something across the deck of the *Kindly Liar.*

Brannoc grabbed her wrist and kicked for the surface, and they came up together in a rush of spray and shouting.

"—sail! Sail on the horizon!" Wren was pointing east, where the evening sun was going down red as a wound. "Black hull, three masts, war-rigged. Pip, whose line did you touch?"

"The one that was *warm*—"

"They strung it fresh," Brannoc said grimly, hauling her over the gunwale. "Tar was soft. That's not a forty-year-old claim-line, that's a *trap*, laid and baited and waiting. They knew the compass would come home eventually. They let us lead ourselves here."

Sable stood at the stern, compass open in his hand, watching the black ship grow against the sunset. He didn't look afraid. He looked like a man confirming something he'd suspected for five tides, and Pip hated how calm he was, and loved him for it, and wanted to shake him.

"That's the flagship," Brannoc said quietly. "*Meridian's Wrath*. Admiral Ash herself."

The wind came off the water carrying the smell of salt and old stone, and far below them, in the green silence, the drowned city waited with its warm bronze door and its silver fish and its forty years of secrets, patient as a snare.

"We can't outrun her," Wren said. "Not loaded, not leaking, not with night coming."

"No," Sable agreed. He closed the compass. "So we don't run."

Pip looked at him. He was smiling, and it was the worst smile she'd ever seen on him — small and sharp and entirely certain.

"Ash wants the compass," he said. "Let's give her a reason to come get it."
