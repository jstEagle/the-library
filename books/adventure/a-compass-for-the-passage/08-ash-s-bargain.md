## 8. Ash's Bargain

*Sable*

The flagship came out of the morning mist the way a verdict comes out of a courtroom — slowly, inevitably, and with everyone pretending they'd known all along.

"Meridian," Brannoc said from the bow, his voice flat as a becalmed sea. "That's *Vigilant*. That's her ship."

"How can you tell?" Pip asked. "It's a big gray boat. They're all big gray boats."

"You can tell because she flies no pennant," Brannoc said. "Admirals who want to be remembered fly colors. Admirals who want to be feared fly none."

Sable stood at the tiller of their leaky, beloved, entirely outmatched skiff and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the sixth tide began. The *Vigilant* had forty guns. They had a crossbow, a kettle, and Wren's collection of maps that were wrong in interesting ways.

"We could dive now," Wren said quietly. She was already coiling rope, already calculating depths in that way she had, lips moving over numbers like prayers. "The archive entrance is forty feet down. If we go under before they board—"

"They'll put divers in after us," Brannoc said. "Fleet doctrine. I wrote half of it."

"Then we run," Pip said. "We're very good at running. It's basically our whole thing."

Sable looked at the needle in its brass housing, resting in his palm. It didn't waver. It pointed past the flagship, past the drowned spires, toward whatever waited below — and it pointed, he was beginning to understand, at him.

"No," he said. "We let them board."

Three faces turned toward him with varying degrees of horror.

"Let them—" Pip started.

"She wants the compass," Sable said. "She's chased it across six tides and burned a customs station for it. A woman like that doesn't sink what she wants to hold. We come aboard alive, we learn what she knows, and Brannoc—" he glanced at the diver, "—you said you knew her. Knew her habits."

"I knew her routines," Brannoc corrected. "Whether she still keeps them is another question."

"Then we find out." Sable tucked the compass into his coat, against his heart, where it sat like a second heartbeat. "And if it goes badly — Wren, you know what to do."

Wren nodded once. Nobody asked Pip what he should do, because everyone knew Pip would do exactly what Pip always did, which was the thing nobody had authorized.

The *Vigilant*'s shadow swallowed them whole.

* * *

*Ash*

Corvane Ash had boarded two hundred vessels in her career, and she had learned long ago that you could read a captain by how he met your gangway. The proud ones came up fast, chins high, dying on the first step. The broken ones crawled. The clever ones sent someone else.

The desert ranger came up at an ordinary pace, an ordinary expression, hands visible and empty, as though boarding an admiral's flagship were something a man simply did on a Tuesday. Behind him came a woman clutching a map case like a shield, a broad diver whose eyes catalogued every gun crew with professional tenderness, and a child who was attempting to look invisible and achieving instead the effect of a lantern in a cellar.

Ash disliked him immediately. It was the ordinariness. Men who looked ordinary were the ones who took things from you when you weren't watching.

"Sable Coldharbour," she said.

"You have me at a disadvantage, Admiral."

"I make it my business never to be at one." She let the silence work. Around them her deck crew went about their business with the smooth indifference of people who had seen prisoners brought aboard before and knew how most of those stories ended. "You've been carrying something of mine across six tides of river. I'd like it back."

"It was never yours."

"Nothing is anyone's," Ash said pleasantly. "Possession is just theft that's been allowed to rest. But some thefts are load-bearing, ranger. Remove them and everything comes down." She extended a gloved hand. "The compass."

He didn't move. She watched him weigh her — not her guns, not her marines, but *her*, the way a man weighs a current before swimming. It was, she admitted privately, the correct instinct.

"You knew my mother," he said.

There it was. Ash had wondered how much the needle had shown him, how far back into the drowned country of his childhood it had dragged him. She had prepared three openings for this conversation. He had skipped all of them and gone straight for the wound.

She gestured, and her marines withdrew beyond earshot. Even admirals, she had found, preferred their crimes unwitnessed.

"Mara Coldharbour," Ash said. "Cartographer-second-class, Vessian Survey Corps. Best field hand I ever commanded, and I include myself. She could find water in stone and truth in ledgers." She smiled without warmth. "You have her patience. I saw it on the hardpan, through my glass, three days ago. You sat still for four hours waiting for a thief. Mara would have done five."

"Don't say her name like you knew her."

"I knew her better than you did. You were seven when she vanished." Ash stepped closer, boots ringing on the deck. "So let me educate you, since the river apparently won't stop until someone does. Forty years ago Vessia existed. You were taught otherwise; that was policy, not ignorance. And forty years ago, in the last autumn of the war, I gave an order at a place called Serel's Ford. Eleven hundred people were in that town. Eleven hundred people are not in it now."

The wind moved. Somewhere above them a sail cracked and settled. The ranger's face did the thing she'd seen soldiers' faces do on battlefields — the long slow collapse behind the eyes, the moment the ground gives way and a man discovers what he's actually standing on.

"You ordered a massacre," he said.

"I ordered a *solution*, and the massacre was the cost, and then I helped bury the word 'Vessia' so deep that no child would ever grow up asking questions about it. Your mother helped. Not the killing — she never knew, not until after. But the erasure. The maps. She redrew her own country into nothing, ranger, because I showed her what happens to nations that insist on being remembered." Ash let that sit. "And then she ran. Took her son, took her guilt, and disappeared into the desert. I let her go. Sentiment. My one extravagance."

"You're lying."

"Probably," Ash agreed. "But you'll never know, will you? Because the only proof is forty feet beneath that green water, in an archive your mother helped seal, guarded by a compass built by people who believed truth should always be findable again." She held out her hand once more. "Give it to me, and you'll never need to know. Some men live happily on solid ground. Others dig."

* * *

*Sable*

The strange thing, Sable thought later, was how reasonable she made it sound.

Because she wasn't finished. She walked him along the rail while her crew pretended not to listen, and she laid it out like a merchant laying out cloth — here the bolt, there the price.

"The fleet rules this river because the Meridian Compact lets it rule this river, and the Compact lets it because the Compact's founders needed Vessia to stay dead. I am old, ranger. Older than my lies. In ten years, perhaps fifteen, I will die, and the Compact will need a new keeper of the secret — someone with my stomach and none of my sentiment." She turned to face him fully. "I'm offering you the chair. Command of the fleet. The salvage rights to an entire drowned nation. Every wreck, every vault, every sunken street — yours to raise or keep drowned, as wisdom dictates."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, the archive stays sealed. The compass comes to me. And Serel's Ford remains a bend in the river that never meant anything to anyone." Her voice softened, which was somehow worse. "Think of what you could *do*, Sable Coldharbour. You could raise Vessia's cities stone by stone. You could give the castoffs of three nations a country. You could build the thing they erased — bigger, better, honest in every way but one. One lie, kept in one flooded room. Is that so heavy a price for a resurrection?"

And here was the terrible part, the part he would carry home like a splinter: it wasn't.

He felt it rise in him, warm and golden as lamplight — the vision of it. Green water parted, spires rising dripping into sunlight. His mother's name spoken aloud in a country that existed again. Himself at the head of it, the lost son returned, the desert ranger made king of everything grief had taken from him. He could almost hear the bells. He could almost see her face, if she were alive to see it, proud—

*If she were alive.*

The thought landed like cold water. Because that was the trick of it, wasn't it? The compass pointed at what you'd lost, and Ash was offering him a kingdom-sized way to stop losing things. And every man who ever built an empire on a grave had stood exactly here, feeling exactly this warm, telling himself exactly these stories. Grief was just ambition that hadn't found its excuse yet.

He thought of Pip, who had stowed away because a skiff going somewhere was better than a dock going nowhere. Of Wren, who'd been ruined for drawing one honest map too many. Of Brannoc, who'd defected rather than write the other half of the fleet's ugly doctrine. Four castoffs on a leaking boat, and somehow more of a nation than anything Ash was selling.

"No," Sable said.

Ash sighed, and the warmth left her face like a tide going out. "I was afraid you'd say that. Mara would have said it too. It's why I liked her." She raised two fingers, and behind him Sable heard the soft ring of steel — marines closing, unhurried, professional. "Take him below. Take the compass. And the others—"

That was when the *Vigilant*'s stern shuddered.

Not a cannon. Something deeper — a groan from the hull itself, from below the waterline, the sound of a great ship discovering it has been introduced to something sharp. Brannoc, Sable realized, and his sabotage schedule, and the phrase *I knew her routines* suddenly meaning *and I knew exactly where she moors her pride.*

Alarms began to scream. Ash's head snapped toward the sound, and for one unguarded instant Corvane Ash looked less like an admiral than like a woman hearing a noise downstairs in the dark.

Sable moved. Not toward the rail — toward *her*, because the compass was against his heart and her fingers were reaching for it, and because Wren was already over the side with the rope, and because somewhere below, small and reckless, a boy was diving toward an entrance that was about to become the most contested forty feet of water in the world.

The rail hit his hip. The green water rose up.

As he fell, he heard Ash shouting orders — and underneath the orders, unmistakably, her voice saying his mother's name like a curse, like a prayer, like a debt.

Then the river closed over him, and took him down toward the truth.
