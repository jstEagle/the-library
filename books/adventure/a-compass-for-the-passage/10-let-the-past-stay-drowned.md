## 10. Let the Past Stay Drowned

The flagship went down the way old empires do — slowly at first, then all at once, with a groan that Sable felt in his teeth rather than heard.

He stood on the skiff's deck, water streaming from his hair, the archive's pages sealed in oilcloth against his chest, and watched the *Meridian's Pride* settle by the stern. Brannoc's sabotage had done its work below the waterline, neat and thorough as everything the man did. Fires still burned along her rail, painting the green river orange. Her crew crowded into longboats, and none of them looked back.

Ash did not come out of them.

Sable had rowed to the wreck himself — against Wren's shouting, against Pip clinging to his arm with a grip far stronger than her size suggested — and found the admiral standing on her tilting quarterdeck like a woman waiting for a coach that was never coming. Water to her knees now. Her uniform coat, forty years of braid and blood, hung soaked and heavy as armor.

"Take my hand," he'd said. "The river doesn't care who you were."

"The river is the only one left who does care," she'd answered, and she had almost smiled. "You have her eyes, boy. Did anyone ever tell you that? Your mother could look at a thing she wanted and make it feel ashamed of itself." She had glanced down at the rising water, then back at him, and her voice had gone quiet in a way he'd never heard from her — quiet the way a room goes quiet after a door closes for the last time. "I burned a nation so no one would ask me what I'd done. You're about to do the opposite. One of us will be remembered kindly. I've made my peace with which."

"Ash—"

"Corvane," she corrected. "You may as well know what to curse."

Then the deck tilted further, and she waved him off with the same crisp gesture she must have used to send divers to their deaths, and Sable had rowed away because staying would only have meant drowning beside her, and she deserved better company than that.

Now the last of the superstructure slipped under, and the river closed over it without ceremony. A few bubbles. A drift of smoke. Somewhere beneath, in the dark where fish schooled through drowned doorways, Vessia's capital kept its silence, and Corvane Ash kept hers.

Pip stood at the rail beside him. "She really wouldn't come?"

"She really wouldn't."

Pip considered this with the grave seriousness she gave to everything, including stolen apples and other people's boots. "Good," she decided. "I didn't want to share the skiff with her anyway. She smelled like a fancy funeral."

"Pip."

"What? I'm being respectful *quietly*."

* * *

They anchored that night in the lee of a half-drowned bell tower, its bronze tongue long since looted, and Sable sat alone in the bow with the compass and the pages and tried to decide what kind of man he was going to be.

It would have been easy. That was the terrible part, the part he knew he'd carry for the rest of his life: how *easy* it would have been. The compass pointed toward what you had lost, and what Sable had lost was a village, a mother, a name spoken softly in another language at bedtime. He could have raised Vessia from the water. He could have been the returned son, the avenger-king, the living proof that erasure fails. There were survivors scattered through a dozen ports who would have wept to see him. There were ships to command and wrongs to right and a whole drowned country's worth of grief that would have happily made him its face.

And underneath all of it, wearing a crown, there would have been a boy in the desert still running from the sound of soldiers.

He turned the compass over in his hands. The brass was warm from his pocket, though the night was cold. The needle swung gently, patiently, toward the sunken archive — toward his mother's handwriting, toward the truth of the massacre, toward everything he had spent nine tides bleeding to find.

"You don't point north," he told it. "You never did. You just point at the hole in a person and wait for them to fall in."

Wren appeared at his elbow without seeming to arrive, the way she did. She settled onto the thwart beside him and studied the compass over the top of her spectacles, which were cracked now and held together with waxed thread, and which she refused to replace because, she claimed, the cracks improved her judgment.

"I've been thinking about its maker," she said. "Whoever built this understood something most cartographers never learn. A map tells you where things are. It can't tell you what they *mean*. So they built meaning into an instrument instead." She tapped the casing. "Brilliant. And cruel. And — I think — meant to be broken."

Sable looked at her.

"Think about it," Wren said. "If they'd wanted a king, they'd have built a crown. If they'd wanted revenge, they'd have built a weapon. They built a thing that finds lost truths and then — what? Hands them to whoever grieves hardest? No. They built it so the truth could always be found *again*. Once. By someone. And then—" she made a small, decisive gesture, the same one she used when striking false coastlines from charts, "—its work is finished. Instruments aren't supposed to become relics. Relics are just maps people stop reading."

The needle trembled between his fingers, pointing faithfully at the hole in him.

"Yeah," Sable said. "I think you're right."

He set it on the thwart, drew his knife, and broke it.

Not dramatically — he was too tired for drama, and besides, Pip was asleep ten feet away and would have been furious to miss it. He simply put the blade against the seam of the casing and leaned, and the brass parted with a soft crack like ice giving way in spring. Inside, beneath the needle's pivot, he found what he somehow already knew he'd find: a recessed chamber, and in it, a sliver of paper gone brown with age, folded small as a seed.

He unfolded it by lantern light. His mother's handwriting. Four words in Vessian, and beneath them, in trade-tongue so that any finder could read:

*The truth keeps better than gold.*

Below that, a list of ports. Eleven of them. Scattered across every chart Wren owned.

"She always planned for this," Sable said quietly. "Even before the end. She knew someone would need to find it, and she knew one copy of anything dies with its keeper." He laughed — a rough, surprised sound. "She hid instructions inside the thing everyone would kill for. The treasure map was inside the treasure."

"That," said Wren, "is the finest piece of cartographic spite I have ever encountered. I would have liked her enormously."

* * *

They spent four days distributing the pages, and it went nothing like a conspiracy and everything like a family errand, which Sable supposed was the same thing.

At Saltmere Reach, Wren charmed a harbor clerk into filing the massacre record among official shipping manifests, where it would be discovered eventually by some diligent auditor and become, irreversibly, paperwork. Paperwork, Wren explained, was the most indestructible substance known to civilization; empires fell, but ledgers persisted.

At Gullwater, Brannoc — who had spent twenty years learning exactly which fleet officers could be bought and which could be blackmailed, and had a talent for telling them apart — ensured copies reached three rival admirals who despised each other, guaranteeing that no single fleet could bury the truth again without the others crying foul.

At Ternwick, Pip stole into a printing house and left a full copy on the pressman's desk with a note reading *you didn't get this from nobody*, which was, she insisted, airtight anonymity.

By the time they turned the skiff upriver toward open desert country, the truth of Vessia was in eleven ports, in eleven hands, in eleven kinds of ink. No one could drown it now. No one could crown it either. It belonged to clerks and printers and squabbling admirals — to the ordinary machinery of the living world, which was, Sable thought, exactly where his mother had wanted it.

The compass pieces he kept. Needle, casing, the little seed-paper with her handwriting. Not as relics, he told himself. As ballast. Every ship needs some.

* * *

They ran aground on a sandbar two days from home, which was fitting, because the skiff had been trying to die since Ternwick and had finally worked up the courage.

While Brannoc waded around the hull swearing in three languages, Pip spread herself across the bow and announced she was starving, and Wren unrolled a fresh chart on the thwart and weighted its corners with boots.

"Here," she said, tapping a stretch of blank water east of the drowned capital. "Everyone charts this as empty. Empty means unexamined. Unexamined means interesting." She glanced up at Sable, and behind the cracked spectacles her eyes were bright with the particular mischief that had gotten her disgraced from three institutions and would, he suspected, get her honored by a fourth someday. "Well, captain? We've got a hull that needs patching, a crew of four, and no nation, no fleet, and no dead empire asking anything of us. Where shall we go?"

Sable looked at the chart. Blank water, waiting to be named. He thought of his mother's four words, keeping better than gold in eleven ports. He thought of Ash going down with her crimes, and of the village he'd fled, and of how the desert had taught him that everything you love is something you'll lose — and how these three impossible people had spent nine tides proving the lesson wrong.

"We go everywhere," he said. "Slowly. We chart it properly. And we stop at every port that has decent food, because our cook is a twelve-year-old who steals seasoning."

"I *procure* it," said Pip, without opening her eyes.

"Procure away." He took the pencil Wren offered and, above the blank eastern water, wrote the first new name on any map in forty years — not Vessia, nothing that grand, just a sandbar's honest name, *Coldharbour's Rest*, because Brannoc was still standing on it and yelling.

The compass piece in his pocket was quiet. It didn't point anywhere anymore. It didn't need to.

Everything he'd lost was honored, and drowned, and free.

Everything he'd found was sitting right here, arguing about dinner.

"Captain," Brannoc called from the sandbar, "when you're quite finished drawing, some of us are patching a ship."

"Some of us," Pip muttered, "are *starving*."

"Some of us," Wren said, already sketching depth-soundings, "are making history."

Sable smiled, picked up a second pencil, and started charting new water.
