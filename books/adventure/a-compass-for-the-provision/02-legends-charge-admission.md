## 2. Legends Charge Admission

The Siren's Rest smelled of peat smoke, spilled stout, and forty years of fishermen arguing about the same three topics. I counted it a good pub on those grounds alone. Good pubs are honest about what they are. They don't pretend to be gastropubs or wine bars or community hubs. They are rooms where people go to be warm and loud, and if you sit quietly in the corner with a notebook, they will forget you're there, which is the closest thing to invisibility a living woman can buy.

I took the corner table by the window, ordered the fish pie because the menu said *homemade* and the handwriting on the specials board said *whatever Marta caught*, and opened my survey notes as a prop. I'd learned long ago that a woman alone in a pub with an open notebook is either ignored or interrogated, and the difference is usually the notebook. Close it, and you're a stranger. Leave it open, and you're a professional, and professionals get left alone. Mostly.

The storm had moved on overnight, dragging its skirts east toward the shipping lanes, and Penhallow was out in the weak gold light assessing the damage the way villages do — in relays, at the harbor wall, with a great deal of pointing. From my window I could see the breakwater where old men were measuring the tide's appetite against the height of the sea wall, and beyond it, the headland where the dune had come apart like a dropped ice cream.

I had gone back that morning, before the pub, before the light was properly up. Professional habit. You always revisit a site the day after exposure, because the sea giveth and the sea taketh away on roughly a six-hour cycle, and whatever it showed you at dawn may be a memory by noon.

The cave mouth was still there. Smaller than it had seemed in the storm light, which is the way of most things. A low arch of dark stone at the base of the headland, maybe four feet at its crown, the floor of it wet sand scattered with wrack and one very indignant crab. I'd stood at the threshold with my boots in the wash of the retreating tide and felt that pull again — the one I'd felt last night, the one I didn't have a clean name for. Not curiosity exactly. Curiosity is a clean instrument. This had grit in the works.

I'd taken photographs. I'd measured the arch, logged the strata of the exposed dune face, noted the midden shells pressed into the bank above the entrance — limpet, whelk, the odd oyster, suggesting someone had eaten well there once, and often. And then, because the tide table on my phone said I had maybe ninety minutes before the water started walking back toward the cave, I'd done the sensible thing.

I'd gone to find out what the locals knew.

* * *

The sensible thing, it turned out, was sitting at the bar.

"You're the one been poking round the headland," said the woman behind the bar. She was perhaps sixty, built like a mooring post, with forearms that suggested she'd hauled more than pint glasses in her time. Her name, according to the chalked rota above the till, was Marta. The fish pie was hers. I was already inclined to like her.

"Coastal survey," I said. "I'm mapping the erosion along this stretch. Your dune's been losing about a meter a year for the last decade, by the look of it."

"Been losing bits of itself since before your lot invented meters." She pulled me a second pint I hadn't ordered and set it down with the authority of a woman who does not take returns. "You went up to the arch this morning."

It wasn't a question. In villages this size, the tide table and the gossip run on the same clock.

"I did. Interesting midden deposits. Possibly Iron Age, possibly earlier."

"Mm." Marta wiped the bar in a slow arc, which I would come to understand was how she bought time to decide how much truth a stranger could carry. "And did you go in?"

"Not far. The tide was coming back."

"Good instinct." She leaned her elbows on the bar, and the room's noise seemed to dip a register, the way a room does when the story is about to start and everyone in it already knows the story. "Though I'd have thought your father would've told you not to."

The pint glass stopped halfway to my mouth. "I'm sorry?"

"Montrose." She said it the way you'd say a word you'd kept in a drawer for years and weren't sure still fit. "Elias Montrose. Came through here — oh, must be fifteen years back now. Tall man, talked like a book, bought the same fish pie you're eating and told me it needed more anchovy, which it does not." She looked at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic — my face against a memory fifteen years old — and arrive at the answer. "You've his chin."

I put the glass down. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Twelve years of practice at this, apparently, and the practice holds even when the floor moves. "He was here," I said. "Penhallow."

"Came two springs running. Asked a lot of questions about the cave. The Provision, we call it." She said the word with a small, deliberate weight, the way you'd set down something breakable. "Then he stopped coming."

The pub noise came back all at once, and I realized how completely it had suspended. A card game at the corner table had gone quiet. Two men by the fire had turned on their stools. I was being watched with the frank, unembarrassed attention of people who had known this story longer than I had.

"He's an archaeologist," I heard myself say. "Was. Is. He — we —" I stopped, recalibrated, started again. "I haven't seen him in twelve years."

Something moved through the room at that, a low shifting, like wind through barley. Marta's face did something complicated — sympathy and vindication wrestling for the same ground.

"Well," she said finally. "Then you'd best hear it proper. Nesta! Bring your tea over. You'll want the old version, and Nesta's got the oldest."

* * *

Nesta Vey was ninety if she was a day, and she took the bench beside me with the ceremony of a queen assuming a throne. Her tea arrived in a mug with a faded picture of a lifeboat on it. Her eyes, in a face gone soft and creased as old paper, were sharp as fishhooks.

"You'll be the Montrose girl," she said. Not a question either. That seemed to be the local dialect — statements wearing question marks as decoration, or the other way round.

"Yara," I said.

"Yara." She tasted it. "He talked about you. Second spring he was here. Sat where you're sitting, near enough, and told me he had a daughter who could read a section drawing before she could ride a bike." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Talked about you the way men talk about things they've lost their receipt for."

I held very still. This is a skill I have. You hold still and you let the words go past you like weather, and you file them, and you don't let your face do anything that costs you.

"The cave," I said. "Tell me about the cave."

Nesta wrapped both hands around the lifeboat mug. Around us, the pub had arranged itself into an audience without anyone officially deciding to. Even the card game had stalled, its players holding their cards face-down like men at a funeral.

"The legend goes back past the church records," she said. "Past the chapel records. Back to when this coast was smiths and smugglers and the two trades got along fine, being that both need a fire and a secret." She sipped. "There was a smith here, generations back — the name's lost, or I've lost it, which comes to the same — who made three compasses. Not ordinary ones. Brass, with the needle set in a ring of letters instead of the usual points. And the story says each compass doesn't point north. It points at what you're owed."

I laughed. I'm not proud of it. It came out of me before I could stop it, a short, sharp bark of professional reflex, and I felt the room cool toward me by several degrees.

"Sorry," I said. "It's just — magnetic anomalies. Lodestone deposits in the headland. There's a band of iron-rich rock through this whole coast, it plays havoc with compasses, it's in the survey literature —"

"You've his laugh, too," Nesta said mildly, and the room warmed again, and I understood I had just been forgiven for the crime of being my father's daughter, provisionally, pending review. "The story's not about the metal, girl. The story's about the cave. They say the Provision shows you what you're owed. Not what you want — the two aren't the same, and the cave knows the difference. Fishermen lost at sea whose wives came asking after them. Farmers in the famine years. A man from up-country who came looking for gold and walked out having found his brother's grave, which is what he was owed, though it's not what he'd have put on the list." She looked at me over the rim of the mug. "The cave charges admission. The price is that you have to actually want the truth. Most people don't. Most people want a version."

"That's a very sophisticated theology for a sea cave," I said, more gently this time.

"It's a very old village," Nesta said. "We've had time to get sophisticated."

"And my father? What did he make of it?"

Marta answered from the bar, not looking up from the glasses she was polishing. "He made of it what you just made of it. Lodestone. Anomaly. He gave a talk in this room — stood right where you're sitting and drew on the back of a poster with a marker pen. Explained the iron band in the rock, the needle deflection, the whole thing. Very kind about it. Very patient with us and our stories." She set down the glass. "Then he went out to the headland that night, on his own, at low tide. And he came back the next morning looking like a man who'd been shown something. And the spring after that, he came back and started asking about the high passes. Sarn Ddu. And then he stopped coming, like I said."

The name went through me like a fishhook of its own.

Sarn Ddu. Black causeway, or black cairn, depending on your root. I knew the name the way you know a word from a language you studied as a child — not the meaning, just the shape of it in the mouth. I was almost certain I'd heard it before, in a context I couldn't place, and the not-placing itched.

"Did he say why?" I asked. "Why the passes?"

"He said the cave was a door," Marta said. "And doors are no good unless you know what's on the other side."

* * *

I don't remember deciding to stay for another pint. I remember the pub emptying around me by degrees — the card game resuming, the fire men going home, Nesta Vey departing with a nod that felt like a benediction or a warning, possibly both — and I remember sitting with my cold fish pie and my notebook, which I had opened at some point and on which I had written, in handwriting less steady than I'd like:

*Three compasses. Needle points at what you're owed. E.M. — two springs — then Sarn Ddu — then nothing.*

And underneath it, before I could stop myself:

*He was forty miles from where I was working the summer of the second dig. Forty miles. I checked the ferry timbers once. It would have taken him a day.*

Twelve years, and I had built a whole architecture of distance between us. He was in one country and I was in another; he was in one life and I was in another; the miles were the point, the miles were the mercy. And now the miles had collapsed to a single pub room, a single bench, a single mug with a lifeboat on it, and the architecture was coming down, and under it was just the old raw fact: he had been *here*. Drinking this beer. Eating this pie. Telling strangers I could read a section drawing before I could ride a bike. And he had not — the thought arrived with its full weight, and I sat with it while the fire ticked — he had not once thought to send a postcard.

I was still sitting there when the door opened and the cold came in with it.

* * *

He was maybe my age or a little older, tall in the way of men who carry things for a living, with a weathered face and the specific economy of movement I'd learned to recognize in people who worked mountains or water and had been let down by both. He crossed to the bar, nodded to Marta, and then, instead of taking his pint to the fire like a sensible local, he turned and looked directly at me.

"You're the surveyor," he said.

"I'm a surveyor."

"Tomas Vey." He didn't offer a hand; he offered the name, flat, like a fact. "Nesta's grandson. She rang me." A pause. "She rings me when strangers start asking about the Provision."

"And you're the village welcome committee."

"I'm the one who pulls people out of it." He set his pint down but didn't sit, which I took to be tactical. "The cave you were at this morning. You went in."

"Four feet. The tide was turning."

"Four feet is where it starts being a cave and stops being an overhang." He said it without drama, a man reporting instrument readings. "The floor drops after the first bend. There's a channel through it that fills faster than the beach does — the tide comes in from two directions, the front and a fissure on the north side. We've had three drowned in my lifetime. Two of them knew the coast. One of them was a rescue swimmer."

I held his gaze. "I read tide tables for a living."

"You read *beach* tide tables. This isn't a beach, it's a lung." He took a slow pull of his pint, and for the first time I saw something in his face besides the flat professional warning — something that might have been tiredness, or might have been the specific exhaustion of a man who has given this speech before and watched it fail. "The legend says the cave shows you what you're owed. You know what it's actually owed, every time? Respect. And it collects."

"Then it's lucky I'm not here for the legend. I'm here for the midden."

"Nobody's here for the midden." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "Your father wasn't here for the midden either."

The room was emptying; Marta had begun stacking chairs with the deliberate loudness of a woman who wanted the next part of this conversation to happen somewhere else, or wanted it witnessed, and I genuinely couldn't tell which.

"Mr. Vey," I said. "I appreciate the concern. I do this work in places a lot less friendly than a cave with a footpath to it. I check the tables, I carry a radio, I don't take stupid risks, because stupid risks are how surveyors end up as statistics. What I *am* going to do is come back at the next spring low and document that site properly, because there's a midden in that bank that predates anything in your county record, and if the sea takes it this winter, it's gone, and that's not a legend, that's just loss." I stood, and was annoyed to find I had to steady myself on the table, the pints having been more numerous than my plan required. "If you'd like to stand on the beach and watch me do it, you're welcome to. Bring a flask."

He looked at me for a long moment. Whatever he was looking for, he didn't seem to find it, and that seemed to bother him more than finding it would have.

"Spring low's Thursday," he said finally. "Four in the afternoon. Window's maybe ninety minutes, and the light goes by five." He picked up his pint. "I'll be there."

"You don't have to be."

"No," he agreed. "But the cave charges admission, and I'm the one who's had to pay the refunds."

He went out into the dark, and I stood there a moment with the fire at my back, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding all evening. Thursday. Ninety minutes. A low arch, a dropping floor, a lung of a cave.

And somewhere above all of it, a black cairn called Sarn Ddu, in a language of iron and letters, a needle that pointed at what you were owed — and a man who had stood where I was standing, heard the same story, laughed the same laugh, and gone up the mountain anyway, and never come back down.

I closed the notebook. The miles were gone; I'd checked. All that was left was Thursday.

I went back to my room and set two alarms.
