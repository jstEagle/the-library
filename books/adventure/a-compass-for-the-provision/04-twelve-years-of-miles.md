## 4. Twelve Years of Miles

I didn't sleep that night. The compass sat on the desk of my rented room like a small brass accusation, its needle swinging in slow, patient arcs, and every time I closed my eyes I was nine years old again, standing in the doorway of a dig house in the Peloponnese, watching my father load a Land Rover at four in the morning.

Here is what nobody tells you about abandonment: it doesn't feel like a door slamming. It feels like a tide going out. Slow, quiet, almost polite. You stand there on the wet sand and think, *any minute now it will come back*, because tides come back, that's the whole arrangement, that's the deal the sea makes with everyone. And then you look up and realize the sea has moved to another coast entirely.

* * *

The digs came first. Before the leaving, there were the years of leaving-together, which is a different animal altogether.

My father was not a warm man, but he was an attentive one, and attention, to a child, can pass for love the way fool's gold passes for coin at a distance. He taught me to read soil stains before I could read sentences. He taught me that a trowel should be held like a question, not a shovel — *you're asking the ground to tell you something, Yara, you don't get to decide the answer* — and he taught me to measure everything in miles.

That last one was accidental, I think. But it stuck.

We measured in miles because miles were honest. Miles didn't lie about how long something would take. When he said the site was forty miles from the nearest hospital, he meant: be careful with your hands. When he said we'd driven three hundred miles since the last proper meal, he meant: eat the biscuits now, don't save them for a tomorrow that may not include biscuits. Miles were a language of care disguised as logistics, and I learned to speak it fluently, because it was the only dialect of affection my father had ever bothered to learn.

The summer I was eleven we worked a Roman villa outside Split, and I remember him crouched over a mosaic floor at dusk, brushing dirt off a dolphin with a paintbrush he'd stolen from my mother's things — she'd been dead two years by then; he never returned her belongings, just carried them from country to country like ballast — and he said, without looking up:

"You know what archaeology is, Yara? It's the study of what people couldn't bear to throw away."

I thought that was very profound. I was eleven. I wrote it in the margin of my notebook and underlined it twice.

It took me twenty years to understand he'd been describing himself.

* * *

The night he left, we were in Greece. The Peloponnese dig house smelled of dust and lemon oil and the particular staleness of rooms where men have argued about stratigraphy for six weeks straight. I remember the argument — not its content, but its shape. Raised voices behind a closed door, his voice among them, and then his voice alone, and then silence, which is always the loudest thing a house can say.

He came out with his duffel already packed. That's the detail that still catches in me like a fishhook. The bag was packed. Which meant the decision had been made hours ago, days ago maybe, while I was sifting pottery sherds ten meters away, happy, humming, believing in the permanence of tents and fathers.

"Where's the site?" I asked. Because that's how our conversations went. Everything was a site. Everything had coordinates.

"There's been a change of plan," he said.

I have replayed those five words so many times they've worn smooth, like a pebble in a pocket. A change of plan. Not *I'm sorry*. Not *this is hard*. Not even a lie, which would at least have been an effort. Just the flat administrative language of a man updating his itinerary, and his daughter filed under miscellaneous.

"I'll send for you," he said. "When I'm settled."

He did not send for me. He sent, over the following twelve years, exactly four postcards — Athens, Cairo, Valletta, and one with no postmark at all, just a hand-drawn sketch of a coastline and no message, which I have decided was either a cry for help or a grocery list, depending on my mood.

I went to live with an aunt in Bristol who was kind in the way weather is kind — present, indifferent, occasionally destructive. And I learned the second lesson of my childhood, the one that followed the lesson of miles: attachment is a debt, and debts get called in, and the only safe position is to keep moving before anyone has time to lend you anything.

So I outran people. Professionally. It became a skill, like flint-knapping or reading soil stains. A dig would end, someone would say *stay in touch*, and I would be on a ferry before the sentence finished landing. Boyfriends lasted as long as field seasons. Friendships expired like permits. I told myself this was freedom. I told myself this with such conviction that for years I actually believed it, the way you can believe a room is warm if you refuse to touch the walls.

Twelve years. Forty-one sites. Nine countries. Four postcards. Zero settled fathers.

You can do the arithmetic yourself. I've done it enough for both of us.

* * *

Here is the thing I lay awake understanding, somewhere around three in the morning, with the compass needle ticking against its glass like a slow clock: the needle wasn't pointing north. It wasn't pointing at Sarn Ddu, or whatever waited in the high passes, either. Not really.

It was pointing at 1998. It was pointing at a Land Rover loaded at four in the morning. It was pointing at a boy who became a man who became a father who became a rumor, and at every mile I'd walked trying to make the arithmetic come out different.

My whole childhood fit inside that brass housing. Every dig, every departure, every *I'll send for you*. The Provision hadn't shown me treasure. It had shown me what I was owed, and what I was owed, apparently, was the truth — which is the one cargo no expedition is ever properly equipped to carry.

And underneath the truth, colder and heavier, sat the other thing. The thing I'd been circling all night the way the needle circled its card.

If Elias Montrose had been within twenty miles of me — if he'd stood in that cave, in that village, breathing the same salt air I breathed every day of my survey season — then he hadn't failed to find me.

He'd chosen not to.

* * *

I got up at dawn and made tea I didn't drink and laid the journal open on the desk beside the compass, because grief, in my family, has always responded well to documentation.

His handwriting was exactly as I remembered it: small, slanted, impatient, the letters of a man writing faster than he thinks. The early entries were pure Elias — measurements, sketches, dry little jokes in the margins (*the landlord claims the well is Roman; the well is a hole*). And then, halfway through, the tone shifted. The entries shortened. The margins filled with crossings-out. And on a page dated the spring before he vanished, one line, pressed so hard into the paper it had torn through:

*They know I know. If anything happens, the answer is where the needle rests.*

I read it four times. Then I read it aloud, because some sentences only become real when you hear them in your own voice, and hearing them made it worse, not better.

*If anything happens.* Fathers are supposed to write *if anything happens, I love you*. Mine had written directions.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in a rented room in Penhallow, at the sheer consistency of the man. Twelve years dead or disappeared or worse, and still he couldn't manage a sentence that wasn't a coordinate.

But my hands were shaking when I set the journal down, and the shaking wasn't anger. Anger I knew how to carry; anger was light luggage. This was the other thing, the thing I'd spent twelve years and forty-one sites outrunning, and it had finally caught me in a village of two hundred people, and it weighed exactly as much as a small brass compass.

Because here was the terrible, simple fact of it: he had wanted me to follow. Somewhere, in whatever remained of Elias Montrose, there had been a version of events where his daughter read these pages. Where she held this compass. Where she understood.

*I'll send for you,* he'd said, loading the Land Rover in the dark.

He just hadn't said it would take twelve years, and a storm, and a legend he once dismissed as tourist bait, and everything I had.

* * *

By seven I was dressed. By half past I was in the pub, because Penhallow keeps early hours and I needed two things: breakfast, and the name of the man who'd warned me off the cave.

Tomas Vey was already there, of course. Men like Tomas are always already there; it's their defining feature. He looked up from his tea with the expression of a man watching a stray dog consider his garden, and I put the compass on the table between us before I lost my nerve.

"My grandfather," I said, "kept maps of this coast."

Something moved behind his eyes — not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition. The look of a man who understands that some inheritances arrive as burdens, gift-wrapped.

"That so," he said.

"And I need someone who knows the high passes." I turned the compass so the needle faced him, pointing its stubborn, impossible way toward the mountains. "Because I'm going after what this is pointing at. And I'd rather not die finding out."

He looked at the needle for a long moment. Longer than a skeptic should. Long enough to tell me he'd seen it before — or something like it — and that the seeing had cost him something once, too.

Outside, the gulls were working the tide line, and the first clouds were coming in off the water, gray and organized, like an expedition with a schedule.

Tomas picked up the compass. Turned it over once, twice. And said: "Sit down. This is going to take longer than your breakfast."
