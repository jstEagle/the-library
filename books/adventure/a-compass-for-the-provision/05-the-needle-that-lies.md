## 5. The Needle That Lies

I want it on the record that I did not invite her to my kitchen. She followed me there the way weather follows a man up a mountain — inevitable, unwelcome, and impossible to argue with once it's arrived.

"Three minutes," I told her, holding the door open against the wind off the harbour. "Then you're out."

"You said five at the pub."

"The pub was being generous. I wasn't."

She came in anyway, and she looked around my kitchen the way archaeologists look at everything — like it was already a ruin and she was already taking notes. The kettle on the Aga. The charts of the coastal range pinned above the dresser, my grandfather's ink gone brown as old tea. The boot rack with three pairs of boots in descending states of honesty about my life.

Yara Montrose set the compass on my table between us like a card she was playing face-up.

"So," she said. "Tell me it's broken."

I didn't touch it. Not yet. That was the whole trick of the thing, I'd learned that much from her father's kind over the years: they bring you an object and they watch how long you can leave it alone. "Why would I tell you that?"

"Because everyone else has. Mrs. Tregowan says it's cursed. The man at the chandlery said it's a fairground prop. And you—" she tilted her head, "—you looked at it for eleven seconds in the pub before you decided it was nothing."

"Fifteen."

"Fine. Fifteen."

"And I decided it was worse than nothing." I put the kettle on because my hands wanted something honest to do. "Nothing doesn't get people killed. Nothing doesn't make grown men walk into the high passes in October with a brass toy in their pocket."

"It's not a toy."

"No," I agreed, and poured the water, and made tea I had no intention of enjoying. "It isn't. Which is the problem."

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me, since apparently we're doing this — since she sat across my table with her hands wrapped around a mug she hadn't asked for, waiting, patient as erosion.

My name is Tomas Vey. For nine years I was mountain rescue out of Penhallow, and for one afternoon of those nine years I was the man who carried a boy named Denny Prowse down off Cwm Llech with his ankle pointing the wrong way and his lips going grey, and I was fast, I was good, I was the best they had, and he died anyway, in the helicopter, twenty minutes from a hospital, because the storm closed in behind us and cost us forty minutes we didn't have. Forty minutes. I have done the arithmetic of that afternoon so many times the numbers have worn smooth as pebbles. Forty minutes is a phone call not made. A window missed. A rule ignored.

After that I stopped rescuing people and started guiding them instead, which sounds like a contradiction but isn't. A guide decides who comes up the mountain. A rescue man takes whoever fell. I prefer choosing. It's the only power the hills ever offered me and I took it with both hands.

And here is the other thing, the thing that matters this morning: my grandfather mapped the coastal range when maps were still drawn by hand and argument. Emrys Vey, surveyor, sheep farmer, stubborn man. His charts hang above my dresser and three more are rolled in a tin under my bed, and I grew up tracing his contour lines with a finger while he told me which valleys lied and which streams moved in winter and which peaks were older than their names. He knew that range the way I know the back of my own hand, except better, because the back of my hand never changed and the range did, every season, every slide, every storm.

So when Yara Montrose turned that compass loose on my table and its needle swung past north without so much as a nod — when it settled, deliberate as a dog lying down, pointing inland and up, toward the high passes — I did not think *broken*. Broken things don't point anywhere. They just lie there.

Broken things are honest that way.

* * *

"Say what you're thinking," she said.

"I'm thinking you should take the ferry home."

"I'm thinking you know something."

"I'm thinking your tea's going cold, which is the only tragedy happening in this kitchen."

She didn't smile. She had her father's face, I realized then — I'd seen photographs of Elias Montrose in the Institute brochures that used to circulate, back when the Institute still thought Penhallow worth charming. Same sharp architecture. Same way of looking at a person like they were a stratigraphy they intended to read down through. But where his photographs showed a man delighted with himself, she looked like someone who'd been reading too long and forgotten why.

"Mr. Vey," she said. "I found that compass sealed in a tin with my father's field journal. My father, who walked out of my life twelve years ago. He was here. In this village. Within — what, six miles? Eight? — of wherever I happened to be standing, and he never sent word. So understand me when I say I am not chasing treasure. I'm chasing an explanation, and I will find it whether or not anyone in this county helps me." She turned the mug a quarter-turn on the table. "But I'd rather not die stupidly finding it. You're the guide. Everyone says so. Even the people who don't like you say so."

"Especially them," I said. "It's how they hurt me."

That got a flicker out of her. Good. A person who won't flicker won't last above the tree line.

I finished my tea. Then I reached across and turned the compass so its face caught the window light, and I let myself look properly for the first time.

Brass case, heavy for its size, no maker's mark — no, wait. There was a mark. Small, low on the casing, worn nearly to suggestion: three strokes, like a bird's footprint. I'd seen that mark before. On a hinge in the church. On a lockbox the museum kept under glass and called *attributed*. On the clasp of my grandfather's surveying instrument case, which I had held maybe four times in my life because he'd told me, at eight years old, that some things you hold less the more you love them.

The smith. Old stories, older than the village's interest in having stories. Three compasses, the tale went, made by a man whose name the telling wore away, each one pointed not at north but at what was owed. Tourist bait, the Institute called it, in the one paper Elias Montrose ever published that mentioned the region. *Folkloric embellishment.* He'd dismissed it in a footnote. Footnotes are where scholars bury things they're afraid of.

The needle lay steady now, quivering only at its tip, aimed at a gap in my eastern window frame as if it could see through stone to Sarn Ddu beyond.

"Where did your grandfather map?" I asked.

Yara blinked. "What?"

"His surveys. Your father's. Where did the Institute send him?"

She hesitated. I watched her decide how much truth cost. "The high passes. Nineteen ninety-eight. An expedition into the range east of here. It was written up as a failure — bad weather, inconclusive results. He left the Institute the same year."

"And you believe the journal tells you why."

"I believe the journal tells me *something*, and I intend to read all of it, including the parts that were cut out."

"Cut out?"

"Pages razored from the binding. Neatly. Recently." Her jaw tightened. "Someone got to the tin before I did, or after someone else hid it, or — I don't know. But pages don't remove themselves."

No. They don't. People remove pages. People with institutions behind them and reasons in their pockets.

I stood, went to the dresser, and took down the chart I'd been avoiding looking at since she'd walked in — the big one, the 1961 revision, my grandfather's last full survey of the range. I spread it over the table, over her tea, weighted the corners with the sugar bowl and two boots' worth of confidence, and put my finger on the high country east of the village.

"There," I said. "Sarn Ddu. Black Cairn, if you want the tourist version. Now watch."

I lifted the compass and set it on the chart itself, on the paper, where any decent needle would sulk and wander. This one did neither. It swung — smooth, unhurried, certain — and settled with its line of sight running straight up the valley of Afon Lloer, past the saddle, dead onto the summit ring of Sarn Ddu.

Not near it. Onto it. To the degree.

"Now watch again," I said, and moved the compass six inches west along the chart.

The needle corrected. Six inches east. Corrected. I rotated the whole instrument a half-turn and the needle swung round through the correction like a man turning to keep his eye on a door.

"That's not magnetism," Yara said quietly. "Or — it is, but—"

"But it's local, and it's strong, and it's *fixed*." I straightened up. My stomach had gone cold somewhere in the last minute and I chose to ignore it, the way you do. "There's iron in these hills, girl, and my grandfather marked every lode he found. Look." I tapped the chart. Here, a ferrous seam below Cwm Llech — the compass ignored it. Here, the old mine workings at Porth Isaf, enough rusted rail and pit gear to pull any honest needle sideways — ignored. The needle lay on Sarn Ddu like a claim staked.

"Except," I said, and here is where I gave away more than I meant to, "my grandfather did map one anomaly he couldn't explain. One." I traced the contour lines up and over, to a spot on the northeast shoulder of Sarn Ddu where the ink changed — where Emrys Vey had drawn a small circle and beside it, in his cramped hand, a note I had read a hundred times as a boy and understood exactly never:

*Needle lies here. Ground does not agree with sky. Ask the smith.*

"He never asked the smith," I said. "The smith was dead by then. So he drew his circle and moved on, because that's what surveyors do with the things that frighten them. They file them."

Yara was leaning over the chart, and I saw her finger come up and stop just short of touching the circle, hovering, the way you hover over a grave. "He wrote *ask the smith*."

"He wrote a lot of things. He also wrote *do not go up there in the white months*, which is the part I'd take seriously, if I were inclined to take anything seriously, which I'm not, professionally."

"When does the snow come?"

"This year? Early. The gulls came down off the tops a fortnight ago and the sheep are grazing low. The hills have been posting notices all autumn and nobody in this village reads them anymore except me." I began rolling the chart back up, because looking at that circle any longer felt like eavesdropping on the dead. "Which brings us to the actual conversation, Dr. Montrose. The one where I tell you no."

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You were working up to it. You've got the look. I've seen it on a hundred faces at the trailhead — the one that thinks *guide* means *witness*." I hung the chart back on its nail. "Here's what happens to people who go into those passes chasing something. I've carried three of them down. Two breathing. And every single one of them had a compass, or a map, or a certainty, and every single one believed the hill would make an exception for them. The hill makes exceptions for no one. It made none for your father."

The silence after that had weight. She took it like a blow, which told me she hadn't yet said out loud what we both now knew: that Elias Montrose had gone up there and not come down, and that the tin in the cave was not a message but a wreckage.

"I'm going regardless," she said finally. "Alone if I have to."

"You'll die alone, then. Or worse — you'll get lost, trigger a callout, and some other poor bastard will carry you off that hill because you wouldn't listen to the one man in Penhallow who knows where the ground stops pretending." I heard my own voice climb and hauled it back down. "I've buried enough of other people's certainties."

"Then help me not be one of them."

"I said no."

"You said you'd carried three down. Two breathing." She looked up at me, and there it was again, her father's face wearing a different expression — not delight. Grief, held the way you hold a tool you've used so long you've forgotten it's not part of your hand. "You stopped rescuing and started choosing. Fine. Choose. Tell me I'm not worth the risk. Tell me the hill keeps what it takes and you sleep fine. Say it and I'll walk out that door and you'll never see me again."

I could have said it. It would even have been mostly true.

Instead I heard myself ask: "Who else knows you have the journal?"

Her eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"Because pages were cut out of it, and because yesterday a landrover with a Cardiff registration drove through the village twice, slow, and because the Whitcombe Institute hasn't sent anyone here since your father left and I don't believe in coincidences that arrive within forty-eight hours of a woman asking questions in a pub." I crossed my arms. "Answer the question."

"...Nobody. Officially. I told the landlady I was surveying coastal erosion."

"And unofficially?"

She was quiet long enough that the kettle ticked as it cooled. "There was a man on the quay this morning. Grey coat. He asked the chandlery owner if any visitors had bought climbing kit. She told him no." A pause. "I bought climbing kit Tuesday. From the ironmonger."

There it was. The cold walking up my spine, the old familiar cold, the one that arrives before weather and before trouble and usually dressed as both.

"Grey coat," I said. "Tall? Soft-spoken? Smiles like he's quoting regulations?"

"You've met him."

"I've met his kind. Institute men come through every few years asking after Emrys Vey's charts, polite as tax inspectors, and every time they leave, something's missing from the archive room and nobody can prove what." I looked at the compass on the table, needle still lying faithful on its invisible bearing, patient as a debt. Three compasses, the story said. If hers was one, and if the Institute had spent decades sniffing after whatever it pointed to—

Then she wasn't the only one following the journal. She might not even be the first.

"Right," I said, before the sensible part of me could lodge a formal objection. "New terms. I don't guide treasure hunters and I don't guide liars, so we'll proceed on the understanding that you're neither. We go up the shepherd's way, not the tourist path. We move when I say move and we turn back when I say turn back, and if the weather turns — and it will turn — we do not negotiate with it. And if I say the hill's keeping you, girl, the hill keeps you, and you'll thank me later or hate me forever, either of which I can live with."

Something in her shoulders came down half an inch. Relief, or the beginning of trust, which looks identical and weighs differently.

"One condition of mine," she said.

"Of course there is."

"You tell me what happened to you up there. Somewhere on the trail. Whatever it is you're not saying when you talk about carrying people down." She held my gaze, steady as her father's compass. "I'm trusting you with my life. I want to know whose I'm trusting it to."

I should have refused. Instead I picked up her empty mug, carried it to the sink, and stood there a moment with my back to her, looking out the window at the range — at Sarn Ddu, black-shouldered under a sky already rehearsing winter, at the circle my grandfather had drawn and the question he'd never gotten answered.

"Fair price," I said. "Now get out of my kitchen. We leave Thursday, ahead of the front, and you've got two days to learn how to pack a rucksack without packing your entire grief into it. It weighs more than you think."

Behind me, faint as a held breath, the compass needle trembled — and held its line, straight up the valley, straight at whatever the ground up there had been keeping all these years.

Straight, I couldn't help noticing, at the exact place my grandfather had written: *ask the smith.*

Thursday, then. God help us both.
