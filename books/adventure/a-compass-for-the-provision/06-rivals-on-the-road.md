## 6. Rivals on the Road

The trouble with small villages is that news travels faster than weather, and weather in Penhallow travels like it's being paid by the hour.

I learned this at half past eight on my third morning back, when Mrs. Tregowan — who ran the post office and, by extension, the village's entire intelligence apparatus — set down my tea with the particular care of a woman about to detonate something.

"You've got visitors," she said.

"I have a room," I said. "Visitors require an invitation."

"Two gentlemen. From London." She said *London* the way other people said *leprosy*. "Been asking after you at the pub. Asking after your father, too."

My hand did something I didn't authorize. It went cold around the cup, and the tea trembled in a way that Mrs. Tregowan absolutely noticed, because noticing things was her profession and she was very good at it.

"Did they give a name?"

"Hartwell and Renner. Said they were from some institute. Whitcombe." She watched me with the frank appetite of a woman collecting material for the evening's distribution rounds. "You know them?"

*Know* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. I knew the Whitcombe Institute the way you know a scar — intimately, unwillingly, and mostly by touch in the dark. It had employed my father for eleven years, funded his digs, published his papers, and then, when he walked out of our lives in 1998, had apparently found nothing worth mentioning about the circumstances. I'd written to them twice as a young woman asking where Elias Montrose had gone. Both letters came back with the same bland institutional courtesy: *Dr. Montrose resigned his position to pursue independent research. We wish him well.*

Nobody wishes anybody well that many times unless they're lying.

"No," I told Mrs. Tregowan. "Never heard of them."

She looked at me the way a good surveyor looks at a bad datum — noting the discrepancy, filing it away — and left me alone with my tea and my pulse.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the 1998 expedition, which was to say: almost nothing, assembled from fragments over twelve years of deliberate not-asking.

That summer, Elias had led a Whitcombe-funded survey into the coastal range. He'd come home late, sunburned, quieter than usual — and I remember this because quiet was so unlike him that even a nine-year-old noticed, the way you notice a clock stopping. He'd spread maps across the kitchen table and traced routes with his finger while I sat on the counter eating toast, and he'd said something I only understood much later: *"Some things are better lost, little bird. Remember that if anyone ever asks you to find them."*

Three weeks later he was gone. No note. His kit bag went with him, but his good boots stayed, which I always took as evidence either of haste or of intent never to walk anywhere again. The Institute sent one letter expressing regret at his resignation. My mother lasted four more years before the drift took her too, and then it was just me and the miles.

So when two men from the Whitcombe Institute turned up in a village of forty people asking questions about a missing expedition from thirteen years ago, I did not think: *what a coincidence.* I thought: *they know about the cave.* And underneath that, quieter and colder: *they know about the journal.*

Which meant I needed to know what they knew, which meant I needed to stop being a woman hiding in a rented room and start being a woman having breakfast in a public house, radiating the innocence of someone whose father had never existed.

* * *

They were easy to spot. Penhallow has a population that could fit in a lifeboat, and anyone wearing a fleece that still had its creases was either a tourist or trouble, and these two managed both.

Hartwell was the older one — mid-fifties, silver at the temples, a face built for trustworthiness the way a lure is built for fish. He had the handshake of a man who'd practiced it. Renner was younger, harder, with the particular stillness of someone who watches doors. He didn't shake hands. He assessed mine instead, briefly, like checking a tool for damage.

"Dr. Montrose?" Hartwell said, and I felt the name go through me like a probe finding wire. "Yara Montrose? Elias's daughter?"

"That depends who's asking, and why."

He smiled as though I'd charmed him. "Marcus Hartwell. This is Dr. Renner. We're with the Whitcombe Institute — I believe your father worked with us, years ago?"

"He worked for you," I said. "There's a difference."

"May we sit?"

There was no graceful way to refuse without confirming there was something to hide, so I sat, and ordered nothing, and let them buy me coffee I had no intention of drinking.

"We're hoping you can help us with a historical matter," Hartwell began. "In 1998, the Institute sponsored a field survey in this region, led by your father. The final report was never filed. Dr. Montrose resigned abruptly, and certain materials — equipment, records — were unaccounted for. We're conducting a retrospective review. Housekeeping, really."

"Housekeeping," I repeated. "Thirteen years later. In person. In the village."

"The Institute takes its obligations seriously."

"And what obligations would those be? Because when I wrote to you asking where my father had gone, your obligations apparently extended to a form letter."

Something flickered behind Hartwell's trustworthy face — recalculation, maybe, or irritation — and was smoothly buried. "That was before my time in administration. I'm sorry. Truly." He leaned forward, confiding. "Ms. Montrose, may I speak plainly? There's been a development. A storm this winter exposed a sea cave along this coast. Local reports suggest features consistent with sites mentioned in your father's preliminary notes. If he conducted unsanctioned work here — work involving Institute property — we'd very much like to recover it. For the record. Nothing more."

"Institute property," I said. "Like what?"

"Survey instruments. Journals, if he kept any under Institute auspices." Renner spoke for the first time, and his voice was flat and patient, a man reading coordinates. "A compass, possibly. Brass. There were three commissioned for the '98 expedition by a local smith. Two were returned. One wasn't."

The room got very slightly smaller. I kept my face doing what faces do — mild, faintly bored — while my mind sprinted through the implications. They knew about the compasses. They knew one was missing. They were here because the storm had done their advertising for them, and they were asking *me*, which meant they suspected — correctly — that the missing compass might have found its way to the daughter.

"I'm sorry," I said, "but I haven't seen my father since I was nine. Whatever he took, he didn't leave it with me. You might try the sea. It seems to give things back lately."

Hartwell laughed, warm as a radiator. Renner didn't laugh at all. He was looking at my hands — at my right hand specifically, which had spent three days carrying a brass compass everywhere I went and now sat on the table like a defendant.

"Of course," Hartwell said, standing. "We wouldn't dream of troubling you further. But if anything should... surface. Anything at all. We're staying at the inn until the weather window closes." He laid a card on the table between us, square and heavy stock. "And Ms. Montrose — the Institute would be prepared to make a generous contribution to any independent researcher willing to share findings. Your father was brilliant. Talent like yours shouldn't want for funding."

*Talent like yours.* They'd looked me up. Of course they'd looked me up. Somewhere in a London office there was a file with my name on it, my publication list, my dig history, twelve years of miles catalogued by people who understood exactly what kind of daughter goes looking for what kind of father.

"I'll keep it in mind," I said, and watched them go, and noted that Renner paused at the door to look back at me once — not hostile, not friendly, just *recording* — before following Hartwell out into the grey morning.

* * *

Tomas Vey found me twenty minutes later on the sea wall, where I'd gone to breathe air that hadn't been negotiated with.

"So," he said, sitting down uninvited. "The London men."

"You heard."

"Mrs. Tregowan heard. Therefore the county heard. Therefore the gulls heard." He looked out at the water. "What do they want?"

"The same thing I want. Or close enough that it doesn't matter."

"They asked about you."

"They asked about my father. I'm just the address he never wrote to." I turned the card over in my fingers. Heavy stock, embossed lettering, a phone number that probably rang in three offices at once. "Tomas. In 1998, my father came here with Institute funding and three brass compasses, and whatever he found scared him enough to abandon his family rather than tell anyone. Now two of his old employers are sitting in the pub saying the word *housekeeping*. What does that sound like to you?"

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. Below us the tide was going out, peeling back from the rocks in long silver sheets, uncovering the day's brief territory.

"It sounds like men who think something valuable is missing," he said finally. "And it sounds like they don't know how much you know."

"Neither do I. That's the problem. I know the journal says he found something above Sarn Ddu. I know the needle points there. I know he went up and didn't come down. What I don't know is whether they know any of that, or whether they're fishing." I looked at him. "But here's what I do know. If they're fishing, they'll catch something eventually. And if they already know — if they've known for thirteen years and only moved now because the storm showed them the door — then every day I stand in this village being polite is a day they spend getting ahead of me."

"The passes close early this year," Tomas said. It wasn't agreement. It also wasn't refusal, and from Tomas Vey, that was practically a signature.

"How early?"

"Early enough that we should have left yesterday." He stood, brushing off his trousers, and looked down at me with those flat rescue-man's eyes that weighed everything and committed to nothing. "Tomorrow, then. Dawn. If you're coming, you come with my rules and my pace, and you don't argue with either."

"And if the London men ask where we've gone?"

Tomas smiled, and it was the first smile I'd seen from him that had any teeth in it. "Then Mrs. Tregowan will tell them we've gone to look at birds. She owes me a favor." He started up the lane, then stopped. "Montrose. The tall one — Renner. Did he mention which compass they have?"

"They have one of the three. Why?"

"Because my grandfather's map marks the anomaly with three bearings," Tomas said. "Triangulation. One compass points at the mountain. But two—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Two compasses meant two needles converging on the same hidden point, and the men holding the second one didn't need a journal, or a legend, or a dead man's apology. They only needed to follow their own instrument up the same slope we were climbing.

The tide was fully out now, and somewhere beneath the grey water the Provision waited with its mouth open, keeping everything it had ever been given.

Dawn tomorrow. Which gave the London men a full day's head start on a mountain that closed early — and gave me one night to decide how fast I was willing to run.
