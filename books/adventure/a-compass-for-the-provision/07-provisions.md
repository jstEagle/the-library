## 7. Provisions

I have a rule about people who come to Penhallow in October looking for the Provision: I don't feed them, I don't house them, and I certainly don't carry their luggage up a mountain. Rules exist for a reason. Mine were built over thirty years of hauling tourists, treasure-hunters, and one memorable German paraglider off the coastal range, and they had kept me alive and mostly unbothered for the better part of my adult life.

By the third morning after she found the cave, Yara Montrose had broken two of the three.

She'd eaten at Mrs. Tregose's kitchen table twice — the second time without being asked, which around here is the same as a marriage proposal — and she'd slept in the loft above the boat shed, which I own, which meant I was housing her, which meant the rule was bleeding out on the flagstones and I was pretending not to watch it die.

The luggage was next. I could feel it coming.

I found her in the yard behind the shed at seven in the morning, sorting gear on a tarp with the methodical violence of someone who'd done this a hundred times in a hundred places. Rope, coiled properly. A primus stove older than she was. A tin of spare mantles. She'd borrowed a frame pack from the fisherman's co-op and was loading it the way soldiers load things — heavy stuff high and close to the spine, everything else in the exact place her hands would go looking for it in the dark.

"You're going up there alone," I said. It wasn't a question. Questions invite answers, and answers invite arguments, and I'd already lost enough arguments that week.

"I'm considering my options."

"Your options are a mountain that kills people in a good year, a compass that lies, and a journal written by a man who went up there and didn't come down." I crouched at the edge of the tarp, not quite entering her workspace. You don't step into another person's gear without permission. It's the same as stepping into their head. "And you're doing it in October."

"September," she said, without looking up. "It's the twenty-ninth."

"Up there, it's October. Up there it's been October since the middle of the month. The bracken's died back, the gulls have gone quiet, and old Cadoc's sheep came down off the high pasture nine days ago, and Cadoc's sheep have never once been wrong." I picked up her rope, checked the coil, and set it down again. "The passes close early this year. I can smell it."

"You can smell it."

"Peat smoke and a certain kind of stillness. Also my grandfather's knees, which have predicted weather in this range for sixty years with better accuracy than the Met Office, and this week they're singing."

She finally looked up at me. She had her father's eyes — I'd worked that out from the photograph in the journal, the one of the younger Elias Montrose grinning on a scree slope somewhere — but where his had been warm, hers were the eyes of someone who'd learned to keep the warmth in a locked drawer and only take it out when the room was empty. "Then come with me," she said. "Or don't, and I'll go alone, and you can spend the winter explaining to Mrs. Tregose why you let me."

"That's blackmail."

"That's arithmetic."

I want it on the record that I said no. I want it on the record that I listed my terms, my conditions, my hard rules, and that I delivered them with the full gravity of a man who has pulled too many bodies out of too much snow to be moved by a woman with her father's eyes and her father's stubbornness. I want it on the record because what actually happened is that by noon I was in the boat shed counting tent pegs, and by evening I'd sent a boy down to Tregose's for a second sack of oats, and by dark I was standing in my own kitchen arguing with a mule.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Rules first. A man should have the dignity of his rules, even while they're being ignored.

* * *

We negotiated in the pub that evening, which was a mistake, because the whole village listens in this pub and pretends it doesn't, and by the time I'd finished talking half of Penhallow would know my terms and the other half would have opinions.

"Rule one," I said. "We turn back when I say we turn back. Not when you say. Not when the compass says. When I say. If I say down, we go down."

"Agreed."

"You're agreeing very fast. People who agree that fast are planning to renegotiate at altitude."

"I'm agreeing fast because it's a reasonable rule and because I want you to keep talking."

"Rule two. We carry out everything we carry in. No caches left on the mountain, nothing buried, nothing abandoned. This isn't 1998. The range isn't a rubbish tip, and the shepherds up top notice everything."

Her jaw tightened at the year. I watched her decide not to ask what I knew about 1998, which was more than I'd told her, and less than my grandfather's maps had shown me. "Agreed."

"Rule three." I turned my glass on the bar. "Whatever's up there — whatever your father found, whatever the Institute wants, whatever the compass is pointing at — we look at it and we come home. We don't take it. We don't move it. We don't stand on the summit at sunset declaring it ours. The mountain isn't a bank. It's a grave with very good acoustics, and I've heard what it does to people who treat it otherwise."

She was quiet for a moment. "You believe the legend," she said. "The cave that shows you what you're owed."

"I believe the sea showed you a tin box, and the tin box showed you your father, and now you're owed an answer. That's the whole legend. The rest is decoration for the tourists." I finished the pint. "Rule four. You tell me the truth when I ask you a question. On the mountain, a lie weighs about forty pounds, and I'll be carrying it."

"And what do I get for all this?"

"A guide who knows every path, every bothy, every spring, and every place the ground likes to eat people. A mule. My grandfather's maps, which show drover's roads that aren't on any Ordnance Survey. And the only weather sense in Penhallow that's never been wrong."

"And if we find him up there?" she asked. Quietly. The pub noise seemed to step back from it. "If we find — what's left."

"Then we bring him down," I said. "Whatever's left gets brought down and buried proper, with his name said out loud over it. That's not a rule. That's just what people do."

She looked at her hands for a while. Then she said, "Rule five. You stop calling it *your father's compass* like you're sorry for me. It's mine now. He left it where I'd find it. That makes it mine, and I'm not apologizing for wanting it."

"That's not a rule, that's a grievance."

"It's the best kind of rule. The kind with teeth." She put out her hand across the bar, and I shook it, and her grip was a dig-site grip, callused and unhurried, and I remember thinking: this woman has been shaking hands with the ground her whole life, and the ground keeps letting her down, and she keeps shaking anyway.

Behind us, Mrs. Tregose — who had absolutely been listening, as had everyone — said loudly to nobody in particular, "Someone'll need to take the second mule, then," and that was that. In Penhallow, a decision isn't real until the village has made it for you.

* * *

The next three days were provisions, and provisions are a religion, and I am a devout man.

Mules first. I took Bramble, who is mine, steady as a sermon and vain about his ears. The second mule was borrowed from Cadoc — a young one called Nell with opinions — and Cadoc handed over the rope with a speech about how his grandfather had packed supplies up to Sarn Ddu in the winter of '36 and come back with frostbite and a story nobody believed, and how he, Cadoc, would like it noted that he believed the story now, always had, and that if we found anything up there with the name Vey on it, he'd thank us to leave it where it lay. I said I'd note it. Yara asked who Vey was. Cadoc looked at me, and I looked at the mule, and the mule looked at the oats, and that was the end of that conversation until a better one.

Gear. Two tents, because I don't share mine and she didn't offer. A storm shelter — the bothies on the high route are sound but the one below the saddle has a roof like a colander. Six days of food, then eight, because the weather window was shrinking while we packed. Rope I trusted and rope I replaced. A stove each, because a shared stove is a shared fate and I've buried enough shared fates. She contributed her own kit: the frame pack, a down jacket that had seen three continents, a first-aid kit that could have done field surgery, and a small folding trowel she handled the way other people handle photographs of the dead.

"You always carry that?" I asked.

"You always carry that rope?" she said, nodding at the coil I'd checked four times.

"Fair."

The compass she kept in an inside pocket, wrapped in a wool sock, and I noticed she checked it the way you check a wound — quickly, expecting it to still be there, still be bleeding. The needle still ignored north. It still leaned toward the high country like a dog pulling at a lead, and I still didn't like it, and I still couldn't say why, except that my grandfather's maps had a pencil line running the same direction and a note in his hand that read, in Welsh, roughly: *the iron under Sarn Ddu is not iron.* My grandfather was not a man who wasted pencil.

The journal she read at night in the boat shed loft, and I know this because I could see the lamplight through the boards when I went out to see to the mules, and because on the second night the lamplight stayed on until three and on the third night it went out at nine and I decided that was worse.

* * *

On the morning of departure the sky did the thing I'd been dreading. It didn't storm. It *cleared*. A hard, bright, windless clarity came down off the sea like a held breath, the kind of day that makes tourists say *what lovely weather for a walk* and makes shepherds start moving their flocks downhill without being told. The barometer on my wall — my grandfather's, brass, older than the maps — had dropped overnight and then gone flat and still, and a flat still barometer in late September is the mountain clearing its throat.

"Window's four days," I told her at the yard gate. "Maybe five. After that, snow, and once the snow starts on the saddle it doesn't stop until spring. The passes close early this year. I'm telling you now so you can't say nobody told you."

"I heard you the first six times."

"Seven. I'm going to say it the whole way up. It's in the rules."

"It's not in the rules."

"It's rule six."

She almost smiled. It was the first one I'd seen from her, and it changed her face so much I understood immediately why she rationed them. "You're inventing rules as we go."

"A good guide does. The mountain invents problems as we go. It's only fair." I cinched Bramble's last strap and looked her over — boots broken in, gaiters on, pack sitting right, the compass a small square weight in her breast pocket that I could see her not touching. "Last chance," I said. "There's a ferry at noon. There's a whole world south of here that isn't vertical. You could have a life that doesn't involve chasing a dead man up a mountain in the wrong season."

She was quiet long enough that I thought I'd actually reached her. Then she said, "Twelve years ago my father walked out of a dig site in the middle of the night and left a fourteen-year-old girl sitting on a crate of pottery shards, and everyone told her he'd send for her, and he never did. I have spent twelve years being the person who leaves first, because leaving first means nobody can leave you. Do you know what that costs? It costs everything. It costs every —" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He was here. Twelve miles from where I've been working all month. He was *here*, Tomas, and the sea knew it before I did. I'm not taking the ferry. I'm not taking the safe life. The safe life is just the long way of being abandoned."

The yard was very quiet. Bramble shifted his weight. Somewhere down the lane, Mrs. Tregose's dog was announcing the postman to an audience of nobody.

"Rule seven," I said finally. "On the mountain, we don't talk about the past unless it's practical. The past is heavy and we've got enough weight."

"Agreed."

"Rule eight. When this is over — whatever we find, whatever we don't — you come down and you stay down. You let the mountain keep its dead. All of them."

She looked up at the range then. The peaks were hard and white-rimmed against the blue, and Sarn Ddu stood at the end of the line like a closed door, and I watched her look at it the way you look at a house you grew up in from the road, knowing you can't go back and going back anyway.

"Agreed," she said, and this time I believed her about half as much as I wanted to, which is the correct amount to believe anyone about rule eight.

We went up the coast path in the hard bright light, two mules, two packs, and the compass in her pocket pulling north-northeast like a finger hooked in her ribs. Behind us Penhallow watched from doorways and didn't wave, which is how Penhallow waves.

By the time we made the first foothills the light had gone the color of tin, and far to the north, over Sarn Ddu, there was a cloud sitting on the summit that hadn't been there at breakfast — flat-bottomed, gray, patient, like something that had been waiting twelve years and could wait four more days.

I didn't say anything. I'd already used up all my rules.

But I checked my grandfather's maps that night in the tent, by lamplight, while she slept, and I looked a long time at the pencil line and the note in Welsh, and at the small cross my grandfather had drawn above the saddle in a place where crosses usually mean one thing only.

*The iron under Sarn Ddu is not iron.*

And underneath it, in smaller writing, almost erased: *and it is not alone.*
