## 8. First Ascent

The foothills of the coastal range do not announce themselves. They arrive the way a debt collector does — politely at first, then all at once. By mid-morning of our first day out of Penhallow, the green lanes had given way to slate scree and gorse, and by noon I was no longer thinking about anything except where to put my next footfall, which is, I've come to believe, the closest thing to prayer that archaeology offers.

Tomas Vey walked ahead of me with the unhurried stride of a man who has never once been late for anything he cared about. He carried his pack like it was part of his skeleton. Two mules followed — Bramble and, inexplicably, a second mule Tomas refused to name on the grounds that "naming them makes them die faster," which told me more about him than any confession could have.

"You're quiet," he said, without turning around.

"I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"Steps. Switchbacks. Miles." I said it lightly, the way you hand someone a coin so they won't look in your purse. "It's how I keep time."

He glanced back at me then, one eyebrow doing the work of an entire sentence. "Most people use years."

"Years are unreliable. They stretch when you don't want them to and collapse when you need them most. Miles stay honest."

"Miles lie too," he said. "They tell you distance is the same as progress." And he walked on before I could decide whether to be annoyed or gutted.

The compass rode in my breast pocket, against my sternum, where I could feel its weight like a second heartbeat. Every hour or so I stopped, made a show of checking the terrain, and checked it instead. The needle did not point north. It had not pointed north since the cave. It pointed inland and up, unwavering, into the teeth of the range, toward a massif that even my road map named only in small gray letters: *Sarn Ddu*.

Black Cairn. Someone had looked at that mountain once and decided not to flatter it.

By late afternoon we crested a shoulder of moorland and there it was, full and unhidden for the first time — a dark pyramid of rock with a scar of old snow down its eastern flank, standing apart from the ridgeline like a man who leaves the party early. The needle in my pocket swung gently, testing, and settled on it the way a dog settles on its master.

"There," I said.

"I see it," Tomas said. "Two days' hard walking if the weather holds. One day if it doesn't hold and we're idiots about it."

"We're not going to be idiots about it?"

"We're going to try very hard not to be," he said, "and I wanted you to know the alternative exists."

We made camp in the lee of a drystone wall that some forgotten shepherd had built along the contour, each stone fitted without mortar, holding its place through nothing but gravity and stubbornness. I thought about that wall a long time while the stove hissed. Things that hold because they choose to. Things that hold because letting go would mean becoming something else entirely.

I took out the journal.

I had been rationing it. That's the truth of it — twelve pages a night, like medicine, because Elias Montrose's handwriting does something to me that I haven't found a clinical term for. It's a cramped, slanted script, all forward momentum, the writing of a man who resented every second the pen spent touching paper instead of moving. My own handwriting is the same. I noticed that years ago and have never forgiven either of us.

That night's pages were dated March 1998, six months before he disappeared. The camp was Penhallow. He'd been lecturing — I remember those lectures, or rather I remember being nine years old and doing homework on the floor of hired halls while his voice filled the room above me — and the journal recorded what he'd been saying:

*Gave the Provision talk again tonight. Same hall, same half-empty chairs, same farmer in the back who asks every year whether the cave will show him what he's owed and every year I give him the same answer: legends charge admission, and the price is your credibility. They laugh. But here is what I did not say tonight, what I will not say until I can say it with evidence: I believe the legend is a palimpsest. Underneath the folk-telling — the cave that shows you what you're owed — there is an older stratum. A real event. I think the "provision" was literal. I think something was carried up into that mountain, generations before Rome drew her maps this far west, and hidden so well that the hiding became the story.*

*A hoard. Not treasure in the vulgar sense — though it may be that too — but provision. Grain records. Debt tallies. Names. The kind of wealth a community keeps against famine. If I'm right, it rewrites everything we assume about these coastal peoples. They weren't waiting to be civilized. They were keeping accounts.*

I read that passage three times. Around me the moor went on with its small night noises — wind in the gorse, the mules shifting, Tomas breathing in the slow rhythm of a man who can sleep anywhere and trusts himself to wake. I read it a fourth time and felt the thing I always feel near my father's mind, which is the vertigo of recognizing your own thoughts in someone else's handwriting, arriving years too late to compare notes.

A pre-Roman hoard. Provision in the oldest sense. He hadn't dismissed the legend after all — he'd spent years dismissing it *in public* while believing it in private, wearing skepticism like a coat over a fever. I knew that trick intimately. I had invented several variations of it myself.

Then I turned the page, and the fever showed.

*Renner was at the lecture. Sat in the back row with that smile he keeps folded away for occasions. Asked afterward, casually, whether I intended to publish the Provision hypothesis. I said I hadn't decided. He said the Institute would want to "manage expectations" on anything so speculative. Manage. That's the word he used. As though the past were a staff that needed cutting back.*

*I know what manage means. It means Halloran got a letter last month questioning my methodology — unsigned, but the phrasing was Renner's, I'd know his semicolons anywhere. It means my field funding for the autumn survey came through at half the requested amount with a note about "priorities." It means someone at the Institute has decided that if there is anything under Sarn Ddu, they would prefer to be the ones who decide what the world learns about it, and when, and whether at all.*

*I am done asking permission from men who think history is an asset class.*

I sat with that for a while. *Renner.* The name meant nothing to me then beyond a face in old Institute photographs — a smooth, patient man at the edge of group shots, slightly better dressed than everyone else. I would learn, later and worse, exactly who he was. That night he was just a semicolon-shaped shadow in my father's margins, and I hated him already, on principle, the way you hate anyone who makes your father write in italics.

"Tomas," I said.

He opened one eye. It was fully dark now, the fire down to a red argument among the stones. Above us the stars came out in the brutal quantity they only manage this far from towns, and Sarn Ddu stood against them, blacker than the sky behind it.

"The man who wrote this. You believe him?"

"About the hoard?" Tomas considered. "I believe he believed it. That's worth more than being right, most days. Being right is cheap. Believing something enough to walk into a mountain for it — that costs."

"And the other part? The Institute trying to bury him?"

Tomas was quiet long enough that I thought he'd gone back to sleep. Then: "My grandfather mapped that range. Forty years, on foot, before the roads. The Institute bought his maps when he died. Paid my mother a fair price and filed them somewhere no one will ever see them." He closed the eye. "So yes. I believe institutions bury things. It's most of what they're good at."

I put the journal away and lay back and did not sleep for a long time, and when sleep came it was full of semicolons.

* * *

The second day the land turned against us politely, then less politely. The path thinned to a sheep-track, the sheep-tracks thinned to sheep-opinions, and by afternoon we were route-finding off contour lines and Tomas's memory of his grandfather's descriptions. The compass never wavered. I checked it obsessively now — I'll admit that — holding it level in my palm while walking, watching the needle hold its bearing toward Sarn Ddu with the fixed devotion of a thing that has waited a long time to say something.

"It's not magnetic north," I said, for perhaps the fifth time that day.

"No."

"It's pointing at the mountain. Specifically. Consistently. Through terrain that should scramble any needle ever forged."

"Yes."

"That's not possible."

"No," Tomas agreed, "it isn't," and kept walking, and I understood that this was simply how he metabolized impossibility: by refusing to grant it the dignity of discussion until it earned one.

But I couldn't leave it alone. Impossible things were supposed to stay in journals and pub stories, safely laminated in the past tense. This one was in my pocket, weighing four ounces, and it was winning an argument with physics.

At the top of a long climb we stopped to eat, sitting on a fallen lintel stone that had probably been a doorway once, back when doorways were worth carving. Below us the valley opened in folds of brown and green, stitched with walls, empty of everything but weather. I got out the journal again, because obsession is mostly just reading the same pages hoping they've changed.

They had changed. Or I had. This time what caught me was a fragment from June 1998, three months before the end:

*Mapped the approach today with the old drover's charts. There is a way up Sarn Ddu that doesn't appear on any modern survey — a shepherd's line, the locals call such things. The smith who made the compasses knew it; the compasses were made FOR it, I'm increasingly sure. Three of them, Vey's grandmother says. Three needles all pointing at the same secret. Why three? Who holds the others?*

*I've stopped writing to the Institute. Let them wonder. Let Renner read my silence however he likes. When I come down from that mountain I'll have proof, and proof is the only letter worth sending.*

*Yara starts secondary school in September. She wants to be an archaeologist. God help her, she's good at it already — found a coin shank on the beach at low tide last week and identified the mint from a reference book twice her reading age. I should tell her about the Provision. I should tell her everything. I keep almost telling her. Then I look at her measuring the tide line with her little tape measure, so serious, so certain the world can be measured, and I think: let her have one more year of a world that adds up.*

I read that paragraph and had to set the journal down on the stone and look at the valley for a while, because my eyes had stopped being reliable instruments.

*Let her have one more year of a world that adds up.*

That was the arithmetic of it, then. That was the equation he'd solved alone in a hired hall in Penhallow while I did my homework at his feet: that the truth about Sarn Ddu and the truth about himself could not both survive contact with his daughter, and he chose — he *chose* — to subtract himself. To make himself the error in my sums rather than teach me that the world didn't balance.

Twelve years I had measured the abandonment in miles. Distance from dig site to dig site, continent to continent, always moving, always keeping the ledger open, always telling myself I was searching for something when really I was refusing to close an account. And all along the actual entry in the book had been written in his cramped, slanting hand, three months before he vanished: *let her have one more year.*

One year. He'd promised himself one year. Come down from the mountain with proof, tell her everything, restore the world that adds up. And the mountain had kept him.

Something in me shifted gear that afternoon, and I recognized the sound of it, because I'd heard it in other people and pitied them for it. Purpose hardening into obsession sounds like boots on scree. It sounds like a decision made below the level of language. I had gone up the mountain looking for evidence of what happened to Elias Montrose. Somewhere between the lintel stone and the evening's cold camp, that stopped being sufficient. Now I needed what he'd found. I needed the chamber itself, the provision, the thing he'd believed in enough to die for — because it was the only remaining room in the world where he and I had ever been in agreement, and I wanted to stand in it.

"Yara." Tomas's voice, careful. "You've been staring at that compass for ten minutes."

"It hasn't moved."

"They generally don't."

"It's not a tool anymore," I said. "Do you understand? It's not pointing at a deposit. It's pointing at—" I stopped, because the honest word was *him*, and I wasn't ready to spend it aloud.

Tomas watched me for a moment with those rescue-man's eyes that triage everything they see. "Tomorrow we cross the river and start the real climbing," he said. "Sleep. Mountains punish the sentimental."

I slept badly and woke before dawn with the journal under my arm like a shield, and the first gray light showed me frost on the tent and Sarn Ddu closer than it had any right to be, filling half the sky, patient as a creditor.

And it was while packing up, stamping feeling back into my fingers, that I saw it — down-slope, maybe half a mile, at the treeline where the river bent: two figures in high-tech shell jackets, moving fast, moving light, moving with the particular economy of men who have done this before and been paid for it.

They weren't following us. They were ahead of us.

Tomas saw them a heartbeat after I did, and I felt him go still beside me, the stillness of a man recalculating everything.

"Well," he said quietly. "Someone gave them the shortcut."
