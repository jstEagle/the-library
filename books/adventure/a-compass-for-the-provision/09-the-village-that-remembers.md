## 9. The Village That Remembers

The hamlet of Cwm-uchaf announced itself the way old men announce themselves at parties: a single light in a window, a dog with opinions, and the smell of woodsmoke that said *you're late and we knew you'd be late.*

We came down off the drover's track at dusk with the mules complaining in that low conversational way mules have, as if they've been rehearsing their grievances all day and intend to deliver them whether or not anyone asked. Yara walked ahead of me, which she'd been doing since morning, holding the compass out like a lantern that only lit one direction. The needle hadn't wavered since the saddle below Pen Gwern. It pointed up-valley, into the dark, toward Sarn Ddu, patient as a debt.

"You could let me carry it," I said.

"I could," she said, and didn't.

Fair enough. It was her father's compass, her father's ghost, her father's bad habit of walking into mountains and not coming back. But I'll admit it stung a little, being trusted with the mules and the route and the weather judgment and not with a lump of brass the size of a pocket watch. Trust is a strange currency. People will hand you their lives on a rope above a scree slope and then guard their grief like loose change.

Cwm-uchaf was four houses, a chapel that had given up on Sundays, and a byre that had been converted to something between a bunkhouse and a museum of other people's patience. The light in the window belonged to the last house, and the woman who opened its door before we even knocked belonged to a category of person I'd stopped believing in until I moved back here: someone who had been watching the path for an hour because she said the mules sounded wrong.

"Nant," she said, looking at me. "Tomas Vey's boy."

"Grandson," I said. "And I'm not a boy."

"You are to me. I changed your nappies at Eisteddfod." She stepped aside to let us in, then looked past me at Yara with the frank appraisal of a woman who has buried two husbands and outlived every doctor who told her to slow down. "And this is Montrose's girl."

Yara went very still. "You knew my father?"

"I knew he ate too fast and listened too little, which is the same thing in a man." She held out her hand. "Mrs. Caradoc. Beti to friends, and you're not one yet, but the night is long."

* * *

Her kitchen was warm in the way kitchens get when they've been warm continuously for a hundred years — heat soaked into the stone itself, so the room felt less heated than inhabited by warmth. She fed us cawl without asking what we wanted, which was correct, because what we wanted was cawl and we were too tired to know it.

I gave her the edited version of our business: survey work, weather window, heading for the high passes before they closed. She listened with her hands folded and her eyes doing something else entirely, and when I finished she said, "That was very nearly true," and refilled my bowl.

Yara set the compass on the table between us. I don't know if it was a decision or an accident — she'd been carrying it in her coat pocket for three days, touching it through the wool like a rosary — but there it sat, and Mrs. Caradoc looked at it the way you look at a face across a crowded room when the face belongs to a funeral.

"Where did you get that?"

"It found me," Yara said. "In the cave at Penhallow. The Provision."

"The Provision." Mrs. Caradoc said the word carefully, the way you handle a tool with a cracked handle. "My grandmother called it that. The sea gives back what you're owed, she said, though in my experience the sea mostly gives back what it's finished with, and it has poor taste."

She picked up the compass. Turned it over. Her thumb found the maker's mark on the case — three small stamps I'd noticed and dismissed as wear — and pressed each one in turn, like reading braille.

"Gwilym Sarn made this," she said. "My great-great-grandfather's cousin, depending on which family you ask, and everyone asks differently because nobody wanted to be related to him after."

"After what?" Yara asked.

"After he made three of them." Mrs. Caradoc set the compass down with more gentleness than she'd shown anything else that evening, including us. "Gwilym was a smith and a clockmaker and a man who believed the mountain owed the valley an accounting. There was trouble in the 1840s — the mine owners, the evictions, the records burned so no one could prove what land was whose. Gwilym took the parish books, the deeds, the debt ledgers, everything that proved who these people were and what they were owed, and he hid them. And he made three compasses to find them again. Needle doesn't point north. Points at what he buried."

I felt the hair on my arms stand up, which annoyed me, because I'm a practical man and practical men don't get gooseflesh over fireside stories. But I'd seen the needle. I'd watched it ignore north for six days with the serenity of a thing that has never once been wrong.

"Three," Yara said quietly. "Where are the others?"

"One is yours, apparently. One went down with Gwilym's son off the Cardigan coast — or so the family said, though families say a lot." Mrs. Caradoc looked at me, and there was something in her face I didn't like, something that had been waiting there since she opened the door. "And the third stayed in the valley. Passed from hand to hand. My father had it, during the war. He used it to find things people had lost. Wedding rings. Sheep. Once, a man who'd wandered off his mind in the snow."

"And now?" I asked.

"Now I don't know where it is," she said, "because my father sold it in 1998 to men who came asking about the mountain. Paid too much for it, which should have told us everything. Men who pay too much aren't buying. They're silencing."

* * *

The fire needed feeding and none of us moved to feed it. Outside, the wind had come around to the northwest, which meant snow within two days, maybe sooner, and I was doing the arithmetic I always do — hours of daylight, weight on the mules, distance to shelter — while part of my brain stood apart and repeated, *1998, 1998, 1998.*

"That was the year of the expedition," Yara said. Her voice had gone flat, which I'd learned was worse than shaking. "The Whitcombe Institute sent a team up. My father was with them. They reported finding nothing and losing nothing, and my father resigned a month later and never spoke about it again."

"He spoke about it here," Mrs. Caradoc said. "Or near enough. He came down twice that autumn. Bought supplies, asked about routes. Sat where you're sitting and drank tea and told me the mountain was full of lies, and then told me the Institute was full of worse ones." She leaned forward. "He asked me about Gwilym Sarn. About the hiding places. I liked him — don't look like that, girl, you can like a man who eats too fast — but I told him nothing, because the last time this valley talked about Gwilym's hoard to outsiders, we lost grazing rights for a generation."

"He wasn't an outsider," Yara said. "He was—"

"He was paid by the Institute, and the Institute wears whatever face gets it through the door." Mrs. Caradoc said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. Yara's jaw tightened and I saw her decide, visibly, not to argue with an old woman on her own hearthstone. Good instinct. I've argued with Beti Caradoc once, about a fence line, and I still lose sleep over it.

"The third compass," I said. "The one your father sold. You never learned who kept it?"

"My father dealt with a middleman. But the men who came asking — there were two of them, then, same as there seem to be two of them now, because institutions are like weather, they repeat themselves—" She paused, and I watched her decide how much to give us. "One of them left a card. My father kept it pinned to the beam there, above the dresser, as a warning to himself. I burned it when he died. But I remember the name, because it's not a name you forget. Renner."

Yara made a sound I'd heard once before, the day she read the journal entry about being silenced — a small exhalation, like air leaving a tire.

"Renner is here," she said. "In the range. Now. With the second compass, if your father sold it to them."

"If they still have it," Mrs. Caradoc said. "And men like that keep everything. That's the whole of what they are."

* * *

Later, when Yara had gone to the bunkroom with the journal and the letter she still carried unopened — I'd seen her take it out twice that evening and put it away twice, a ritual as fixed as prayer — Mrs. Caradoc and I sat with the last of the tea and the truth, which is stronger.

"You knew," I said. "When we walked in. You knew whose girl she was before she said a word."

"She has his walk. Elias Montrose walked like the ground owed him money." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "And I knew because I've been expecting her for twelve years. Or someone like her. The mountain sends someone every few years. Usually they turn back at the tree line. Usually they're fools."

"And this one?"

"This one's his daughter." She said it as if that settled the question, and maybe it did. "Tomas Vey. Your grandfather mapped that anomaly in 1911 with a theodolite and a stubbornness I respected, God rest him. He wrote in the margin that the needle pointed at something that *answered*. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your own blood drew the map these people are following."

"I know what my grandfather wrote."

"Then you know why you're really taking her up there, and it isn't the fee." She looked at me over the rim of her cup, and I looked at the fire, because Beti Caradoc has a way of looking at a man that makes lying feel like a skill you haven't practiced enough.

"There's a version of this," I said, "where I tell her to go home. Where the passes close, and the Institute men have the other compass, and the sensible thing is the coast."

"Yes. And there's the version where you remember that your grandfather spent forty years telling anyone who'd listen that the valley was robbed, and died with the margin note unfinished." She stood, joints announcing themselves, and banked the fire. "You're not a rescue man anymore, Tomas. But some things still need finding. Take her up. Come back down. In that order, preferably."

* * *

I didn't sleep well, which was standard, but the quality of not-sleeping was new. I lay in the byre loft listening to the wind test the roof slates and thinking about the shape of the trap we were walking into.

Here's what I kept turning over: two expeditions, twenty-five years apart, both armed with a compass that points at the same buried thing. The first one lost a man — or produced a man who never came home, which amounts to the same arithmetic for the daughter. The second one had Dr. Renner, whom I'd met exactly once, in the Penhallow pub, buying rounds for locals and asking questions with the specific gentleness of a man defusing something. He'd shaken my hand. His hand had been cold, and I remembered thinking at the time, *here's a man whose body knows something his face won't say.*

Now I knew what his face wouldn't say. It wouldn't say that he was carrying the twin of the instrument in Yara Montrose's pocket, and that somewhere under Sarn Ddu the two needles were pointing at the same silence.

Near midnight I heard the bunkroom floor creak, and looked down through the loft boards. Yara sat at the little table with a candle, the journal open, and she wasn't reading it. She was holding the folded letter flat on her palm, weighing it, the way you weigh a stone before deciding whether to throw it or build with it. Then she put it away again, blew out the candle, and lay down in the dark.

Twelve years, and she couldn't open four pages. I understood that better than I wanted to. There are letters you don't open because the contents might be worse than the silence, and worse silences than the contents, and you can live a long time in the gap between those two fears. I'd done it myself, with my grandfather's field notebooks, which sat in a box in my mother's house for nine years before I could read the last one.

In the morning the wind had backed to the north and gone quiet, which in this valley is not good news; it's the mountain holding its breath. Mrs. Caradoc packed us bread and cheese and a jar of something medicinal, and refused payment, and told Yara at the door, quietly, in Welsh, which she pretended not to understand and I pretended not to hear:

"*Mae'r mynydd yn cofio'r hyn y mae'r dynion yn ei anghofio.*"

The mountain remembers what men forget.

Then she took Yara's wrist, turned it over, and looked at the compass in her palm one last time.

"When you find what Gwilym hid," she said, "remember it isn't treasure. Treasure is what you take. This is what you *return*." She released her wrist. "There's a difference. Most people die without learning it. Your father, I think, was learning it at the end."

Yara's face did something complicated and then went steady. "Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me. Thank the mule. He's carrying everything."

We took the east track out of Cwm-uchaf as the sun came up hard and white behind Sarn Ddu, and the compass needle lay against the glass pointing at it, faithful as ever. It was only later, checking the load on the second mule, that I found what Mrs. Caradoc had slipped into the pannier among the oats: a folded paper, old and soft as cloth.

It was a copy of a page from my grandfather's 1911 survey book. The margin note, the one she'd quoted, complete at last.

Beneath it, in a different hand — older ink, older paper — someone had written a single line:

*The third compass went up the mountain in 1849 and never came down.*

Which meant the Institute men weren't carrying the twin.

They were carrying the original.
