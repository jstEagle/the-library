## 10. Weather Turning

The storm announced itself the way Tomas did everything: without apology and slightly ahead of schedule.

We had made the high hamlet of Cwm Bychan by early afternoon, and I was feeling quietly pleased with our progress — a dangerous emotion, as it turned out, because pleasure on a mountain is an invitation for the mountain to correct you. Tomas stood in the yard of the first cottage, sniffing the air like a man reading a letter addressed to someone else.

"That's not cloud," he said. "That's weather with an opinion."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning we're not sleeping here." He pointed east, where the ridge above the hamlet had gone the color of wet slate. "There's a bothy over the col. Old shepherd's shelter. Stone walls, iron roof, a hearth that mostly draws. We can make it before dark if we stop admiring the view."

I wanted to argue. Partly on principle — I'd learned long ago that agreeing with people too quickly made them comfortable, and comfortable people asked questions — but mostly because my legs had already voted and the vote was unanimous. So I shouldered my pack, checked the compass in its oilcloth wrap against my chest, and followed him up out of the village while the last of the light drained away behind us.

The bothy sat in a fold of the hill like something the landscape had swallowed and half-digested. One room, one window glazed with something between glass and memory, a stone trough outside that had probably watered sheep before either of us was born. Inside: two sleeping platforms, a table scarred by generations of knives and initials, and a hearth with a backlog of driftwood and peat left by some considerate ghost.

Tomas got a fire going with the brisk economy of a man who'd done it in worse places for people he liked less. I unpacked our food onto the table and pretended not to notice how little there was.

"How long?" I asked.

He glanced at the window, where the first spits of rain were already testing the glass. "Two days. Maybe three."

"Three days," I repeated. "In a room this size. With you."

"I've survived worse company."

"You haven't met my company. I'm told I'm difficult."

"You're told correctly." He hung his wet coat by the fire and sat down across from me, and for the first time since Penhallow he looked tired in a way he wasn't hiding behind competence. "Get some sleep, Montrose. Storms are honest that way. They don't care what you're chasing."

* * *

The storm arrived at midnight with the full weight of its conviction.

I woke to a sound like the sea deciding to become the sky — wind slamming the bothy's iron roof in long flat blows, rain turning to sleet against the single pane, the whole building shuddering but holding, the way old things do. The fire had burned to embers. In their light I could see Tomas sitting up on his platform, awake, watching the window.

"You knew," I said. My voice came out rougher than intended. "Before anyone else. How?"

"My grandfather mapped this range for forty years. He said weather up here is a conversation, and most people only listen when it's shouting." He fed a split of peat onto the embers. "You learn to hear it getting louder."

I pulled my blanket around my shoulders and joined him near the hearth. For a while neither of us spoke, which was its own kind of conversation. There's a specific intimacy to being trapped somewhere small with another person while the world outside tries to tear itself apart — you either talk or you go mad, and silence only postpones the choice.

It was Tomas who broke first, which surprised me.

"You measure everything in miles," he said. "I've noticed. Distances, elevations, hours to the saddle. You never say years."

The ember-light made his face hard to read, which I suspected was deliberate. I considered lying. It would have been easy; I'd had twelve years of practice.

"My father left when I was nineteen," I said finally. "Twelve years ago. He walked out of a dig site in Portugal with a duffel bag and no explanation, and I've been walking ever since. Site to site, contract to contract. If you keep moving fast enough, you don't have to arrive anywhere." I turned my hands toward the fire. "Miles are just years you can count without flinching."

"And the journal? The compass? That's not miles. That's going back."

"Don't romanticize it. I'm chasing a man who couldn't be bothered to send a postcard. That's not reconciliation, that's an audit."

"Is it?" He poked the fire, sending up a fan of sparks. "Because from where I sit, you're carrying his things against your chest like they're warm."

I didn't answer that, mostly because he was right and we both knew it. Outside, the wind found a loose sheet of roofing and played it like a bad orchestra. Somewhere under all that noise, I thought, was the mountain where my father had stopped existing as a person and become a mystery instead. Mysteries were easier. Mysteries didn't have daughters waiting up.

"Your turn," I said. "Fair's fair. You've spent three weeks telling me what not to do. A man doesn't develop that much caution without a reason."

Tomas was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he leaned back against the wall, and when he spoke, his voice had the flat evenness of someone reciting coordinates.

"Nine years ago I was mountain rescue. Glen Torren side of the range. We got a call-out in February — two climbers overdue, storm coming in exactly like this one." He nodded at the window. "My partner and I went up. Found one of them, a girl of twenty-two, hypothermic under an overhang. We were roping her down when the slope cut loose above us."

He stopped. I waited. The fire cracked through the pause like a clock.

"Avalanche took her. Took my partner too. I was on the anchor end — I held, which is the cruel joke of it. Held perfectly. Came down the next morning with the recovery team and dug them both out." His jaw worked once. "The inquiry cleared me. Said I did everything right. And I thought — right according to whom? Because she's still dead, and I still held."

"So you quit."

"So I quit. Took over my grandfather's maps and my mother's cottage and decided the safest thing to do with mountains is study them from below." He looked at me then, and there was nothing guarded in it, which somehow made it worse. "Until a woman walked into the Penhallow Arms asking about a legend I'd spent my whole life pretending was nonsense. And I said yes anyway. Still not sure why."

"Because you're curious."

"Because you looked like someone who'd hold the rope," he said simply. "Even if it killed you."

I hadn't expected that. I looked away first, into the fire, because there are things a face does in response to kindness that you don't want witnessed.

* * *

Morning came gray and furious, the storm settled in for the duration. We established a routine the way prisoners do: fire, tea, rationed food, the window checked hourly as though the weather might apologize and leave. Tomas produced a battered pack of cards from his kit and beat me at every game we tried, which I chose to attribute to cheating rather than skill, and he chose to accept as a compliment.

By the second afternoon the confinement had stripped us down to essentials. He told me about his grandfather's survey work — the anomaly in the range that official geology had dismissed as instrument error, the three brass compasses a local smith had made to track it, the way the old man had marked certain valleys *do not ask* in his field notes. I told him about digs in Crete and Anatolia, about learning stratigraphy the way other children learned prayers, about the particular loneliness of being raised by a man who loved the past more than the present and then having him prove it by joining it.

"He never wrote," I said. We were watching the sleet trace paths down the window. "Twelve years. Not once. And now I find out he was within fifty miles of me, in a cave I could have walked to, and he still—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I stopped and restarted. "What kind of man finds his daughter's postcode in a legend and doesn't use it?"

"The kind who thinks he owes her something too large to fit in a letter," Tomas said. "Or the kind who's a coward. Sometimes those are the same man."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No. Comfort's not on offer. Honesty is." He turned to face me fully. "You want him to be a monster, because monsters are simple. But the journal reads like a man terrified of something, and terrified men do stupid, unforgivable, human things. You'll have to decide which version to bury when we find him. Or find what's left."

The words landed harder than the storm outside. I got up abruptly and busied myself with the stove, and Tomas had the grace not to watch me do it.

That night, unable to sleep, I took out the compass and opened it by firelight. The needle swung, steadied, and pointed — as it always did — toward Sarn Ddu, patient as a dog at a door. Twelve years of miles, and every one of them had apparently been aimed here, by a needle that had known before I did.

"What are you," I whispered to it.

"Tomorrow we'll ask it properly," Tomas said from his platform. I hadn't realized he was awake. "Storm breaks by dawn. My grandfather's rule — third morning, always clear. Get some sleep, Montrose. You'll want your legs."

* * *

He was right, of course. Dawn came rinsed and cold and impossibly clear, the whole range standing sharp against a sky scrubbed blue, snowline glittering far above us like a promise or a warning. We packed in silence, efficient now, two people who'd learned each other's rhythms in a room the size of a grave.

At the door I paused and looked back at the bothy — the scarred table, the dead hearth, the place where two strangers had accidentally become something else.

"Tomas," I said. "The rope. When it cut loose. You held."

"I know."

"Well." I stepped out into the cold. "For what it's worth — I'm glad you're the one holding ours."

Behind us, the bothy kept its silence. Ahead, the path climbed toward the col, and the compass in my pocket tugged faintly northward-toward-nothing, toward everything, toward whatever my father had died trying to reach.

And far below, half-hidden in the fold of the valley we'd left that morning, I caught a glint of movement — canvas, a mule, two figures moving fast along the trail we'd taken yesterday.

Someone was following our line exactly.

Tomas saw them a heartbeat after I did, and his face went grim.
