## 11. The Journal's Last Entry

The bothy smelled of peat smoke and old sheep, which is to say it smelled like every bothy I'd ever slept in, which is to say it smelled like the inside of my father's absence. Tomas had gone out at first light to check the mules and argue with the weather. I stayed by the stove with the journal open on my knees, and for three hours I did not turn a single page.

This is a thing people don't understand about reading the dead. It isn't that you can't. It's that each page is a door, and you know exactly how many doors are left.

I made tea I didn't drink. I re-laced my boots. I counted the cracks in the slate floor — eleven, one of them shaped like a river delta if you squinted, and I squinted at it for an embarrassingly long time because squinting at geology felt like work and work was safe. Then I ran out of ways to be a coward with dignity, and I turned the page.

* * *

The entries after the cave changed. I want to be precise about this, because precision was his religion and I inherited it like everything else he left me — as a burden with no instructions.

Before Penhallow, Elias Montrose wrote like a man building a case. Dates, grid references, strata descriptions, the occasional dry joke aimed at some colleague's haircut or the Institute's expense forms. Reading him was like listening to a man talk to himself in a locked room, comfortable in the lock.

After Penhallow, the room wasn't locked anymore. Or maybe it was, and something else was inside it with him.

*14 October. The Provision is real. Not the legend — the legend is a coat they hung on it later, all that nonsense about being shown what you're owed, designed to keep children out and romantics in. What's real is older than the story. The cave is a door someone built on purpose, and doors on purpose mean owners, and owners mean records.*

*The Institute knows. That's the part I keep circling back to. Halloran didn't send me here to disprove the sea cave. He sent me here hoping I wouldn't find it.*

I sat with that name for a while. Halloran. Director Halloran, whose portrait still hung — I would have bet money on it — in the Whitcombe Institute's front hall, all silver hair and benevolent jowls, the face of a man who endowed two scholarships and buried at least one expedition. I'd shaken his hand when I was nine. He'd told me I had my father's eyes, which even then struck me as a strange thing to say, since my father was standing right there and could presumably confirm it himself.

*17 October. Found the smith's mark again, third time, always facing inland. Three compasses, the hamlet woman said. She wouldn't say who held the others. People up here measure what they tell you very carefully, like tea leaves.*

*The compass doesn't point north. I've stopped pretending that's a malfunction. It points along the old drover roads, the ones that don't appear on any Ordnance map after 1840. Someone wanted a way home that couldn't be burned.*

Someone wanted a way home that couldn't be burned. I read that sentence four times. My father, who had once dismissed the entire legend of the Provision as tourist bait in a lecture hall full of paying adults — I'd been there, twelve years old in the back row, swollen with pride at how clever he was — my father had sat in some cold room with this same tin box and written down the truth like a man setting down something heavy.

And then he hadn't sent it to anyone.

* * *

*21 October. Renner's predecessor — Voss — has arrived in Penhallow asking after me. He carries the second compass. I know because he showed it to the innkeeper like a warrant. These men think paper makes them legal. Up here the only law is weather, and weather doesn't take bribes.*

*I've decided to go up ahead of them. Alone. If I'm wrong about what's in the chamber, no harm done except to my reputation, which is already a ruin with good bones. If I'm right—*

He never finished that sentence either. I noticed that. My father, the most complete man I ever knew, the man who annotated his grocery lists, kept leaving sentences unfinished in this notebook like doors left open behind him.

*23 October. Tomas Vey's grandfather walked me as far as the tree line. Good man. Suspicious of me, rightly. Told me the mountain keeps what's given to it and returns what's owed, and I told him that was superstition, and he looked at me the way you look at a man who hasn't yet lost anything worth keeping.*

*He was right, of course. I'm writing this at the saddle below Sarn Ddu and I have already lost the ability to pretend I came here for scholarship.*

*What I actually came here for—*

Another break. But this time, instead of moving on, he'd drawn a line under it. A hard rule of ink, the kind he used to separate sections in his site reports. Below the line, the handwriting changed. Slower. The letters rounder, less guarded. As if he'd put down the archaeologist and picked up the man.

*Yara,*

My own name, sitting there in blue-black ink on yellowed paper, hit me harder than any revelation in the whole journal. Not *my daughter*. Not *the child*. Yara. He'd written my name the way he used to say it across dig sites, two syllables, the second one always slightly louder, so I'd know he meant business and also that he was pleased.

*If you're reading this, then the sea gave you the tin, which means the sea decided you were owed it, which means I finally believe in something your grandmother would have called nonsense and I spent forty years calling methodology.*

*You were twelve when I left. You'll want to know why, and I could give you the version with funding cycles and professional jealousy, and all of it would be true and none of it would be the truth. The truth is that I found the first letter from the chamber's records — a debt forgiven, a family restored, names written down by a blacksmith who understood that what a community loses first is its memory of itself — and I realized I had become a man who catalogued other people's inheritances while abandoning my daughter to drift through mine.*

*That's not an excuse. Excuses are what we hand people instead of the bill.*

*Voss wants the chamber emptied and erased. The Institute has spent seventy years making sure the high valleys forget what was done to them — the clearances, the evictions, the debts invented out of spite and enforced with lawyers. A vault of restored names is a weapon pointed at everything they've built. I am going up tomorrow to make sure it stays buried until it can be protected. I don't know what that costs. I'm writing this in case it costs everything.*

*You should know that I measured the distance from Penhallow to your last known dig site every week. Four hundred and six miles. I told myself I wasn't coming to you because you were safer without me, and that was true, and it was also the smallest kind of cowardice wearing a large coat.*

*Whatever you decide to do with what I've left you — come up or turn back, forgive me or don't — I want you to know that the compass was never pointing at treasure. It was made to point at what you're owed. And what you're owed, Yara, is not gold. It's the truth about where you come from, and an apology from the man who made you go looking for it alone.*

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm s—*

The entry stopped there.

Not faded. Not torn. Stopped, mid-word, the pen pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the next page, the way a hand does when it stops being a hand and becomes a fist. There was no date after it. No tidy sign-off. Just the triple apology breaking off like a rope under load, and then nothing, and then — tucked into the binding's gutter, folded twice, soft-cornered from years of being handled by a man who apparently took it out and read it and couldn't finish that either — an envelope.

My name on the front. Nothing else. No address, because there was nowhere on earth he could have been sure of finding me, and we had both known it, and he had written to me anyway.

* * *

I held the letter for a long time without opening it.

Outside, the storm that had pinned us in the bothy threw its weight against the walls, and Tomas's mules complained down in the lean-to, and the stove ticked through its cooling, and I sat in the middle of all that sound holding a piece of paper the way you hold a bird — too tight and it dies, too loose and it's gone.

Here is the thing I could not explain to Tomas, and can barely explain now. The journal was evidence. Evidence is manageable. Evidence goes in reports, gets cited, gets argued over by people with titles. I could carry evidence up a mountain.

But the letter was not evidence. The letter was my father's voice, unmediated, addressed to me, and it contained — I knew this the way you know the weight of a pack before you lift it — the apology he'd broken off three times in ink. The fourth attempt was in the envelope. And some superstitious animal part of me believed, absolutely, that if I opened it before I reached wherever he'd written it *for*, I would lose something. That the words needed the mountain between us the way a seed needs soil. That grief, delivered early, curdles.

Or maybe I just wasn't brave enough. Both explanations fit. They usually do.

"You haven't turned a page in an hour."

Tomas stood in the doorway, snow in his hair, looking at me with the particular expression of a man who has learned not to touch other people's open wounds but reserves the right to comment on the bleeding. He'd taken off his gloves and was flexing his fingers, and I noticed — the way you notice things when you're desperate to notice anything else — that his knuckles were scarred in a pattern I recognized from frostbite tables in field manuals. Old damage. His own unfinished sentence, probably.

"There's a letter," I said. "From my father. To me. He never sent it."

Tomas came in, stamped the snow off, and crouched by the stove without looking at me. "And?"

"And I can't open it."

"Can't or won't?"

"Is there a difference that matters to you?"

"Aye." He fed a split log to the fire. "One of them gets you killed on a mountain."

I almost laughed. It came out as something else, something with more air in it, and I bent over the envelope so he wouldn't see my face do whatever it was doing. Twelve years of practice at not crying in front of strangers, undone by a fold of paper and a fire.

"He wrote it up there," I said, when I trusted my voice. "At the saddle, maybe higher. He wrote it knowing he might die delivering it, and then he died anyway, and it sat in a tin in a sea cave for twelve years waiting for me to walk in out of a storm." I turned the envelope over. The seal had long since failed; the flap lifted at a breath's touch. "It's right here. All I have to do is—"

"Don't," Tomas said.

I looked at him.

"Not because of superstition," he said, though his grandfather's proverb was plainly standing somewhere behind his eyes. "Because you'll need it. Whatever's up on Sarn Ddu — and your journal says it's worse than treasure, it's men like Voss and whoever Voss answers to — you'll need something that isn't a map or a weapon. Something you haven't opened yet." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own eloquence, and jabbed the fire harder than it deserved. "My grandmother used to save the last of the jam for the worst morning. Same principle."

"That's not a principle, that's hoarding."

"It's strategy." He glanced at the envelope, then away, giving it privacy, which was somehow the kindest thing anyone had done for me in years. "Eat something. Storm's supposed to lift by midnight. We move at first light, and after that there's no shelter above the tree line worth the name."

He went back out to the mules. I sat with the letter in my lap and the journal open to the broken word — *I'm s-* — and I thought about my father pressing that pen into the page, and what had interrupted him. Not death, probably. Death doesn't interrupt mid-syllable. More likely footsteps. Wind. The sound of someone coming up the scree who shouldn't have been there. Voss, maybe, or the mountain itself, or just the hour when a man runs out of courage and calls it nightfall.

I wrapped the letter back in the oilcloth with the compass, and I noticed — for the first time, and it stopped my breath — that the needle had moved.

All evening it had lain steady toward Sarn Ddu, patient as a dog. Now it trembled, swinging in small agitated arcs, southeast and back, southeast and back. Toward the pass. Toward the route we'd take at first light.

Toward something that was moving too.

I got up and looked out the bothy's small window at the white churn of the pass, seeing nothing but weather, understanding perfectly well that weather was not what the needle was pointing at. Somewhere out in that murk, other feet were climbing. Other hands held the second compass, the one Voss had shown the innkeeper like a warrant, and it was pointing at the same chamber, and the race I had been telling myself was metaphor had just become arithmetic.

I laced my boots. I packed the oilcloth against my spine, where it would be hardest to steal.

First light was five hours away, and for the first time since Penhallow, I wished it were farther.
