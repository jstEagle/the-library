## 12. Cut Off

There is a particular sound a river makes when it has decided against you. It's not dramatic — no roar, no thunder. It's a kind of cheerful indifference, water going about its business over stones that used to be your stepping-stones and are now somewhere downstream doing someone else's harm.

I heard that sound on the third morning above the tree line, at the crossing my grandfather's maps called Pont y Lludw — the Bridge of Ashes, which is either a reference to an old charcoal burn or a warning, depending on how much you trust names.

I trusted this one.

"Cache should be under the east bank," I said, pointing with my chin because my hands were busy keeping me upright on wet scree. "Grey boulder with a split face. I wrapped everything in oilcloth and weighted it with a stone cairn."

Yara was ahead of me, moving the way she'd moved all week — fast, economical, like a woman paying off a debt to distance itself. She'd barely spoken since the bothy. Something had shifted between us in those two storm-bound days, something honest, and honesty, in my experience, is like a fire in a bothy: warm while it lasts, and then you have to go back out into the cold and pretend you didn't see each other's bare faces.

She reached the boulder first. She stopped. And I knew before she said anything, because her whole body changed — the way a dog changes when it catches a scent it doesn't like.

"Tomas." Her voice was very level. Level voices are worse than shouting. Shouting means there's still something left to burn. "The cairn's down."

I came up beside her. The stones of my cairn lay scattered along the bank like teeth knocked loose, arranged with none of the casual disorder a flood would leave. A flood throws things. This had been *placed*. And where the cache had been, there was nothing but a rectangle of pressed grass and one item left behind with what I could only describe as courtesy: a single ration tin, dented, empty, set upright in the middle of the hollow like a headstone.

"They took it," Yara said. "All of it?"

I crouched. Checked the second cache point, thirty yards downstream, tucked in a hawthorn root. Also stripped. Rope gone. Fuel gone. Half our food gone. And —

"The journal," she said.

I hadn't wanted to say it first. There is a difference between losing your supper and losing the only map to your father's last days, and I'd been hoping to break the news gently, the way you'd rather tell a man his house burned than hand him the ash piece by piece.

"They took the journal," I confirmed.

Yara stood very still. Up here stillness looks different than it does in a valley. In a valley, stillness is rest. Up here it's an animal conserving heat, or deciding.

"Renner," she said.

"Or his man. Fenn. Renner doesn't strike me as the type to do his own digging." I looked at the ground around the cache. Two sets of boot prints, one heavy-heeled, one light. They'd come up from the south side, from the lower track — the sensible route, the one I'd rejected because it was exposed for two miles to anyone watching from the ridge. Which meant they'd watched us choose our route, and chosen theirs accordingly, and arrived here after we'd passed and camped below. We'd walked past our own grave goods without knowing.

"When?" Yara asked. "We crossed yesterday afternoon. We camped—"

"Two hours below. They waited until dark, came up, took it, went back down or pushed on. Either way they've got twelve hours on us and everything we owned." I said it flatly, because there was no version of saying it softly that helped.

Then Yara Montrose did something I did not expect, though perhaps by then I should have. She laughed. One short, ugly syllable of it, aimed at no one.

"Of course," she said. "Of course. Twelve years I've been chasing this man's ghost, and the first thing I inherit from him is enemies."

* * *

Let me tell you what we had left, because I counted it twice, the way you count money when you already know the number and are hoping it will be embarrassed into changing.

One stove, because I carry mine inside my jacket rather than in a cache, an old habit from rescue work that has more than once been the difference between a bad night and a last night. Half a canister of fuel. Three days of food if we ate like monks, two if we ate like people. My knife, Yara's knife, sixty feet of thin cord I'd braided spare from the rope offcuts in my pack. First aid kit. One tarp. And the compass.

The compass. Brass, heavy for its size, with a needle that ignored north the way a cat ignores an instruction. It sat in Yara's pocket now, and every so often her hand went to it — checking, I think, that it was still there, the way you check a wound.

"So," she said, when I'd finished the inventory. "Options."

"We go back. Down to the hamlet, resupply, come round by the shepherd's path a day later. Cold, humiliating, survivable."

"And they reach Sarn Ddu first. With the journal. With everything he wrote." She turned to look up-valley, toward the massif, where Sarn Ddu stood with its head already in cloud. "They'll strip the site. Whatever's up there, whatever he found — Renner will make it Institute property by Thursday and a footnote by Christmas."

"Maybe there's nothing up there. Maybe he chased a legend into a hole in the ground and—"

"Don't." She said it quietly, and I shut up, because I've been told *don't* in exactly that tone exactly twice in my life, and both times the speaker had been right and I had been about to say something unforgivable.

Here is what I knew that she didn't say aloud: she was thinking about turning back. I could see it assembling itself behind her eyes — the whole architecture of retreat, load-bearing excuses going up floor by floor. *It's not safe. We're unequipped. The passes close early. It's what Tomas wants anyway.* And underneath all of it, the foundation stone: *he left me once; the mountain will just do it again.*

I understood that foundation stone better than she knew. I'd built on it myself, after Cader. After the boy I couldn't reach. You tell yourself the mountain teaches humility, and it does, but mostly it teaches you to stop wanting things, because wanting things up there gets people killed, and it's easier to kill the wanting than to bury the people.

So I did the thing I least wanted to do, which was argue her out of the sensible choice.

"There's another way," I said.

* * *

My grandfather, Emrys Vey, was a drover's son and then a drover, and then, when the roads ate the trade, a shepherd, and then, when age ate that, a maker of maps that no Ordnance Survey would ever certify. He drew the paths nobody official needed: the sheep tracks, the shortcuts, the routes that existed because a hundred generations of animals and the humans following them had worn them into the hillside the way water wears limestone — invisibly, patiently, permanently.

One of those routes left the valley half a mile above us and climbed the north wall through a fold called Cwm Cerrig — the Hollow of Stones — and came out on the high shoulder of the range well above the lower track. No bridge needed. No cache. Nothing anyone could steal, because there was nothing there to take except altitude.

"It's unmapped," Yara said.

"It's mapped. Just not by anyone whose maps get printed." I tapped my temple. "It's mapped here. My grandfather walked it forty times with sheep and once, memorably, with a lost geologist and a broken ankle."

"How long?"

"A day and a half instead of half a day. Steep. Exposed in weather. If the cloud comes down in the Hollow, we feel our way by touch and pray the stones stay friendly." I looked at her. "And if we take it, we're committed. There's no turning back halfway. The terrain doesn't allow for regret."

Yara looked up the valley for a long time. The river went on being indifferent. Somewhere above, a raven said something unkind about us both.

"If we go back," she said slowly, working it through the way she worked a stratigraphy, layer by layer, oldest reasoning first, "we lose the season. The passes close early this year — you said so yourself. And even if we got back up in time, they'd have had the journal for days. They'd know every note he made, every bearing, every—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "He wrote about me. In that journal. Things he never said to my face. And now it's in *Fenn's* pocket."

"I know."

"Do you?" She rounded on me, and there it was, the anger finally arriving, late and enormous, like a train making up time. "Twelve years, Tomas. He couldn't post a letter. He couldn't send a card. But he wrote pages and pages, and now some Institute man is reading them on a mountainside, reading my father's handwriting about *me*, and I'm standing here with three days of food and half a fuel canister, and everyone keeps telling me the sensible thing is to go home. Everyone always tells me the sensible thing is to go home."

The wind picked at us. I let the silence sit until it stopped being a weapon and started being a space.

"My grandfather had a saying," I offered. "He said the mountain strips you down to what you actually brought. Not the gear. The gear's just weight. What you brought." I shrugged. "You've been carrying this twelve years. Turns out it weighs less than the food they stole."

She stared at me. For a moment I thought she'd throw me in the river, and honestly the river deserved the company. Then something in her face settled — not softened, settled, like sediment finding its bed.

"The Hollow of Stones," she said. "Show me."

* * *

We buried what we couldn't carry. That sounds more ceremonial than it was: we wrapped the tarp's spare cord, the empty tins, a change of socks, in the oilcloth scrap Fenn had so thoughtfully left, and stowed it under the hawthorn root, against the possibility — remote, I judged, but I've been wrong about remoteness before — that we'd come back this way needing it. Yara set the empty ration tin upright on top of the little pile.

"For them?" I asked.

"For us," she said. "A marker. So if anyone comes looking, they know two people stood here and decided not to be finished."

We crossed the river where it ran shallow over a gravel bar, cold enough to make the bones of our ankles file complaints, and climbed. The Hollow of Stones began as a suggestion between two crags and became a corridor, and the corridor became a staircase built by no one for everyone, and by midday we were in the heart of it, walled in by rock the colour of old iron, with the sky reduced to a bright ribbon overhead.

Yara walked in front. I noticed she'd stopped measuring the distance in miles. She'd started counting steps instead — I could see her lips move sometimes, marking hundreds — and I thought: there it is. That's what grief does when it finally stops running. It starts counting. It starts believing the count will end somewhere.

Halfway up the Hollow, she stopped at a place where the walls pinched together, and there, wedged in a crack at shoulder height, was a flat stone with letters cut into it. Old letters, lichen-filled, but legible to someone who'd spent her life reading surfaces.

"*Ffordd yr Defaid*," she read. "The Sheep Road. Someone marked it."

"My grandfather's grandfather, most likely. Or his. The Veys kept this path alive when everyone else forgot it existed." I touched the stone. It was warmer than the air, holding the sun. "Families keep strange inheritances."

Yara looked at the mark a long moment. Then she took out the compass, opened it, and held it flat on her palm.

The needle swung, hesitated, and settled — not north, never north — pointing up the Hollow, deeper into the range, toward Sarn Ddu and whatever waited behind its summit. Steady as a pulse.

"It knows where we're going," she said quietly.

"It knows where *it's* going," I corrected. "Whether we follow is still our decision."

She closed the lid with a click that sounded, in that stone corridor, like a door being locked from the inside. Then she put the compass away, faced uphill, and started counting again.

I followed. What else does a guide do? Though I'll admit, privately, to a feeling I hadn't had since my rescue days — that tight, awake sensation of walking toward something rather than away from it, and not knowing yet whether that was courage or just a different shape of foolishness.

Behind us, far below, the Bridge of Ashes gleamed in the sun, and the river kept its cheerful indifference, carrying our stolen supplies' story downstream to no one.

Ahead, the Hollow narrowed, and narrowed, and the mountain leaned in to hear what we'd decide next.
