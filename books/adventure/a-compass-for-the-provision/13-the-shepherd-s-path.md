## 13. The Shepherd's Path

The river took everything except our dignity, and I have to tell you, dignity weighs nothing in a pack.

We stood on the north bank of the Afon Llwyd at dawn, Tomas and I, surveying the wreckage of our cache like mourners at an open grave. The oilskin had been slit with a knife — not torn, not worried open by foxes, but cut with the tidy efficiency of people who considered theft a professional service. Our food gone. The spare rope gone. The tent gone. And the journal, my father's journal, twelve years of his handwriting, gone into the pockets of two men from the Whitcombe Institute who would read it the way auditors read ledgers: looking for the discrepancy, the number that didn't add up, the thing they could take.

"They left the mule," Tomas said.

The mule, whose name was Brân and whose opinion of all humans had been made clear to me on day one, stood hipshot among the boulders, chewing something with the air of a creature who had witnessed the robbery and decided not to get involved.

"They left the mule," I repeated.

"They're carrying what we can't carry. We're carrying what they can't." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "They took the food because they intend to be up there a long time. They left us the animal because they don't think we'll last the week."

I looked at the mountain. Sarn Ddu sat behind its foothills like a creditor waiting politely at the end of the table, and between us and it lay thirty miles of country that had already eaten one expedition whole.

"Tomas," I said. "Is there another way?"

He was quiet for long enough that I thought the answer was no. Then he did something I'd never seen him do: he took off his hat. Not to scratch his head, not because of the sun. He took it off the way you do in church, or at a graveside, and he looked east, toward a fold in the hills that looked to me exactly like every other fold in the hills.

"There's a path," he said. "It isn't on any map. The Ordnance Survey walked past it twice and their men were standing practically on top of it both times, which tells you something about maps."

* * *

His grandfather's name was Emrys Vey, and by the time we'd climbed an hour above the river I knew more about him than I knew about some members of my own family, which is to say I knew about his hands and his habits and the specific quality of his stubbornness. Emrys Vey had been a drover, then a shepherd, then — during two winters that Tomas described only in fragments — something else, something that involved walking ridgelines in weather that killed sheep and should have killed him.

"He moved stock this way for forty years," Tomas said. "Cattle up in spring, down in autumn. The drove roads are older than the churches out here. Older than the names on the maps. When the surveyors came, the old men didn't show them. Why would they? A map is a door, and you don't cut a door into a wall that's keeping something safe."

"And your grandfather showed you."

"My grandfather showed me when I was nine, and told me if I ever drew it, he'd know, and he'd come back from wherever dead shepherds go specifically to make my life difficult." Tomas stepped over a runnel of snowmelt without looking down. "I believed him about the coming back part. I still do."

The path, when I finally learned to see it, was everywhere and nowhere. It wasn't cut into the hillside so much as agreed upon — a tendency in the land, a line the grass grew thinner along, a sequence of stone stiles that no farmer had built and every farmer maintained. It climbed where no climb should be easy, threading between crags that would have turned back anyone reading the terrain honestly, and twice it dropped into hollows so deep and green they felt like held breath.

"You can't navigate this," Tomas said, watching me try. "That's the point of it. You can only remember it. There's a cairn shaped like a broken tooth — you keep it on your right shoulder until you smell peat, then you turn uphill at the rowan. That's a day's instruction. My grandfather spent three summers teaching it to me and hit me once with a crook for getting it wrong near a bog that would have swallowed me."

"A compassionate pedagogy."

"He was a shepherd. Compassion was for lambing season." But he said it softly, and I understood that the crook and the bog and the three summers were the most valuable inheritance Tomas Vey had ever been offered, and that he was now spending it on me.

I didn't thank him. Thanking him would have made it a transaction, and transactions could be regretted. Instead I memorized the broken-tooth cairn and the smell of peat and the rowan, and I kept my mouth shut, which for me constitutes a kind of prayer.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being robbed: it clarifies.

For eleven days I had carried the journal like a talisman, checking it at every halt, touching the tin in my pack the way you touch a bruise. My father's handwriting. His abbreviations, his terrible little sketches of stratigraphy, his marginal notes in Latin for no reason except that he liked how Latin made him feel. Losing it should have ended me. Instead, somewhere on the second day of the shepherd's path, I noticed that my shoulders had come down from around my ears, and I resented the discovery deeply.

Because here was the truth underneath: the journal had been a wall between me and the mountain. As long as I had his words, I didn't need my own eyes. I could follow his route, confirm his findings, walk in his footprints like a good daughter doing her homework. The Institute men hadn't just stolen a document. They'd stolen my excuse, and now the compass was the only voice I had left, and the compass had never once said anything comforting.

We checked it at noon, on a shelf of rock above a corrie full of cloud. I held it level in both hands, the way I'd learned to, and watched the needle swing off true north by a wide, contemptuous margin and settle — pointing east-northeast, up-slope, toward a shoulder of the range that hid Sarn Ddu completely.

"It hasn't wavered," I said. "Not a degree. Twelve days."

"Magnetic anomalies drift," Tomas said. "Weather changes them, solar storms change them. My grandfather mapped this one in 1931 and again in 1954 and swore it hadn't moved a hair either time." He looked at the needle, then at me. "Whatever it's pointing at, it isn't geology. Or it isn't only geology."

"That's not reassuring."

"I'm not in the reassurance business. I'm in the getting-you-there-alive business, and those are different industries entirely."

But that evening, when we camped in a hollow of wind-bent hawthorns and I woke at three in the morning to find him sitting up with the compass in his hands, turning it slowly, watching the needle hold its bearing like a dog holding a point — that evening I understood that he was no longer guiding me out of obligation, or even out of the rescue man's reflex that apparently lived in his spine like a second skeleton. He wanted to know. He'd never say it. Shepherds don't say things. But he turned the compass in the dark the way my father used to turn a newly cleaned artifact under lamplight, and I lay there pretending to sleep and felt something in my chest that I refused, for several more days, to name as hope.

* * *

The path tested us on the fourth day, though I suspect it had been testing us since the first step and simply saved its best material.

It brought us to a ravine — the mapless kind, the kind that exists only where the old drovers needed it to exist — and the crossing was a fallen ash tree, furred with moss, spanning perhaps eight meters of white water and bad air. Below it the river ran green and furious through a slot no wider than a coffin.

Tomas went first with Brân, talking to the mule in that low continuous mutter he used instead of rope, and got her across with a composure that suggested the tree was a bridge and always had been. Then it was my turn, and I want to be honest about what happened, because honesty is the only currency this story trades in.

I froze.

Not dramatically. No trembling, no tears. I simply stood at the edge of the log with my hands empty and my mind supplying, in my father's voice, a complete technical analysis of moss coefficients and load dynamics and the precise angle at which a body rotates when it falls eight meters into moving water. Twelve years of competence, and it abandoned me on a mossy log over a green river, because competence was always just grief wearing work clothes, and grief had finally found a gap wide enough to speak.

"Yara." Tomas's voice came from the far side, level. "Don't look at the water."

"I'm not—"

"You're looking at the water. Look at me. The log doesn't care about the water. The log cares about your feet. One step. The moss ends a meter in; after that it's bark."

"How do you know where the moss ends?"

"Because I've crossed this tree since before you were born, and my grandfather crossed it forty years before that, and nobody has ever fallen off it, because falling off it is not something this path permits." He crouched, making himself small against the sky. "This road has carried cattle. It has carried coffins. It has carried my grandfather in a whiteout with a dead ewe on his back. It will carry you. Now stop arguing with it and walk."

And the strange thing — the thing I think about still — is that it worked. Not his logic; his certainty. I put my foot on the log and I did not look at the water and I did not calculate anything, and somewhere in the middle of the span, with the river roaring up through the slot and the spray cold on my face, I felt the strangest sensation: that the path itself had opinions, and that I had finally stopped insulting them.

On the far side my legs shook for a full minute. Tomas pretended to check Brân's hooves and gave me the minute without comment, which is, I have come to believe, the highest form of kindness available to a certain kind of Welshman.

* * *

That night we camped high, on a shelf below a crag the old maps called nothing at all, and Tomas cooked the last of the oats with snowmelt and a pinch of salt and declared it, with a straight face, *cawl*. I ate it and let him have the joke.

"Do you know why I agreed to this?" he said suddenly, to the fire rather than to me. "In the pub, that first night. You probably thought I was after the fee."

"I assumed you were after the fee."

"The fee was insultingly small and you knew it." He poked the fire. "My grandfather talked about the Provision. Not often. Once, that I remember, when I asked him why he never took the drove road past Sarn Ddu anymore — it's the direct route, you understand, the old road runs right under the mountain, and after 1954 he always went around, two days out of his way, every single year. And he said—" Tomas paused, and I realized his hands weren't steady. "He said, *There are debts on that mountain that aren't mine to collect.*"

The fire cracked. Somewhere below us, a fox screamed and thought better of it.

"What does that mean?"

"I've had thirty years to decide," Tomas said. "I've decided it means he went up there. Once. In '54, the same year he re-mapped the anomaly. And whatever he found — or whatever he was shown — he came down and never spoke of it and never went back, and he made his compass—" He stopped.

"Made his what?"

Tomas reached into his shirt and pulled out a cord I had taken for a bootlace, and on the cord hung a small brass object, dull with age, that I had genuinely never seen despite sharing camps with this man for two weeks. He turned it over once, letting the firelight catch it, and put it away again.

"Three compasses," he said. "The smith made three. Your father's journal said so, before the Institute men took it. One points to the chamber. One points home — that's the one the hamlet keeps, the one the old woman showed us. And the third one my grandfather wore under his shirt for the rest of his life and never once let me see the face of." He looked at me across the fire, and his eyes were very tired and very awake. "Until tonight. Because tomorrow we cross onto the high ground, and after that there's no path, only bearing, and I'll not lead you blind into my grandfather's debts."

He passed it to me. The third compass. Its needle didn't tremble the way the other one did; it lay still, pointing — I checked against the stars — almost exactly opposite to ours. Away from Sarn Ddu. Toward Penhallow. Toward home.

"It's not broken?" I asked.

"No," Tomas said quietly. "I don't think any of them are broken. I think each one only knows what it's for. Yours wants what's buried. This one wants what's left behind." He took it back and knotted the cord around his neck again, under his shirt, over his heart. "When we come down off that mountain — if we come down — I want to be sure I still know which direction home is."

I lay awake a long time after that, listening to the wind learn the shape of the crag above us, thinking about a man who carried a compass that pointed away from everything he'd survived, and a father who carried one that pointed toward everything he couldn't face, and a daughter who'd inherited the wrong instrument and was only now learning to read it.

* * *

We gained the tree line on the sixth morning, and the world changed its rules.

The shepherd's path thinned to a suggestion, then to a memory, then to nothing but Tomas's certainty and the compass's flat insistence. The birches gave out. The rowans gave out. Even the grass surrendered to stone and lichen and a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse negotiating. Above us Sarn Ddu revealed itself in pieces through moving cloud — black shoulder, white scar, summit lost — and the needle swung once, twice, and locked onto it like a sentence finally finding its verb.

"We're being followed," Tomas said.

I'd felt it too, for hours — that prickle between the shoulder blades, the sense of the land holding more than it displayed. I'd blamed wildlife. I'd blamed altitude.

"How far?"

"Half a day behind. Maybe less. They're moving well." He shaded his eyes and scanned the slope we'd climbed, and I watched his jaw tighten. "They've got someone reading ground sign. That's not Renner. That's the quiet one."

"Halloway."

"Halloway," he agreed grimly. "The one who never talks. The dangerous ones never talk."

We stood a moment in the thin bright cold, the compass steady in my hand, the mountain huge above us, and behind us the whole green world we'd crossed, and somewhere in it two men walking our trail backward toward us like a tide coming in.

"Tomas," I said. "How much farther?"

He looked up at Sarn Ddu, at the black shoulder where the cloud tore and healed, and when he answered, his voice had gone quiet in the way it did when he was quoting someone dead.

"One day," he said. "If the weather holds. Two, if it doesn't. And Yara—" He nodded east, toward a shallow valley below the ridge we'd reach by nightfall, where — I saw it as he pointed, a thread of grey against the brown — smoke was rising. Thin, deliberate, contained. A camp, pitched with discipline, in the exact place the mountain's own geometry would force us to pass.

Someone was already above us on the mountain.

And they were waiting.
