## 14. Above the Tree Line

The trees gave out on us the way a conversation gives out between people who've said too much — gradually, then all at once.

One morning there were birches, stunted and apologising for themselves, and by noon there was nothing but stone and a kind of grass that looked like it had given up on being green and was just going through the motions. The air thinned with every switchback. I felt it in my chest first, then in my temper, which is the usual order for me.

Yara felt it in neither, or hid it well. She climbed like a woman settling an old argument with the mountain, one handhold at a time, and she didn't waste breath on talk. I'd stopped wasting mine on complaints somewhere below the last proper forest, when I'd said something about the gradient and she'd looked at me with that expression — the one that says *you volunteered* — and I'd decided to save my lungs for the business of not dying.

The mules had stayed behind at the hamlet, as arranged. Old Mrs. Carrow's nephew was walking them back down the shepherd's road for a fee I considered reasonable and Yara considered extortion, which I told her was the definition of a fair price in these hills. So now it was two packs, two people, one compass, and a mountain that had been waiting a long time for somebody foolish enough to come and ask it questions.

We made camp that first night above the tree line in a hollow the wind hadn't found yet, and I built the fire small, the way my grandfather taught me — *a fire you can cover with your hat is a fire that won't be your undoing* — and we ate cold oatcake and hot tea and said very little.

It was Yara who broke the silence, which surprised me. She'd been quieter than usual since the hamlet, since the old woman put her cracked hands on the compass and named it.

"Tomas." She was turning the compass over in her fingers, the brass catching the last of the light. "Your grandfather. The man who mapped the anomaly. Did he ever say what he thought it was?"

I fed a twig to the fire. "He said it was a lie the mountain told to iron."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer he ever gave me that I've found to be true." I looked at her. "You're hoping it's a hoard. Gold, Roman coins, whatever the journal promised."

"I'm hoping it's *something*," she said. "Something that was worth twelve years."

And there it was, laid out between us like a stone on a grave. I didn't pick it up. I'd learned in the bothy, in those two days of storm and confession, that Yara Montrose didn't want comfort. She wanted witnesses. So I nodded, and poured her more tea, and we slept in shifts because I'm a careful man and she was starting to trust my carefulness, which frightened her more than the mountain did, I think.

* * *

We came over the shoulder of the ridge on the second morning and I saw the smoke before I saw anything else.

A thin grey thread, rising from a hollow perhaps half a mile below and east of us, in the lee of a crag. Campfire smoke, banked low, tended by someone who knew what they were doing. My stomach did what it always does when I see other men's fires in country I thought was empty: it dropped.

"Yara." I put my hand on her shoulder and pointed. She went very still, the way she does — the surveyor's stillness, all her attention funnelled down to a single point — and then she was beside me behind a boulder, binoculars out of her pack, moving low and smooth.

"Two tents," she said. "Maybe three. Blue and orange. Institute colours." Her voice was flat, but I'd been listening to her long enough now to hear the floor of the flatness, the thing underneath. "Renner."

"How far ahead does that put them?"

"Half a day. Maybe less. They've got the journal — they've had the route." She lowered the binoculars. Her knuckles were white around them. "They've had everything."

"Not everything." I tapped the compass case at her belt. "They've got a compass that points at the same mountain we're standing on. What they haven't got is my grandfather's road, or the sense to use it."

She almost smiled. Almost. "So what do we do? Go down there and take it back?"

I looked at her properly then. She meant it. I could see it in her — the fury banked down to coals, the twelve years of miles all pointed at that camp like the compass needle pointed at the peak. She wanted to walk down there and put her hands on Dr. Renner's throat, and she was strong enough and angry enough that she might even succeed, right up until his friend put a boot in her ribs or a rope around hers.

"No," I said.

"Tomas—"

"Listen to me. Two men down there, maybe three, fresh, supplied, and holding your father's journal. We are two people, half-fed, at altitude, with a week of hard ground between us and help. If you go down that slope, you don't get the journal back. You get a broken ankle and a long conversation, and I get to explain to the shepherds why I brought you up here to die." I kept my voice level. "There's another way. There's always another way in these hills. That's the whole point of them."

She stared at me, and I watched the argument happen behind her eyes — the fury and the sense, going at each other like two billy goats on a narrow ledge. The fury had been winning for twelve years. I was gambling that the sense had been getting stronger lately. It was, a little. I'd seen it. She'd started sleeping through the night, for one thing.

"The shepherd's path," she said at last. "It comes around above them?"

"It comes around above everything. That's what shepherds understood that road-builders never did. You don't fight the mountain for the low ground. You take the high ground and let the mountain fight for you." I nodded down at the camp. "We go wide, we go quiet, we go above them, and we don't stop. Every hour we're ahead of them is an hour they spend in bad country wondering where we went."

"And the journal?"

"Is paper," I said. "You've read it. It's in here now—" I touched my own temple, then, before I could think better of it, touched hers, lightly, at the edge of her hair. She didn't pull away. "Whatever's written in that book, you already carry it. Renner's carrying paper. Paper burns wet and blows away in wind. You know what I'd call that, where I come from? A bad trade he made."

She looked at my hand near her face for a moment, and then she shouldered her pack, and I knew I'd won, though I also knew that with Yara the winning was never final. It was more like a truce that had to be renegotiated every morning at dawn.

"Stealth," she said. "Fine. But if we get above them and there's a moment — a real moment —"

"If there's a real moment, we'll take it. I'm not a monk, Yara. I'm just not a corpse."

* * *

We moved for the rest of that day the way I'd been taught to move in rescue work, back when I was the kind of man who ran toward avalanches: low, spaced, silent, using the wind to carry our noise away from the camp rather than toward it. Yara was better at it than I expected. She'd spent twelve years on dig sites, she told me later, where the worst thing you could do was leave a mark, and she brought the same discipline to leaving no mark on a mountainside. She stepped where I stepped. She breathed when I breathed. By mid-afternoon we'd gained the ridge above the crag and the Institute camp was a toy village far below us, and I let myself feel, for exactly one minute, like a competent man.

That was when the wind turned.

It does that. It's the mountain's oldest trick — the wind swings with the sun, and a voice that was carried away from you at noon comes straight up the hill at three o'clock. I heard it first as a shape without words, a human cadence rising out of the hollow, and I grabbed Yara's arm and pulled her down behind a slab of rock furred with grey lichen, and we lay there like two stones among stones.

The voices came up to us in pieces. Wind took most of it. But pieces were enough.

"—Montrose's daughter, I'm telling you. Vey's the local guide, the one with the family in the high hamlet. They went up the drover's road — the old man's maps, probably—"

That was a voice I didn't know. The second voice I knew from the pub in Penhallow, from the two men in good boots asking careful questions. Dr. Renner. He had a voice like a well-kept ledger: everything in its place, nothing wasted, nothing warm.

"Then they're ahead of us and under-supplied," Renner said. A pause, the wind filling it. "Good. Let them. They'll slow down when the food runs out, and we'll collect what's left. The journal tells us everything we need. Montrose documented the approach in detail — he was thorough, right up until he wasn't."

Something in me went cold. Not at the threat. At the *tense*.

"Thorough," the other man said. "You make it sound like he finished."

"Montrose found it, Hollis. That's what the 1998 report doesn't say and what the Institute has spent fifteen years not confirming. He found it, and he came down off that mountain alive, and he hid it — sealed it, moved it, did *something* — because he was a sentimental man and a frightened one, and then he spent the rest of his life making sure no one could follow his own trail. Including us. Including—" a dry pause, "—including his daughter, apparently. The compass is the key. It always pointed at the site. He kept it because he couldn't destroy it. Sentiment again."

I felt Yara go rigid against the stone beside me. Not a flinch. Something worse — a stillness so complete it was louder than movement. I put my hand flat on her back, between the shoulder blades, the way you steady a person on a ledge, and I felt her breathing change, and I kept it there.

Below, Renner went on, patient as erosion.

"The site exists. The anomaly confirms it. Montrose hid the hoard somewhere on Sarn Ddu and died — or disappeared, it hardly matters — keeping its secret. His daughter is following his trail to it right now, and she has the second compass, and when she finds it, we will be there. We don't need to hurry. We don't need to fight. We need to *follow*." A sound that might have been a laugh, filed and labelled. "The sentimental always do the work. We just arrive at the end of it."

The wind swung again, and the voices dropped away, and the mountain was quiet, and Yara was shaking.

Not crying. I want to be clear about that, because she'd have denied it and she'd have been right to. It was rage, the pure structural kind, the kind that has a load-bearing function in a person. She was shaking the way a bridge shakes — something enormous passing over something holding.

"They think he came down," she whispered. "They think he *came down* and hid it and walked away and never—" Her voice caught on the word and she pushed through it. "Twelve years. Twelve years he could have—"

"Yara."

"They're using him." She turned her head, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "They're using my father as a map. They're using *me* as a map. They don't even have to climb. They just have to follow the sentimental."

"Yes," I said, because she deserved the truth and not a softness. "That's what they're doing. And now you know it, and knowing it is worth more than the journal, because now you know they'll never rush and never risk and never do the one thing that would actually find it."

"Which is?"

"Think like your father." I held her gaze. "Renner thinks Montrose hid the hoard and came down. So Renner is climbing a mountain to find something a living man buried. But if your father never came down — if the last entry breaks off mid-sentence the way you told me — then Renner is following a ghost up a dead end, and the only person on this mountain who knows it is you." I let that land. "You're not the sentimental one he can use, Yara. You're the one who knows the truth. That's not a weakness. That's the whole game."

She lay there a long moment, breathing the thin air, and I watched the fury reorganise itself into something with a plan attached.

"He's up there," she said finally. Not a question. Not grief, either, or not only. A fact, being set down in its proper place, the way she'd set a datum line. "Whatever's in that chamber — he's up there with it. And Renner thinks he isn't. Renner's climbing toward a man who's been dead for twelve years, and I'm climbing toward my father."

"Yes."

"Then we go now. While they're eating their lunch."

* * *

We went. Wide, high, and quiet, along a shelf of ground that my grandfather's map called *Yr Esgair Llwyd* — the grey ridge — and that no Institute survey had ever bothered to name, because you can't put a name on paper until you've earned it with your feet.

By evening the Institute camp was a memory of smoke below us and behind us, and Sarn Ddu stood up into the last light ahead like a held breath, and the compass at Yara's belt was pulling so hard at its needle that she'd taken to resting her hand on it as she walked, the way you'd rest a hand on a dog that wanted to run.

She didn't say anything for a long time. Then, as we made our cold camp in the shadow of the first real scree, she said, without looking at me:

"Tomas. If we find him up there — if we find what's left of him — I want you to know I'm not turning back. Whatever we find. Whatever it costs."

"I know," I said.

"And I'm not sorry about that."

"I know that too." I handed her the tea. "That's why I'm here. Somebody has to be sorry on your behalf, or you'll do something permanent."

The ghost of a smile. Then she looked up at Sarn Ddu, black against the bruised sky, and her hand closed around the compass, and she said, so quietly I nearly missed it:

"He's *up there*, Tomas. Twelve years, and he's been up there the whole time, and Renner is following a dead man's shadow while I—" She stopped. Started again. "Tomorrow we find out which of us was right about him."

Above us, the first stars came out over the peak, and the wind coming down off the icefields carried a smell I knew and had been hoping not to know yet.

Snow was coming. A day early, maybe two.

We'd need every hour Renner's patience bought us.
