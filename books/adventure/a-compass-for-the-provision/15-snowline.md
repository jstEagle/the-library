## 15. Snowline

The first snowflake landed on the back of my hand at eleven minutes past noon, and I know this because I was looking at my watch instead of where I was going, which is how Tomas found me flat on my face in a scree field with the compass wedged under my ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Watching the time," he said, hauling me up by the pack straps. "That's new. Usually you watch the miles."

"I was calculating our margin."

"And?"

"And we have less of it than I'd like."

He grunted, which from Tomas was a full paragraph of agreement. Above us Sarn Ddu had stopped pretending to be scenery. For three days it had been a shape on the horizon, a gray tooth we walked toward the way you walk toward a dentist's appointment — steadily, and with a low-grade dread. Now it filled half the sky, its summit lost in cloud that wasn't cloud, or wasn't only cloud, and the air had gone thin enough that each breath felt like borrowing something I'd have to pay back later.

The snow came down properly within the hour. Not the fat theatrical flakes of a coastal storm but small, dry, businesslike snow, the kind that means to stay. It hissed on the stones around us and collected in the hollows of the old drover's road until the path itself became a rumor.

"There," Tomas said, and pointed with his chin, because his hands were busy with the mule's lead — no, not the mule; the mule had been left two days ago at the hamlet, and I still missed her more than I'd admit. He pointed with his chin at a shallow depression between two shoulders of rock. "The saddle. We make camp there or we don't make camp at all."

We made camp. It took an hour in the wind, and by the end of it my fingers had stopped being hands and become implements — useful for pegging canvas, useless for anything requiring tenderness. Tomas got the stove going inside the shelter of a boulder overhang, and we sat shoulder to shoulder watching the flame do its small stubborn work while the world outside went white.

I got out the compass because I always get out the compass, the way some people check their phones. Twelve days now since Penhallow. The needle had been steady for most of it — a thin dark line pointing up-valley, up-slope, always toward Sarn Ddu, patient as a dog waiting at a door. I'd stopped questioning it somewhere around the third day. That should have frightened me more than it did. A woman who stops questioning a compass that doesn't point north is a woman who has already decided what she believes, and I hadn't noticed myself deciding.

But tonight, when I opened the lid, the needle was mad.

It spun. Not wavering — spinning, full rotations, quick and frantic, like something trapped. I tilted the case. It spun faster. I set it flat on my knee and it kept spinning, a small brass carousel, and the sound it made against the pivot — a faint dry ticking — was suddenly the loudest thing in the world.

"Tomas."

He looked. His face did something complicated. "That's new."

"That's *new*, yes, thank you—"

"Set it down. Flat. Don't touch it."

I set it down on the flat stone beside the stove and we both watched it whirl like it was trying to escape the case entirely. The firelight caught the brass rim and threw little coins of light across the rock. Outside, the snow thickened. Somewhere above us, wind found a crack in the mountain and played it like a flute, one long note that rose and rose and didn't resolve.

And then it stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. The needle swung once, wide, overshot, came back — and locked. Rigid. Pointing straight up.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last thing I own: the needle pointed at the sky. Ninety degrees vertical, quivering slightly, as if straining against its own weight, as if whatever it had spent twelve years and however many generations pointing toward was directly overhead and it could barely contain itself.

"The map's wrong," I said.

"The map's fine." Tomas leaned close, then closer, until his nose nearly touched the glass. "The map's just been lying about the direction. We've been reading it flat." He sat back. His breath came out slow, and in the cold it hung between us like a small ghost. "My grandfather's notes said the anomaly was deep. Everyone assumed deep meant far. Deep can mean *high*."

"Or underneath us."

"Or overhead." He looked up, through the canvas, through the snow, at the black mass of Sarn Ddu invisible above us. "Whatever your father was following, Yara — it's not ahead of us anymore. It's above."

*Above.* The word did something strange to my sense of the world. All day we had climbed toward a destination, and destinations are comforting even when they're terrifying; they sit politely in front of you and let you measure the distance. But a thing overhead isn't a destination. It's a presence. I stepped outside the shelter — Tomas swore at me, I ignored him — and stood in the falling snow and looked up.

Sarn Ddu rose into the murk and vanished. There was no summit visible, no ridge line, nothing to fix the eye to. Just white falling into gray rising into black, and somewhere in all of it, a point my father had died trying to reach, hanging over my head like weather.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me sideways, startled out, and Tomas appeared in the entrance with the stove-light behind him and looked at me like I'd finally gone round the bend, which perhaps I had.

"What?" he said.

"He used to say it." My breath fogged. "In lectures, when I was small enough to sit in the back and pretend to take notes. 'The land keeps its accounts vertically, Yara. Archaeologists dig down; the truth goes up.' I thought it was just a thing he said. They're mostly just things he says." I wiped my face, which was wet, which might have been snow. "He was telling me the answer twelve years before I asked the question."

Tomas came out and stood beside me. He didn't touch me, but he stood close enough that his shoulder broke the wind off mine, and after twelve days of him I understood that this was his version of holding someone's hand.

"We go up at first light," he said. "Before the storm decides what it wants to be. If it's a two-day blow, we wait it out here and lose the window. If it's a one-day—" He stopped. Listened. The flute-note in the rocks had changed pitch, dropped lower, and there was a new sound under it now, a vast soft sliding, like the mountain turning over in its sleep.

"Tomas."

"I hear it."

"Is that—"

"Avalanche country," he said quietly. "Yes. It's been avalanche country since the tree line. Everything above us is avalanche country. Welcome to the top of the world; mind the furniture."

* * *

We didn't sleep much. You don't, at that altitude, in that wind, with a compass pointing at heaven six inches from your head. Around what I guessed was midnight — my watch had given up caring — Tomas fed the stove and told me about the winter his grandfather had mapped the anomaly.

"He was a shepherd first, surveyor second. The Institute paid him a season's wage to walk the high passes with instruments and write down what they did. He wrote it all down. Then the Institute wrote him a letter saying his figures were erroneous and his contract wouldn't be renewed." Tomas turned the stove key a quarter turn. The flame shrank. "His figures weren't erroneous. His figures were inconvenient. There's a difference, and my family has had seventy years to learn it."

"Inconvenient how?"

"How do you think? If there's a magnetic mass in the high passes big enough to pull every needle in the valley, then either it's iron ore worth a fortune, or it's something else worth more. The Institute has never liked *something else*. Something else can't be owned cleanly. Something else comes with people attached — shepherds, villages, graves, stories. Iron ore doesn't testify."

I thought of Renner's men on our trail, of the other compass — the second of the smith's three — riding in some Institute rucksack behind us, pointing its owners toward the same overhead nothing. "They'll have seen the needle flip too," I said. "When they reach the saddle."

"If they reach the saddle. They're a day behind and carrying more weight and less reason." He glanced at me. "Reason weighs nothing but it's the heaviest thing on a climb."

"That's very wise."

"It's my grandfather's. I steal from him constantly. It's how I keep him alive."

The wind screamed past the overhang and the canvas cracked like a whip and settled. In the stove light, Tomas's face was all planes and shadow, and I had the sudden vertiginous feeling of having known him for years rather than days — of having skipped whole seasons of knowing him and arrived somehow at the middle of the book. I didn't say so. Some things you don't say at four thousand meters in a storm; they'd be too heavy to carry back down.

Instead I said, "When the needle locked — for a second I felt it in my chest. Like a hook setting."

"That's the altitude."

"It wasn't the altitude."

"No," he admitted. "Probably not." He poked the stove. "Things that are true often announce themselves physically first. Cold in the stomach. A hook behind the ribs. My grandfather called it the land shaking your hand."

* * *

First light was a rumor, then a fact: a bruised gray seeping through the snow-haze, enough to move by, which was all it promised and all we needed. The storm had spent itself in the night into something steadier and meaner — a continuous fine fall, wind-driven, horizontal at the gusts. Visibility maybe thirty meters. Maybe twenty.

Tomas stood at the edge of the overhang reading the slope the way I read stratigraphy, layer by layer, his eyes moving in long patient sweeps. "We traverse east along the saddle, then cut up through the rocks there — see the dark band? Wind-scoured. Snow won't lie deep on it. It'll be ice in places, so crampons, and you rope to me and don't argue."

"When have I ever argued?"

"Constantly. It's one of your better qualities. Don't argue anyway."

We packed. And here is the thing I keep returning to, the thing I've turned over every night since like a stone looking for what's underneath: when I picked up the compass and opened it, the needle was still pointing straight up, and my first feeling — before awe, before dread, before the cold hook behind my ribs — was relief. Pure, simple, almost childish relief. Because a compass pointing up was a compass that had finished running away. For twelve years everything in my life had pointed elsewhere — away from digs, away from villages, away from anyone who might stay long enough to leave. Even my grief had been directional: always receding, always further down the road behind me. And now, finally, one true thing in the world pointed *here*. At this saddle. At this snow. At me standing under it.

The map inverted, and I inverted with it, and it turned out I'd been upside down for years without noticing.

"Yara." Tomas was already ten meters out, roped, waiting. "Daylight's spending faster than we are."

We moved. The saddle ran east beneath the massif like a shelf, and the mountain rose on our left so steeply and so continuously that I stopped looking up — partly to save my neck, partly because looking up had become unbearable in a way I couldn't name. Every time I raised my eyes to that wall of black rock and moving white, the compass ticked against my chest inside its case, and I felt the hook set again, and I thought: *he climbed this. He stood where I'm standing. He looked up at exactly this nothing.*

The journal — Renner's men had taken it, and the loss of it still woke me at night like a missing limb — but the journal had described this stretch. *The saddle is a liar,* Elias had written, in that cramped forward-leaning hand of his. *It promises a route and delivers a wall. Trust the dark band. Trust the smith's compass. Trust nothing else, least of all yourself.*

Trust the smith's compass. He'd written that twelve years ago, and I was living it, and he had never known that I would, and the unfairness of that — the sheer logistical unfairness of a man leaving instructions for a daughter he'd abandoned — nearly buckled me at the traverse's worst corner, where the drop fell away sheer below my boots and the wind tried its best to peel me off the rock like a label.

"Move," Tomas said, calm as tide. "Feel sorry for yourself at the top."

"I'm not feeling sorry for myself."

"You're doing the thing where your eyes go distant and your jaw sets. It's either self-pity or you've spotted a bear. I'm betting self-pity."

"It's neither. It's—" I hauled myself around the corner onto the dark band, boots crunching on wind-bare rock, and stopped. "It's that he knew. He knew I'd come. He wrote to me like I'd follow him. Why write instructions for someone you walked out on?"

Tomas came up beside me, unclipped, and stood breathing hard in the thin air. Snow streamed off the band behind us. Below, the saddle we'd crossed was already erased, white on white, as if we'd never walked it.

"Maybe," he said, "because walking out was the one thing he got wrong, and he knew it, and instructions were the only apology he could carry that high." He nodded at the compass case at my chest. "Or maybe he knew you'd come because you're his daughter, and Montroses follow needles. Either way — the letter's still at the top, presumably. Ask him yourself."

* * *

We gained the shoulder of Sarn Ddu by midday, and that was when the mountain showed us what it had been hiding.

The cloud tore — just for a minute, just a rip in the gray — and through it I saw the summit, or what I'd assumed was the summit: a rounded crown of rock, ordinary, achievable. But below it, folded into the mountain's north face like a secret kept in a cheek, was a scar. A vertical gash in the rock, darker than the stone around it, too regular to be natural and too eroded to be recent. From this distance it looked like a wound. From any distance, I suspect, it looks like a wound.

The compass against my chest went insane.

Not spinning this time — worse. It thrummed. I felt it before I heard it, a vibration through the case, through my coat, into the bone, and when I fumbled the lid open the needle wasn't pointing up anymore. It was pointing at the scar. Straight at it, across the intervening air, rigid with intent, trembling like a struck string.

"Tomas—"

"I see it." He had the binoculars up. A long silence. When he lowered them his face had gone carefully blank, which from Tomas meant it was full. "There's a ledge system below it. Old workings — look at the spoil fan at the base. That's not geology, that's *spoil*. Someone mined that face, and long ago." He paused. "And there's something else. On the ledge. Metal. Glinting."

"Ore?"

"Ore doesn't glint in a straight line." He passed me the glasses. My hands shook — cold, I told myself, just cold — and I found the ledge, and the glint, and my heart performed a maneuver I felt in my teeth.

A rope. Fixed anchors, driven into the rock below the scar, and from them a line descending — frayed, snow-crusted, but unmistakable. Twelve winters of weather had failed to take it down.

Elias's rope. Elias's anchors. Elias, twelve years dead or twelve years gone, had stood on that ledge and hammered pitons into Sarn Ddu's face, and part of his equipment was still up there, still holding, still waiting, the way everything he'd ever left behind had waited for me.

The cloud closed. The scar vanished. The compass settled back to its vertical lock, patient again, a dog at a door.

"Well," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened by wind and altitude and something else entirely. "At least we know he made it this far."

"At least," Tomas agreed. He checked the sky, the rope, the angle of the light, all the small professional devotions of a man who intends to bring someone home alive. "One pitch of climbing, weather closing, and two men with a stolen journal somewhere behind us." He clipped my harness to his with a snap that sounded final. "After you, Yara. Your father left the light on."

Above us, unseen behind the cloud, the scar waited. And the needle pointed straight up at it, and did not waver, and neither — I decided, stepping onto the first hold — would I.
