## 16. The Other Compass

I have stood on ice that groaned like a dying man, and I have stood in pubs when the whole room went quiet because someone walked in who shouldn't have. The icefield gave me both at once, which I consider a personal failing of the mountain's sense of timing.

We had made the saddle by mid-morning, Yara and I, moving slow over the blue-grey tongue of glacier that spilled down from Sarn Ddu's western shoulder. The snow from two days ago had crusted and refrozen into a surface that rang under our boots like crockery. Good going, if you didn't think about what was underneath. Glaciers are like banks that way — everything looks solid until you want something back.

Yara was ahead of me by twenty paces, checking the compass every few minutes with the particular patience of someone who has learned not to trust good news. The needle had been steady since dawn, holding its line toward the summit face, and she'd stopped commenting on it, which worried me more than if she'd been talking about it all day. A quiet Yara Montrose is a Yara doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

That's when I saw them.

Two figures coming up the glacier's spine from the east, small as punctuation marks against the white. They moved well — too well for tourists, too fast for anyone carrying an honest load. Even at that distance I recognized the walk of men who'd been told where to go and were annoyed about the scenic route.

"Tomas." Yara had seen my face before she'd seen what I was looking at. She turned, shaded her eyes. "How did they—"

"The river crossing. After they took our cache, they had your journal. Your father drew his route in it, didn't he?"

She was quiet a moment. "He drew *a* route."

"Then we're about to find out how accurate it was."

There wasn't anywhere to go. That's the thing people don't understand about icefields — you can see trouble coming for an hour and still have nowhere to be except where you are. Behind us the saddle rose toward the summit face, steep and fresh-loaded with snow. Ahead, the glacier ran down into crevasse country that even I didn't fancy. To either side, seracs leaned like drunks deciding whether to fall.

So we stood and waited, which is its own kind of decision, and watched the Institute come to us.

* * *

The tall one was Dr. Renner himself. I'd only seen him once before, through the window of the Penhallow pub, asking questions with the flat courtesy of a man reading from a list. Up close he was worse. He had the kind of face that had been handsome twenty years ago and had spent the time since practicing looking reasonable. His companion was younger, broad, with the patient hands of someone who handled rope or cargo or people, whichever needed handling.

"Miss Montrose." Renner stopped a polite ten feet away, breathing easily, which told me he'd been pacing himself. "Dr. Renner. We met briefly in the village. And Mr. Vey." He said my name like a receipt he was filing. "You've made remarkable time."

"We had motivation," Yara said.

"So did we." He smiled. It was a good smile, maintained, like a boat. "Though I confess ours was partly professional. You have something of ours."

"Your men took our supplies," Yara said. "And my father's journal. If anything's been taken, it's been taken by you."

"The journal, yes. Institute property. Field equipment issued to Elias Montrose in 1998 remains Institute property regardless of—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—regardless of subsequent events. You'll understand we had no choice but to recover it."

"I understand you sent two men to rob a supply cache at a river crossing," I said, "which in this country we call theft, whatever letterhead you do it under."

Renner turned to me with the mild interest of a man noticing weather. "Mr. Vey. Mountain rescue, formerly. Your grandfather surveyed this range, I believe. Old Gwyn Vey's maps. Remarkable man. The Institute holds copies of several of his surveys." He let that sit. "You come from a family of people who know where things are."

"And you come from a family of people who want them," Yara said. "Where does that leave us?"

"It leaves us," Renner said pleasantly, "with a shared interest. Which is why I'd rather talk than fight. Fighting at altitude is inefficient. Someone always falls, and then everyone spends the rest of the day feeling terrible about it." He glanced up at the summit face, at the dark seam behind the snowfields where the old mine workings showed like a scar. "You've worked out where it is. So have we. The question is only who stands there first, and I'll be honest with you, Miss Montrose — my employer's patience ended some years ago."

"With respect," I said, "your employer can wait another twelve years like everybody else."

Something moved behind Renner's reasonable face — quick, cold, gone. "You know what Elias found?"

"We know what he looked for," Yara said carefully.

"Ah." Renner nodded slowly, and I watched him decide to spend some truth, the way a gambler spends chips he can afford. "Then let me tell you what he *took*. In 1998 the Institute funded a survey of these passes. Geological, archaeological, routine. Elias Montrose was our lead field man. At the end of that season he filed a report — partial, evasive — resigned by letter, and disappeared with Institute instruments, including a survey compass of significant value. My predecessor pursued the matter. Pursuit was... interrupted." He spread his hands. "Now the matter has reopened itself. A storm opens a cave in Penhallow, and Miss Montrose comes home carrying her father's compass. You can see how it might look like fate. Or design."

"You mean you've been waiting," Yara said.

"I mean the Institute keeps its accounts open longer than most families keep their grief." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "The compasses, Miss Montrose. There were three. Made by a smith in the high hamlets — your hamlet, Mr. Vey, the one that still remembers. One belonged to the smith. One was sold south and lost. And one the Institute commissioned, for the original survey." He reached into his coat, unhurried, and brought out a brass instrument that caught the light.

It was the twin of the one hanging at Yara's chest. Same size, same worn casing, same glass clouded with age. But where hers pointed — had always pointed, stubbornly, impossibly — toward Sarn Ddu, this needle swung lazy circles, drunk, undecided, hunting for something it couldn't find.

Or so it seemed. I looked closer. The needle wasn't spinning free. It was oscillating — swinging wide past its mark, correcting, overshooting again. Like a dog on a long lead, circling the spot where its owner stood.

And its owner, near as I could tell, was standing right in front of us.

* * *

Here is what I knew, and what I decided to do with it.

My grandfather's maps marked the anomaly — a magnetic heart in the rock beneath Sarn Ddu, big enough to pull any iron within miles off true north. He'd written one word beside it in his careful hand: *owings*. I never asked him what it meant. He died before I got around to it, which is the way of grandfathers.

But there was a second thing on those maps, higher up, above the anomaly: a note about the approaches. Three ways onto the summit face. The glacier route, which looked easiest and wasn't — it funneled into an icefall my grandfather had crossed out with a single line and the word *no*. The eastern couloir, honest but exposed, a chimney of snow and loose rock that killed you politely if you slipped. And the shepherd's path — the drover's road, hidden, unmapped, the way we'd come.

The Institute men had the journal. Elias's route notes. But Elias had come up the hard way, alone, and his notes would show the couloir or the icefall, because he hadn't known about the drover's road. Nobody knew about it. My grandfather had kept it that way on purpose, and I'd kept it that way out of habit and inheritance.

So when Renner asked — reasonably, reasonably — whether we might pool our knowledge of the route, I lied to him with my whole face.

"The icefall," I said. "It's the only way up. My grandfather mapped it. There's a channel through the seracs, maybe two hours' work with ropes, and then a snow ramp to the seam." I described it in detail — invented detail, but detailed the way real things are detailed, with the specific hesitations of memory. Men trust hesitation. It sounds like honesty costing something.

Renner listened with his head tilted, and I watched the younger man watch me, and I understood that the younger one was the dangerous one. Renner would believe what was convenient. The young one would check.

"That matches the journal," Renner said at last, satisfied.

"It would," I said, "if your journal is worth the paper Elias wrote it on."

Yara shot me a look. I kept my face still. She didn't know what I was doing yet, but she knew I was doing something, and to her credit she added nothing, contradicted nothing, just stood there with the wind pulling at her hood and let me dig the hole deeper.

"Then we proceed together," Renner said. "Shared route, shared risk. The Institute will provide rope and fuel — you seem to have lost yours." A small smile, acknowledging his own men's work without apologizing for it. "And when we reach the site, we settle the question of ownership like civilized people."

"Civilized people," Yara said, "don't sabotage caches."

"Civilized people," Renner replied, "write histories. The uncivilized write footnotes. I'd hoped you'd choose to be history, Miss Montrose. Your father chose footnotes." He turned the twin compass over in his hand, once, and put it away. "We'll camp here tonight. Move at first light. I suggest you do the same — separately, if you prefer. The mountain doesn't care whose flag is on the tent."

They withdrew fifty yards down-glacier and began, with infuriating competence, to pitch a dome tent the colour of dried blood.

* * *

Yara waited until we'd walked back to our own ledge, until our backs were to them and the wind covered our voices, and then she rounded on me.

"There is no channel through the icefall."

"No."

"You told them—"

"I told them exactly what they wanted to hear, which is the cheapest gift you can give a man like that." I sat down on the pack I no longer fully trusted and started checking what remained of our gear. Half a stove. Rope enough. Food for three days if we were disciplined, two if we were honest. "They'll spend tomorrow cutting a route through an icefall that doesn't have one. That's a day. Maybe two, if the young one is careful, and he is, which is the problem."

"They'll figure it out."

"Eventually. When they do, they'll be angry, tired, and behind us. All three are improvements on their current condition."

Yara crouched beside me. Close enough that I could see the frost in her eyelashes and the arithmetic still running behind her eyes. "The needle on their compass," she said quietly. "Did you see it?"

"I saw it."

"It wasn't pointing at the summit."

"No. It was pointing at *you*." I kept my voice low. "Or at yours. Two needles cut from the same bar of iron, tuned to the same heart in the rock — they'll find each other across a room, across a valley. His was circling because yours was close. Which means theirs works fine, and it means they don't need your journal to follow you. They need only to follow the needle that follows you."

She was silent for a while. Below us, the blood-red tent went up taut and square, and smoke rose straight from their stove in the windless cold.

"My father carried one of these," she said finally. "And Renner's institute carried the other. Both pointing at the same place. Twelve years ago they were on this mountain at the same time, Tomas. This isn't the first time they've stood where we're standing."

"No," I said. "It's the first time they've stood here with you."

She looked at me then, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the anger that had carried her out of Penhallow, not the grief she wore like a coat. Something harder and cleaner. "Then we go up tonight," she said. "While they're sleeping off their confidence."

I should have argued. Night travel on a glacier, above the tree line, with early winter loading the slopes — every rule I'd ever laid down as a rescue man, every rule I'd made *her* swear to, said wait for light. I opened my mouth to say so.

And that was when the mountain spoke first.

It came from the east, from the couloir side — a deep, muffled whump, felt through the boots before it was heard, the sound of a slope shedding its new snow. Then a second report, farther off, and a third, and the whole basin answered in a slow rolling conversation of settling snow. Above the summit face, a plume lifted off the ridge and hung there, backlit, like breath in cold air.

The young Institute man came running toward us across the ice, no pretense now, shouting over the noise: "Avalanche! On the couloir — the whole couloir's gone!" He skidded to a stop, breathing hard, and looked from me to Yara with something that had stopped being professional. "Your route. The one in the journal. It's buried. All of it."

Behind him, Renner stood at his tent, the twin compass already in his hand, watching the needle swing and settle, swing and settle — and then lock, sudden and certain, pointing past us both, up toward the summit face and the dark seam behind it.

The only way left was the one I hadn't told them about.

And now they were looking at me, waiting for me to say so.
