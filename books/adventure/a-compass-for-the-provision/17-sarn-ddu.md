## 17. Sarn Ddu

The mountain had been lying to us all morning, and by noon it stopped bothering to.

From the saddle, Sarn Ddu had looked like a straightforward climb — a black shoulder of rock rising maybe four hundred meters above the snowline, steep but honest, the kind of face you could read like a page. Tomas had studied it through his binoculars for a long time and said nothing, which from Tomas was a full paragraph of bad news.

"It's the weather I don't like," he said finally, handing me the glasses. "Snow's coming in off the sea. By two o'clock you won't be able to see your own boots."

"Then we climb fast."

"That's what they all say." He said *they* the way other men say *the dead*, and I didn't ask. We'd traded enough wounds in that bothy to know which questions were worth asking and which were just ways of hurting each other politely.

We roped up at the base of the face. The snow was the worst kind — not fresh powder but old snow crusted over ice, the kind that holds your weight right up until it decides not to. Tomas led. I watched his boots and copied them exactly, placing my feet where his had been, trusting him the way I'd spent twelve years refusing to trust anyone: completely, and with a backup plan.

The compass hung around my neck on its cord, and every few moves I'd pull it out of my jacket and check it. The needle had gone strange since the saddle — not spinning anymore, but straining, quivering against its glass like a bird thrown against a window. Pointing up. Always up.

"You're going to break it," Tomas called down.

"It's been broken since Penhallow."

"Aye, but now it's *our* broken thing."

I almost laughed. That was the trouble with Tomas Vey — he kept doing that, kept making me laugh at altitude, where laughing is a genuinely dangerous activity. I put the compass away and climbed.

* * *

Two hundred meters up, the snow started.

It came sideways first, then from everywhere at once, the way mountain weather does — not falling so much as arriving, as if the sky had simply decided to relocate. The world shrank to a circle three meters across: Tomas's rope, the rock in front of my face, the white noise beyond. My hands were inside mittens over liner gloves, and I could feel the cold working at the seams anyway, patient as interest on a debt.

Tomas found the gully — a chimney of dark rock cutting up through the face — and we went into it gratefully, out of the wind if not out of the storm. And it was there, wedged in the back of the gully with the snow hissing past the entrance like static, that I saw the first anchor.

A piton. Old iron, driven into a crack at chest height, furred with rust and rimed with frost. A sling of rope hung from it, bleached nearly white, stiff as wire.

"Tomas."

He turned. Looked. His face did something complicated.

"That's not recent," he said, though I'd already done the arithmetic myself.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Twelve years. Give or take a season. The rope had the particular gray pallor of cordage that has lived through a decade of ultraviolet light and freeze-thaw cycles, the nylon degraded to something more like felt than fiber. Nobody had climbed this gully since before I'd finished my degree. Since before I'd stopped waiting for him to call.

Since Elias Montrose stood exactly where I was standing, hammered that piton into exactly that crack, and went up.

I touched the sling. It was like touching a bone.

"He was here," I said. My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "He actually — he found it. He got this far."

Tomas didn't say anything comforting, which is one of the things I'd come to value about him. He just checked the piton with a practiced hand, testing the torque, and grunted. "Iron's sound. Rope's dead. We use our own." Then, after a moment: "Your father climbed well, from the look of the placement."

Something in my chest cracked along an old fault line. Because that was the thing, wasn't it? All those years I'd built him into a monster or a coward or a man running from something, and the whole time he'd been *this* — a competent man placing gear well, climbing carefully, coming to a mountain in winter because some part of him couldn't not. I'd spent twelve years measuring the distance between us in miles, and it turned out the distance was vertical, and I was climbing it.

"Keep moving," I said, because if I stood there much longer touching that rope I was going to come apart at four hundred meters, and that would be inconvenient for everyone.

* * *

The gully steepened. The snow thickened until it wasn't weather anymore but a condition, a fact of existence, like gravity. We moved from anchor to anchor, and I made myself look at each of them — because there were more, that was the terrible tender truth of it, there were anchors all the way up. Pitons and slings and one place where he'd threaded a flake of rock with cord, the knot still holding its shape under twelve years of ice like a signature under glass.

He'd climbed this route alone, in winter, carrying whatever he'd carried, and he'd protected every pitch like a man who intended to come back down.

That was the detail that undid me, more than any of it. Not the skill — I'd known about the skill since I was six years old and he taught me to read stratigraphy in a road cut. But the *protection*. Every anchor placed for the descent. Every piece of gear set with a return in mind. This was not a man climbing toward death. This was a man who fully expected to walk back down this mountain, find his daughter, and explain himself.

And hadn't.

"Why do you think he never came back?" I heard myself ask. The wind ate half of it; Tomas leaned close to hear.

"Could be lots of reasons," he said. "Or one reason with a lot of weight." He nodded upward. "Whatever's behind that summit, maybe it doesn't let people leave easy. Legends usually have a practical core, in my experience. Usually it's weather, or rockfall, or a hole in the ground."

"The legend says the cave shows you what you're owed."

"Aye, well." He tested the next hold. "My grandfather always said the Provision doesn't give you what you want. It gives you the bill."

I thought about that for the rest of the pitch. About bills. About what a man owes a daughter he left in a dig-house cot in Turkey with a neighbor woman and a promise to return by September. About what a daughter owes herself after twelve years of telling everyone — including, mostly, herself — that she wasn't waiting for anything.

The compass burned cold against my sternum. Up. Up. Whatever it wanted was directly overhead now, and I understood suddenly that the needle hadn't been pointing at treasure at all. It had been pointing at this climb. At these anchors. At the exact coordinates of everything he'd never told me.

* * *

Near the top, the gully spat us out onto a ledge no wider than a kitchen table, and the summit of Sarn Ddu rose above us — a rounded dome of black rock, deceptively modest, wearing perhaps ten meters of snow-crusted stone like a hat.

Except it wasn't a summit. I saw it before I understood it, the way you sometimes see a thing whole before your mind consents to assemble the parts. The dome was too regular. Too smooth on one flank, too abrupt on the other. And there, near the base of the final rise, half-swallowed by scree and snow: a lip of cut stone. Dressed blocks. Mortar lines running under the rubble like veins.

"Tomas. Look at the shape of it."

He looked. I watched him arrive at it a beat behind me, watched his jaw tighten.

"That's not a natural profile," he said slowly. "That's a spoil heap. And that—" he pointed at the dressed stone, at the shadowed gap beneath the collapsed mass where the blocks had fallen away from something older and darker— "that's a shaft mouth. Collapsed. Old mine, or older. Your 'cave' is a hole somebody dug."

The Provision. The cave that shows you what you're owed. Not a sea cave translated inland by legend, not some karst freak in the middle of a granite range. A mine. Dug by human hands, sealed by human hands, hidden behind a summit on purpose — because whoever buried what was buried down there had wanted it deep, and dark, and difficult to reach.

And my father had stood on this ledge, in weather very like this, and worked out the same thing, and gone up anyway.

The last anchors confirmed it. Two pitons at the top of the final pitch, and between them, frozen into the ice of the shaft's collapsed throat, the glint of something metallic. I climbed to it with my heart going like a trapped bird and scraped the rime away with my mitten.

A carabiner. Steel, rust-bled, gate seized solid with corrosion. Attached to it, descending into the black gap between the fallen blocks, a rope — his rope, the good rope, the one he'd bought the summer I turned fourteen and let me belay him on the quarry walls at Gower while he talked about stratigraphy and responsibility and how the two were the same subject.

He'd gone down. Into the shaft. On that rope.

And the rope — I followed it with my eyes until the darkness took it, thirty meters down, forty, until even the glint of it drowned — the rope was still anchored at the top. Still rigged. Still hanging there in the dark like a question that had been asked twelve years ago and never answered.

Because you rig a rope like that when you're coming back up.

I knelt on the ledge with the storm screaming past above us and the black mouth breathing its faint, mineral-smelling exhale below, and I pressed my forehead against the frozen rock, and I will not pretend I didn't cry. Not much. Enough. Twelve years' worth, measured out in the only currency that had ever made sense to me, spent in about ninety seconds at the top of a mountain in Wales.

Tomas stood beside me and said nothing and put his hand on my shoulder, and that was exactly the right amount of comfort, and I have never been able to tell him so without my voice doing something embarrassing.

* * *

Afterward — five minutes, ten, I don't know — I wiped my face inside my mask and became functional again, because grief is a luxury you ration at altitude, and I had inherited at least that much from him.

"We go down," I said.

"On a twelve-year-old rope?" Tomas's eyebrows disappeared under his hood. "Absolutely not. We rig our own line, we descend on our own gear, and we treat that old rigging as a historical artifact, which is what it is."

"I know. I know that." I pulled out the compass one last time. The needle strained upward, quivering, insistent — pointing not at the shaft mouth ahead of us but at something below it, deeper, past the collapse, down in the dark where his rope vanished. "It's down there. Whatever the needle wants. Whatever he found."

"And whatever Renner wants," Tomas said grimly. He jerked his chin back down the face, into the whiteout we'd climbed out of. "They'll have seen our tracks below the saddle, if the snow hasn't filled them. Which it has. Which buys us hours, not days." He was already uncoiling rope, already moving, because Tomas processed fear by getting busier. "So we go down, we see what your father saw, and we get back up this rope before the weather finishes deciding whose side it's on."

I took one last look at the anchors. At the pitons standing their posts after twelve winters, faithful as gravestones. *He meant to come back,* I thought. *Every single placement says he meant to come back.*

Then I swung my legs over the lip of the shaft, into the dark that had taken him, and the cold air rose up to meet me — and somewhere far below, so faint I might have invented it, I caught the smell of old timber and lamp oil.

Someone had been living down there.

Not twelve years ago.

Recently.
