## 20. Descent

There is a particular sound a mountain makes when it decides it's finished with you. It isn't thunder. Thunder is honest — it announces itself, gives you time to feel small. No. This is quieter than that: a long exhalation of wind through the col behind us, the snow beginning to move in sheets instead of flakes, and somewhere above, the deep structural groan of Sarn Ddu settling its shoulders after Yara brought half its face down on top of the only thing anyone had climbed it for in three hundred years.

I heard it and I thought, *right then, we're done being interesting.*

"Yara." I grabbed her arm. She was still staring at the fresh scar of rubble where the chamber entrance had been, where Renner had been screaming things I will not repeat even in my own head, where her father's last twelve years had been sealed under forty tonnes of mountain like a letter posted to no one. "Yara. We go now or the mountain keeps us too."

She turned. Her face was grey with cold and something else, something that looked less like grief than like a man setting down luggage he'd carried so long he'd forgotten his arms were free. She patted her chest pocket once — the compass, the letter, the whole of her inheritance in two objects small enough to lose down a crevasse — and said, "Lead on, Vey."

So I did.

* * *

You want to know what descent looks like when the storm has your scent? It doesn't look like anything. That's the truth nobody puts on the recruitment posters for mountain rescue. You put your goggles down and from that moment you live inside two circles of scratched plastic, and the world shrinks to whatever your boots can confirm exists. Everything else is white noise and guesswork.

I'd come down this shoulder of the range twice before, both times in summer, both times with a rope team and a radio and the smug confidence of a man who hadn't yet learned what the mountain does to people who trust their last trip's memory. Now I had none of those things. I had Yara Montrose, one ice axe between us, a compass whose needle had finally gone quiet — pointing, as far as I could tell, straight at my own boots, which I chose to read as an endorsement — and roughly four hours of daylight that the cloud was already eating.

We roped together with the short length of cord Yara had salvaged from Elias's old anchors. Twelve years on the mountain and the knots still held. I found that harder to think about than I expected, so I didn't think about it. There's a discipline to survival, and most of it is deciding what not to think about.

The icefield was the first trial. Going up, we'd cut steps over two hours of careful work. Coming down, with the surface already glazed over by new snow blown hard as shellac, we simply sat and slid — the old glissade, axe buried to the head, boots up, pray quietly. Yara went first because she weighed less and because she'd stopped asking my permission somewhere around the saddle, which I took as a compliment. She shot down the slope like a stone off a shovel, vanished into the spume, and reappeared thirty seconds later whooping — actually whooping, this woman who hadn't laughed since Penhallow — and I thought, *well, if the mountain takes us now, at least she'll die having made a noise about it.*

Then my turn, and there was no more thinking, only speed and the bite of the axe and the white roaring past, and then we were both on the scree below, alive, absurd, dusted in powder like a pair of loaves.

* * *

The trouble started at the tree line, as trouble usually does.

Going up, the forest had been shelter. Coming down, in wind that had swung round to the northwest and begun throwing snow horizontally like it held a grudge, the trees were just obstacles with opinions. Visibility dropped to ten metres, then five. My hands had stopped reporting in around the second hour — I'd been flexing them inside my mitts on a schedule, because frostbite doesn't announce itself either; it just quietly closes accounts. Yara's lips were split and her left ear had gone waxy-white where her hood kept slipping, and every time I told her to fix it she fixed it for ninety seconds and then forgot again, because exhaustion eats the front of the mind first and leaves the stubbornness.

"We should stop," she shouted at me at some point. "Tomas. We should *stop.*"

"No."

"It's getting dark—"

"It's three in the afternoon." I shouted it right into her ear. "That's how dark it is. If we stop, we don't get up. You know this."

She knew this. Her father had taught her, presumably, before he taught her everything else the hard way. She put her head down and walked.

Here is what I remember of the next stretch, in fragments, because memory in conditions like that comes home in pieces like wreckage after a flood:

A stream crossing where the water ran black under thin ice and we crossed on all fours like animals, because dignity is a fair-weather friend.

Yara falling — properly falling, a full-length collapse into a drift — and lying there a beat too long, and me hauling her up by the collar and shouting her name until her eyes focused on something other than the middle distance. She said, "I was just resting," and I said, "You were dying, and it's rude to do it on my watch," and she almost smiled, which frightened me more than the fall.

And once — I'm sure of this, though she denies it — she said, "He waited for me. In there. All this time, he was waiting for me to come and collect him." And I said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound, and we kept walking.

Somewhere in that white blindness I lost track of whether we were following the drover's road or merely following each other, and I understood, with a clarity that felt almost luxurious, that it no longer mattered. The compass was in her pocket. The needle pointed at my boots. We were, by every instrument available to us, exactly where we were supposed to be.

* * *

The dogs found us first.

I heard them before I saw anything — that high, carrying belling that shepherds' dogs make when they've got something cornered or something found, and I stopped dead and grabbed Yara's sleeve and said, "Listen," and for a moment we both stood in the roaring white like idiots, grinning into our goggles.

Because I knew that sound. Every child in that valley knows that sound. Those were Dafydd Rhys's dogs, the ones he runs on the high pasture above Cwm Aelwyd, the hamlet where the old woman had read our compass like scripture. Which meant we had come off the mountain further east than I'd reckoned — blown sideways, probably, for the last hour without knowing it — and were standing, at that moment, within shouting distance of the one place in the world where people would come out into weather like this for strangers.

They came out anyway. Of course they came out. I should have known they would. That's the thing about the hamlet folk that the Institute men never understood, the thing Renner with his maps and his claims and his lawyers would never understand: out here, the provision isn't gold in a vault. It's the fact that when someone is missing, you go looking, and you don't invoice anybody afterward.

Dafydd himself materialised out of the murk like a boulder that had decided to walk — a big man in his sixties wrapped in sacking and oilskin, two dogs wheeling around his knees, a third already circling Yara with its nose going like a metronome. He looked us up and down, took in the state of us, and delivered his assessment in Welsh, which I caught maybe a third of but whose meaning required no translation whatsoever. Then he put Yara's arm over his shoulders, put mine over the other dog's back — I was past pride by then, well past it, I'd have taken the arm of the dog itself — and walked us down into the valley while the storm closed the door behind us.

I don't remember much of the walk. I remember heat, eventually. The longhouse at Cwm Aelwyd, the peat fire, someone forcing sweet tea into my hands and watching until I drank it, which is the shepherd's version of triage. I remember the old woman — Bronwen, the smith's great-granddaughter, keeper of the story — kneeling in front of Yara and working the frozen boot off her left foot with the patience of a woman defusing something, and Yara hissing through her teeth, and Bronwen saying, in English this time, slow and clear:

"You went up with nothing and came down with less. Sit still. The mountain took its toll; now let the house pay you back."

Yara's toes were bad. Not lost — I want to be clear about that, because she'd tell you herself they're fine now, mostly — but bad enough that Bronwen spent the night doing the slow, agonising rewarming, feet in tepid water that rose degree by degree, while Yara sat white-knuckled and made jokes. Bad jokes. Terrible jokes. About how her father had dragged her up mountains as a child and now the mountains were dragging bits of her back down as interest. Bronwen didn't laugh. I did, once, and got a look that could have caulked a boat.

My hands were luckier. Two fingertips blistered, one nail I'd say goodbye to, and a fortnight of clumsiness ahead of me. I sat by the fire working them open and shut, open and shut, like a man practising a very small accordion, and watched Yara across the room.

She had the letter out.

Not reading it. Just holding it, turning it over, the paper soft as cloth now from handling. The firelight moved on her face and I saw her mouth move once — *Da* — and stop. And I thought about saying something, and then I thought about all the years I'd spent not saying things, after Nerys, after the rescue that failed, after I decided that silence was the same as strength, and I got up and crossed the room and sat down next to her and said nothing at all, which is sometimes the loudest thing a man can offer.

After a while she leaned her head against my shoulder. Just briefly. Just long enough.

"He sealed it," she said. "All of it. The names, the debts, the records. Renner can't claim a vault that's under a hundred tonnes of rock, and even if he could—" She stopped. Started again. "It was never treasure, Tomas. He knew. He died knowing, and he left it for me to find out the same way."

"And was it worth the toes?"

She laughed — a real one, cracked and wet at the edges. "Ask me in spring."

* * *

Renner's party, we learned two days later, had fared worse and deserved it.

Dafydd brought the news up from the valley with the milk: the Institute men had come down the western flank, off-route, badly equipped, arguing with each other loud enough that a shepherd had heard them a mile off through the storm. One of them — the young one, Pell, the one who'd looked genuinely sick during the standoff on the glacier — had walked into Cwm Aelwyd half-frozen and confessed everything to the first person who gave him soup, which is the fastest confession method ever devised and I recommend it to any court in the land. The hoard, the sabotage at the river crossing, the stolen journal — which, it emerged, Renner had lost himself, dropped somewhere on the icefield in the whiteout, so that the mountain now held Elias Montrose's field journal under the same snowfall that held everything else, and I confess I found that fitting. Let the archive keep its own archive.

Renner himself had made it down alone, abandoned his men, and was reportedly in the village below, telephoning solicitors and telling anyone who'd listen a story about Institute property and criminal destruction. He'd find listeners, too. Men like Renner always do. But he'd also find Pell's statement, and Bronwen's testimony, and Dafydd's grandfather's maps, and a hamlet full of people whose grandparents' names were written in the vault Yara had sealed — and I have lived long enough to know which side of that ledger holds the weight.

On the third morning, the storm broke. Properly broke — blue sky, hard sun on the white slopes, the whole range laid out clean and glittering like it had never done anything wrong in its life. Sarn Ddu stood at the head of the valley with its new scar already filling with snow, and you'd never know, to look at it, that it had swallowed a legend whole and kept the change.

Yara stood in the doorway of the longhouse a long time, looking at it. The compass hung on a cord around her neck now; she'd taken to wearing it like that, and the needle lay quiet, pointing nowhere in particular, off duty at last.

"They'll want statements," she said. "The tribunal. The Institute will send people, proper ones, and the villagers will need someone to stand up and say what happened up there. What he did. What Renner tried to do."

"Yes."

"I'm not good at standing up and saying things, Tomas. I'm good at walking away from them."

I looked at her — this woman who had outrun attachment for twelve years, who measured her life in miles because miles couldn't hold her accountable, who had climbed a mountain to collect an apology and come down owning something better than one — and I said, "Then it's fortunate you've got witnesses. And a guide who talks too much."

She almost smiled. Then, from the valley path below, we heard the dogs start up again — the finding-bark, the one that means strangers on the road — and we both turned to look, and down through the bright morning, small and inevitable as weather, came the first riders from Penhallow.

Word travels fast in these valleys. Faster than storms. And whatever was coming up that path toward us — questions, accusations, justice, or all three wearing the same coat — it was coming for Yara Montrose, and this time, for the first time in twelve years, she wasn't going anywhere.
