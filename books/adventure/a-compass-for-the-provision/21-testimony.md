## 21. Testimony

The hearing was held in the Penhallow village hall, which was less a hall than a chapel that had lost its faith and gained a badminton net. Someone had folded the net up against the rafters for the occasion, and someone else had arranged forty-odd chairs in rows facing a trestle table draped with a green cloth that I suspected had last seen service at the harvest supper. The air smelled of woodsmoke, damp wool, and the particular institutional anxiety of men who have traveled a long way to be lied to and suspect it.

I sat at the front with Tomas beside me, his grandfather's maps spread between us under weights borrowed from the fish shed — two hams, a horseshoe, and a stone that might once have been a doorstop. The maps were beautiful. That's what struck me first, before their evidentiary value: they were *beautiful*, drawn in iron-gall ink gone brown as old tea, every contour of the range rendered by a hand that had walked every contour first. Cadwaladr Vey, 1911 through 1934. In the corner of the largest sheet, in careful copperplate: *For whoever comes after. The mountain does not lie to those who ask it properly.*

The Institute delegation sat opposite us. Three of them. Dr. Renner in the center, gray-suited and gray-faced, with the particular composure of a man who has rehearsed being reasonable. His colleagues flanked him like punctuation. On the table before them lay a leather document case, a laptop they couldn't get a signal for, and — this detail I will treasure until I die — a second brass compass, its needle swinging in small confused circles, homesick for something it would never find again.

Mrs. Trelawny chaired the hearing, on the grounds that she chaired everything in Penhallow, including, according to local rumor, two marriages and one divorce. She was seventy-something, built like a drystone wall, and wore a hat that suggested she had dressed for weather rather than ceremony, which told me everything about how this day would go.

"Dr. Montrose," she said, when the room had settled. "You'll give your account in your own words. Take as long as you need. We've nowhere better to be."

There was a ripple of laughter at that, warm and local, and I understood suddenly that I wasn't standing before a tribunal at all. The Institute had brought a tribunal. Penhallow had brought an audience.

* * *

I told it from the beginning, because there was no other way to tell it.

The storm, the dune, the cave mouth like a held breath. The tin box and my father's handwriting. The compass that ignored north. I kept my voice level through all of it, and mostly succeeded, though when I described reading the journal's final entry — the sentence that broke off mid-word above Sarn Ddu — I heard my own voice do something complicated around the name, and stopped, and drank water, and went on.

Tomas spoke after me. He was better at this than I'd expected; twelve years of mountain rescue briefings had taught him to deliver hard facts without decoration. He laid out the route on his grandfather's map, traced the drover's road that appeared on no modern survey, marked the bothy where we'd sheltered, the river crossing where our cache had been cut, the saddle where the needle pointed straight up.

"The anomaly is real," he said. "It's been real since my grandfather mapped it in 1928. He called it the Iron Heart. He thought it was a meteorite. It isn't." He looked at Renner. "It's a vault. Built into the old mine workings behind Sarn Ddu, lined with whatever the smith used to line it, and it holds every record this valley ever trusted to it. Debts forgiven. Names restored. Land claims settled before the county offices existed."

"You cannot know that," Renner said quietly.

"I know it because I stood in it," I said.

"Then you'll forgive me," Renner said, "if I observe that a woman who spent eleven days alone in the mountains with no witnesses, pursuing a treasure her father died concealing, may not be the most objective narrator of what she found there."

The room went still in the way rooms do when someone says something true and unforgivable in the same breath.

"That's fair," I said.

Renner blinked. I think he'd prepared for outrage.

"It's fair," I repeated. "So let's talk about evidence instead of testimony. Dr. Renner, you arrived in Penhallow asking about a missing 1998 expedition. You knew Elias Montrose had come here. You knew he'd gone up the mountain. Your institute sent a team after him twenty-six years ago and reported them all lost to weather. But you didn't report the expedition missing because it went missing. You reported it missing because your predecessor, Dr. Halloran, came down alone, told everyone Elias had turned back, and then spent the rest of his career filing acquisition requests for the Sarn Ddu mineral rights." I let that sit. "How do I know? Because it's in my father's journal. And because your own compass proves it."

I nodded at the second compass on their table.

"There were three made," I said. "By the same smith who built the vault. One points to the Provision cave. One points to the vault itself. My father carried one. You're carrying the other. Ask yourself who gave it to the Institute, and why, if the Institute genuinely believed Elias died in a storm with nothing found."

Renner's colleague — the younger one, whose name I'd already forgotten — shifted in his chair, and I watched the exact moment the arithmetic finished behind his eyes.

* * *

What saved me, in the end, was not rhetoric. It was Mrs. Trelawny's cousin Enid, who was ninety-one and had been waiting her whole life for a room full of important people to be wrong about something in public.

Enid stood up during the recess — the Institute had asked for one, presumably to regroup — and said, in a voice like a gate hinge, "My grandmother sold eggs to Cadwaladr Vey. He drew our farm on that map. Drawed the well that isn't on any county record neither. If that girl says the vault's there, the vault's there."

Then old Rhys Prosser, who had been silent all morning, stood and said, "My grandfather drove stock over the shepherd's path. It's real. It's just not for people who don't ask properly." Which was, I realized, the closest thing to a blessing the mountain's keepers could offer in a legal proceeding.

And then Mrs. Trelawny herself produced, from a biscuit tin under the green cloth, a letter. Dated 1999. From Dr. Halloran to the parish council, requesting — in language so smooth it squeaked — the transfer of "certain customary access rights" over the Sarn Ddu common land to the Whitcombe Institute, in exchange for a donation toward the new roof.

"We never signed," Mrs. Trelawny said. "We never answered at all. But we kept the letter. We keep everything. It's the one thing we've got more of than anyone."

She set the letter on the table between the two compasses, and it lay there like a verdict.

* * *

Renner did not confess. I want to be honest about that, because triumphalism would be a lie and I've had enough lies for one winter. What happened was smaller and colder and more permanent than confession.

He asked for another recess. He made two phone calls from the step outside, in the rain, and we could all see him through the fogged window, pacing, holding the phone away from his ear, holding it close again. When he came back, his posture had changed by perhaps two degrees, but two degrees is the difference between a man standing on a ridge and a man falling off one.

The Institute's position, he announced, was that the Sarn Ddu site should be assessed by an independent archaeological body, with the Institute's cooperation, and that pending such assessment, all claims to mineral or historical rights would be "held in abeyance." He thanked the village for its hospitality. He did not look at me.

Afterward, in the lane outside, he caught up with me. Just the two of us, rain coming off the sea in long gray sheets.

"You understand what you've done," he said. Not angry. Almost gentle. "That chamber is a research site of international significance. It needed conserving. Cataloguing. Instead it's sealed behind rubble, and the only access is through a legend."

"No," I said. "Through a vow. There's a difference, and it matters."

"And your father? You've sealed him in too. Is that the apology you wanted?"

I thought about the letter in my coat pocket, soft at the folds now from handling. I thought about the rope anchors, twelve years old, and the camp, and the way he'd written my name.

"He sealed himself in," I said. "I just agreed to hold the door. There's a difference there too."

He stood in the rain a moment longer, and then he said, very quietly, "Halloran believed your father found it alive. He spent thirty years believing it. Belief is a kind of inheritance, Dr. Montrose. You should know — you seem to collect them."

Then he walked down the lane toward the car park, and the rain took him, and I noticed that his compass stayed behind on the table inside, because the Institute had decided, in its wisdom, that it was institute property and must be surrendered pending review — and that nobody in Penhallow intended to surrender anything to anybody.

* * *

That evening the village fed us. There is no other word for it. The pub put out food that no one had ordered and everyone had cooked, and I sat at a table with Tomas and Enid and Rhys Prosser and half a dozen others whose names I was still learning, and somewhere between the third story about Cadwaladr Vey and the fifth accusation that Mrs. Trelawny's hat was "a disgrace even by her standards," I realized that no one had asked me a single question about the treasure.

They'd asked about my father. They'd asked about the climb. Enid had asked whether I'd eaten properly on the mountain and refused to accept my answer. But the hoard — the records, the names restored, the debts forgiven — that belonged to them already. It had always belonged to them. I hadn't brought it home. I'd only confirmed the address.

"You'll stay till spring, then," Enid said. It was not a question. Nothing Enid said was ever a question.

"I haven't decided—"

"She'll stay," Tomas said, not looking up from his pint.

I looked at him. He looked back with the expression of a man who has carried someone down a mountain and considers the matter settled.

"I'll stay," I heard myself say, "until spring. To finish the survey. Properly. With the parish's permission."

"Permission granted," said Mrs. Trelawny, who had materialized with the inevitability of weather. "On condition you teach the children something. They can't read a contour line between them, and it's a scandal."

Later, walking back along the sea wall with Tomas, the wind pushing at us like a large friendly dog, I asked him why he'd answered for me.

"Because you'd have hedged," he said. "You hedge when you want something. It's the only dishonest thing about you."

"And what do I want?"

He considered the horizon, where the range stood black against the last light, Sarn Ddu among them, keeping its secret the way good mountains do.

"Spring," he said finally. "Same as everyone."

At the cottage door, I took out my father's letter — not to read it again, but just to feel its weight, the way you touch a scar to confirm the wound has closed. And beneath my hand, through the wall, I felt something I had not felt since the chamber: a faint, steady pull, like a needle finding its bearing at last.

Except it wasn't the compass. The compass hung on my wall upstairs, quiet.

It was the sound of the sea, filling the cave beneath the dune, keeping its appointment — and, fainter still, from the direction of the village, someone laughing.
