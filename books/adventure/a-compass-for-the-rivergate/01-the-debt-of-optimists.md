## 1. The Debt of Optimists

The storm came off the flats like a debt collector — no warning, no negotiation, just a wall of bruised cloud eating the horizon at forty miles an hour. Zephyrine Halloran watched it from the cab of her truck and did the math she always did: wind speed against distance, hail core against roof integrity, her own hunger against the price of copper.

"Come on, sweetheart," she told the sky. "Be generous."

She'd named the storm *Petunia*, because naming them made them feel like customers instead of creditors. Petunia was a fast one, a straight-line wind event with a hail signature blooming red on her dash-mounted radar, and where hail went, roofs failed, and where roofs failed, salvage followed. Sheet metal. Copper flashing. Whole greenhouses peeled open like sardine tins if you got there before the other chasers did.

She drove into it.

This was the part people never understood about storm chasing — that it wasn't bravery, it was bookkeeping. Every mile toward the core was a line item. Every minute of hesitation was interest accruing. Zephyrine had gotten very good at bookkeeping over the last three years, ever since the Rivergate Syndicate had bought her loan from a bank that had gone under, and very good at pretending that good was the same thing as happy.

Petunia hit the flats at 4:17 in the afternoon and spent herself like a drunk heir. By six, the light was going gold and soft over a landscape littered with her generosity: a grain elevator dented like a thumbprint, power lines down in lazy cat's cradles, and half a mile of greenhouse complex outside Harrow Flats stripped to its ribs, glass glittering across the fields in drifts that looked almost like snow.

Zephyrine stood in the wreckage with her pry bar and her gloves and felt the old lift in her chest. This was honest work, or close enough to honest that she could sleep after it. The sky broke something; she carried away the pieces. There was a whole economy in that, an entire philosophy. Things fall apart, and someone has to pick up the metal.

She filled the truck bed until the springs groaned. Aluminum sheeting, bundled. Copper wire, coiled and weighed. A door, absurdly, still hanging on its hinges, which she kept because doors sold better than you'd think to people rebuilding. By full dark she had maybe four hundred credits' worth stacked and tarped, and for the first time in weeks the number in her head wasn't negative.

She drove back toward town singing.

* * *

The impound yard sat on the edge of Harrow Flats behind a chain-link fence topped with razor wire that nobody had ever needed, because nobody in Harrow Flats had anywhere to run. Zephyrine saw the empty space where her truck should have been parked before she saw the sign, and for a moment she simply stood there in the road with her keys in her hand, feeling the evening tilt under her feet.

Then she read the notice zip-tied to the fence.

*NOTICE OF SEIZURE. RIVERGATE SYNDICATE, SECURED CREDITOR. VEHICLE HELD AS COLLATERAL PENDING REVIEW OF ACCOUNT STATUS.*

Account status. She almost laughed. Her account status was eleven months delinquent, renegotiated twice, and currently held together by the same kind of hope that made people chase storms. She'd known this was coming. She'd known it the way you know a storm is coming — watching it build on some far horizon, telling yourself it would turn.

It hadn't turned.

She found Vess at the Syndicate's field office, a converted rail depot that still smelled of creosote and cold iron. Vess was at a desk too big for the room, doing nothing in particular with a ledger, and he looked up at her with the expression of a man who had been waiting exactly as long as it suited him to wait.

"Halloran." He closed the ledger. "Good haul tonight?"

"You took my truck."

"I secured an asset." Vess had a way of speaking that made theft sound like weather. "You're eleven months behind. The review board met this morning. Your collateral is now under Syndicate management pending—"

"Pending what? You know what I make. You know what the last two seasons cost me. Hail took my rig's transmission in spring, and I've been driving on borrowed time and borrowed parts ever since. Give me a week. I've got four hundred credits of salvage in that bed—" She stopped, recalculated, started again, quieter. "Give me ten days."

Vess leaned back. Behind him, through the office's one window, the last light was going out of the flats, and Zephyrine thought, absurdly, of all the copper out there in the dark, slowly becoming worthless as more chasers reached it.

"Ten days," Vess said. "Here's my problem, Halloran. My problem is that you're the best salvager working these flats, and my problem is that you're also the worst debtor, and those two facts have been having a very interesting conversation above my head for about a year now." He opened a drawer. Took out a folder. Set it on the desk between them without opening it, the way a gambler sets down cards. "So I have a proposal. One job. Off the books, off the ledger, off every record the Syndicate keeps — which should tell you something about the job."

"What kind of job?"

"Investigation. Low risk, allegedly." He tapped the folder once. "Two days ago a survey team flying the coast north of Greywater Point noted a cave exposed by the receding tide. Not on any chart. Not on any map going back sixty years, and we have maps going back further than that, Halloran, believe me. The tide went out further than anyone can remember and left a hole in the cliffs, and the survey drone caught something inside it on thermal. A heat source. Old stone holding warmth, maybe. Something else, maybe."

"And the Syndicate cares because?"

"The Syndicate cares because the Syndicate cares about everything that isn't on a map. That's most of our business, if we're honest." Vess smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and Zephyrine had learned the hard way that the warmth was real — that was the trap of Vess, that he genuinely liked her, and liked her best when she was desperate enough to say yes. "You go in. You look around. You tell me what's there. If it's worth anything, the Syndicate handles extraction and your account gets restructured — not forgiven, restructured, let's be adults — and your truck comes home tonight."

"And if it's not worth anything?"

"Then you've had a nice walk on the beach and we resume our usual arrangement, by which I mean the truck stays here and the interest keeps compounding." He slid the folder toward her. "One condition. You go tonight. Tide's out again at midnight and won't be out that far again this season. Window's closing, Halloran. It usually is."

She looked at the folder. She looked at the window, at the darkening flats where her whole livelihood was stacked under a tarp with no truck to haul it. She thought about the arithmetic of her life, how every number in it pointed the same direction, down.

Desperation, she'd learned, didn't feel like panic. It felt like clarity. It felt like the moment in a storm when the wind stops arguing and just decides, and all you can do is drive.

"One trip," she said. "I look, I report, I don't touch anything valuable without you hearing about it first."

"That's the spirit of partnership." Vess pushed the folder the rest of the way across the desk. "There's a vehicle out front. Fuel's paid. Tide window opens at midnight, closes at roughly two. After that the sea takes the cave back, and whatever's in it goes back to being nobody's business."

She picked up the folder. It was lighter than it should have been — a single page, a coastal chart with a hand-drawn X, coordinates, nothing else. No survey photos. No thermal data. Just the mark, like a treasure map drawn by someone who wanted plausible deniability.

"Vess," she said, at the door. "Why me? You've got enforcers. You've got survey teams. Why send your worst debtor to poke around a mystery cave?"

He considered her with that warm smile, and for once he seemed to answer honestly.

"Because if there's something dangerous in there," he said, "it's cheaper to lose you than them."

* * *

The coast road north of Greywater Point was washboard and fog, and Zephyrine drove it with the windows down, tasting salt, listening to the sea work the cliffs somewhere below in the dark. The Syndicate vehicle handled like a filing cabinet, but it moved, and by half past eleven she could see the tide line far below, dragged back from the rocks like a sheet pulled off furniture.

She parked where the chart said to park and climbed down a goat track that the survey team must have used, boots skidding on wet stone, until the cave opened up ahead of her — a black mouth in the cliff face, breathing cold air, its floor ribbed with wet sand and old, old stone.

Her lamp found marks on the walls within the first ten steps. Survey marks. Brass pins driven into the rock, their heads worn smooth, numbered in a notation style she didn't recognize — older than the Syndicate's, older than the Republic surveys, maybe older than anything she'd seen outside a museum. Someone had been here, measuring, a long time ago, and then the sea had swallowed the doorway and everyone had agreed to forget.

Deeper in, past a bend where the lamplight couldn't follow, the air went still and dry, and the floor changed from sand to flagstone, and Zephyrine stopped walking because the light had found bones.

Small ones. Arranged. Not scattered the way things die, but placed the way things are meant to be — in rows, in patterns, deliberate as text. And beyond them, on a stone shelf like an altar or a desk, something caught her lamp and threw it back in a hard brass gleam.

A compass. Old, heavy, cased in engraved brass gone green at the seams, lying face-up as if someone had set it down mid-journey and stepped out for a moment.

Zephyrine stood in the dry dark with the sea booming faintly beyond the bend, and she heard herself thinking in Vess's voice: *worth anything?*

She reached out. Her fingers had barely closed around the case when the needle swung — not settling, not pointing, but *swinging*, dragging itself around like a thing waking up, and it came to rest pointing directly at her.

Not north. Not the entrance. At her.

Behind her, at the mouth of the cave, gravel clicked under a boot that wasn't hers.
