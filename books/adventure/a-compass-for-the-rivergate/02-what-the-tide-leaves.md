## 2. What the Tide Leaves

The tide went out farther than Zephyrine had ever seen it go, and like everything else that year, it left her holding the bill.

She came down the cliff path at dawn with her boots laced double and her breath steaming, and the sea had pulled back so far it looked embarrassed about something. The flats below Rivergate stretched out gray and glistening, pocked with tide pools like spilled mercury, and there — a black seam in the headland where no seam had been in her thirty-one years of walking this coast — a cave.

The Syndicate's job sheet had said *investigate, document, salvage*. Vess had said it with that particular smile she kept for debts, the one that said *you don't have a choice, but let's pretend*. Zephyrine had said fine. She said fine a lot. It was cheaper than the alternatives.

She checked her kit at the mouth of the cave out of habit: lamp, chalk, measuring tape coiled on its brass reel, a pencil worn to a stub. Her whole life fit in a canvas bag and a repossessed truck she no longer owned. She stepped inside.

The dark took her the way the sea took things — completely, and without apology.

Her lamp found walls that weren't natural. Somebody had cut this place, ages ago, and the cuts had healed over with mineral bloom until the stone looked like the inside of a throat. Survey marks climbed the walls in faded lines: not chalk, something older, a rust-red pigment that someone had applied with care. She ran her fingers along one. Numbers. A bearing. A date worn to nothing.

"Okay," she said to the dark, because talking to the dark was free. "Somebody was measuring."

Measuring was a language she spoke. Storm chasers measured everything — pressure, distance, the shrinking gap between what you owed and what you had. There was comfort in it. A number didn't lie to you. A number didn't smile at you over a contract and use the word *forgive* like a knife.

She followed the marks deeper.

The bones came first as a shape her mind refused, then as a fact. A ribcage, seated against the wall with a dignity that suggested someone had arranged it that way, or that the dead man had chosen his chair. Beside him: a lantern long gone to rust, a ledger swollen with salt, and a coat that had given up being a coat sometime before her grandmother was born.

Zephyrine crouched. She didn't touch the bones. You didn't salvage a person.

The ledger, though. The ledger was fair game. She pried it open and the pages crackled like old ice. Survey notes. Bearings that made no sense — not wrong, exactly, but *stubborn*, each one corrected and re-corrected in a hand that got shakier down the page. The last legible line read: *It does not point where I want. It points where I need. God forgive the difference.*

"Cheerful," she murmured.

Then she saw what the dead man's hands had been folded around.

A compass. Brass, the size of her palm, heavy in a way that cheap instruments never were. The glass was unscratched. The needle — she tilted it, turned it, held it flat as her father had taught her before he taught her anything about debt — the needle did not care.

North was somewhere behind her, toward the cave mouth. The needle pointed deeper in, steady as a held breath.

She turned in a slow circle. The needle swung with her, patient, correcting, always finding the same bearing: into the dark, toward a wall with no door in it.

"Broken," she said.

The word felt like a lie the moment she said it. Nothing about the instrument felt broken. It felt *decided*. She'd chased storms for eleven years and she knew instruments the way Vess knew contracts, and this one wasn't malfunctioning. It was *pointing*.

She sat down on the cold stone with the compass in her lap and let herself want it. That was the dangerous part — not the wanting, but the arithmetic after. Salvage rights said the Syndicate owned whatever she found. Vess would appraise it at forty credits and sell it at four hundred, and Zephyrine's debt would shrink by a rounding error. Unless she didn't report it. Unless she kept it. Unless—

The light changed at the cave mouth.

Not the sun. Lanterns. Three of them, coming down the cliff path, and beneath the lantern light the sound of boots — the particular unhurried boots of men who had never once hurried for anyone.

Zephyrine did what she always did. She measured. Distance to the entrance: forty meters, no cover, no chance. Depth of the cave: unknown. Time: none.

She killed her lamp and pressed into an alcove behind the dead man's chair, and that was when a hand closed over her mouth from the dark behind her.

* * *

"Don't scream," a voice breathed, low and accented and very close. "They're not here for the bones."

She should have screamed. She'd like to tell you there was a calculation behind what she did instead, some chaser's instinct, some cold arithmetic. There wasn't. There was just the fact that the hand over her mouth was shaking, and she knew what shaking hands meant — she'd had them herself often enough — and she bit the scream down.

The stranger was thin, dark, maybe her age, pressed into the alcove beside her like he'd been poured into it. He had a surveyor's satchel and the salt-crusted look of a man who'd been hiding in a cave for days, and his eyes went straight past her to the compass in her hands with an expression she recognized because she'd worn it in mirrors: the look of someone seeing their own debt come home.

"That's mine," he whispered.

"Everything's someone's," she whispered back. "That's the whole problem."

The lanterns reached the cave mouth. Light raked across the survey marks, the bones, the ledger. A voice she knew — Dreech, enforcer, second shift, a man who'd once repossessed a widow's boat in a hailstorm because the paperwork was in order — called out in the flat tone of official business.

"Survey team. Syndicate claim. Come out where we can see you."

Nobody moved. Zephyrine held her breath and the compass and the stranger's arm, in ascending order of difficulty.

Dreech's light found the ledger. She heard him open it, heard the crackle, heard the pause. Then, softer, to his men: "He found it. The old notes say he found it. It's here."

The stranger's hand found hers in the dark and squeezed — not affection; instruction. *Get ready.*

"Here's what happens now," Dreech said to the cave, conversational, terrible. "Whoever's in here walks out with the instrument, and the Syndicate thanks you for your cooperation. Or we walk in, and we stop being polite. Your choice. You've got until my lantern burns down to the second ring."

Zephyrine's mind did what it always did under pressure: it made lists. *Give it up. Go back to the truck she didn't have. Take the forty credits. Owe Vess for the rest of her measurable life.* The needle in her hands, she noticed, had not wavered. It pointed deeper into the cave, at the blank wall, at nothing, at something.

"Second ring," Dreech said.

The stranger leaned in. "There's a passage behind the far fall," he breathed. "My family cut it. I can't find it in the dark."

She looked at the compass. She looked at the wall it insisted on. She thought: *this is insane.* She thought: *so is owing a syndicate your whole life for a truck they already took.* She thought about the dead man's last line, worn smooth by a dying thumb: *it points where I need.*

"Left or right at the wall?" she whispered.

"Left. Why?"

"Because it's pointing right."

She stood up out of the alcove, held the compass flat, and walked — not toward the lanterns, but away from them, into the black, following a broken needle toward a wall, with a stranger's grip on her sleeve and three enforcers shouting her name behind her, a name they shouldn't have known, which meant Vess had sold her location before she'd even reached the cave, which meant the job had never been a job. It had been a net.

The wall, when her free hand found it, was not a wall. It was a door wearing a wall's clothes, and her fingers found the seam the way the needle had found it all along, and the stone swung, and she fell through into a passage that smelled of deep earth and old air, pulling the stranger after her, and the last thing she heard from the world she'd understood was Dreech's voice cracking off the stone:

"Halloran! You're making it worse for yourself!"

She was already laughing — a thin, unhinged sound, swallowed by the dark. Because that was the thing. It had been worse for herself for eleven years, carefully, measurably worse, and this was the first time in her life she had no idea what happened next.

The needle glowed faintly in the dark ahead of them, pointing down and away, toward whatever she needed.

She was afraid it was right.
