## 4. The Range Ahead of the Snows

**Zephyrine**

The map Ivo carried was older than the road it described, and that was the first problem.

We'd stopped at a waystation called the Kettle — a low stone building with a stove, a bored keeper, and a wall of notices pinned three layers deep. Ivo spread his family's chart across a table scarred by decades of cups, and I watched him smooth it flat with both hands like he was calming an animal.

"Here." His finger landed on a pass drawn in faded ink. "Carrow Gate. The only way through the range before the snows."

The station keeper leaned over, squinting. "You want Carrow Gate this time of year?"

"I want Carrow Gate any time of year," Ivo said.

"Then you want it six days ago." The keeper tapped the notice board, where a weather service bulletin had been pinned over something older and more official-looking. "Early snows this season. Survey teams pulled out two weeks back. Gate closes when the white line comes down, and the white line's coming down early. You've got maybe six days of clear travel. After that, nobody crosses till spring."

Six days. I did the arithmetic the way I always did — fast, cold, in the part of my head that never stopped running numbers. Six days to cross terrain the map gave eight. Two days short, unless we pushed hard, and pushing hard meant horses blown and boots ruined and no margin for anything going wrong.

Something was always going wrong.

"And behind us?" I asked.

Ivo didn't look up from the map. "Behind us is Vess."

"Behind you," the keeper said, "is four riders who came through yesterday asking after a woman with a storm chaser's coat and a man who talks too much about maps. Paid for descriptions. Paid well."

I felt my spine go cold in a way the mountain air hadn't managed. "What did you tell them?"

"What everyone tells riders who pay well." He shrugged. "Enough to be remembered, not enough to be blamed."

So they knew. They were a day behind us at most, and gaining, because Syndicate horses were fed on Syndicate money and ours were fed on what was left of mine.

I looked at the compass sitting on the table between us. Its needle lay steady, pointing not north but east-northeast, off every marked route, into a fold of mountains the map dismissed with blank paper.

"It keeps doing that," I said.

"It's been doing that since the rockslide," Ivo said. "It wants us northeast."

"Northeast is nothing. Northeast is blank paper."

"Blank paper is where my family's maps get interesting." But he said it without conviction, and I caught the flicker in his eyes — the tell I'd learned on our second day running, when he lied about how far we'd come. Ivo lied small when he was scared. Big claims, small confidence.

"You don't know what's out there," I said.

"No," he admitted. "But I know what's behind us, and I know what's ahead, and neither one leaves six days of good choices."

* * *

**Ivo**

She counted everything. That was the thing about Zephyrine Halloran that took a stranger a day to learn and a friend longer to forgive. Miles, money, minutes, odds. She measured her life in units because units could be paid down, and somewhere in her past someone had taught her that anything unmeasured would be taken.

I came from a family that drew maps, which meant I came from a family that believed the world could be known. We were both liars, she and I. We just lied about different things.

That night, in the loft above the Kettle, I woke to find her standing at the window with the compass open in her palm, its brass catching starlight.

"It moved," she said, without turning around. "An hour ago it swung. Settled again, but further east than before."

"That means whatever it's pointing at got closer. Or more important."

"Or it's broken in a new way."

"Or that." I sat up. "Zeph. Ask me what you actually want to ask."

She was quiet long enough that I heard the wind change against the shutters. Then: "If we follow it — if we go northeast, into the blank — are we running or are we being led? Because those aren't the same thing, and I've spent my whole life being led by people who promised it was my idea."

I didn't have a clean answer. My family's records said the compass pointed toward survival — toward what its bearer needed, not wanted. But the records also said the last bearer died in a cave with it in his hand, so clearly the instrument had opinions about need that didn't match mine.

"We're being led," I said finally. "And we can stop following whenever we choose. That's the difference between a compass and a debt."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Vess would say the same thing."

"Vess would say it better. That's why she's dangerous."

Below us, in the yard, a horse stamped. Neither of us moved for a long moment. Then the sound faded into ordinary night noise, and we both pretended we hadn't gone rigid.

They were close. A day, maybe less now. And the snows didn't care whose side we were on; snows closed passes for everyone equally, which made them the most honest thing in these mountains.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

We rode out at gray dawn, and by midmorning the choice stopped being theoretical.

The fork came where the map showed a junction: west to Carrow Gate, the known route, the six-day race against the white line. East-northeast, the compass's stubborn insistence, into country with no roads and no guarantees.

Ivo reined up and stared at the needle like it owed him money. "It hasn't wavered all morning. Not a degree."

"The gate's west."

"The gate's west *if we beat the snow*. If we don't, we die in a white line on a mountainside with Syndicate riders walking up to our frozen bodies and taking the compass off my corpse." He said it flatly, without drama, which was worse. "Or we go where it points and trust that whatever it knows, we don't."

"Trust an instrument built by your family, that your family lost, that the Syndicate wants badly enough to send riders." I held up fingers. "Trust stacked on trust stacked on trust."

"You trusted a storm once. Chased it across the flats for salvage money. That's trusting something that kills people for a living."

"That was different. Storms don't pretend to be kind."

"No," Ivo said quietly. "They just are what they are. Like this." He turned the compass toward me. The needle held its bearing, patient as gravity. "It doesn't lie, Zeph. It lies *kindly*. There's a difference, and I think you already know it, because you haven't sold it yet. You could have. Any town, any buyer, debt gone. You've had three chances."

I hated him a little for saying it out loud. I hated him more because he was right. Three towns, three buyers I'd scoped and priced and walked away from, each time telling myself the market wasn't right yet.

The truth was the needle had started to feel less like an asset and more like a promise, and I'd been broke too long to mistake the feeling for safety.

Behind us, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been dreading: hooves. More than two. Moving at a pace that meant business.

"There," Ivo breathed.

I looked west, at the pass that might save us if we were fast enough and kill us if we weren't. I looked east, at the blank paper, at whatever the needle wanted.

Then I looked at the compass itself, and made the kind of decision I used to make chasing storms — the kind where you commit before you're sure, because hesitation is just another way of choosing wrong.

"East," I said. "But if that needle leads us off a cliff, I'm haunting you specifically."

"Noted," Ivo said, and turned his horse.

* * *

**Ivo**

By afternoon I understood why the mapmakers had left the northeast blank.

Not because the land was empty. Because it was *steep* — a climbing country of switchbacks and scree, pine giving way to bare granite, the air thinning with every hour until my lungs burned and the horses walked with their heads low. But the trail, when there was a trail, was real. Old cairns. Cut steps. Someone had lived up here once, and traveled often.

And the needle kept bending. Not wavering — *bending*, curving slightly south of east as we climbed, as though the thing it sought had shifted, or as though we were circling closer to it along some arc only the instrument understood.

Zephyrine noticed before I said anything. She'd gone quiet in the way she went quiet when her arithmetic stopped working.

"It's not pointing at a direction anymore," she said at dusk, from a ridge where the whole eastern range opened below us. "It's pointing at a place. Feel the difference?"

I felt it. For three days the needle had given us a heading. Now it gave us a destination, pulling like a hooked fish.

Below us, tucked into a fold of the valley where two streams met, smoke rose from chimneys. A village. Small, terraced, unmarked on any map I'd ever drawn or inherited — the kind of place that existed by not being found.

The needle pointed straight down at it.

"That's not on a route," Zephyrine said. "Any route. Why would it—"

And then I saw it too, the way you see a thing once the compass has taught you where to look: the village sat under the high peaks, in the shadow of the very pass we'd been racing toward. Carrow Gate loomed above it, a gray notch in the ridgeline, already dusted white.

The village wasn't a detour from the pass. It was the last door before the pass. Whatever happened there, whoever lived there, they stood between the snows and everyone on the wrong side of them.

"They're going to be trapped," Zephyrine said slowly. "When the early snows come down, this valley gets sealed. Everyone in it."

"Yes."

"And the Syndicate sent riders asking about us, not about villages." Her jaw tightened. "But Vess doesn't ride a day behind us out of spite. Vess rides a day behind because the Syndicate wants something in this valley, and the compass knows it before we do."

The wind came up the ridge, carrying the first true bite of winter. Below, the village lights were coming on, one by one, warm and oblivious.

Zephyrine stood there a long moment, counting — I could see her counting, miles and hours and odds — and then, for the first time since I'd known her, she stopped.

"Six days of clear sky," she said. "One day behind us, four riders. One village that doesn't know what's coming." She pocketed the compass and turned her horse down toward the lights. "We can't outrun all of it. So we'd better figure out which fight is ours."

I followed her down, and the needle stayed steady against her ribs, pointing home to a place it had never been — and I understood, with a dread that sharpened into something almost like hope, that the compass had never been leading us away from the Syndicate at all.

It had been leading us toward the reason they couldn't be allowed to win.
