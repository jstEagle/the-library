## 5. Honest Work at Bad Altitudes

Lowmark smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone, and it was the first place in three days that hadn't tried to kill us.

The village sat in a fold of the valley like something the mountains had decided to keep — forty-odd houses with slate roofs, a mill wheel turning slow in a creek the color of old glass, and terraced fields climbing the slopes in steps so narrow they looked carved by patience rather than tools. Goats regarded us from a wall. A dog made a show of deciding whether to bark and settled for following us at a distance, official and suspicious.

"They're not on any map," Ivo said, meaning it as an accusation.

"Neither are you."

He didn't laugh. He hadn't laughed since the rockslide, since the moment the compass needle had bent left and we'd gone left and the mountain had come down exactly where we would have been standing. Belief had arrived in him sideways, the way weather does, and now he kept checking it like a man prodding a bruise.

The compass sat in my jacket pocket, warm as a coin held too long. All morning its needle had been pulling off our line to the pass — dragging west, then south-west, insistent as a hand on the elbow. I'd argued with it. Ivo had argued with me. The pass was east. The snows were coming. Every hour we spent not climbing was an hour the Syndicate's riders gained on us.

But the needle didn't care about our schedule. It pointed into Lowmark like a fishhook set in flesh, and by early afternoon we'd stopped pretending we had a choice.

An old woman met us at the well, hands blue with cold, eyes doing arithmetic on us the way Vess did arithmetic on debts. "Travelers," she said, in the tone other people said *trouble*.

"Passing through," I said.

"Nobody passes through." She wrung out her rag. "Road's washed below the ford. You came over the sheep track, or you didn't come at all." Her gaze flicked to Ivo's mud-caked boots, to the way he stood with his weight off his left ankle, and back to me. "You came over the sheep track."

"We came over the sheep track."

"Then you'll want soup before you want lies. Come on."

Her name was Maren Ostrow, and her kitchen had a ceiling black with sixty years of smoke and a table scarred by knives and arguments. She fed us barley soup with a heel of bread each and asked nothing for it, which unsettled me more than any price would have. In my experience, free things were invoices that hadn't been typed yet.

Ivo, who could lie beautifully when it suited him, told her almost the whole truth — that we were fleeing men who wanted what I carried, that we needed the pass before the snows closed it. He left out the compass. Some instincts you don't unlearn.

Maren listened while she worked, kneading dough with the flat violence of a woman who'd buried two husbands and outlived both their opinions. Then she said, without turning around: "There's men in the valley already. Bought rooms at the Harrow Inn under a timber company's name. Surveying the water."

"Surveying it how?"

"Asking what it's worth. Asking who'd sell." She slapped the dough down. "Creek runs the whole valley. Every field, every beast, every winter store depends on it. And they're asking questions like men writing down answers they already have."

My stomach went cold in a way the altitude couldn't explain. *Buy the valley's water rights once the snows strand everyone.* That was the Syndicate's oldest play — not robbery, never robbery, just arithmetic applied until people signed. Vess used to say the best collateral was a thing a man couldn't live without, because then he'd pay anything and call it a choice.

"How long," I said, "before the snow closes the pass?"

Maren finally looked at me. "Six days, if the sky keeps its word. Four if it doesn't."

Behind her, on the wall, hung a framed survey map of the valley, drawn in faded ink — and I saw Ivo go very still looking at it, the way he went still around anything with grid lines. His family had made maps. His family's maps had been stolen, sold, scattered. Somewhere in his face was a whole argument about whether this one was kin or counterfeit.

The compass needle, when I checked it under the table, hadn't moved. It pointed past the village, up-slope, toward the terraces and the dark seam of the treeline beyond them.

Toward the mine.

* * *

We found the collapse by sound before we found it by sight — a low groaning in the rock, and then voices, thin and wrong, carrying down the slope like smoke through a crack.

The Greywater Mine had been worked out years ago, Maren's nephew Tam told us, running ahead of us up the track with a lantern that shook more than his hands did. Worked out, sealed, forgotten — until three days ago, when six villagers had gone in to strip the old supports for the winter timber quota and the mountain had shrugged.

Two had climbed out. Four hadn't.

Now thirty people stood in the trampled snow above the adit, and none of them were digging. That was the thing I noticed first, before the shattered timber, before the fan of rubble sealing the tunnel mouth. Nobody was digging. They were standing in a loose ring, passing a lantern, arguing in the low exhausted register of people who have run out of ideas but not out of night.

"Why aren't you in there?" I demanded.

A broad man with a foreman's gray temples turned. "We've been in there. Twice. Second time the roof spoke to us." He gestured at the pile — tons of it, slabs and scree packed tight as mortar. "We need proper shores, proper braces, iron props. We sent a boy to Karst Hollow for the smith. He won't be back for two days. Two days, and the air in there—" He stopped. Everyone stopped. The word *air* just hung there, doing its own arithmetic.

Four people. Forty-eight hours. A day lost waiting for iron.

I looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at the mountain. And in my pocket, absurdly, faithfully, the needle pointed straight into the rock.

"It's not telling us where treasure is," I said quietly. "It's telling us where the living are."

Ivo crouched at the rubble edge, pressed his palm flat against the scree like a man taking a pulse. "Void behind here," he murmured. "Feel it? The pack's loose near the crown, solid at the base. They dug wrong. You don't dig toward trapped men — you dig *around* them, take the pressure on a spine." He glanced up at me, and for the first time since the cave there was no argument in his face, only a kind of furious clarity. "Timber. Give me every plank in this village and I'll build the spine."

They gave us everything. Doors off sheds, planks from the mill, a fence someone would curse about in spring. Ivo directed the shoring like a man conducting an orchestra he'd been banned from for years — and maybe he had; maybe cartography and mining were cousins, both trades about knowing what lay under what. I hauled timber beside Tam and the foreman's daughter, a wiry girl named Sef who dug like the mountain owed her money personally, because her father was inside.

We dug through the afternoon. We dug through dusk, by lantern light, in snow that started soft and got serious. My palms split and bled and I wrapped them in rag and kept hauling, and somewhere around the second hour I realized I wasn't counting. Not miles, not hours, not what the work cost against what it paid. I was just hauling, because Sef's father was behind the rock, and that was the entire ledger.

I hadn't done unpaid work since I was nineteen. There had always been a rate, a debt, a handler's voice converting my sweat into somebody else's column. Now my back screamed and my hands were ruined and nobody was going to give me a single coin for any of it, and I felt — I searched for the word and it embarrassed me — *rich.*

At midnight the spine held and the last slab came free and a lantern beam stabbed out of the dark and found Sef's face, and the sound the mountain made then wasn't a groan at all.

Four men came out filthy and upright, one limping, one weeping without noise, all four alive. The village made a sound I'd only ever heard crowds make for storms — that same awed roar — except this one was aimed at us, and there was nowhere to put it. Sef hugged me hard enough to crack a rib and I let her. Tam pressed a mug of something hot and alcoholic into my ruined hands. Maren Ostrow appeared at my elbow and said, gruffly, "Soup's on," as if she'd known all along it would come to this, as if feeding us had been a down payment.

Ivo sat apart on a timber stack, gray with exhaustion, watching the firelight. "Your needle," he said when I sat down. "It pulled us here. Off the pass. Into this."

"I know."

"A day, Zephyrine. It cost us a day, and the Syndicate's riders are a day closer, and the snow doesn't care whose side it's on." He rubbed his face. "And I'd do it again. That's what frightens me. It points at what you *need*, and apparently what I need is to be the kind of man who digs."

I turned the compass over in my bandaged palms. The needle swung, steadied, and pointed — not at the pass, not at the mine, not anywhere useful at all. It pointed across the firelight, down the slope, toward the Harrow Inn, where men with a timber company's name were buying rounds and writing down answers they already had.

"Ivo," I said slowly. "It's still pointing at something we need to survive."

He followed the needle with his eyes and understood at the same moment I did. The danger wasn't only behind us anymore. It was sitting in a warm room down the hill, pricing the creek, waiting for the snow to do its negotiating.

Down in the valley, a door opened at the inn, spilling yellow light onto the snow, and a figure stepped out into the cold to look up the slope — unhurried, familiar even at this distance, the particular tilt of the head of a woman who had never once in her life lost track of where I was.

Vess had found the valley first.
