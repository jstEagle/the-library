## 8. Snowbound Bargains

The waystation had been a toll house once, back when the pass belonged to no one worth robbing. Now it was a stone box with a stove, two bunks, and a door that the wind kept trying to renegotiate. Zephyrine sat with her hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea that had gone bitter an hour ago, and across the room, Vess sat with her boots off and her pistol on the table between them, in plain view, the way you'd set out a dish of nuts.

"Comfortable?" Vess asked.

"I've been comfortable in worse."

"You have. I remember the culvert outside Marrow Flats." Vess smiled, small and genuine, which was the worst thing about her. She wasn't a monster. That was the trap of her. Monsters you could hate cleanly. Vess you could almost like, right up until you remembered what she was for. "Your truck's still impounded, by the way. Engine's fine. I made sure nobody stripped it."

"That's a strange kindness."

"It's not kindness. It's inventory management." Vess turned her cup by its rim, one slow degree at a time. "You're inventory too, Zeph. Always have been. The question is whether you come home as an asset or a write-off."

Outside, the storm they'd outrun for three days finally caught them. It hit the waystation like something with shoulders — a long white slam that filled the single window and made the stove's flame lean sideways in its draft. Somewhere up-valley, Lowmark was under this same sky, four hundred people and whatever food they'd managed to haul to the chapel loft.

Zephyrine thought about the compass. It was in Vess's coat, because of course it was. Vess had taken it at the truce flag with an apology so smooth Zephyrine hadn't even felt the theft until her hand was already empty.

* * *

They ate stew from tins and talked around the thing between them like two people sharing a bench on a long train ride. Vess was good at that. She'd been Zephyrine's handler for six years; she knew exactly how much silence a person could stand before they started filling it themselves.

"The valley," Vess said eventually, "is going to be fine."

"Lowmark?"

"Lowmark, the shepherds' camps, the whole water shed. The Syndicate has winter protocols. Supply sleds, medical rotation, fuel drops. We've done this before in the eastern ranges." She said it warmly, like a woman describing a school she was proud of. "Better than fine. Fed, warm, organized. All anyone here has ever lacked is management."

"And the price."

"Water rights. Long lease, fair terms, signed before the deep snow makes everyone unreasonable." Vess shrugged. "People sign faster when they're cold. That's not cruelty, Zeph, that's just physics."

Zephyrine looked into her tea. "And if they don't sign?"

Vess was quiet for a moment. Not guilty — Vess didn't do guilty — but careful, the way she got when a number didn't flatter itself.

"The pass closes anyway," she said. "Snow does what snow does. If the valley wants to be stubborn about paperwork, well. Spring is a long way off. People get flexible by March."

The words went into Zephyrine like a splinter, small and clean, and she understood that she would be carrying them for the rest of her life. *Seal the pass.* Not an accident of weather. A schedule. The Syndicate hadn't come to rescue the valley or rob it. They'd come to let the mountain do the negotiating.

"You're going to close the road," Zephyrine said. "You're going to sit down-valley with your sleds and your fuel and watch them starve until the ink dries."

"I'm going to watch nobody starve," Vess said, "if they sign. Which they will. Everyone signs, Zeph. You did. Six times." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a second the handler mask slipped and there was just a tired woman who'd spent too many winters in stone rooms. "I'm offering you the same deal I offered everyone. Walk away. Hand over the compass as Syndicate property — recovered asset, case closed — and your debt dies tonight. Every cent. You know what that means. No more salvage runs. No more storms. You could buy a new truck with cash and drive somewhere it doesn't snow."

The temptation was real, and Zephyrine hated how real it was. She thought of six years of arithmetic — every job measured against interest, every storm chased not for wonder but for margin. A life where the numbers finally came out even. She wanted it the way you want air.

"What happens to Ivo?" she asked.

"He goes home. In custody. His family's claim is a paper dispute; paper disputes take years, and he'll spend them somewhere with walls." Vess said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "That's the best version. There are worse versions. Don't make me find them."

* * *

Vess took first sleep, because Vess always took first sleep — she trusted the room, the lock, the pistol, the arithmetic. Zephyrine lay on the far bunk listening to the wind work the door and doing the math she'd done her whole life: risk against reward, loss against loss, the cold ledger where she kept herself.

Except tonight the ledger wouldn't balance. Every column ended the same place: a valley sealed in white, a chapel loft running empty by February, Ivo behind walls, and her own debt paid in full with other people's hunger. It balanced perfectly. That was the obscenity of it. Survival arithmetic always balanced, if you were willing to put enough lives on the other side of the equals sign.

She thought about the compass. About the needle that didn't point north but pointed toward need — hers, once, and then the village's, and then, in the whiteout, toward her, when Ivo had followed it through a screaming wall of snow to find her half-buried and blue-lipped. An instrument that lied kindly. That measured not distance but desperation, and always pointed at the next true thing.

Her debt was eleven thousand marks. She'd known the figure so long it had stopped being money and become a fact of her body, like a scar. Eleven thousand marks against four hundred people's winter.

She'd never had such cheap odds in her life.

* * *

She waited for Vess's breathing to go long and even. Then she waited another hour, because Vess faked sleep better than most people slept. The stove ticked. The wind changed keys. Finally Zephyrine rose, barefoot on cold stone, and crossed the room.

The compass was in Vess's coat, inner pocket, left side, over the heart. Of course it was. Vess kept everything valuable where she could feel it beat.

Zephyrine lifted it out with two fingers, slow as pulling a splinter, and the brass was ice-cold and alive in her palm. The needle swung, steadied, and pointed — not north, not down-valley, not toward any exit — but up-slope, into the worst of the storm, toward Lowmark, toward the chapel, toward the people who needed it more than she'd ever needed anything.

It pointed at her choice. It had been pointing at it all night.

"Put it back, Zeph."

Vess's voice, quiet, awake, from the bunk. Zephyrine closed her eyes. Of course.

"You were awake."

"For the last hour. You breathe differently when you're deciding something." Vess sat up slowly, and her hand went not to the pistol on the table but to the table's edge, gripping it. "One last time. Give it here, and tomorrow we walk down together and you're free. Free, Zeph. You've never once been free. I'm offering you the only thing you actually want."

"You don't know what I want."

"I've read your file. I wrote half of it." Vess stood, and the room felt smaller. "You want out. Everyone wants out. This is the door."

Zephyrine held the compass against her chest and found, to her surprise, that her hands weren't shaking. "Six years ago you'd have been right. But I spent a day digging miners out of a collapsed shaft, and nobody paid me, and it was the first honest work I've done since I signed my first contract. And I learned something in that hole, Vess. I learned that I've been measuring my life in marks. Marks owed, marks earned, marks of margin. And marks are just — weight. Dead weight." She stepped back toward the door. "I'm done weighing. I'm going to start counting in years. Theirs."

"They'll die without the Syndicate's sleds."

"They'll die with them, just slower and on paper. At least dying free is honest." She pulled the bolt. Wind and white poured in. "I'm sorry about your trust, Vess. Genuinely. But I notice I'm not sorry about anything else, and I think that's the tell."

Vess didn't reach for the gun. That was the strangest part — she just stood there in the stove light, watching Zephyrine become a write-off, and something in her face was almost grief.

"You'll freeze before the tree line."

"No," Zephyrine said. "I won't be navigating alone."

She stepped into the storm, and the door slammed shut behind her, cutting off the warmth, the offer, the old life entire. The needle burned steady against her palm, pointing up into the white, and Zephyrine Halloran — debtor, chaser, thief, and for the first time in six years, a free woman owing everything — walked into the snow toward the sound of a bell ringing somewhere above her, thin and human and stubborn, from the direction of Lowmark.

Behind her, muffled by the wind, she heard the waystation door open again, and boots in the snow, following.

Not Vess's.

Lighter. Faster. Running.

"Ivo?" she called into the white.

The bell rang louder. And the storm answered with a sound she knew too well — the deep, rolling crack of the mountains letting go.
