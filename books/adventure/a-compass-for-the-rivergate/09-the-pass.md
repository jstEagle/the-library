## 9. The Pass

The storm hit the pass an hour before dawn, and Zephyrine stood at its mouth with a broken compass in her pocket and forty-one villagers behind her.

The wind came down the col in long white gusts, erasing the trail, erasing the ridgelines, erasing everything except the next ten feet. Behind them, far below, torchlight moved along the valley road — Syndicate riders, six of them, closing at a pace that said they'd stopped pretending to be polite. Ahead, the pass narrowed to a throat of black rock where the snow already lay hip-deep and drifting.

"Tell me again," Ivo shouted over the wind, "why we didn't take the southern route."

"Because the southern route is where Vess is waiting."

"And this is better?"

"This is *true*." She pulled out the compass. The needle swung once, twice, then steadied — not north, not toward the road, but up and through, into the white throat of the pass, toward whatever was on the other side of it. Toward survival. It had never lied to her. It had only ever told her things she didn't want to hear.

She held it up so the front rank of Lowmark's families could see. Old Sefa with her granddaughters. The Kell brothers, who'd spent two days hauling their mother on a sledge. Tam Broadfoot, who hadn't slept since the mine, since Zephyrine and Ivo had dug him and eleven others out of the dark. Forty-one people who had looked at her like she was something other than a debt in motion.

"The needle points to what you need," she called. "Not what you want. Not what's easy. What you need. Stay between me and Ivo. Nobody wanders. Nobody stops."

Tam raised his lantern. "And if the needle lies?"

"It doesn't lie kindly or cruelly," Ivo said. "It just tells the truth. That's worse than lying, most days."

Zephyrine turned into the wind and walked.

* * *

The first hour was the easy part, which meant it only tried to kill them twice.

A cornice let go above them as they crossed the first shelf — a soft whump, like the mountain clearing its throat — and the compass needle shivered and bent left before anyone heard anything. Zephyrine read it, screamed the turn, and thirty seconds later the slope behind them came down in a long white slide that buried the trail they'd just left. It also buried the Syndicate riders' line of approach, which bought them time, though nobody cheered. Time bought with avalanches was time spent standing in the avalanche's neighborhood.

They climbed. The pass rose in switchbacks cut by hands long dead — old survey marks on the rock, faded paint, the same kind Ivo had shown her in the cave. His family had mapped these mountains generations ago. He touched each mark as he passed it, like greeting relatives.

At the second shelf the wind found a gap between the peaks and came through it like a blade. The Kell brothers' sledge skidded; their mother slid free; for one horrible moment she was a dark shape sliding down a white slope, and then Tam Broadfoot threw himself flat, caught her wrist, and held on while three men anchored him until the slide stopped. Nobody spoke about how close it had been. There wasn't time, and there wasn't breath to spare.

Zephyrine checked the compass every hundred steps. The needle never wavered from its bearing: up, through, onward. But she noticed something else, something that made her stomach drop each time she looked.

The needle was dimming.

Not much. A faintness at the tip, like a lamp running low on oil. She showed Ivo without showing anyone else.

"It's been doing that since the waystation," he said grimly. "Since you took it back off Vess."

"What does it mean?"

"I don't know. My grandmother said the compass spends itself. That it isn't an instrument, it's a *promise*, and promises run out." He met her eyes through the streaming snow. "How much farther do forty-one people need it?"

Zephyrine did the arithmetic she hated. Six hours to the far side, maybe eight in this. The needle might have four in it. Maybe five.

Behind them, faint under the wind, she heard the crack of a signal flare. Red light bloomed through the whiteout. The riders had reached the slide and were finding their way around it.

* * *

They came to the throat of the pass at midmorning, and it was worse than the maps promised — a slot barely wide enough for the sledge, walled in ice-slick rock, with the mountain hanging over it like a held breath. And across the slot, where the old trail should have continued, there was nothing. A fresh slide had taken it sometime in the night. Forty feet of smooth, steep, featureless snow, and beyond it, somewhere in the white, the descent to the valley on the far side.

Ivo stared at it. "There's no trail."

"There's a bearing." Zephyrine held up the compass. The needle pointed straight across the blank face of the slide, unbothered, certain, and dimmer than it had been an hour ago.

"That's not a path, that's a *suggestion*."

"It's the only suggestion we have." She turned to face the villagers, huddled in the wind like a flock deciding whether to scatter. They were looking at her. All of them. They'd been looking at her since Lowmark, since the mine, since she'd come back for them instead of selling the thing in her hand to the people who wanted it. She had spent her whole life measuring distances — miles to salvage, days to payday, years to freedom. This was the first distance she'd ever measured in lives.

Forty-one. Plus hers. Plus Ivo's.

And the needle had maybe four hours left.

She knelt in the snow and took off her pack, and her hands were steady in the way they only got when the decision was already made and all that remained was the doing.

"Zeph," Ivo said quietly. He knew. Of course he knew. Two liars learn each other's tells, and hers was steadiness.

"It holds true for whoever carries it," she said. "That's what it does. So it doesn't get carried anymore. It gets *shared*."

"You'll break it."

"It's already breaking itself. I'm just choosing where the pieces land."

She stood, held the compass high so they could all see it — brass case gone green with age, glass crazed, needle burning its last faint light toward the far side of the white — and she brought it down on the rock at her feet.

It cracked like a shot. Once, twice — she struck again — and the case split along seams no one had ever seen, coming apart not like a machine but like a loaf, like something made to be divided. Inside, beneath the needle, the old mechanism revealed itself: not gears but a lattice of thin brass splinters, dozens of them, each one threaded with a filament of that same impossible guidance. Each shard trembled in her palm, tugging gently, all of them pointing the same direction. Across the slide. Through. Home.

The villagers crowded close. Zephyrine went family to family, pressing a shard into each palm, folding fingers over it.

"Hold it flat. It'll pull. Follow the pull, not your eyes — your eyes will lie to you in this weather, the shard won't. When you reach the trees on the far side, it goes quiet. That means you're done." She looked at old Sefa. "You know the valley roads over there better than any map. Lead them down."

Sefa closed her hand around the shard like it was a coal she meant to carry a long way. "And you?"

"We're the rear guard. Somebody has to make sure the riders don't follow us through."

It was half true. The rest of the truth was that there were only shards enough for forty-one, and the two of them would share the last one, the largest, the heart of the thing with the needle still faintly alive in it — enough for two, if they stayed together, if they moved fast, if the mountain allowed.

The first family stepped onto the slide. Then the second. Forty-one people walking across a blank white face of the mountain, each following a private tug in their own fist, strung out in a ragged line like beads on an invisible thread. It was the strangest and finest thing Zephyrine had ever seen: navigation as faith, distributed, held in forty-one open hands.

* * *

The last family cleared the slide as the red flare bloomed again, closer now. Two riders had made the shelf below the throat, dismounted, climbing on foot with ropes and rifles.

Ivo pulled her behind an ice ridge. "Rear guard," he whispered. "You actually planned something."

"I improvised something. There's a difference, and it's usually fatal."

They rolled stones. It wasn't heroic. It was loud and graceless and effective — a small avalanche of their own making that swept the two climbers off the shelf and closed the throat behind them in a roar that shook the whole col. After that, the pass was theirs alone, and empty, and the storm swallowed everything.

They crossed the slide together, sharing the heart-shard, Ivo's hand over hers, the dying needle pulling them down and eastward into the falling light. Twice the shard flickered and nearly died, and twice Zephyrine spoke to it — to the whole broken promise of it — *come on, come on, almost, almost* — the way you'd speak to a truck engine in cold weather, because some things respond to being needed.

The trees appeared all at once, black and sudden, like a wall raised out of nothing. Below them, lamplight. Smoke from chimneys. The far valley, and shelter, and forty-one shards going quiet in forty-one palms as their owners walked out of the white.

The shard in Zephyrine's hand gave one last pull, gentle as a hand on her shoulder, and went still.

She stood at the treeline, snow-blind, frostbitten, holding a piece of a broken thing that had never once pointed at her debts, and laughed until Ivo had to hold her up.

Below, a door opened. Old Sefa stood in the lamplight, counting heads as they came down out of the storm — and when she reached the end of her count, her face changed, and she started back up the slope toward them, calling a name that wasn't either of theirs.

Someone was missing.
