## 1. Miles, Not Years

The tide went out at dawn the way it always did along the Serrano flats—reluctantly, like a guest who'd rather stay—and Orsen Underhill was already out in it, boots sunk to the ankle in gray mud, driving her survey stakes with the patience of a woman who had long ago made peace with being alone.

Twelve years of practice, if she was honest. And she was honest with herself about most things these days. It was cheaper than lying.

"Left foot, right foot," she murmured to herself, working the stake driver in a slow rhythm. "Complain about the mud. Complain about the mud again." Her father used to say a surveyor who talked to herself was either mad or thorough, and that the two were closer cousins than anyone liked to admit. She'd been nineteen when he said it, standing hip-deep in a dig trench on the other side of the coast, and she'd laughed.

She did not laugh now. She hadn't laughed at his sayings in twelve years, which was, she supposed, its own kind of measurement.

The contract was a small one: map the tidal channels where the county meant to lay a drainage line, three weeks of solitary work for pay that would keep her fed through winter. It suited her. Contracts like this came to her because her reputation had a peculiar shape—*the best tracker on the coast, if you can find her, which you mostly can't.* She took work nobody else wanted, in places nobody else could read, and when the job was done she folded her instruments into their oiled canvas and moved on before anyone could ask her to stay for supper.

Miles, not years. That was how she measured her life now. Distance was cleaner than time. A mile never asked you what you were running from.

She was halfway across the second channel, marking a sandbar's edge, when she saw the boat coming up the flat—a government skiff, gray hull, one oarsman, and a passenger in a city coat that had no business within ten miles of salt water. Orsen stood very still and let them come to her, because people wading toward you tell you more than people you walk toward.

The passenger was a clerk-shaped man, thin as a ledger line, holding his hat against the wind as though it might file a complaint.

"Miss Underhill?" he called, twenty feet out, sinking slightly with each word.

"Depends who's asking."

"Mr. Pellory. Office of Territorial Surveys." He arrived at last, breathing hard, and looked down at his shoes with an expression of genuine grief. "They said I'd find you out here. They said you'd be *in* this."

"I usually am."

He produced a sealed envelope from inside his coat, dry and crisp, and held it out. "Then perhaps you'll want to read this standing up."

Orsen didn't take it. "If it's another boundary dispute, send it to my post drop in Corlow. If it's about the drainage line, I'm ahead of schedule."

"It's about the Halloway team." The clerk's voice dropped, and something in it made her look at him properly for the first time. "Six days overdue. Six days past any margin, Miss Underhill. Seven people. Instruments, provisions, a full complement of signal flares. Nothing. The office wants to mount a search, and the office wants—" he swallowed— "*you* to lead it."

The wind moved across the flats, pushing little wrinkles into the mud water. Orsen looked north, where the coastline rose into the broken country she knew better than her own hands—the shale hills, the drowned valleys, the old dig sites.

Her father's dig sites.

"No," she said.

Pellory blinked. "You haven't heard the terms."

"The terms don't matter. I don't go back there."

"Back where? I only said—"

"You were going to say the Kestrel Reach. Or the Vane coast. Somewhere north of the Salt Line." She pulled her next stake from the satchel and drove it home harder than she needed to. "I know every job that comes out of your office, Mr. Pellory. I know which quadrants they touch. Your missing team is walking around in the one piece of country I spent my childhood learning to hate."

The clerk was quiet a moment. Then, instead of arguing—which would have been the sensible thing—he crouched, wincing, and set the envelope on a flat rock at her feet.

"There's a manifest," he said. "Inventory of what the search party found at the abandoned camp. The office thought it might... inform your decision." He straightened, dripping, miserable. "For what it's worth, Miss Underhill, the men who found the camp wouldn't go back in. Grown men. Surveyors. One of them resigned by letter from the tavern at Millbrook, and his letter was four words long."

"What were they?"

"*It watches you leave.*"

He turned and splashed his way back to the skiff, and Orsen stood alone in the widening daylight with the envelope sitting on the rock like something washed ashore.

She should have left it there. She knew that the way she knew tide tables—absolutely, and without needing to check.

But she was a tracker, and trackers are curious the way rivers are wet, and after a while—she couldn't have said how long—she picked it up.

* * *

She didn't open it until evening, in her camp under the leaning elm at the edge of the flats, with a fire small enough to hide and tea strong enough to be rude about. The manifest was written in the tidy bureaucratic hand of someone cataloguing other people's misfortune:

*Item: two wall tents, intact. Item: theodolite, mounted, lens capped. Item: mess kit, six settings; seventh setting laid but unused. Item: cook stove, cold, fuel remaining. Item: personal effects, seven lots, undisturbed. Item: no tracks departing camp in any direction. Ground conditions: soft clay, recent rain. Note appended by field officer: "Ground should show everything. Ground shows nothing."*

Orsen read that last line twice, and the hair on her arms stood up in a way that had nothing to do with the sea air.

Soft clay after rain. She had worked ground like that her whole life. Soft clay after rain will hold the print of a gull, of a fox, of a man who crossed the camp in his sleep. For seven people to leave such a camp without leaving a mark, they would have had to leave as smoke.

Or be carried as cargo, over planks laid down and taken up again. Which was worse.

She set the pages down and told herself, firmly, the way you tell a dog to stay: *Not your problem. Not your country. Not your name.*

And then she reached the final page, and the firm instruction failed her entirely.

It was a sketch—not part of the official inventory, but copied in by some conscientious clerk, a pencil rendering of an instrument found mounted at the camp's center beside the abandoned theodolite. A secondary device, brass and walnut, unfamiliar to the field officers, unlisted in any supply record. The clerk had noted: *Function unknown. Maker unknown.*

Orsen knew the maker.

She knew him the way you know a scar.

The instrument in the sketch was a tide-compensating level—a sighting level that corrected itself for the pull of the water, so a reading taken at dawn would agree with one taken at dusk. There were perhaps four in the world. Her father had invented the design in a rented room above a chandlery when she was eleven, and she had held the first one while he soldered it, burning her thumb and not caring, watching him work with the fierce, embarrassing devotion of a child who believes her parent can bend the world.

He'd called it the *meridian eye*. He'd built every one himself. He'd never sold the design, never patented it, never let it out of his own crates.

So what was one doing bolted to a tripod in a vanished surveyor's camp, forty miles north, in the country where he'd dug in secret for half his life?

Orsen sat with the fire cracking and the tide whispering back in over the flats, and felt twelve careful years begin to lean.

She had left him at a dig site at dawn, with a pack and a hard mouth and one sentence thrown over her shoulder like a discarded tool. She had built a life whose entire architecture was *away*. Every mile she'd put between them had been load-bearing. And now here was his handiwork sitting in the middle of a mystery like a signature on a letter addressed to no one—to everyone—to her.

"He didn't send it," she said aloud, to the fire. "He doesn't even know I exist anymore. That's the arrangement."

The fire declined to comment.

But she caught herself doing the arithmetic anyway, the way she caught herself humming his old field songs sometimes, and hating it. Forty miles north. Two days' hard walking along the coast, one day if the weather held and she cut inland at the Salt Line. The Halloway team had gone missing six days ago. Whatever window there had been was closing or closed—but windows, her father used to say, were just doors that hadn't decided yet.

She hated that she remembered his sayings. She hated more that they were still useful.

Orsen banked the fire, laid out her bedroll, and stared up through the elm branches at stars she'd navigated by since she was eight years old. The sensible thing—the thing the twelve-years version of her had already decided—was to finish the drainage contract, collect her pay, and let the government send some other poor soul into the Kestrel Reach to find nothing.

She fell asleep deciding exactly that.

She woke before dawn with her pack already half-packed, and stood in the gray light looking at it with the expression of a woman betrayed by her own household staff.

"Oh, stop," she told herself. "You're going for the instrument. That's all. Someone stole his design, or borrowed it, or bought it off some assistant, and I want to know which, and then I'm coming home. Him and I remain what we've always been, which is finished."

The tide was turning. Out on the flats, the water was pulling back from the land, exposing mile after mile of dark, patient ground—ground that kept every footprint ever pressed into it, if you knew how to ask.

Orsen shouldered her pack, checked her compass, and set her bearing north, toward the country she'd sworn off and the father she'd buried alive in her memory, both of them waiting under the same low hills.

The mud took her first footstep and held it, clean and deep, pointing the way she'd gone.

Somewhere behind her, faint on the wind off the water, she almost thought she heard her father's voice saying something about mad women and thorough ones, and how close the cousins ran.

She walked faster.
