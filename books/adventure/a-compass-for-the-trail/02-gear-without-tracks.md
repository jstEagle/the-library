## 2. Gear Without Tracks

The survey camp sat in a fold of the badlands like a thing that had been set down by a careful hand and then forgotten.

Orsen saw it first from the ridge, a quarter mile out, and she stopped there the way she always stopped—letting the place come to her before she went to it. Wind off the water combed through the sage. Three canvas tents, taut and staked properly. A theodolite on its tripod, still standing at the edge of the flat like a patient bird. Smoke-stain on a cook ring but no smoke now. And no movement. No figure stepping out to shake out a bedroll, no one cursing at a jammed instrument case.

Twelve days since anyone had reported seeing them alive. In coastal country like this, twelve days was long enough for weather to erase a great deal. But not everything. Not if you knew what to look for, and Orsen Underhill knew what to look for the way other people knew their own hands.

She came down off the ridge in a wide arc, deliberately, approaching from downwind of nothing in particular because habit was habit, and she read the ground the whole way in. The last two miles she'd been reading it: coyote tracks stitched along a wash, old gull prints near a seep, the crushed chamise where some government mule had decided it owned the right-of-way. All of it ordinary. All of it accounted for.

What she could not account for was the camp itself.

She stood at its edge for a full minute before entering, which would have looked strange to anyone watching, and no one was watching, which was its own kind of answer. Then she stepped inside the perimeter and let the place talk.

Tents first. She unzipped the nearest one—no, not zipped, she corrected herself; laced, old-fashioned canvas with leather toggles—and found four bedrolls, three of them slept in, one rolled tight and unused, which meant someone had either left early or never arrived. Boots lined up by the tent wall. A wool coat folded over a crate. A paperback novel facedown on a cot, spine cracked, holding its place at page one hundred forty-something. Nobody packs up a camp and abandons their book at a good part.

The second tent held instruments. This was where her chest did something complicated, because laid out on the folding table, cleaned and oiled and ready for morning work, was a transit of a design she knew better than her own signature. Brass fittings turned down fine as watchwork. The leveling screws with the knurled collars that wouldn't slip when your fingers were cold. Her father had built six of them in his workshop behind the house, back when the workshop smelled of solder and cedar shavings and his pipe, back when she'd been allowed to hold things. This one had a hairline repair on the left standard—a silver-soldered seam she recognized the way you recognize a scar on a sibling.

*He sold them,* she reminded herself. *He sold them to universities, to agencies, to anyone with a budget. Half the surveyors on the coast are carrying his work without knowing whose hands made it.* It didn't help. Standing over that transit felt like standing over a sleeping animal from a life she'd walked away from, and she had to make herself turn around and leave the tent.

The third tent was stores. Rations stacked, water cans mostly full, a lantern with oil in the reservoir. Nothing looted. Nothing scattered. Whatever had happened here, it hadn't happened in a hurry, and it hadn't happened with violence—or if it had, it had happened somewhere else entirely.

Outside again, she circled the cook ring. Two tin plates sat on the flat rock beside it, half-eaten. Beans gone gray and hard, bread dried to a fossil. She crouched and studied them the way another woman might study a letter from a lover. Both plates eaten from with appetite and then abandoned mid-meal. Gulls hadn't touched them, which told her the birds had learned to avoid this place, or the plates had been covered until recently, or—

Or the meal had been interrupted by something the men had walked toward willingly. You don't leave food when you're dragged. You leave food when someone says *come look at this* and you go, expecting to come back hungry.

She got down on her knees then, and she did the thing she was actually being paid for, though the government man who'd hired her had clearly expected her to fail. She read the ground.

Inside the camp: boot prints everywhere, overlapping, days deep, edges slumped and softened by two cycles of sea fog. She sorted them by depth and wear into five distinct walkers—the sixth man's prints were absent entirely, confirming the empty bedroll—and followed each set outward.

That was where the camp stopped making sense.

Every trail ended. Not faded—ended. Four sets of boots walked to the eastern edge of the camp, toward the low bluff above the tidal flats, and there they simply stopped, all four within a few feet of one another, as if the men had gathered at that spot, stood for a while, and then evaporated. No scuffing. No sign of struggle. No drag marks. Beyond the last prints, the ground continued—hardpan crusted with salt, ground that would have held a track for weeks in this dry air—and it held nothing at all.

She walked the line of it twice. Knelt. Touched the hardpan with her fingertips like a blind woman reading a face. Clean. Not brushed clean, not raked clean—there was no sign of tooling, no broom strokes, none of the fussy erasure a guilty party leaves behind because they're thinking about trackers. Just absence, as complete and deliberate as a held breath.

People who walk away leave trails. People who are carried leave trails. People who are taken by flood or slide or animal leave wreckage. These men had left a table set, coats folded, an instrument running, and four lines of footprints that walked to the edge of the world and quit.

"Deliberate," she said aloud, just to hear a voice, even hers. "They left on purpose."

But nobody walks off a survey job barefoot, and their boots were still in the tents. Nobody walks anywhere in this country without leaving a print unless the ground refuses to say so, and ground only refuses when someone has taught it to. She knew exactly one person who could walk across soft silt and leave less trace than a heron, because he'd invented the technique, and he'd taught it to exactly one student before she'd stopped being his student.

Her jaw tightened. Twelve years, and the man could still reach into her day and rearrange the furniture.

She worked the rest of the light anyway, because thoroughness was the only inheritance she'd kept on purpose. She catalogued the camp, photographed the dead meal and the ending trails, and pried open the instrument case in the second tent looking for the field logbook, because a survey team without its logbook is a body without a pulse.

It wasn't in the case. It took her an hour to find it, wedged under the floor canvas of the third tent, wrapped in oilcloth—which meant someone had hidden it, and hidden it well enough that whoever came after hadn't bothered to search, or hadn't known to. She unwrapped it by feel, sitting cross-legged in the failing light, and opened it to the last written page.

Routine entries. Sights taken, elevations run, a complaint about the coffee in a hand that sloped and hurried. Then, three pages back, the handwriting changed. Same pen, same ink, but the letters leaned differently—tighter, more certain, the hand of a man writing something he didn't want to be legible to casual eyes. Coordinates. A latitude and longitude rendered to fractions of a degree, far more precise than any coastal survey required, circled twice in ink.

And Orsen went cold, sitting there in the darkening camp with gulls complaining somewhere below the bluff, because she knew that hand. Not the man's actual writing—she'd burned her father's letters years ago, or told herself she had—but the *style* of it. The way he formed his fours with the open top, the way his sevens crossed twice, the little upward kick at the end of every numeral like each number was trying to become the next one. She had spent her childhood copying that style at a kitchen table while he corrected her grip. She had spent twelve years trying to write like anyone else in the world.

Someone on this team had gotten these coordinates from him. Or from his notes. Or from a man who wrote like him, which amounted to the same thing, because there was only ever going to be one such man.

She read the surrounding entries. The surveyor—R. Calloway, per the flyleaf—had noted the coordinates arriving by post, unsigned, with instructions to verify soundings at the marked position during the spring low-tide series. He'd noted, in his hurried hand, that the position fell outside their contracted grid, and that he'd gone anyway, because the payment enclosed had been generous and the return address was a records office.

The last entry was dated eleven days ago. *Sounding party of four proceeding to marked station at first low water. Remaining two hold camp.* And beneath it, in different ink, added later, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*They did not come back.*

Orsen closed the logbook and sat with it against her knees while the dark came up out of the washes and the first star showed over the water. Somewhere past that bluff, past the flats, at coordinates circled twice in her father's handwriting, four men had walked out onto the tideflats at low water and the sea had apparently swallowed them whole—and then, presumably, given them back, because the government wanted its people found, and Voss's salvage fleet wanted whatever else was out there found faster.

She should take the logbook to the port authorities. That was the correct move, the professional move, the move of a woman who had spent twelve years building a life with clean edges and no family name attached to anything.

Instead she sat down with her own notebook and copied the coordinates three times—in code, in cipher, and once backwards, because paranoia was also an inheritance—and then she calculated the tide tables for the marked station with fingers that were steadier than she wanted them to be.

Spring low water. Six days out. The lowest series of the year, the only water shallow enough to expose whatever her father had circled twice and told a stranger to find.

Six tides between her and it. And somewhere between here and the horizon, she'd have bet the transit with the scarred standard, she wasn't the only one doing the arithmetic.

She banked the cook fire she allowed herself—one small flame, because cold made for careless thinking—and spread her bedroll inside the dead men's tent, among their folded coats and their abandoned book, and lay awake listening to the sea work the reef beyond the bluff.

Somewhere out there, her father's numbers were waiting in the water. And Orsen Underhill, who had crossed half a continent to stop hearing his voice in her head, discovered that dread and longing sound remarkably alike at low tide, and that she could no longer tell which one was pulling her toward the reef.

* * *

Forty miles south, in a cabin lit by a single green-shaded lamp, a harbormaster counted bills into a rubber band and said, "Underhill. The tracker's name is Underhill."

Merrick Voss turned the word over once, the way he turned over every piece of information—slowly, and with an eye to what it might be worth.

"Underhill," he repeated, and smiled at his charts.
