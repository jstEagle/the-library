## 3. The Wreck Beneath Six Tides

The tide table said she had four hours. Orsen Underhill had learned long ago to trust tide tables the way other people trusted scripture—completely, and with a running commentary of doubt.

She came down to the reef in the gray hour before dawn, when the water pulled back from the rocks like a curtain drawn by a tired stagehand, and what it revealed stopped her on the last dry shelf of stone.

The wreck lay across the reef flat like the skeleton of something that had once been magnificent.

She had seen wrecks before. The coast was strung with them, the way old houses are strung with ghosts—fishing boats, a mail steamer, one unlucky brigantine that local schoolchildren were taught to call *the* brigantine as though it were the only ship ever to have an accident. She'd mapped three of them herself, professionally, for insurance men who cared about tonnage and nothing else.

This was not that.

The timbers rose from the sand in black ribs, longer than any coastal trader, curved in a way that spoke of shipwrights who worked by eye and prayer rather than by plan. Copper sheathing still clung to parts of the hull, gone green as garden statuary, and where the low water lapped at the frames, Orsen could see the faint ghost of carved work along what had been the stern—a scroll, maybe, or a name worn down to suggestion.

No registry listed a vessel this size lost on this coast. She knew because she had checked, twice, sitting up half the night in her camp with the coordinates from the survey team's logbook burning a hole in her pocket like a coal. She had checked the colonial registers, the admiralty lists, the insurance rolls going back a hundred and forty years. Nothing. A ship this size did not simply fall out of the record.

Unless someone had made sure it fell out.

"Hello, Papa," she said quietly, to no one, and started down onto the wet sand.

* * *

Her father had taught her to read a site the way he read people: slowly, suspiciously, and always looking for what was missing rather than what was present. *Any fool can see what's there, Orsen,* he used to say, tapping his pipe against his boot heel. *The work is seeing what's been taken away.*

So she walked the wreck and looked for absences.

The first absence was order. Storm-tossed wrecks scatter; they come apart the way arguments do, all at once and everywhere. This hull had been opened deliberately—cut, not broken, the planking removed in neat sections stacked against a boulder like firewood waiting for winter. Someone had excavated this vessel with the patience of a surgeon.

The second absence was sediment. In the sheltered pockets between the frames, where sand should have drifted deep and undisturbed, there were clean scoops, recent ones, their edges still sharp. Days old. Maybe less.

The third absence was the one that made her sit down on a rib of black timber and laugh, once, without any humor in it at all.

There were no artifacts. Not a coin, not a nail, not a shard of crockery. A wreck this age should have shed its contents across a hundred yards of reef like a piñata. Instead: nothing. Either the sea had swallowed everything whole, which the sea does not do—it hoards, it scatters, it teases—or someone had spent years removing every last piece, cataloguing it, and carrying it away in secret.

And she knew exactly one man with the patience, the skill, and the monumental arrogance to do it.

Twelve years of silence, and here he was anyway, under her boots, in every careful scoop mark and every stacked plank. He hadn't even had the decency to be a stranger. He'd been a habit she couldn't break, written into the coastline itself.

"You couldn't have dug somewhere else," she told the wreck. "Anywhere else. There's an entire ocean."

The wreck declined to answer, which was more courtesy than her father had ever shown.

She worked inward along the hull toward the stern, where the survey logbook's circled coordinates pointed—not at the wreck itself, she noticed now, but at a point just beyond it, where the reef wall met a tumbled shelf of basalt. And there, half-hidden behind a curtain of kelp and rockfall, was a crack in the world. A cave mouth, dry at this stage of the tide, breathing out cold air that smelled of stone and salt and old paper.

Old paper. Her heart did something unprofessional.

She was reaching for her lamp when she saw them: footprints in the sand near the cave mouth. Two sets, both fresh, both leading in and not out.

One set was small. Booted. Deliberate. She recognized the stride immediately—the weight on the outer edge, the short economical steps—and felt the recognition land somewhere below her ribs like a dropped anchor.

The second set was larger, and wrong. Too heavy. Too even. Boots that walked like they were being paid by the step.

Someone else had found the cave. Someone else was already inside, or had been, recently enough that the tide hadn't yet erased them.

Orsen stood very still on the reef flat while the water ticked upward around her ankles, and let the awe curdle into its close cousin, suspicion. Her father had hidden his work for years. The survey team had vanished leaving gear but no tracks. And now paid boots stood at the door of the only thing on this coast worth hiding.

Coincidences, her father liked to say, are just patterns you haven't finished reading yet.

She read this one fast, and didn't like the ending.

* * *

Forty miles north, aboard the salvage flagship *Corvane*, Merrick Voss received the news over breakfast, which was how he preferred to receive all news—seated, unhurried, with good coffee and better butter.

His informant was a fisherman named Dole, a man whose loyalty was priced by the pound and whose discretion was, mercifully, also for sale. He stood dripping in the wardroom doorway, hat in hand, delivering his report in the tone of a man reading a grocery list.

"She's on the reef. Woman, alone, came down at first light. Been walking the wreck two hours now. Headed for the cave."

Voss buttered his toast with great care. "Underhill, you said. Did you catch a first name?"

"No, sir. Kept my distance, like you said."

"Pity." Voss considered the toast, found it acceptable, and ate it. Around him the wardroom went about its morning: charts being rolled, engines being coaxed, crew moving with the quiet efficiency of men who were paid well and understood why. "It doesn't matter. One woman with a knapsack is weather, not a problem. We'll be there on the afternoon tide."

"There's the other matter, Captain." Dole shifted. "Port office sent word ahead. There's a government inquiry forming about that survey team. Missing persons. They're asking questions at the harbor."

"Are they?" Voss poured a second coffee, entirely for the pleasure of pouring it. "How exhausting for them. And how fortunate that we're already conducting our own investigation into the team's disappearance. Diligently. At sea. Where the answers are."

Dole blinked. "We are?"

"We will be by Thursday." Voss smiled the way a ledger balances—everything accounted for, nothing visible. It was, he felt, the great misunderstanding of his profession: people imagined salvage captains as rough men with crowbars, shouting over rails. In truth, the entire trade lived or died on paper. Wreck rights, salvage claims, inheritance chains, registry omissions. A crowbar got you a hull. Paper got you everything the hull had ever carried, plus the hull, plus damages from anyone who objected.

He had spent eleven months and a frankly indecent sum acquiring the papers that gave him the Underhill wreck. The claim rested on an inheritance chain traced through a family name that had, amusingly, once meant something on this coast—Underhill. Some clerk in the records office had needed only the gentlest encouragement to find the connection plausible. Voss had found the irony delicious: the daughter would arrive to contest his claim and discover that, legally speaking, she was contesting herself.

"Anything else?" he asked.

"She had a boat anchored past the point. Old sloop. And—" Dole hesitated, and Voss learned everything from the hesitation, "—she talked to the wreck, sir. Out loud. Like she knew it."

Voss set down his cup. For the first time that morning, he looked genuinely interested.

"Knew it," he repeated softly. "Well. That settles the question of whether we hurry." He rose, straightened cuffs that did not need straightening, and addressed the room at large in the mild tone he saved for orders that mattered. "Gentlemen, we'll make the reef by the afternoon tide. Full complement. Mr. Harrow, bring the strongbox papers. And someone find out everything there is to know about a woman named Underhill—starting with whether she has family, and ending with whether anyone would miss her."

The *Corvane*'s engines turned over with a sound like a large animal deciding to be patient.

* * *

On the reef, the water had reached her knees before Orsen made her decision, which was itself unusual—normally her decisions arrived fully formed, like gulls. This one took its time.

Go into the cave now, with unknown boots possibly still inside and six hours of rising tide sealing the entrance behind her? Or withdraw, watch, wait for the pattern to finish revealing itself?

Her father would go in. That settled it; she went in.

The passage was tight and dry-floored, sloping down beneath the reef wall, and her lamp found marks on the walls that stopped her breath: surveyor's chisel cuts. Bench marks. Her father's bench marks, cut in his own idiosyncratic style—shallow, angled left, each one numbered in a cramped hand she could have picked out of a police lineup of handwriting. He had been coming here for years. He had built a road into the dark, one careful notch at a time, and never told a soul.

At the bottom, the passage opened into a chamber where the lamp light fell across stacked crates, waterproofed, sealed, numbered in sequence—and one crate already open, its lid pried and tossed aside, its contents gone.

Beside it, pressed into the mud floor, the heavy boot prints ended at a scuff mark and a smear of blood, small and dark and days old.

Not her father's blood. His prints weren't among them.

Which meant whoever had come for the crate had taken something from someone else—and that someone else had likely belonged to the government survey team.

Orsen stood in the cold dark with her lamp trembling slightly, twelve years of practiced solitude pressing down on her like the water overhead, and understood with terrible clarity that she was standing inside the reason the survey team had disappeared.

Above her, muffled by forty feet of basalt and tide, she heard it: engines. More than one. Deep-hulled, heavy, coming down the coast with the afternoon tide.

They were early. Or she was late.

Either way, she thought, killing her lamp and listening to the fleet grow louder in the dark, she was no longer alone on this coast—and whatever her father had buried here, somebody wanted it badly enough to make a government survey team vanish for it.

She had perhaps one tide to decide what she was willing to become to stop them.
