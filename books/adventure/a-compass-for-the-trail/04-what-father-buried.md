## 4. What Father Buried

The tide gave me four hours and lied about three of them.

That's the thing about tidal flats—they're honest in their dishonesty. A chart will tell you low water comes at 9:14, but the reef off Cape Verren keeps its own counsel, and my father taught me that lesson before he taught me to read. *The sea doesn't lie, Orsen. It just doesn't answer questions you haven't learned to ask.* I'd spent twelve years trying not to hear his voice, and here it was anyway, narrating my every step across the exposed shelf like the world's most unwelcome ghost.

The wreck lay in a trough between two ridges of black rock, its ribs fanned out like something that had died mid-scream. Older than any registry—I'd checked the harbor archives twice before coming, and the oldest vessel on record for these waters was a whaler lost in 1841, which this very much was not. The timbers were black oak gone silver at the edges, fastened with treenails, and along the keel ran a line of carving so worn it might have been decoration.

It wasn't decoration. I'd seen that mark before, pressed into clay tablets on a kitchen table while a man with ink-stained fingers said, *Remember this shape, little wren. It'll matter someday.*

It mattered now. It was also, unmistakably, freshly disturbed. Someone had been digging here—recently, methodically, and then had stopped mid-work, tools abandoned in the manner of people who don't expect to come back. The survey team's instruments sat in a neat row on a tarp, still running, still logging. Whatever had called them away hadn't given them time to pack.

I knelt beside the excavation and let my hands do the thinking they were better at than my head. The spoil heap told me what I needed: they'd been following a seam of ballast stone down toward the hull's broken stern, where the rock shelved into shadow. And behind the shadow—

There. A fissure in the cliff face, half-hidden by a curtain of kelp, breathing cold air against my wrist when I held it close. A cave. Flooded at high water, walkable now, if I didn't dawdle and didn't mind the dark and didn't think too hard about the fact that the survey team had walked into it too.

I thought about all three things. Then I went in anyway, because apparently twelve years of distance had taught me nothing except how to be distant about it.

* * *

The cave floor sloped down in shelves, each one slick with weed and dripping, and the light from the entrance died within twenty paces. I worked by lantern, one hand on the wall, counting my steps the way you count anything you might need to retrace in a hurry. At forty steps the passage turned, and at fifty-two it opened into a chamber tall enough to stand in, where the lantern flame steadied and threw gold across—

Across a room.

Not a cave. A room. Shelves cut into living rock, a table of planed timber, a canvas cot folded against the wall, jars of pitch and lamp oil ranked like soldiers. My father's field camp, built to last decades instead of seasons, because August Ishikawa never did anything by halves and never expected anyone else to finish what he started.

And there, on the table, weighted under a piece of ballast stone, a leather case with brass corners and my family name tooled into the cover in his handwriting.

I stood there longer than I should have. Long enough for the drip of water to become a kind of clock, long enough to notice my own breath going ragged. Twelve years is a long time to rehearse a conversation with a man who isn't present, and I had rehearsed it in every register from fury to indifference, and none of those rehearsals had prepared me for the simple, stupid ache of seeing his handwriting on something he'd meant—someday, somehow—for me.

*Orsen.* Not even written. Just implied, in the way the whole room was implied: *you found it, so you were meant to.*

"Damn you," I said quietly, to no one, to everyone, and opened the case.

* * *

The notes inside were his, all right—the small precise script, the marginal sketches, the habit of dating everything twice because he trusted neither calendars nor himself. But they weren't archaeology. Not exactly. They were testimony.

The wreck predated the colony's founding charter by eleven years. He'd proven it through timber analysis, coin hoards, a ship's bell with a founder's mark he'd traced to a Lisbon foundry. Which made it older than the land grants. Which made it evidence.

Because the wreck had been carrying documents—sealed, oil-clothed, bound for a colonial office that burned conveniently shortly after arrival. Copies of the original land-grant surveys. And according to the copies, the boundaries that made four families wealthy beyond arithmetic—including, on page after maddening page, a name that appeared in my grandmother's Bible and on my father's bookshelf and, God help me, possibly in my own bloodline—had been drawn from surveys that never happened, signed by surveyors who never walked the ground, certified by an office that took payment in the same silver the wreck had been carrying.

My father had spent nineteen years excavating proof that the ground we stood on was stolen, and that the theft had been laundered through the very profession he'd taught me to love. Surveying. The honest measuring of honest land. Except the land wasn't honest, and neither, it seemed, were some of the men who'd held the chains.

I read until the lantern guttered, then fed it more oil and kept reading. Somewhere in the middle pages I stopped standing and sat down on the rock shelf without deciding to. There's a particular vertigo that comes from learning your inheritance is a crime scene. Every acre I'd ever mapped, every boundary stone I'd ever set, suddenly had ghosts standing on it, and some of those ghosts wore my face.

He'd known. That was the worst of it. Page 61, in the margin, in the hand I'd know anywhere: *If O. ever reads this — I did not hide this from you to keep it. I hid it because keeping it is the dangerous part. Knowing is safe. Owning is not.*

"He could have just written a letter," I told the empty cave, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, which the empty cave was gracious enough not to mention.

But of course he couldn't have. A letter could be intercepted. This room, flooded twice a day behind a curtain of kelp on a reef no chart acknowledged—that was my father's idea of a mailbox. Nineteen years of dives and dewatering pumps and lamp oil hauled over black rock, all so that someday his daughter would find a leather case with her name on it and understand why he'd chosen stones over words.

I understood. Understanding and forgiving, as it turns out, are different rooms in the same house, and I wasn't ready to walk through the connecting door yet.

What I did next, I did as a surveyor, because the surveyor part of me was the only part still functioning. I photographed nothing—I had no camera—but I copied the key coordinates and names into my own logbook, wrapped the case in oilcloth from his stores, and noted the tide tables with the mechanical calm of a woman whose hands know their work even when her heart has resigned.

Which is when I heard the boat.

* * *

An engine, out past the reef line, running quiet the way engines run when they don't want to be heard. I killed my lantern and moved to the fissure, and through the kelp curtain I saw them: two vessels, riding high and unlit, holding position beyond the surf line with the patience of predators who know the tide will do their work for them.

They weren't fishing boats. Fishing boats don't hold station in the dark. Fishing boats don't carry men whose silhouettes wear the particular stillness of people being paid to wait.

The tide had maybe ninety minutes left in it. When it turned, the cave mouth would drown, the reef would vanish, and any evidence of my presence would go under with it—which was fine for me, less fine for the fact that I'd have to exit past whatever those boats were waiting for.

I weighed my options with the cool professionalism I'm famous for among the three people who know me, which is to say I panicked efficiently and briefly, then got to work. There was a second passage—my father's notes mentioned it, a blowhole shaft rising to the clifftop, "passable, barely, bring rope and humility." His notes always did assume I'd inherit both.

I took the case, took the rope from his stores, took one last look around the room he'd built for me across nineteen silent years, and felt the full weight of what he'd buried settle onto my shoulders. Legacy, it turns out, isn't a gift or a poison. It's both, in the same box, and you don't get to open it selectively.

The shaft was exactly as passable as advertised, which is to say I left skin on it in three places and said several words my father would have pretended not to hear. I came out on the clifftop under a sky going grey at the edges, the case heavy against my back, and looked down at the two dark boats below.

One of them was launching a skiff.

Toward shore. Toward the reef. Toward, I realized with a lurch, the survey team's abandoned camp—and whoever might come looking for it. Whoever already had.

Me.

I turned inland and ran, because the tide was turning too, and somewhere behind me a man named Voss—or whoever sent those boats—had just begun to learn that someone else knew what Father buried.

Six tides, the harbormaster's clerk had said in Port Calloway. Six tides before the salvage fleet's claim papers cleared.

I intended to be a great deal harder to find than a piece of paper.
