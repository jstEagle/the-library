## 5. The Salvage Fleet

Merrick Voss believed in paperwork the way other men believed in God: reverently, and with an eye toward its practical applications.

He sat at the great table in the Corvane's aft cabin — a table of salvaged teak, older than most nations, which he had acquired through a bill of sale that was itself a small masterpiece — and turned the folder in his hands. Leather. Government issue. The embossed seal of the Coastal Records Office still crisp enough to catch the lamplight.

Around him, his officers waited. He let them wait. Patience was not merely a virtue; it was a business model.

"Mr. Quill," he said at last, to the thin man with the ink-stained cuffs who served as his purser and, less officially, his archivist. "Read it aloud. Our people work better when they hear things in their own language."

Quill cleared his throat and unfolded the document with the tenderness of a man handling a first edition.

"By authority of the Coastal Records Office, Port Meridian, this instrument confirms transfer of salvage rights, inheritance claims, and all appurtenant interests in the vessel known variously as the *Sable Ann*, wrecked upon the Kestrel Reef, to Merrick Ansel Voss, captain and salvor, by lineal descent from Aldous Voss, merchant, deceased, whose interest in said vessel was established by grant of—"

"Thank you." Voss raised a hand. "The date?"

"Filed Tuesday last. Stamped, sealed, countersigned."

"And unimpeachable?"

Quill smiled the smile of a man who had spent eleven years making documents unimpeachable. "Three signatures, Captain. The registrar's is genuine — he signed it himself, after dinner, after the second bottle, believing he was signing something else entirely. The witness statements are from two respectable citizens who do not exist. And the genealogical abstract traces your descent from Aldous Voss through four generations of parish records that were, regrettably, damaged in a fire."

"There was a fire?"

"There will have been. The parish clerk has already been paid to remember one."

Voss nodded, satisfied. This was the craft of it. Anyone could steal. Stealing was for children and desperate men, and desperate men ended up in harbormasters' cells or at the end of ropes. But a *claim* — a claim was a different animal altogether. A claim walked into a courtroom wearing a clean coat. A claim had ancestors. A claim could sit across from a magistrate and say, in effect, *I am not taking anything; I am receiving what was always mine,* and magistrates, being men who themselves owned things they would rather not explain, found such arguments deeply comforting.

He had learned that from his father, who had learned it from watching better men than himself. His father had been a wrecker on these same shores, lighting false beacons to lure ships onto the rocks, and he had died poor and hated because he had never understood the essential truth of the trade: the wreck was never the prize. The *paper* was the prize. The man who owned the paper owned the wreck, the cargo, the coast it lay against, and — if the paper was good enough — the silence of everyone who might say otherwise.

Merrick Voss had improved on his father in every particular. He lit no false beacons. He simply became the kind of man to whom things legitimately belonged.

* * *

The chart table occupied the center of the cabin, and Voss rose now and beckoned the others close. Brannigan, his boatswain, broad as a door and nearly as talkative. Sorrel, the mate, who kept the fleet's ledgers and its quieter arrangements. Young Pell, the signal officer, who blushed easily and shot well.

"The Kestrel Reef," Voss said, tapping the chart. "Six tides from now, the spring low exposes the hull. After that, another month before the water cooperates again. We will be there for the fourth tide — not the first."

Brannigan frowned. "Captain, if we're claiming her anyway, why not take her at first low? Every hour is another hour someone else—"

"Someone else," Voss said mildly, "is precisely the point. There is a tracker working the coast. Orsen Underhill. Do any of you know the name?"

Pell shifted. "The surveyor's daughter? The one who mapped the tidal flats for the Crown office?"

"The same. She was offered the contract to find our missing government friends, and — according to a reliable source in the port town — she took it." He watched their faces. Brannigan looked puzzled; Sorrel, as always, looked like a man doing arithmetic behind his eyes. "Which tells us she has found something. Or will. Miss Underhill does not lose trails. It is, I'm told, her single virtue."

"You want her to lead us to it," Sorrel said.

"I want her to *have* led us to it. Past tense, Mr. Sorrel. By the time we arrive, the story must already be written." He straightened, and let the warmth drain out of his voice by degrees, the way he did when he wanted them to understand that the pleasant part of the evening was over. "Here is what occurred, as it will be understood by every magistrate, harbormaster, and newspaperman between here and the capital. A government survey team, tasked with assessing the reef, discovered evidence of criminal activity — smuggling, perhaps, or worse. One of their own number, or a party hired by them, silenced the team to protect that secret. That party is now fleeing along the coast, destroying evidence as she goes."

"And that party is—" Pell began.

"Orsen Underhill. Who, conveniently, was the last person seen near the camp. Who has no fixed address, no family connections anyone respects, and a twelve-year history of wandering alone through exactly this country. Who was, I remind you, estranged from her father — a man whose excavations in those hills were themselves of, shall we say, irregular authorization." Voss smiled. "The story writes itself, gentlemen. All it requires is a body or two, a witness or three, and time. Time is the one ingredient I cannot forge."

* * *

After the officers withdrew, Sorrel lingered, as he always did, standing at the cabin's stern windows where the harbor lights smeared across the black water.

"You're not going to kill her," Sorrel said. It wasn't a question. Sorrel had served him nine years and had earned the right to observe aloud the things the others were paid not to notice.

"No."

"But the story needs her dead."

"The story needs her *gone*," Voss corrected. "Dead is crude. Dead invites questions, inquiries, relatives — although in her case, mercifully, only the one relative, and he's an old man living on other people's charity. No. She'll run. People like her always run; it's the whole architecture of them. And a fugitive explains herself far more efficiently than a corpse ever could. Every mile she flees is a confession. Every campfire some shepherd reports is corroboration." He poured two fingers of brandy and did not offer any. "Besides, Mr. Sorrel, there is the matter of the documents."

Sorrel went very still. "You believe the old man's notes are true, then. About what's in the wreck."

"I believe," Voss said, "that a colonial land-grant fraud made six families wealthy enough to buy governors, and that the proof of it has been sitting under six fathoms of salt water for a hundred and forty years while those families' descendants sit in parliament. I believe that proof is worth more than the gold everyone assumes is down there. Gold spends. Paper *rules*." He swirled the brandy. "And I believe that if Orsen Underhill finds those documents first, she will read her own family's name among the thieves — and that knowledge, Mr. Sorrel, is worth more to me than her death. A woman who learns her legacy is rotten becomes either an enemy or a recruit. I can work with either."

"And if she burns it instead?"

Voss considered the question honestly, which was rare for him. Somewhere below, the tide was turning; he could feel the Corvane shift at her moorings, patient as everything else he owned.

"Then she burns a hundred and forty years of leverage, and I am left with a wreck, a claim, and whatever cargo the sea has left. A bad quarter. Not a ruin." He set down the glass. "That is the difference between my father's trade and mine, Sorrel. He bet everything on each ship. I bet only what I can afford to lose, and I make certain the sea keeps the rest."

* * *

At dawn the Corvane weighed anchor, and the fleet came alive around her — the *Corvane* herself, black-hulled and slow-looking the way strong things often are; the *Petrel*, lean and fast, built for running down smaller vessels; the lighters and derricks that would do the actual lifting when the reef gave up its dead. Voss stood on the quarterdeck with the wind pulling at his coat and watched the port fall away behind them, and felt the particular contentment that only came from a plan proceeding at the speed of bureaucracy.

Quill joined him at the rail, folder under arm.

"A message from our informant in the port town," Quill said. "The harbormaster's office confirms a woman answering Underhill's description bought passage east two days ago. Small boat. Single crew."

"East," Voss repeated. East meant the hills. East meant the old dig sites, the sea caves, the places her father had loved too much and documented too well. "She's going for the notes."

"Shall we alter course to intercept?"

"No." Voss watched the coastline slide past — grey rock, green scrub, the white scars of old wrecks bleached into the cliffs like bones. "Let her find them. Let her carry them. A woman alone, traveling fast, avoiding the roads, in possession of stolen government property connected to a murdered survey team." He smiled at the water. "Every step she takes makes my papers truer, Quill. Run up the signal for the Petrel. Tell Captain Ames to shadow the coast at a distance — visible enough to be remembered, distant enough to be deniable. If she reaches the reef before we do, so much the better. We'll want witnesses to what she's done down there."

Quill hesitated. "And if she destroys the documents first?"

Voss thought of the registrar signing his name after the second bottle. Of the parish fire that had not yet happened but already had. Of the long, slow machinery of ownership, grinding finer than any justice.

"Then the sea will have swallowed a lie," he said, "and the world will go on believing it. That, Mr. Quill, is what the sea is *for*."

He turned to give the order himself, and it was then that Pell came down the shrouds at a run, signal flags snapping behind him, his young face carefully arranged into professional calm and failing at it.

"Captain. The Petrel's lookout. Smoke on the eastern headland — a campfire, cold by now, but fresh. And tracks, sir. Boot prints, two sets, heading inland toward the old quarry road." Pell swallowed. "One of them's favoring a leg. Badly."

Two sets. Voss absorbed this with the mild interest of a man reading a ledger that balanced almost perfectly. The tracker had picked up a companion — wounded, by the sound of it. A survivor, perhaps. A loose thread with a memory in it.

Well. Loose threads were simply stories that hadn't been filed yet.

"Signal Ames," Voss said pleasantly. "Tell him to follow the quarry road. And tell him — gently, Pell, we are not barbarians — that Miss Underhill is to be taken alive, if convenient."

He looked east, where the hills stood hazy against the morning, and somewhere in them a limping man and a woman who measured the world in miles were making for the same coast he was.

"Four tides," he said to no one in particular. "Plenty of time for everything."
