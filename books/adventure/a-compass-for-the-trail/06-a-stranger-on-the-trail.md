## 6. A Stranger on the Trail

The hills above the reef were old country, folded and patient, and Orsen climbed them the way her father had taught her to read a page—slowly, twice, trusting nothing at first glance. Gorse held the light like a grudge. Somewhere below, the sea worked at its six-tide arithmetic, and she counted what it had already spent: two tides gone since she'd stood in that flooded cave with her father's field notes pressed against her chest like a coal.

Two tides. Four left, if the weather held, and the weather out here had never once held for anyone.

She'd meant to go straight down to the port town. She'd meant to walk all night. But the smoke changed her mind—a thin gray thread rising from a gully half a mile east, too small for a camp, too deliberate for an accident. Someone was hiding in her father's hills, and doing it badly.

Orsen considered walking away. It was, she reflected, practically her signature move.

Instead she circled downwind, quiet as a rumor, and came up behind the gully with her hand resting on the knife at her belt—not drawn, just introduced.

The man crouched by a fire no bigger than a cupped palm was younger than she expected and in worse condition than she feared. He sat the way people sit when sitting is a negotiation: weight off his left side, one arm cradled close, three days of stubble over a face that might have been handsome before something had opened his brow like an envelope. He heard her—she saw him hear her, shoulders stiffening—but he didn't turn around.

"If you're one of Voss's," he said, "you can save yourself the trouble. I've got nothing left worth taking."

"Voss's men don't announce themselves," Orsen said. "They also don't build fires you can see from the ridge."

He turned then, slowly, favoring the ribs. His eyes went from her face to her gear to the surveyor's chain slung across her shoulder, and something in them flickered—recognition, or its desperate cousin, hope.

"You're the tracker," he said. "The one they sent for. Underhill." He laughed, and it cost him; he winced through it. "Of course. Of course it's an Underhill."

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"Ilya Renner." He said it like a confession. "Junior cartographer, government coastal survey. Or what's left of one."

* * *

She made him move up-slope first, into a hollow where the gorse grew thick and the firelight wouldn't leak, and only then did she let him talk. He talked the way drowning men breathe—in gulps, without order, apologizing between sentences as though his own story were an imposition.

They'd been nine days into the coastal survey when Captain Merrick Voss's launch appeared off the point. Friendly at first. Voss claimed salvage rights pending, said the government team was mapping waters he had papers for, offered to escort them to the port town for the filing. Their team lead, Aldous Wren—careful, decent Aldous—had gone aboard to review the documents.

"He came back an hour later," Ilya said, staring into the fire. "Gray. Wouldn't look at any of us. Said we were breaking camp, that there'd been a change in the schedule." His voice thinned. "That night their men came ashore. Six of them. They took everyone but me. I'd gone up to set a benchmark on the headland—I always drew the short straw for benchmarks—and when I came down the camp was exactly as you found it. Tents up. Instruments running. Supper on the plates."

"No tracks," Orsen said.

"They swept them. There was a man with a rake and a bundle of brush, working the sand like a gardener. I watched him do it. That's how I know they weren't in a hurry." He looked up at her. "You found the camp. You must have thought—"

"I thought they'd walked into the sea," Orsen said. "It was the only reading the ground gave me. I hated the ground for lying, and then I realized it wasn't lying. It was telling me someone had made it lie."

Ilya nodded slowly. "Wren tried to warn me. Before they took him, he shouted one word up the hillside. I thought he was calling my name." He swallowed. "It wasn't my name. It was 'run.'"

The fire popped. Out beyond the gorse, the sea kept its own counsel.

"They're saying I killed them," Ilya added quietly. "There's a warrant. Harbormaster's men were asking after a surveyor with a head wound in Millbrook two nights ago. I've been moving at night since. Eating gorse pods and self-pity."

"The gorse pods are the worse of the two," Orsen said, and dug into her pack, and handed him hardtack and dried fish without ceremony. He ate like a man remembering food existed, and she watched him with the particular wariness she reserved for things that might be true.

Because here was her problem, laid out plain in the firelight: everything he said fit. The swept camp, the patient gardener, the forged claim she'd already traced through the records office to Voss's name—and threaded through all of it, her family name, borrowed like a coat from a closet. But she had learned from her father, of all people, that a story fitting perfectly was itself a kind of warning. True stories usually arrived scuffed.

"What do you want?" she asked him.

"Justice," he said, without hesitating, and she believed him, and wished she didn't.

"And after justice? You'll testify, they'll try him, maybe hang him, and then what?"

"Then—" He stopped. Looked at her. "What do you want?"

"To disappear," Orsen said. "I was disappearing quite successfully before your survey team vanished out from under me. I had a contract. A tent. A kettle. I intend to have them again."

"That's it? You find out a salvage captain murdered eight people using your family's name, and your plan is a kettle?"

"My plan was always a kettle. Your plan got eight people killed."

The words landed harder than she'd aimed them, and she saw it hit him—the guilt he'd been carrying up and down these hills for days, alone, wounded, hunted for a crime committed against him. He didn't argue. That was the worst part. He just looked back at the fire and said, "Yes. I know. I've been counting them. Eight. I say their names at night. It doesn't help, but I do it anyway."

Orsen sat with that. The wind moved through the gorse. Somewhere in her chest, twelve years of practiced distance shifted its weight uncomfortably, like a passenger who'd been asleep.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That was cruel, and it wasn't even accurate. They were dead the moment Voss decided they were. You surviving isn't the failure. Him failing to kill you is the whole point of you."

Ilya huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You comfort people often?"

"Never. You're my first. How am I doing?"

"Terribly. Keep going."

* * *

They took watches through the night, though neither slept much. Around the coldest hour she heard him reciting under his breath—Wren, Okafor, Lindqvist, the others, names she hadn't known and now would carry whether she wanted to or not—and she understood that this was not grief performing itself. This was bookkeeping. He was keeping accounts the way she read ground: because somebody had to, and nobody else would.

Near dawn she asked him about the wreck.

"Wren figured it out the second day," Ilya said. "The soundings didn't match any registry. Old hull, older than anything charted. He got excited—Wren got excited about sediment strata the way other men get excited about horses. Then Voss's launch showed up, and Wren stopped being excited and started being frightened." He paused. "There was something else. Papers Wren found in the records office before we shipped out. He wouldn't show us. He just said, 'If anything happens to me, find the Underhill woman. Her father will know what it means.'"

Orsen went very still.

"He said that? Those words?"

"'Find the Underhill woman.' Yes."

The tide turned somewhere below them in the dark, and she felt it turn in herself too—her father's handwriting in the survey logbook, her father's hidden notes in the flooded cave, and now a dead man's last instruction pointing at her like a compass needle. She had spent twelve years building a life with no room in it for August Ishikawa's daughter to be anyone's destination. And here she was, a landmark. A fixed point other people navigated by.

"I need to reach the port town," she said finally. "Voss has a forged claim. If it gets filed, the wreck becomes his legally, and legal is so much harder to fight than armed. Three tides. Maybe four."

"I'm coming."

"You can barely walk."

"I can barely walk *toward* things," Ilya said. "It's a direction problem, not a mobility problem." He pushed himself upright, swayed, refused her arm, accepted her arm. "Besides. You said it yourself. I'm the point of me. My testimony is the only thing that makes Voss a murderer instead of a businessman. You get me to that records office alive, and he hangs. That's not duty talking. That's arithmetic."

"It's suicide talking with an abacus."

"Then we'll be quick."

And there it was—the thing she'd been circling all night like water finding a level. She could leave him. She was good at leaving; she had references. She could be over the ridge by full light and gone down the coast alone, and in ten years the memory of this hollow would be one more thing she'd surveyed and abandoned.

But he'd said the names. All night, in the cold, he'd said the names, and she had listened, and listening was a debt.

"All right," she said. "Together. But we do it my way. Off-trail, night marches, no fires bigger than a cupped hand. And if I say run, you run, and if I say hide, you become geology. Agreed?"

"Agreed." He tried to grin through the split in his lip. "You know, for someone who wants to disappear, you keep showing up exactly where you're needed."

"That's the tragedy of competence," Orsen said, kicking dirt over the fire. "Now come on. We've got three tides, a warrant with your description on it, and a harbormaster on Voss's payroll between us and the truth."

"How do you know the harbormaster's bought?"

"Because," Orsen said, shouldering her pack and turning toward the coast road, "everyone worth buying already has been. My father taught me that too. It's the only lesson of his I ever thanked him for."

Below them, the sea began its long pull toward low water, and they started down.
