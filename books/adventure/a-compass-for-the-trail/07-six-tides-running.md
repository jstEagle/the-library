## 7. Six Tides Running

The tide chart told the truth the way I liked it: in ink, in columns, without apology. Three tides left before Voss's claim papers could be filed at the port records vault, and the coast between us and that vault was forty miles of everything the sea had ever decided to keep.

Ilya stood at the edge of the bluff watching me fold the chart, favoring his left side where Voss's men had opened him up two days ago. He'd stitched himself with a fishhook and sail thread, which told me more about him than any conversation had.

"Forty miles," he said. "On foot, with this side of me leaking, that's three days."

"Then we don't go on foot." I nodded down the cliff path to the cove, where a fishing boat sat on its mooring like a patient dog. "We go by water."

"That's Danner's boat."

"Danner owes me. I mapped his oyster beds for free when the county tried to tax them out from under him."

"He'll want payment."

"He'll get a story worth telling." I shouldered my pack. "Come on. Every hour we stand here admiring the view is an hour Voss's clerk spends practicing his signature."

* * *

Danner met us at the waterline with a lantern and the expression of a man who had already lost the argument in his head and was only waiting for his mouth to catch up.

"I heard about the survey team," he said. "Whole coast's heard. They're saying a woman did it."

"They say that every few years. Keeps the tourists honest."

"Orsen." He lowered the lantern, and in its small circle of light his face went old and worried. "There was a man here yesterday. Smooth coat, smooth boots, paid cash for crab like he knew what crab cost. Asked if anyone unusual had come through. I said no."

"But?"

"But he left money anyway. For 'the courtesy of continued silence.'" Danner spat into the foam. "I didn't take it. But Orsen—money like that doesn't leave. It waits."

I looked at the boat, then at Ilya, who was watching Danner with the flat attention of a man cataloguing exits.

"We'll take the *Kestrel* out past the point and beach her at Grayhaven," I said. "She comes back to you or she doesn't come back at all."

"She'd better come back." He thrust the keys at me, then caught my wrist. "Orsen. The smooth-coat man asked about you by name."

The wind came off the water cold enough to make the question unnecessary. I took the keys.

* * *

The first hours were almost kind. The *Kestrel* ran the coast with the tide under her, and Ilya sat in the stern holding the tiller the way he held everything—like it might be taken from him. Which, given recent history, was reasonable.

"You know these waters," he said over the engine noise.

"My father taught me charts before I could write. He said a coastline is just a promise the land makes to the sea, and the sea keeps breaking it." I checked our bearing against the running lights of Grayhaven far ahead. "I thought that was poetry. Turns out it was surveying."

He laughed—a short, surprised sound, like the laugh hadn't been used in a while and the hinges were stiff. "He sounds like a hard man to walk away from."

"The easiest people to walk away from are the ones who never ask you to stay. You just have to do it twelve years earlier than planned."

He let that sit. The water did most of the talking after that, slapping the hull in its patient way, and somewhere in the dark between the point and the shoals I caught myself thinking that failing alone had a certain clean shape to it, and that I had chosen that shape deliberately, the way you choose a coat. And that sitting ten feet from a wounded stranger who trusted me with his life because I'd fed him and not turned him in—I found I didn't mind the fit of this new one either.

I kept that thought below decks, where I kept most things.

* * *

Grayhaven's harbor appeared at gray dawn, all weathered pilings and gull argument, the records vault a squat stone building above the town like a jaw full of paper teeth. We had one tide of margin. Maybe less.

The harbormaster met us at the dock, a broad cheerful man named Pell whose handshake arrived a half-second before the rest of him.

"Rough crossing?" he boomed. "You'll want breakfast. There's a place two streets up does eggs like your mother made, assuming your mother could cook, no offense intended."

"We need to reach the records vault when it opens," I said. "It's official business."

"Official!" He said it like a toast. "Vault opens at eight. You've got time to eat. Leave the boat with me—I'll see she's tied proper, fuel her up even, no charge. Coast service, eh? We look after our own."

Every word was warm. Every word was fine. And underneath it ran the same current I'd felt off Danner's dock—the smooth-coat man's money, waiting.

Ilya felt it too. On the street above the harbor he stopped under a lamp post gone dark with morning and said quietly, "He didn't ask our names."

"Men who are going to sell you don't need your names. They need your boat."

"So we eat fast and walk to the vault."

"We eat fast," I agreed, "and we watch the boat."

We ate fast. The eggs were, annoyingly, excellent.

* * *

At seven-forty we came back down the hill and the *Kestrel* was already moving.

Not leaving—moving. Swinging on her mooring lines cut loose, engine turning over, and at her helm a man in a smooth coat, and beside him Pell, gesturing with the cheerfulness of a man directing traffic rather than theft. Two more figures waited at the end of the pier, and beyond them, out past the breakwater, a black-hulled ship sat at anchor with the patient bulk of a coffin choosing its spot.

The *Corvane*. He hadn't even needed to chase us. He'd bought the ground out from under our feet while we ate eggs.

"There's the skiff," I said. A rowboat tied at the far piling, half-full of rainwater. "There's our whole future, and it has a hole in it."

"Orsen." Ilya's hand closed on my arm. "If they have the boat, they have the field notes' location from whatever Pell tells them. They don't need us alive anymore. We run inland—"

"And lose the tide, and lose the vault, and spend the rest of our lives as the two people who killed the survey team, according to whatever papers Voss filed this afternoon." I was already moving along the fence line, low, toward the pier's blind side. "No. We take the skiff, we row around the breakwater, and we put ourselves between the *Kestrel* and open water. He wants his claim filed clean. A wrecked boat in the harbor channel delays filing. Delays are what we have instead of guns."

"That's not a plan, that's spite with navigation."

"Spite with navigation has gotten me this far."

He stared at me, and then—he grinned. Actually grinned, white and crooked in his bruised face, and something in my chest turned over like a compass needle finding north after years of pointing wherever it pleased.

"All right," he said. "Spite with navigation."

* * *

The skiff shipped water the whole way. I bailed with a tin cup while Ilya pulled at the oars with his bad side screaming, judging by the sound he made through his teeth, and we came around the breakwater just as the *Kestrel* cleared the harbor mouth with Voss's men aboard and Pell waving from the dock like a friend seeing off relatives.

I stood up in the rocking skiff—which every textbook says not to do—and raised both arms.

"Channel's fouled!" I shouted. "Skiff adrift! You hit me, you explain the delay to Voss!"

For one beautiful moment the *Kestrel* wavered. Her helm hesitated. And then the man at the wheel did the arithmetic men like him always do—delay versus mess—and chose mess.

The bow swung for us. Ilya grabbed the gunwale. "Row!"

We rowed. God, we rowed. The skiff was a colander with ambitions, but she was light, and the tide was going out, and I knew this water the way Voss's hired hands never would—knew the sandbar that showed its shoulder at half-tide right off the point, knew it the way you know a liar's face.

"Hard to starboard," I gasped. "Trust me."

"I've known you four days."

"Three and a half. Hard to starboard!"

He hauled the oars and the skiff leapt sideways and the *Kestrel*, drawing two feet more than we did, kissed the bar at eight knots with a crunch that threw her crew forward and swung her beam-on to the ebb. She'd float again on the next tide. She'd float there, blocking nothing, mattering nothing, while we came apart at the seams toward shore.

We grounded in the shallows below the town, and Ilya half fell, half climbed out, and we lay in six inches of freezing water laughing like idiots—laughing because the other option was screaming, and because for one unguarded moment neither of us was carrying anything alone.

"Orsen." He rolled his head toward me, seawater in his hair, breath smoking. "Twelve hours ago I was planning how to disappear south and die quietly somewhere with decent weather. Now I'm drowning in a harbor with a woman who fights boats with boats." His hand found mine in the wet shingle and held on. "I'm supposed to be terrified. I keep waiting for it. It won't come."

"It will," I said. "That's how you know it matters."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Failing alone—that frightened me for weeks. This—" he squeezed my hand, "—this frightens me less. That's the part I can't figure out."

I looked at him, and I didn't figure it out either. I just stopped trying to.

"Vault opens in twenty minutes," I said.

* * *

We came up the hill soaked to the ribs, and the records clerk took one look at us and reached, not for the ledger, but for the telephone.

The line was dead. Cut clean, at the junction box outside—fresh copper shine on the severed ends, visible from the window.

Pell stood in the doorway behind us, flanked by two men from the *Corvane*, still smiling his harbor smile.

"Storm damage," Pell said. "Terrible thing, storms. Best wait inside, folks. Captain Voss would very much like a word before anyone files anything."

And out past the breakwater, the *Corvane* began to raise anchor.
