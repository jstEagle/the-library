## 8. The Weight of a Name

The lighthouse keeper's cottage at Gray Harrow had a stove that smoked, a roof that leaked in exactly one place, and a landlady who charged double for both inconveniences and called it charm. Orsen paid it because the cottage had one other feature: a telegraph line, and a keeper—Mrs. Pellworthy, seventy if she was a day, with forearms like dock rope—who asked no questions as long as you didn't ask about her late husband.

Orsen sent three wires on the second evening after Ilya's fever broke.

The first went to the port records office under a name she'd used for contract work: *Requesting certified copy of salvage claim, vessel unknown, reef coordinates 44-19. Urgent.*

The second went to a surveyor friend in the capital: *If a claim is filed against me, how fast can it move?*

The third she sat over for an hour, the blank form in front of her, the stove ticking, Ilya asleep in the back room with a pistol under his pillow and a fever he kept insisting was "basically gone."

She had not spoken to her father in twelve years. She had rehearsed this conversation roughly four thousand times—in the shower, on trains, once mid-avalanche—and every rehearsal ended differently. In some she shouted. In most she said nothing at all, which was somehow worse.

In the end she wrote what a stranger would write:

*Documents found at your old site. Voss fleet inbound. Claim forged under Underhill name. Need everything you know about the grant. Come alone. —O.*

No *Father*. No *love*. Just the facts, which was its own kind of inheritance.

Mrs. Pellworthy took the wire without comment, then paused at the door. "Underhill," she said, tasting it. "There was an Underhill dug up half the coast twenty years back. Mad as a gull, they said. Brilliant as one too."

"He'd be flattered by half of that."

"Mm." The old woman squinted at her. "You've got his chin. The stubborn kind. That'll be sixpence for the wire and none of my business."

* * *

He came on the third morning, on the mail coach, wearing a coat that had been fashionable when she was nine years old and a hat that had never been fashionable at all.

Orsen watched from the window as he climbed down, paid the driver, argued briefly about the fare—he lost—and stood in the lane looking up at the cottage with the expression of a man reading a map of terrain he'd once known well and no longer trusted.

Twelve years had done to Aldous Underhill what twelve years does to everyone: it had made him older. His hair had gone from iron to ash. He walked with a stick now, though she noted he held it like a tool rather than leaned on it like a confession. But his eyes were the same—pale, quick, always slightly disappointed by the horizon for not containing more.

She met him at the door. Neither of them moved for a long moment.

"You got shorter," he said.

"You got old."

"I prefer *vintage*." He looked past her into the cottage. "Is there tea? I've been on a coach since Tuesday and the man beside me kept a ferret in his coat. I have opinions about this and nowhere to put them."

"There's tea."

"Then we're already having a better reunion than I feared."

* * *

They did not talk about anything important until the third cup.

This was, Orsen realized, their old choreography. Her father had never been able to approach a difficult subject directly; he circled it the way he circled a dig site, testing the ground, reading the strata, waiting for the soft place to open. As a child she'd found it maddening. Now she recognized it as fear wearing patience's clothes.

Ilya stayed in the back room, ostensibly resting, audibly listening. At one point a floorboard creaked and her father called out pleasantly, "If you're going to eavesdrop, young man, at least do it near the tea."

"I'm wounded," Ilya called back.

"You're *dramatic*. There's a difference. Wounded men don't argue with their bandages."

Orsen set down her cup. "The documents, Father."

"Yes." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, aligning the handle with the table's edge, a habit she'd forgotten and hated remembering. "You found the sea cave."

"I found your field notes. Half of them waterlogged. You might have waterproofed them."

"I waterproofed the important ones. If you only found the damaged half, then the vault held. Good." Something flickered across his face—not quite pride, but adjacent to it. "What did you read?"

"Enough. A colonial land grant. Fraudulent. Signed over coastal territory to five families on papers that were forged before the ink on the originals dried." She leaned forward. "And our name on the beneficiary list."

"Not *our* name." He said it quickly, and she heard the crack in it. "Your great-grandmother's maiden name. A clerical error I could have corrected forty years ago and chose not to, because correcting it would have meant explaining why I was looking."

"Why *were* you looking?"

He rose, went to the window, stood with his back to her—a posture she knew intimately, the posture of every lecture he'd ever delivered to himself instead of to her.

"Because thirty years ago I excavated a wreck off that reef thinking it was Spanish merchantman. It wasn't. It was a colonial packet boat carrying correspondence, and among the correspondence was the original grant—the true one—with annotations proving the forgery. Five families built fortunes, towns, *governments*, on that fraud. And the proof of it was sitting in my hands in a crate of brine-soaked paper." He laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what I did?"

"You hid it."

"I hid it. Because a man came to my camp within the week. Pleasant fellow. Brought good whiskey and a very reasonable offer for 'any curiosities' I might have recovered. And when I declined, he stopped being pleasant in a way I've spent thirty years not describing to anyone." He turned. "Two months later my funding vanished. My university appointment evaporated. My wife—" his voice caught, steadied— "your mother began receiving letters suggesting that accidents happen to archaeologists who don't know when a dig is finished. So I buried the documents where no claim, no court order, no salvage fleet would ever reach them, and I spent the rest of my career being publicly eccentric and privately vigilant."

"And me?" Orsen's voice came out flatter than she intended. "Where did I fit in the vigilance?"

He flinched. It was small, but she'd learned to read him the way he read ground, and she saw it land.

"You fit in the part I failed," he said quietly. "I thought distance would protect you. I thought—if the Underhill name was never attached to the wreck, never attached to *me*, they'd have no reason to look at you. So I let you walk away rather than explain why walking away was the safest thing you'd ever do." He spread his hands. "It was cowardice dressed as strategy. I've had twelve years to find a better word for it and haven't managed."

"The name didn't protect me." She stood now too, the old anger rising warm and familiar, an instrument she knew how to play. "Voss filed a claim under *Underhill*. Forged papers through a complicit records office. Your silence didn't hide me—it gave them a name to steal. They're framing me for the deaths of a government survey team, Father. Six people. Because someone finally followed your breadcrumbs to the reef, and the trail runs through us."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the survey team went missing. Why do you think you're talking to me at all instead of reading a letter?" He crossed the room, and for the first time she saw how carefully he was moving, how much the journey had cost him. "Orsen. Listen to me. The documents in that cave are worth more than the treasure anyone thinks is down there. Not money—*power*. Proof that could unravel five family fortunes and half the courts that protect them. Voss doesn't want gold. He wants leverage, and he wants it quietly, and the people he works for want it quieter still. If those papers surface through proper channels, they'll be disputed, tied up, bought, or burned. If they surface through *him*, they become a weapon."

"So we destroy them."

His head came up sharply. "We—"

"Collapse the cave. Let the tide take the rest. The fraud is thirty years cold; the families are old or dead; the harm is baked into the coastline itself. No document un-bakes it. But a living document in Voss's hands—" she shook her head— "that's a wound that keeps bleeding."

"That evidence could clear the survey team's names. Could convict Voss."

"Could. Through courts that his employers own." She held his gaze. "You taught me to read ground, Father. Read this one. What's the honest forecast?"

He was silent a long time. Outside, gulls argued over something dead, and Mrs. Pellworthy could be heard telling them so.

"The honest forecast," he said at last, heavily, "is that you're right, and I hate it, and I raised you too well to pretend otherwise." He sat down slowly, the stick hooked over his knee. "But understand what you're choosing. Everything I am—the reputation, the career, the whole architecture of my life—I built around protecting that cave. Destroying it means thirty years meant nothing except keeping a poison out of hands that wanted it. There's no glory in that. No discovery. Just a hole in the ocean where a secret used to be."

"Good," Orsen said. "Some things are worth more found than kept. You wrote that. Page two hundred and eleven of your own book, in the chapter where you also claimed to enjoy camping."

That startled a laugh out of him—rusty, surprised, real. "You *read* it."

"I memorized it. I was angry at you, not illiterate."

For a moment the twelve years thinned to almost nothing. He reached across the table as if to take her hand, and she almost let him, and neither of them acknowledged the almost, which was perhaps the most honest thing between them.

"Forgiveness isn't on the table yet," she said. "Maybe not this season. But the work is. Come with me to the reef. Help me finish it."

"Finish it," he repeated softly. "Yes. That's the right verb." Then his eyes sharpened, the old surveyor's glint cutting through the weariness. "One condition. We go at low water tomorrow, and we go *early*, because—"

The door banged open. Ilya stood in the frame, gray-faced, one hand pressed to his bandaged side, the other holding a scrap of paper.

"Mrs. Pellworthy just brought a wire up from the port office." His voice was rough. "A salvage claim was filed this morning. Under the name Underhill. It's been *certified*."

Her father closed his eyes. "Then we're out of time."

"No," Orsen said, already reaching for her pack. "We're out of *tides*. There's a difference—and it's the only thing left we control."

Outside, far down the coast, the first white line of the incoming swell was visible even from the cottage window, rolling toward the reef like something with intent.
