## 9. Low Water

The tide had a schedule, and Orsen Underhill had learned to read it the way other people read letters from home.

She crouched at the mouth of the sea cave with her father's field notes pressed flat against her chest inside an oilskin wrap, watching the water pull back from the reef in long silver sheets. Six tides. That was what they'd had. Now there was one left—the last low water before the spring tide would drown the reef so thoroughly that nothing above it would matter and everything below it would be Voss's for the taking.

"You're doing the thing," Ilya said behind her. He sat propped against the cave wall, his wounded leg stretched out on a folded tarp, whittling a stick into something that was either a fishhook or a very ambitious toothpick.

"What thing."

"The face you make when you're arguing with the ocean."

"I'm not arguing with the ocean. The ocean and I have an understanding." She checked the charges one more time—her father's old blasting kit, salvaged from a dig site that hadn't been touched in fifteen years, every fuse coiled with the neatness of a man who'd taught his daughter that carelessness underground was just death with better manners. "It's the fleet I'm arguing with."

Through the morning haze, far out past the reef line, she could see them: three hulls moving with the patient arrogance of money that has never once been told no. The *Corvane* led, black and clean as a magistrate's boot.

Ilya set down his stick. "You're sure about this. What's down there—once it goes, it goes forever."

"That's rather the point."

"Your father spent twenty years finding it."

"My father spent twenty years *hiding* it." She looked at him, and some of the fury she'd been carrying since the empty camp softened, because he deserved honesty more than he deserved her temper. "He wasn't preserving history, Ilya. He was sitting on a land-grant fraud that made four families rich enough to buy governments. Including mine. Every year he kept it buried, he was keeping me safe and calling it silence." Her jaw tightened. "I'm done inheriting other people's secrets. This is the only inheritance I intend to keep: the choice itself."

Ilya nodded slowly. Then, because he was Ilya: "The survey team would have wanted the documents saved, you know. For the trial."

"The survey team is going to want *witnesses* alive instead. You're my witness. The logs are your testimony. Voss's forged claim dies the moment those papers do—and so does anyone's reason to put a bullet in us over them." She held out her hand. "Help me with the fuse lines. And if you quote my father's safety lectures back at me, I'm leaving you here."

"He did write excellent ones," Ilya said, hauling himself up. "Very thorough. Chapter three is basically poetry."

* * *

Merrick Voss stood on the deck of the *Corvane* with a cup of coffee he had no intention of drinking, because a captain who drank while closing a transaction was a captain who might spill on his own paperwork.

The reef came up slowly through the haze, gray and indifferent, and Voss regarded it the way he regarded all obstacles: as a filing problem. Somewhere under that water lay documents that could unmake him, and somewhere on that shore stood a woman who had cost him two boats' worth of patience and one harbormaster's worth of bribe money. The harbormaster, notably, had been cheaper.

"Mr. Quill," he said.

His first mate appeared at his elbow with the punctuality of a man who had learned that Voss noticed lateness the way sharks noticed blood. "Sir."

"When we make the anchorage, I want six men ashore before the boat touches water. The woman and the survey man are to be taken alive if convenient." He sipped the coffee anyway; it was terrible, which felt appropriate. "If inconvenient, the paperwork becomes simpler. There were already killers loose on this coast—we've established that with the port authorities. It would be a shame if they claimed two more."

"And the old man?"

Voss watched the reef slide closer. "Edmund Underhill is an archaeologist, Mr. Quill. Archaeologists are sentimental about holes in the ground. Put him somewhere he can watch us work. Sentiment is leverage."

Quill hesitated. "And if the woman's already destroyed the—"

"She won't." Voss said it with the calm of a man reading a deed. "People don't burn inheritances, Mr. Quill. They think about it. They stand near the fire with it. But in the end, everyone keeps what's theirs." He smiled at the reef, which declined to smile back. "That's the whole secret of power. Nobody ever throws away their own name."

* * *

The fuse ran forty feet along the cave floor to a junction box Edmund Underhill had installed when Orsen was nine years old, back when the two of them had spent summers mapping this reef and he'd called the blasting kit "the argument-ender" and let her hold the plunger on practice charges, both of them laughing as the gulls scattered.

She knelt at the junction now with salt water seeping through her boots and her hands perfectly steady, and she hated how steady they were. Steady hands were her father's hands. Everything useful in her was his, and everything painful too.

"Orsen." His voice came from the inner chamber, where he'd been arranging the documents one final time—not to save them, she understood now, but to say goodbye to them properly, like a man seeing off old friends. "The tide's turning."

"I know."

"There's still time to take the grant papers out. Just the fraud evidence. We could leave the rest, let the water have the—"

"Dad." The word came out rougher than she meant it to. Twelve years of not saying it, and now it fell out of her like a dropped tool. "We're not negotiating with it. We're not keeping half. Half a poison is still poison."

A long silence, filled by the slap of water against stone. Then, quietly: "Your mother said almost exactly that. About me."

Orsen closed her eyes. "I know."

"I never told you enough that—" He stopped. Started again, in the voice he used for findings he wasn't sure he could prove. "I hid these things to protect you. But hiding is its own kind of theft. From you. I knew that. I did it anyway."

"We'll litigate it later," she said, and heard him huff—a real laugh, small and startled, the first one in twelve years—and something in her chest cracked open in a way that had nothing to do with explosives. "Fuse is set. Get to the mouth. We blow it on my mark, and then we are *gone* before the water comes back."

They were halfway to the cave entrance when the light changed.

Not the tide. Shadows—men, six of them, coming across the exposed reef flat with rifles slung and the unhurried confidence of people who had been told the outcome in advance.

* * *

Ilya saw them first from his position among the fallen rocks at the cave mouth, where he'd insisted on standing despite a leg that had filed multiple complaints. He counted six, noted their spacing—sloppy, arrogant, used to prey that didn't shoot back—and did the arithmetic he'd done a hundred times as a government surveyor, which was the arithmetic of men who measured things honestly: two of them against six was not a fight. Two of them against six *in a narrow place* was a negotiation.

"Orsen!" he shouted. "Company!"

The first rifle shot cracked off the rocks and took a chip of stone an inch from his ear, which struck him as rude. He fired back, deliberately wide, driving the lead man behind cover, buying seconds. Seconds were the whole currency now. Seconds, and one narrow place.

The cave mouth was maybe eight feet across, walled by fallen basalt. He dragged himself into the gap and made himself the geography.

"Renner!" One of Voss's men, a big fellow with a harbor accent, called out. "Captain says come quiet and the woman walks free! His word on it!"

"His word," Ilya called back, reloading, "is a document I've seen him forge! I have references!"

Another shot. He answered it. Behind him, in the dark, he could hear Orsen's voice—calm, clipped, running through the firing sequence—and beneath it, unbelievably, Edmund Underhill humming. The old man was *humming*, some dig-site tune, steady as a metronome, as though gunfire were weather.

Two of Voss's men tried the left flank through the tidal pools. Ilya shifted, fired twice, and made them regret the geology. His leg screamed. He informed it that it would be compensated later.

Then, from the reef flat, a shout of pure fury: "UNDERHILL!"

Voss himself, wading ashore ahead of his men, coat flapping, no longer a filing problem but simply a furious man in expensive boots. "The claim is MINE! The papers are MINE! You're destroying your own family's property, you mad little—"

"It was never property!" Orsen's voice rang off the rock, and Ilya, pinned in his gap, thought he had never heard anything so much like a verdict. "That's the entire fraud, you idiot—that's what the papers SAY!"

* * *

Later, Orsen would remember it in fragments: the plunger cold under her palm. Ilya holding the gap, bleeding from a graze along his scalp and grinning like a man winning an argument. Her father stepping out of the shadow beside her—stepping *forward*, into the open, toward the muzzle flash she hadn't seen, toward the shot meant for the hand on the plunger.

The crack. The impact. Edmund Underhill folding sideways with a sound that was not a word but was somehow her name anyway.

She caught him. She caught him and she brought the plunger down with her free hand, and the world ended in white noise and falling stone.

The cave mouth collapsed inward with a roar like the reef clearing its throat, tons of basalt sliding down onto the flooded chambers, onto the documents, onto the proof, onto the inheritance—burying the fraud so deep that no salvage fleet, no forged claim, no court in the world would ever dig it out. Dust rolled across the reef flat. Voss's men staggered back from the settling rock. And Orsen knelt in the gray fallout with her father's head in her lap, blood on her hands, pressing down on the wound below his shoulder and shouting at him with a fury that was entirely love wearing armor:

"You *idiot*. You absolute—you taught me better than to stand in front of a firing line, you taught me *everything*, you don't get to—"

"Orsen." His voice was thin, but underneath it, infuriatingly, was the hum again. Still there. Still steady. "Sweetheart. Breathe. You're talking over the data."

She laughed—or sobbed, or something between, some sound twelve years overdue—and kept pressure on the wound, and looked up through the dust at Ilya limping out of the smoke with his rifle empty and his eyes wide, and beyond him at Merrick Voss standing frozen on the reef flat, staring at the collapsed cave with the expression of a man watching his life's paperwork turn to gravel.

The tide was turning. She could feel it, the first cold tongue of water licking at her knees, the reef beginning its slow drowning.

And out past the point, cutting through the haze with all the subtlety of a tax notice, came a government cutter flying the survey service colors—with Ilya's missing teammates, she'd have bet her compass, and a warrant.

Voss saw it too. For one long moment, the salvage captain looked at the cutter, at the collapsed cave, at the woman kneeling in the rising water with blood on her hands and nothing left to steal.

Then he turned and ran for his boat, and Orsen, holding her father's life together with both hands, began to laugh.
