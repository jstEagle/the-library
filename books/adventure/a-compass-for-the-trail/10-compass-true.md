## 10. Compass True

The tide came in the morning after everything ended, as tides do, indifferent to whether anyone had died for it. Orsen stood on the shingle above the reef and watched gray water fill the place where the sea cave had been. There was nothing to see now. That was the point of what she'd done.

Her father sat wrapped in two blankets on a driftwood bench the harbormaster's men had carried down for him, because he had refused to be carried himself and had nearly fallen twice proving it. The bullet had gone through his shoulder, clean through, the surgeon said, as if even the lead had been in a hurry to get away from him. He was seventy-one years old and he complained about the blankets.

"They're wool," he said. "Wool holds salt. Salt holds damp. In three days I'll smell like a pickled herring."

"You already smell like a pickled herring," Orsen said. "You have since I was nine."

He laughed, and then winced, and then laughed again anyway, which was very much the man she remembered and also not, because the man she remembered would never have let her see him wince.

Down at the waterline, Ilya Renner was talking to the magistrate's clerk for the fourth time that week. She could see the shape of it from here — the patient back-and-forth, the clerk's pencil scratching, Ilya's hands moving when he got to the part about the night Voss's men came to the survey camp with lanterns shuttered and rope coiled over their shoulders. Six times he had told it. Six times he had told it exactly the same way, which Orsen knew was the hardest kind of telling there was. The easy thing, after trauma, is to let the story drift. Ilya held it like a bearing.

The surviving survey logs had done the rest. Merrick Voss had been meticulous — that was the horror of him, how *tidy* the corruption had been, every forged signature filed in triplicate — but meticulous men make one mistake: they assume no one else reads carefully. The logs showed the survey team had found the wreck on their own, before Voss ever arrived, and had recorded its position honestly. Which meant Voss's claim papers, dated three weeks earlier, described a vessel whose location he could only have known through the records office he owned. The magistrate, a dry woman named Corliss who drank her tea like it owed her money, had read the dates aloud twice in open session just to watch Voss's face.

He hadn't cried. Orsen had expected him to cry, or rage, or do something operatic. Instead he had straightened his cuffs and said, "I'll want my solicitor," as though the whole proceeding were a scheduling inconvenience. Power, she was learning, rarely knows how to fall. It just assumes someone will catch it.

They took him away in a plain boat, not the Corvane. The fleet itself was impounded, its crews paid off from the sale of the flagship, and half of them, Orsen heard, had already signed on with honest coastal work rather than go home and explain what they'd been doing all season.

* * *

Three days later, her father tried to help carry a crate of recovered instruments up from the beach and made it four steps before Orsen took the other end without a word and steered him back to sitting.

"I'm fine," he said.

"You're a liability," she said. "Sit."

"Your mother used to say that."

"My mother never met you."

That made him laugh until he had to hold his ribs, and she pretended not to notice that his eyes were wet, and he pretended not to notice that she noticed, and between the two pretenses there was something almost like peace.

The truth was, she didn't know how to do this. Twelve years of silence builds habits deeper than any trench she'd ever surveyed. She knew the exact number of miles between herself and this man at every stage of her adult life — she had measured them the way she measured everything, precisely, obsessively, as if distance were a form of safety. And here he was, forty feet away, smelling of herring and wool, asking for nothing.

That was the strangest part. He asked for nothing.

On the fifth day he finally said what he'd come to say, out on the bench with the tide going out below them.

"I hid the documents for you," he said. "Not for the families. Not for history. For you. Because I knew that the moment those papers surfaced, every name in them would become a target, and one of those names was yours." He looked at the water. "I did it badly. I did it without telling you, which meant I did it *instead of* you, and a father has no right to live his daughter's choices for her. Even to protect her. Especially to protect her."

Orsen said nothing for a long time. A gull screamed somewhere and thought better of it.

"Why the dig sites?" she asked finally. "Why bury them under our own work? You made me walk past them for years."

"Because I'm a vain old man," he said, "and some part of me hoped you'd find them. And a larger part hoped you never would." He shrugged, wincing. "Archaeology is mostly hoping two contradictory things at once. I'd have thought you'd remember that."

"I remember everything," she said. "That's the problem."

He reached over — slowly, the shoulder still bad — and put his hand over hers on the bench. His fingers were still strong. Surveyor's fingers, digger's fingers. Her own fingers, forty years on.

"One season," he said. "Work beside me one last season. No legacy, no names, no buried anything. Just the work. If you hate it, you walk away at the end and I won't follow. I've gotten quite good at not following. Twelve years of practice."

She looked at the reef, where the water was pulling back to expose black rock and the memory of a cave that no longer existed. She had spent twelve years measuring the distance between herself and everyone who might hurt her. It occurred to her, sitting there, that she had never once measured the distance between herself and the people worth the walk.

"One season," she said.

* * *

Ilya found her that evening mending a torn tent seam by lamplight, a skill she'd learned at fourteen and resented at fourteen and was oddly grateful for now.

"Heard you signed on with the old man," Ilya said, dropping onto a crate across from her. "One season?"

"One season."

"And after?"

"After, I imagine he'll try to negotiate for two, and I'll pretend to resist, and we'll both know how it ends." She pulled the thread tight. "What about you? The magistrate says your testimony closes the case. You're free. You could go anywhere."

Ilya was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on his face. "I spent six weeks hiding in the hills thinking about anywhere," he said. "Anywhere is very large. It turns out most of it is empty." He looked at her. "I thought maybe I'd stay where the work is. There's a survey contract opening up, I hear. Coastal mapping. Tidal flats. Terrible pay, terrible company."

"The company's not terrible," Orsen said. "The company is one archaeologist who smells like herring and one tracker who talks too little."

"And one survivor who talks too much," Ilya said. "We'd balance."

She smiled down at the tent seam. Then, because she was who she was, she said, "Renner. If you're staying, there's something you should know about how I am. When things get close, I measure exits. It's not personal. It's just the shape of me. Some mornings I'll already be halfway to leaving before I notice I'm doing it."

"I know," Ilya said. "I watched you do it at the harbor. You counted the boats twice before you'd step on one."

"That obvious?"

"To a man who spent six weeks learning to read one person's tracks? Yes. Very obvious." He leaned forward. "Here's what I know about exits, Underhill. They're only useful if something's chasing you. Nothing's chasing you anymore. So the next time your feet start doing the arithmetic, ask them what they're measuring. And if the answer is nothing—" he spread his hands— "stay for breakfast."

Orsen looked at him for a long moment. Then she went into her kit, found her oldest compass — her father's gift, brass-cased, the needle worn pale from thirty years of true north — and set it on the crate between them.

"What's that for?" Ilya asked.

"New rule," she said. "No more counting miles. We navigate by this or not at all."

Ilya picked it up, turned it over, and handed it back. "It doesn't point at anything," he said.

"It points north," Orsen said. "Everything else is walking."

* * *

They left for the first survey ground a week later, three of them and a mule named Biscuit who ate a map on the second morning and was forgiven, eventually, because the map had been wrong anyway. Her father walked at the rear with his bad arm strapped and his pace slow and his eyes bright as a boy's, calling out rock strata like greetings to old friends. Ilya walked point. Orsen walked in the middle, which was where she had always been and where, she understood at last, she had always belonged — not ahead fleeing, not behind guarding, but between the people she loved, holding the line.

At the top of the first ridge she stopped and looked back the way they'd come, out of habit, measuring.

Then she stopped measuring.

Ahead, the coast unrolled in long gray curves, unmapped, waiting, and her father's voice drifted up from behind — *"that shale is younger than it looks, mark my words"* — and Ilya laughed at something Biscuit did, and the wind came off the water carrying salt and the smell of beginning.

Orsen turned north, opened her compass, and walked.
