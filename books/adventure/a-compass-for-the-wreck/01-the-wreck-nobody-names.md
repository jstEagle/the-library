## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The delta gave up its dead slowly, and it never gave them up clean.

Theo Marchetti hung in the green half-light nine fathoms down, his fins barely moving, and watched the survey vessel *Meridian Star* take shape out of the murk the way a photograph develops — first the suggestion of a hull, then the hard geometry of it, then the details that made it a ship instead of a shape. Silt had claimed her to the gunwales. River grass grew from her deck seams in pale banners that waved when the current thought about it, and a mat of duckweed had stitched itself across her superstructure like a green shroud someone had laid on in a hurry and never come back to straighten.

She'd been down eleven weeks. Long enough for the river to finish the navy's paperwork, whatever that paperwork said. Long enough for the salvage permit — the one folded in oilcloth inside Theo's chest harness, the one that paid four times what a delta job usually paid — to feel less like a license and more like a bribe.

He checked his gauges out of habit, though he'd been down less than ten minutes. Air was fine. Depth was fine. The current, for once, was fine. Everything was fine, the way things were fine right before they weren't.

"Bottom's soft today," Rennie's voice crackled in his earpiece, thin and bubbly through the hardwire line. Rennie was working the bow section, forty yards off, and his voice always sounded like it was coming from inside a jug. "You getting anything?"

"Structure's intact," Theo said. "Going in through the cargo hatch."

"Copy. Watch your line."

The hatch was a square of deeper dark in the already-dark hull, and Theo slipped through it the way he slipped through most things — sideways, patient, with his shoulders turned to the world. Inside, the *Meridian Star* was a cathedral of drowned equipment. Survey vessels carried expensive instruments: echo sounders, gyrocompasses, chart tables, hydrographic recorders, the whole apparatus of a company paid to know a river better than the river knew itself. Meridian Consolidated wanted all of it back. Not for the value — most of it was saltwater-ruined, or in this case freshwater-ruined, which amounted to the same thing — but because the navy's salvage order had specified *recovery of all proprietary instrumentation*, and when a mining company spent that kind of money on lawyers, it wanted its money's worth of lawyers.

Theo's lamp swept the chart room. The table was bolted down and still level, a small miracle of the wreck's gentle settling. Drawers hung open like tongues. He started with the recorder cabinets, working methodically, unbolting the units and sliding them into the mesh lift bag clipped to his harness. Each instrument went in wrapped in its own cable, the way he'd been taught — treat the gear like a body, respectfully, because somebody upstream was going to want to know exactly how it died.

That was the joke he'd made to Rennie on the first day, and Rennie hadn't laughed. Rennie had looked at him with that flat river look of his and said, *Everything on this boat died, Theo. Question is what else did.*

He was on the third cabinet when he saw the holes.

The first one was in the bulkhead at shoulder height, a neat puncture the size of a fist, its edges bent *outward*. He put his glove against it and felt the torn metal, the petals of steel flared into the corridor like a blown kiss. Water pressure doesn't work that way. Water pressure pushes in. Every hull breach Theo had ever catalogued in eleven years of salvage — and he'd catalogued plenty, groundings, collisions, the slow unzipping of old welds — every one of them opened inward, crushed by the weight of the river wanting in.

These opened out.

He found the second one two compartments aft, low near the deck plate, same size, same flare. The third was in the engine room, punched through the fuel tank's casing, and beside it lay a wrench — a long-handled one, the kind with a hammer face on the back — snagged under a pipe clamp as if someone had dropped it in the dark and been in too much of a hurry to look for it.

Theo hung there in the engine room with his own heartbeat loud in his ears, louder than his regulator, and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do.

Ships sink. That was the whole business. Ships sink and men like Theo go down and take them apart, and nobody asks hard questions because the questions were asked by the insurance men and the coast guards and they'd all signed their papers. But a ship that sinks herself — a ship with holes punched from the *inside*, in a calm delta channel, with a wrench left behind like a signature — that wasn't a sinking. That was a burial. Somebody had gone through this vessel compartment by compartment with a hammer and put her down on purpose, quietly, in water deep enough and muddy enough that nobody would ever want to look too closely at what she was carrying.

And then the navy had sealed the channel, and Meridian Consolidated had hired Theo Marchetti to go down and bring up every instrument that might say why.

He stayed down there longer than the job required. He photographed the breaches with the wrist camera he wasn't supposed to be using — the salvage contract said *documentation for recovery purposes only*, and there was a clause about photographs, he was almost sure there was, though he'd signed fast because the pay was fast. The flash strobed green in the flooded corridor, and each picture felt less like documentation and more like testimony.

On the way out he paused at the chart table. One drawer was still half-shut, jammed, and inside it lay a single chart, curled and swollen with water, the ink mostly run but the grid lines stubborn. He didn't take it. He told himself that was because taking it was theft and he wasn't a thief. He told himself a lot of things down there in the dark, and the river didn't argue, which was one of the things he liked about it.

* * *

The surface of the delta at dusk was a different country from the one below. The water went bronze and then bruise-purple, and the birds came in off the reed flats in long ragged lines, and the crane barge — a rusted flat-deck relic called the *Percy B*, which had been hauling salvage out of southern rivers since before Theo was born — sat on her spuds with her floodlights just coming on, throwing a yellow island into all that gathering dark.

Theo came up the ladder and stood dripping on the deck while Rennie hosed the silt off the lift bags. The instruments went into a locked cage amidships, because the contract said so, and the lock was a good one, because the contract said so, and Theo had stopped believing in locks around the time he'd found the third hole in the hull.

Dutch Kessler was waiting by the cage with two cups of coffee and the expression he wore when he was about to say something he'd already rehearsed. Dutch ran the crew — three divers, a tender, a cook who doubled as deckhand — and he was sixty if he was a day, with a face like a dried riverbank and a way of talking that made every sentence sound like it had been approved by a lawyer first.

"Good haul," Dutch said, handing over a cup.

"Good haul," Theo agreed.

"Four units out of the chart room. Rennie says the bow gear's coming up tomorrow."

"That's the plan."

Dutch sipped his coffee and looked out at the water, at the place where the *Meridian Star* lay under all that pretty purple light, invisible, unnamed. "Anything down there I should know about?"

There it was. Not *how'd it look*, not *any complications* — *anything I should know about.* Theo had worked for Dutch three seasons and had never once heard him ask that question, because Dutch had spent thirty years perfecting the art of not needing to know things. Not needing to know was Dutch's whole religion. He tithed to it regularly.

Theo considered his answer. He was a careful man by trade — careless men don't last in black water — and careful men know that the first lie is the cheapest and the most expensive thing you'll ever buy.

"Hull's clean," he said. "Silt's the only problem. She'll give up the rest of the gear in two days, maybe three."

Dutch nodded, slow, and Theo watched him decide to accept it, and watched him know, at the same time, that it wasn't true. That was the strange thing about Dutch. He wasn't a stupid man and he wasn't a blind one, and whatever he suspected — and he suspected plenty, you didn't run a crew on a sealed navy channel without smelling something — he had decided, somewhere between the contract and tonight, that his name was going to stay clean of it. The job paid well. The job paid *four times* well. And Dutch Kessler had buried a wife and a boat and a good many years in these delta towns, and he had learned the local theology: the river forgives what it's paid to forgive.

"About the log," Dutch said.

Theo's cup paused halfway to his mouth.

"Standard practice, you know that. Dive conditions, bottom composition, recoveries, times in and out." Dutch's eyes stayed on the water. "That's what goes in the log. Conditions, composition, recoveries, times. You write it neat, I countersign it, the company's rep signs it Thursday when he flies in, and everybody goes home paid."

"Sounds like a log," Theo said.

"It is a log." Now Dutch looked at him, and there was something in the old man's face that wasn't hardness, exactly. It was more like a man standing at the edge of a hole in the ice and telling you, without saying it, that he'd once gone through. "Theo. Eleven weeks ago a survey boat goes down in a channel with no rocks in it, on a calm morning, and the navy closes thirty miles of river to do it. You think anybody in this business believes the navy's version? I've heard three versions. None of them agree. None of them *can* agree, that's how you know they're all lies." He drank his coffee. "But here's the other thing I know. The last man who asked Meridian Consolidated a question out loud was a fisherman up at Teller's Bend, and his boat burned at the dock, and his insurance said electrical, and he's selling bait now out of a shack and doesn't talk about it. That's the whole story. That's the *whole* story, and it's a short one, and I'd like to keep my story short too."

"So I log the weather."

"You log the weather. You log the recoveries. You log your bottom times." Dutch set his empty cup on the rail with a small precise click. "You don't log holes."

The word landed between them like a dropped tool.

Theo didn't confirm it. Dutch didn't ask him to. That was the arrangement, and they both stood there in the floodlight knowing it, two men on a steel island in a drowned crime scene, drinking coffee.

"Get some sleep," Dutch said, and walked aft toward the wheelhouse, and the deck seemed to rock a little after he'd gone, though the water was flat.

* * *

Theo didn't sleep. He sat in the crew fo'c'sle with the diesel generator thudding through the bulkhead and turned the day over the way he'd turn a recovered instrument — looking for the seam, the place where it came apart.

The pay was real. That was the trap of it, and he was honest enough with himself to see the trap's teeth. Four times rate, sixty days guaranteed, and the *Percy B*'s engine bills paid off, and his own debt to the outfitting yard in Corrick's Landing cleared with the first draw. He was thirty-four years old and he owned a skiff, a dive kit, and a duffel bag, and Meridian Consolidated had offered him more money in one contract than he'd made in the three years before it. Men had sold their souls for less. Men sold them every day for *exactly* that.

But the holes had opened outward. That was the fact sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone, and no amount of money moved it. Somebody had been inside this ship in her last hour, in the dark, swinging a wrench, and they had not been in a hurry to leave — the breaches were spaced, methodical, a workman's spacing — and then they had left, and the ship had gone down, and however many men had been aboard had gone down with her or had somehow, impossibly, not been aboard at all. The navy's report, the one page of it that had leaked into the delta towns, said *foundering, cause undetermined, all hands lost, three.* Three men. Theo had stood in that chart room and looked at the jammed drawer and thought: three men, and one of them was still fighting with a drawer when the water came. Or one of them had been trying to *hide* something in a drawer when the water came.

He got up, quietly, and went out on deck.

The delta at night was a black mirror with a few stars stitched into it. Somewhere across the flats a frog chorus ran like a loose bearing. The salvage cage sat amidships under its tarp and its good lock, full of drowned instruments that knew the truth about the last eleven minutes of the *Meridian Star*, and Theo stood over it for a long time with his hands in his pockets, feeling the shape of the choice he'd already made without admitting it.

He wasn't going to quit. He knew that much. Quitting meant walking away from the pay and the debt and the questions both, and the questions would still be there, sitting in the river, patient as the river. He wasn't going to report it either — not yet, not to anyone, because Dutch's story about the fisherman at Teller's Bend was sitting right beside the stone in his chest, and Theo Marchetti had a healthy respect for burning boats.

But he was going to remember. Every breach, every measurement, every photograph on that wrist camera. He was going to log the weather and the bottom times and the recoveries, neat as a hymnbook, and underneath all of it he was going to keep a second log, the kind that doesn't get countersigned, the kind that lives in a man and waits.

"Can't sleep either?"

Rennie came out of the fo'c'sle rubbing his eyes, a big slow-moving man with forearms like dock pilings and a face that had never in his life hurried. He leaned on the rail next to Theo and spat into the water, a habit the river had never once objected to.

"Thinking about the job," Theo said.

"Everybody thinks about the job." Rennie squinted at the dark water. "You know what I did today, down at the bow, while you were playing in the chart room? I found the galley. Little room, aft of the mess. Table was set." He said it flatly, but his hand on the rail had gone still. "Three plates. Cups. A pot of something on the burner, still sitting on it. They were eating, Theo. Whatever happened to that boat happened while they were eating breakfast."

The frog chorus ran on across the flats. Theo looked at the black water and saw, again, the flare of torn steel bending the wrong way, out, out, out.

"Log the weather," he said quietly.

"Yeah," Rennie said. "Log the weather." But he said it the way men say a prayer they've stopped believing in, and then he went below, and Theo stayed on deck a while longer, alone with the cage and the current.

It was two nights later — two more dives, four more instruments, two more neat pages in the log — when the launch came up the channel flying no colors and carrying one passenger, a small man in a canvas coat who said he was a prospector and asked, in a voice like dry leaves, whether anybody on the *Percy B* ran passengers upriver. And that, though Theo couldn't have known it yet, was the river starting to hand him the rest of the story.
