## 2. A Promise at Gunpoint of Time

The delta towns all smelled the same after dark — diesel and brackish water and fried something, the grease of a hundred kitchens hanging in the air like weather. Port Anselm was no different from the others Theo had worked out of in eleven years of salvage diving, except that it was smaller, meaner, and closer to the navy cordon, which meant it was full of men who had been told *no* and were looking for ways around it.

He'd come ashore for three things: diesel for the compressor, a new cutting torch tip, and a night's sleep on something that didn't rock. He got the first two by sundown. The third was already going wrong by nine o'clock, because Rennie had a talent for finding trouble the way diviners found water, and tonight his trouble had a name and a barstool at the Saltmarsh Room.

"You want to hear something funny?" Rennie said, sliding onto the bench across from him with two glasses of something amber and illegal. "There's an old boy upstairs in the Delmar House who's been telling anybody who'll buy him a drink that he knows where the gold actually is. Not where Meridian says it is. Where it *actually* is."

Theo turned his glass a quarter-turn on the scarred wood. "Meridian's concession is surveyed. Staked. Filed with the territorial office."

"Sure it is." Rennie grinned. He was thirty-two and had the kind of face that would stay boyish until it suddenly wasn't, and he believed, with the unshakable faith of a man who had never lost anything he couldn't replace, that the world rewarded curiosity. "And I'm saying there's a half-dead prospector on the second floor of the Delmar who says the survey's crooked. Says it loud, too. Loud enough that somebody shot him for it, if you ask me."

"Nobody asked you."

"Somebody always asks me eventually." Rennie drank. "Come look at him. He keeps asking for a diver. Said it twice to the landlady before she stopped going up."

"A diver." Theo let that sit. Downriver, forty fathoms down in green-black water, the survey vessel *Kestrel* lay on her side with her instruments stripped into his salvage lockers, and every hour he spent ashore was an hour the navy's permit clock ticked against him. He had no business with dying prospectors. He had a manifest to falsify quietly and a crew boss named Halloway who had told him, in words so mild they'd taken a moment to register as a threat, to log nothing unusual about the hull.

He should have finished his drink and gone to bed.

Instead he heard himself say, "What's his name?"

* * *

The Delmar House was three stories of salt-rotted clapboard leaning against its own history, the kind of place where the rooms rented by the week and the week was often paid in stories. The stairwell smelled of kerosene and cat. The landlady, a broad woman with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair, looked Theo over — the rope-scarred hands, the salt-stiff canvas jacket — and jerked her thumb at the stairs without being asked.

"Room nine. He's been asking for a diver since Tuesday." She lowered her voice as though the walls were customers too. "I sent for a doctor Wednesday. Doctor came Thursday, looked at him, left without taking off his coat. That tell you anything?"

"It tells me the doctor knows who did the shooting."

"It tells me the doctor has a family," she said, and went back to her ledger.

Room nine was at the end of the hall, under a bulb wrapped in wire cage. The door was unlocked. Theo knocked anyway, out of habit more than manners, and when nothing answered he pushed it open onto air gone thick with fever and old blood.

The man in the bed was a ruin. Elias Voss — Rennie had gotten the name out of the landlady between drinks — must have been sixty, maybe seventy, but the thing in the bed looked older than geology. He was gaunt as a winter branch, skin the color of tallow, one arm bandaged in strips of bedsheet gone rust-brown at the shoulder where the bullet had gone through and through. His breathing came in long, tearing drags, like a man hauling on a rope that kept paying out.

His eyes found Theo in the doorway and fixed on him with a sudden terrible clarity, the way drowning men are said to see clearly just once.

"You're a diver," Voss said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "I can tell. The hands. The way you stand, like the floor might not hold."

"I'm passing through."

"Nobody passes through Port Anselm. They get stuck here, or they're hiding here, or they're hunting here." A cough took him then, deep and wet, and bent him nearly double, and Theo found himself crossing the room without deciding to, holding a tin cup of water to lips that shook. Voss drank like it was a debt he intended to repay. "Which one are you?"

"Hiding, maybe. Working, mostly."

"The wreck." Voss's eyes didn't leave him. "You're working the wreck. The survey boat the navy sank and then pretended they didn't."

Theo set the cup down carefully, because his hands wanted to do something else. "That's a sealed site. Salvage permits don't exist for it. So whatever you think you know—"

"I know what I staked." It came out flat and certain, and it stopped Theo's mouth. "Thirty-one years I worked this river. Thirty-one years, and eight years ago I found it. The headwater spring. Gold in the gravels below it, coarse as birdshot, running a seam you could follow with your boot toe. I filed my claim. I filed it legal, with the territorial office in Corvane, with my own hand-drawn coordinates because I couldn't afford a licensed surveyor." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; Theo watched the pain move through him like weather. "Couldn't afford one. Remember that. It matters later."

"I don't know what any of this has to do with me."

"Everything." Voss's hand came out from under the blanket — trembling, yellow-nailed, absurdly strong when it closed on Theo's wrist. "You're the first man in four days who isn't afraid to be in this room. That makes you either brave or stupid, and both will do."

Outside, somewhere down the street, a door slammed. Both of them flinched. Voss's grip tightened.

"They've come twice already," he said, quieter now. "Two men, nice coats, city boots. First time they asked polite questions through the door. Second time they didn't knock. I was coming back from the privy and saw them on the stairs, so I went out the window and they caught me in the alley. One bullet. Sloppy work, or maybe they wanted me slow instead of dead. Dead men can't be made to sign things." His breath whistled. "They want my claim signature. My mark on a transfer. Then whatever happens to me happens clean."

Theo looked at the bandaged shoulder, at the sheets stained brown, at the window with its warped shutter standing open a hand's width to the night. He thought about holes punched from the inside of a hull, and a crew boss's soft voice saying *log nothing unusual*, and how complicity arrived not as a decision but as a series of small refusals to look.

"What do you want from me?" he asked, and hated how much it sounded like surrender.

Voss reached beneath the mattress.

Theo's whole body went tight — he'd seen enough riverside violence to know what lived under mattresses — but what came out was small and flat and wrapped in oilskin, tied with tarred twine, the package of a man who expected everything he owned to get wet someday. Voss laid it on the blanket between them and rested his hand on it like a man resting his hand on a child's head.

"My map," he said. "Drawn it myself, from eight years of walking that country. Every channel, every deadfall, every sandbar that moves in flood season. And the source. The true coordinates, to the minute of arc, checked against the stars because I didn't trust my chain measurements and I was right not to trust them." His eyes were burning now, fever-bright, and Theo understood with a cold settling feeling that the fever was doing what fevers do — stripping away everything but the load-bearing beams of the man. "Meridian Consolidated filed their resurvey last spring. Their coordinates put the seam two miles south of where it lies. Two miles, onto land they'd already optioned from the territory. My claim sits on empty ground now, according to the record. Their dredges are sitting on mine."

"That's—" Theo started, and stopped, because the word was *impossible*, and it wasn't. He had held the *Kestrel*'s survey logs in his hands. He had noticed, without letting himself notice, that the sounding charts in the chart room had been replaced with newer ones whose ink was fresher than the voyage dates allowed. He had logged nothing unusual.

"You've seen the papers," Voss said, watching his face. "Haven't you. You've seen something."

"I strip wrecks," Theo said. "I don't read them."

"Then read this." Voss pushed the oilskin package toward him and immediately began to cough again, harder, and this time there was blood in it, bright threads in the spittle, and Theo understood that the conversation was happening on borrowed time and both of them knew it.

When Voss could speak again, he spoke fast, hoarding words like a man loading a boat ahead of a storm.

"I have a daughter." Each word cost him. "Mara. Mara Voss. Her mother took her to Corvane when she was six, and I let them go because I thought the river would still be here when I was ready, and then the years ate me alive the way they do." His hand found the oilskin again, gripped it. "She'll be hard to find. She changes her name, maybe. Her mother was good at disappearing. But she's in the concession country — I hired a man to trace her last year, before I knew I was dying, and he found her living upriver. In the company town. Working, God forgive me, for them."

"For Meridian?"

"For Meridian." Something moved across the ruined face — grief, or shame, or the terrible arithmetic of fathers. "I don't know what she is to them. Clerk. Surveyor. I don't care. What I care about is that this map doesn't die in this room, and it doesn't end up in Crane's safe, and it doesn't rot in some evidence locker while lawyers argue about whether paper is paper. It goes to her. To my blood. Whatever she does with it after that is hers to carry, same as it was mine."

"Crane," Theo said. "Aldous Crane?"

"You know him." It wasn't a question. Voss read the answer in Theo's silence and nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis. "Then you know what I'm asking you to swim through."

Down the hall, the caged bulb buzzed. Somewhere below, the landlady's pen scratched. Theo became aware that he was holding the oilskin package — that at some point in the last minute Voss had pressed it into his hands and closed the diver's fingers over it, and the tarred twine was biting into his palm, and the thing weighed almost nothing and felt like it weighed everything.

"Take it back," Theo said. "Get a lawyer. Get a notary. There are legal—"

"Lawyers cost money I buried in the ground eight years ago trying to prove my own claim." Voss's voice cracked and steadied. "Notaries get bought. Judges in this territory eat from Meridian's hand. I've watched it happen to better men than me for thirty years, Mr. Diver, and I am done watching." He sagged back against the headboard, spent, and for a moment only the fever-breath filled the room. When he spoke again it was barely above a whisper. "I'm not asking you to fight them. I'm asking you to carry. There's a difference. Carrying is all I ever did — carried the pan, the chain, the tent, the hope. Somebody else can swing the hammer."

"Why me?" Theo asked, and it was the honest question, the one underneath all the others. "You don't know me. I could sell this map by morning. I probably should."

Something almost like humor surfaced in the old man's eyes. "Because you knocked," he said. "Four days of people coming to this room — the doctor who wouldn't take off his coat, the landlady's nephew who wanted to buy the story for a bottle, the two in the nice coats — and you're the first one who knocked." He coughed, wet and long. "A man who knocks first is a man who still thinks doors belong to other people. That's worth something. That's worth everything, right now."

Theo looked down at the oilskin. Through the wrapping he could feel the stiff grain of paper, folded many times, and something harder beneath it — a compass, maybe, or a tin of matches, the small ironies a man packs for his own funeral.

"If I take this," he said slowly, "and I find her, and she takes one look at it and throws it in the stove — then what? I've hauled your ghost two hundred miles upriver for nothing."

"Then you've hauled it for nothing," Voss said simply. "That's what a promise is. It's not a contract. Nobody enforces it. That's the whole point of it." His hand rose, shaking, and touched the back of Theo's wrist, light as a moth. "Say it. Say you'll take it to Mara Voss. Say it out loud, because I need to hear it in a living voice, and then I can stop holding on. Do you understand? I have been holding on *for this*. Nothing else. The bullet I could have died from Tuesday. The fever I could have died from yesterday. I am holding this river back with my teeth, and I am so tired, and I just need to hear it said."

The lamp buzzed. The river breathed somewhere beyond the walls, vast and indifferent, carrying its silt and its secrets down to the sea.

Theo Marchetti had made exactly two promises in his adult life. He'd kept the first and been ruined by keeping it, and he'd broken the second and been ruined by breaking it, and he had concluded from these experiments that promises were like anchors — useful only in the specific circumstances that made them fatal. He opened his mouth to explain this.

What came out was: "I'll take it to Mara Voss. Your daughter. I give you my word."

Elias Voss exhaled — a long, shuddering release, thirty years of river going out of him at once — and smiled, and the smile transformed the ruin of his face into something almost young.

"There," he whispered. "Now I can sink."

He was asleep within minutes, or unconscious — with fever there was no telling the difference, and Theo stood a long time in the caged lamplight watching the torn breathing and telling himself it was the same thing. Then he tucked the blanket higher over the bandaged shoulder, a useless gesture, the gesture of a man arranging deck chairs, and picked up the oilskin package, and left.

The landlady looked up from her ledger as he passed the desk. "You'll come back?" she asked. Not *is he dying*. She'd already decided that. Just: *you'll come back.*

"At first light," Theo said, and was surprised to find he meant it.

* * *

Outside, Port Anselm had gone soft and blue with fog off the delta, the lamps haloed, the water invisible and everywhere. Theo walked toward the docks with the package inside his jacket, against his chest, and told himself the weight he felt was imagination.

At the corner by the chandlery, two men stood talking under the eaves. Nice coats. City boots, wrong for the mud. They weren't looking at him. They were looking, with the patient attention of men being paid by the day, at the second-floor window of the Delmar House — at the warped shutter standing open a hand's width to the night.

Theo kept walking, unhurried, a tired diver heading for his bunk. But his hand stayed flat against his chest the whole way down to the water, over the little oilskin weight of another man's life, and by the time he reached the dock he had already begun, without quite admitting it, to plan a route upriver.
