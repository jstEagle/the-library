## 3. What the Instruments Recorded

The rain came in off the delta at dusk the way it always did that time of year — not falling so much as arriving, a gray curtain drawn across the water, swallowing the far shore and the masts of the salvage barges with it. Theo Marchetti sat in the wheelhouse of the *Magpie* with the salvaged instruments spread across the chart table like organs removed from a body he'd been paid to gut.

He had been avoiding them for two days.

That was the truth of it, though he'd have dressed it up differently if Rennie had asked him straight. The instruments sat in their crates in the hold, wrapped in oilcloth and bubble film, tagged and logged exactly as the contract specified — *hydrographic survey suite, one lot, condition fair* — and every hour they sat there they hummed at him. Not literally. He wasn't a superstitious man; thirty years underwater taught you superstition was just fear wearing a diver's face mask. But there was a weight to them, the way there's a weight to a sealed room you know somebody died in.

The *Meridian Surveyor* had gone down in eleven fathoms of brown water at the delta's throat, where the river braided itself into a dozen channels before giving up and becoming sea. Official story: grounding during a storm surge, hull opened on a bar, crew evacuated with minor injuries. Insurance settled. Navy sealed the site for eight months — environmental assessment, the paperwork said — then quietly let Meridian Consolidated's salvage contract float loose on the open market, and Theo's bid had been the lowest and the fastest and the least inclined to ask questions.

He was asking questions now.

He poured himself the last of the coffee from the thermos and pulled the first crate toward him. Outside, the rain worked at the deck plates, and somewhere aft Rennie was arguing with the winch hydraulics, his voice rising and falling in the familiar rhythm of a man losing gracefully to machinery.

The instruments were beautiful, in the way of expensive things built for a purpose. A multibeam echo sounder, its transducer array cracked but intact. A gyrocompass, its gimbals still swinging lazily from the motion of the barge. Two GPS receivers, a sound velocity profiler, and — the piece Theo kept coming back to — the data logger itself, a ruggedized black box the size of a bread loaf, its casing dented where it had been torn free of the bulkhead mount.

Salvage law was simple: instruments were cargo, cargo was value, value went to the salvor. Nobody wanted the data. Data was liability. The contract said explicitly — he'd read it four times since the night on the flophouse floor — *all recorded media to be surrendered or destroyed at owner's discretion.*

Owner's discretion. Aldous Crane's discretion, though the name hadn't meant anything to Theo when he signed.

He found the access panel and looked at it for a long moment. Then he got out his smallest screwdriver, the one he used for working inside flooded camera housings, and opened the box.

* * *

Three years ago, off the coast of Baja, Theo had done a job for a marine archaeologist named Dr. Okonkwo — a woman who paid badly and talked endlessly and taught him more about reading wrecks than twenty years of commercial work ever had. She'd had a saying: *the ship tells you how it died, if you let it.* Most salvage divers didn't want to hear how a ship died. They wanted the brass, the copper, the propeller bronze. But Okonkwo would hang in the water above a wreck for an hour, just looking, her light moving slow along the hull lines, and she'd say things like *see how the plating buckles inward here, outward there — she hit stern-first, going backward, someone was trying to reverse off the bar,* and Theo would feel the wreck rearrange itself in his mind from a pile of junk into a story.

He'd thought of her down there, three weeks ago, hanging in the green-black murk beside the *Meridian Surveyor*'s hull while his cutting torch idled and the silt drifted past like snow falling upward.

Because the holes hadn't made sense.

A grounding opens a hull from the outside — plating torn, buckled, peeled back the way a can opener works, ragged edges pointing inward into the ship. What Theo had found were six penetrations along the starboard side below the waterline, each one clean-edged, each one punched *outward*. From inside. And around each one, on the interior framing he'd accessed through the engine room, the marks of a hydraulic ram — deliberate, methodical, spaced like a man following a checklist. Somebody had stood inside that hull in the dark and broken her open on purpose, and then somebody else had written *grounding* on the report.

He'd surfaced with the thought and said nothing. His crew boss, Dario Ferro, a fat comfortable man who'd been running delta salvage since before Theo could tie a bowline, had met him at the ladder with a towel and a look.

"Log says grounding," Dario had said, not quite a question.

"Log says grounding," Theo had agreed.

"Then log says grounding." Dario had squeezed his shoulder once, hard, the way you'd grip a man to keep him from walking somewhere stupid. "We're being paid to strip metal, Theo. Not to write history."

Now, in the wheelhouse with the rain coming down and the black box open under his hands, Theo understood that he had been writing history after all. Just not his own.

The logger's memory module was a sealed cartridge, military-spec, rated for saltwater immersion. It had never flooded. He connected it to the field laptop he used for sonar processing — a violation of the contract so casual it barely registered — and waited while the directory tree unspooled across the screen.

Survey files. Hundreds of them, dated across fourteen months. Bathymetric runs up every channel of the delta and fifty miles of the main stem. Sediment cores. Magnetometer sweeps. And in a folder marked with a project code — MC-771 — a set of files labeled *FINAL POSITIONING — CONCESSION STAKEOUT.*

Theo opened them without knowing what he expected to find.

What he found was numbers. Columns of coordinates, eastings and northings, the dry language of land surveying translated into water. He almost closed the file. Then his eye caught on something — a discrepancy between two columns that should have been identical. One column headed *RAW OBSERVED*, one headed *ADJUSTED FINAL*. The adjusted figures differed from the raw by a consistent offset: roughly 1.4 kilometers south-southwest, applied uniformly across every stakeout point in the concession.

Shifted. Every coordinate in the final survey had been shifted the same distance in the same direction.

He sat back. Rain drummed. Rennie swore at the winch.

A shifted survey wasn't an error. Errors scattered; they wandered; they had texture. This was translation — the whole map picked up and moved, like a card sharp sliding the deck. Any claim staked on those adjusted coordinates would sit 1.4 kilometers from where the instruments had actually measured. Whoever held the paper held ground that wasn't the ground. And whoever's claim lay at the true coordinates — the raw ones — would find their legal position describing empty swamp while the real source sat inside Meridian's boundary line.

It was elegant. It was the kind of fraud you could only commit with a survey vessel, because only a survey vessel generated both sets of numbers, and only the people holding the logger could choose which set became the world.

Theo reached into his jacket pocket and took out the oilskin packet.

* * *

Elias Voss had weighed nothing. That was what Theo remembered most — not the smell of the flophouse room, not the pistol-gray color of the old man's lips, not even the gunmetal gleam of the wound dressing some amateur had taped over his belly. It was the weight. A prospector who'd spent forty years hauling gear up rivers should have been dense as driftwood, and instead he'd been hollowed out, a man already half-ghost, kept upright by fever and fury and nothing else.

"You're the salvage man," Voss had said. Not a question either. Everyone in that part of town knew the salvage crews; everyone knew which boats ran which channels. "The Marchetti boy. Your father ran the *Cormorant*."

"He did."

"Good boat. Honest skipper." Voss had coughed then, a wet terrible sound, and pressed the oilskin packet against Theo's chest with a hand that shook like a compass needle near iron. "There's a girl. Mara. My daughter. She's in the concession town — works for them, I know it, don't care. You take this to her. You put it in her hands. Not the company's. Not the navy's. Hers."

"What is it?"

"A map." Voss's eyes had burned out of the ruin of his face. "My map. Forty years of my life, and they shot me for it in a doorway like a dog. You swear. Swear on your father's boat."

And Theo — tired, uneasy, wanting only to get back to the *Magpie* before the tide turned — had sworn.

Now he laid the oilskin flat on the chart table beside the laptop and unwrapped it. Inside was a hand-drawn chart on waterproofed linen, ink faded to the brown of old tea, annotated in a cramped engineer's hand. It showed the headwater country above the canyon — country no official chart bothered with, because no concession reached that far and the navy didn't patrol above the falls. A spring-fed pool, circled twice. A network of tributaries. And in the margin, coordinates. Six pairs of figures, precise to the second, the kind of numbers a man writes when he has checked them a hundred times and staked his life on their being right.

Theo copied the first pair into the laptop's coordinate converter and felt his pulse change before his mind caught up.

Voss's coordinates matched the *raw observed* column. Exactly. To the second.

The old man's map described the true positions — the positions the *Meridian Surveyor*'s own instruments had recorded before someone slid the whole world 1.4 kilometers to cover the theft. The concession papers, the permits, the boundaries Meridian had defended with lawyers and, apparently, with bullets, all rested on falsified data. And the proof of it sat in two places in the world: in a dying prospector's hand-drawn linen, and in a black box Theo had been contractually obligated to destroy.

He thought of the holes punched outward through the hull. Of Dario's hand squeezing his shoulder. Of the eight months the navy had spent "assessing the environment" over a wreck whose real secret was in her logs. Of the insurance settlement, clean and quick, that had paid Meridian for the loss of the very instrument that could undo them.

They hadn't sunk her on the bar. They'd scuttled her — and then, when the navy seal expired and the hulk had to be disposed of, they'd hired a salvage crew to make her disappear entirely, plate by plate, until nothing remained that anyone could examine. Theo wasn't the salvor of the *Meridian Surveyor*. He was the eraser.

The map wasn't treasure. He'd carried it for two days telling himself it might be — gold claims, placer deposits, a strike worth killing for, and hadn't men killed for less? But gold was the smallest part of it. The map was testimony. It was Elias Voss's affidavit written in ink and linen, corroborated by the victim's own instruments, and Theo was holding both halves of a crime that had murdered at least one man and possibly a ship's worth of consequences besides.

Outside, the rain thickened. The navigation lights of a passing barge smeared yellow through the wheelhouse glass and slid away downstream. Theo became aware, gradually, of how exposed the *Magpie* was — tied up at a commercial dock, her holds full of Meridian's instruments, her crew boss on Meridian's payroll history, her master holding a document that could cost Meridian everything.

He wrapped the oilskin carefully and put it back inside his jacket, against his skin, where he'd been carrying it since the flophouse. Then he copied the entire contents of the logger onto two drives — one into the false bottom of his tool chest, one into a waterproof canister he'd once used for blasting caps — and wiped the laptop's connection log.

Rennie stuck his head in, dripping, grinning, smelling of diesel and defeat. "Winch won. Again. You eating?"

"In a minute."

"You look like a man doing arithmetic," Rennie said, and laughed, and shook the rain out of his hair, and went below.

Theo watched him go. Twenty-six years old, asked too many questions, laughed too easily. Theo thought of the doorway where Elias Voss had been shot, and of the phrase *owner's discretion*, and of how many people knew what was in the black box now — Dario, who'd want plausible deniability; Rennie, who knew nothing yet; and whoever at Meridian counted the crates when the salvage was delivered and understood that a data logger's memory cartridge, unlike gold, could be traced.

The arithmetic was simple and ugly. The moment the *Magpie* delivered her cargo, the clock started. Either Crane's people assumed the logger was dead — drowned, corrupted, wiped by the sinking — or they didn't, and a man who'd seen the inside of it became a loose end in a business that tidied up.

Theo looked at the chart table, at the ghost of Voss's coordinates still glowing faintly on the screen saver, and made the decision the way he did everything: slowly, all at once.

He'd run the river. Upstream, to the concession town, to Mara Voss, before his permit window closed and before anyone decided to check whether the logger had drowned with the ship.

He was reaching for the radio to call Dario when the wheelhouse door opened behind him and Dario himself filled it, rain streaming off his cap, and the look on the fat comfortable man's face was neither comfortable nor fat.

"Theo," Dario said quietly, closing the door. "There's a launch been sitting off the point since dark. Running no lights. Somebody's watching us."
