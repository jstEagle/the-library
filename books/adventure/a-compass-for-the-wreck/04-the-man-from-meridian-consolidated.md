## 4. The Man from Meridian Consolidated

The man arrived on a Tuesday, which Theo would remember later because Tuesdays were when the supply launch came up from Port Anselm with diesel and fresh bread and nothing else worth noting. This Tuesday it brought bread, diesel, and Aldous Crane.

Theo was on the *Magpie's* aft deck, hosing silt out of his dive suit, when he saw the launch put in at the company dock across the channel — not the public landing, but Meridian Consolidated's own timber pier, where the dredge crews moored and where men like Theo were not encouraged to tie up. A figure stepped off before the lines were even fastened, which told Theo something about how the man regarded boats, water, and the people who worked both: as inconveniences that ought to hold still for him.

He came down the dock road in a linen suit that had no business being within two hundred miles of a river delta. Pale gray, pressed sharp enough to draw blood, with a panama hat tilted at an angle that suggested he'd practiced the tilt. Behind him walked two of Meridian's security contractors in matching khakis, carrying themselves with the loose-shouldered swagger of men paid to be noticed.

"Marchetti." Crane said the name like he was tasting it. He stopped at the foot of the *Magpie's* gangplank without asking permission to board, hands clasped behind his back, surveying the salvage barge the way a man surveys a horse he intends to buy. "Aldous Crane. Meridian Consolidated. I hope I'm not interrupting your... housekeeping."

"You are." Theo shut off the hose. Water dripped from the suit hanging over the rail, darkening the deck in slow spots. He didn't offer a hand. He didn't smile. He had learned years ago, working wrecks off the coast where the insurance men circled like gulls, that the first man to smile lost leverage.

Crane smiled enough for both of them. It was a good smile, white and even, warm as a bank lobby. "I admire directness. Saves time." He produced a card from his breast pocket — heavy stock, embossed, absurd out here where everything was rust and mud — and held it between two fingers rather than handing it over, so that taking it became Theo's decision. "May I?"

"You're standing on my gangplank."

"I am." Crane stepped aboard uninvited anyway, and the two contractors followed as far as the rail and then stopped, flanking the plank like gateposts. On the deck, Crane removed his hat, revealing silver hair combed back with surgical precision, and looked around with frank appraisal. At the dive gear. At the open crate of recovered instruments lashed under canvas near the wheelhouse. At the chart table where Theo's logs lay weighted under a brass lamp.

His gaze lingered there perhaps half a second longer than anywhere else. Theo counted it.

"You've been productive," Crane said. "Three weeks on site and you've already stripped the survey deck. My colleagues in the recovery division are impressed. Efficiency is a virtue we reward at Meridian."

"The contract says instruments, fittings, and non-ferrous scrap. I've been efficient within the contract."

"Within the contract. Yes." Crane drifted along the rail, fingertips trailing the wood as though testing it for rot, and paused beside the crate. He didn't lift the canvas. That restraint was its own kind of message: *I already know what's under there.* "Here is my situation, Mr. Marchetti. The *Corvus Survey* was a tragic loss. Seven souls. The navy's inquiry has concluded, the families have been compensated, and the company wishes only to close this sad chapter and move forward with the concession work upstream. Closure requires tidiness." He turned, and the smile remained but the warmth drained out of it, leaving something architectural. "Tidiness means that whatever came off that wreck should stay consistent with the official record. Charts, logbooks, personal effects, curiosities of any kind. If your crew recovered items of a... documentary nature, I'm authorized to make that tidy for you. Triple your contract rate. Cash, today, no paperwork beyond a simple receipt describing, let us say, miscellaneous debris."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere across the channel a dredge coughed through its cycle, and a heron lifted off the reed bank with a sound like a sail filling.

*Curiosities.* Theo kept his face still, kept his breathing easy, and felt the word settle into him like a fishhook finding flesh. Three weeks ago he'd have said curiosity meant portholes and brass fittings. But he'd spent last night in the wheelhouse with the salvaged survey logs spread under the lamp, and Voss's oilskin map open beside them, and he knew now that the most valuable thing on the *Corvus* wasn't brass. It was paper. And this man in the linen suit had just told him, in the politest possible language, that the paper was worth triple anything.

Which meant someone above Aldous Crane was afraid of it.

"Documentary nature," Theo repeated. He scratched his jaw, buying a moment, letting a diver's dullness settle over his features like silt. "You mean the logbooks? Those went to the navy liaison with the first manifest. Signed receipt, both copies. You can check with Lieutenant—"

"I don't care about manifests." Crane said it lightly, almost fondly, a teacher correcting a promising student. "Manifests are for honest cargoes. I'm asking about what a careful man might have set aside. Divers find things. Things the record doesn't anticipate." He let that hang, then produced a slim envelope from inside his jacket and laid it on the chart table, weighting it with the brass lamp itself — a small presumption, deliberate, Theo was sure. "Consider it a finder's fee for tidiness. There would be more where that came from, and a standing retainer afterward, if you proved to be a discreet contractor. Meridian values discretion very highly. We pay for it accordingly."

Theo looked at the envelope. Thick. He could guess the denomination of the notes inside by the way it sat on the table, dense as a brick.

And beneath the thought of the money, quieter and colder, another thought surfaced: *he knows.* Not suspects — knows. Knows there was something on that wreck worth buying, which means someone read the same scuttle marks Theo found punched through the hull from the inside, or heard the same rumors, or — worse — has eyes closer than Theo wanted to believe. Rennie had asked too many questions at the fuel dock last week. Old Tam had been drinking and loud about the holes in the plating. Theo himself had logged nothing unusual, exactly as the crew boss instructed, but logging and knowing were different animals, and Crane was here because somebody understood the difference.

"Mr. Crane," Theo said slowly, "I'm a salvage man. I pull metal out of water and sell it by weight. Paper rots, ink runs, and it doesn't weigh enough to bother with. Whatever was in those offices drowned with the officers who kept them." He picked up the envelope, weighed it once in his palm — letting Crane see him consider it, because a refusal too quick would smell of fear — and set it back down on the table. "But I appreciate the offer. And if anything turns up, you'll be the first call."

It was a lie built like a well-laid mooring: every line attached, nothing visible. Crane studied him for a long three seconds, and Theo met the look with the flat patience of a man who had spent twenty years staring into green water waiting for silt to clear.

Then Crane laughed — a short, genuine-sounding bark — and reclaimed the envelope. "You know, I believe you might be exactly what your reputation says. That's rare, in my experience. Most men take the money and then become a problem anyway; the money teaches them their own price." He settled the panama back on his head and adjusted the tilt with one finger. "Keep diving, Mr. Marchetti. Finish the contract. Be efficient. And if the river gives you any curiosities—" he tapped the rail twice, a small intimate gesture, "—you know where to find me. Everyone does."

He went down the gangplank without looking back. The contractors fell in behind him, and the three of them recrossed the dock road toward the Meridian pier, where a black-hulled launch waited with its engines already turning over. Crane never hurried. Men who sent other people to hurry didn't need to.

Theo stood at the rail until the launch rounded the channel marker and vanished into the haze upstream. Then he went below, locked the wheelhouse door, and pulled the floor panel over the bilge access where he'd wrapped the oilskin map and the copied survey pages in a dive bag weighted with chain. Everything was there. Everything was dry. His hands weren't quite steady doing the check, and he noted that with clinical displeasure, the way he'd note a hairline crack in a tank fitting.

*Triple pay,* he thought. *For curiosities.*

Companies didn't send men in linen suits to buy garbage.

* * *

That night the river fog came in thick off the delta flats, beading on the rigging, swallowing the channel markers, pressing against the wheelhouse glass like something breathing. Theo cooked rice and salt fish in the galley while Rennie played dominoes with old Tam and lost badly and complained about it loudly, and for two hours the *Magpie* felt like what she was supposed to be: a working boat full of tired men, nothing more.

Rennie went ashore around ten to see about a girl in the stilt village, and Tam turned in early with the bottle he pretended nobody knew about. Theo stayed up. He told himself it was to finish reconciling the manifest, and he did some of that, but mostly he sat with the lamp low and listened to the fog drip and thought about the weight of an envelope thick enough to sink.

He was almost asleep in the watch chair when he heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. An absence. The night herons that roosted on the *Magpie's* davits had gone quiet, all at once, the way birds do when a shape passes over. Then — soft, rhythmic, muffled by fog — the knock of a hull kissing a hull. Someone alongside.

Theo killed the lamp without moving, slid off the chair, and crossed the deck plates on the balls of his feet. Through the wheelhouse window he made out a skiff riding low against the *Magpie's* starboard quarter, two shapes in it, and one of the shapes already over the rail and onto his deck, moving with a crouched economy that said *this isn't the first boat we've boarded.*

He should have gone out. Part of him — the part that had stared down crocodiles in the shallows and cut away fouled lines at sixty feet — wanted to go out with the boathook and settle it man to man. But he'd stood across a chart table from Aldous Crane that afternoon, and he'd understood that this was not a fight conducted with boathooks. These were accountants' hands. They did their arithmetic quietly, at night, and left no bodies where bodies raised questions.

So Theo watched.

For forty minutes they worked his boat. He tracked them by sound: the wheelhouse drawer sliding, the chart table lid lifting, the forecastle hatch, the lazarette, the engine room. Methodical. Patient. Once a flashlight beam swept the wheelhouse glass in a thin blade and passed within a hand's width of his face, and Theo stood motionless in the dark behind the door and breathed shallow and thought about nothing at all, the way he'd learned to during long decompression stops when panic was a luxury billed at compound interest.

They found the bilge panel. He heard the screws come out — he'd left them finger-tight on purpose, a decoy since the afternoon Crane left — and heard the dive bag come up, heard the chain shift, heard a low exchange of words too muffled to parse. Then a longer silence. Then the bag going back down, the screws going back in, clumsy in the dark, cross-threaded — he'd feel that tomorrow and say nothing about it.

The skiff withdrew into the fog. The herons came back to the davits one at a time, shuffling, resettling.

Theo waited until the gray edge of dawn before he opened the panel. The map was there. The copies were there. Nothing taken, nothing added that he could find — though he turned every page against the light looking for a hair, a mark, a substitution, and found none. They hadn't been searching to steal. Stealing would prove he had something worth stealing. They'd been searching to *confirm*. To learn whether the quiet salvage contractor was holding out, and where he kept it, and how hard it would be to reach.

Now they knew. Now Crane knew that Theo had lied to his face, and knew exactly which floorboard the lie slept under.

Tam found him on the aft deck at first light, coffee in hand, watching the fog burn off the channel. The old man took one look at Theo's face and lowered himself onto the coil of line beside him with the groan of bad knees.

"They went through her," Tam said. Not a question.

"Last night. Two of them. You hear anything?"

"Heard the boarders' fenders squeak around midnight. Figured it was you checking lines." Tam drank his coffee and looked out at the water where the mist was peeling back from the far bank, exposing the green wall of the delta forest, the dredge towers, the thin thread of the river running north toward places the permits didn't cover. "In my day," the old man said, "when a company searched your boat, it meant they'd decided you were either for sale or for sinking. And they'd already priced both."

Theo turned the coffee mug in his hands. Across the channel, the Meridian pier stood empty in the early light, neat as a drawn ledger. Somewhere upstream, past the navy cordon, past the falls country, a dying prospector's daughter was living under a name that authenticated stolen charts, and a folded oilskin map in a bilge locker was the only true survey of that whole river.

He had told Crane the truth about one thing: he was a salvage man. He pulled things out of water and sold them by weight. But some cargo, once you've brought it up, changes the displacement of everything around it — and you either jettison it and dive clean, or you ride lower and lower until you find out what you're actually carrying.

"Tam," Theo said, "how much fuel we got aboard?"

The old man looked at him sideways, and something in the look said he'd been waiting days for the question. "Enough to run the cordon," he said. "If we run it dark."
