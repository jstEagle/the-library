## 5. Dead Water

Rennie was late.

That alone should have told Theo something. In eleven years of working the delta together, Rennie Calloway had been late exactly twice — once for a funeral he didn't want to attend and once because a water moccasin had taken up residence in his boots overnight and he'd refused to evict it by hand. Otherwise the man rose with the river like something amphibious, already smoking his first cigarette on the dock before the mist had lifted off the pilings.

Theo stood at the rail of the *Magpie* at seven in the morning, then eight, watching the brown water slide past the hull with that patient, indifferent strength it always had, as if the whole delta were one slow animal digesting everything that ever fell into it. The salvage barge sat low behind him, half-loaded with brass fittings, copper pipe, and the survey instruments crated under canvas where nobody would ask about them. The navy cordon's buoys bobbed out past the point, red and white, cheerful as carnival floats marking a grave.

By nine he'd stopped pretending to work. By ten he'd started asking questions he didn't want answered.

"Anybody see Rennie last night?" he asked Pauley, who ran the fuel skiff and knew every tide of gossip between the fish camps and the dredge town.

Pauley wouldn't look at him. That was the first bad sign. The second was how long it took him to answer, and the third was the answer itself: "He was down to the Meridian office yesterday evening. After dark."

"After dark," Theo repeated. "What business does Rennie have with Meridian after dark?"

"Same business everybody has with Meridian eventually." Pauley coiled his fuel line with elaborate attention. "Money or trouble. Usually both."

Theo remembered now — he'd been underwater when Rennie came back from the supply run two days ago, and Rennie had been different since. Quieter. He'd asked questions over dinner that had seemed casual at the time and were not casual at all, Theo realized now, replaying them in the flat light of this morning. *Who hired us for the wreck job, exactly? You ever meet the man? What did the survey logs say, the ones you pulled off the chart table — you read them yet?* And then, the one Theo had laughed off: *You ever wonder why they're paying triple rate for scrap metal, Theo? Scrap don't ask for triple.*

*I don't wonder about money,* Theo had said. *I count it.*

Rennie had smiled at that, but it hadn't reached anywhere near his eyes, and he'd gone up on deck alone with his cigarette and stayed there a long time, looking at the dark line of the river.

Now the dock at the fish camp, midday. A knot of men standing around something at the end of the mooring channel, and the smell reaching Theo before the sight did — gasoline and char and the sweet-rot undertone of burned paint. Rennie's skiff. Or what had been Rennie's skiff: a blackened hull sitting in six inches of water, burned to the waterline and sunk on its own keel, the outboard a fused lump of slag. Fire on water leaves strange evidence; the tide had carried ash and flakes of scorched fiberglass into the reeds like black snow.

No body. Theo heard someone say that twice, the way people repeat things to make them true. No body, so maybe he swam out. Maybe he walked away. Maybe —

But Theo had grown up on this river, and the river taught arithmetic early. A burned boat at night, in a mooring channel, with no body, meant one of two things: the man who owned it was already gone somewhere else, or the man who owned it was never going to need a boat again. And Rennie Calloway had slept on the *Magpie* every night for eleven years except when he went home to his sister in Marsh Landing, and his sister hadn't seen him either. Theo had called her from the camp office an hour ago, and the silence on the line before she spoke had been worse than anything she actually said.

"He came by Tuesday," she'd told him. "He was scared, Theo. He didn't say scared, but he asked me if I'd mind keeping some papers. He left an envelope. I haven't opened it. I thought — I thought I'd wait till he came back for it himself."

Papers. Rennie had copied something off the wreck. Theo was almost sure of it — the chart room had been Rennie's territory, the paper salvage, while Theo worked the machinery below. Rennie had spent an extra forty minutes down there on the last dive, long enough that Theo had surfaced first and waited, annoyed, watching his partner's bubbles march along the hull toward the chart table again.

An envelope in Marsh Landing. A burned skiff in the mooring channel. A company man named Aldous Crane who smiled like a bank manager and paid triple for silence.

Theo stood at the edge of the blackened water until the crowd thinned, and then he did what divers do, what his whole trade had trained into his bones: he went down into the cold and looked.

* * *

The water in the mooring channel was four feet deep and warm as bathwater and opaque as tea, but Theo had worked worse. He went down with just a mask, no tank, breath held, feeling along the silt bottom around the burned hull while the surface wavered above him like the ceiling of another world. His fingers found glass — melted bottles, probably nothing. Found a hinge. Found, hooked in the roots of a dead pilings stump ten feet from the hull, a canvas tool roll that he recognized because he'd stitched the torn corner himself two seasons back. Rennie's. It was heavy.

He came up gasping, hauled the roll onto the dock, and knelt over it dripping, aware of Pauley watching from a careful distance and of the wide empty river beyond, which had never once in his life felt like it was watching back and did now.

Inside the roll, wrapped in an oil rag gone soft with age: a notebook. Cheap spiral binding, swollen with damp, and inside it Rennie's cramped left-slanting handwriting filling page after page — dates, dive times, air mixes, and then, starting three days ago, other entries. Coordinates. Two sets of coordinates, side by side, labeled in Rennie's blunt shorthand: *wreck charts* and *old charts.* And beneath them, underlined twice: *same riverbed. different rivers. somebody moved the map.*

And on the last written page, dated the day before last night: *Asked the wrong man a question today. Crane's clerk got real friendly all of a sudden. If anything happens, Theo knows. Tell Theo it wasn't the diving.*

Tell Theo it wasn't the diving.

Theo sat back on his heels on the sun-bleached planks and the notebook sat in his wet hands and the river went on sliding past, brown and patient, and somewhere upstream a dredge barge thumped away at the bottom of the world like a slow heartbeat. Grief hit him sideways, the way it always does — not as sorrow first but as anger wearing sorrow's clothes, hot and useless and demanding action. Eleven years. The man had taught him how to read current against wind, how to know a snag by the way the water wrinkled above it. He'd eaten a thousand dinners off Rennie's stories, most of them lies, all of them good.

And now there was a burn scar on the water and an envelope in Marsh Landing and a sentence in a drowned man's handwriting — *if it was a drowned man; if it was a man at all anymore* — telling Theo that Rennie had known, had understood the shape of the thing before Theo did, and had died of the understanding.

Or disappeared. Theo made himself hold both possibilities in his hands like two weights, because the second one was the only kind of hope available, and hope made a man stupid, and stupid men on this river ended up like Rennie's skiff.

* * *

He didn't go to the constable. There wasn't really a constable — there was a parish deputy who leased his patrol boat's fuel from Meridian Consolidated, and everyone knew it the way everyone knows which houses flood first. He didn't go to the navy cordon, because the cordon existed to keep people away from the wreck, not to answer questions about it. He sat in the *Magpie*'s wheelhouse with the notebook open on the chart table beside the salvaged survey logs he'd been avoiding reading closely, and he forced himself to do the thing he'd been refusing to do since the day he'd found those scuttle holes punched outward through the hull, from the inside, deliberate as signatures.

He compared the numbers.

The wreck's charts — the ones recovered from the chart table, the official record of the hydrographic survey that had justified Meridian's concession boundaries — placed the headwater spring, the source, the gold-bearing gravel bar that fed the whole upper river, at a set of coordinates in the north fork. Clean numbers. Confident numbers. Numbers bearing the authentication stamp of the senior land surveyor, M. Voss.

Voss. The name went through him like cold water in the ear. Elias Voss, dying in a flophouse bed, pressing an oilskin packet into Theo's hands with fingers that shook too hard to be trusted and eyes that were steady as anchors. *My daughter. Mara Voss. Take her the map. She'll know what it means.* And here was her name, stamped on the fraudulent charts, authenticating the lie.

Except — Theo turned pages, laid Rennie's notes beside the salvaged logs, checked and rechecked until the numbers blurred — except the old charts, the pre-survey charts Rennie must have dug out of the county records or the wreck's own locked archive drawer, put the spring a full nine miles south and west. Nine miles. Enough distance to move a gold concession from one man's claim onto company land. Enough distance to steal a fortune and call it geology.

Somebody had moved the map. Somebody had moved it, stamped it, filed it, built permits on top of it, and when the survey vessel that carried the original instruments and the original honest logs became inconvenient — became evidence — somebody had scuttled her in the sealed reach of the delta and hired a salvage crew to strip her clean before anyone official looked too hard.

Hired me, Theo thought. They hired me to erase the crime scene, and I took the money, and Rennie asked one question too many, and now Rennie is a burn scar on the water.

He closed the notebook. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Divers learned that — the body stays calm in emergency because panic spends air, and somewhere along the years the lesson had sunk deeper than diving. But underneath the calm, something had finished setting, like cement poured in the night. He could feel it hardened in him when he stood up.

They had killed Rennie — or vanished him, which on this river amounted to the same verdict — over paper. Over coordinates. Which meant the coordinates were worth killing for, which meant the true location of the spring was worth more than the salvage contract, more than the triple pay, more than everything Theo had planned to do with it. And Elias Voss's oilskin map, sleeping in the false bottom of Theo's duffel bag, pointed at that same spot from the other direction, from thirty years ago, drawn by a dying prospector's own hand.

The map, the wreck, the permits. Three roads to one crime. And Theo Marchetti standing at the crossroads holding pieces of all three.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon with a deliberateness that fooled everyone, including, mostly, himself. He inventoried the salvage, signed the manifest, watched the crane swing crates aboard the company barge that would carry them downriver to Meridian's warehouse. He let Pauley think he was shaken and quiet and compliant. He ate supper at the fish camp among men who talked about fuel prices and the weather and said nothing about burned skiffs, because that was how fear worked in a small place — not loudly, but as a hole in the conversation shaped exactly like a missing man.

At dusk he climbed to the *Magpie*'s wheelhouse and took stock like a man planning a dive, because that was the only planning he truly trusted. Bottom conditions: his salvage permit ran eight more days, and after that he was a trespasser on the sealed reach. Air: two days' worth of bottles, irrelevant. Money: four hundred dollars and change, plus what the manifest had already paid. Boat: the *Magpie* drew too much water for the upper channels; he'd need a shallow-draft skiff and a pilot who knew the river above the cordon, and pilots who knew the river above the cordon were either smugglers or dead or both.

Against all that, one asset: a map that nine miles of falsified paperwork couldn't explain away, and a promise made to a dying man whose daughter's name was stamped on the fraud.

He could sell it. The thought arrived without shame, because Theo was honest with himself the way he was honest with tide tables. Aldous Crane would pay for that map. Crane had offered triple for "curiosities" already, smiling his bank-manager smile, and that offer had been made before Rennie burned. Whatever the map fetched, it would buy a new boat, a new life, a coast far from this river and its patient brown memory. Sell it, forget the promise, let the dead bury their own arithmetic.

But Rennie's handwriting sat in his memory like a hook set too deep to back out. *Tell Theo it wasn't the diving.* Rennie had believed Theo would understand. Had counted on it. And Elias Voss, feverish and shot and dying, had pressed an oilskin packet into a stranger's hands because he had no one else, because the river sends its errands to whoever's fool enough to accept them.

Theo looked downstream, toward the delta mouth and the sea and every easy exit in the world. Then upstream, where the water came from, where the truth came from, where the cordon buoys bobbed red and white in the last light like warnings written in a language he'd decided, sometime in the last twelve hours, to stop reading as a stop sign.

Eight days of permit. Less, really, because the first day was already gone.

He went below and began pulling out his gear, checking each piece with a diver's ritual care — mask, fins, knife, the waterproof bag for documents. From the false bottom of the duffel he took the oilskin packet and unwrapped it just far enough to confirm what he already knew: the hand-drawn map, ink faded to the color of old tea, the true spring marked in Elias Voss's stubborn hand nine miles southwest of where every official chart in the territory claimed it lay. Evidence. Treasure. A dead man's errand. All three, and he was done pretending they were separable.

On deck, the river breathed its evening breath, cool and mineral. Somewhere out in the dark, a boat engine turned over, ran a minute, and shut off — nothing, probably, or everything. Theo lashed the hatch, set his alarm for four, and wrote one line in the *Magpie*'s logbook in his plain diver's hand, the last honest entry he intended to make for a while:

*Crew of one departed. Destination upriver. Reason: personal.*

Then he blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the water slap the hull, and made his list of pilots. There was only one name on it that mattered — Sef Okafor, who ran the shallows above the cordon and had never in his life been known to say more than six words together, and who owed Theo a favor from a bad night two winters back that neither of them talked about.

Four o'clock would come early. The river was high, the moon was dark, and the permit clock was running.

He closed his eyes and saw, instead of sleep, the burn scar on the mooring channel, and let it become what Rennie would have wanted it to become — not a warning to turn back, but a heading. Upstream. Before the window closed. Before the company finished erasing the paper and started erasing the people who'd read it.

Starting, very possibly, with him.
