## 6. Upstream Rules

Theo Marchetti had learned long ago that the most dangerous thing a man could do was buy too much of anything.

Buy too much rope and the chandler remembered you. Buy too much fuel and the dockmaster wondered where you were bound. Buy two weeks of hardtack, salt pork, lamp oil, and spare shear pins all in one morning, and by nightfall half the delta would know that Theo Marchetti was going somewhere he shouldn't. So he spread it out the way a card cheat spreads his tells — thin, careless, unremarkable. A sack of rice here. A coil of polypropylene line there. A jerrican of diesel from one station, another from a station six miles down the coast road, paid for in small bills, always with some plausible complaint about the price.

He did his shopping with Rennie's burned skiff sitting in his memory like a stone in the throat.

Three days since they'd found it on the mudflat below Pelican Reach, gutted to the waterline, fire-blackened down to the rivets. Three days since anyone had seen Rennie Cole at all. The constable in the fishing village had written *runaway debts* in his ledger without looking up, because that was the easiest thing to write, and Theo had stood there on the shingle with salt wind coming off the flats and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water closing over his head, that the company didn't erase paper. It erased people. And it had started a list, and Rennie's name was crossed off, and somewhere further down that list, in a hand already waiting, was his own.

So: provisions bought quietly. A boat acquired quietly — an old river freighter called the *Magpie*, thirty feet of shallow-drafted steel with a cranky Lister diesel and a hull that had been aground more times than its owner would admit, which was exactly what Theo wanted. Something that could take a beating and keep its opinions to itself.

And now, quietly, a pilot.

* * *

Sef Okafor worked out of a lean-to boatyard on the far side of the delta town, where the channel silted into reed beds and nobody official had a reason to go. He was repairing a punt when Theo found him, a broad-shouldered man past fifty with gray shot through his close-cropped hair and hands that moved over the wood with the unhurried certainty of someone who'd been fixing boats since before Theo was born. He didn't look up when Theo stopped at the edge of the yard.

"Heard you were asking about me," Okafor said.

"Word travels."

"Word travels wrong." He set down his plane, wiped his hands on a rag that had once been white, and only then raised his eyes. They were dark, level, and entirely without warmth. "You're the salvage man. The one diving the navy wreck."

"I'm a man who needs to go upstream," Theo said. "Past the cordon."

"No."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"Don't need to." Okafor picked the plane back up. "Navy seals a river, there's a reason. Man who wants past a navy cordon is either a smuggler or a fool, and I don't carry either."

Theo had expected this. He'd rehearsed nothing, though, because rehearsed words came out sounding rehearsed, and what he said instead was simply true. "The wreck wasn't scuttled by accident. It was murdered. Punched from the inside, valves opened by hand. Somebody wanted that ship on the bottom, and somebody else has been killing people to keep the reason down there." He watched the plane stop moving. "Rennie Cole asked questions about it. His skiff burned Tuesday. Nobody's seen him since."

A long silence. Out in the reeds a heron lifted off, unhurried, wings slapping the still air.

"The ferryman's boy says you've got a map," Okafor said at last, not turning around.

"The ferryman's boy says a lot of things."

"Does the map go all the way up? Past the Split Channel?"

Theo weighed it. Every instinct said give away nothing, but he'd learned something watching Aldous Crane work a room with his soft voice and his triple pay — that men like Crane bought silence, and men like Sef Okafor weren't for sale, and the only currency that meant anything to them was truth offered plainly. "It goes to the headwater," Theo said. "Wherever that is. I haven't seen the top of it yet."

Okafor set the plane down again. He looked at Theo for a long moment, and whatever he was reading there — grief, maybe, or the particular stubbornness of a man who'd decided he was already dead so he might as well be useful — seemed to settle something.

"Two hundred," he said. "Half now. You provision your own boat. I don't touch anything with a name on it. We run at night, we run dark, and if I say beach the boat, you beach the goddamn boat. And if the navy takes us—" he shrugged, a small economical motion, "—you never hired me. I was stealing your diesel."

"That's the plan?"

"It's a good plan. It's worked twice." He held out a hand, palm open, and Theo counted a hundred into it. Okafor folded the bills away inside his shirt without counting them back, which was either trust or contempt, and Theo suspected it was both. "Be at the north slip after full dark Thursday. Moonless. Bring nothing you can't swim with."

* * *

Thursday came up moonless as promised, the sky a low lid of cloud pressing down on the delta, and the *Magpie* sat at the north slip blacker than the night around her. Theo had spent two days making her anonymous — scrubbing the old name off the transom until only a ghost of letters remained, tarping the deck gear, painting the wheelhouse windows with a thin coat of dull varnish that killed reflection. She looked like every other working scow on the river, which was the whole point. A hundred boats ran these waters. The trick was to be one of them.

Okafor arrived at midnight without ceremony, carrying a canvas bag and nothing else, and took the helm without asking whether Theo wanted him to. That suited Theo fine. He'd steamed rivers before, but this one he knew only from charts and from Voss's map, and charts lied, and maps made by dying men deserved a professional's skepticism.

"Side channel starts two miles up," Okafor said, easing the *Magpie* off the slip with barely a wash against the pilings. "Main stem's got the cordon — two cutters, floodlights, a checkpoint boom at Marsh Landing. We won't go near it. There's an old logging run through the reed marsh, dredged sixty years ago, abandoned forty. Half silted. Wide enough for us if the pilot knows where the middle is."

"And you know where the middle is."

"I grew up knowing where the middle is." He said it flatly, without pride, the way a man states a fact about weather. "Kill the running lights. Keep your voice down from here."

They slid north through the sleeping delta, past fish camps where cook fires had died to orange eyes, past moored punts rocking on their lines, past the drowned forest at the river's edge where dead snags stood up out of the black water like the masts of a fleet that had gone down standing. The air was warm and thick, smelling of mud and rot and green growing things, and the engine ticked over at a murmur, and Theo stood at the rail with Voss's map rolled in his jacket and felt the exhilaration come up through the deck plates like vibration.

He hated himself a little for it. Rennie was missing and probably dead, and here he was feeling alive. But there it was. Running dark past a navy cordon with a stranger at the helm and a dead man's map against his ribs — it woke something in him that honest salvage never touched. Fear and joy, braided tight, indistinguishable. He'd felt it once before, years ago, first time he'd gone down on a wreck with his air running shorter than he'd planned and found the hold full of brass instead of silt. The river gave and the river took, and being on it at all was a wager, and God help him, he loved the wager.

Then the reed marsh closed around them and the loving stopped.

* * *

The old logging run was no wider than a farm road and no straighter than a snake's apology. Okafor took it at walking speed, standing at the wheel with his head tilted, reading the water the way other men read print. Theo couldn't see what he was reading. To Theo the whole channel was one uniform black gloss, broken here and there by the pale blur of reed heads, and then — suddenly, when Okafor swung the wheel hard left for no visible reason — by a snag surfacing off the starboard bow, a drowned oak trunk lying just under the skin of the water, its broken limbs reaching up like fingers. It passed close enough that Theo could have touched it. Close enough that he heard the water hiss along its grain.

"You saw that."

"Heard it." Okafor didn't look at him. "Current piles up against a log. Makes a different sound. Smooth water sings, water over wood growls. Listen long enough, you stop needing eyes." A pause, and then, grudgingly, as if paying out a rope: "My father fished this marsh. Taught me the channels before I could swim. Said the river talks to anybody patient enough to shut up and listen."

"Your father sounds like a wise man."

"My father is dead. The dredging company poisoned his grounds twenty years ago and he died poor and angry, and the company is still rich, and that is everything I will say about my father." The wheel turned; the *Magpie* leaned gently and came true again. "Watch the depth gauge. If she reads under four feet, tell me before I feel it. My knees are older than my ears."

They ran the marsh for two hours. Twice the channel shallowed until Theo could hear weed dragging the keel, a soft whispering scrape that set his teeth on edge, and twice Okafor found the deep thread again by some argument he was having with the water that Theo couldn't follow. Once they passed a campfire burning on a hummock fifty yards off — figures around it, a dog barking once, falling silent — and Okafor killed the engine and let them drift, dead quiet, until the firelight fell behind. Nobody spoke. The dog's single bark seemed to hang over the whole marsh like a question nobody wanted answered.

And then, near the top of the marsh where the old logging run bent east toward the main river again, the night lit up.

It came without warning — a white blade of light sweeping across the reeds a quarter mile ahead, sweeping back, steadying. A patrol spotlight, mounted high, cutting the fog into slow golden columns. Underneath it, faint across the water, the idle throb of a cutter's diesel.

Okafor put the *Magpie* aground.

There was no drama to it. He simply eased her into the reeds at the channel's edge until the hull kissed mud and held, and killed the engine, and pulled Theo down by the sleeve onto the deck plates. They lay side by side in the bilge smell and the wet, and Theo's heart was going so hard he was sure it must be audible across the water, sure the spotlight would find them any second, sure of nothing except the weight of the map against his chest and the cold of the steel under his cheek.

The spotlight swept toward them. Held. The beam crawled along the reed tops twenty yards short, close enough that Theo could see individual seed heads trembling in the light, could see the mist turning slowly in it like milk stirred into tea. Somewhere out on the main stem a loudspeaker crackled — numbers, coordinates, the bored cadence of a routine sweep — and went quiet.

"They're checking the boom," Okafor breathed, so close his voice was mostly breath. "Every hour, on the hour. Somebody's been running cargo past them, so they sweep the side channels after each check. Sloppy. They sweep the obvious channels." His teeth showed briefly in the dark, not quite a smile. "This one isn't obvious anymore."

"How long do we lie here?"

"As long as the light wants." He settled his chin on his forearm, comfortable as a man napping in a hammock. "Breathe slow. Fear makes noise."

They lay there forty minutes. Theo counted his own heartbeats and lost count and started over, and somewhere in the losing and starting he found a strange calm, the calm that had come over him once in forty feet of green water when his regulator froze and he'd had to switch to bailout in the dark — the understanding that panic was just another current, and you could fight it or ride it, and fighting it drowned you faster. The spotlight swept away south. The cutter's diesel faded. The marsh exhaled.

Okafor waited ten minutes more, by his own clock, then backed the *Magpie* off the mud with a gentle surge of reverse and took them on, and within the hour the reed marsh opened into the main river above the cordon, wide and dark and empty, running strong between banks that were no longer delta flat but beginning to rise, the first low hills shouldering up out of the fog with trees crowning them like dark hair.

Above the cordon. Committed. Everything behind them now was evidence, and everything ahead was the crime.

Theo unrolled Voss's map on the wheelhouse chart table and pinned it with a coffee mug, and ran his finger up the inked line of the river — past the marks Voss had made in a shaky hand, *snags*, *shallow bar*, *good water*, past a bend annotated *watch for patrols, they get keen above the falls*, to a place where the river narrowed and split and the old prospector had written three words in letters pressed deep enough to tear the paper:

*Only one lives.*

"What's at the Split Channel?" Theo asked.

Okafor glanced at the map, and for the first time since the north slip something moved behind his flat dark eyes — not fear exactly, but respect, the way a man respects a dangerous animal he's decided to walk past anyway.

"Fifty feet of whitewater and two ways to die," he said. "We'll want daylight for that. And luck." He turned back to the wheel. "Get some sleep, salvage man. From here on, the river gets serious."

Theo rolled the map away and went forward to the fo'c'sle, and lay down in the dark with the engine ticking beneath him and the hills sliding past beyond the porthole, and he was nearly asleep when he heard it — faint, upstream, carried on the current the way Okafor said the river carried everything — the sound of another engine. Not behind them. Ahead.

Running dark, running fast, and coming down.
