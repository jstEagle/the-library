## 7. The Investigator Who Drowns Slowly

Lieutenant Cora Ives had been on the river eleven days when she finally saw the man she'd been sent to find, and he was drowning.

She heard him before she saw him — a sound carried down the gorge ahead of the water itself, the flat crack of a hull striking stone, then nothing, which was worse. She was standing on the navy launch's aft deck with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand, watching the morning fog lift off the Split Channel in slow rags, and the sound came out of that fog like something thrown.

"Hard to starboard," she said, and Ensign Brandt, who was twenty-three and eager and had not yet learned to be afraid of her, threw the wheel over without asking why.

The rapid announced itself as a change in the river's voice. For six days above the delta the water had run brown and sullen, deep enough to hide its own intentions, but here it shallowed all at once and began to talk — a long chattering hiss over drowned rock, white water braiding through a field of granite teeth. And in the middle of it, half-submerged, turning slowly in the boil like a dead thing deciding whether to be one, was an upriver skiff with its stern stove in.

A man clung to the hull.

He was not waving. That was the first thing Ives noticed, and it told her more about him than any dossier could have. A drowning man who waves is asking strangers for help; a drowning man who doesn't wave has already decided what strangers are worth. He had one arm hooked through the remains of the gunwale and his chin just clear of the surface, and every time the skiff rolled he went under with it and came up again without any visible change in his expression, as though being drowned were simply weather.

"Throw line," Ives said. "Not too close. If we put the bow into that boil we'll join him."

Brandt threw well — better than she expected, the coil opening clean against the wind — and the rope fell across the man's shoulder. He looked at it. She watched him look at it, watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes: rescue or trap, and no way to know which, and the water making the decision for him either way. Then his free hand closed on the line and he let go of the wreck, and the current took him downstream toward them like a fish on a jig.

They hauled him aboard at the eddy below the falls. He came over the gunwale in a rush of brown water and lay on the deck plates coughing the river out of himself, and Ives crouched beside him and got her first good look at Salvage Diver Theo Marchetti.

The file photograph had been two years old and flattering. This man was leaner, sunburned to the color of old rope, with a week of beard and a fresh cut over one eyebrow that was going to need closing. His hands were diver's hands — scarred knuckles, nails cut short — and they were empty, and she noticed that too, because she had been paid by the navy to notice things, and noticing was the only part of the job she still believed in.

"Theo Marchetti," she said. Not a question.

He stopped coughing. Just like that, mid-spasm, the way a man stops when he hears his own name in a voice he doesn't know. His eyes came up and found hers, gray and level and doing calculations of their own.

"Lieutenant," he said, when he could. "You've got me at a disadvantage."

"Cora Ives. Naval Investigative Service." She let that sit. "You're under arrest."

* * *

The charge sheet took four minutes to write and would take months to unravel, and both of them knew it. Unauthorized salvage within a restricted naval zone. Removal of government property from a wrecked vessel. Failure to report. She read the charges aloud while Brandt stood behind him with his hand nowhere near his sidearm because Ives had told him that morning, in the flat tone she used for instructions she did not intend to repeat, that if Marchetti wanted to hurt anyone he would have done it before they ever saw him, and that the performance of pointing guns at dripping men was beneath the service.

Marchetti listened with his hands cuffed loosely in front of him — she'd cuffed him for the record, and loosened them for the truth — and when she finished he said, "That's it?"

"That's it."

"No charge for running your cordon at night?"

"You ran the cordon?" Brandt said, startled.

"He ran the cordon," Ives said. "Through the eel-grass channel past Marker Nine, which means he had a pilot who knows this river better than our charts do, which means Sef Okafor, which is a conversation for later." She folded the paper. "I'm not charging you with that yet because I haven't finished deciding whether it happened."

"It happened," Marchetti said.

"I know it happened. I'm establishing whether you're stupid enough to lie to me about things I can prove, so I'll know how to weigh everything else you say." She sat down across from him on the bench bolted beneath the wheelhouse window, and outside the glass the river unspooled, wide and green and patient, carrying its cargo of fog. "So let's skip ahead. You're a professional salvager with seventeen years of clean permits. You take a contract nobody will confirm the origin of, to strip a survey vessel in a stretch of river the navy sealed for reasons nobody will explain to me either. You do the work. You get paid. And then, instead of going home to spend the money, you burn your own boat's registration papers, hire Okafor, and run a military blockade at night in a borrowed skiff." She leaned forward. "Help me with the part where that makes sense."

For a long moment he said nothing. The launch thumped gently against the mooring buoy where they'd tied up below the rapids, and the sound was very loud.

"You pulled me out of the water," he said finally. "Why? Your orders are to keep people out of this river. Cheapest way to do that is to let the river do it for you."

It was, Ives thought, a fair question, and the fact that he asked it instead of thanking her told her he had spent the last several days recalibrating his assumptions about who wanted him alive. She could have given him the regulation answer. Instead she gave him the true one, because she had already decided — somewhere between hearing the hull crack and hauling the line in — that this transaction was going to run on truth or not at all.

"Because three weeks ago a navy hydrographic team surveyed that wreck and called it an accident," she said. "Grounding on an uncharted bar during a storm surge, flooding, loss of all hands. Clean report, signed, filed. Except I've read the tide tables for that night, and there wasn't a storm surge. I've read the channel soundings, and the bar they grounded on doesn't exist. And last Tuesday I interviewed the widow of the ship's chief engineer, who told me — after an hour of telling me everything was fine — that her husband had called her from that boat ten days before it sank, drunk on fear, saying the instruments were lying and he didn't know how to make them stop." She held his gaze. "So when I see a man run my blockade heading upstream toward whatever that boat was hiding, I don't pull him out because regulations value human life. I pull him out because he might be the only witness I'm going to get."

Marchetti looked at her for a long time. Water dripped from his hair onto the deck plates, counting seconds.

"They punched the holes from inside," he said.

The words dropped into the cabin like a stone into a well. Beside the wheel, Brandt went very still.

"Say that again," Ives said.

"The survey vessel. *Meridian Star.* Whatever sank her hit from the inside out. Seacock fittings backed off with a wrench — I found the wrench marks, fresh bright metal in the threads, no corrosion. Two places in the hull plating where somebody drove a spike from inside the void spaces, then patched the outside with tar so it wouldn't show on inspection. And the sea valves were opened in sequence, bow to stern, deliberate as a signature." His voice stayed flat, a workingman's voice reciting inventory, but his hands had tightened against the cuffs. "I've raised eleven wrecks, Lieutenant. Accidents are messy. That boat was murdered."

Ives felt something turn over in her chest — not surprise; she had suspected it since the widow's kitchen, since the impossible bar, since the report signed by a reviewing officer whose signature appeared on Meridian Consolidated's concession filings as well — but confirmation is its own kind of shock, the way a diagnosis is worse than a symptom. Someone had scuttled a naval-chartered survey vessel with her crew aboard, and someone with reach had made the navy itself write the obituary.

"How many aboard when she went down?" she asked quietly.

"Six, according to the manifest I recovered. Six sets of gear in the lockers. But there were seven bunks made up." He met her eyes. "Somebody got off before the valves opened. Or somebody was never supposed to be on the list."

The fog was burning off outside. Through the wheelhouse glass Ives could see the Split Channel falling away downstream, and beyond it, invisible under sixty miles of brown water, the wreck sitting on the bottom with its murdered hull and its lying instruments, waiting for her to decide what it was going to mean.

"Here's where we are," she said. She unlocked his cuffs herself, deliberately, letting him watch her do it. "Officially, you're my prisoner. You stay aboard, you eat navy food, you sleep in a bunk I can lock, and when we reach the district station you go in a cell pending charges. That's the version that keeps both of us alive, because if Crane's people learn I've turned you loose, you become a body in the river and I become a lieutenant who lost her prisoner."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, I need what you pulled off that wreck. The logs, the instruments, anything with numbers on it. And you need what I have, which is jurisdiction, a radio that reaches people Crane can't buy, and the fact that killing a naval investigator creates paperwork even Meridian can't bury." She stood. "I'm not offering friendship, Marchetti. I'm offering a trade. Your evidence for my protection, until one of us finds a reason to betray the other."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might live on a different face. "You always negotiate like this?"

"Only with men I've fished out of rivers." She turned for the wheelhouse, then stopped. "One question first. The bunks. Seven made up, six on the manifest. Did you find anything belonging to the seventh?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing it.

"A seabag. Stenciled *E. VOSS.* Empty except for a oilskin wrapper, and the wrapper was empty too." He looked at her steadily. "But the pocket of it smelled like lamp oil and pencil shavings. Somebody kept paper in there. Paper they cared about."

Ives stood very still. Elias Voss. The name was in her files, third from the top of the list of people connected to the *Meridian Star*'s final survey who had subsequently died, disappeared, or gone silent — shot in a flophouse in Delta Row, according to the local constable's one-line report, a drunken accident, case closed by a coroner whose office leased its building from a Meridian subsidiary.

A dying prospector. An empty oilskin. A salvage diver who ran a blockade the same week the prospector died, heading upstream toward ground Voss had spent thirty years claiming was his.

"You know something," she said.

"I know a lot of things," Marchetti said. "Right now I'm deciding which ones keep me breathing."

"Then decide fast." She nodded downstream, toward the concession town waiting beyond the next bend, its company smokestacks standing up out of the haze like masts of ships that never sailed. "Because Crane's gunboats left the town dock an hour ago, two of them, running heavy. They're coming up this channel. And whatever you're carrying, Lieutenant Commander Aldous Crane has already decided it's worth killing for — the only question left is whether he thinks I'm in the way."

Behind her, she heard Marchetti rise from the bench, cuffs hanging open from one wrist, forgotten.

"How long until they reach us?" he asked.

Ives checked the river, the light, the fog still clinging to the far bank like a curtain not quite drawn.

"If they're fast — less than an hour," she said. "Get your evidence ready. We're going to need it sooner than I planned."
