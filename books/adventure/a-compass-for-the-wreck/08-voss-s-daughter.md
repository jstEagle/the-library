## 8. Voss's Daughter

The concession town announced itself before he saw it — a brown smear of dredge tailings bleeding into the river, then the smell: diesel, wet clay, and something chemical underneath that made the back of his throat tighten. Meridian Consolidated had built its town the way it did everything else, Theo decided. On top of what was already there.

He came in slow, running his skiff down the main channel with a load of nothing worth stealing — a few drums of fuel, a crate of chain, the ordinary freight of an ordinary salvager. The permit stitched to his jacket pocket said he had eleven days left on his window. The map sewn inside the lining of his duffel said he had considerably less than that, if Crane's people were watching the water as closely as Theo suspected they were.

They were watching. He counted three men on the fuel dock who stood like they'd been paid to stand, and a fourth in a canvas chair by the company office whose eyes tracked him the whole length of the waterfront without once moving his head. Theo gave them a wave that meant *just another working boat* and tied off at the public landing, downcurrent from the company docks, where the pilings were rotten and nobody important bothered to look.

Concession towns all ran on the same skeleton, whatever river they squatted beside. Company store. Company office. Company saloon with prices set to bleed a man dry at exactly the rate he earned. And somewhere, always, a flophouse or a boarding room where the people the company had used up went to wait out their endings.

Elias Voss had told him, between the fever and the blood, in sentences that kept losing their footing: *Mara works for them. That's the joke of it, Marchetti. My own girl, signing their papers.* Theo had taken it for delirium at the time — dying men say bitter things about their children — but now, standing on the landing with the company's flag hanging limp over the office roof, he felt the words rearrange themselves into something heavier.

He asked around carefully. Not about Voss — a dead or dying prospector's name would travel fast in a town this size, and faster still to the wrong ears. He asked instead about surveyors.

"Senior land surveyor?" The clerk at the general store didn't even look up from her ledger. "That's Miss Voss. Office is the stone building past the assay shed. She keeps her own hours." A pause, and then, with the particular contempt small towns reserve for anyone the company favors: "Keeps them late, too."

*Miss Voss.* His stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

* * *

He spent the afternoon not going to see her.

He walked the waterfront instead, hands in pockets, doing the slow arithmetic of a man whose promise has just changed shape. The flophouse in the delta port seemed a hundred years away. He could still feel the weight of Elias Voss against his arm — light as kindling, burning hot with fever, gripping Theo's wrist with a strength that had no business surviving in that ruined body. *You'll take it to her. Swear it. She'll know what it means even if she hates me.*

Theo had sworn. It hadn't been a hard oath to make at the time. A dying man, a paper map, a daughter somewhere upriver — it had felt like carrying a letter, not carrying a verdict.

But the woman the store clerk described wasn't a letter's recipient. She was, if the gossip overheard at the saloon counter could be trusted — and it usually could, in towns built on paychecks — the most trusted pair of hands in the district. *Miss Voss signs every chart that comes out of this valley,* a dredge hand said, nursing his beer like it owed him money. *Company wouldn't file a permit without her name on it. Old man Corliss himself says she's got the straightest eye on the river.*

The straightest eye on the river. Theo turned his glass slowly on the scarred wood. Straight enough to have authenticated charts that put a gold concession forty miles from where God and geology had actually put it. Either Mara Voss was the greatest fool ever issued a transit and a stamp pad, or she was the keystone of the whole fraud — the respectable name that made stolen ground look legal.

And either way, Theo thought, setting down the glass untouched, she was the last person on earth he could hand an oilskin-wrapped map to.

Unless.

Unless there was a third possibility, one he didn't yet have the evidence to believe: that she signed what she was given, the way any employee signs what they're given, and never looked beneath the surface of it. That she was a tool, not a partner. That the bitterness in her father's dying voice had been the bitterness of a man who assumed the worst of a daughter who'd chosen the company over him, and not the bitterness of a man who knew.

He needed to look at her face. That was the whole of it. He needed to stand in front of Mara Voss and read her the way he read water — the way a channel tells you where the rocks are, if you're patient and honest with yourself about what you're seeing.

* * *

The stone building past the assay shed had once been a bank in some other town's imagination; now it held drafting tables under tall windows, and the smell of ink and lamp oil. Theo knocked at the door marked SURVEY OFFICE at six o'clock, when the clerks' footsteps had thinned out along the boardwalk, and a voice said, "It's open," in a tone that suggested the door being open was itself an imposition.

She was standing at the far table with her back to him, bent over a chart, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hair pinned up in a style that had surrendered to the workday sometime around noon. Lamplight fell across half-drawn contour lines. She didn't turn around.

"If you're here about the boundary stakes on the lower claim, they were placed per the resurvey filing, and I'm not relitigating it with every prospector who—"

"I'm not here about stakes."

She turned then. And Theo understood, in one cold drop of recognition, why Elias Voss had said *she'll know what it means even if she hates me* — because the woman looking at him had her father's face rebuilt by thirty years and a different life. Same deep-set gray eyes. Same jaw that looked like it had been drawn with a straightedge. But where the old man's face had been weathered soft and broken open, hers was composed, guarded, professional — a face that had learned to be a wall.

"You're not from the company," she said. Her eyes went over him the way she probably went over a chart: quick, systematic, missing nothing. The diver's shoulders. The rope-burned hands. The delta-port mud still dried on his boot seams. "Salvage?"

"Theo Marchetti. I run a salvage boat out of the delta." He let the silence do a little work. "I've been working the wreck of the *Corvus Survey*."

Something moved behind her eyes — fast, controlled, gone. But not gone fast enough. "That's a sealed site."

"It is."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I pulled instruments off her that somebody wanted at the bottom of the river. Because the holes in her hull were punched from the inside. And because—" he watched her face with everything he had, "—because your father sent me."

The lamplight hummed. Somewhere outside, a dredge coughed and settled back into its rhythm. Mara Voss did not move for the space of three full breaths, and when she finally spoke, her voice was level in the way that ice is level.

"My father is dead."

"He was alive nine days ago. Shot, and fevered, in a flophouse in Port Anselm. He might be dead by now. I couldn't stay to find out — staying would have killed us both." He took a step forward. "He gave me something for you. A map. Hand-drawn. Coordinates."

"Don't." The word cracked out of her sharper than she'd meant it, and he saw her hear herself, and press the crack closed. When she spoke again it was quieter, and colder, and worse. "You don't understand what you're carrying, Mr. Marchetti. Or maybe you do, and that's why you came to my office instead of my door. Either way — you should think very carefully about who you show that thing to in this town."

"That's what I'm trying to figure out," Theo said. "Because the coordinates on that map match the original strike. And the concession permits on file in this district — the ones with your signature authenticating the survey data — put the source somewhere else entirely. Forty miles downstream. Ground Meridian already owns."

Her face didn't change. That was the terrible part. He'd seen divers lie under interrogation, seen smugglers stare down navy officers, and none of them had held a face this still. Only her hands betrayed anything: she had picked up a pencil, and the pencil was not drawing anything, just turning, turning between her fingers.

"Those are serious accusations," she said.

"They are. And I haven't decided yet whether you're the victim of them or the author."

"And which do you want me to be?" Something flickered there at last — not guilt, not innocence, but a tired, furious intelligence that recognized the trap from the inside. "Think about it, Mr. Marchetti. If I tell you I'm innocent, you want to believe me, because you made a promise to a dying man and promises want to be kept. If I tell you I'm guilty, you walk out of here and take that map to someone who'll pay for it, and my father's last act on this earth becomes a transaction. You can't lose either way, can you? That's a comfortable position to interrogate a woman from."

"It's not comfortable at all," Theo said quietly. "Believe me."

For a moment the wall in her face trembled — not falling, just trembling, the way a piling trembles when something big moves against it in the dark. She set the pencil down with exaggerated care.

"Get out of my office."

"Mara—"

"*Get out.*" Her voice stayed low, which somehow made it worse. "If my father is alive, then he sent a stranger to do what he couldn't be bothered to do himself in twenty years — write a letter, come upriver, knock on a door. Instead he sends a salvager with a story about maps. Well. You found me. I'm right here, same as I've been for six years, same address, same town, while he was off chasing his strike and his grudges." She picked the pencil back up. Turned to the chart. "Tell him the door was never locked. Tell him I said that."

Theo stood in the doorway a moment longer. "Your name is on those charts," he said. "Every one of them. When this comes apart — and it's coming apart, Miss Voss, I've seen the bottom of the ship they sank to hide it — that name is going to be the first thing they read out loud."

He heard the pencil stop.

He let himself out.

* * *

The evening came down on the concession town like silt settling. Theo walked the long way back to the landing, taking his time, letting the lamplight and the shadows sort themselves around him, and did the only honest thing left to do, which was admit how badly shaken he was.

Not by her anger. Anger he could account for — a daughter hearing after twenty years that her father lived, delivered by a stranger with accusations in his mouth. No. What shook him was the precision of her. She hadn't denied the fraud. Go back over the conversation, he told himself, walking past the assay shed, past the company store closing its shutters. She'd deflected it. She'd attacked his motives. She'd thrown her father at him like a weapon. But nowhere in it — nowhere — had she said *the coordinates aren't shifted*. Nowhere had she said *the survey was sound*.

A woman with clean hands says that first. It's reflex. It's the first sentence out of the door.

He stopped at the rail of the public landing and looked down at the black water sliding past the pilings. Inside his duffel, sewn flat against the canvas, the oilskin packet rode against his spine like a second heartbeat. Forty miles of river between him and the true source. Eleven days of permit. One enforcer named Crane who already suspected what Theo carried. And now a surveyor with her father's eyes and the company's signature, who might be the fraud's chief architect or its next casualty, and who had ordered him out of her office with a voice like a closing door.

There was a version of tonight where he burned the map. He made himself consider it honestly, standing there in the dark: burn it, log his salvage, collect his pay, let the river keep its secret and the company keep its town. There was a version where he sold it — Crane had offered triple pay for curiosities, and a man could triple his way clear of this country entirely. Both versions were simple. Both versions required only that he stop hearing Elias Voss's voice saying *she'll know what it means*, stop seeing the old man's grip on his wrist, stop remembering that the only person who had asked nothing of him except a promise was the person everyone else in this story had already betrayed.

He was still standing there when he noticed the skiff.

It was moored two slips down from his, a company boat, unlit, riding low. He hadn't seen it arrive. He hadn't heard an engine. And now, in the dark, he made out the shape of a man sitting motionless in the stern, facing him across fifty feet of black water, and the ember-glow of a cigarette that flared once and died without being smoked.

A voice carried across the water, pleasant and unhurried, pitched so precisely that it reached Theo and no one else on the whole waterfront:

"Mr. Marchetti. Might I buy you dinner tomorrow? I find that conversations about curiosities go better over a good table." A pause, and the ember flared again, briefly lighting a polished, patient face. "Oh — and do give Miss Voss my regards. I understand you paid her a call. Aldous Crane makes it his business to know everyone who matters on this river. I'd hate for you to matter the wrong way."

The cigarette dropped, hissed, and went out. By the time Theo crossed the fifty feet of planking, the skiff was empty, the mooring line looped neat as a signature, and the river slid on in the dark, keeping — as it always did — whatever it was told.
