## 10. Crane's Hospitality

The invitation came by hand, which was the first strange thing about it. No telegraph to the outfitter's office, no note slipped under his door at the Delta Rest boarding house — but a boy of perhaps twelve, barefoot and solemn as an altar server, standing on the porch with a cream-colored envelope held out in both hands like something that might bite.

*Mr. Aldous Crane requests the pleasure of Mr. Theo Marchetti's company at dinner. Tomorrow evening, seven o'clock. The Meridian Consolidated field residence.*

Theo read it twice under the mosquito coil's blue smoke. The paper was heavy stock, the kind that cost more per sheet than the boy would earn in a week, and it smelled faintly of some cologne that had traveled a long way to die in this heat.

He thought about the night before last — the small sounds from the dock at two in the morning, the way his duffel had been repacked with a precision his own hands never managed, the compass case sitting half an inch off its usual angle. Someone had gone through everything he owned and put it back almost perfectly. *Almost* was the message. The word itself.

"Tell him I'll come," Theo said.

The boy nodded once and padded away down the boardwalk, and Theo stood watching the river go gold and then copper and then dark, turning the invitation over in his fingers the way he'd turn over a coin of doubtful weight.

You didn't refuse a man like Crane. That much the delta had taught him already. You also didn't accept anything from him without counting your fingers afterward.

* * *

The Meridian field residence sat on the highest ground in the concession town, a two-story timber building with wraparound galleries and glass windows — actual glass, shipped upriver at ruinous expense — that caught the last light and threw it back in long amber panes. It looked less like a company office than a plantation house transplanted from somewhere with gentler weather and worse sins. Maybe that was accurate either way.

A man met Theo at the foot of the steps. He was introduced only as Ferro, and he had the build of someone who'd done violence professionally and retired from it into supervision. He patted Theo down with the thoroughness of a customs officer and the tenderness of a man checking fruit for bruising.

"No weapons at the table," Ferro said. "House rule."

"I dive for a living. My weapon is a knife for cutting line."

"Then the knife stays too."

Ferro took it without ceremony, sliding it into a canvas roll that already held, Theo noticed, a revolver and a second knife of better make than his own. A collection. A trophy shelf. He filed that away.

Inside, the dining room was absurdly civilized. Linen. Silver. A ceiling fan turning with the lazy authority of money. Candles burned in holders that must have been packed in straw for six hundred miles, and the table was set for two. Through the open gallery doors came the smell of the river — mud and fish and green rot — pressing against all that refinement like a reminder that the wilderness was not invited tonight but knew perfectly well it was outside.

Crane rose from behind a decanter to greet him.

Up close, Aldous Crane was harder to dismiss than his reputation allowed. Theo had imagined a soft man, a desk man, the kind who sent others into the water. But Crane was lean in the way of men who kept themselves out of vanity rather than necessity, silver at the temples, a face built for photographs — even, reasonable, trustworthy. His handshake was dry and exact, calibrated to the ounce.

"Marchetti. Thank you for coming. Sit — please." He poured wine without asking, a deep red that had no business surviving the journey. "I hope you'll forgive the formality. Out here we lose so much of civilization that when a piece of it drifts within reach, I find myself greedy for it."

"It's a long way from anywhere," Theo agreed, taking the chair Ferro pulled for him.

"So is most of what matters." Crane smiled. "That's practically my company's motto."

They ate. Crane talked easily, and well — about the delta's hydrology, about the engineering of the new dredge barges waiting downstream, about a wine region in the old country he'd visited before the war made such travel unfashionable. He asked questions too, and they were good ones: What did Theo think of the current? How deep did the channel run below the wreck site? Had the navy's cordon interfered with his work?

Each question landed softly. Each one was a sounding lead dropped into dark water, measuring depth.

Theo answered with the truth where the truth was harmless and with boredom everywhere else. He described the salvage in flat professional terms — hull plates, brass fittings, the galley stove, a crate of chronometers ruined past recovery. He watched Crane watch him, and understood that the man across the table was doing the same arithmetic he was: *what does he know, what will he sell, what will it cost to make him stop knowing.*

Dessert came — a custard, of all things, chilled somehow, glistening — and with it Crane dismissed the steward and leaned back, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat, and stopped performing.

"You found something in the wreck," he said.

Theo let a silence sit there, long enough to be rude, then cut his custard with the dessert spoon. "I found a ship somebody sank very thoroughly."

"Ships sink. It's practically their vocation." Crane's voice stayed pleasant. "This one sank from the inside, though. Holes punched through her bottom plates from within the engine compartment. You noticed. Of course you noticed — Marchetti, you have a reputation for noticing. It's why you were hired." He turned his glass by its stem, a slow quarter rotation, eyes never leaving Theo's face. "What else did you notice?"

"That whoever scuttled her wanted her to look storm-damaged and didn't understand how storms work."

Something flickered behind Crane's evenness — not anger. Interest. The interest of a man hearing a colleague describe technique.

"Yes," Crane said quietly. "They didn't. I did understand, later. By then the lesson was expensive." He said it the way another man might admit to a golf score.

Theo set down his spoon. "You're telling me you sank her."

"I'm telling you I supervised the operation. There's a difference, and the difference is that I've spent eleven years making sure no one can prove the first sentence." Crane refilled both glasses himself, waving off the returning steward with a flick of two fingers. "But you strike me as a man who respects plain dealing between professionals, so let me deal plainly. The survey vessel carried instruments. Instruments record. And the recordings of that particular voyage contain data that certain parties — including, regrettably, the parties who commissioned the survey — would prefer did not exist in any recoverable form."

"The charts were falsified."

"The charts were *revised*," Crane corrected, with the mild patience of a tutor. "Coordinates are a language, Marchetti, and languages can be translated. The original survey translated them one way. Our revised edition translated them somewhat... south and east. Forty kilometers, give or take. Enough that the concession permits we subsequently secured cover land that is geologically worthless, while the true source sits outside our boundaries on unclaimed ground." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was worse. "Unclaimed except by dead men and dreamers."

"Elias Voss."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Crane didn't flinch, didn't glance away, didn't do any of the things a guilty man does. He simply looked at Theo with renewed attention, the way a card player looks when an opponent shows his hand early.

"There it is," Crane said softly. "So you've met him."

"He's dying."

"They usually are, by the time they matter." Crane drank, unhurried. "Shot, I heard. Terrible business, these river towns. So much random violence." He let the irony hang just long enough to be deniable, then set the glass down with a click. "Here is my offer, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide anything, because it is genuinely generous. Whatever Voss gave you — papers, a map, coordinates scratched on a scrap of hide, I don't know and I don't care to pretend I do — bring it to me. Not copies. The original, and your silence, and I will pay you triple what Meridian has paid you for the salvage, plus a bonus that will buy you a boat of your own and a crew to run her. You'll never dive another dead man's hull in your life unless you feel like it."

"And if I don't?"

Crane sighed, and for a moment the polish thinned and Theo saw the machinery underneath — patient, industrial, entirely without malice, which was the most frightening thing about it. Malice gets tired. This wouldn't.

"Then nothing happens to you," Crane said. "Nothing at all. You'll finish your permit window, log your salvage, collect your pay, and go home a moderately wealthy man. And Elias Voss's death will become a pattern. Do you understand what I mean by that?"

"I understand English."

"Do you?" Crane tilted his head. "Because men in your position always imagine threats are aimed at them. They're not. Threats aimed at a man only stiffen him — you divers are a stubborn breed, you spend your lives arguing with water, you don't frighten well. No. If you keep the map, I won't touch you. But everyone adjacent to you becomes interesting. Your dive partner — Rennie, isn't it? Charming fellow, asks a great many questions in riverside bars. The pilot you're thinking of hiring — yes, we know about Sef Okafor, we know about everyone on this river, it's our river. The boarding house woman who leaves extra rice on your plate because she likes your manners. None of them signed anything, Marchetti. None of them took company money. They're just... near you. And nearness, in my experience, is the entire content of tragedy."

The candles guttered in a draft off the river. Somewhere out in the dark a night bird called, three notes, descending, like something falling.

Theo found his voice was steady, which surprised him. Underneath the steadiness his pulse was a diver's pulse, deep and slow and enormous, the heartbeat you get at forty meters when the body knows exactly how much trouble it's in and has decided to conserve everything anyway.

"You killed Voss's partner," Theo said. "Years ago. In the caves above the headwater. And now you've shot Voss himself, and you sank a survey ship with her own crew's knowledge or without it, and you're sitting here telling me about it over custard because you believe there's no version of events where I can use any of it."

"I believe," Crane said, "that evidence requires witnesses, witnesses require credibility, and credibility requires being alive. I believe the navy sealed that wreck for reasons of their own and will thank me for keeping the delta quiet. And I believe you are a practical man, because impractical men don't survive forty meters of black water." He stood, smoothing his waistcoat, and the host returned to his face like a mask settling. "Take three days. Think like a businessman. When you're ready, leave word with the outfitter — just the word *yes*. Nothing written. We're both careful men."

At the door, Ferro returned Theo's knife, handle first, with a small bow that managed to be a threat.

"One more thing," Crane said from behind him, and Theo turned. Crane stood by the candlelight with his glass, looking younger suddenly, almost wistful. "Voss. Did he suffer?"

"He's suffering now."

Crane nodded slowly, absorbing that, and Theo watched him search himself for remorse and find, at best, an inventory. "Eleven years ago he was the best prospector on this river," Crane said. "Better than us. Better than the government surveyors. He found something the rest of us needed a fleet and a fraud to steal." He raised the glass an inch — a toast, or an apology, or just a habit. "Good night, Marchetti."

* * *

The walk back through the concession town took twelve minutes and lasted approximately a year. Theo moved through pools of lamplight and darkness, past the closed assay office, past the company store with its barred windows, and every shadow had a shape in it, and every shape was Ferro or worse. At the corner by the church — a church, in a town built on theft; the delta had a sense of humor — he stopped and pressed his palm flat against the warm boards of a wall until his hands stopped shaking.

Three days. Crane had given him three days, which meant Crane believed three days was enough, which meant Crane believed the map was already as good as his.

Which meant Theo had maybe one.

He reached the boarding house and climbed the outside stairs to his room, and only when the door was locked and the lamp lit did he pull the oilskin packet from its hiding place inside the false bottom of his instrument case. Voss's map lay in his hands, the hand-drawn lines fine as spider silk, the true source marked in ink that had faded to the color of old blood. Above it, in the old man's cramped script, a second set of markings Theo hadn't fully deciphered — routes, symbols, a network threading through country no permit covered.

And beneath the map, folded separately, a page torn from a cheap notebook, in the same hand:

*If you're reading this, boy, you've met Crane. Don't trust his manners. Manners is what he leads with. The rest of him is the hole punched from the inside.*

Theo sat down slowly on the edge of the bed.

Voss had known. Dying in a flophouse with a bullet in him, feverish, trusting a stranger with his daughter's inheritance — he had still taken the time to warn him. Which meant Voss had known Crane would come. Which meant Voss had known exactly what Theo was walking into tonight.

Outside, far downriver toward the navy cordon, a boat engine started — muffled, throttled low, moving fast against the current's grain. Then a second engine joined it, and a third, running without lights.

Theo blew out the lamp and stood in the dark listening, map in one hand, and understood that whatever window he'd imagined having had just closed ahead of schedule. Crane wasn't waiting three days.

Crane was closing the river.
