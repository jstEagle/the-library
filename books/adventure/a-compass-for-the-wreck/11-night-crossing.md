## 11. Night Crossing

Theo counted the lamps along the concession town's single street the way he'd count air remaining in a tank. Seven burning. Two of them belonged to men who didn't drink, didn't laugh, and hadn't taken their eyes off Okafor's moored boat since sundown.

He crouched in the reeds behind the fish-drying racks and watched the water. The river at night was a different animal than the river by day — wider somehow, blacker, its surface moving with a slow muscular patience that made him think of something digesting. Fog was coming off it in ropes, thickening where the current met the cold air off the hills. Good. Fog was the only ally in this town that couldn't be bought by Meridian Consolidated.

Behind him, boots on gravel — soft, deliberate, the tread of someone who'd learned to walk quietly in institutions rather than swamps. He didn't turn. He'd known she would come; that was the trouble with Lieutenant Cora Ives. She was predictable in exactly the ways that made her dangerous.

"You're leaving tonight," Ives said. Not a question.

"I'm leaving tonight."

"Your permit expired at noon."

"My permit died at noon. There's a difference." Theo kept his eyes on the water. "A dead permit means they can charge me. An expired one just means nobody's watching anymore. Nobody official."

"Crane's watching."

"Crane doesn't count as official until he kills me, and then there'll be paperwork, and you'll have to fill it out." He glanced back at her then. In the dark her navy coat made her a shape without edges, but he could see her hands, and her hands were not relaxed. "You didn't come here to arrest me. If you wanted to arrest me, you'd have brought men."

"I brought myself. That should worry you more."

"It does." He turned fully now, keeping his voice under the sound of the current. "Here's what I know, Lieutenant. Rennie is dead because he asked Crane questions about the survey logs. Voss is dying in a flophouse because he knew where the gold actually comes from. And somewhere in your navy's files there's a report on the *Meridian Survey* that says 'foundered, cause undetermined,' when we both know those holes were punched from the inside. You've read my salvage manifest. You know what I pulled off that wreck."

Ives said nothing for a long moment. A night bird called from the far bank, three notes, like a question asked twice too many.

"The holes were punched from inside," she said finally. "I dove it myself two days after you left the delta. Before you ask — no, that's not in any report either." Her jaw worked. "My commanding officer told me the matter was closed. Meridian holds the supply contracts for half the patrol fleet on this river. Closed matters stay closed when they're profitable."

"So you need my evidence more than I need your protection."

"I need *the truth*," she said, and there was something raw under the words, something that had been ground against authority long enough to bleed. "And you need to not die in this town. Those are compatible interests. Barely."

Theo studied her. Trusting a navy investigator was like trusting a crocodile to mind your fish trap — it might work, right up until it very much didn't. But the gunboats were already being provisioned at the company dock; he'd watched the crates go aboard at dusk. Alone, in an open skiff, through fog and deadfalls, he was dead water. With her — with her rank, her knowledge of the patrol schedules, her stubbornness — he had maybe a chance shaped like a knife's edge.

"Sef's boat leaves from the old ferry landing in twenty minutes," he said. "There's room for one more person who doesn't mind getting wet."

"I don't mind getting wet," Ives said. "I mind getting lied to. Whatever's on that map of yours — whatever Voss gave you — I see it before we reach the headwaters. That's the price."

"That's a negotiation for calmer water."

"Then we'll negotiate in the fog." She stepped past him toward the landing, coat whispering against the reeds. "Coming, Marchetti? The tide of your life is going out."

* * *

Sef Okafor took one look at Ives climbing aboard and spat over the side, which Theo understood to be a form of greeting.

"Navy," Sef said.

"Investigator," Ives corrected.

"Same thing, wetter." Sef cast off the line without waiting for an answer and let the current take the bow. He steered with a single sweep oar, standing, his body rocking with some rhythm older than the river towns, and the skiff slid out into the fog like a coin dropped into milk.

For the first hour there was nothing but water-sound and the creak of the oarlock and the fog pressing close enough to drink. Theo sat in the bow with a hand on the painter, listening. Every sound had to be named: that was a snag scraping, that was a bird, that was the river eating a cutbank somewhere to starboard. The fog made the world small and enormous at once. Somewhere behind them, downriver, engines were warming.

"They'll run dark," Sef said quietly, not turning his head. "No lights till the last channel buoy. Crane's boats are shallow draft, twin screws, forty knots if the pilot's brave. We make eight on a good stretch."

"Then we don't take good stretches," Theo said. "We take ones their pilots won't."

Sef's teeth showed briefly in the dark. "Now you're talking my language."

Ives sat amidships with her service pistol across her knees and her eyes on the fog, and Theo caught himself cataloguing her the way he'd catalogue a dive site — depth, hazards, currents, what could kill him and how fast. It wasn't fair, probably. She'd pulled no trigger yet. But neither had the river, and he respected the river precisely because it might.

The first deadfall came an hour before dawn, where a great fig tree had come down across a side channel, its root ball standing up out of the water like a wall of black fists. Sef took them through a gap no wider than the boat, threading between two submerged limbs he clearly knew by heart, and Theo heard Ives exhale slowly behind him.

"You've run this water before," she said.

"Everyone's run this water before," Sef said. "The question is whether the water remembers you kindly."

Then the fog ahead lit up white.

It came without sound at first — a searchlight beam stabbing through the murk from downstream, sweeping, finding nothing, sweeping again. Then the engine noise arrived, low and many-throated, and beneath it a voice amplified into distortion:

"*Vessel in the narrows. Heave to. This is a Meridian security patrol operating under concession authority.*"

"Concession authority," Ives repeated softly. "They've given the company police powers on open water. That's not in any statute I've read."

"Statutes are written downstream," Sef said. "We live upstream." He bent to the oar and the skiff leapt forward, swinging hard to port into a channel so narrow the reeds brushed both gunwales.

Theo went flat. Behind him he heard Ives do the same, her pistol still in her hand, her breathing controlled and even — a woman who had been shot at before, or at least trained by people who assumed she would be. The light swept overhead once, twice, painting the fog red-gold, and then the main channel swallowed it and they were in the reeds, engine noise fading, hearts loud.

"They'll split up," Ives whispered. "One boat stays on the main channel, one takes the cutoffs. They know this water almost as well as—"

"As well as me," Sef finished. "Almost. But almost is a whole country away."

* * *

Cora Ives had arrested forty-one people in her career, and every one of them had taught her the same lesson: nobody thought they were the villain of their own story. She'd expected Marchetti to be the same — another salvager with a rationalization, another man whose greed wore the costume of grievance.

Instead, four hours into the flight, crouched in the bottom of a leaking skiff while a hired gunboat's wake slapped the reeds fifty yards away, she found herself believing him. That frightened her more than the gunboats did. Belief was how investigators got ruined. Belief was how careers ended up like hers — a lieutenant at thirty-four, exiled to a river district because she'd once used the word *falsified* in a report about a company with friends.

Marchetti lifted his head above the gunwale, listened, and dropped back down. His face in the pre-dawn grey was all planes and salt, a man carved by weather and work. "Boat's gone upstream," he breathed. "They think we ran the cutoff."

"They'll figure it out."

"Sure. In an hour. Maybe two." He settled back beside her, shoulder nearly touching hers in the narrow hull. "That map you want to see. When we stop, I'll show you. Not because you demanded it. Because you dove the wreck alone and put it in no report, and a person who does that isn't hunting a salvage violator."

"No?"

"A person who does that is hunting whoever scuttled her. Same as me."

She wanted to argue. The argument was right there, tidy and professional: *I am an officer of the Navy Riverine Service and you are a criminal fleeing jurisdiction.* But the words wouldn't form, because underneath them lay the true shape of things — the sealed file, the closed matter, the commanding officer's flat voice saying *let it go, Cora*, and the memory of diving that wreck alone in green half-light, running her fingers along punched steel, feeling the murder in the metal.

"Voss," she said instead. "Elias Voss. He filed an original claim twenty-two years ago. I found the record last week, buried under three resurveys. His coordinates were struck through and replaced. Someone signed off on the replacement." She paused. "Someone with a surveyor's license."

Theo was quiet a moment. "Mara Voss," he said. "His daughter. She works for Meridian."

"And you were carrying her father's map to her."

"Was?"

"She's the one whose signature buried him. Think about who you're delivering to."

"I have been thinking about little else," Marchetti said grimly. "Which is why the map stays wrapped until I look her in the eye."

Dawn came up brown and slow through the fog, and with it the deadfalls — a mile of river where the floods of some past decade had stacked fallen timber into a labyrinth that no charted pilot would attempt. Sef took them in anyway. For two hours Cora Ives watched a man fight a river with a stick of wood and win, over and over, the bow swinging through gaps measured in inches, bark shrieking along the hull, and she understood that she was seeing something the company could never buy: competence that came from belonging. Sef Okafor wasn't escaping on this river. He was home, and the river knew it.

The second gunboat found them at the far end of the deadfalls.

It came out of the fog wall like a rumor becoming fact — the flat grey bow, the mounted light, two men aft with rifles slung but hands near. Sef swore in a language Ives didn't know and threw the skiff hard to starboard, into a chute of white water she hadn't even seen coming.

"Hold on!" Marchetti shouted, and then the river took over.

* * *

Theo had shot rapids before, but never backwards, which was effectively what Sef did — using the eddy lines, backing and filling, letting the current spin the skiff so the heavy end took the waves. The world became noise and cold spray. A deadfall limb raked the starboard gunwale close enough to shave splinters off the wood an inch from his knuckles. Ives, beside him, had wedged herself into the bilge with one arm locked through a thwart and the pistol still somehow in her other hand, and when a wave broke clean over the bow she didn't scream, just blinked the water out of her eyes and kept watching the gunboat.

The gunboat did not follow.

They heard why before they saw why — a grinding crunch, a shout, the sickening hollow note of an aluminum hull meeting a submerged log at speed. The sound carried over the water like a bell tolling for somebody else's church. Sef laughed, short and fierce, and didn't slow down.

By midmorning the fog had burned off and the river had gentled, sliding wide and brown-green between forested bluffs hung with mist that refused to leave. They bailed the skiff with a dented pot. Ives wrung out her coat. Theo checked the oilskin packet inside his shirt, dry as a sermon, and felt the map's corners through the cloth like a heartbeat.

Ives held out her hand. "A deal's a deal."

He looked at her hand. Small, scarred across the knuckles, steady. Then he unwrapped the packet and laid the map on the thwart between them — Elias Voss's careful ink, the spring-fed pool marked at the head of the canyon, the smuggler routes threaded through the rock like veins, and in the corner, in a shakier hand, the words *for my Mara, who will know what to do.*

Ives read it for a long time. When she looked up, something had changed in her face — the investigator receding, the person surfacing, tired and resolute.

"This isn't treasure," she said. "This is a confession, a deed, and a death warrant, all in one page."

"Now you understand why Crane wants it."

"And why you can't sell it." She folded the map along its original creases with surprising gentleness and handed it back. "All right, Marchetti. Here's my position, stated plainly so there's no confusion later. Officially, I lost you in the fog and I'm pursuing leads downriver. Unofficially—" she glanced at Sef's broad back, at the bluffs sliding past, at the country ahead where the canyon walls would soon rise up and swallow the sky "—unofficially, I'm going to see this through to the source. And when we get there, whatever happens, it happens in daylight, with witnesses, on the record. No more bodies in rivers. Agreed?"

Theo took the map and tucked it away against his chest, next to the promise that weighed more than gold.

"Agreed," he said. "But Lieutenant — daylight at the source cuts both ways. What we find there, everyone finds."

Ahead, the river bent north, and the first dark shoulder of the headwater canyon rose against the morning haze like a door beginning to open. And on the bluff above the bend, small in the distance but unmistakable — a man in a white suit, standing beside a parked launch, watching them come.

Sef saw him too. The oar stopped mid-stroke.

"That's not possible," Sef said quietly. "Nobody camps on that bluff. Nothing lives on that bluff."

"He does," Theo said, and his mouth had gone dry. "Crane doesn't chase, Lieutenant. He waits. He was never behind us at all."

The fog behind them, far downstream, filled suddenly with engine noise — three engines, four, closing fast.
