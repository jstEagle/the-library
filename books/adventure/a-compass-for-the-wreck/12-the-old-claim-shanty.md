## 12. The Old Claim Shanty

The storm found them an hour after dark, and the shanty found them by accident.

Cora Ives had been navigating by the river's sound more than its shape — the water talking to itself around snags, the particular hush where a channel deepened — when the rain came down like a dropped ceiling and the world contracted to the circle of Theo Marchetti's hand-lamp. Sef Okafor, poling them up a side slough that smelled of rot and iron, grunted once and swung the bow toward a darkness that was darker than the rest.

"Roof," he said. That was all he ever said that wasn't necessary.

It was barely a roof. The structure crouched at the base of a cutbank like something that had crawled there to die: peeled-log walls gone silver with weather, a sod roof sprouting grass, one window shuttered with a piece of flattened kerosene tin. A claim post leaned against the door frame at a drunk angle, and even in the rain Ives could read the faded stencil on it — *E.V., Survey 44-C* — before Marchetti's shoulder hit the door and it gave with a groan of swollen wood.

Inside, the air was dry and close and old. Dust, pipe smoke soaked into the logs over years, and under it something faintly medicinal — camphor, maybe, or the ghost of a liniment a man had rubbed into his shoulders every night for a decade. Ives stood in the doorway with her service lamp raised and let herself have three full seconds of being out of the rain, which was three seconds longer than she'd allowed herself since pulling Marchetti out of the white water two days ago.

"Light's bad for the window," Okafor said from outside. "I'll turn the boat."

She heard him work the skiff upstream along the bank, unhurried, competent, a man who had done this in worse storms than this one and would do it again without comment. Then she was alone in the shanty with Marchetti and whatever the place wanted to tell her.

He was already moving through it like a man revisiting a room he'd dreamed about. His hand went to the stove first — a squat cast-iron thing, cold — then to a shelf where three tin plates sat stacked with a precision that said the owner had been a tidy man right up until he stopped coming back. A bunk against the far wall, blankets folded. A chair with a woven seat, mended twice with wire. On the wall above the bunk, pinned by rusted nails, were maps. Not charts — maps. Hand-drawn, ink on butcher paper, the river rendered in a confident looping line with depths penciled in the margins and little crosses marking gravel bars.

"Voss," Marchetti said quietly. It wasn't a question. He'd told her about the dying prospector in the flophouse, the oilskin packet pressed into his hands, the promise extracted between fever and gunshot. He'd told her the way guilty men tell stories — too fast, watching her face, waiting for the word *accomplice* to land.

Ives hadn't given him the word yet. She hadn't given him anything except handcuffs, officially, and a set of orders she'd written herself and signed herself and could unwrite whenever she decided to.

"You knew about this place," she said.

"I knew there was a claim shanty somewhere on the upper sloughs. The map shows it." He tapped his chest, where the oilskin packet rode inside his jacket like a second heartbeat. "I didn't know if it would still be standing."

"Things Meridian wants forgotten tend to keep standing," Ives said. "They just get lonely."

Marchetti looked at her then — really looked, the way he'd been doing since the rapids, trying to price her. She let him. She'd spent eleven years in the navy letting people try to price her; it was useful to know what they thought she cost.

"Sit down, Lieutenant," he said finally. "Before you fall down."

She almost refused on principle. Then her knees filed a formal objection, and she sat on the mended chair while Marchetti knelt at the cold stove and coaxed a fire out of split cedar shakes stacked under the eave outside. The rain hammered the sod roof with a sound like applause from a very large, very indifferent audience.

* * *

The journals were in the stove.

Not hidden — stored. When the fire caught and threw real light into the shanty's corners, Ives saw what she'd missed in the dark: the stove's ash pan had been emptied and lined with waxed cloth, and inside it lay four ledger books wrapped in oilcloth, their spines cracked, their covers furred with damp that hadn't quite gotten in. Beside them, a canvas ammunition pouch, empty of anything but a folding knife and a pocket watch that had stopped at some hour nobody would ever need to know.

Marchetti saw them at the same moment she did. Neither of them moved for a moment. The fire ticked. Rain applauded.

"You're the investigator," Marchetti said. "Technically, everything in this room is yours."

"Technically," Ives said, "everything in this room is evidence in a salvage case I invented to keep you out of a cell." She reached for the topmost journal. Her hands were steady. She made sure of it first.

The cover read, in pencil, in a hand that started strong and got weaker across the years: *E. VOSS — RIVER BOOK — DO NOT LOSE.*

She opened it to the first page and began to read aloud, because some things needed to be heard in a voice to be believed.

*"April 9th. Struck color in the bar below the split channel at last — coarse gold, ragged, not river-worn. This is not placer. This is spill from a source. Danny says stop dreaming, but Danny has said that about everything since '09 and been wrong about everything since '11."*

Danny. She turned pages. Danny acquired a surname — Okonkwo — and a history, and a claim stake recorded in Voss's careful hand with coordinates laid out to the second of arc, witnessed by a river pilot named Sef Okafor's father, whose signature Ives recognized because she'd seen it an hour ago on the boat's registration papers, written in the same hand that had taught that same pilot to read water.

*"May 2nd. Assayed the source samples. If the seam runs half of what the spring wash suggests, this is the richest strike in the territory and everyone we know is standing on top of it without knowing."*

Then the entries changed.

*"June 14th. Men in company clothes came upriver asking after survey work. Told them nothing. Danny talked. Danny always talks."*

*"June 30th. A man named Crane bought drinks for the whole camp at Fort Alder. Friendly as a dog. Asked me twice where my samples came from. Twice is once too many."*

*"July 19th. Filed our claim with the land office. Witnessed, sealed, paid. Nobody can move us now. Danny laughed at me for paying the sealing fee. We'll see who laughs."*

Ives turned the page and stopped reading aloud.

The next entry was dated five weeks later, and the handwriting had changed — not degraded, changed, pressed harder into the paper, each letter stabbed rather than written.

*"September 3rd. The resurvey came through today. New coordinates. New datum. Our claim ground now falls outside the concession boundary by four hundred meters — exactly four hundred, as if measured by a man with a ruler and no soul. The land office clerk wouldn't meet my eye. He kept looking at the door. There was a car outside the land office. I have seen that car before."*

*"September 4th. Went to the new survey office to protest. A young woman countersigned the resurvey documents. Voss, her nameplate said. MARA VOSS. I asked her if she was any relation. She said her father was dead to her. She said it like she was quoting someone."*

Ives looked up. Marchetti was sitting very still on the bunk, watching her read, and his face had the expression of a man watching a fuse burn toward something he couldn't defuse.

"She didn't know," he said. "You can't know that from a journal—"

"I can know that she countersigned it," Ives said. "That's not opinion. That's a signature on a document that stole this man's claim." She turned back to the page. "What I can also know is what happened next."

*"September 20th. Danny went down to Fort Alder to file the appeal papers. He never came back. They say he drowned at the landing. Danny couldn't swim a stroke but he'd worked that landing twenty years and never wet his boots. They say he drowned. I say they should dig up whoever they buried."*

*"October 1st. Letter under my door tonight. No name. It said: THE RIVER TAKES CARE OF ITS OWN. IT CAN TAKE YOU TOO. I have put my books where no one will look for them. If anything happens to me, whoever finds this — the coordinates are true. The resurvey is a lie. Make them look at the spring."*

There were no more entries. The remaining pages held only pencil sketches — the split channel, the sinkhole country above the canyon, a cave mouth drawn with a care that bordered on devotion — and, on the final leaf, a single line added in a hand so shaky it might have been written yesterday:

*If you are reading this, I am sorry I am not there to buy you a drink.*

Ives closed the book. For a long moment the only sound was rain and fire, and the small human noise of Marchetti breathing.

* * *

She took the journals outside under the eave, out of Marchetti's hearing — though she suspected hearing wasn't the issue; the man had the stillness of a diver, and divers heard everything. She sat on the woodpile with her lamp hooded and read the middle volumes straight through, entry by entry, year by year, and what she built in her head was not a salvage case. It was a spine.

Here was the original strike, documented, assayed, witnessed, sealed. Here was the fraudulent resurvey, its motive (four hundred meters, measured with a ruler and no soul), its mechanism (a land office clerk afraid of a car), its instrument (a countersignature from a daughter who'd been taught her father was dead). Here was Danny Okonkwo, drowned at a landing he'd worked for twenty years. Here was a threat letter in a hand she'd bet her pension matched the correspondence in Meridian Consolidated's own files — files she'd requisitioned three weeks ago and been denied, denied formally, by an admiral whose brother-in-law sat on Meridian's board.

And here — she flipped to the last volume, the thinnest one, covering the most recent eighteen months — here was Elias Voss writing less and less, and then writing things like *"Crane's men asked at the trading post whether I still kept my river books"* and *"moved the books again"* and, in the final entry, dated six weeks ago: *"A young diver has been hired to strip the survey wreck. Word travels. If he is honest, he will find what I found. If he is honest, God help him."*

Ives sat with that for a long time.

God help him. The old man had watched the navy seal the delta, watched the salvage permits get issued through channels that shouldn't have issued them, watched a stranger walk into the current — and had died betting on that stranger's honesty, in a flophouse, with a bullet in him, pressing a map into his hands.

And she had pulled that stranger out of the water and arrested him.

Not arrested, she corrected herself. Detained pending investigation. Her own paperwork, her own fiction, maintained with a straight face while she decided what he was. Evidence. Accomplice. Or the only honest man in a story that had been eating honest men for thirty years.

The rain slackened. Through the shanty's one window she could see Marchetti's silhouette moving inside — feeding the stove, checking the door bar, doing the small domestic work of staying alive. Once he paused at the wall of hand-drawn maps, and even silhouetted she could read the gesture: head tilted, finger tracing the river's loop, a man learning a dead friend's handwriting.

Her orders said to bring him in. Her orders said the wreck was a navigational hazard to be cleared, the salvage legal, the inquiry closed. Her orders had been drafted by the same machinery that had denied her the Meridian files, and she had known it when she signed them, and she had signed them anyway because a lieutenant commander who refuses orders stops being a lieutenant commander and starts being a problem, and problems get handled the way Danny Okonkwo had been handled — quietly, at a landing, with a story attached.

But orders were not the oath. She'd recited the oath at nineteen with a chaplain's hand on her shoulder, and the words had been about the Constitution, not the chain of command, and she had been too young to notice the difference and too stubborn, apparently, to stay ignorant of it forever.

Chain of command. She turned the phrase over like a stone. A chain was what you put on a thing to hold it. It was also what you put on a thing to drag it.

Inside, Marchetti had stopped moving. She realized he was standing at the bunk, and that the bunk's blanket had been folded back, and that beneath it — visible now in the stove light through the window, visible because he'd lifted it — lay a second bundle. Small. Oilcloth again. He was looking at it the way men look at graves.

She came back inside dripping, and he didn't startle, which she noted and filed.

"There's more," he said.

"There's always more. That's what makes it a conspiracy instead of a crime."

He unwrapped it on the blanket. Inside was a photograph, foxed and curling: two men on a gravel bar, arms around each other's shoulders, laughing at the camera. One was unmistakably the younger Elias Voss — the jaw, the stubborn set of the shoulders. The other was a big man with a grin like a broken fence, and on the back, in pencil: *Me & Danny, the day we struck it. Before.*

Beside the photograph lay a child's drawing, crayon on butcher paper — a river, a stick-figure girl, a smaller figure labeled *PAPA* in a wobbly adult hand guiding the crayon. Someone had drawn this together with a daughter, long ago, before the daughter learned her father was dead to her.

And beneath both, a letter, sealed, addressed in that weakening hand: *MARA.*

Marchetti looked at it and did not touch it.

"That's hers," he said. "Whatever it says. I've carried it this far and I'm not opening it." He glanced up at her, and for the first time since the rapids there was no calculation in his face at all — just exhaustion and a kind of battered decency that she was beginning to suspect was genuine, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "So. Lieutenant. You've read the books. You know what I'm carrying and why. Question is whether you're taking me downstream in irons or helping me finish the run. Permits expire in four days. Mine, yours, everybody's. After that, Meridian owns the whole river on paper and none of this—" he touched the journals "—matters ever again."

Ives stood in the doorway of a dead man's house, holding a dead man's truth, and felt the two halves of her life — the officer and the oath — pull apart like a channel splitting around a snag.

Four days. Crane behind them with gunboats. A canyon ahead with God knew what sealed inside it. And a letter with a daughter's name on it, waiting in an oilskin wrap, patient as the river.

She picked up the final journal, the thin one, and tucked it inside her coat, against her own heartbeat, where evidence belonged.

"We leave before first light," she said. "And Marchetti — if I find one thing in these books that puts you beside the bullet in Elias Voss, I will drown you myself and write it up as the river's work."

He almost smiled. "Fair terms."

Outside, the rain stopped altogether, and in the sudden silence they both heard it: far downstream, faint but unmistakable, the low diesel knock of a boat running dark against the current.

More than one boat.

Okafor appeared in the doorway without ceremony, rain streaming off him, and said the longest sentence Ives had heard from him yet: "They found our trail. We go now, or we don't go."
