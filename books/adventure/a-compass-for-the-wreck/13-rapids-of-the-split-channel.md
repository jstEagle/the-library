## 13. Rapids of the Split Channel

The river tore itself in two a half mile below the last bend, and Theo saw it before Sef said anything — a long gray tongue of gravel bar splitting the current like a blade, the water dividing into two channels that ran screaming away from each other into the mist.

"Left or right," Rennie used to say when they faced water like this. *There's no third option, Theo. The river doesn't do maybe.*

Rennie was ash somewhere downstream, or smoke, or nothing at all, and the thought came with its usual cold weight and then passed, because there was no room in Theo's chest for it now. Not with the engine hammering. Not with the sound behind them.

He turned. Through the thinning fog, maybe six hundred meters back, the first gunboat had come around the bend — a black, low-slung hull with a mounted lamp that swept the water in long white blades. Behind it, a second. Crane hadn't even bothered to disguise them. He wanted them to see what was coming. Fear was cheaper than bullets, and Crane was a man who counted everything.

"Left channel's wider," Theo called over the engine noise.

Sef didn't answer. He was standing at the bow with one hand on the rail, head cocked, listening — actually listening — to the river the way another man might listen to a conversation in a language only he spoke. His eyes moved across the water the way Theo's moved across a wreck's hull: reading damage, reading history, reading what the surface refused to say.

"Sef. Left or right?"

"Left is wider." Sef's voice was flat as slate. "Left is also dead. Look at the foam line."

Theo looked. The left channel ran broad and deceptively smooth for the first two hundred meters, and then the water simply *ended* — dropped away into a churn of white that no boat came back from. A falls, or something like one, hidden by the mist until you were committed. The foam didn't drift. It circled. Water that circulated instead of moving downstream was water over a hole, and holes kept what they caught.

"Right, then."

"Right is narrow. Right has teeth." Sef crouched, pulled Voss's map from the oilskin sleeve where it lived now, wrapped in waxed cloth inside Theo's jacket when they weren't studying it. But Sef didn't need the map. "Right channel — my grandfather called it the Throat. He said the old people ran it empty, at first light, with a man on the bow and everyone else holding rope. Nobody ran it loaded."

Theo looked at their boat: low in the water, stuffed with fuel drums, canned food, the diving gear he couldn't make himself abandon, rope, tarps, the whole fragile apparatus of their survival. Everything they had was in this hull.

"Nobody ran it loaded," Sef repeated. "So we don't run it loaded."

"There's no time to portage."

"I didn't say portage." Sef rolled the map and handed it back. "I said we don't run it loaded. We run it *light*. We put what we can't lose in the bow, lash everything else to the frame so it can tear free, and we accept that the river takes its tax."

Behind them, the lead gunboat's lamp found them — a white slap of light across the water, gone, then back, steadying. A voice came across the fog through a megaphone, distorted and cheerful, the voice of a man enjoying his work.

"*You're entering closed water! Heave to or we'll disable your vessel!*"

"Disable," Theo said. "He means shoot the engine."

"He means shoot *us*," Sef said, and took the wheel.

* * *

They worked fast, the way men work when the alternative is dying slowly. Sef killed the engine and they drifted in an eddy behind the gravel bar while they stripped the boat — not of everything, but of everything that could kill them by being aboard. The fuel drums went over the side, lashed to a single line with a quick-release knot Sef tied in the dark with fingers that didn't hurry. The food crates went into the bow, wrapped in tarps, lashed high. The dive gear — Theo's hands stopped on it, and for one stupid second he thought about the wreck, about the instruments in his cabin, the brass and glass that had started all of this, the proof sitting in a waterproof case that was itself a liability now, weight where they needed none.

He kept it. He told himself it was evidence, and that was true, but he also knew it was something else: he had not come this far, lost what he'd lost, to let the river have the last word on what survived.

"Marchetti." Sef was watching him. "Whatever you're keeping — if it's between that and the boat, the boat wins."

"I know."

"Say it."

"I know, Sef." He lashed the case flat under the bow thwart, low, where water would pass over it and keep going.

Ives sat amidships with her service pistol wrapped in oilcloth and her expression doing the thing it did — that careful, professional blankness that Theo was learning to read the way Sef read water. She'd taken off her uniform jacket somewhere in the last two days. It was folded under the food crates, and Theo understood that she'd folded it away the way you fold away a version of yourself you're not sure you'll need again.

"The Throat," she said. "You've run it?"

"I've *seen* it," Sef said. "Twice. From the rocks. Once, when I was fourteen, I watched a logging crew try it with a loaded barge." He paused. "There were four men. We found three."

"And the fourth?"

"The river kept him." Sef said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather. "My grandfather said the Throat is the river's mouth, and a mouth has to be fed. He believed you could talk to it. Pay it. I don't believe that." He looked down the channel, where the mist moved in slow white columns. "But I don't run it without saying something anyway. It costs nothing to be polite."

The gunboats were closing. Theo could hear the difference in their engine note now — they'd stopped being cautious, stopped weaving through the fog banks, and were coming straight up the middle of the river with the arrogance of machines that assumed nothing natural could stop them. The lead boat's lamp pinned them again, and this time it stayed.

"They'll follow us in," Ives said.

"They'll try," Sef said. "Those boats draw too much for the right channel. They'll have to take the left." He smiled, and it was the first time Theo had seen him smile, and it was not a comforting sight. "The left channel is the river's mouth too. It just doesn't tell you before it bites."

* * *

They came into the Throat at speed, Sef at the wheel with his whole body leaning into the turn like a man leaning into a wind, and the first three hundred meters were almost kind — fast, narrow, loud, the walls of the gorge closing in on both sides with wet black stone that threw the engine noise back at them doubled and tripled until it felt like being shouted at by the whole river at once.

Then the water stood up.

Theo had run rapids before. He'd shot the Murchison in flood, ridden a flooded creek in Borneo that the pilot swore was a shortcut, and he thought he knew the vocabulary of big water — the standing waves, the holes, the seams where two currents argued. The Throat did not speak that language. The Throat was a single sentence, and the sentence was *no*.

The channel narrowed to less than twice the boat's width, and the water in it was moving faster than anything he'd ever been on — not white so much as *green*, deep and glassy and terrifying, with a ridge of standing waves down the middle like the spine of some animal swimming just under the surface. Sef didn't fight the current. That was the thing Theo would remember afterward, the thing he'd think about for years: Sef didn't fight it. He *agreed* with it, constantly, adjusting the wheel in small fast corrections, giving the river its head and then taking it back a degree at a time, threading the boat along a seam that existed for maybe two seconds at a time before it closed and another opened.

"LEFT!" Sef roared, and the boat went left, and a rock the size of a house slid past Theo's shoulder close enough that the backwash off it slapped his face.

The first loss came fast. The quick-release knot did its work — a wave took the boat broadside for one horrible half-second, the gunwale dipping until the river was *in* the boat, and Sef pulled the release and the fuel drums tore away downstream, dragging the lashings with them, and the boat came light and jumped like a released animal. Theo heard Ives swear — a single word, precise, almost academic — and then they were through the first gate and into the second, and the second was worse.

The channel dog-legged. There was no way to see it coming; the water simply bent around a stone spur, and beyond the bend the river *dropped* — not a falls, but a long steep slide, three hundred meters of accelerated chaos, waves stacked on waves, and somewhere in the middle of it a hole that Sef had been watching for, calling it under his breath in a language Theo didn't know, the way a man counts fence posts.

"HOLD ON TO SOMETHING AND DON'T LET GO NO MATTER WHAT I DO."

The hole took them at the bottom of the slide. Theo felt the bow pitch down into water that wasn't there — a trough, a pit, a moment of free fall — and then the whole boat slammed into the back of the standing wave below it and stopped. Not slowed. *Stopped*, as if the river had picked them up and set them down on a shelf, and for two full seconds they sat in the trough with the water standing on both sides of them like walls, and Theo understood with perfect clarity that this was the moment the logging crew's fourth man had met, and that the difference between this moment and a grave was about four knots of forward speed.

Sef gave the boat everything. The engine screamed, the propeller chewed aerated water that gave almost no grip, and the boat crept — *crept* — up the face of the wave behind them, and for a moment the bow pointed at the sky and Theo was looking straight up into fog, and then the hull came over the crest and they were through, and the river, having tasted them, spat them out and let them go.

* * *

The lower Throat ran out into a wide, braided floodplain, and the boat came down the last of it half-full of water and missing things.

Theo took inventory the way he'd take inventory of a flooded hold, mechanically, because the mechanical was the only thing keeping his hands from shaking. The food crates: one gone, torn from the bow lashings, along with half the tarps. The rope: gone. The spare propeller: gone. The dive gear case: still lashed under the thwart, and when he hauled it up he heard the instruments shift inside with a sound like teeth in a jaw, and he set it down without opening it, not yet, not with Sef watching.

Ives was bailing with a bucket, methodical, her sleeves rolled, a bruise coming up on her forearm where she'd been thrown against the thwart. She caught him looking.

"Log it as a training exercise," she said.

They drifted the floodplain in the gray light, wrung out and lighter, and Theo turned and looked back upriver.

The Throat was invisible from here — just mist and the sound of it, a long continuous roar like distant traffic. But the gunboats weren't in the sound. He waited. Sef let the boat drift in an eddy and they all three listened, and the roar went on and on, and then, underneath it, faintly, a different sound: engines, laboring, and a shout, thin with distance, and then the unmistakable crack of something heavy hitting rock, once, and then a silence where an engine had been.

"The left channel," Theo said.

"Or they tried the Throat after all." Sef's voice held no satisfaction, only a kind of grim accounting. "Either way, they've lost a boat. They won't quit. Crane doesn't quit — that's not how men like him are built. But they'll have to go back, or portage, or wait for the river to give their boat back." He looked up at the sky, reading the light. "We've bought a day. Maybe two."

"Two days to the headwater," Theo said. "And four days on the permit."

"Then we don't stop," Sef said, "and we don't sleep more than the river allows."

Ives came forward and sat on the thwart across from Theo, and for a while none of them said anything. The fog was burning off. The floodplain opened ahead of them, the river gathering itself back into a single channel, and the light came up gold and ordinary, as if the world hadn't just tried to kill them and half-succeeded.

Theo opened the dive case. The instruments were intact — the theodolite, the log books in their wax sleeves, the brass fittings green with river rot. He touched the log book with the falsified coordinates, Voss's map folded inside it now like a splint holding a broken bone straight, and he thought about the fourth man of the logging crew, the one the river kept, and about Rennie, and about the bones they hadn't found yet but that he was beginning to understand were waiting for them upriver, because rivers like this one kept everything, and everything kept had a price.

"Your grandfather," he said to Sef. "The man who talked to the river. What did he say you pay it with?"

Sef was quiet for a long moment. He put his hand flat on the thwart, on the wet wood, the way a man touches a table when he's deciding whether to tell the truth.

"Not today," he said. "Today we go. But there's a place ahead — my family's water, what's left of it. When we get there, I'll tell you what Meridian took, and what I took for letting them." He turned the wheel and brought the boat out of the eddy and into the current, and the river took them. "You should know who's carrying you before we get where we're going."

The channel narrowed ahead into a gorge the map marked with a single word in Voss's cramped hand — a word Theo had taken for a place name, and was beginning to suspect was a warning — and the first gunboat, the one they'd thought was dead, came around the bend behind them with a hole in its hull and its lamp still burning.
