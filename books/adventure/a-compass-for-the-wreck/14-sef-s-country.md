## 14. Sef's Country

The river went quiet an hour after the rapids, the way it always did, as if it were ashamed of what it had done and wanted to be forgiven. Sef Okafor sat at the stern with one hand on the tiller and let the boat find its own way through the slack water, because this stretch — the long bend below the drowned cypress, where the current spread out thin and brown over a shelf of red clay — he could have run blindfolded. He had learned it before he learned to read.

Behind him, Marchetti slept against the gear crates, one arm thrown over his eyes, his hands still white at the knuckles even in sleep. The lieutenant dozed sitting up, which Sef found interesting. Navy men didn't usually sleep sitting up unless they'd learned somewhere that lying down was a mistake you only got to make once. He'd have to ask her about that someday, if they all lived long enough for conversation to become a luxury again.

For now there was just the river, and the smell of it, and the thing he had been carrying in his chest since the night Marchetti had counted out his fee in a back room that smelled of diesel and dried fish.

He had not told them. Not really told them. They knew he was a pilot; they knew he took money and asked no questions; they knew he could read water the way priests read scripture. What they did not know was why a man with his hands would sell those hands so cheaply to strangers running from a mining company, and why he never smiled when the work went well.

Sef watched the clay banks slide past and decided that today, in this quiet, he would tell them. Not because they deserved it. Because the river deserved it, and because a debt carried too long starts to rot the man carrying it.

* * *

He had been seven years old the first time his father put a hand-line in his fist and said, *feel it, don't watch it.* The line was braided from his grandmother's hair and cotton, three strands each, and it hummed against his fingers like something alive. They were fishing the oxbow behind Kessuma Landing, the village where his mother had been born and her mother before her, a scatter of stilt houses and smokehouses tucked into a loop of the river so old the maps had stopped bothering to draw it correctly.

The oxbow was the richest water Sef would ever know. It bred fish the way a good woman breeds rumors: constantly, generously, without much effort. Bream thick as dinner plates. Catfish the length of a man's leg. In the flooded season the whole bowl of it filled with young fish sheltering in the drowned grass, and every family on the landing ate until the smokehouses overflowed, and the smell of that curing fish — sweet, heavy, a little like caramel gone to earth — was the smell of Sef's entire childhood.

His father was a patient man with hard hands and a laugh that came out of him sideways, like water finding a crack. He taught Sef the channels by singing them. There was a song for every reach of the river between the landing and the headwater canyons, verses that meant *deep here* and *snag below* and *the bank is eating itself, keep off.* His father had the songs from his own father, who had them from a time before anyone wrote anything down, and Sef learned them the way you learn your own heartbeat — without knowing you were learning, until one day you noticed it had been going on all along.

That was the country. That was Sef's country. Not owned, not deeded, but known — known down to the silt, the way you know the inside of your own mouth.

The dredge came when he was eleven.

* * *

It came upriver in the dry season, riding low and ugly, a steel island with a crane on its spine, flying the colors of Meridian Consolidated. The men aboard wore clean clothes and spoke of the river as if it were a quantity. Gravel, they said. Pay gravel. Alluvial deposit. They anchored above the oxbow and began to eat the bottom of the world.

Sef remembered the sound first. A grinding, wet and endless, like the river being chewed. It ran day and night. You could feel it through the floorboards of the stilt houses, a tremor in everything, in the cooking pots, in your teeth. His grandmother, who was old enough to remember soldiers, said it sounded like war and turned her face to the wall.

Then came the water itself. The dredge chewed the bottom and spat the tailings back out in long gray fans, fine ground rock and silt and whatever else the bottom had been holding onto for ten thousand years. The plume spread downstream like smoke poured into clear glass. Within a month the oxbow ran the color of dishwater. Within two, the bream stopped coming up the connecting channel. The catfish that remained grew listless and strange-tasting, and then some of them began to die, floating belly-up in the gray shallows with their gills flared like small red flowers.

The elders went to talk to the company men. Sef went along because there was nowhere else to go and because he was eleven and furious in the way children are furious, which is total and articulate and completely without power. He remembered standing behind his father's leg and looking up at the company man — a pale, sweating official with a clipboard and a smile that never reached anywhere near his eyes — and hearing him say the words that Sef would carry for the rest of his life:

*Your people will be compensated.*

Compensated. As if the river were a mule that had been shot and could be replaced by another mule. As if thirty years of smokehouse mornings and hand-lines humming in a boy's fists could be weighed out in cash and receipts and be found equivalent.

They took the compensation. Most of them. What else do you do? The fish were dying either way, and children have to eat, and pride is a crop that grows poorly on an empty stomach. Sef's father took it last of all, and he took it the way a man takes medicine he knows won't cure him, and he drank for a year afterward, and the year after that he walked into the flooded timber above the landing during high water and did not come out, and everyone knew what that was even though nobody ever said it.

By then Sef was fourteen, and Meridian's agents had come around again, quieter this time, asking different questions. Who moved on the river. What boats passed. Which pilots would take cargo up past the navy cordon and which ones couldn't be trusted. They paid for answers, and Sef, who had a mother and two younger sisters and a grandmother whose medicine cost more than the whole family earned, sold them what they wanted.

Not information about his own people — he kept that back, always, a small country inside himself they never got to annex. But he told them about other pilots, other boats, other men's business, and he took their money, and he told himself the oldest lie a bought man tells: *it changes nothing. They'll do what they're doing whether I speak or not.*

His silence was bought. That was the truth of it, plain as a dead catfish in gray water. For eleven years he had taken Meridian's coin to keep his eyes closed, and the river had gone on being poisoned, and the oxbow behind Kessuma Landing had gone on being a memory, and every season the gray fan of tailings crept a little further downstream, patient as sin.

* * *

Marchetti woke near midday and climbed forward to sit across from him, working a strip of dried fish between his teeth. The diver's face was hollowed from the rapids, bruised along one side where the water had handled him roughly, but his eyes were steady. Steadier than most men's after nearly drowning.

"Where are we?" Marchetti asked.

"Deadman's Reach." Sef nodded at the drowned cypresses standing hip-deep off the starboard bow, their gray trunks furred with moss. "Another six hours to the split channel, if the water behaves."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we learn something new."

Marchetti almost smiled. Then he looked at Sef properly, the way he sometimes did — a diver's look, weighing, reading pressure — and said, "You've been quiet since the rapids. Quieter than usual."

"I'm going to tell you something," Sef said. "And the lieutenant too, when she wakes. And I want you to understand that I'm not telling you so you'll forgive me. Forgiveness is yours to give or not. I'm telling you because you should know who's piloting your boat."

Marchetti stopped chewing.

So Sef told him. All of it, in a flat voice, watching the water instead of the man — the oxbow, the dredge, the compensation, the father in the flooded timber, the eleven years of bought silence. He told it the way his father had taught him to read channels: by feel, not watching, letting the words come through his hands on the tiller while his eyes stayed on the current.

When he finished, the river made its small noises against the hull, and Marchetti was quiet for a long moment.

"You could have stayed bought," Marchetti said finally. "Crane's people would pay more than I can."

"They would." Sef turned the tiller a degree, easing the boat wide of a submerged claybar that showed itself only as a faint smoothing of the current, a wrinkle in the water's skin that no one born elsewhere would ever see. "But Crane pays me to stay blind. You pay me to see. It's the first money in eleven years that buys my eyes open." He glanced at the sleeping lieutenant, then back. "You understand? This isn't work anymore. This is repayment. To the river. To my father. Every mile upstream is a coin I'm paying back."

"And if we fail?" Marchetti asked. "If Crane takes the source and the map burns and Mara Voss signs nothing?"

Sef considered the question honestly, because the man deserved honesty now that he'd gotten the rest.

"Then the river loses again," he said. "But it has lost before, and it's still here. Rivers are patient. They outlast companies." He looked down at the brown water sliding beneath the hull, and something in his chest loosened — not joy, nothing so bright, but the feeling of a splinter coming out of a wound. "My grandmother used to say the river keeps accounts. Not court accounts. River accounts. Slow, and exact, and payable in full. Maybe that's what we're doing. Maybe that's what she's doing—" he tipped his chin toward Ives, "—and what the girl with the signature is doing. Not law. Something older than law. The river collecting."

Marchetti looked at him for a long time. "Ives should hear this."

"She should. Wake her."

* * *

Lieutenant Cora Ives woke the way she seemed to do everything — instantly, completely, one hand already finding the pistol under her coat before her eyes finished opening. When she understood where she was and that no one was shooting at her, she listened. She listened the way Sef imagined she did everything else: taking notes behind her eyes, filing things in drawers he couldn't see.

When he finished, she was silent a while, watching the drowned cypress stand slide past.

"What you're describing," she said at last, "is criminal negligence, environmental destruction, and witness tampering spanning a decade. If you gave formal testimony—"

"No," Sef said.

She looked at him.

"If I testify, it goes into a file. Files go into buildings. Buildings belong to governments, and governments sign concessions." He shook his head slowly. "I watched the law come up this river once already, Lieutenant. It came on a dredge with a clipboard. Whatever happens at the headwater, it happens there, at the source, witnessed by the water itself. Afterward — afterward, write whatever you like. Put me in your report. Hang me with the rest of them if that's your justice. But first the river gets its account settled."

Ives studied him for a long moment, and Sef saw something shift behind her eyes — the same shift he'd seen in Marchetti's, the look of a person recalculating what kind of man was holding the tiller. Whether it was approval or alarm, he couldn't say. Perhaps both. Perhaps, he thought, those were the same thing in a navy investigator.

"The affidavit needs witnesses," she said finally. "At the source. That's the legal requirement — execution at the point of the claim, attested." Her mouth twisted slightly, the ghost of irony. "It seems the lawyers and your grandmother agree on something."

"The river keeps accounts," Sef said. "Lawyers just borrow the idea."

Ahead, the channel narrowed and began to braid, the water splitting around a growing archipelago of sandbars and snag heaps, and Sef felt the change in the tiller before he saw it — that faint muttering in the wood, the river beginning to talk in its faster voice. He stood, rolling his shoulders, and pointed with the tiller toward the haze upstream where two channels forked away from each other like the legs of a compass opening.

"There," he said. "The split. My father's song says the left channel eats boats. The right channel eats boats slower." He looked back at Marchetti, and then at the far haze beyond the fork, where the land lifted into the blue shoulders of the canyon country, and lowered his voice as though the river might be listening. "But there's a third way. Barely a way. My father sang it, and his father sang it, and no pilot has run it in twenty years because the entrance drowned in the flood of '09." He paused. "If the gunboats are behind us — and they are — we may need it."

Marchetti followed his gaze, then dug into his jacket and brought out the oilskin packet, unwrapping it with careful hands. Elias Voss's map, ink faded to the color of old tea, covered in the dead prospector's cramped annotations. Marchetti spread it flat on the crate between them and weighted the corners.

Sef leaned over it — and stopped.

There, in the lower corner, in handwriting he hadn't seen in fifteen years but would have known anywhere, in any dark, in any grave: a short sequence of marks along the right-hand fork. Depth notes. Snag warnings. A verse rendered in symbols instead of words.

"That's my father's notation," Sef said quietly. His voice came out strange, scraped thin. "That's the smugglers' channel. Voss ran it with my father. Before the dredge. Before everything." He touched the marks with one finger, barely, the way you touch a photograph of the dead. "Elias Voss learned this river from the same man who taught me."

Marchetti looked up at him. Behind them, far downstream, faint as a thought, came the sound Sef had been dreading since dawn — engines, more than one, running hard against the current.

"Well then," Sef said, and pushed the tiller hard over toward the right-hand fork, toward the drowned entrance and the old song. "Let's go pay our respects."
