## 15. The Turncoat's Bargain

Mara Voss had signed her name eleven thousand times in nine years, and she had never once thought of it as a weapon.

That was the arithmetic she could not stop running, hunched in the stern of the borrowed skiff as it ground up the last miles of overland trail toward the headwater country. Eleven thousand signatures. Every one of them a small, clean act of authorship — the confident loop of the M, the Voss tail on the final s that her father had taught her when she was six years old, tracing letters in the mud of a claim shanty floor while he laughed at how seriously she took it. *A signature is a promise, little bird. It's the only thing a man owns outright.* She had built a career on that promise. She had built it on his grave, though she hadn't known it was a grave until four days ago.

Now her hand ached from the copying.

She had done it in the records annex at the concession office, in the dead hour after the night clerk made his rounds and before the dawn shift arrived — three hours with the permit files spread under a single green lamp, her fountain pen moving in the old shorthand her father had drilled into her, the surveyor's cipher that wasn't taught in any licensing course because it wasn't a surveyor's cipher at all. It was a prospector's. Elias Voss had invented it to keep his strike locations out of the mouths of drunken partners, and he had taught it to his daughter the way other fathers taught prayers. She had thought the knowledge dead weight for twenty years. Instead it had been waiting for her like a loaded rifle in a closet.

The files told the story in her own handwriting.

That was the part that made her hands shake — not the fraud itself, which she had half-suspected since the night Theo Marchetti looked at her across the survey office and she saw him decide not to trust her, but the fact that the fraudulent resurvey of the upper concession carried her countersignature. Dated. Witnessed. Filed under her professional seal. She remembered the week it happened — three years ago, the Kestrel Basin resurvey, rushed through under a deadline Crane had manufactured out of thin air, the field data arriving already compiled, already reduced, already *corrected* by the Denver office. She had checked the arithmetic. She had not checked the arithmetic's ancestry. The coordinates had come in shifted — she could see it now, plain as a scar, the whole grid rotated seven minutes of arc east — and she had signed the certification because the numbers were internally consistent and because Aldous Crane had been standing behind her chair with coffee and compliments, and because the alternative was to accuse a billion-dollar company of falsifying a federal submission on the word of a feeling.

A feeling. Her father had died of a feeling — of a bullet and a fever in a flophouse, according to the story Marchetti told, holding a map that said the truth had been stolen out from under the Kestrel Basin by seven minutes of arc and one woman's signature.

She had copied everything. The original claim filing — Elias Voss, locators Voss and Hargrave, staked twenty-two years ago, *legally valid to this day*, never contested, never extinguished, simply buried. The resurvey certification with her own name on it. The internal memo from the Denver office marked ROUTINE ADJUSTMENT — FIELD DATA RECONCILIATION, which was a sentence that meant nothing until you laid it against a dead man's map and understood it meant everything. And the chain of title, the beautiful, damning chain: if the original claim was valid, then every permit Meridian had ever pulled for the upper basin was void. Every dredge lease. Every extraction authorization. Eleven thousand signatures, and the whole edifice stood on one of hers.

She could unsign it.

That was the thing she had understood in the green lamplight, the thing that had made her put down the pen and sit very still while the night clerk's boots scraped past the door. A countersignature authenticates; it does not create. The law — the actual, dusty, indifferent law she had sworn her professional oath to and never once tested — allowed a certifying surveyor to file a corrective affidavit repudiating her own certification, *provided the repudiation was executed at the site of the survey and witnessed by disinterested parties of standing.* She had looked up the statute twice to be sure she wasn't dreaming it. The law wanted her to go to the source. The law wanted witnesses. The law, in its ancient, unglamorous way, wanted exactly what her father's map wanted: for someone to stand at the headwater and say *this is where it is, and this is where it isn't, and I am the one who signed the lie.*

So she had copied the files, sealed them in oilcloth, burned the carbon papers in the annex stove along with a season's worth of her own drafts, and walked out of the concession office at dawn with her career in a satchel and nothing else.

She had not told anyone. That was the loneliest arithmetic of all. There was no one to tell. The company owned the town's bank, the town's store, the town's silence. If she breathed a word to the wrong clerk, Crane would know by lunch, and the files would be ash, and she would be ash with them — she had seen what Meridian did to people who asked questions. Marchetti's dive partner. The burned skiff. She had signed permits that covered those waters. She had signed the *search grid* for the missing man, professionally, competently, a woman drawing tidy lines around a hole where a human being used to be.

* * *

She caught them at the mouth of the old portage trail, where the river bent north into the canyon country and the forest came down to the water like a crowd pressing against a rope line.

She had traded her surveyor's boots for a prospector's — bought off a dead man's porch, secondhand, still molded to someone else's feet — and she had walked two days through country she had only ever known as contour lines, which was its own private humiliation and its own private revelation. The map in her head had said *steep, timbered, drainage west*; the map under her boots said *mud to the knee and a silence so complete she could hear her own heart apologizing for it.* She had followed the river the way her father's shorthand had taught her to follow water: on the high side, moving at dawn and dusk, sleeping cold. She was forty-one years old and she had never slept outdoors in her life until three nights ago.

So when she stepped out of the treeline onto the gravel bar and saw the beached skiff, the lean-to of tarped branches, and the tall, hollow-eyed man who came up off the ground with a knife already open — she was too tired to be afraid, and too resolved to be anything but direct.

"Put it away, Mr. Marchetti. If I wanted you dead I'd have let Crane's people find you first."

He didn't put it away. He stood there in the failing light with the river talking behind him and studied her the way he must have studied wrecks — for what was broken, for what was load-bearing, for what would kill him if he trusted it. Behind him, half in the shadow of the lean-to, sat a woman in a navy greatcoat with a lieutenant's insignia and a rifle across her knees: Ives. The investigator. Mara had read the incident file on her too. Everyone in the concession country was a file to her now; it was the only way she knew how to see.

"You walked here," Marchetti said. Not a question. He was looking at her boots, at the mud to her thigh, at the satchel strap cut into her shoulder.

"Three days. There's a trail along the west bench that doesn't show from the water. My father used it." She heard herself say *my father* out loud for the first time in twenty years and was surprised it didn't cost more. "I need to talk to you. Both of you. And then I need you to take me upriver, because what I'm carrying is worthless anywhere else in the world."

Ives hadn't moved, but her thumb had come off the rifle's safety in a way that managed to be both threatening and conversational. "You're Meridian's senior land surveyor," the lieutenant said. "Your name is on the fraudulent certification. You understand how that reads from where I'm sitting."

"From where you're sitting it reads like a trap." Mara set the satchel down on the gravel between them, slowly, and stepped back from it with her empty hands visible. "So don't sit there. Look inside."

Marchetti crouched, knife still open, and worked the oilcloth ties one-handed. The lamplight — a shielded candle, the kind of light that made everything look like evidence — fell across the copied pages. Mara watched his face while he read. She had spent nine years watching men read her certifications, and she knew every weather a face could make: the bored nod, the flattered frown, the quick arithmetic of what a signature was worth. Marchetti's face did none of those things. He read the original claim filing and his jaw tightened. He read the resurvey with her name on it and went very still. He read the Denver memo and looked up at her with an expression she had never been on the receiving end of before, because it wasn't suspicion.

It was recognition. The look of a man finding the missing instrument.

"The countersignature," he said. "You can revoke it."

"I can file a corrective affidavit. Repudiating my certification, restating the original coordinates, attesting the fraud." She recited it the way she'd rehearsed it on the trail, to the trees, to the dark, because saying it to another person made it real and she needed it real. "But the statute requires execution at the surveyed site. At the source itself. And it requires two witnesses of standing — not employees, not claimants, not me. Disinterested parties." She looked at Ives. "A federal investigator with an open case file would qualify. So would a licensed riverman with no interest in the concession. I'm told you have a pilot."

Ives's face did something complicated — the war between the badge and the thing underneath it that Mara had already guessed at from the incident files, the thing that suspected murder and hated its own suspicion. "You're asking me to witness the destruction of the company I'm technically contracted to support."

"I'm asking you to witness the truth at the only place the law says it can be spoken. What you do about it afterward is between you and your oath."

Marchetti stood up out of his crouch. He had closed the knife at some point; she hadn't seen when. He looked at her for a long moment, and then he said, quietly, "Your father made me promise to deliver his map to his daughter. I've been carrying it up this river trying to figure out how to keep that promise to a woman whose signature is on the thing that killed him."

"And now you've met her," Mara said. "And you can see I didn't know. And you can see that doesn't matter, because the signature is mine regardless." She held out her hand, palm up. "I burned my career to get here, Mr. Marchetti. I have no job, no license that survives this, and probably no life in that town again. All I have is the one thing Crane can't forge: my name, given back. Give me the map. Let me read my father's hand. And then let's get to the headwater before Crane figures out what I've done — because he will, and when he does, he won't send lawyers."

Marchetti looked at Ives. Ives looked at the river, north, toward the canyon country and whatever was waiting there. The candle guttered. Somewhere upstream, faint under the water-talk, came a sound that none of them wanted to name: a low, rhythmic thudding, like a pump, like a dredge, like a fist knocking on a door at the head of the world.

Mara kept her hand out.

"The map," she said. "Please. It's the only inheritance I have, and I intend to spend every cent of it."
