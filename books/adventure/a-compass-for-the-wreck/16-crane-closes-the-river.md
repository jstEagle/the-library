## 16. Crane Closes the River

The message came by pigeon, which was the first wrong thing.

Nobody used pigeons on the upper river anymore — the runs were too short, the birds too easily shot out of the air by anyone with a grudge and a twelve-gauge. But there it was at dawn, sitting on the rail of Sef's moored barge like something that had died and been propped up as a warning: a gray bird with a paper tube strapped to its leg, its eye already clouding, stiff when Theo lifted it. It had flown through the night to die at its destination. He supposed he had to respect that.

The note inside was written in a clerk's careful hand, the kind of penmanship that belonged behind a desk in a company town:

*MR. MARCHETTI — YOUR PILOT IS OUR GUEST AT THE HEADWATER STATION. HE IS COMFORTABLE AND WOULD LIKE TO REMAIN SO. YOU ARE INVITED TO DISCUSS TERMS. BRING THE CHART AND THE SURVEYOR. ALDOUS CRANE.*

Theo read it three times while the mist burned off the water and the river ran silver and indifferent past the hull. Then he read it a fourth time, because his hands had started to shake and he wanted to be sure the shaking wasn't changing the words.

Sef. They had Sef.

He thought of the taciturn pilot's face — that broad, unreadable calm, the way he read water the way other men read scripture. Sef had grown up on this river. Sef had confessed it only days ago, kneeling over the chart in the lamplight of the old claim shanty, telling them about the dredge spoils that had killed the fishing grounds, about the money Meridian had paid for his silence, about how this run upstream was the first thing he'd done in twenty years that felt like repayment instead of surrender. And now the company had him, and the note called him *our guest*, and Theo knew exactly what kind of hospitality Aldous Crane provided. He'd sat at Crane's table once. He'd seen the porcelain and the crystal and the way Crane's hand rested near the decanter, and he'd understood that everything soft in that room was a surface laid over something with edges.

Mara came down the bank a few minutes later, boots wet with dew, the copied permit files wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like an infant. She'd taken to sleeping in her clothes. They all had. She saw the dead pigeon, then Theo's face, and stopped walking.

"Tell me," she said.

He handed her the note. He watched her read it — watched the color leave her face in stages, the way dawn leaves a mountain, top down. Her jaw tightened around something she didn't say. When she looked up, her eyes were dry and hard and frightened all at once, which was, Theo was learning, simply what courage looked like on Mara Voss. Not the absence of fear. Fear, held very still.

"He'll kill him," she said. "Not quickly. Not dramatically. He'll just — stop feeding him, stop letting anyone see him, and eventually there'll be a story about a drowning."

"I know."

"Then we can't go." She said it fast, the way you pull a bandage. "If we go, we give him everything. The map, my signature, the affidavit — the whole case dies at that station and so do we, probably, because dead witnesses don't countersign anything twice. If we *don't* go—"

"Sef dies anyway."

The words came out of Theo flat and final, and they hung between them over the water. Mara opened her mouth, closed it. She turned and looked upriver, toward the headwater canyon, invisible from here but present in the sound of it — a low, constant grinding where the whole river funneled through rock, the voice of the place they'd been trying to reach for eleven days.

"The permits expire in four days," she said quietly. "And now the canyon's closed."

"And now the canyon's closed," Theo agreed.

* * *

They spent the morning taking inventory of their ruin, because that was the only honest thing left to do.

It hadn't been a traitor among the outfitters, exactly — or rather, it had, but not the way Theo had imagined treachery. Treason, it turned out, rarely wore a black hat. It wore an apron and sold dried beans. The outfitter at the last supply post before the canyon country, a round, friendly woman named Doss who'd charged them fair prices and asked no questions, had also kept a ledger of every party that passed through heading upriver, and every ledger entry had a duplicate page that went into a locked box, and every locked box had a man from Meridian who paid for the copies. Theo pieced it together from the details: Crane had known their route, known their schedule, known they'd bought lamp oil and rope in quantities that suggested cave work — which meant Crane knew about the sinkhole system, or at least suspected it, which meant the second layer of Voss's map, the smuggler routes, might already be worthless. You couldn't sneak through a door someone had thought to lock.

So Crane hadn't chased them upriver after all. He'd simply gone ahead, arrived first at the throat of the canyon with hired men and gunboats and a week's head start, and shut the door behind himself. The rapids of the split channel had cost them most of their supplies; the closure of the canyon had cost them the river itself. Between those two losses, Theo calculated, they had maybe enough food for six days, ammunition for one bad afternoon, and a legal case that required them to stand at the source within four days or lose the window entirely — permits expiring, the resurvey locking into permanence, Voss's claim dissolving back into Meridian's paperwork like sugar into hot water.

"We could go overland," Mara said. They were crouched over the map in the shanty's single room, the good lamplight pooled on the oilskin. "South rim. There are trails — my father marked some of them, the ones he used before the road went in—"

"It's ninety miles of ridge country with no water," Theo said. "We'd take eight days if nothing went wrong. Something would go wrong."

"Then the caves."

"Crane knows about the caves. Or he suspects. Either way, he'll have men at every entrance he can find, and the entrances he can't find—" Theo tapped the map, at the blank spaces where Voss's ink gave way to guesswork, "—we can't find either. Not in four days. Not without Sef."

That was the worst of it, worse than the hunger arithmetic, worse than the guns. Sef had been more than a pilot. He'd been the river's own memory — he knew which channels lied, which gravel bars shifted overnight, which side of the fog held the deep water. Without him they weren't a crew. They were two people standing on a raft, holding a piece of paper that promised them a place they couldn't reach.

Mara sat back on her heels. In the lamplight her father's features showed plainly in her face for a moment — the same stubborn set of the jaw, the same way of going still when the world went wrong. Elias Voss had gone still like that too, Theo remembered, in the flophouse, with a bullet in him and fever climbing, pressing the oilskin map into a stranger's hands because there was no one else. Stubbornness running down through the blood like a vein of ore.

"There's a third option," she said.

"Name it."

"We give him what he asked for." She met Theo's eyes. "Partially. We go to the station. We bring the map. I bring papers — real ones, countersigned, looking legitimate. And we trade. Sef for the map. His life for the document. Then we find another way to the source, or we file from downstream, or we—" She faltered. "Or we lose, but Sef lives, and my father's killer doesn't get two bodies instead of one."

"You'd sign away your father's claim."

"I'd sign away a *copy*," Mara said. "I've had four days to think about forgery, Theo. I'm a surveyor. I've been making documents look true since I was twenty-two years old." A bitter ghost of a smile crossed her face. "Meridian trained me for exactly this. It seems a shame to waste the education."

Theo looked at her for a long moment. The plan was thin and it was desperate and it put her inside arm's reach of Crane, which meant it was likely suicide dressed in clever clothes. But it was also the first idea in two days that moved at all, and despair, he'd learned, was just motionlessness with better lighting.

"They'll search us," he said. "Crane will know a forgery. He's spent his whole career making false things look true — he'll recognize the craft."

"Then we need him to see what he expects to see." Mara pulled the copied permit files toward her and began, with a surveyor's neat hands, to lay out the architecture of a lie. "Which means we need to understand what he thinks winning looks like. And Theo — I think I finally do."

* * *

The answer came to him that night, lying awake on the shanty floor with the river talking to itself outside, and it came not as a thought but as a memory: Crane at dinner, decanter in hand, saying, *I don't want the gold, Mr. Marchetti. Gold is a rumor until it's documented. What I want is the paper that ends the rumors.*

That was it. That was the whole machine.

Crane didn't need the source. The gold in the ground had never been the point — the point was the concession, the permits, the valuation, the stock, the enormous paper edifice built on coordinates that had been shifted nine miles east by a dying survey vessel's falsified charts. If the true source surfaced — if Mara signed a corrective affidavit *at the spring itself*, witnessed, dated inside the permit window — the edifice came down. Every valuation, every investor, every signature Crane had collected for fifteen years, invalidated by one woman's pen in one specific place. That was why the location mattered more than the metal. That was why Crane had sealed the canyon instead of simply buying the map off some intermediary. The affidavit had to be executed at the source to bind, and so the source had to stay unreachable until the window closed.

Which meant — Theo sat up in the dark, heart hammering — which meant Crane's deadline and theirs were the *same deadline*. Four days. After that, Crane didn't need Sef, didn't need the map, didn't need any of them. The permits lapsed, the resurvey stood, and the whole question dissolved into legality. Everything Crane was doing — the hostage, the invitation, the theater of negotiation — was aimed at one thing: getting Mara's signature somewhere, anywhere, on something that looked like a voluntary relinquishment, before Thursday.

He woke Mara. She listened with her arms wrapped around her knees, and when he finished she was quiet for a long time.

"So we don't have to beat him to the source," she said slowly. "We just have to keep him from closing the window. If the permits expire with the question open — with no relinquishment filed, no corrective affidavit, nothing — then the original claim reverts to contest. My father's filing is still in the registry. Buried, but valid. Expired windows reopen disputes." Her eyes had changed — the fear still there, but moving now, working. "He needs my signature more than I need his permission. That's leverage. That's actual leverage."

"It's leverage he'll try to take off the table with a gun."

"Everything is a gun with him. That's not new information." She unfolded herself and stood, and in the dark her voice came steady and cold as well-water. "But it means we go to his station. We accept the invitation. We walk in with the map and my forged relinquishment and we trade it for Sef — and the moment Sef is clear, the moment he's on the water and away, I tell Crane the signature's a fake and watch his face do something it's never done."

"That's insane."

"Yes," Mara agreed. "It's also the only door he's opened for us. He wants us to come. Nobody has ever gotten anywhere with Aldous Crane except through a door he opened himself." She paused. "There's just one problem."

"One?"

"Sef." Her voice caught on the name, and she mastered it. "Whatever we're planning, whatever clever architecture we build — he's the collateral, and he's been collateral for two days already. Every hour we spend being clever is an hour he spends in a room with men who answer to Crane. We can't wait. We can't prepare. We go tomorrow."

Theo lay back down after she'd returned to her corner, and sleep did not come, and he didn't expect it to. He listened to the river instead — that low grinding voice from the direction of the canyon, patient as geology — and he thought about Elias Voss dying in a flophouse bed, trading the only valuable thing he owned to a stranger for a promise. *Take it to my daughter.* Theo had thought, back then, that the promise was the hard part. Deliver the map, shake hands, done. He understood now that promises were like rivers: they looked simple from the bank, and then you got in them, and they took you places the map never showed, and the only way out was through.

Four days. One hostage. One forged signature. Ninety miles of closed canyon, and somewhere above it, a spring-fed pool that knew the truth.

Outside, far off, a boat engine coughed once in the dark and went silent — too far to identify, too close to ignore. Theo reached in the darkness for the rifle he'd taken to sleeping beside, and found Mara already awake, already listening, her silhouette rigid against the faint gray of the doorway.

"They're not waiting for our answer," she whispered.

"No," Theo said, and began loading the boat.
