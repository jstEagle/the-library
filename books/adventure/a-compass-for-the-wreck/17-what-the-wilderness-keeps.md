## 17. What the Wilderness Keeps

The second layer of the map revealed itself the way all of Voss's secrets had: reluctantly, and only when Theo stopped looking for what he wanted.

He'd been studying the oilskin by lantern light in the lee of a limestone overhang, Mara beside him with her surveyor's patience, when she tilted her head and said, "Turn it toward the flame. All the way."

He did. And there it was — faint brown lines beneath the blue ink of the river, like veins under skin. Not water at all. A network of dotted passages threading east from the deadfall above the canyon's mouth, looping through symbols he'd taken for decoration: circles within circles, a chain of them, each marked with a tiny cross-hatched square.

"Sinkholes," Mara said quietly. "Cenotes. My father drew smuggler routes once — he told me that much before I stopped listening to him. Tin and salt, before the gold. The old runners used the karst. The river goes underground for three miles through that ridge; the sinkholes are its breathing holes." She traced the chain with one finger, not touching the paper. "And this—" the last circle, set above the canyon walls where Crane had sealed the headwater "—this would come up inside the gorge. Above his barricade."

Theo stared at the map until the lantern guttered. Three days left on the permits. Crane holding Sef somewhere in the canyon below, a rifle at his back and a price on all their heads. And here was a door Voss had drawn forty years ago, waiting.

"He knew," Theo said. "Even then, he knew someone might have to come at it from behind."

Mara said nothing, but her jaw tightened in the way he was learning meant grief held under pressure, like air in a flooded suit.

* * *

They climbed through the night.

The ridge above the deadfall was a broken spine of gray rock and root-hung soil, and the first sinkhole announced itself as a cold breath out of the ground — air moving where no wind should be, smelling of wet stone and something older, mineral and dark. Theo went down first, boots finding handholds in the collapsed rim, and dropped into a chamber where his lantern seemed ashamed of itself, the light swallowed whole by blackness that went on breathing around him.

Mara followed without a word. She carried her father's map in an oilcloth satchel against her chest now, and Theo had noticed she touched it the way other people touched amulets.

The passage floor was gravel and ancient silt, dry enough to walk, sloping always downward and inward. Overhead, in places, the ceiling opened into shafts where roots dangled like the hair of drowned men and a coin of daylight fell through, trembling when the wind moved topside. Between those coins of light they walked in the lantern's small kingdom, and the mountain closed over them like a hand.

"The runners came through here with tin," Mara said, her voice flattened by the stone. "My father said you could walk from the delta to the headwaters underground if you knew the holes. He learned it from a man named Corben." She paused. "His partner. From the first strike."

"I've read the name," Theo said. "In the journals Ives found. Corben Halloran." He hadn't told her everything those journals contained. Some of it he'd kept back without deciding to — the way you keep a wound covered.

"They split the claim between them, originally," Mara said. "Father and Halloran. Fifty-fifty, on paper, before Meridian existed." Her boot crunched on something — a shard of clay bottle, glazed blue, whole along one curve. She picked it up, turned it in the lantern light, and set it down again exactly where it lay. "Then the resurvey happened, and suddenly there was only one name on the claim."

They walked on. The silence between them had changed texture since the shanty, since the journals, since she had burned her career in a company office and come overland alone to find him. It was not comfortable silence. It was the silence of two people carrying weight they could not put down.

An hour in, the passage forked, and the map resolved it: the left-hand route bore a small notation in Voss's cramped hand — *low crawl, do not stop* — while the right curved toward a symbol Theo now understood. A circle. A breathing hole.

But beside that circle, in ink so old it had gone the color of dried blood, Voss had written two words:

*Corben's rest.*

Theo stopped. Mara, a step ahead, felt him stop and turned.

"What is it?"

He turned the map so she could see. He watched her read it. He watched her father's handwriting tell her, across forty years, that there was something down here her father had never said aloud to anyone.

"That's not on any version I ever saw," she whispered. "He added it later. After—" She didn't finish. After the resurvey. After the fraud. After whatever had happened between two partners and a mountain full of gold.

The right-hand passage breathed cold air at them, patient as a held note.

"We should keep to the low crawl," Theo said. "We're losing hours we don't have."

"Yes," Mara said. But she was already walking toward the circle.

* * *

The chamber opened like a cathedral that had never been meant for God.

Their lanterns found walls flowing with pale calcite, columns grown over centuries drop by patient drop, and a floor of fine white silt that took their footprints like fresh snow. In the center, beneath a shaft where daylight fell straight and clean from the world above, something caught the light and threw it back dull and wrong.

Bones.

They were arranged — or had settled — with a terrible dignity. A skeleton reclined against the base of a flowstone column, seated, as if the man had simply chosen his spot and waited. The ribs were bleached amber. A skull rested at a gentle tilt, facing upward toward the thread of sky. Beside the remains lay a rusted carbine, its stock rotted to lace, and a canvas pack gone to cloth and buttons. Two bullet holes in the sternum, close together, deliberate. No animal had done this. No accident of the river.

Mara made a sound Theo had never heard from her — small, involuntary, the sound of a foundation cracking.

She knelt in the white silt, careful, the way she did everything, and lifted a locket from where it had fallen against the femur. Its hinge was fused with corrosion, but the face was engraved: *E.C.H.* Elias Corben Halloran. She worked it open with her thumbnail and a patience that shook only at the edges. Inside, a curl of hair, copper-brown even now, and behind the portrait glass a miniature painting of a young man with a laughing face and Voss's same stubborn jaw.

"My father's face," she said. "Younger. They looked alike. People used to ask if they were brothers." Her voice had gone very level, which Theo understood now was worse than shaking. "He shot him. He shot him twice in the chest and sat him up against the rock and he never — he never said. Forty years. He let me believe my father abandoned me because he was weak, because he drank, because he couldn't be bothered with a daughter—"

"Mara."

"And all along it was because he was carrying *this*. Because he'd watched his partner get murdered for a claim and knew if he spoke the name of who did it, he'd be next." She looked up at Theo, and the lantern light made her eyes bright and hard. "Who? Who killed him?"

Theo crouched beside her. He had turned the question over through every mile since the shanty, fitting pieces together: the journals' final entries, the dates, the names of the men who'd gone on to found Meridian Consolidated, the pattern repeating itself decades later in a scuttled survey vessel and a burned skiff and Rennie's empty bunk.

"The journals name a land agent," he said. "A man who handled the paperwork for both claims. Who offered to buy Halloran out the week before he died, and bought Voss's silence instead — not with money. With a threat. With the promise that the claim would die with both their names on it unless Voss signed it away." He paused. "That man's son runs Meridian now. Aldous Crane didn't invent the method, Mara. He inherited it. His father buried the original crime, and Aldous has spent his life burying everything that grew out of it. The falsified charts. The wreck. Rennie. Maybe your father too, soon, if he isn't already."

Mara looked back at the bones. At the laughing painted face in the locket.

"Generational," she said softly. "It's a family business. Greed." She closed the locket with a click that echoed off the vaulted dark. "And so is justice, if we make it one."

* * *

They buried what could be buried.

There was no digging in the white silt — it lay over solid stone — but there were stones themselves, loose ones tumbled into the chamber's corners, and between them they raised a cairn over the remains, working in near silence, passing rocks hand to hand. Theo had buried men before, at sea and ashore, and knew the strange arithmetic of it: the body does not weigh more than the stones, but somehow it always does.

Mara placed the locket on top of the cairn, wedged into a gap where it would sit forever in the falling thread of daylight.

"He should have had a grave with his name on it," she said. "This will have to do until the law can come and do it properly. Ives will want to see this. Photographs, records — it's evidence, Theo. It's the murder the whole edifice stands on. Everything Meridian owns traces back through the fraudulent resurvey, and the resurvey traces back to a dead man in a hole in the ground."

"Voss knew," Theo said. "That's why he wrote it on the map. Not for treasure. For testimony. He drew us a route and left a witness at the halfway point." He looked up at the coin of sky above the cairn. "He couldn't say it aloud for forty years. So he wrote it where only someone coming to the source would ever find it."

Mara stood, brushed the white dust from her knees, and did something Theo did not expect. She took the map from her satchel, unrolled it on the flat top of a flowstone shelf, and produced a pencil from her surveyor's kit — the same kind of pencil, he thought, that had signed the fraudulent charts, though her hands had never shaken doing that work the way they trembled now.

Below *Corben's rest*, in a surveyor's precise hand, she wrote the date, the coordinates of the chamber, and four words:

*Remains verified. M. Voss.*

"There," she said. "Countersigned. First of two signatures I owe this river."

They shouldered their packs and returned to the fork, to the low crawl and the long dark ahead of it, to the source waiting above the canyon where Crane held Sef Okafor and a deadline burned down like a fuse.

At the mouth of the crawl, Mara stopped and looked back once at the thin daylight falling on the cairn.

"Hold on, Uncle Corben," she said quietly. "We're almost there."

Then the mountain took them, and the dark ran on and on, and somewhere far above, dawn was coming to the headwater pool where everything would end or begin.
