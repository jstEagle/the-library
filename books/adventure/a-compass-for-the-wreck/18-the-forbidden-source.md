## 18. The Forbidden Source

They climbed out of the dark in the last hour before dawn, and the world above the canyon was a different country.

Theo came up the sinkhole's throat behind Mara, her boots scraping limestone somewhere above his head, a gray coin of sky widening with every meter. His shoulders had forgotten how to be anything but bruised. The cave air clung to him — cold stone and old water and the mineral breath of a place that had never seen sun — and when he finally hauled himself over the lip onto wet grass, he lay flat on his back for a long moment just breathing the open night.

Mara knelt beside him, Voss's map spread across her knees even though she no longer needed it. She'd memorized it somewhere in the black hours of the tunnel, tracing the smuggler routes with her fingertip while Sef's lantern guttered, and now she stared at the eastern horizon as if she could will the light up faster.

"There," she said.

The mist was lifting off the headwater meadow in long ribbons, and through it Theo saw the pool.

He had imagined it a hundred ways since the delta — since Voss's fevered hands pressing oilskin into his own — and none of them had prepared him for its plainness. It wasn't large. Perhaps forty meters across, a lens of black water set in a bowl of granite and sedge, so still it held the fading stars on its surface like a second sky. No river ran into it. That was the thing the falsified charts had erased, the thing Meridian Consolidated had spent a man's life and a ship's hull burying: the pool fed everything. He could see it now, in the graying light — the outflow at the western rim where water slid over smooth rock without a sound, gathered itself, and began the long journey down through the canyon, gathering tributaries, becoming the river that turned the delta's wheels and drowned the survey vessel and carried all their fates seaward like driftwood.

Every drop of it started here. And Elias Voss had found it first, thirty years ago, with a mule, a pan, and a stubbornness that had gotten him shot in a flophouse at the end.

"He was right," Mara said quietly. Her voice cracked on the second word. "The old fool was right to the meter."

Theo sat up. His ribs protested. Somewhere below them, faint as memory, the river spoke against the canyon walls.

* * *

They used the waiting hours the way soldiers use the dark before an assault — checking what could be checked, saying little.

Sef had gone ahead along the rim to find the trail down to the meadow and confirm what the map promised: a game path, ancient and half-swallowed by alder, descending to the pool's edge. He came back with dawn in his beard and bad news in his mouth.

"Smoke," he said. "Downriver side of the canyon. Two fires, maybe three. They're coming up the water route." He looked at Theo. "Your friend Crane travels loud."

"Not my friend."

"No," Sef agreed. "But you keep meeting him anyway. That's worse than friendship."

Ives stood apart from them at the rim, field glasses raised, her navy coat swapped two days ago for oiled canvas that made her look like any river rat, though nothing about her posture did. She lowered the glasses slowly.

"Gunboats can't reach this stretch — the falls below the split channel saw to that. So they've come overland or they portaged the shallows." She turned. "Which means Crane is on foot, which means he brought only the men willing to climb. That narrows him."

"To how many?" Mara asked.

"Enough," Ives said, and did not soften it.

Theo crouched at the map again, though there was nothing left to read from it. The second layer — the smuggler routes Voss had inked in faded brown — had gotten them here, up through the sinkhole system while Crane sealed the canyon mouth below. But maps were honest about terrain and silent about men. There was no legend for what Aldous Crane would do when he arrived and found his prize already standing on it.

"We should go back down," Mara said. "Into the caves. Hide until—"

"Until what?" Theo asked gently. "He'll seal the sinkhole too, once he knows we used it. And we'd be trading a standoff we might survive for a tomb we certainly wouldn't." He touched the oilskin roll inside his jacket, where it had lived so long it felt like part of his skeleton. "No. We stay in the open. Witnesses need daylight."

"Witnesses," Ives repeated. It wasn't quite a question. She had been turning the word over since the shanty, since Voss's journals, since the night she chose conscience over chain of command and burned her own career to do it. "You're betting everything on paper and signatures, Marchetti. On a woman's countersignature and a dead man's map. Crane is betting on rifles."

"I know which bet I like," Theo said. "I've been underwater with rifles pointed at me. Paper never once drew blood."

"That's because paper gets other people to draw it for you," Ives said. But something in her face shifted — the investigator's arithmetic running behind her eyes, charges and witnesses and jurisdiction aligning like instruments on a wreck's bridge. "Fine. We hold the source until they arrive. When they arrive, nobody shoots first. That's not mercy, it's strategy. Every man who keeps his finger off a trigger up here is a witness against Crane downstream."

"And if Crane shoots first?" Sef asked.

Ives didn't answer that either.

* * *

Dawn came up gold and slow, burning the mist off the meadow, and the pool changed with it — black to green to a blue so deep it seemed less like water than like a piece of sky that had fallen and decided to stay. Theo walked down the game path alone while the others watched the trails, and stood at the outflow, and put his hand in the current.

It was cold enough to ache. It was clean in a way water almost never was, tasting of stone and snowmelt and nothing else. He thought of the delta, sixty leagues downstream — the brown wide water, the mudflats, the rusting hulk of the survey vessel where he'd first punched through a rotted deck and found holes punched from the inside. All of that began under his hand. Every barge, every dredge, every poisoned fishing ground Sef had grown up on, every fraudulent coordinate Mara had signed in good faith — all of it flowed from this quiet bowl of granite, and none of it had ever been allowed to say so.

"You should see it," he called softly up the slope, and Mara came down to stand beside him.

She didn't speak for a long time. The light moved across her face, and Theo watched her father's stubborn jaw work through something grief-shaped.

"He described it to me once," she said finally. "When I was small. Before he—" She stopped, restarted. "Before the company took the claim and took me with it. He used to say he'd found the wellspring of the whole country and that someday he'd bring me to drink from it. I thought it was a story. Prospector's talk, the way other fathers talked about buried treasure." Her hand hovered over the water without touching it. "He wasn't lying. He was never lying. Everyone just preferred the version where he was."

"Mara." Theo turned to face her. Now, while the light was kind and the guns were still distant, was the time, and he knew it. "There's something I have to give you. I should have given it to you the day I found you in that office with Meridian's name on your door."

Her eyes went sharp. "The map."

"The map." He drew the oilskin roll from his jacket. It was lighter than it had any right to be, for the weight it had carried — Rennie's face, Voss's blood, the scuttle marks, the burned skiff, all of it folded into a few ounces of waxed cloth. "He made me promise. Dying man's grip on my wrist, gunpoint of time, the whole liturgy. Take it to my daughter, he said. I've been trying to figure out since whether he meant deliver it or avenge it."

"What did you decide?"

"I decided he meant both." He pressed it into her hands. "It's yours. Not leverage, not evidence — yours. Your father's handwriting, your inheritance. What you do with it is between you and him."

Mara unrolled it with the care of a surgeon. In the strengthening light the true coordinates stood clear in Voss's cramped hand, and beside them, in a different ink, the numbers Meridian had published — shifted, Theo knew now, by eleven kilometers east and four south, enough to steal a spring and orphan a claim. Mara read it twice. Then she folded it along its old creases, rolled it, and tied it, and when she looked up her eyes were dry and terrible.

"Then let's finish it," she said. "Get me the affidavit. Get me witnesses. My signature dies worthless anywhere but here — Crane made sure of that when he wrote the filing requirements himself. At the source, witnessed, or nothing." A sound came up the canyon then, faint and wrong: boot heels on stone, many of them, and the metallic knock of something being dragged. Mara went still. "And it seems," she said, "we're about to have our quorum."

* * *

Crane came up the western trail at mid-morning, and he had dressed for the occasion.

That was the thing about Aldous Crane that Theo had never managed to make peace with: the man treated atrocity as a matter of tailoring. His boots were polished despite three days of river travel. His linen collar sat crisp above a grey traveling coat. Six armed men flanked him in a loose crescent, river-mud to their knees, carbines slung with the casualness of tools rather than weapons — and in the center of them, wrists bound in front of him with boat line, walked Sef Okafor.

Theo's stomach dropped through the granite.

They had taken Sef an hour ago, it emerged later — taken him off the rim trail where he'd circled wide to watch their flank, taken him quietly, because Crane did not waste noise on things he intended to trade. Now Sef stood at the meadow's edge with blood at his hairline and his chin up, and his eyes found Theo's across the water with an expression that said, plainly: *don't you dare pay for me.*

"Mr. Marchetti." Crane stopped ten paces off, on a shelf of rock above the outflow, and removed his hat as if arriving at church. "You've made me climb a mountain. I want you to know I noticed the discourtesy."

"You could have saved yourself the walk," Theo said. He positioned himself slightly forward of Mara, slightly downwind of the carbines, the geometry of a man who'd spent his life putting his body between trouble and other people. "Everything you came for is already here."

"So I see." Crane's gaze traveled the pool with genuine appreciation, and that was somehow worse than menace. "Do you know, in nineteen years with Meridian I have never once seen this? I've signed the permits, filed the resurveys, buried the original strike under eleven kilometers of creative cartography — and I never laid eyes on the actual spring." He smiled. "It's smaller than the ledgers imply. Everything true usually is."

"Let him go," Theo said. "Sef has nothing to do with this."

"Sef has everything to do with this; he's your pilot, your guide, and until this morning your good right arm. Which makes him exactly the sort of asset one brings to a negotiation." Crane nodded pleasantly at the bound man. "Though I confess his usefulness is depreciating by the minute. As is everyone's. Four days, Mr. Marchetti. Did you know? The concession permits expire in four days, and after that the land reverts to provincial review, and provincial review means clerks, and clerks mean years. Whatever happens on these rocks today decides who owns this water for a generation. I find that clarifying."

Behind Crane, Ives had drifted left along the rim, oiled canvas blending with granite, one hand resting easy near the navy pistol she was technically forbidden to carry on this stretch of river. Mara stood at the outflow with the map hidden in her coat and her father's jaw set like the stone underfoot. Six carbines. One pistol. One affidavit waiting for ink and witnesses.

"Here's my offer, and it's better than the last one," Crane said. "The map, and the surveyor's cooperation — a corrective amendment, properly framed, that relocates the concession boundary to include the spring while preserving Meridian's title. Miss Voss signs, I retire certain liabilities, and everyone walks down this mountain rich. Your salvage share triples. The pilot goes home to his nets. Even Lieutenant Ives finds her report writes itself favorably." He paused, and the civilized mask thinned just enough to show the machinery beneath. "Or we do this the way the company did the survey vessel. From the inside. Quietly. And Mr. Okafor becomes the third body this river has swallowed for me, and I promise you, Marchetti, I sleep wonderfully after such things. Ask anyone."

"I believe you," Theo said. "That's the worst part. I believe every word."

He glanced at Mara. She gave the smallest nod. He glanced at the rim, where Ives had stopped moving, waiting. The pool lay between them all, holding the morning sky on its surface, indifferent as scripture.

"Then here's mine," Theo said, and stepped forward onto the flat rock at the water's edge, where the light fell full and the whole canyon could see. "You get neither the map nor her signature. What you get instead is witnesses. Right here, at the source, in full daylight — the affidavit, signed lawful and proper, with a navy officer and a free man standing witness. Your fraud ends today, Crane. The only question left is whether it ends with you walking down this mountain under your own power or carried."

For a moment the meadow was perfectly still. The outflow whispered over its rocks. Crane's smile remained exactly where it was, fixed and pleasant, while behind his eyes something closed like a ledger.

"Mr. Marchetti," he said softly, and lifted two fingers.

Six carbines came off six shoulders at once, and Sef shouted a warning, and the morning broke like glass.
