## 19. Witnesses at the Headwater

The dawn came up gold and slow over the canyon rim, and Mara Voss stood at the edge of the spring-fed pool with her father's map open in her hands, and for a long moment she could not breathe.

She had imagined this place her whole life without knowing it. Elias had described it to her when she was small enough to sit on his knee — *a pool like a coin, sweetheart, minted by the mountain itself* — and she had thought it a bedtime fancy, one of the embellishments prospectors wove around their failures. But here it was. The water rose from a fissure in the limestone floor, impossibly clear, impossibly blue-green, and where it spilled over the lip of rock it turned the color of old pewter and began its long journey down through the delta, past the wreck of the survey vessel, past the town that had been built on a lie, all the way to the sea.

Every drop of it. Every claim along every mile of it drew its life from this single hidden heart.

"Coordinates match," Theo said quietly behind her. He had his salvaged survey instruments out on a flat stone, the brass still crusted with river silt, and he was sighting across the pool with the transit as though he needed confirmation from the dead men's tools. "To the second decimal. Voss wasn't guessing."

"He never guessed." Mara's voice came out rougher than she intended. She folded the map along its worn creases — the paper gone soft as cloth from thirty years of handling — and looked at the second layer beneath, the smuggler routes inked in faded brown, the route they had crawled through half the night: sinkhole to sinkhole, cave throat to cave throat, following her father's careful annotations in a hand she recognized now from letters he'd sent before the estrangement, before the silence, before everything. *Watch for the false gallery at the third junction. The water will tell you when to turn.*

The water had told them. She had listened.

Lieutenant Cora Ives crouched at the pool's edge, filling a sample vial with hands that were steady but not calm. Her navy coat was torn at the shoulder from the crawl through the limestone, and there was limestone dust in her cropped hair, and she looked, Mara thought, like a woman who had finally stopped drowning slowly.

"It's real," Ives said. "That's what matters legally. A source this size feeding the whole drainage — Meridian's resurvey put their intake eleven miles south of here. Eleven miles. There's no honest way to make that error." She capped the vial. "Mara. Are you ready?"

Mara took the affidavit from the oilskin packet inside her jacket — her own drafting, copied from the permit files she'd burned her career to steal, corrected against her father's original claim numbers, every fraudulent figure struck through in red. At the bottom, above the line marked *COUNTERSIGNATURE OF RESIDENT SURVEYOR*, the space waited for her name.

Her name. The name that appeared on forty-one falsified charts. The name Aldous Crane had spent years polishing into an instrument of theft.

"I've signed my name to their lies for six years," she said. "One more signature won't kill me."

"It might," said Sef Okafor from the rocks above.

They all looked up. Sef stood at the high shelf overlooking the pool, where the canyon narrowed to a throat of gray stone, and he was very still in the way Mara had learned meant danger. His hand rested on the bow he'd carried since the delta, unstrung, casual — too casual.

"Boats," he said. "Two of them. Coming up the lower channel. Crane's colors."

* * *

Theo felt the arrival in his chest before he heard it — the deep-water thud of engines working hard against current, echoing off the canyon walls. He'd been on salvage runs long enough to know the sound of a boat pushed past its design speed, hulls slamming, crews exhausted and angry. These were not boats coming to negotiate.

"How did they find us?" Mara asked, already moving, stuffing the affidavit back into the oilskin.

"The outfitters," Theo said. "Someone sold us. I told you the gear manifest had been copied." He looked at Ives. "How long do we have?"

Ives was already climbing toward Sef's shelf, pulling out the battered field glasses she'd kept dry inside her coat through the caves. She glassed the lower channel for a long ten seconds.

"Twenty minutes. Maybe less if they've got a pilot who knows the water." She lowered the glasses. Her face had gone very still. "It's not just gunboats. There's a third boat behind them. Civilian hull. Crane's aboard — I can see him at the rail. And they're carrying something off the lead boat."

"What?"

"A man. Bound at the wrists."

Sef made a sound low in his throat, and Theo understood it a half-second before the words came: they had Sef's family's fishing grounds burned into memory, and now they had something else of his.

"They took him alive," Sef said. "Why would they take him alive?"

"Because dead hostages don't bargain," Theo said. "Crane wants us here. He wanted us to reach the source. He needed Mara's signature on the original filings so he could destroy them in front of witnesses — or destroy the witness. Either way, the paperwork dies with us."

"Then we don't wait twenty minutes," Ives said. She slung the glasses and turned to face them, and in the flat morning light Theo saw something in her expression he hadn't seen before — not the investigator's wariness, not the transactional arithmetic of their alliance, but decision. Complete and irreversible. "We do it now. Before they arrive. Mara signs, we witness, and the moment that ink dries, this is no longer a company secret. It's a matter of record. Three witnesses, sworn statement, filed copies already lodged with the naval magistrate downstream — I sent them yesterday from the shanty, by pigeon. If anything happens to us, the record survives us."

"You sent it already?" Theo stared at her.

"I've been deciding since the shanty," Ives said. "I finished deciding last night in the dark. Get the paper out, Miss Voss."

* * *

Mara spread the affidavit on the flat stone beside the pool, weighting the corners with four pebbles, and the wind off the water tried to lift it and could not.

She read it aloud, because Ives said the law liked words spoken as well as written, and because some part of Mara wanted her father to hear it wherever he was — dying in a flophouse bed, or already gone, or standing somewhere in the country of the dead with his hand-drawn maps and his stubborn faith.

"*Corrective Affidavit of Survey,*" she read. "*Regarding concession permits 114 through 119, Meridian Consolidated Mining. I, Mara Voss, senior land surveyor in the employ of Meridian Consolidated, do swear that the resurvey of record, bearing my countersignature, was executed upon falsified coordinate data supplied under direction of Aldous Crane, Director of Acquisitions; that the true headwater source of the river lies at the coordinates herein described; that the original claim of Elias Voss, staked and registered nineteen years prior, is valid and prior in law; and that the said resurvey was undertaken with knowledge of its falsehood for the purpose of extinguishing the said prior claim.*"

Her voice held. She was surprised by that.

Ives stepped forward first. "*Witnessed, Cora Ives, Lieutenant, Royal Naval Investigative Service,*" she recited, and signed beneath Mara's name in a hand like a blade cut. Then Sef, who could not write more than his mark, pressed his thumbprint into the ink pad Ives carried and rolled it onto the page with great care, as though the print itself were a prayer.

Then it was done. Four names and a thumbprint on a page weighted with river stones, at the true source of the river, in the first honest light.

Mara looked up and saw the gunboats rounding the bend below the falls, and her first thought — absurdly — was of her father's handwriting. *The water will tell you when to turn.* The water had turned. Everything had turned.

* * *

Aldous Crane came ashore the way he did everything: beautifully.

He stepped from the civilian launch onto the wet stones in polished boots that would be ruined in an hour, in a linen jacket pressed despite three days on the river, and he smiled at them across the pool as though greeting guests at a dinner party rather than cornering thieves at the end of the world. His hired men fanned out behind him — eight of them, rifles slung with the loose professional ease of men who had done ugly work before. Two of them dragged Sef's cousin between them, wrists bound, face swollen.

"Mara." Crane's voice carried warmly over the water. "You've made me chase you across half a province. I hope the scenery justified it."

"You know why I'm here," Mara said.

"I know you've done something foolish with a piece of paper." Crane walked to the edge of the pool and looked down into it, and for a moment his reflection floated there among the stones, distorted, blue-green, insubstantial. "Do you know what this water is worth? Not the gold in it — the water itself. Concessions, intakes, dredging rights, power leases. An empire, Mara. Your signature built half of it. One signature can't unbuild it."

"One signature already has," Ives said.

Crane turned to her with genuine curiosity, the way a man looks at an unfamiliar insect. "Lieutenant Ives. You're a long way from your jurisdiction."

"So are you. Further, actually. Mine follows me." She held up the affidavit. "Sworn, witnessed, and copies lodged with the naval magistrate as of yesterday noon. You can take this page, Mr. Crane. You can take all of us. The record stands."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes — the first crack in the polish. He glanced at his men, and Theo watched him do the arithmetic of violence: eight rifles against four people, one hostage, one document. Easy numbers. Except.

Except Ives had already begun reading aloud.

"*Warrant particulars in preparation,*" her voice rang off the canyon walls, pitched deliberately to carry, "*for the murder of Rennie Callow, salvage diver, whose skiff was burned on the delta flats; for the murder of one Elias Voss, prospector, shot and left to fever in the town of Harrow Bend; for the scuttling of the survey vessel* Meridian Star *with loss of two crew; and for conspiracy in each and all.*"

She let the names hang in the cold air. Then she looked past Crane, directly at the eight hired men, and spoke to them alone.

"You heard the names. Callow. Voss. Two crew who went down with a punched-out ship. Some of you were on those crews. Some of you were paid to look away afterward. Here's what waits downstream for the men who pulled triggers: murder carries the rope. Accessory carries fifteen years. *Witness for the crown carries a pardon.*" She paused. "I only need two of you. The rest can take their chances."

The silence stretched. The river filled it, pouring endlessly over the lip of the pool, indifferent and patient.

And Theo watched the arithmetic change. Watched it move through the hired men like a current — a rifle barrel dipping, a man glancing at his neighbor, another shifting his weight off the line. They were professionals, and professionals knew a losing position when they stood in one. Eight men could shoot four. Eight men could not shoot four and then all go home and sleep.

"Mr. Crane," said the nearest gunman, a heavyset man with a gray beard and river-scars on his knuckles. He didn't raise his rifle. He didn't lower it either. "My contract says security work. Nobody said nothing about rope work." He spat into the water. "I'm standing down."

A second man followed. Then a third. The line dissolved not with a battle but with a kind of quiet collapse, men stepping aside one by one, until only two remained at Crane's shoulders, and even they had stopped looking at him and started looking at Ives.

Crane's smile stayed fixed, but Theo had seen drowned men wear that same expression — the face of someone whose lungs had decided the struggle was over without consulting them.

"This is theater," Crane said. "Signatures in the dirt. You think paper beats—"

"You taught me that it does," Mara said, and her voice cracked across the pool like a whip. "You taught me that whoever holds the survey holds the ground. Well, Crane. Look at the ground."

And Crane looked — at the pool, at the affidavit, at his own men standing down, at the lieutenant with the warrant list, at the diver and the pilot and the daughter of the man he'd ruined — and whatever lived behind the polish finally broke cover.

He lunged.

Not for the guns. For the affidavit itself, snatching for the page on the stone, and Ives was fast but Crane was desperate, and his hand closed on the corner of the paper just as Mara threw herself at his arm. They went down together on the wet rocks at the pool's edge, the document tearing — one corner ripping free in Crane's fist while the body of the page stayed pinned under Mara's elbow — and Crane scrambled up, wild-eyed, backing away along the slick stone shelf above the sinkhole channel, the torn corner raised like a trophy.

"It's nothing!" he shouted, to his men, to himself, to the canyon. "A scrap! The rest is worthless without—"

The stone gave way beneath him.

It happened without drama, which was somehow worse. One moment he was standing on the shelf declaiming; the next the limestone, hollowed by centuries of hidden water, simply declined to hold him, and Aldous Crane dropped into the sinkhole channel with a sound like a door closing. The black water took him at the throat. His hand came up once, the torn corner of paper still clutched in it, whitening, and then the current — the same current that fed every claim, every lie, every mile of river below — pulled him down into the throat of the mountain, and the surface closed over him smooth as poured glass.

Nobody moved. Even the two loyal gunmen stood frozen, rifles forgotten.

Sef reached the edge first and lay flat, peering into the dark water churning down into the cave system. After a moment he shook his head slowly.

"The channel runs under the ridge," he said quietly. "Three miles of it. No man comes out."

* * *

Afterward, Mara sat on the flat stone with the affidavit in her lap — whole except for its missing corner, every signature intact, every word legible — and she found she was shaking, and found also that she could not stop looking at the pool.

The water had gone still again. Already still. As though the mountain had swallowed a man and barely noticed.

Ives crouched beside her, holstering the pistol she'd drawn at some point Mara couldn't remember. "Are you hurt?"

"No." Mara's voice sounded far away to her own ears. "He tore the corner. Does the corner matter?"

Ives took the page, examined it, and handed it back. "Names, dates, signatures, seal. The corner was blank." She almost smiled. "The law is strange, Miss Voss. It wants everything but the margins."

Down at the waterline, Theo was cutting Sef's cousin's wrist bindings with his dive knife, and the two gunmen who had stood down were sitting on the rocks with their hands visible, waiting — not arrested yet, but surrendered to something larger than either side. One of them, the gray-bearded man, was talking to Ives in a low voice, and from the set of her shoulders Mara knew: he was giving her names. Dates. The scuttling crew. The order-givers. Everything.

The catharsis she had imagined — justice as a thunderclap — had instead arrived the way the river arrived at the sea: gradually, by channels, wearing things down.

But it had arrived.

Mara looked down at her father's map, still open on the stone, its creases soft as skin, its brown-inked routes running everywhere like veins. Nineteen years Elias Voss had carried the truth of this place and died within sight of delivering it. She pressed her palm flat against the paper.

"We're not finished," she said aloud, to Ives, to Theo, to the pool. "The affidavit's signed. Now it has to survive the trip downstream — and Crane's company doesn't know yet that their director is dead. When they do, they'll burn everything they can reach. Permits. Files. Witnesses."

Ives straightened, and the investigator came back into her face, hard and bright.

"Then we go downstream faster than the news does," she said. "There's a naval cutter at the confluence, and I intend to be on it before Meridian's lawyers finish their first cup of coffee."

Theo waded out of the shallows, wringing river from his sleeves, and looked at the two of them, and then at the pool — the true source, the heart of everything, glittering in the full morning light.

"Four days left on the permits," he said. "Let's not waste them."
