## 20. Permits and Promises

The affidavit was four pages long, and it took Mara Voss eleven minutes to sign her name to all of them.

Theo counted the minutes without meaning to. He had a diver's habit of counting — bottom time, decompression stops, the slow arithmetic of air remaining — and so he stood at the headwater pool with his arms folded against the morning chill and counted while she bent over the field table Ives had commandeered, the pen scratching in the silence that follows gunfire. The ink was ordinary. The paper was ordinary. But when she finished and blew across the signature the way a woman blows across a hot spoon, the whole river seemed to exhale with her.

"There," she said. Her voice was rough. She'd been crying earlier, or laughing, or both; Theo hadn't asked which. "It's done."

"It's not done until it's filed," Ives said. She took the pages with both hands, the way you take something breakable, and slid them into an oilskin wallet that had once belonged, Theo suspected, to Elias Voss himself. "But yes. It's done."

Sef Okafor sat on a rock above the spring-fed pool with his wrists still raw from Crane's bindings, watching the water come up out of the earth. He hadn't spoken since the hired men dropped their rifles and walked away down the canyon trail, since Crane went into the sinkhole channel with a sound like a door closing. Now he said, quietly, in the direction of the pool: "My grandmother used to say this water was older than the names we give things." He looked at Theo. "I believe her again."

Below them, far below, the river ran brown and patient toward the delta, carrying the news of what had happened here at whatever speed water carries news.

* * *

They came down off the headwater two days later, because mountains don't care about your schedule and Sef's shoulder needed splinting properly, and by the time they reached the navy post at the confluence the river had already changed hands.

There were gunboats at the mouth of the canyon — navy ones, real ones, flying proper colors — and there were Meridian Consolidated barges tied up under guard, their dredges silent, their crews sitting on their own equipment smoking cigarettes and waiting to find out who they worked for now. A lieutenant with a clipboard met them at the landing and read Ives's name like it was a password he'd been given, and then everything happened quickly and bureaucratically: statements taken, the oilskin wallet receipted, Theo's salvage permit confiscated with a courtesy that somehow made it worse.

"Your rights to the wreck are suspended pending investigation," the lieutenant said. "Likely permanently. The vessel is evidence now. Everything aboard it is evidence."

"Everything aboard it," Theo repeated. "You mean the instruments."

"I mean everything."

He should have felt the loss like a wound. That was the strange part — he didn't. He'd run the numbers on that salvage a hundred times in his head during the upstream run: the brass survey transits alone would have covered a season's wages, the chronometers triple that, and the sealed strongroom charts were worth more than the boat he stood on. He had bled for that wreck. Rennie had died for asking about it. And now a man with a clipboard was telling him it was all just *evidence*, which was a word that meant it belonged to nobody, which meant it belonged to the truth, which meant Theo Marchetti was walking away from the richest dive of his career with nothing but a receipt.

"Marchetti." Ives touched his elbow. "For what it's worth — your testimony is going to bury them. Voss's journals, the falsified logs, Mara's affidavit. It's a complete chain. You did that."

"I held a map," Theo said. "Other people did the rest."

"Don't do that." She said it flatly, no warmth in it, which was how he knew she meant it. "I've watched men take credit for rivers they never ran. Don't hand yours back."

* * *

Mara found him that evening on the dock behind the navy post, where he was doing the thing he always did after a job ended badly or well — checking his gear, piece by piece, as if inventory could tell him who he was. His mask. His weight belt. The little brass depth gauge with the cracked face that had never once lied to him.

She sat down beside him on the pilings without asking, which was a family trait apparently, because her father had done exactly the same thing in a flophouse room that smelled of fever and blood, pressing an oilskin bundle into a stranger's hands.

"You haven't given it back to me yet," she said.

Theo's hands went still on the depth gauge.

"The map," she said. "The original. My father's hand-drawn one. Ives has the journals and the affidavit copies, but the map itself — you've kept it. Through all of it. Through Crane, through the rapids, through the cave." She turned to look at him, and in the last light her eyes were her father's eyes, and that was almost unbearable. "I'm not accusing you. I want to understand why."

He thought about lying to her. He was good at lying; he'd lied smoothly to Aldous Crane once, in another life, three weeks and one dead friend ago. But lying to Mara Voss felt like lying over open water — nothing underneath to catch you.

"Because for most of the way upriver," he said, "it wasn't yours yet. And it wasn't mine either. It was leverage. That's the honest answer. There were nights I sat in Sef's boat doing the arithmetic. What Meridian would pay. What a newspaper would pay. What some rival syndicate would pay for proof their competitor staked a concession on shifted coordinates." He turned the depth gauge over in his fingers. "A dying man gave me a promise, and I carried it around like ballast, and half the time I hated him for it. Because the map was worth more than the promise. Anybody sensible would have sold it."

"But you didn't."

"No. And I want to be clear that I don't entirely know why. Maybe because Rennie burned. Maybe because your father held my hand too hard when he made me swear, and dying men grip harder than living ones. Maybe because somewhere between the cordon and the canyon I stopped thinking of it as salvage." He looked out at the darkening river. "Salvage is taking what the water gives up. That's the trade. But that map was never given up. Your father held onto it through being shot at and cheated and erased, and he gave it away himself, on purpose, to a stranger, because he trusted the stranger more than he trusted the company. That's not salvage. That's inheritance. And you don't sell inheritance. You deliver it."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. Down the dock, a navy sentry struck a match, and the small flame showed the river running black and endless.

"He talked about you," she said finally. "In the journals. Not by name — he called you 'the delta man.' He wrote that if he ever had to trust someone fast, it would be a salvager, because salvagers are the only people who make their living off what other people abandon, and they never forget what abandonment costs." Her voice caught. "He was right about you."

"He was right about the coordinates," Theo said. "That's what matters. Everything else is weather."

She laughed — a short, wet sound — and then held out her hand, palm up, and waited.

Theo reached inside his jacket and brought out the oilskin bundle. It was lighter than he remembered. It had gotten lighter the whole way upriver, or he had gotten stronger, or promises simply weigh less the closer you come to keeping them. The wrapping was soft with age and sweat and river water, and the string was the same string Elias Voss had tied in a flophouse room with shaking hands.

He put it in her palm and let go.

"There," he said, and found his throat had gone tight around the word. "Delivered."

Mara unwrapped it right there on the pilings, careless of the dark, and the map caught the sentry's matchlight — her father's careful lines, the second layer of smuggler routes, the true source marked in a hand that had been dead for weeks and had been right about everything. She pressed it flat against her knee and read it the way you read a letter from someone you loved, slowly, twice.

"He drew this the year I left home," she said. "I know because of the ink. He switched inks that winter. I used to think he was stubborn about everything. Turns out he was stubborn about the right things and I just wasn't looking." She folded it along its old creases, gently, and tucked it inside her coat, against her chest, where maps belong. "The claim re-stakes tomorrow. Legally, properly, in my name as his heir — his *original* coordinates, the ones the resurvey buried. Sef's agreed to be warden of the water. And Ives—" she shook her head— "Ives is filing a report that's going to end her career, naming every man on the scuttling crew, and she's doing it anyway."

"And you?" Theo asked. "What happens to the senior land surveyor of Meridian Consolidated?"

"She resigns by telegram tonight." Mara smiled, and it was her father's smile too, crooked and unkillable. "Then she becomes a prospector's daughter with a surveyor's license and a grudge, which I'm told is a dangerous combination in this country."

* * *

He went back to the wreck one last time before leaving the delta — not to salvage, just to look. The navy had divers of its own now, and they waved him through the cordon line with something like respect, because the story had traveled, the way stories travel along rivers, faster than boats.

The *Meridian Star* lay on her side in forty feet of green murk, exactly where scuttle holes punched from the inside had let her die. Theo swam her length slowly, fins barely moving, and at the instrument bay he stopped and hung in the water and looked at the brass transits and the chronometers and the chart cabinets, all of it tagged with navy evidence markers that fluttered in the current like pale weeds.

Worthless to him now. Seized. Evidence.

He unclipped the little cracked-face depth gauge from his belt — his own, not the wreck's, the one thing down here that was truly his — and held it up in the green light, reading the needle out of pure habit. Forty feet. Same as always. Some things didn't shift their coordinates.

He thought about Rennie, who had asked one question too many. He thought about Elias Voss, who had gripped his hand too hard. He thought about a promise made at gunpoint of time, and kept at last, and what it had cost — a fortune, a partner, three weeks of terror — and what it had bought, which was a signature on four pages and a daughter with a map against her heart.

*Worth it,* he thought, and was surprised to find he believed it.

On his way out of the delta the next morning, running light and empty with Sef at the tiller, he passed the new claim marker standing on the east bank — fresh-cut timber, paint still bright, bearing a name in letters tall enough to read from the water:

**VOSS — ORIGINAL SURVEY**

Mara stood beside it, small in the distance, one hand raised. Not waving, exactly. Acknowledging. The way one river runner acknowledges another who has come through the same water.

Sef glanced at him. "Where to, delta man?"

Theo looked downstream, where the river widened toward the sea and the work was ordinary and the wrecks were only wrecks, and then upstream, where a spring-fed pool fed everything and belonged to no company at all.

"Downstream," he said. "For now."

But even as he said it, his eyes went once more to the east bank, to the marker, to the woman standing beside it — because Mara Voss had mentioned, casually, on the dock, that the original claim file referenced a *second* parcel her father had staked years ago, farther upriver, in country no surveyor had ever legally recorded.

And Theo Marchetti had never in his life been able to leave a coordinate unread.
