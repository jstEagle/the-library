## 22. Downstream

The river had forgiven nothing and forgotten everything, which was, Theo had come to believe, the only honest way for a body of water to behave.

He stood on the deck of the *Magpie* — his boat, or what remained of his boat, refitted with a new transom and a borrowed winch and a name he hadn't bothered to change because changing it would have felt like admitting the old one had died — and watched the morning fog lift off the delta in slow rags. Six months. Six months since the headwater, since the affidavit, since Crane went down into the sinkhole channel with the river closing over him like a ledger being balanced. Six months since Theo Marchetti had last put on a rig and gone down into the green dark where the survey vessel lay on its side in the silt, the wreck nobody names.

Now everybody named it. That was the strange part.

The navy had strung buoy lines around the site in orange and white, official as a courtroom. A corrugated shed stood on the near bank with a naval ensign over the door and a bored sentry outside it, and a sign stenciled in three languages: RESTRICTED WATERS — INVESTIGATION IN PROGRESS. Divers from the naval survey unit had been working the wreck since spring, cataloguing, photographing, recovering. They'd sent Theo a letter — an actual letter, on navy stationery, signed by Lieutenant Cora Ives in her small, ruthless handwriting — informing him that as the salvor of record whose evidence had been material to the case, he was granted one supervised descent to the site, for purposes of "personal closure," a phrase he suspected Ives had written with her tongue firmly in her cheek.

Personal closure. Theo turned the phrase over the way he'd once turned over river stones, looking for what lived underneath.

Below him, through forty feet of tea-colored water, the wreck waited. He could feel it the way you feel a scar in cold weather — a dull insistence, a place where the past hadn't finished with the present yet.

"You're stalling," Sef Okafor said from the wheelhouse. He leaned in the doorway with a mug of coffee he'd made from Theo's coffee, which was the arrangement they'd had since Sef had come back downriver with him in the autumn, ostensibly to help refit the *Magpie*, actually because a man who had spent his life on that water did not sleep well in a town where the river was only a rumor. Sef had taken the warden's post Mara offered him, but the post didn't start until the thaw, and a man needed something to do with his hands in the meantime.

"I'm checking my gear," Theo said.

"You checked your gear twice. You checked it a third time while I was watching you check it a second time." Sef drank his coffee. "Down there, everything is where you left it. That's the whole point of going. So go."

Theo looked at the water. The fog was burning off, and the current ran brown-green and patient, carrying last year's leaves and this year's promises in the same unhurried grip. Somewhere under that surface lay the instruments that had started everything — the gravimeter, the magnetometer, the chart recorder whose falsified traces had stolen a river's birthright and put three men in prison and one man, if the river's own verdict was to be believed, at the bottom of a sinkhole. Somewhere down there was the hole punched from the inside, the scuttle mark he'd found on his first dive, back when the job paid well and he hadn't known that well-paying jobs could be a form of complicity.

He'd sold his salvage rights. That was the arithmetic nobody at the outfitters' could understand. The wreck's contents, once evidence, would eventually be released — and a court order had confirmed his salvor's lien, which meant that when the investigation closed, every instrument, every fitting, every scrap of brass and steel he'd have the legal right to strip would be his to sell. A fortune, the outfitters called it. A year's work, a diver's retirement, a boat with a new engine and a name painted fresh.

He'd signed it over. All of it, to the court's custody, to be held as evidence in the trials, and after the trials — Mara had arranged this, with the particular ruthless competence that was apparently hereditary — to be turned over to the river authority as a memorial site. A museum you couldn't visit. A wreck that would stay a wreck, teaching nobody and reminding everyone.

Rennie would have laughed at him. Rennie, whose burned skiff had been found in the reeds with nothing left of him but a boot and a question. The trials had answered the question, mostly. Crane's crew, three of them, had given up Rennie's name in exchange for lesser charges, and the navy had dragged two miles of the delta in April and found what the delta keeps. There was a grave now, with a stone, with a name spelled correctly, which was more than the river had ever given anybody.

"Rennie would've laughed," Theo said aloud.

"He'd have laughed and then taken half," Sef said. "Go down. I'll watch your line."

* * *

The water closed over him the way it always did — a soft concussion, then silence, then the world narrowing to the circle of his own breath.

Forty feet was nothing. He'd worked two hundred in the old days, in the black cold under freight channels, where the silt was so thick your light just made a brown wall an arm's length away. This was easy water, warm at the surface, cool below, and clear enough that the wreck rose up to meet him out of the murk like a building in a dream — the survey vessel on its port side, half-buried, her name plate furred with a season's growth, the navy's survey tags hanging off her rails like strange fruit.

They'd been thorough. He could see where they'd cut, where they'd tagged, where they'd photographed. Numbered placards were cable-tied at intervals along the hull, little white rectangles that glowed in his torch beam. Evidence. The whole wreck was evidence now, and evidence was the one cargo Theo Marchetti had ever carried that he couldn't sell, and the strange thing — the thing he'd been turning over for six months like a stone in his pocket — was that it felt lighter than anything he'd ever salvaged.

He swam the length of the hull. His torch found the scuttle marks, three of them, punched outward from inside, and he paused at each one the way you'd pause at a grave you'd helped dig without knowing it. On his first dive he'd put his glove against that steel and felt the wrongness of it and said nothing, because the crew boss had told him to log nothing unusual, and he'd told himself the lie divers tell — that it wasn't his business, that the water was just water, that a man had to eat.

A man had to eat. Rennie had eaten his last meal asking questions.

Theo hung in the green light and let the guilt come, because he'd learned that it came anyway, whether you invited it or not, and it was easier to look at than to dodge. Then he swam on, through the breach, into the wreck.

Inside, the navy had already been. Their lights had scoured the compartments; their tags marked everything. But they hadn't taken the instruments — those were still under the court's seal, still awaiting the end of the trials, and there they lay in the chart room exactly as the river had arranged them: the gravimeter in its shock frame, the chart recorder bolted to its desk, the reels of paper fused into a single wet brick by six months of immersion. Theo played his light over them. The falsified traces were still legible where the ink had held — the smooth, confident, lying lines that had moved a gold concession eleven miles from its true source and called it survey work. Eleven miles. He thought of Elias Voss dying in a flophouse with a bullet in him and a map in oilskin, trading the only thing he had left for a promise from a stranger. He thought of Mara Voss standing at the headwater pool at dawn, her father's map open on the rocks, signing her name with a hand that shook and did not stop.

The instruments had recorded all of it. The instruments had recorded the lie and, in their stubborn mechanical honesty, had kept enough of the truth between the lines to hang the liars. They'd done their work. They'd done more than their work.

Theo unclipped the tool roll from his harness.

He'd told himself, on the way down, that he was only going to look. That the decision had been made months ago and he was just here to confirm it, the way you confirm a scar. But his hands had their own opinions. His hands had spent twenty years learning that a diver's job is to bring things up — that the whole religion of salvage is that what's down belongs up, that value is realized by raising, that a man who leaves brass on the bottom is a man who doesn't understand his own trade.

His hands hovered over the chart recorder. The bolts were soft with corrosion; he could have them out in four minutes. The gravimeter would come up in its frame, intact, worth more than the *Magpie* herself.

He thought about the outfitters' faces when he'd signed the release. He thought about a winter of beans and patched drysuits, about the engine he wouldn't be able to afford, about the arithmetic of a life spent underwater that never quite added up to shore.

Then he thought about the pool at the headwater. About the spring-fed clarity of it, the way the light came down through forty feet of the cleanest water he'd ever seen and lit the gravel gold — actual gold, flecks of it in the shallows, the true source Voss had found and lost and found again, the thing the whole rotten edifice of Meridian Consolidated had been built to steal. Mara owned it now. Legally, cleanly, with a claim marker driven into the bank and her father's name on the filings and her own countersignature beneath it, invalidating the fraudulent resurvey the moment the ink dried. The gold was hers. The river was becoming hers, in the slow way rivers belong to anyone — by tending them.

She hadn't sold it. She could have; Meridian's successors had offered, Crane's old partners had offered, men with briefcases and soft voices had offered numbers with commas in them. She'd staked it instead. Hired Sef to warden it. Put a shanty on the old foundation — the same foundation where the bones of Voss's first partner had waited forty years in the dark for somebody to carry them out — and started the long, unglamorous work of taking gold out of a river without killing the river.

Some things are worth more than salvage. He'd heard himself say it to Ives, that last day on the rocks, and she'd looked at him with her flat investigator's stare and said, *That's the first true thing you've said since I pulled you out of the water,* and he'd realized she'd been waiting the whole time to hear it — that her arrest warrant and her alliance had both been, in their different ways, the same question asked twice.

His hands came off the recorder.

He took the tool roll and, instead of a wrench, drew out the little brass tag he'd brought — a salvor's tag, the kind he'd tied to a thousand recoveries, his name and boat stamped into it. He'd had it engraved new, last week, at the jeweler's in the concession town, and the engraver had asked twice if he was sure of the wording.

He tied it to the chart recorder's frame, where the navy's evidence men would find it on their next sweep, and where anyone after them would find it, forever. It read:

**RECOVERED FROM NOTHING. LEFT WHERE IT BELONGS. — T. MARCHETTI, SALVOR**

It wasn't poetry. It wasn't meant to be. It was a log entry, and Theo Marchetti had always believed that a man's log should tell the truth even when the man couldn't.

He swam out through the breach one last time. At the scuttle marks he stopped, and did the thing he'd wanted to do on his first dive and hadn't dared: he put his bare hand flat against the steel, over the hole punched from inside, and held it there while his breath went out slow through the regulator, three full cycles, a diver's version of a bell tolling. For Rennie. For Voss. For the partner whose name nobody had known until the journals gave it back. For the river, which kept everything and forgave what it chose.

Then he went up, into the light.

* * *

Sef didn't ask. That was the thing about Sef; he'd grown up on this water, and people who grow up on water learn that some hauls don't come up, and that the diver who surfaces empty-handed and easy-faced has made the richer catch. He just took the tag of Theo's line, coiled it, and nodded at the wheelhouse.

"Current's with us," he said. "We can make the lower channel by dark."

They ran downriver with the afternoon behind them, the delta unrolling in its patient green miles. The navy buoy lines slid past, orange and white, official. The corrugated shed with its ensign. The sentry, who had watched Theo go down and now watched him come up and had, Theo noticed, logged nothing unusual — which was, he supposed, the navy's version of the courtesy he'd once been paid to extend, and a better use of it.

They passed the mouth of the side channel where he and Sef had slipped the cordon at night, a lifetime ago, with the patrol spotlight sweeping the reeds and his heart going like a pump with a cracked valve. They passed the burned skiff's old mooring, where the reeds had grown back so thick you'd never know anything had died there. The delta was like that. It grew over. It kept everything underneath.

Near the last bend before the lower channel, Sef eased the throttle down without being asked, and Theo saw why.

On the point of land where the river split — where the map had shown one survivable channel and the water had proved the map right — a new post stood in the bank, fresh-cut and tarred at the base, with a brass plate screwed to it that caught the low sun and threw it back in a small, stubborn flash. Mara's marker. He'd seen it in the filings, in the survey photographs, but photographs didn't carry the sound of the current pulling past it, or the smell of the cut wood, or the way the plate was set at exactly the height of a man's eye, so that anyone coming up this water would have to look at it.

**VOSS — ORIGINAL CLAIM, RESTORED. BY RIGHT OF DISCOVERY, BY RIGHT OF BLOOD, BY ORDER OF THE RIVER COMMISSION.**

By order of the river commission. He smiled at that. Mara had fought for those words for four months, and the commission had resisted, and she'd won, because Mara Voss won things the way her father had lost them — stubbornly, at cost, and on paper that would hold.

Below the plate, smaller, in her own hand rather than the engraver's, someone had nailed a second board. Weather was already graying it. It said: *Elias Voss found this. The river kept it for him.*

Theo stood at the rail while Sef held the boat in the current, and he looked at the marker for a long time.

He'd had the map in his hands once — the oilskin, the hand-drawn lines, the second layer of smuggler routes that had taken them through the sinkhole caves and past the bones of the dead. He could have sold it a dozen times over. Crane had offered; the outfitters had offered; there had been one bad night in the concession town when he'd sat with it on the table and a buyer's money on top of it and the lamp turned low, and the only thing that had stopped him was the memory of a dying man's grip on his wrist, which had been stronger than a dying man had any right to.

He'd delivered it. That was all. He'd done the one simple thing he'd promised, and the one simple thing had turned out to be the whole job — the way a single bolt, properly set, holds a hull together. Everything else had come out of that: the affidavit at the headwater, the trials, the wreck with its orange buoys, the woman on the point of land who was rebuilding a life on the foundation of a promise a stranger had kept.

"Take us down," Theo said.

Sef opened the throttle. The *Magpie* came about, and the marker slid astern, the brass plate flashing once more and then going dark, and the current took them the way it took everything — without judgment, without hurry, toward the sea.

Theo went into the wheelhouse and got out his logbook, the paper one, the one he'd kept since his first season. He wrote the date, the site, the descent, forty feet for nineteen minutes, visibility fair, bottom conditions unchanged. Then, in the remarks column, where a salvage man records what he brought up, he wrote: *Nothing. All accounted for.*

He capped the pen. Through the wheelhouse glass the delta opened ahead of them, wide and brown and gold-flecked in the evening light, running down to the sea with everything it knew held safe in its silt — and for the first time in longer than he could measure, Theo Marchetti felt the particular lightness of a man whose hands are empty, whose debts are paid, and whose river, at last, is behind him and ahead of him in equal measure.

Sef glanced over. "Where to?"

Theo looked at the chart, then away from it, out at the water.

"Downstream," he said. "Just downstream for a while."
