## 1. The Long Way Around Stopping

The trick to leaving a place is to start before you arrive.

I'd learned that at fourteen, watching my father pack a dig site the way other men pack suitcases — everything catalogued, nothing left behind but the holes. Edmund Lockridge never unpacked anywhere. He *deployed*. I used to think it was dedication. Later I understood it was the same disease I caught from him: if you never fully arrive, you can never fully be left.

So when the salvage boat *Kestrel's Due* nosed into Greyharbour harbour under a sky the color of old dishwater, I was already standing at the rail with my duffel packed and my exit mapped. Ferry runs on Tuesdays and Fridays. Bus depot two streets up from the chandlery. One road out, which is either a town's worst feature or its best, depending on how badly you want to go.

"You're doing it again," said Marta Voss, our crew chief, coming to stand beside me. She's sixty, built like a bollard, and has the rare gift of being able to read me without pretending it's a compliment.

"Doing what?"

"Planning your funeral for a town that hasn't even met you yet."

"I'm planning my *departure*."

"Same posture." She nodded at my bag. "You've been holding that thing since we cleared the headland. You know what they call people who keep their bags packed, Lockridge?"

"Prepared?"

"Lonely."

I didn't answer, because the honest answer was in there somewhere, and I've spent thirty-one years learning not to hand honesty to people who might use it.

Greyharbour came up out of the mist the way drowned things come up — gradually, then all at once. A crescent of grey stone houses stacked against a hillside like crockery in a careless cupboard. A harbour wall with a lighthouse at one end, white paint gone the yellow of old teeth. Gulls wheeling and screaming their one opinion about everything. Fishing boats, maybe forty of them, riding at moorings with the patient resignation of animals that have learned weather isn't negotiable.

It was beautiful, in the way that places are beautiful right before they take something from you.

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about a life spent moving: you don't collect places. You collect departures.

There was Port Ellinor, when I was nine — salt marshes, my mother teaching me to read the mudflats, which birds fed where, what the different silts smelled like. She could read soil the way priests read scripture. Then she was gone between one Tuesday and the next, and my father told me she'd gone to her sister's, and I believed him because nine-year-olds believe their fathers, and we left Port Ellinor three weeks later because staying near her absence was apparently unbearable to him. I never saw the marshes again. Sometimes I still smell them in my sleep.

There was Kettering Vale, when I was sixteen — first real friend, a boy named Tomas whose family ran the pub by the quarry. I stayed a full year, which for us was practically homesteading. Then my father found something in the quarry cuts, got his funding pulled over a dispute about provenance, and we were gone inside a fortnight. I wrote to Tomas. He wrote back twice. The third letter came back marked *no such address*, and I understood that distance doesn't just stretch, it dissolves.

There was the dredging job off Corran Point when I was twenty-four, where I almost married a woman named Sefa who laughed like weather breaking. I actually unpacked for her. Bought a bookshelf. You have to understand what a bookshelf means to someone like me — it's a load-bearing declaration of intent. Then the salvage contract collapsed, the money dried up, and I watched her face do the arithmetic of long-distance and decide against it. I sold the bookshelf to the landlady. It felt like amputating something that had only just finished healing into place.

After that, the rule hardened into bone: work the job, learn the town, love nothing that can't fit in a duffel. Read the soil, read the tides, read the people — but read them the way you read a current, for what they'll do to you, not for what you wish they'd do.

It works. That's the miserable part. It works perfectly. I have never once been blindsided by leaving, because I'm always halfway gone already.

What it doesn't do is fill the flat hours. The ones on trains, on night watches, in rented rooms where the walls are the color of apology. In those hours the rule feels less like armor and more like a shell I grew so thick I can't hear anything through it, including myself.

But you can't say that out loud on a working deck, so instead I said, "Greyharbour's a six-week contract, tops. Hull survey on the *Maren Lee*, some recovery work on the wreck field. Then we're south for winter."

Marta looked at me for a long moment with those eyes that had watched forty crews come apart and reassemble. "You know," she said, "the saddest thing I ever saw wasn't a drowning. It was a man who survived one and spent the rest of his life swimming away from water."

"The sea's fine," I said. "It's the shore that gets you."

She snorted and went below, and I stood there holding my bag like a talisman, and Greyharbour held still in the mist and let me misjudge it.

* * *

We lodged above the Salt & Anchor, a pub that smelled of diesel, yeast, and forty years of arguments that had mostly resolved themselves. The landlady, a broad woman named Mrs. Tregowan with forearms like hawser rope, took my name down in a ledger with a pencil worn to a stub.

"Lockridge," she repeated. "Any relation to the professor?"

The question landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. "Which professor?"

"Edmund Lockridge. The archaeologist. Big noise in the papers a few years back — the Verren find, or whatever he called it." She waved the pencil vaguely seaward. "Word is he's coming here himself. Been buying up charts at the chandlery. Asking after the bay."

The room did the thing rooms do when your past walks into them — contracted, tilted, filled suddenly with too much air. I kept my voice level through sheer professional habit. "Never heard of him."

Mrs. Tregowan looked at me the way Marta looked at me, the way everyone eventually looks at me, with that patient expression that says *liar, but an honest one about it somehow.* "Well," she said, and handed me a key on a wooden fob shaped like a fish. "Room three. Hot water till eight. And mind the third stair, it lies."

Everything lies, I thought, climbing it. Stairs, fathers, towns. The trick is knowing which lies are structural.

My room was small and clean and faced the harbour, which meant I could stand at the window and watch the water do its slow gray breathing while I unpacked — properly unpacked, clothes in the dresser, boots by the door — and then repacked, because habits are the only inheritance I trust.

Except this time, halfway through folding my second shirt, I stopped.

Through the glass, the bay lay exposed under the ebbing tide further than any bay has a right to lie bare. Sandbanks I'd charted as hazards that morning stood dry and glistening. The channel markers lolled in inches of water. Old men on the harbour wall were pointing and muttering, and even from here I could see the particular quality of their gesturing — not the casual pointing of people noting weather, but the urgent, half-afraid pointing of people witnessing something their grandparents had warned them about.

And far out, at the edge of the revealed seabed, something dark broke the sand. An arch, maybe. A doorway. Too regular to be geology, too far to be sure.

I should have turned back to my packing. I should have finished the shirt, checked the ferry times, done all the small faithful rituals of a man already leaving.

Instead I set the shirt down, picked up my camera, and went downstairs into a town that hadn't met me yet, toward a shoreline that was showing more than it meant to.

Later — much later — I would understand that this was the moment I stopped leaving Greyharbour and started arriving. But standing there on the wet cobbles with the wind pulling at my coat and the sea drawn back like a held breath, all I knew was the old familiar itch under my sternum, the one that had never once led me wrong.

Curiosity. Which is just loneliness wearing better clothes.

The old man on the harbour wall — weathered down to leather and whiskers, mending a net with hands that moved without him — watched me approach and shook his head before I'd said a word.

"Don't," he said.

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're thinking." He jerked his chin at the exposed sands, at the dark arch far out. "Tide's out further than it's been in a hundred years, and folk are already walking out there like the sea won't notice. Sea notices." He tied off a knot with terrible gentleness. "Sea always notices."

I lifted my camera instead of answering, and framed the arch in the viewfinder, and pressed the shutter.

Somewhere behind the old man's eyes, something that might have been pity crossed like a shadow of a gull.

"Bram Corrigan," he said, as if introducing himself to a headstone. "And you'll be wanting the story, then. They always want the story."

The tide breathed out further. Far out on the bared sands, the Hollow of Saint Verren stood open in the evening light like a mouth deciding whether to speak.
