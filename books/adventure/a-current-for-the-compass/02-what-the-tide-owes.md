## 2. What the Tide Owes

The sea went out like a guest who'd remembered an urgent appointment elsewhere.

I was on the *Kestrel's* deck, drinking coffee that tasted of rust and regret, when Devan Osei shouted from the bow. "Lockridge! You want to see this or not?"

I did not, particularly. I had seen the sea do plenty of things in my years on salvage crews — retreat sulkily after storms, surge with theatrical menace, lie flat and glassy as a liar's face. But I climbed down anyway, because the alternative was staying aboard with my own thoughts, and I'd spent thirty-one years learning to avoid that particular company.

Greyharbour's bay was emptying. Not draining — *emptying*, the way a pocket empties when someone turns it inside out to prove they've got nothing left. The water pulled back past the harbor wall, past the moorings, past the sandbar where we'd been told never to take the dinghy, and kept going, revealing a seabed no living person in that town had ever seen. Kelp forests lay flattened like hair combed by a giant hand. Old wrecks surfaced — ribs of them, black and split, ships that had gone down before anyone thought to write their names down. A forest of stone pinnacles stood up out of the muck, dripping, hung with weed, looking like the fingers of something that had been waiting a long time to be counted.

And at the far end of the bay, half a mile out across ground that should have been under twelve fathoms of water, there was an archway.

I'd like to tell you my first instinct was scientific interest. It wasn't. My first instinct was the same one that had kept me alive through every job from the Orkneys to the Azores: *that is exactly the kind of thing that gets people killed.* My second instinct, arriving late and unwelcome as always, was: *but look at it.*

"Once-in-a-century tide," Devan said, grinning. He was twenty-six and treated everything the sea did as a personal invitation. "Harbormaster says his grandfather saw it once, when he was a boy. Says the old folk called it God turning over in His sleep."

"And what do the old folk call that?" I nodded at the archway.

Devan's grin flickered. Just for a second. "They don't call it anything," he said. "That's sort of the point."

* * *

You learn things fast in a small fishing town, mostly by saying nothing and buying rounds. By noon I knew three facts about Greyharbour's new landmark. One: nobody would walk out to it. Two: nobody would say its name, if it had one. Three: everybody watched me watching it, the way you watch a stranger reach toward a stove you know is hot.

Bram Corrigan found me on the seawall that afternoon. He was seventy-something, built like a bollard, with hands that looked like they'd been carved from driftwood by someone who'd run out of patience halfway through. He sat down next to me without asking, which I respected, and handed me a flask, which I respected more.

"You're the salvage lot," he said. "Up from Portsmouth."

"That's us."

"Heard your skipper's thinking about walking out to the arch while the tide's down."

"He's thinking about it."

"Don't." Bram said it the way you'd tell someone not to step off a cliff — no drama, just arithmetic. Then he took a long pull from the flask and looked out at the thing itself. From the seawall it was smaller than it had seemed from the boat, a dark semicircle maybe fifteen feet high, set into a headland of rock that the retreating sea had peeled open like a letter. But even at this distance it had a quality I couldn't name — a kind of *attention*. Standing water pooled black in its mouth. The light didn't seem to go in so much as get politely declined.

"What is it?" I asked.

Bram was quiet long enough that I thought he wasn't going to answer. Then he said, "My grandmother called it the Hollow of Saint Verren. Saint Verren was a hermit, back when. Lived out here when this whole bay was dry land, before the sea came in. They say he dug himself a church into the rock, and the sea took it, and what's left of the door is what you're looking at." He shrugged. "Whether any of that's true, I couldn't say. What I can say is what my grandmother said, and hers said before her."

"Which is?"

"The Hollow shows you what you most need to see." He turned and looked at me properly then, and his eyes were pale grey and very steady. "And it keeps what you can't bear to lose."

I laughed. I'm not proud of it, but I laughed — a short, professional sound, the laugh of a man who has heard a lot of sailors' stories and filed most of them under *weather*. "That's convenient," I said. "A cave that does exactly what legends need it to do."

"Aye," Bram agreed mildly. "Convenient. That's one word for it." He stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked down at me. "Your father teach you the tides, lad?"

The question landed somewhere below my ribs, which annoyed me considerably. "Something like that," I said.

"Mm." He nodded out at the bay. "Tide'll be down till near six tomorrow morning. After that she comes back, and she won't come back like this again in your lifetime or mine. Whatever's out there will be underwater again, and everyone can go back to pretending it isn't." He tucked the flask away. "People are good at pretending. It's the one harvest this coast grows better than fish."

He walked off along the seawall, and I sat there a while longer, watching the light fail over the archway, telling myself I felt nothing in particular.

* * *

Here's the thing about me that my father never understood, and that I only understood myself around my fourth year of not staying anywhere: I read ground the way other people read faces. Give me a cut bank, a spoil heap, a test pit, and I'll tell you what happened there and roughly when and who paid for it. Soil doesn't perform. Soil doesn't have anything to gain by lying to you. My father taught me that — taught me stratigraphy before I could ride a bike, taught me that the earth keeps honest accounts even when people don't. It's the one lesson of his I kept, and I kept it the way you keep a knife: useful, and careful where you hold it.

So the next morning, a little before five, with the tide still holding its extraordinary breath, I walked out across the bay floor.

I told myself it was survey work. That was even true, in the way that most of my excuses were true — technically accurate, morally evasive. The *Kestrel*'s charter did include mapping exposed features, and a previously unrecorded structure was exactly the kind of thing that made a report look thorough. I carried a camera, a notebook, a ranging pole, and a headlamp whose batteries I checked twice, because I am many things but not stupid.

The bay floor was astonishing. Anemones the size of dinner plates lay stranded and closing slowly, patient as monks. A whale's rib cage arched up out of the mud, ancient, its bones gone the color of old ivory. I stopped twice to photograph wrecks, once to photograph a mirror-still pool full of trapped silver fish, and each time I caught myself moving faster than the work required, drawn forward like a man walking slightly downhill.

The archway was bigger up close. Much bigger. And older — that I could read plainly in the stone, in the way the weathering ran, in the barnacle lines that marked centuries of high-water marks climbing the jambs. Someone had built this door when the land here was dry, which meant someone had built it before the sea arrived, which meant the geology of this coast had a story the charts didn't tell. My pulse picked up. This was real. Under all the legend and the locals' superstitious silence, this was *stratigraphy*, this was honest ground, and honest ground was the one thing in the world that had never once let me down.

Behind the arch, the rock opened into a passage. Not a hole — a passage, cut, with tool marks faintly visible under the weed, descending into the dark at a gentle grade. Water gleamed at the bottom of it, forty feet in, black and still, waiting with the patience of everything on this coast.

I stood at the threshold for a long time. Long enough for the cold coming off that opening to soak through my jacket. Long enough to notice that my heart was doing something complicated.

Because here's what I hadn't told Bram, or Devan, or anyone: I knew what I most needed to see. I'd known for eleven years. It had a face, and it had left when I was twenty, and it had taken my mother's side of every story with it. If some hole in the world wanted to show me that — well. That was precisely why I didn't believe in holes in the world. You don't hand your worst grief to a stranger, and a cave is a stranger.

But the camera was in my hands, and photographing the entrance was sensible, defensible, report-worthy. So I raised it and took six frames of the archway, the tool-marked jambs, the waterline stains, the dark passage going down. Professional. Thorough. Nothing a man would need to explain to himself later.

Then, because the light was good and the tide wouldn't come again in my lifetime, I took a seventh frame — closer, straight down the throat of the entrance, flash off, exposure long. In the viewfinder the darkness looked less like absence than like a held breath.

When I lowered the camera, I could have sworn — could have *sworn*, and I don't swear things lightly — that the stillness at the bottom of the passage had moved. Risen, maybe. Come nearer. Or perhaps it was only my eyes adjusting, only the ordinary treachery of wanting to see something.

I wrote the coordinates in my notebook. I wrote *arched entrance, worked stone, pre-inundation origin, passage descends approx. 40 ft to standing water.* I did not write *it looked back.*

Walking back across the bay floor with the sun coming up behind me, I passed Bram Corrigan mending nets on the harbor steps. He looked at me, then at the mud on my boots, and shook his head slowly — not angry, not surprised. Sad, almost, the way you're sad about weather coming.

"Told you not to," he said.

"You told me people are good at pretending," I said. "I'm just doing my part."

That earned me half a smile, gone as soon as it came. "Tide turns tomorrow night," he said. "After that, the Hollow goes back to sleep, and whatever you saw out there, you'll carry it whether you like it or not." He bent back to his nets. "Best decide now what you're carrying it *for*."

I didn't have an answer then. I had a camera full of photographs, a notebook full of coordinates, and a low-grade fever of curiosity that I recognized, with sinking certainty, as the same sickness that had put my father's name on expedition permits all over the world.

Two days later, a chartered research vessel came into Greyharbour flying survey flags, and a man with my jawline and my mother's impatience stood at the rail, scanning the shore.

I knew before anyone told me. Some currents you feel before you see them.
