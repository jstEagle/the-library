## 3. Lockridge, Senior

I'd been in Greyharbour nine days when the news came up the hill like weather — the kind you smell before you see it. I was on the *Merry Adeline*, prying a brass fitting off a wrecked kelp trawler, when Devan Rook climbed the ladder two rungs at a time with his face doing something complicated.

"You'll want to hear this sitting down," he said.

"I'm sitting down."

"You'll want to hear it *more* sitting down." He braced himself on the rail. "Edmund Lockridge's boat docked an hour ago. The *Verity*. Whole expedition crew, university banners, a journalist. He's giving a speech on the quay."

I put down the wrench. I picked it up again, because my hands needed something stupid to do. "He's not here for me."

"Course not," Devan said, in the tone of a man agreeing with a child about the tooth fairy. "Small fishing town, once-in-a-century tide, mysterious cave that half the county won't name. Why would the most famous archaeologist on the coast come *here*?"

I looked at the brass fitting in my hand. It was green with sea rot and worth maybe four pounds. I set it down carefully, the way you set down something you might need to throw later.

* * *

I want to be clear about something before I go on, because the story people tell about my father and me has a shape to it — estranged child, famous parent, dramatic reconciliation — and shapes are how lies get comfortable. There was no reconciliation coming. I'd stopped expecting one around the time I turned nineteen, which was also, not coincidentally, the last time I'd seen him.

What there was, instead, was a habit. His and mine both. He collected discoveries; I collected exits. Every town I'd ever lived in, I could tell you the exact day I'd decided to leave it, and the exact day I actually left, and the gap between those two days was always the part that hurt. Greyharbour was supposed to be a six-week salvage job. I'd already scouted the bus schedule to Inverness. I had a duffel packed under my bunk like a fire drill.

So when I walked down to the quay that afternoon, I told myself I was going for the same reason everyone else was: free entertainment. A famous man making a speech about rocks. I even brought a sandwich.

The quay was packed. Greyharbour doesn't get much, and it knows how to show up for it. The *Verity* sat at the end of the pier like a white tooth, all gleam and money, and in front of her stood a cluster of people in expedition jackets — navy blue, gold trim, the kind of clothing that says *we have funding and you don't* — and at the center of them, smaller than I remembered and somehow louder, was Edmund Lockridge.

He'd gone grey at the temples. He'd gained a certain authority in the hands, if that makes sense — the hands of a man who'd spent thirty years being photographed holding things. He was mid-sentence, and even from thirty feet away I could hear the machinery of him working: the warm gravel voice, the pauses calibrated to let applause land, the way he said *the Hollow of Saint Verren* like he was introducing a person he was fond of.

"—and I want to say, before anything else, that I came here because of the people of Greyharbour. Your grandparents kept the stories alive when the universities laughed at them. Well." A smile, practiced down to the millimeter. "We're not laughing now."

The crowd made a sound like a tide coming in.

And that's when he saw me. I want to be honest about the mechanics of it, because it wasn't magic and it wasn't accident — or it was accident, but he turned an accident into a plan in under two seconds, and that's the most honest thing I can tell you about my father. His eyes found mine over the heads of the crowd, and something moved behind them, fast, like a man checking a lock, and then he raised one hand and pointed directly at me.

"There," he said. "You see that young person there, in the salvage oilskins? That's my child. Oscar Lockridge." A beat, perfectly placed. "The best reader of ground I ever trained."

The crowd turned. Two hundred faces, all at once, all of them mine. Somewhere in the back Bram Corrigan muttered something that was probably a blessing and was probably not. I stood there with my sandwich going warm in my hand and understood, with the clarity of a man watching a trap close, that I had just been hired. There had been no question asked. There had been no *yes* given. And I was hired.

That was Edmund's genius, if you want to call it that — and God knows I've gone back and forth on the word. He didn't ask people for things. He announced them as already true, and then dared the world to disagree. It had made him famous. It had made our whole family, what there was of it, into a series of things he'd announced.

I should have walked away. I want that on the record too. Any sensible person would have walked away, eaten their sandwich, gone back to the *Merry Adeline*, caught the Thursday bus to Inverness, and lived a small clean life with no Lockridges in it.

Instead I stood there while the crowd parted around me like water around a post, and he came down the pier to meet me, and I heard myself say, "Hello, Father," in a voice I didn't recognize — flat, and polite, and steady as a table.

"Oscar." He said my name like he was testing the weight of it. Up close he smelled of cedar and pipe smoke and, under it, the specific salt-and-ink smell of expedition planning. "You look well."

"I look employed."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth — the old acknowledgment, the one that used to pass between us over dinner tables when I was small, the secret language of two people who'd noticed the same absurdity. I hated that it still worked. I hated that some muscle in my chest unclenched at the sight of it, like a dog that hadn't been walked in years and still remembered the leash.

"The tide's coming in Thursday," he said. "The lowest in a century. The Hollow opens, and I intend to be the first trained archaeologist through that arch. I need diggers. Good ones." He looked at my hands, which have my mother's knuckles and his patience. "I need *you*."

"You need my surname," I said. "It photographs well."

"I have always needed your surname. That's what surnames are *for*." He said it lightly, and that was the wound of it — he could say true, cutting things lightly, the way other people passed the salt. "But I need the rest of you too. The part that reads soil like scripture. I taught you that."

"No," I said. "You taught me to pack. Mama taught me the soil."

Something went through his face then, quick as a fish under ice — there and gone, and I'd have missed it if I hadn't spent my whole childhood learning to read that particular water. Grief, maybe. Or guilt. Or the performance of grief, which after thirty years of practice is indistinguishable, even to the performer.

"I don't have time for this," he said quietly, and it was the truest thing he'd said all day, and somehow it was the thing that got under my skin, because it was *true of him always*, all our lives — no time, always mid-expedition, always a departure board flickering somewhere behind his eyes.

"No," I agreed. "You never did."

He looked at me for a long moment. The crowd had gone tactfully distant, a ring of eavesdroppers pretending to admire the *Verity*. Down the quay, gulls argued over fish guts. The whole scene was ordinary and unbearable.

"Six weeks' wages," he said. "Full crew share of anything we find. And Oscar —" and here his voice dropped into something almost human, almost the voice from before, from the years when he still came home between digs and taught me to read strata in the garden like other fathers taught football — "I'm asking. Not announcing. *Asking.*"

And there it was. The hook, baited with the one thing he'd never once offered me, offered now, publicly, on a quay, where refusing it would cost me something in front of two hundred people. I understood the mechanism completely. I could see every gear of it turning.

I said yes anyway.

I want to be able to tell you I said yes for a good reason. That I meant to watch him, to keep an eye on the old man, to protect the expedition from his shortcuts. I've told myself all of those since, and some of them might even be true. But the truth is simpler and uglier and warmer: he asked, and he had never asked, and some starved thing in me that I'd been feeding on bus schedules and packed duffels for fifteen years stood up and howled *yes* before my brain could vote.

I took the contract he held out. I signed it against the bollard with a pen June Okafor — the expedition photographer, sharp-eyed, amused — handed me without being asked, like she'd been waiting for the moment with a pen ready, which I suspect she had.

"Welcome aboard, Lockridge," she said. "Which one are you?"

"The one who reads soil," I said.

"The famous one," said a big man behind her — Devan Rook, though I didn't know his name yet — "or the honest one?"

I looked at my father, already turning back to the crowd, already mid-sentence, arms rising toward the cave he hadn't entered yet, the discovery he hadn't made yet, talking about it in the past tense the way he always talked about everything, as though the world were simply catching up to what he'd already decided was true.

"Ask me Thursday," I said.

* * *

That night I repacked the duffel. Not unpacked — I want to be clear — repacked. Tighter. The bus schedule to Inverness stayed folded in my jacket pocket, soft as cloth now from handling, and I didn't throw it away, and I didn't look at it either, which is its own kind of looking.

Bram Corrigan found me on the sea wall around midnight, where I'd gone to do what I always do in a new place: sit with the water and let it tell me what it's doing. He lowered himself down beside me with the sound of a ship taking on cargo, and we watched the bay for a while.

"You signed on with him," he said. Not a question. News travels in Greyharbour faster than light, and with less accuracy.

"I did."

"He'll want the Hollow." Bram's hands were knotted around an unlit pipe, an old habit of a man who'd given up the tobacco but not the wanting. "And the Hollow gives what it gives. Shows you what you most need to see. Keeps what you can't bear to lose." He turned and looked at me with eyes the color of old rope. "You thought about what you can't bear to lose, Oscar Lockridge?"

I thought about a duffel packed under a bunk. A bus schedule soft as cloth. Fifteen years of leaving before I could be left.

"No," I said.

"Then you're the one it'll want," Bram said, and got up off the wall, and left me alone with the water, which was already, quietly, beginning to go out.
