## 4. Into the Hollow

Slack tide came at 6:14 in the morning, and I was awake for it, which felt like a personal failing.

I'd slept four hours in a rented room above the chandlery, on a mattress that had opinions about my spine, and spent the other three staring at the ceiling running the arithmetic of escape routes. That's what I do. Every town I land in, I map the exits before I unpack — bus schedules, ferry times, which roads flood, which roads lie about not flooding. Greyharbour had one road out and a ferry that ran Thursdays if it felt like it. Not ideal. But I'd been here three days and hadn't lost anyone yet, so by my standards the place was practically a vacation.

The team assembled at the archway while the sea held its breath. Edmund stood at the front in his expedition jacket, the one with his name embroidered over the heart like he was afraid of forgetting it, and gave us the speech. The one about history and patience and what we owed the past. He'd given versions of it at every dig of my childhood. I used to mouth along under my breath like a kid at mass.

"Oscar." He caught me at the threshold, hand on my shoulder, brief as a brand. "Stay close to the rear. You've got the best eye for ground."

It was a compliment shaped like an order, which was the only shape he knew. I said nothing and went to the rear.

The archway was taller than it had looked from the ridge — a black doorway in living rock, its lintel furred with barnacles that must have taken centuries, which meant the sea took this cave back more often than it gave it up. The water lay flat as poured glass all the way to the horizon. Somewhere out there, the tide was turning around to come home. We had maybe five hours. Everyone kept saying *five hours* like it was a lot.

"Anyone who wants to wait outside," Edmund said, "now's the time."

Nobody moved. Curiosity is a debt collector; it always finds you eventually.

We went in.

June Okafor

My brother Tomás hated caves. That was the joke of my whole life — the photographer married to darkness, the diver afraid of overhead rock. He'd hold the torch for me on night shoots and complain the entire time, beautifully, the way he complained about everything, turning fear into performance until you forgot he was scared at all.

So when the passage opened up and the walls lit blue — veins of something luminous threading through the stone like lightning frozen mid-strike — my first thought wasn't *geology*. It was *Tomás would lose his mind.*

"Strontianite, probably," said Dr. Vashti, our geologist, running her light along a vein. "Or fluorite. The phosphorescence—"

"It's beautiful," I said, because someone had to say the obvious thing or the obvious thing was going to crack us all open.

Eleven of us went in. Lockridge senior at the point, Oscar sweeping the rear, and the rest strung between like beads on a wire. The floor was smooth as a church aisle, worn by ten thousand years of tide, and the air tasted mineral and cold, like drinking from a well. My headlamp made a small trembling circle on the rock ahead of me, and beyond that circle the blue veins did their slow work, brightening and dimming in a rhythm I couldn't quite catch. Almost like breathing. Almost like something deciding whether to notice us.

We walked twenty minutes and the entrance behind us shrank to a grey coin, then a thread, then nothing. Nobody said anything about that. Everybody thought it.

Then the passages started lying.

Vashti marked junctions with chalk arrows, standard practice, but twice we came around a bend and found her own arrows pointing back the way we'd come. She swore she'd turned left. The wall said otherwise. The third time it happened, Devan laughed — one sharp note, no humor in it — and Edmund said, "Acoustics disorient people. Stay tight. Count your steps."

I counted mine. At step four hundred I stopped hearing the sea.

That was the moment, I think. When the sound of the ocean fell away and there was nothing underneath us but silence and blue light, and Tomás stepped out of the dark ahead of me.

Not a hallucination. I know what those look like — I've been sleep-deprived on three continents. This was worse, because it was *correct*. His bad knee, the way he favored it. The scar through his eyebrow from the boat winch. The exact crease at the corner of his mouth when he was about to say something kind disguised as something annoying.

"You're dead," I told him. My voice came out steady, which surprised us both.

"And you're talking to a rock formation," he said, in his voice, with his grin. "One of us is handling it better."

Behind me, someone whimpered. Ahead, the blue veins pulsed once, slow, like a great eye deciding to stay open.

I raised my camera. Old habit, armor, excuse. Through the viewfinder: empty passage, glowing stone, nobody there.

Through the viewfinder, he waved.

Oscar

Here's what I know about my mother's voice: I have not heard it since I was nine years old, and I could still pick it out of a crowd of thousands. Some sounds the body files under *emergency* and never deletes.

So when it came drifting down the passage — *Oscar. Oscar, love, mind the edge* — I did what any reasonable person does. I stopped walking and gripped the rock until my knuckles went white, and I told myself, in my own voice, firmly: *cave acoustics. Echoes. The human ear pattern-matches, it's a documented phenomenon, you are fine.*

"Oscar, love, mind the edge."

There was no edge. There was flat stone and blue light and eleven people pretending they weren't hearing things, and my mother's voice saying the exact sentence she said every time we walked the sea wall at Whitsand, when I was small enough that falling in was a real possibility and her hand was the fixed point of the entire world.

I minded the edge anyway. There was nothing to mind. I minded it thoroughly.

Ahead, June had gone rigid, camera up, shooting at nothing. Devan was reciting dive tables under his breath like a rosary. And Edmund — Edmund walked on with his chin up and his eyes forward, jaw set, listening to whatever the cave was saying to him and refusing, with his whole spine, to let it matter.

That, more than the voice, told me what kind of place we were in. Because I knew that posture. I'd grown up behind it. It was the posture of a man hearing an accusation and planning his rebuttal.

"Lockridge." I caught up to him. Kept my voice low. "How far to the chamber?"

"Survey says four hundred meters past the second gallery."

"The survey your funding paid for. The survey based on work done—" I stopped myself. Not here. Not in front of the crew. "Fine. Four hundred meters."

He glanced at me then, just once, and in the blue light his face looked carved and old and briefly, horribly honest. "You hear it too," he said. Not a question.

"I don't hear anything," I said. "Neither do you. Keep walking."

June Okafor

Tomás walked with me for a while. Or the cave wore him like a mask; I understood that even as I matched my stride to his. Understanding didn't make it stop hurting. It just meant I hurt clearly.

"How's Mum?" he asked, and I laughed, wet and awful, because that was the question he'd ask first, always, before asking anything about himself.

"She's good. She plants too many tomatoes."

"Course she does." He nodded like this confirmed some cosmic order. Then, quieter, in the tone he saved for the true things: "June. You should turn around."

"We just got in."

"You should turn around *soon.*"

And that was when the water spoke.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. A sound like a long exhale somewhere behind us, deep in the dark where the entrance had been, and then a hiss, and then Devan shouting from the rear — Devan, who never shouted — *"Water! Water in the low gallery, MOVE!"*

We ran. Eleven people who had entered a cave as an expedition now ran through it as prey, headlamps swinging, blue veins strobing past, and behind us the sound grew from a hiss into a roar with weight behind it, the sea coming home early, hours early, filling the low ground like it had an appointment.

I saw the entrance chamber last, from the top of the rise — saw the archway that had been a doorway into wonder now a black slot vomiting white water, the tide slamming shut behind us like a door with a lock on the inside.

Edmund got us counted. Eleven heads, eleven lamps, all present. His voice held. I'll give him that; his voice always holds, it's the rest of him I'm less sure about.

"Secondary passage," he said, pointing deeper in. "The survey shows a high route east. We move away from the water and we wait out the cycle. Nobody panics."

Nobody panicked. We were all too busy listening to the sea settle into the space where the way out had been, patient as rent.

Tomás was gone. Of course he was. But his last words hung in my ears with the salt smell rising off the new water, and I noticed that Oscar Lockridge had drifted to the rear again without being ordered there, and was looking back at the flooded archway with an expression I recognized from mirrors on bad mornings.

Calculating exits. Finding none.

"Five hours," Devan said weakly. "You said five hours."

"The tide came early," Oscar said quietly. "Tides don't come early."

Behind us, deeper in the dark, the blue veins pulsed — once, twice — slow and deliberate, like something breathing. Like something that had finally decided to notice us.

Like something that had been waiting a very long time to be paid.
