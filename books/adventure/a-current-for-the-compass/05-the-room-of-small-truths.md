## 5. The Room of Small Truths

The cave had stopped pretending to be a cave. Somewhere behind us, the passage we'd come through was a memory with water in it, and ahead, the phosphorescent veins in the rock threw light like a fever dream — green and blue, pulsing faintly, as though the Hollow had a heartbeat and it was bored of us already.

"Everyone breathe," I said, because it was that or say something less useful.

There were six of us left in this branch: me, Edmund, Devan, June, and the two grad students whose names I'd learned an hour ago and would now certainly never forget — Priya and Callum, huddled together under June's camera light like the world's most academic litter of kittens.

We'd been walking for what felt like a day and was probably forty minutes. The passages here didn't rearrange, exactly. They just declined to be the same twice. I'd been marking the walls with chalk every thirty meters, and twice we'd come across my own marks going in a direction I had no memory of walking. The third time, I stopped writing it down.

"Lockridge." Devan's voice, low and flat. He'd been flat since the water came in, which was better than panicked, but only just. "That light ahead. That's not mineral."

He was right. The glow ahead had edges. Corners. The tidy, domestic glow of a camp lantern.

We rounded a bend and there it was: a chamber maybe ten meters across, dry-floored, high-ceilinged, and furnished. A folding camp table. Two sleeping bags, rolled and strapped. A camp stove. Crates — good crates, expedition-grade, stenciled with the university's logo. And strung along one wall, a line of electric lanterns, plugged into a battery bank that someone had carried in on their back, piece by piece, through passages that rearranged themselves for strangers.

The chamber knew its owner. That was the only explanation I could construct. The cave had been rude to us and hospitable to him.

Edmund stopped walking so abruptly that Priya walked into his back.

"Dad," I said. "Whose camp is this?"

And here is the thing about my father that I have spent thirty-one years trying to explain to people who think famous means simple: Edmund Lockridge is not a good liar. He is a great one. A good liar fidgets. A great liar goes still, and warm, and puts his hand on your shoulder like the truth is a heavy thing he's offering to carry for you.

"Oscar." My name in his mouth, pitched low, meant only for me. "I can explain."

"You can explain." I looked at the crates. At the sleeping bags — two, both used, both his brand, the kind he'd bought in bulk after Mother left because he said making decisions about bedding was beneath him. At the date scratched into the crate lid in his handwriting: *March.* Four months ago. "You can explain why your camp has been sitting in a cave that was under nine fathoms of water until yesterday."

"Oscar—" Devan said.

"One moment." I held up a hand without looking at him. Bad manners. I'd regret it.

Edmund moved to the table and, God help him, put the kettle on. The hiss of the little burner was obscene in that silence. "Sit down. All of you. You're wet, you're cold, and the water outside that gallery won't drop for hours. We have time. Let me do this properly."

"Properly," Devan repeated.

"Properly," Edmund said, with dignity, and poured tea into six tin mugs like a man performing civilization at the edge of the world. It was, I will admit, the most fatherly thing he'd done in a decade. It was also, I would learn in the next ten minutes, the most criminal.

* * *

He told it as a story, because everything with Edmund is a story. He'd found the Hollow in the winter. A fisherman's chart anomaly, a hunch, a rope and a headlamp and three days alone. He'd found the antechamber — this one, or one like it; the cave apparently kept several — and in it, carvings. Pre-Christian maritime glyphs, a culture nobody had documented on this coast. The find of a generation. The find of his life.

Except.

"Except the carvings were shallow," he said. "Weathered wrong. I've spent four months convincing myself otherwise, and I'm done convincing. They're not old, Oscar. They're not even real. Someone carved them — or *I* — " and here his voice did something I'd never heard it do, which was crack, "— I don't even know anymore. I came back in March with plaster and a chisel and I told myself I was making a record of them before the sea took them. And then the sea took the entrance, and I told the university the site was intact and time-critical, and the money came, and here we are."

"Here we are," I said.

I walked to the crates. I opened the first one. Inside, packed in straw like eggs: artifacts. A soapstone lamp. A bone comb. A shard of pottery with a painted wave motif I recognized because I'd seen it in the expedition's funding proposal, in a photograph so crisp it could have been taken in a studio.

It had been taken in a studio. The straw was wrong. Archaeologists pack finds in acid-free tissue; this was craft-store straw, the kind sold by the bale for wreath-making. I knew because I'd packed wreaths for a living for one terrible autumn in a town I would not be naming.

I lifted the lamp. It was light. Too light. I turned it over and found, on the base, a seam — a casting seam, hairline-fine, the kind you only see if you've spent your childhood in a conservation lab learning how fakes are caught.

Plaster. Painted plaster. An entire civilization, cast in plaster, in a workshop somewhere, and planted in a cave by a man the journals called *the conscience of the field.*

"Say it out loud," Edmund said quietly. "Whatever you're thinking. Say it, and then hear me."

"I'm thinking," I said, "that you hired me. Publicly. In front of the whole crew. The estranged child, the humanizing detail, the story the funders loved." I set the lamp down with a care that surprised me. "You didn't hire me because you wanted me back. You hired me because nobody audits a family reunion."

The chamber got very quiet. Even the phosphorescence seemed to dim, as though the cave were leaning in.

"That's not true," he said.

"Which part?"

"Any of it. All of it. I don't—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I needed you. Both things are true, Oscar. I needed the digger and I needed — I wanted — " He gestured helplessly at the space between us, the whole estranged width of it. "You think I don't know what it looks like? I know exactly what it looks like. I've had four months in this cave to look at it."

"The cave shows you what you most need to see," Priya said faintly, from the wall. "Bram Corrigan's story. I thought it was a story."

"It keeps what you can't bear to lose," Callum added, and then, catching Devan's look, shut up.

"Here's what I need you to see," Edmund said, and now he crossed to me, and took my shoulders, and his hands were shaking, which for Edmund was like another man weeping. "The tide gate is shut. The secondary passage is a maybe. If we get out of here and I report the fraud, the expedition dies, the funding is clawed back, and everyone in this room spends two years in depositions. But if — *if* — the artifacts come out documented, catalogued, published, and the truth comes out *later*, quietly, after everyone's safe and paid and the university has salvaged what it can —"

"You want me to help you carry the lie out of this cave."

"I want you to help me carry six people out of this cave," he said. "The lie is ballast. It's already aboard. All I'm asking is that you don't sink us with it before we clear the harbor."

And there it was. The old arithmetic. The same one from the night Mother left, when he sat me down at fourteen and explained, gently, reasonably, that she'd gone to her sister's, that she'd be in touch, that some marriages needed air — the same voice, the same reasonable architecture of a lie, built to be load-bearing, built so that the truth underneath it would collapse the whole house and it was *my choice*, always my choice, whether to be the one who knocked it down.

I had spent my whole adult life leaving places before they could become that house again.

"Oscar." June's voice. She had her camera in her hands, and she wasn't pointing it at anything. "The water's rising. I can hear it."

She was right. Beneath the hiss of the kettle, beneath the cave's slow pulse, there was a new sound: water, moving with intent, somewhere in the gallery behind us.

"Devan," Edmund said. "You've been quiet."

I turned. Devan was standing by the crates, and he had the bone comb in his hand, and he was looking at the casting seam the way a man looks at a scar he's just found on his own body. He'd been close enough to hear everything. He'd probably heard everything twice.

"Dr. Lockridge," Devan said, very evenly, in the voice of a man counting to ten and arriving at eleven, "I left a fisheries job to work on this dig. I told my wife this was the find of the century. I told my *daughter*."

"Devan," I said.

He set the comb down. Picked up a lantern. "Get out of my way, Lockridge. Either of you. I'm going to find that secondary passage, and when I do, I'm deciding who I tell the world about first — the fraud, or the flood."

Behind him, in the gallery, the water said something back.
