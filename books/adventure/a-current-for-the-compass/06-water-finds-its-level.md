## 6. Water Finds Its Level

The water came for us the way bad news does — quietly at first, then all at once.

I noticed it as a sound. I was crouched over my camera, checking whether the phosphorescence on the walls was strong enough to shoot without flash, when I heard it: a soft, wet, patient lapping, like something licking its lips behind us. I turned my headlamp back toward the passage we'd come through and saw the black water sliding over the stone where Devan had stood not ten minutes before.

"Um," I said, which has never once in my life been the beginning of good news.

Oscar got there before I could say anything useful. They crouched at the waterline, touched it with two fingers, and held the fingers up to the light like a man tasting a wine he already knows he hates.

"Salt," they said. "Full strength. That's not seepage. That's the tide."

"The tide's not due for hours," Devan said. "Bram said—"

"Bram," Oscar said, "is a hundred yards above us, dry, and drinking tea. The tide doesn't care what Bram said."

I want to be fair to Edmund here, because what happened next was not entirely his fault, though it was also, in the way of most things Edmund Lockridge, not entirely not his fault. He came forward with his lantern held high — he'd never surrendered to a headlamp, always that old brass lantern, like a man from a different century who'd wandered into the wrong expedition — and he looked at the water with the expression of a scholar reading a footnote he'd rather skip.

"There's a second way out," he said.

Everyone looked at him.

"I surveyed this system in the spring," he went on, too smoothly, the way he did when he was building something on ground that hadn't been checked. "There's a passage off the eastern gallery. It climbs. It comes out above the high-water line on the headland side."

"Did you survey it," Devan asked, "or read about it?"

The pause was small. Most people wouldn't have caught it. I'd spent three years photographing Edmund Lockridge's digs, and I had learned that his pauses were where he kept the things he didn't want found. I caught it. I think Oscar caught it too, because Oscar went very still, in the way of a person doing arithmetic they don't like.

"Doesn't matter," Oscar said, before Devan could sharpen the question. "If it exists, it's our way out. If it doesn't, we'll know soon enough. How do we get to it?"

"The eastern gallery. Through the low chamber." Edmund was already moving, already pointing, already the great man again. "We should hurry."

And then the water made the decision for us, because water is honest that way.

It came around the corner of the passage not as a trickle now but as a current, knee-deep and shoving, and behind it came a sound like the cave clearing its throat. The floor we'd crossed on the way in — that wide, flat shelf of limestone — was already gone under a foot of black, moving water, and the current ran toward the deep of the cave, not out of it.

"We can't go back," Priya said. She'd been quiet the whole trip, the geologist, the one who'd measured everything and liked what she measured. Her voice had a crack in it now. "The way we came in — that's gone. Look at it. That's not wadeable. That's not survivable."

"Then we go forward," Edmund said. "Everyone. Together. The eastern passage—"

"Look at the chamber," Oscar said.

We looked. The low chamber — the crawl Edmund wanted us to funnel through — was already taking water, a thin sheet of it sliding over the lip of the entrance like a spilled drink. In twenty minutes, maybe thirty, it would be a siphon. A drowning machine.

Oscar stood there in the rising water with their headlamp beam steady on that sheet of flooding stone, and I watched them do something I had never once seen Edmund Lockridge do, in three years and eleven digs.

They changed their mind out loud.

"Split," Oscar said. "It's the only call. Dad takes anyone who can swim the gallery crossing to the east passage. The rest of us go up — there's a ledge system on the north wall, I saw it on the way in. Higher ground. We hold there until the tide peaks and drops, and we go out the way we came, when it lets us."

"You'd split a team in a flooding cave," Edmund said, and there was something in his voice I couldn't name at first. Then I could. It was the sound of a man hearing his own methods quoted back at him by the one person he'd hoped would never learn them.

"You'd push eight people through a drowning crawl on a passage you *read about*," Oscar said. "I'd rather split a team than sink one."

Nobody argued. That was the strange part. Devan looked at Edmund. Priya looked at the water. Tomas, the youngest, just looked scared, and I put my hand on his shoulder because someone had to and because my own hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"June," Oscar said. "You're with me. You too, Priya. Tomas. Devan—" They hesitated, and I saw the cost of it cross their face like a shadow. "Devan, you're the strongest swimmer. Go with him. Keep the group honest."

"Define honest," Devan said, but he was already moving to Edmund's side, and I understood then that Oscar had just done the hardest thing in the arithmetic: they'd given their father the stronger half and kept the weaker one. Kept me. Kept the people who needed keeping.

Edmund looked at his child across the black water, and for one unguarded second his face did something complicated and old.

"Oscar," he said.

"Don't," Oscar said. "Save your breath. You'll need it for swimming."

* * *

We climbed.

That's the whole of what we did for the next hour, and I could fill pages with the mechanics of it — the ledge system was real, barely, a seam in the north wall where the limestone had failed a million years ago and left a staircase only a goat or a Lockridge could love — but what I remember is not the climbing. What I remember is watching Oscar work.

They went last. Always last. I'd been on a dozen digs and I'd watched Edmund Lockridge walk point on every single approach, first through every doorway, first into every chamber, because first was where the credit lived. Oscar went last, and not because they were afraid. Because last was where the counting lived. They counted us up the ledges. They counted us across the gaps. When Tomas's lamp failed — and God, the sound he made when it did, in that dark, with the water talking below us — Oscar was beside him in four seconds with a spare they'd been carrying the whole way, a spare I hadn't known they had, because Oscar Lockridge carried weight for other people and never mentioned it.

"Talk to me, Tomas," they said, clipping the new lamp to his harness. "What's your mum's name?"

"Wh—what?"

"Her name. Tell me about her. Climb and talk."

And Tomas climbed and talked — about a woman named Rosa who made bread every Sunday and cried at football matches — and the fear went out of his voice by degrees, the way air leaves a room when you open a window.

I shot maybe forty frames in that hour. I didn't need to. I knew I'd remember it all without them.

* * *

We reached the high ledge near midnight, or what I decided was midnight, since time down there was a rumor. The ledge was a shelf of dry stone maybe four meters wide, walled behind us by rock and fronted by nothing but air and, far below, the black water rising with terrible patience. We were a family of castaways on a mantelpiece.

Priya cried a little, quietly, and Oscar sat next to her and let her, and said nothing, which was exactly right. Tomas slept against the wall with Rosa's bread and Rosa's football still murmuring on his lips. I sat next to Oscar at the edge, our legs hanging over the drop, and for a while neither of us spoke.

"You gave him the swimmers," I said finally.

"Devan's the only one who can keep him alive if the passage is a dead end." Oscar's voice was flat. "And if it isn't a dead end, Devan's the only one who'll tell us the truth about what's down there."

"You think it's a dead end."

"I think my father said *I surveyed it* with a pause you could park a truck in." They were quiet a moment. The water below caught my headlamp's stray light and threw it back up at us, wobbling, like the cave was showing us itself. "I think he found something in the spring. Or made something. And I think we're standing on the edge of it."

"Oscar." I turned to look at them. Their face in the lamplight was their father's face, the same strong jaw, the same deep-set eyes — and nothing like it at all. "Whatever's down there. Whatever he did. We get out first. We deal with it after. That's the deal, right?"

They looked back at me, and something in their expression shifted — surprise, maybe, or relief, the look of a person who has spent their whole life bracing for abandonment and keeps being startled when people stay.

"That's the deal," they said.

And then the water below us surged — a long, deep, groaning swell that climbed a full meter up the far wall and fell back — and from somewhere in the eastern dark of the cave, faint as a memory, we heard a sound that stopped my heart.

A voice. Shouting. Devan's voice, cracked and echoing, carrying over the water the way sound does when it has nowhere else to go:

"*It's a dead end! The passage — there's no passage — Oscar — OSCAR —*"
