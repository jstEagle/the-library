## 7. Payment in Memory

The water took my boots somewhere past the third bend, and I decided that was the cave's way of charging admission.

I'd gone in after the others — or rather, after the *idea* of the others. When the gallery flooded and the group split, Edmund had shouted something about a secondary passage, and Devan had shouted something less charitable, and June had shouted my name, and in all that noise I'd made the mistake of turning back to check on the stragglers. By the time I turned forward again, the water had rearranged the room. The passage I'd come through was gone. The passage ahead was new. And the phosphorescent veins in the rock had shifted from their cold blue to something warmer, amber, like lamplight seen through a window you're not invited into.

"Very funny," I told the cave.

The cave, being a cave, did not answer. But the water kept rising, calm and patient as a debt collector, and the amber light kept leading, and I kept following, because the alternative was standing chest-deep in the dark doing arithmetic about lung capacity, and I've never been good at arithmetic.

The gallery narrowed, then opened — that trick again, the cave breathing in and out — and I hauled myself up onto a shelf of rock that was almost, *almost* dry, and found myself in a chamber that had no business existing.

It was round, or roundish, the way things are round in dreams. The ceiling was lost in darkness. The water pooled in the center of the floor like a mirror that had given up reflecting, and the walls — the walls were moving.

Not the rock. The *light*. The amber veins pulsed in patterns, and the patterns had edges, and the edges had meaning, and I stood there dripping and furious at myself for the word that arrived in my head, which was *home*.

"Right," I said. "I know how this works. Bram told us. You show a person what they most need to see."

I'd said it like a skeptic. I'd said it the way you say *sure, sure* to a fortune-teller, humoring the room. But the room wasn't humoring me back. The light on the far wall gathered itself, the way fog gathers, the way a crowd gathers, and became a kitchen.

Our kitchen. The one on the farm outside Harlow Bend, with the wallpaper my mother had fought about and won — yellow, with a pattern of small blue birds that my father called frivolous and she called the only frivolous thing she owned. I was seven in that kitchen. I knew it the way you know your own hands. There was the table with the burn ring from the kettle. There was the window over the sink with the crack shaped like a river delta.

And there was my mother, at the sink, washing dishes, humming.

I did not move. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept: I did not move toward her. Some part of me — the part that had learned to read soil, the part that knew a sinkhole when it opened — understood that this was not a door. It was a display. A museum of me, curated by geology.

She turned off the tap. She dried her hands on the towel with the rooster on it. And she looked — not at me, at the *wall*, at the version of the wall where the memory lived — and said, in her ordinary voice, the voice I had spent nineteen years failing to forget: "Oscar. Come here a minute."

I came. Of course I came. You can keep every other promise to yourself; you will break that one.

The scene shifted the way scenes shift in memory — no walking, no transition, just the kitchen becoming the kitchen-later, the dishes done, the suitcase by the door. I knew this night. I had spent my whole life knowing this night in fragments, the way you know a song from a neighbor's radio, never the whole thing through.

My mother was crouched in front of seven-year-old me, her hands on my shoulders, her coat already on. And she was saying the things I remembered her saying — *I have to go, sweetheart, this isn't about you, you are the best thing I ever made* — but the cave, with its terrible courtesy, was filling in what memory had worn smooth. I could see her face. Not the blur I'd carried, not the photograph gone soft at the edges, but her actual face, wet-eyed and steady, and I could see what I had never been old enough to read: that she was terrified. Not of leaving. Of what leaving would make of me.

Behind her, in the hallway shadow, stood my father.

He was not arguing. That was the thing the cave showed me that I had never known, the thing I had rebuilt wrong in nineteen years of reconstruction. He was not arguing, not pleading, not standing between her and the door. He was standing with his arms crossed and his jaw set, and he was saying — quietly, in the voice he used for field notes, the reasonable voice, the worst voice he had — "If you walk out that door, you walk out of this family. I'll tell him whatever I have to. I'll tell him you chose it. I'll tell him you didn't look back."

And my mother had looked back. That was the detail. That was the thing the cave returned to me like a receipt for nineteen years of overpayment. She had looked back, twice, from the end of the drive, and my father had stood in the doorway between us with his hand on seven-year-old my shoulder, and he had turned my body away from the window with a firmness he probably believed was kindness, and he had said, *don't watch, son, it's easier if you don't watch* — and then he had told me, for nineteen years, that she never did.

The chamber was silent except for water dripping somewhere, keeping its own slow time.

I sat down on the rock shelf. My legs had opinions about standing.

"You lying bastard," I said. I didn't specify to whom. The cave accepted the ambiguity.

Because here's the thing — here's the thing I'd been carrying like a stone in my boot since the Room of Small Truths, since the plaster casts and the planted artifacts and my father's wet-eyed *Oscar, please, until we're out*: I had thought the fraud was the first lie. I had thought Edmund Lockridge had broken bad somewhere in his fifties, that the fabrication was a recent corrosion, a weakness of the late career. But it wasn't. It was the same lie, the founding lie, the load-bearing lie. He had faked my mother's leaving the way he'd faked the Hollow's artifacts — because the truth was inconvenient to the story he needed, and because he had decided, somewhere deep in that brilliant arrogant mind, that he knew what the record should say, and the record would say it.

He curated people the way he curated digs. He planted what he wanted found.

And I had spent nineteen years believing I was the reason she left. Seven years old, and my father's reasonable voice explaining that she'd found farm life small, that she'd wanted things we couldn't give her, that some people just aren't — and the *isn't* had never finished itself, and I had spent two decades completing it in my own head with my own name.

The amber light shifted. The kitchen dissolved, and I braced for more — the funeral, the boarding schools, the parade of every place I'd stopped and lost someone. But the cave, to its credit, had some sense of mercy in its curation. Instead it gave me the drive, the last one. My mother's old car, the one with the bad starter, and her hands on the wheel at the end of the drive, and her looking in the rearview mirror — not at the house. At the window. At the small face she knew was there, because mothers know.

She was mouthing something. I had never known what. Nineteen years of not knowing what.

The cave lit it letter by letter, the way dawn lights a field.

*Come find me. When you're ready. I'll be easy to find.*

I made a sound I'm not proud of. The cave, generously, filed it under water noise.

*Come find me.* Not *goodbye*. Not *be good for your father*. Not any of the things the silence had let me imagine. An invitation. A door left unlocked for nineteen years, and me standing outside it in the rain because I'd been told the house was empty.

I understood the legend then. Bram had it backwards, bless him, or maybe he'd had it right all along and I'd heard it wrong, the way you hear things wrong when you're braced for a blow. The cave doesn't take payment in memory. It doesn't *take* anything. It returns. It's not a thief; it's a customs office, and everything you lost in transit comes back to you stamped and declared, and the payment — the actual payment — is that you have to stand there and accept the delivery. You have to hold what you most needed to see and carry it out of the dark, and carrying it changes the weight of everything else.

My mother hadn't abandoned me. My father had edited her.

And I had spent my whole life running the pattern he'd installed — leaving before I was left, unpacking with one hand on the door, because every place I stopped became a place I lost someone. But the losses weren't the pattern of the world. They were the pattern of *him*. The places didn't take people from me. He'd taught me to misread the soil, to see sinkholes where there were only fields, and I had built a whole restless life on his faulty survey.

The water in the center of the chamber had risen while I sat. It lapped at the edge of my shelf, patient, insistent. The amber veins were dimming, the exhibition closing, the museum sweeping the floors.

But I wasn't finished. I put my hand flat on the wet rock and said, out loud, because some things you have to say out loud or they don't take root:

"She looked back."

The light pulsed once. Acknowledgment. Receipt.

"And I'm going to find her."

Another pulse. And then — and I swear this on my father's fraudulent artifacts — the water in the center of the chamber went still, perfectly still, and showed me not a memory but a direction. A reflection of the passage behind me, the flooded gallery, and at the far end of it, faint in the phosphorescent murk, the amber glow of a secondary passage. The one Edmund had sworn existed. The one the others had gone toward.

The cave had shown me what I needed. Now it was showing me where I was needed.

I got up. My legs had stopped having opinions and started having convictions. Nineteen years of misread soil, and I finally knew what I was standing on: not a son's loyalty, not a fraud's protection, but the truth, which turns out to be the only ground that doesn't shift under you when the tide comes in.

I waded back into the gallery, toward the amber, toward the others, toward the reckoning I now knew was coming whether my father was ready for it or not.

Behind me, the chamber went dark. Paid in full.

* * *

The secondary passage was exactly where the water had said, and it was exactly as bad as Edmund's track record suggested — narrow, rising, half-drowned. But there were voices in it. Angry ones. Devan's, mostly.

I put my shoulder to the current and started climbing, carrying the heaviest thing I'd ever been given, which weighed nothing at all.

My father had lied about my mother. And somewhere up that passage, he was lying to five more people about the Hollow — and I was done letting his edits stand.

"Devan!" I shouted into the dark. "Wait. Before you do anything — you need to hear what I heard."
