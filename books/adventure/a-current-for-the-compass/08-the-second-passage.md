## 8. The Second Passage

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my head because paper gets wet down here — that I did not start this fight. I finished it, yes. I finished it the way you finish a bad meal: quickly, and with a certain grim satisfaction, and then you feel worse than before you ate.

The fraud was Edmund Lockridge's. The shouting was mine.

We were in a chamber the size of a chapel, if chapels were built by the sea and then fired. The water had come up past our ankles in the low channel behind us, and Edmund had herded his half of the team here with talk of a secondary passage — a crack in the north wall, he said, that climbed toward daylight. He said it the way he said everything: with the confidence of a man who had never once been interrupted by reality.

I interrupted him.

"You've been here before," I said. "Months ago. Alone, or near enough. You know this passage because you *made* it — the story of it, I mean. The question is whether you made the passage too."

The headlamps swung toward him. Eight of us in that chamber, and every beam of light found his face, which was doing something complicated. Grief, maybe. Or the expression of a man doing long division with his soul.

"Devan," he said, gently, the way you'd speak to a dog you intended to have put down later, "now isn't the time."

"Now is exactly the time. Now is the only time we have left that the sea hasn't already claimed. You planted those artifacts. I carried two of them up from your little camp back there — plaster, Edmund. I *tapped* them. They rang like cheap crockery. The discovery that funded this whole circus is a lie, and you knew the tide schedule was wrong, and you brought us in anyway because you needed witnesses to your miracle."

"Devan—" June started.

"No." I turned on her, and I'm not proud of what my voice did. "No, June. He's had months to tell the truth. He had years, if you count the last one. We have maybe hours, and I am not going to die in a hole in the ground so that a great man can keep a secret."

Edmund looked at me for a long moment. Then, and this is the part I keep returning to, he looked past me — toward the flooded channel, toward the direction Oscar had gone when the water split us — and something in his face went from complicated to simple.

"You're right," he said.

I had prepared for denial. I had prepared for the speech, the charm offensive, the great man's talent for making his sins sound like sacrifices. I had not prepared for three flat words, and they knocked the wind out of me worse than any argument would have.

"You're right," he said again, "and you deserve better than to learn it in a drowning cave. All of you do. Sit down if you can. Listen, because I'll only say this once, and then we move."

And he told them. All of it. The Hollow, months ago, on a scouting trip he'd never logged. The chamber of small truths — the one Oscar found, the one the cave apparently keeps like a ledger. How he'd stood in it and seen what he most needed to see, which was, he said, the end of his own career, and how he had decided that if the sea was going to take his reputation anyway, he'd rather it took the real one. So he'd made casts. Planted them. Built a discovery out of plaster and nerve and the certainty that no one who loved him would check.

"The cave doesn't take memory," he said, and his voice cracked on the word *memory* in a way I will be thinking about for the rest of my life, however long the sea allows. "It returns it. And I looked at everything it gave back, and I chose the lie anyway. That's the whole of it. That's the man you followed down here."

Silence. The kind with weight. Somewhere above us, water moved through stone with a sound like slow applause.

Priya, our geologist, sat down on a ledge and put her head in her hands. Tomas swore in three languages, none of them fluently. June just looked at Edmund with an expression I couldn't read, and I thought: she's seeing her brother again, or the shape the cave made of him, and she's wondering what else in here is a lie.

And I — I stood there with my fury in my hands like a tool I no longer had a job for.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about being right. It's cold. You build the whole fire of it, you feed it everything, and then the truth arrives and it doesn't warm anybody. It just stands there, smoking.

"Fine," I said, when I could speak. "Confession received. Now the part that matters." I pointed at Edmund. "You know this passage. You've walked it. If you're lying about that — if there's no way through — say it now, because I will leave you at this wall and take the others up that channel and take my chances with the water. I mean it. I will."

"Devan." June's hand on my arm.

"I mean it," I said again, quieter, and the quiet was worse and I knew it.

Edmund met my eyes. Whatever else the man was — fraud, father, fool — he did not flinch.

"The passage is real," he said. "I've never been through it. It was flooded when I came, and I turned back. But it rises, and it breathes — there's air moving through the crack, cold air, from above. Air means sky. It's our best chance and I think it's our only one." He paused. "And if it isn't, you can leave me at the bottom and tell them Devan made the honest call."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half a sob, and I hated him for it and hated myself more.

* * *

That was when Oscar arrived.

I heard them before I saw them — a splash in the channel, then a voice cutting through the dark with the calm of someone who had already done their panicking in private, ahead of schedule.

"Edmund. Devan. Everyone. Sound off."

They came around the wall with the water to their thighs and their lamp beam steady, and behind them, wading, the others — the half of the team Edmund had left on the far side, now reunited, soaked, alive. Oscar had found them a route through. Of course they had. Oscar Lockridge reads water the way their father reads ruins, and the difference — the whole difference, the one that matters — is that water doesn't lie to Oscar, because Oscar never asked it to flatter them.

"Oscar." Edmund stepped forward, and for one terrible second I thought he was going to embrace them, here, shin-deep in the sea's front hallway. "I've just told them. About the camp. About everything."

"I know." Oscar's voice gave away nothing. "Devan heard you. The whole cave probably heard you. Sound carries in limestone." They swept the beam across all of us. "Here's what carries next. In about forty minutes this chamber is under four feet of moving water, and in an hour it's under ten. So we are going to have the rest of this conversation *while walking*."

"Oscar—" Edmund began.

"No." And there it was — not anger, exactly. Something older and more tired than anger, something that had been carried a long way. "You confessed. Good. That's the first true thing you've given any of us in months, and I'm not going to waste it by letting you die before it can matter. But I'm not going to pretend it un-does anything, either, and I'm not going to let this team tear itself apart in a flooding cave over which of you gets to be angriest." The beam found me. "Devan. You threatened to leave him."

"I did."

"Are you going to?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Looked at Edmund — at this old man with plaster on his hands and seawater in his boots, who had built a lie so large it needed a crew to carry it, and who had, when cornered, told the truth without once checking who was watching.

"I want to," I said. "God help me, I want to. But I've seen what leaving people behind does. It doesn't make you lighter. It just makes you the one who left."

Oscar nodded, slow, like I'd passed some test I hadn't known I was sitting.

"Then we move together," they said. "Everyone. Nobody's speed is anyone else's problem — we go at the pace of the slowest, and the slowest knows it, and the fastest swallows it. Edmund, you're second. You know the passage, so you call the turns, but Oscar—" and here their voice did something I didn't expect, something almost gentle, "—you don't walk behind him. You walk beside him. If he's wrong about a turn, we all hear it at the same time."

"Understood," Edmund said. And then, barely audible: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Climb."

* * *

The secondary passage was exactly what Edmund promised and nothing like what I'd hoped: a crack in the north wall, barely shoulder-width, breathing cold air into our faces like something alive. We went in single file, lamps bobbing, the sound of the sea fading behind us until it was just a rumor in the stone.

Edmund called the turns. Left at the split rock. Right where the wall went smooth. He was right every time, and I could hear how much it cost him to be right in front of Oscar, to offer up the one competence he had left like a man paying a debt in coins he'd stolen.

An hour in — or what I judged was an hour; time down here is a rumor too — the crack opened into a shaft.

Not a chamber. A *shaft*. Vertical, maybe thirty feet, its walls streaked white with old waterlines, and at the top, impossibly, absurdly, a coin of grey light. Daylight. Real daylight, thin as broth and beautiful as anything I've ever seen.

"It rises," June breathed. "It actually rises."

"It rises," Oscar said, "and the water's rising with us. Look at the walls." And we looked, and the fresh waterline was only a few feet below our feet, and climbing.

Priya started to laugh — the high, unhinged laugh of a woman who has been in a cave too long — and Tomas put a hand on her shoulder, and I looked up at that coin of grey light and thought: *we can make it. We might actually—*

Then the light flickered.

Not dimmed. *Flickered* — the way light does when something passes in front of it.

Oscar's lamp snapped up to the top of the shaft. Mine followed.

Against that thin coin of grey, silhouetted, unmistakable, was a rope. New rope, marine line, paying out over the lip of the shaft from above — descending toward us, slow and deliberate, thrown by hands we could not see.

Nobody had known we were down here. Nobody *could* have known.

The rope kept coming down.
