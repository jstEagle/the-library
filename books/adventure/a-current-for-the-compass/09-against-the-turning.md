## 9. Against the Turning

The water in the gallery had stopped rising, which should have been good news and wasn't. Standing water meant equilibrium. Equilibrium meant the sea outside had reached its peak and was only waiting for permission to come down on us like a verdict.

I crouched at the edge of the flooded passage with my headlamp beam flat against the surface. Somewhere under that black glass was a siphon — a tunnel that dipped below the waterline and came up again on the far side, if my father's old field notes were worth the paper I'd spent twenty years pretending not to read.

"Talk to me, Oscar." June's voice came from behind, thin but steady. She'd wedged herself into a dry notch of rock with Devan's jacket around her shoulders. She'd stopped shaking ten minutes ago, which I chose to interpret as composure rather than hypothermia.

"It's about forty feet," I said. "Maybe fifty. It goes under, comes up in the next chamber."

"You've done this before?"

"I've read about it extensively."

Devan snorted from his perch near the shaft mouth. "Extensively. Great. We're going to die extensively."

"We're not going to die." I said it with more certainty than I felt, because certainty is contagious and panic is more so. "The cave gave us the room of small truths. It gave us the second passage. It's not trying to kill us. It's trying to be *paid*."

"And what does it want this time?" June asked quietly.

I didn't answer, because I didn't know yet, and because the answer was already forming somewhere below my ribs where I kept the things I didn't say out loud.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being taught by Edmund Lockridge: he never taught you anything gently. When I was eleven he put a surveying instrument in my hands and said, "Water finds its level. Your job is to know where level is before the water does." Then he walked away and let me figure it out, and when I got it wrong he made me do it again until I got it right, and when I got it right he said "adequate" and moved on to something else.

I hated him for it. I still hate him for it. But standing there in a flooding cave with four lives balanced on arithmetic, I did the math the way he taught me, and the math held.

Siphons work on pressure differential. The air pocket on the far side pushes back against the water in the tunnel. If the chamber beyond is higher than the water here, the siphon self-clears — you swim through a short stretch of submerged rock, trusting the geometry, and pop up gasping on the other side. If the chamber beyond is lower, the siphon is a throat, and it swallows.

I measured the waterline against the ceiling of the far passage with my lamp, then measured the depth of the flooded section by dropping a line weighted with a carabiner. Forty-one feet of tunnel, deepest point maybe six feet below the surface. Six feet. You can hold your breath for six feet of swimming if you're calm. If you're calm.

"Calm is a skill," my father told me once, in one of his rare unguarded moments, watching me fail to tie a bowline in a gale on the Orkney coast. "It isn't the absence of fear. It's fear that's been given a job."

I gave my fear a job. It counted seconds.

* * *

We went one at a time. Devan first, because he was the strongest swimmer and I needed someone on the far side who could pull the others up. He stripped to his shirt, took three breaths, and went under without ceremony. The water swallowed him whole. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Twenty—

He surfaced in the next chamber with a whoop that echoed like a church bell. "It's a pool! Oscar, it's a whole dry room!"

"One at a time," I called back. "June next."

She hesitated at the edge. Her hands were trembling again.

"My brother couldn't swim," she said. "Did I tell you that? He drowned in a reservoir when we were kids. I haven't been under water since."

"You don't have to do this alone," I said. "But you do have to do it."

She looked at me, and something passed between us that had nothing to do with words — the look of two people who had both lost someone to water and were deciding, together, not to let it win twice.

She went under clean. Came up coughing on the far side, laughing, actually laughing, wet and wild-eyed. "It works! Oscar, it—"

That's when Edmund moved.

He'd been hanging back all morning, silent since the confession in the second passage, since Devan stood over him with fury coming off him like heat off a road. Now he stepped past me toward the water's edge, and I saw his eyes go not to the siphon but to June — to the way she was treading water, to the way her head dipped once, twice, lower than it should have.

Cramp. Cold-water cramp, in a woman who hadn't swum in twenty years.

Edmund went in without taking off his boots.

* * *

I heard it before I saw it — the wrongness of the sound, a slap and a choke where there should have been steady breathing. June went under. Edmund reached her in three strokes, got an arm across her chest, and turned her face-up the way they teach you, the way he must have learned decades ago in some other life, some other cave, some other version of himself.

And then the current took them.

Because of course it did. The tide had peaked; now it was turning, and a turning tide doesn't drain evenly. It pulls. The water in the gallery began to move — slowly at first, then with intent — dragging back down the siphon toward the sea, and Edmund and June were in it, tangled together, his boots filling with water, her body rigid with cramp, both of them going under and coming up and going under again.

"OSCAR!" Devan screamed from the far side.

I was already moving.

I want to tell you I thought it through. I want to tell you I measured the angle and calculated the drag and gave my fear a job. I did none of those things. I dove into black water after my father, the man whose approval I'd chased across half my life like a dog chasing a car it could never drive, and I swam.

The water was cold enough to stop thought. My lamp swung wild beams through murk. I found June first — her hair, a hand, the fabric of Devan's jacket ballooning around her — and hauled. She was dead weight, unconscious or close to it, and Edmund was wrapped around her and sinking, his face a white smear in the dark, his mouth open in a scream that made no sound underwater.

I got my arm under June's chin. I kicked for the surface. The current pulled the other way, patient and enormous, and for one long moment I understood exactly how a person drowns: not dramatically, not screaming, just gradually running out of the strength to argue with physics.

*Level*, said a voice in my head. Eleven years old, a gale on the Orkney coast, a man walking away. *Know where level is before the water does.*

The current was pulling us down-tunnel. Fine. Let it. I stopped fighting it and steered with it, angling our bodies downstream, letting the turning tide carry us the length of the siphon instead of fighting to cross it. The water would deposit us where the geometry said water goes. All I had to do was keep our faces up and our bodies straight and trust the math.

Forty-one feet. I counted every one.

Then the tunnel rose, and the water rose with it, and we came up all three of us together in a rush of spray and gasping — into the entrance chamber, the original one, the archway visible ahead of us as a grey rectangle of dying daylight.

The tide was going out. And it was taking the cave's patience with it.

* * *

Devan dragged June onto the shelf of rock below the archway while I hauled Edmund up by the collar like a landed fish. He collapsed on his side, coughing seawater, his boots streaming, and for a moment none of us could do anything but breathe.

June woke coughing, then crying, then swearing at Edmund with a fluency that would have impressed Bram Corrigan. He took it without flinching. He deserved worse.

"The arch," Devan said, pointing. "Oscar — look at the arch."

I looked. The stone mouth of Saint Verren's Hollow was closing. Not magically — mechanically, tidally, the way it had opened: the sea pouring back into the bay, the waterline climbing the ancient stones inch by inch. In twenty minutes, maybe thirty, the Hollow would be sealed for another century, and everything inside it — the phosphorescent veins, the room of small truths, the plaster casts and planted artifacts that had bought my father's redemption and nearly cost five lives — would belong to the ocean again.

"We need to go," I said. "Now. While the floor's still above water."

Nobody argued. Even Edmund got up without being told.

We waded toward the light. And at the threshold, Edmund stopped. Turned. Looked back into the dark with an expression I couldn't read — grief, maybe, or relief, or the particular hollow look of a man watching his greatest lie get swallowed by the truth.

"Come on," I said. Not gently. But I said it.

He came.

* * *

We came out of the Hollow into evening light so ordinary it hurt — gulls, grey water, the smell of fish and tar, Bram Corrigan standing on the shingle with a lantern like he'd been waiting there all day, which knowing Bram, he had.

He looked at the five of us — soaked, bleeding, alive — and then at the sea closing over the archway behind us, and he nodded once, as if a debt had been settled.

"Tide's turning," he said. "Best time for it. Water takes back what it lent."

June sat down on the stones and laughed until she cried. Devan put his jacket around her again. And Edmund Lockridge stood at the edge of the water, dripping, diminished, watching the place where his fraud had gone under, and I realized I was waiting for him to say something — an apology, an explanation, anything.

Instead he looked at me. Just looked. And in his eyes was something I'd spent thirty years waiting to see and discovering, now that it was finally there, that I no longer needed.

"Oscar," he started.

"Don't," I said. "Not yet. Not here."

He closed his mouth. Nodded. And we walked up the shingle together, apart, toward the lights of Greyharbour — where tomorrow I would sit down with Bram's stationery and write the report that would end him.

Tonight, though, tonight we were just survivors. And the sea, having taken its payment, gave us back to each other.
