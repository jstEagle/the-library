## 10. A Current for the Compass

The report was eleven pages long. I wrote it three times.

The first draft was a scalpel — every fraud laid out in clinical order, the plaster casts, the planted artifacts, the dates that didn't hold, the photographs Edmund had staged in a quarry two hundred miles from the Hollow. I wrote it at June's kitchen table while she slept, and by dawn I'd read it back and heard my father's voice in every line. Precise. Cold. A performance of rigor that was really just a different kind of lying, because it pretended I felt nothing.

The second draft was a plea. I'll be honest about that, at least with myself, if nowhere else. I wrote four pages explaining what he'd been afraid of, what the funding deadlines had done to him, how a man can start believing his own legend until the legend is all that's left of him. It read like a son begging the world to go easy. It read like the same old story: Oscar, cleaning up after Edmund Lockridge, making excuses for a man who had never once made excuses for anyone, least of all me.

The third draft I wrote standing up, in the harbor office, with Bram Corrigan's coffee going cold beside me and the tide clock ticking on the wall. I told the truth. All of it. What he did, and what it cost, and what it nearly cost — four names on a list of people who almost didn't come home. I signed it, dated it, and mailed it before I could pick it apart.

Then I walked down to the water and threw up behind Bram's shed, which I mention only for completeness, because the wry narrator in my head insists that heroism should be documented honestly too.

* * *

The letter from the university came nine days later. Then the calls from journalists, which I didn't take. Then the calls from Edmund's colleagues, which I also didn't take, except for one elderly professor who said, quietly, "He taught you well, whoever taught you to do that," and hung up before I could decide whether to be angry.

Devan found me on the sea wall that evening. He sat down without asking, which by then was his way. He'd been doing that for weeks — appearing beside me with the unspoken assumption that my company was a thing he was allowed to have. It still startled me every time.

"They revoked the Verren discovery," he said. "Formally. It's in the journals."

"I saw."

"He's not fighting it."

I looked at him. Devan had spent a decade in Edmund's orbit; he knew the man's silences better than his speeches. "That's worse, isn't it."

"It's worse." He picked at the flaking paint on the wall between us. "A man who fights it still thinks he's right. A man who doesn't fight it knows." He was quiet a moment. "You know he didn't contest a single line of your report. Not one."

"I know."

"Is that mercy or is that punishment?"

"I think," I said, "it's the first honest thing he's done in twenty years."

Devan nodded slowly, like I'd handed him something heavy and he was testing its weight. Then he said, "June's making that fish stew again. The one that tastes like punishment. You're coming."

It wasn't a question. I noticed, distantly, that I didn't want it to be.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about staying: it's terrifying.

I had built my whole life on the long way around stopping. Unpack light, learn the exits, love in pencil. Every town I'd ever lived in, I'd left before it could take something from me, and I'd told myself that was freedom. It took a flooded cave and a father's ruin to show me the arithmetic was backwards. I hadn't been keeping my whole heart. I'd been keeping it in a bag by the door.

Greyharbour didn't ask me to unpack. It just kept handing me things until I had no choice.

Bram taught me the tides. Not from a book — he had opinions about books, most of them unprintable — but by making me stand in the same spot on the harbor wall at the same hour, day after day, until I could read the water the way I'd once read soil. "The sea doesn't lie and it doesn't hurry," he said, watching me misjudge a slack. "Everything you got wrong in that cave, you got wrong because you were listening to a clock instead of the water. The water was telling you the whole time."

He was right, and he enjoyed being right, and he made me say so out loud, which I think was the actual lesson.

June taught me other things. How to develop film in a converted closet. How to sit with grief without trying to fix it — hers for her brother, mine for a man who was still alive, which is its own strange country. Some nights we didn't talk at all. She'd be at one end of the sofa with her feet on the table and I'd be at the other end with a tide chart, and it was the most comfortable silence I'd ever kept company with.

And Devan — Devan, who had every reason to hate me, since my report ended the career he'd given his life to — Devan started calling me on Tuesdays for no reason. "Checking you're still here," he said, the first time. "Habit," he said, every time after, and we both let the word *habit* do work it wasn't built for.

* * *

The letter came in the fourth week.

I knew the handwriting before I knew what it was. That looping, self-important script, the capital E's like small cathedrals. I carried it around for two days unopened — in my jacket, on the table, under a mug, back in my jacket. June watched me do it and said nothing, which was its own kind of kindness.

When I finally opened it, it was one page. No cathedrals in the margins, no performance. Just his hand, smaller than I remembered, like the writing itself had been defunded.

*Oscar.*

*Your mother didn't leave because of you. I told you she did because it was easier for me to be the abandoned one than the abandoned-er. That's the shape of me. I know that now, in the place where knowing doesn't help.*

*I am not asking you to come back. I am not asking for anything, which is new for me, and you may quote me.*

*You were the best thing I ever found, and I never once treated you like a discovery. That's the fraud I can't file a report on. I'm sorry. It's not enough. I know that too.*

*— E.*

I read it eleven times. I read it on the sea wall, in the rain, until the ink started to feather and I had to stop. Then I went home and put it in the tin box where I keep the things I can't throw away and can't yet use — my mother's hair ribbon, the cave photograph, a piece of phosphorescent mineral Devan chiseled out for me before the sea took the entrance back.

I did not answer it.

I want to be clear that this wasn't cruelty, and it wasn't cowardice, or not only. It was that some letters need to sit in the water a while before you can read what they actually say. He'd had twenty years of my silence to interpret however he liked. He could have a few more months of the honest version.

* * *

Winter came early to Greyharbour, the way it does, all at once and then gradually for six months. The morning the first real storm rolled in, I stood on the harbor wall with Bram and watched the sea stand up on its hind legs over the place where the Hollow had been.

"You know it's still down there," Bram said. "The cave. Tides like this, it opens maybe once a century. Might not be your lifetime. Might not be mine."

"Good," I said, and meant it.

He glanced sideways at me. "Most people would want to go back. Prove something."

"I proved what I needed to." I watched the water hammer the archway's ghost. "It doesn't show you what you want. It shows you what you need, and then it keeps what you can't bear to lose. That's what Bram Corrigan said, anyway. Wise old fisherman. Terrible coffee."

"Heard that."

"You were meant to."

He laughed, and the storm laughed with him, and for a while we just stood there, two people watching the sea take back its own.

That evening there was stew at June's — the good batch, at last, the recipe finally surrendered its secrets — and Devan brought a bottle of something expensive that he claimed was a gift from a grateful colleague and we all agreed not to examine too closely. We argued about whether the Hollow was magic. June said yes. Devan said geology. I said it didn't matter, because either way it had done what it did.

"And what did it do to you, exactly?" June asked, in the way she asked things — genuinely, with her whole attention, like the answer was going in a book.

I thought about it. The fire was going. My boots were drying by the door — *my* boots, drying by *a* door, in a place where I had a key and a chair and a Tuesday phone call and a standing invitation to bad stew.

"It gave me back," I said.

Nobody laughed. Devan raised his glass an inch, and June put her feet back on the table, and outside the wind came off the water and found the town ready for it, the way it had been ready for a hundred years before any of us, and would be after.

Later — much later, when they'd gone and the fire was down to its red arithmetic — I took out the letter one more time. I didn't read it. I just held the tin, and listened to the sea working the harbor mouth, steady as a pulse.

In the morning I'd go down to the wall and Bram would be there, and the tide would be doing what tides do, which is leave, and come back, and leave, and come back. I'd learn something from it. I always did now.

I got out a pen. Not to answer him. Not yet. Just to write the date at the top of a blank page, the way my father taught me to begin a field journal, back when the only thing he ever gave me freely was a method.

*Day one,* I wrote. *Still here.*

And for the first time in my life, that wasn't a report. It was a plan.
