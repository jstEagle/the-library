## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The supercell was worth eleven thousand dollars, give or take a hailstone, and Freya Dunmore intended to collect every cent of it.

She could tell from forty miles out—the way it stood up off the prairie like a man rising from a chair to settle an argument. The tower was going vertical with that greedy, muscular convection she'd learned to read the way other people read bus schedules. Anvil spreading flat and wide, overshooting top punching through the tropopause, a beaver-tail inflow curling in from the southeast like the storm was beckoning her closer. Which, in fairness, it was. Storms always beckoned her closer. It was the one relationship in her life that had never once asked her to stay.

"Data's clean," Tomas said from the passenger seat, not looking up from his tablet. He had the particular calm of a man who had made peace with the fact that his job description was *sitting in a truck pointed at the worst weather on the continent*. "Updraft velocities are stupid. Like, insultingly stupid."

"Insulting to whom?"

"To my retirement plan, which is apparently 'chase this.'"

Freya smiled and did the math instead, because Freya always did the math. The Vane Syndicate's client—a grain futures consortium that wanted hyper-local precipitation forecasts six hours before the weather services could manage them—paid by the verified dataset. A supercell of this quality, properly instrumented, was worth eleven thousand. Her outstanding balance with the Syndicate, the number that lived in her head the way other people kept a hymn or a phone number, was two hundred and forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars and forty cents.

The forty cents mattered. She'd added it in a diner outside Marlow two winters ago, a piece of pie charged to the account because the account was already ruined and pie is pie. She kept the forty cents in the figure as a form of honesty. Debts rounded down were debts lied about.

Twenty-two more storms like this one. Twenty-two perfect supercells, no mechanical failures, no dry spells, no months where the atmosphere sulked and produced nothing but blue sky and invoices. At her current rate of one good chase every nine or ten days through storm season, that was—

"Seven months," she said aloud.

"Of what?"

"Of everything going exactly right."

Tomas lowered his tablet and looked at her the way he looked at weather models that showed something impossible. "Freya. Nothing has ever gone exactly right. Not once. I've known you six years. The closest we came was the Pampa cell, and a cow fell on the mast."

"The cow was a statistical anomaly."

"The cow was a *metaphor*," Tomas said, "and I think the universe was being clear with us."

She let him have that, mostly because the road was washboarded and arguing cost concentration, and concentration was the only asset she owned outright. The rig was a salvage job three times over—a decommissioned research truck she'd won in a bankruptcy auction, armored with scrap plate, crowned with a sensor mast that looked, Tomas said, like a church antenna designed by a paranoid engineer. Which was accurate. Everything Freya built was designed by a paranoid engineer, because paranoia, unlike optimism, had never once cost her money.

The Vane Syndicate had cost her a great deal of money, but that had been her own doing, and she tried not to think about the specifics. The specifics went like this: a bad season, a worse loan, a syndicate that lent money to storm chasers at rates that assumed the chaser would either pay or die, both outcomes being acceptable to the accounting department. Three years ago. One signature. She had signed as F. Dunmore because even then, even drowning, some instinct in her had kept the rest of her name back like a card held close to the vest.

Her full name was a thing she spent like cash: carefully, rarely, and never all at once.

* * *

They took the storm from the southeast, the way you're supposed to, staying on the inflow side where the light was gold and the wind came steadily into your face like a handshake. This was the part of the work Freya loved without reservation or irony—the approach, when the storm was still a cathedral rather than a catastrophe, when the air smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet and the whole sky was organized around a single rotating column like a congregation facing one altar.

"Deploy sequence," she said, and Tomas repeated it back, and for four minutes they moved together with the wordless efficiency of people who had done this two hundred times. Chocks. Mast. Grounding stake hammered into soil that had been waiting all year for exactly this kind of weather. The instrument array came alive panel by panel: wind profiler, electric field mill, disdrometer, the hail spectrometer she'd rebuilt herself from parts that had formerly belonged to a hospital and, before that, possibly a submarine.

"Field mill's reading thirty-two hundred," Tomas reported. "And climbing."

"Good. Be angry at us, big guy," Freya told the storm, patting the truck's fender, and Tomas gave her the look he reserved for her conversations with inanimate objects, which was a look of profound and practiced resignation.

The data started flowing, and it was beautiful. That was the thing nobody understood about her job—the beauty of it. Not the lightning, which the clients liked, and not the tornadoes, which the clients loved in the way financiers love anything that moves markets. The data. The clean, layered, honest data of a storm telling you exactly what it was doing and why. Every other system in Freya's life was opaque: the Syndicate's interest calculations, the client's actual intentions, the reason her own chest went tight every time she thought about staying anywhere past the season's end. But a storm held nothing back. A storm was legible. She had built her entire adult life inside that legibility, the way a hermit crab builds its life inside a shell it did not make and cannot fully control.

"Six-hour precipitation forecast," she said, working fast, fingers cold despite the warm air. "Localized. They wanted it by eighteen hundred, we're two hours early. That's a bonus clause."

"That's the early-delivery clause," Tomas confirmed. "Which is—let me check my notes—eleven percent."

"Eleven percent of eleven thousand." Freya did it in her head because she did everything in her head, receipts and distances and the exact interest accrual on her soul. "Twelve hundred and ten dollars off the principal. Which brings us to two hundred and forty thousand, four hundred and two dollars and forty cents."

"You know the number to the cent," Tomas said. It wasn't a question. It was never a question. He said it the way you might say *you know the storm's rotating*—an observation about the natural world.

"Forty cents," Freya agreed. "The pie."

"The pie," Tomas said gravely, and for a moment they were both quiet, watching the storm breathe.

Here was the thing about Tomas that Freya had never said out loud: he was the only person who had ever stayed. Not stayed *with* her—nobody stayed with her, that was the design, that was the architecture—but stayed *near*, in the adjacent seat, in the adjacent motel room, in the adjacent life, for six years, through seasons in five states, without once asking the question everyone else eventually asked, which was some variation of *what are you running from?*

He'd asked once, in year two, and she'd said "the hail," and he'd laughed, and he had never asked again. That was the whole contract between them, unsigned, and it was worth more to her than the truck, the rig, and possibly the data.

* * *

The storm turned at 4:47 in the afternoon, the way storms do, without apology or notice, and the afternoon stopped being a job and started being a negotiation.

"Right turn's gone," Tomas said, watching the wall cloud eat the road ahead. "It's eating the county road. The whole road, Freya, there's just—there's no road now."

"There's a road. There's a road *under new management*." She was already calculating: the cell was turning right, classic, the inflow dragging it southeast, and their escape routes were folding up one by one like chairs after a wedding. South was cut. East was marginal. West meant driving through the hail core, and the hail core was throwing back radar returns that made her think unkind words about her insurance situation.

"We bail east," she decided. "Through the rain. We'll lose the mast data for ten minutes."

"We'll lose the *mast*," Tomas said, "if we don't drop it first."

"Drop it."

They dropped it, the hydraulics whining the mast down flat against the rack, and Freya took them east into the rain at a speed she would later describe to no one, through curtains of water so dense the world became a gray room with a windshield in it. And this was fine. This was the job. She had done this in Texas and Nebraska and once, memorably, in a hailstorm in South Dakota that had left the truck looking like it had been chewed by something with opinions.

The lightning was the variable.

You can respect lightning, plan around lightning, ground yourself against lightning, and lightning will still, on some afternoons, simply take an interest in you. At 5:12 p.m., as Freya guided the rig down a farm road with ditches on both sides and water running in them like small determined rivers, the electric field meter—which she had been watching, she would insist later, she had *absolutely* been watching—went from angry to apoplectic, and the air above the truck went the particular color of a held breath.

"Tomas."

"I see it."

"Tell me you see it."

"I see it, I see it, I—"

The strike came down two hundred meters to their left. Not on them. *Near* them, in the way that a bullet near you is still very much your problem, because a cloud-to-ground strike of that magnitude doesn't need to hit you to ruin your week—it only needs to hit the earth you're standing on, the air you're breathing, and the delicate, expensive, irreplaceable electronics you have welded to the roof of your truck.

The world went white and then silent, which is the order lightning does things in, and then the sound arrived like a door slammed by God, and the truck shuddered, and every light on the instrument panel died at once.

Freya pulled over. Her hands were doing their job without her, downshifting, braking, engaging the brake, and her mind was already somewhere else, already running the inventory, the way it always did, the way it had learned to during the bad years: *what's lost, what's left, what's owed.*

"Talk to me," she said.

Tomas was cycling the power, his face lit green by the emergency display. "Field mill's dead. Disdrometer's dead. Hail spectrometer is—" He paused, and Freya knew from the pause. "The spectrometer is making a sound."

"What kind of sound?"

"A sad sound."

"Everything on this truck makes a sad sound. That's the brand." She got out into the rain, because the rain had gone gentle again, the way it does after it's made its point, and walked the length of the rig with her hood up doing the arithmetic of ruin. The strike had come through the ground current and cooked the array's lower bus—the shared nervous system of half her instruments. Field mill, gone. Disdrometer, gone. Spectrometer, sad. The wind profiler and the radar were on a separate circuit, because Freya was paranoid, and paranoia, unlike optimism, had never once cost her money.

She stood in the rain and did the math.

Replacement bus: four thousand. New field mill: three thousand two hundred. Disdrometer: two thousand. Spectrometer: either a rebuild, which was two weeks and her whole patience, or a replacement, which was nine thousand and did not exist in her budget the way the moon does not exist in a wallet. Total loss: somewhere between nine and eighteen thousand, depending on how much of the work she did herself, and she would do all of it herself, because labor she had and money she did not.

Against the eleven thousand the storm had earned them.

She stood there in the prairie rain, next to a storm that was already walking away eastward, magnificent and indifferent, having paid her eleven thousand dollars and charged her fourteen back, and she laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, a short, sharp, unamused sound, and Tomas leaned out the window to look at her with concern.

"You're laughing."

"I'm doing accounting," she said.

"Freya."

"Two hundred and forty-four thousand," she said. "Give or take. We went *up* tonight, Tomas. We chased a beautiful storm, we did everything right, we delivered early, and we ended the day deeper in the hole than we started. That's not bad luck. That's the *business model*."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. Rain dripped off the truck's scrap-plate armor in long silver threads. Behind them the supercell was going gorgeous in the sunset, backlit, its anvil gilded, putting on a show for an audience of two bankrupt people and one very confused herd of cattle.

"You know what my grandmother used to say," Tomas offered. "'The sea gives and the sea takes.' Very comforting woman. Terrible at funerals."

"The sky gives and the sky takes," Freya said. "And then the sky charges a handling fee."

* * *

They camped that night on high ground above the flooded river bottoms, because the client's forecast had been right and the rivers were up everywhere, brown and fast and carrying fence posts and somebody's propane tank south toward the coast. Freya sat in the truck's cab with the dome light on, rebuilding the field mill's control board by hand, because sleep was a thing she did after the work was done and the work was never done, and Tomas sat across from her cleaning the spectrometer with the tenderness of a man tending a wounded animal.

"Question," Tomas said, after a while.

"Answer's no."

"You don't know the question."

"The question is 'why don't we ever stay anywhere longer than a season,' and the answer is no."

Tomas set down his cloth. "I was going to ask if you wanted tea."

"Oh." Freya soldered a connection that didn't need soldering. "Yes. Tea. And—no. To the other thing. We don't stay because staying is how the Syndicate finds you. Staying is how the mail finds you. Staying is how a town gets familiar, and familiar people get names, and named people get—" She gestured with the soldering iron at the general concept of her life. "A season is the right length. Long enough to work. Short enough to be a rumor by the time anyone asks questions."

"You've been a rumor in nine towns," Tomas said. "I've counted."

"Ten. You forgot Cedar Bluff."

"I didn't forget Cedar Bluff. I've *chosen* to remember Cedar Bluff differently." He handed her the tea, and she took it, and for a while the only sounds were rain on the roof and the river moving in the dark below them, big and restless, doing whatever rivers do at night when nobody's watching.

"Two hundred and forty-four thousand," Freya said eventually, to the tea, to the dark, to the number itself, which was the closest thing she had to a prayer. "Twenty-two more storms. Seven months of everything going right."

"Nothing goes right," Tomas said comfortably. "That's the deal. But hey—look at it this way. Tomorrow the sun comes out, the roads dry out, and we go find another storm."

"Another storm that costs more than it pays."

"Another storm that costs more than it pays," he agreed. "It's almost poetic. We're like—what's the word—altruists. For the Syndicate."

Freya laughed despite herself, a real one this time, and it surprised her how easily it came, how the warmth in the cab of the truck could hold off, for one evening, the arithmetic waiting outside in the dark.

Below them, the river kept rising. It had come up over its banks an hour ago, quiet and businesslike, spreading into the willow flats where the water moved in slow braided channels, picking things up and carrying them along—fence posts, branches, a propane tank, the small unclaimed property of the flooded country. Freya glanced down at it once, professionally, noting the rate of rise, and thought nothing of it at all.

By morning, the river would be offering her something she had spent her whole life making sure no current could ever reach her with.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was tea, and a broken board under her hands, and a number in her head with forty cents on the end of it, and the storm walking away east with its gold anvil burning down toward the horizon like a promise the sky had no intention of keeping.
