## 2. What the River Keeps

The river crested at four in the morning, and by seven it had begun to apologize.

Tomas Veyra stood on the levee road with a thermos of coffee going lukewarm in his hands, watching the water pull back from the fields like a guest remembering an appointment elsewhere. It left behind the usual confessions: a drowned fence line, somebody's patio furniture arranged with surreal dignity in the middle of a beet field, a canoe wedged twenty feet up a poplar. The flood had come through the valley the way debt came through a life—loudly, taking what it wanted, and then receding just enough to make you think maybe things would be fine.

They were not fine. Tomas did the arithmetic anyway, because arithmetic was his job and his coping mechanism and, Freya said once, his entire personality.

"Fuel's down to a third," he told her, when she emerged from the truck cab with her hair still wet from a sponge bath and her eyes already scanning the sky out of habit. "We've got two hundred and change after the client wire clears. That covers the crossing fees at Brannock and maybe half a tank if prices haven't gone stupid."

"They'll have gone stupid." Freya leaned against the fender and drank from his thermos without asking, which was how he knew she'd slept badly. "Flood season. Everyone's buying fuel and selling panic."

"We could sit this one out. Dry out the rig. I could re-solder the sensor leads that got chewed—"

"We're not sitting anything out." She said it lightly, the way you'd decline a second helping of pie, but Tomas had learned to hear the steel underneath. Sitting still meant being findable. Being findable meant the Syndicate's letters caught up to them, and Freya Dunmore would rather drive into a hail core than open another envelope with the Vane crest on it. "Weather's building over the highlands. Two days, maybe three. We chase it, we bill it, we keep breathing."

"That's not a plan, that's a pulse."

"It's worked so far." She handed back the thermos, and something in her face softened by a degree—the closest thing to an apology their arrangement allowed. "Eat something, Tomas. You get philosophical when you're hungry."

"I'm always philosophical."

"And I'm always right. One of us has to be."

She went off to walk the rig's tires, checking for the cuts that floodwater roads liked to hide, and Tomas stood alone on the levee with his coffee and his arithmetic and the river doing its slow, embarrassed retreat below him.

He should have gone back to the truck. That was the whole survival routine, the one they'd built over three years of seasons: keep moving, keep small, touch nothing that isn't yours, want nothing you can't carry. Freya had the rules down to scripture. He'd helped write them. Rule three, as he remembered it, was *if it looks like trouble, it is trouble, and trouble has your name on it.*

But the willows along the cutbank were doing something odd.

Tomas noticed it the way he noticed most things—not dramatically, but persistently. The water had parted around one particular root mass near the bend, leaving a pocket of calm where everything else ran brown and fast, and snagged in those roots, half-lifted now that the river was dropping, was a shape. Rectangular. Dark. About the size of a footlocker, if footlockers were built by people who took footlockers seriously.

It could have been anything. A dead man's luggage. A piece of someone's barn. Half the valley's possessions were currently distributed across three counties, and the polite thing, the *routine* thing, was to let the river finish its errand.

Tomas looked over his shoulder. Freya was crouched by the rear duals, absorbed, muttering about tread depth. The morning was gray and mild and smelled like wet earth and diesel, and nobody in the world was watching him except, possibly, the chest.

That was the other thing. He'd been looking at it for a full minute before he admitted to himself why he couldn't look away: it didn't look like debris. Debris tumbled. Debris got scuffed and split and wore its history openly. This thing sat in the roots with the composure of something that had been placed there—or at least something that intended to be found. Its corners were banded in metal gone green-black with age, and where the mud had sloughed off its lid, there was a mark. He couldn't see it clearly from the levee. He could only see that it was deliberate, symmetrical, and old in a way that made the hair on his forearms stand up, which was ridiculous, because he was thirty-four years old and a professional navigator and he did not get goosebumps from furniture.

"Freya," he called, before his caution could catch up with his mouth. "You seeing this?"

She straightened, followed his pointing arm, and he watched the exact moment her expression closed. It was like watching a shopkeeper shutter windows ahead of a storm. Her whole posture changed—weight shifting back toward the truck, chin down, eyes doing that quick perimeter sweep she thought he never noticed.

"Logjam junk," she said. "Leave it."

"You don't know that."

"I know rule three."

"Rule three says trouble has our name on it." Tomas set down the thermos on the guardrail, which even he recognized as a significant gesture, a man putting down his coffee in preparation for foolishness. "I'm pretty sure nothing down there has our name on it. Nothing ever does. That's sort of our whole situation."

"Tomas."

"Two minutes. I'll wade in, look, and if it's somebody's grandma's hope chest full of wet quilts, I'll leave it for the county and never speak of it again."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. There was a version of this conversation they'd had before, in various forms, usually ending with her winning, because she was Freya and winning arguments was load-bearing for her. But there was something else in her face this morning—fatigue, maybe, or the specific loneliness of a woman who had spent three years making sure no one anywhere could describe her accurately—and when she finally spoke, her voice had dropped its edge.

"The water's fast under that bend. The drop-off past the roots goes down six feet if it goes down an inch. And whatever that is, it's been in a river for who knows how long, and rivers keep things for reasons." She said it flatly, not superstitious exactly, but fluent in superstition the way storm chasers had to be. "We pull weird things out of floods, Tomas, and weird things pull us into stories. Stories have witnesses. Witnesses have names."

"You've been reading my rulebook."

"Somebody has to, since you clearly stopped." But she sighed, and rubbed the back of her neck, and looked at the sky again—that habitual upward glance, checking for weather, checking for aircraft, checking for the world's attention—and then, quietly, in the voice of a woman losing an argument to herself: "Rope. Harness. And if the current takes you, I am not writing your mother a letter explaining I let you fetch a box."

"My mother would be thrilled anyone explained anything to me."

* * *

The water was colder than the season justified, the kind of cold that came down off mountains early and uninvited. Tomas went in slow, rope hitched around his waist with the knot Freya insisted on tying herself because, she said, his knots were "decorative." The current grabbed at his thighs immediately, insistent as a creditor, and he understood her point about the drop-off very quickly—one step the riverbed was there, the next it wasn't, and suddenly he was swimming more than wading, breath punched shallow, hands out, the willow roots arriving faster than he'd planned.

He got a boot wedged into the root mass and held on, gasping, feeling profoundly stupid and also, beneath the stupidity, awake. That was the honest truth of it, the thing he'd never say to Freya: three years of running small had sanded something down in him. Caution kept them alive, yes. But caution was also a room, and lately he'd begun to notice the walls.

The chest was right there, cradled in the roots like something the river had been minding. Up close he could see it was oak under the mud, iron-banded, and heavy in a way that spoke of contents rather than construction. The lid bore a plate, tarnished almost black, and Tomas wiped it with his palm and the river water and his heart did something complicated.

Two birds, facing away from each other, wings raised, crowned by a single star between them. Struck deep enough into the metal that no amount of river could wear it smooth.

He didn't know the mark. That was the strange part—he knew he didn't know it, and he also knew, with the certainty of a man who had spent his life reading maps, that he *should*. It had the grammar of a national seal. Nations put marks like that on things, and nations were careful about which things carried them, and none of that added up to a chest floating loose in a flooded tributary in the middle of nowhere.

"Tomas!" Freya's voice, tight, from the bank. "Time!"

"Give me a second—" He got both arms under it and lifted, and the chest came free of the roots with a reluctance that felt less like suction and more like opinion. For one bad moment the current took them both, swinging him out over the drop-off, and then his boot found stone and he hauled, hand over hand, Freya pulling the rope on the bank with curses he could hear over the water, until the two of them collapsed together on the cutbank gravel with the chest between them, streaming, enormous, real.

For a while neither of them said anything. They just breathed. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher made its opinion known.

"Well," Tomas managed finally. "Somebody's grandma's hope chest."

Freya sat in the gravel, soaked to the elbows, staring at the seal on the lid. He watched her go very still—the particular stillness she got when data contradicted a model, when the storm did something the numbers said it couldn't. Her fingers hovered over the two birds without touching.

"That's not a freight mark," she said slowly. "That's not anybody's brand. That's heraldry."

"You recognize it?"

"No." A pause. The pause went on too long. "And that bothers me more than recognizing it would."

They got it up to the truck together, grunting, leaving a dark drag-line in the mud like a signature. Freya spread a tarp in the cargo bed and they laid the chest on it, and then they both stood there dripping, looking at it, at an impasse without having discussed anything.

"We should open it," Tomas said.

"We should drive away from it."

"It survived a river to land practically at our feet. That's either coincidence or invitation."

"Coincidence." Freya's jaw set. "Tomas, listen to me. Things that arrive are things that were sent. Things that were sent have senders. Senders want something. Every time in my life that something has wanted something from me, it has cost me more than I had." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse; it was plainly a ledger entry, a sum she'd already done. "We take a photo, we log the location, and if some county clerk comes asking about salvage, we were never here."

"And if nobody comes asking? If it sits in that bed for a month and we both pretend we can't hear it?" He gestured at the chest, at the seal, at the whole unreasonable fact of it. "Freya. We run from everything. I've gotten good at it. I'm *great* at it. But I'd like, just once, before the money runs out or Rook's people find us or the passes close—to open one door instead of locking it. Even a bad door. Even a door that bites."

She looked at him then, really looked, and Tomas realized he'd never quite said that much out loud before, and neither, judging by her face, had she heard it. Three years of partnership built on shared silence, and here he was cracking it over a muddy box.

"The light's wrong anyway," she said at last, gruff, turning away toward the cab. "Sun's flat. We won't get good photos till evening."

Tomas blinked. "So—"

"So we're driving to the far side of the levee where there's cover, and we're drying out, and *then* we'll see." She yanked the driver's door open, then paused, half in, half out, and gave him a look over the truck's roof that was equal parts warning and surrender. "One look inside. Curiosity satisfied, box logged, story over. That's the deal."

"That's the deal," Tomas agreed solemnly, and climbed in beside her, and did not mention that he'd already noticed the keyhole under the seal plate—a keyhole shaped like nothing he'd ever seen, a shape like a star with too many points—and that some traitorous part of him had already started wondering what, in all the drowned world, might fit it.

Behind them, the river kept sliding past the emptied willow roots, patient as ever, keeping what it kept.

And in the cargo bed, under the tarp, the chest rode with them into the gray morning—silent, sealed, and heavier than oak and iron had any right to be.
