## 3. A Seal No One Should Recognize

The chest sat on the tailgate of the rig like a guest neither of us had invited.

Freya had spent the morning doing everything she could to avoid looking at it directly. She'd re-torqued the surviving half of her sensor array twice. She'd inventoried the fuel cans, counted them, counted them again, and arrived at the same number both times, which was somehow disappointing. She'd even cleaned the windshield, an act so unprecedented that Tomas had come out of the cab to watch, arms folded, wearing the expression of a man observing a solar eclipse.

"You're stalling," he said.

"I'm maintaining equipment."

"You're cleaning glass. You've never cleaned glass in your life. I once watched you drive four hundred miles through a locust swarm without wiping the bugs off."

"The locusts added texture," Freya said. "It was aerodynamic."

"Freya."

She sighed and turned to face the chest.

It was a good chest, she'd give it that. River-logged for who knew how long, and still sound—oak banded in iron, corners capped in brass gone green with age. The floodwater had scoured it clean enough that whatever markings it bore stood out sharp against the wood, and that was the problem. Because Freya knew what those markings were. She'd known from the moment Tomas hauled it up the bank, dripping, triumphant, grinning like a man who'd won a raffle he didn't remember entering.

A double-headed eagle, wings spread, rendered in stamped brass. Each head faced away from the other, as if the bird couldn't decide where it was going, which struck Freya as the most honest piece of heraldry she'd ever seen.

"That's not a real country," Tomas said, for the fourth time since yesterday.

"It was a real country."

"Countries don't just stop being countries."

"They do when bigger countries get hungry." Freya ran a thumb along the eagle's wing. "Karstia. It's in exactly none of the atlases printed after 1931. Before that—" She stopped herself. She'd already said too much, and they both knew it, and Tomas was too polite to say *how do you know that* out loud, which was worse than if he'd asked.

He asked anyway. Of course he did. "How do you know that?"

"I read."

"You read weather models and debt notices."

"And sometimes other things." She popped the latch before he could follow up. Avoidance only worked if you committed to it fully, and she was, historically, a woman of incomplete commitments.

* * *

The lid came up on a breath of cold air that smelled of river mud and old paper and something underneath both—brass polish, maybe, or cedar, some stubborn sweetness refusing to die.

Three things lay inside, nested like organs in a body.

The first was an instrument. Brass, about the size of a dinner plate, mounted on a folding stand: rings within rings, small spheres on curved arms, a central pinion worn bright by hands. An orrery—a model of the heavens—but wrong somehow. The planets were there, roughly, but the gearing was dense, overbuilt, more teeth than any solar system required. Freya lifted it out and felt its weight settle into her palms with the particular gravity of something expensive.

"Tomas," she said. "Do not touch this."

"I wasn't going to touch this."

"You were absolutely going to touch this. Your hand was already moving."

"My hand was considering moving," he said, wounded. "There's a difference."

The second thing was a ledger. Leather-bound, swollen slightly with damp but intact, its pages edged in gilt that had survived better than it had any right to. Freya set it beside the orrery and didn't open it yet. Not yet. There was a sequence to dread, and she intended to observe it properly.

The third thing was an envelope. Heavy paper, wax-sealed, and the wax bore the same double-eagle, pressed deep. Someone had written across the front in faded brown ink, in a hand that slanted hard to the right, like a person writing against a wind:

*For the Dunmore line.*

Freya put the envelope down on the tailgate very carefully, the way you'd put down a snake you weren't sure was dead.

"Well," said Tomas.

"Well," said Freya.

"That's your name."

"It's a common name."

"It is not a common name. I've known you six years, Freya. In six years I have met one other Dunmore, and that was you, being sarcastic to a loan officer under a false surname."

"That was Dunmore-adjacent."

"That was you lying badly." He picked up the envelope, turned it over, and held it out to her. "Open it."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because nothing good has ever arrived in an envelope with a dead nation's seal on it, addressed to my family, inside a chest fished out of a flooded river by a man who also once brought home a dog we could not afford."

"We could not afford the dog," Tomas agreed. "But Biscuit worked out."

"Biscuit ate my storm data."

"Biscuit ate one printout, and honestly the model was flawed anyway—"

"*Tomas.*"

He set the envelope down between them. The wind moved through the willows along the cutbank, and somewhere downstream the river kept doing what rivers do, which is give things back whether you want them or not.

Freya looked at the envelope for a long time. Then she opened it, because the alternative was thinking about the Vane Syndicate's interest calculations, and a dead nation's mail was frankly preferable to live debt.

* * *

The letter was two pages, written in the same wind-slanted hand, and whoever had written it had written fast, the way people write when they believe the door might open at any moment.

Freya read it once silently. Then, because certain sentences refused to be believed quietly, she read parts of it aloud.

"'To whichever of the line survives to read this,'" she recited. "'If you are reading it, then the water has done what we could not, and carried our trust past their checkpoints, past their maps, past their memory. They have taken the country. They cannot take the keeping of it.'"

Tomas had gone very still, the way he did when a forecast turned. "Keep reading."

"'The orrery is not a toy. It is a key, though not of any lock you can hold in your hand. The ledger names those who kept the archive safe, generation upon generation, custodians all. If your name is written in that book, then you are one of us now, whether you wish it or no. Wishing has never been part of the arrangement.'" Freya paused. "Whoever wrote this was not an optimist."

"They were a realist with good penmanship."

"'Bury it deep. Move often. Trust water and winter, and trust no one whose interest arrives before their name does.'" Freya lowered the page. "That last bit sounds like advice aimed specifically at me."

"Everything sounds like advice aimed specifically at you. That's called guilt."

"It's called pattern recognition, and I resent it."

She read the rest in silence. There was a signature at the bottom, a name she didn't recognize—*E. Vasari-Karsti*—and beneath it a date from before she was born, and beneath *that*, smaller, almost an afterthought, a single line that made the skin on her arms prickle despite the warm afternoon:

*The Dunmores kept faith when keeping faith cost everything. We do not forget our own.*

Freya folded the letter along its original creases, which felt like handling a sleeping animal, and put it back in the envelope.

"So," Tomas said gently. "Your family. Karstian."

"I don't know."

"Freya."

"I don't *know*. My grandmother—" She stopped. Started again, slower. "My grandmother never talked about where she was from. Not wouldn't. Couldn't, maybe. When I asked, she'd change the subject to rainfall. Which, for a woman who otherwise treated weather as a personal enemy, was saying something." Freya looked out at the flatland, at the sky doing nothing useful in any direction. "I thought she was just private. People are private. But private people leave *something*. A town name. A recipe. A swear word in another language. She left nothing. Nothing is a choice, Tomas. Nothing takes work."

Tomas nodded slowly. He was good at silence, Tomas. It was one of the three reasons she kept him around. The other two were navigation and the fact that he made coffee strong enough to strip corrosion.

"And the ledger," he said. "Are we going to look at the ledger?"

Freya looked at the ledger. It sat there, gilt-edged and patient, radiating the smugness of objects that know they'll be opened eventually.

"Yes," she said. "But first I want you to understand something. Whatever's in that book, we are not going to do anything dramatic about it. We are going to look at it, close the chest, and continue our lives, which consist of chasing storms for money we immediately lose to people who employ men named after kitchen implements. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Tomas, in the tone of a man agreeing to nothing.

* * *

The ledger opened to a page of names in columns, dates beside them, and Freya's plan to be undramatic lasted approximately ninety seconds.

Because the names went back centuries. Page after page, hand changing decade by decade—the spidery iron gall of the 1800s giving way to fountain pen, then ballpoint, then pencil during what she guessed were the war years, then back to ink, stubbornly, as if someone had decided pencils were surrender. Custodians. *Custodians all.* And each entry followed the same format: a name, a date of taking up the charge, and a place.

And there, three-quarters through, in handwriting that made her stomach drop through the tailgate and into the topsoil, because she recognized it—of course she recognized it, she'd seen it on every recipe card and birthday card and ration-book margin her grandmother had ever touched—

*Dunmore, Althea. Taken up the charge, spring 1929. Halvett station.*

Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother's actual name, written in a dead language's alphabet in a book that had spent decades underwater, entered the year before Karstia vanished from the maps and, apparently, from her family's mouths forever.

Freya sat down on the tailgate. Her legs made the decision independently and she let them.

"Freya?" Tomas crouched in front of her. "Talk to me."

"She was here." Freya's voice came out strange, flattened. "Before. She was one of them. She *kept* something. And then she ran, and she changed everything about herself down to the way she talked about rain, and she raised a family on nothing, on blankness, and she died blank, and I grew up thinking that was normal. Thinking *I* was normal." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I built a whole life on her blankness, Tomas. Never staying. No records. No forwarding address. I thought I was careful. I thought I was *free*."

"You thought you were hiding."

"I thought I was hiding *well*."

Tomas said nothing for a moment. Then: "The letter said the orrery is a key."

"It said a lot of things."

"It said a key to something the Syndicate has hunted for decades." He said it carefully, watching her face. "You recognized the seal on sight, Freya. Before you opened anything. And last night, when I mentioned the chest, you checked the mirrors twice before sleeping. So let's skip to the part where you tell me what you actually know, and I promise not to say 'I told you so' about any of it."

"There's nothing to—"

"Freya."

She exhaled. Looked at the orrery gleaming on the tailgate, its overbuilt gears, its worn pinion, its patient brass certainty.

"I know the stories," she admitted. "The ones kids traded at school when teachers weren't listening. That Karstia wasn't conquered—it was *sold*. That the people who arranged it got rich, and their families are still rich, and still powerful, and they've spent eighty years making sure nobody finds the proof. And that the proof exists. Somewhere. That the archive wasn't burned—it was *hidden*, by people like—" she gestured at the ledger, at her grandmother's name, "—like her. And that anyone who helps find it ends up either wealthy or dead, depending on which side finds them first."

"And the Syndicate?"

"The Vane Syndicate buys debt the way other people buy stamps. And their clients are never named. And their collectors are very thorough." Freya met his eyes. "I owe them eleven months of my future, Tomas. Maybe fourteen, with penalties. And now I'm sitting on a riverbank holding a key to the one thing their clients are afraid of."

The wind picked up, rattling the willows. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered—not a storm, just the sky clearing its throat—and Freya felt the old reflex rise in her, the good reflex, the *read-the-sky-and-move* reflex that had kept her alive and broke for fifteen years.

Except this time the thing coming wasn't on the horizon. It was in her hands.

"We should go," Tomas said quietly.

"We should go," she agreed, and started packing the chest back together—orrery wrapped, ledger sleeved, letter sealed—and it was as she lifted the ledger that she saw it, on the inside back cover, in fresh ink compared to the rest. Recent. Decades fresher. A single line added long after the last custodian's entry, in handwriting she didn't know:

*If the water brings this to you, they are already looking.*

Headlights appeared on the county road, a mile out, moving slow. Too slow for this road. Nobody drove that road slowly except people looking for something.

Tomas saw them too. "Friends of yours?"

"I don't have friends," Freya said, closing the chest. "I have creditors."

The car turned onto the track toward their camp, kicking up dust, and Freya reached for the rig's ignition with a hand that was almost steady.
