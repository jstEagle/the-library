## 4. The Optimists Call

Tomas Vey saw the car before Freya did, which was unusual, and he didn't like it.

He'd been washing mugs in a basin of river water—three mugs, one basin, the whole of their domestic economy—and he'd looked up out of habit, the way you do in country where nobody comes, and there it was: a grey sedan moving up the gravel track at exactly the speed of someone who has never once been in a hurry. Not police. Police drove like they were apologizing. Not a farmer. Farmers drove like the road owed them money. This car moved with the patient, unhurried confidence of a chess piece.

"Freya," he said, without raising his voice. He never had to raise his voice; she heard weather coming from two counties over and trouble from one.

She came out of the truck with the chest's ledger still half-tucked under her arm, and Tomas watched her do the thing she always did—read the sky first, then the road, then him, in that order of trust. The sky said nothing. The road said plenty.

"That's not a client," she said.

"No."

"Clients call ahead. Clients are excited." She set the ledger down on the tailgate, casually, the way you'd set down a sandwich. "Clients don't drive grey cars that clean this far off pavement."

The sedan stopped forty meters out, close enough to be friendly, far enough to be smart. A woman got out of it. She was maybe fifty, built like a schoolteacher who'd once won something violent, wearing a jacket that cost more than the truck, and she smiled at them both with the particular warmth of a person arriving at a picnic she had personally organized.

"Ilse Rook," she called, hands open and empty. "Vane Syndicate, Recovery Division. And before anyone reaches for anything—I'm unarmed, I'm alone, and I've brought coffee. Actual coffee. Not that chicory punishment you're drinking. I could smell it from the county line."

Freya's hand had drifted, very naturally, to rest near the toolbox where the flare gun lived. "We didn't invite company."

"Nobody ever does. That's what makes this job interesting." Rook set a thermos on the hood of her car and stepped back from it, a little pantomime of harmlessness that Tomas found more alarming than a drawn weapon would have been. "Ms. Dunmore. Mr. Vey. May I?"

"You may not," Freya said pleasantly. "But you will anyway."

Rook laughed—a real laugh, or an excellent forgery of one—and walked the forty meters like she was crossing her own garden. Up close she was smaller than she'd seemed, and older, with laugh lines that hadn't finished being earned. Her eyes went over the rig with a professional's affection: the patched sensor mast, the solar panels zip-tied into obedience, the antenna Freya had salvaged from three different wrecks.

"Beautiful machine," Rook said. "You keep it alive on spite and baling wire. I respect that. I ran field ops for eleven years before they gave me a desk and a smile budget."

"What do you want?" Freya asked.

"Straight to it. Good. I'll match you." Rook clasped her hands behind her back, a woman settling comfortably into a subject she loved. "Your debt. Let's talk about your debt, Ms. Dunmore, because I've read the file and it's a masterpiece. Fourteen thousand for the array you lost to lightning last spring. Nine for the tow out of the Kessel flats. Interest structured so creatively that my own accountants asked if it was legal. You owe the Syndicate thirty-one thousand six hundred and change, and every season you chase weather for our clients, we take sixty percent, which means—" she did a small, apologetic arithmetic with her fingers— "you can never catch up. It's not designed to be caught up. It's designed to be a leash. You know that. I'm not telling you anything you don't calculate every night in that truck."

Tomas glanced at Freya. She was standing very still, which was how she shouted.

"So here's the offer," Rook continued, "and it's a good one, so let me say all of it before you say no. The chest. The one your partner pulled out of the river three days ago—the one you've been sleeping ten feet away from like it's a bomb. Hand it over, contents intact, and the debt dissolves. All of it. Clean slate. We even throw in a contract renewal at honest rates, because the Syndicate believes in leaving people better than we found them." She smiled again. "That's why they call us the Optimists' Division."

"Who calls you that?" Tomas asked.

"We do," Rook said cheerfully. "It's on the letterhead."

* * *

They made her drink her own coffee while they talked—or rather, while Freya talked and Tomas stood where he could see both Rook and the tree line, because a woman who drove alone into the middle of nowhere did not arrive alone, whatever her hands said.

"The chest isn't ours to sell," Freya said.

"It washed out of a river onto public land. That's about as ours as anything gets."

"It has names in it. Names of families. There's a letter addressed to a bloodline, and we happen to be standing in it." Freya crossed her arms. "You want me to sell other people's mail."

"I want you to stop drowning," Rook said, and for a moment the smile dropped and something almost kind looked out. "Ms. Dunmore. Freya. I've read your file the way you read a storm front. You're competent, you're careful, and you have spent—I'm guessing—six years making yourself small enough that nothing big could find you. Am I close?"

Tomas felt Freya go quiet beside him, the specific quiet of a person deciding whether to lie.

"And then," Rook went on softly, "a river hands you a box with your grandmother's name in it, and suddenly you're visible again. That must feel like the sky falling. But here's what I know that you don't: visibility is expensive. Other people pay for it. People who've been looking for this chest since before you were born, and who are very patient, and who sent me instead of sending the men they usually send—which, believe me, is a courtesy. Take the deal. Walk away rich in the only currency that matters to you, which is unowned."

"You make it sound generous," Freya said.

"It is generous. It's the most generous thing I'll offer this week." Rook picked up her thermos, screwed the lid on with small precise movements. "I'll be in Halvett until Friday. If I don't hear from you by Thursday night, I'll assume the answer is no, and I'll have to inform my superiors, and they are less pleasant than I am. Considerably less pleasant. They don't bring coffee."

She left the thermos anyway. Freya wouldn't touch it, so Tomas poured two cups that evening, mostly out of curiosity, and it was, annoyingly, excellent coffee.

"She's right, you know," he said.

"Don't."

"Not about selling it. About the leash." He turned his cup slowly. "Thirty-one thousand. Sixty percent. I've done the sums too, Frey. Every year we chase, we fall further behind. She didn't threaten us today. She described us."

Freya stared across the river at the willows where the chest had snagged, where everything had started, and her jaw did the thing it did when she was holding words down like hatch covers in heavy seas.

"My mother used to say the safest place is the one nobody's looking at," she said finally. "We built our whole lives in that sentence, Tom. And it took one flood to wash it out."

* * *

He woke at 2:40 in the morning because the crickets stopped.

Not faded—stopped, all at once, the way they do when something moves through the grass that isn't wind. Tomas lay still in his bedroll behind the truck cab and listened, and after a moment he heard it: footsteps, plural, unhurried, circling the camp at the radius of a polite stranger. Then the soft click of the truck's rear latch.

He rolled out of the bedroll without a sound—he'd gotten good at that, years ago, back when being quiet had been a job skill—and moved along the shadow side of the vehicle. Two figures at the tailgate. One held a flashlight with a red filter; the other lifted things out and passed them over: the spare fuel cans, the data crates, and then—

Then the chest.

The man holding the flashlight opened it, and the red light fell on the brass orrery, and the orrery did something Tomas had never seen it do in daylight. The little rings, the nested arms with their planets of worn brass, began—slowly, silently—to turn. Not freely. Against some resistance, like a clock being wound by something underneath it, and the needle at its heart swung off north and settled, quivering, pointing east-northeast. Toward the Escarpment.

The flashlight man went very still. Then he said something low in a language Tomas didn't know but recognized the shape of—it had the clipped, old-fashioned vowels of the letter in the chest—and his companion took a photograph of the instrument with a phone whose screen he shielded with his body.

Tomas should have stayed hidden. Every sensible instinct, six years of training, the entire philosophy of Freya Dunmore's life, said stay hidden, let them take what they take, survive.

Instead he knocked over the fuel can.

It wasn't graceful. It wasn't planned. His elbow caught it and it went over with a boom like a drum, rolling, and both men spun, and Tomas came around the corner of the truck with the tire iron already swinging—not at them, at the tailgate, slamming it down hard enough to wake the dead and possibly the neighbors, though there were no neighbors, there never were, that had always been the point.

"FREYA!" he bellowed. "COMPANY!"

What happened next happened fast and badly. The taller man grabbed for him; Tomas ducked inside the reach and shoved him into the truck, which bought two seconds. The second man had a hand inside his jacket and was deciding something when the camp light snapped on—Freya, barefoot in the dirt with the flare pistol in her fist and a look on her face that Tomas had only seen directed at tornadoes.

"Touch that chest again," she said, very calmly, "and I put a flare through your windshield, and then everyone within twenty miles knows exactly where you are. Including whoever you work for. Including the people who sent you. Do you think they reward attention?"

The men looked at each other. The red-filtered flashlight lay in the dirt, still burning its small dishonest light on the orrery, whose needle still pointed steadily, patiently, northeast, as though it had been waiting years for someone to ask it the question.

The taller man straightened his jacket with wounded dignity. "Thursday," he said, in accented English. "Madam Rook said Thursday. After that, madam Rook stops being the one who visits."

They walked out into the dark toward the track, unhurried even now, chess pieces retreating in good order, and the crickets started up again behind them like a curtain falling.

Freya lowered the flare gun. Her hands, Tomas noticed, were shaking now that it was over, which was the correct order for shaking hands.

"They photographed the orrery," he said. "And it moved, Frey. On its own. It turned and pointed—"

"I saw." She crouched by the open chest, and the needle's faint gleam reflected in her eyes. When she spoke again her voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, the voice she used for readings that didn't fit any model. "Northeast. Into the high country. Tom—we leave tonight. Before Thursday. Before they stop smiling."

Tomas looked at the chest, at the brass rings still turning their slow patient circles, at the road south where a grey sedan sat parked with its owner asleep in Halvett, believing she had all the time in the world.

"Pack light," he said. "And Frey? Next time a river gives us something, we're throwing it back."

But he was already reaching for the map.
