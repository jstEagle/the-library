## 5. Ledger of the Vanished

The chest sat on the tailgate of the truck like a guest who'd overstayed its welcome, and Freya Dunmore circled it with the same wariness she'd give a tornado that had decided to park.

They'd driven two hours north after Rook's visit, far enough that the river country gave way to scrub pine and gravel pull-offs, and had made camp in the lee of an abandoned grain elevator that smelled of rust and old pigeon decisions. Tomas had built a fire small enough to hide in a coffee can. Freya had eaten half a tin of beans without tasting them. And now there was nothing left between her and the ledger except the ledger itself.

"You could just not open it," Tomas offered from his bedroll, in the tone of a man who knew exactly how useful his advice was going to be.

"I opened it already."

"You know what I mean."

She did. He meant: you could put the lid back on, drive until the chest could be buried in some reservoir, and go back to being nobody, which was the family business. Three generations of Dunmores had perfected the art of leaving before anyone learned their names. Freya had refined it into a science—she never signed a lease longer than ninety days, never used her real surname at fuel stops, never told a client where she'd be next week. Invisibility wasn't a habit. It was load-bearing architecture.

But the letter had said *the Dunmore line*, and you couldn't unhear your own name spoken by a dead woman's handwriting.

"Hand me the light," she said.

Tomas sighed the sigh of a man whose conscience had been overruled again, and passed her the headlamp.

* * *

The ledger was bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked in three places from decades of opening. The pages inside were dense, careful script—two columns per page, names on the left, entries on the right—and the handwriting changed every few pages, as though the book had been passed hand to hand down a long chain of people who each believed, correctly, that they might be the last.

Freya read slowly at first, the way you'd walk unfamiliar ground.

The left column held names. Hundreds of them. *Vasari, Ilonka — custodian, third rank.* *Brenner, Oskar — custodian, second rank, western route.* *Halvett registry transferred, see annex C.* Each name had dates beside it, and beside the dates, small annotations in a shorthand she didn't recognize but could feel the shape of: *passed north. passed east. lost. lost. LOST.* One entry simply read, in a shakier hand than the rest: *I am the only one left who knows the order of things. God forgive me if I am wrong.*

"It's a relay," she said aloud, mostly to keep the dark from feeling so large. "A chain of custody. Somebody handed this off, person to person, for—" she turned pages, counting decades the way she counted barometric drops, "—forty years. Maybe fifty."

"Relay of what?"

"That's the part I don't have yet." She flipped backward, toward the earliest entries, where the ink was brown-black and the paper had the texture of a leaf. "There's a word that keeps showing up. *Arkhiv.* Same root as archive, I think. And another one—*Skrinjë.*"

"Chest," Tomas said quietly.

Freya looked up. His face was firelit and unreadable, which for Tomas meant he was working hard at looking calm.

"You speak Karstian?"

"My grandmother did. A little. She sang lullabies in it." He shrugged, elaborately casual, which was how Tomas did things that were not casual at all. "It's a mountain language. Nobody wrote it down much."

Freya filed that away—*nobody wrote it down much*, from a man who noticed everything—and went back to the ledger. The entries thinned as she moved forward in time. Names spaced further apart. More *lost*. The chain was breaking, link by link, and this chest had been the last handoff, the final runner passing the baton into floodwater because there was no one left to hand it to.

Except there was. Except the letter had been very specific.

She found the page an hour past midnight, when the fire had burned down to orange eyes and her neck ached from hunching.

The entry was newer than the others—not new, but newer, maybe twenty-five years old, in a firm upright hand with a slight forward lean, like someone writing against wind. The name in the left column was:

*Dunmore, Alba — custodian, first rank. Keeper of the Orrery's purpose.*

Freya stopped breathing in the way she'd trained herself out of years ago, because storm work punishes people who forget to breathe. She breathed. She made herself breathe. Her hands were not steady anyway.

"Tomas."

He was already sitting up. He'd learned, somewhere in six years of partnership, to read the specific quality of silence that meant *come look at this and also possibly hold my hand.*

"That's my grandmother." Her voice came out flat, which was how she kept it from coming out broken. "Alba Dunmore. She died when I was nine. My mother never talked about her. I thought—" She gestured vaguely at the ledger, at all of it. "I thought our family was just good at running. I thought running was all we were."

Tomas crouched beside her and read the entry twice. Then he reached past her and turned the page, because Tomas had the nerve of a man who'd once talked his way past a roadblock using only a cooler of bait.

Below Alba Dunmore's name, in the same forward-leaning hand, was a paragraph. Not shorthand. Plain words, written for whoever found them, which apparently was always going to be a Dunmore.

*To my granddaughter, if the line holds and the river is kind:*

*You will want to sell what we kept. Everyone does, at first. Sell it, and it buys you a year. Two, if you're frugal. Then you are exactly what you were, minus one thing that cannot be replaced, and the people who took our country will still own the arithmetic of everyone else's life.*

*What we kept is not treasure. It is testimony. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

*Under the Escarpment, in the vault the surveyors called Kestrel's Door, lies the arkhiv—the whole confiscated record of Karstia. Every census. Every land grant. Every photograph of every wedding the Annexation erased. The Vane Syndicate has hunted it since before you were born, not for gold—for leverage. Proof is the one commodity they cannot mint. The orrery is the key. It does not open the door; it finds it. Learn to read it the way you read weather, and it will show you what the mountains were made to forget.*

*I am sorry for the weight. I carried it light so you would never feel it. That was cowardice dressed as kindness, and I answer for it now, here, at the end of the chain.*

*You are not inheriting a fortune. You are inheriting a duty. Forgive me both.*

*— A.D.*

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a coyote made its opinion known about something.

Freya read it four times. On the fourth pass, she started laughing—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"What?" Tomas asked.

"She *knew*. She knew about the Syndicate, she knew about the vault, she knew somebody would come hunting—and instead of telling my mother, instead of telling *me*, she put it in a box and let a river decide." Freya pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "I spent my whole life thinking I chose this. The moving, the hiding, the ninety-day leases. Like it was my personality. Turns out it's an inheritance. I'm the fourth generation of a witness protection program nobody officially joined."

"Hey." Tomas's voice was gentle, but his eyes weren't; they were doing the thing they did when he was about to say something true. "You could still put it down. Right now. We drive to the reservoir, we sink it, we go chase hail for the Syndicate until they get bored of us. Nobody would ever know. That option doesn't expire tonight."

"Yes it does." She tapped the letter. "*Sell it, and it buys you a year.* She's telling me there's no version of this where I stay invisible. The chest found us, Tomas. Out of every river in the world, it snagged on the willows fifty meters from our camp. You don't survive that kind of coincidence by pretending it isn't aimed at you."

"So what do we do?"

Freya looked at the orrery.

It sat where they'd placed it that morning, wrapped in Tomas's spare shirt atop the ammo crate, brass rings nested in brass rings like a metal flower closed for the night. In the firelight it looked inert, ornamental, the kind of thing rich people bought to prove they understood astronomy. But she'd been reading instruments for fifteen years. She knew the difference between decoration and function the way she knew the difference between a pretty cloud and a dangerous one, and this was not decoration. The gearing was too fine. The needle assembly at its heart was jeweled-bearing, surveyor-grade, sensitive enough to sulk at a passing truck.

An instrument that finds rather than opens. An instrument you read like weather.

"Give me your phone," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I need the geomagnetic readings for this grid square, and because if I use mine, the Syndicate's client apps will log the query and Rook will know exactly where we are. Yours has the burner SIM."

Tomas handed it over without argument, which showed how far trust had come since the river. Freya pulled up the magnetic declination data, cross-referenced it against the elevation model of the Escarpment she kept cached for storm positioning, and set the orrery on the tailgate beside the ledger.

Then she did the thing she'd been avoiding since Halvett's lights disappeared behind them. She unwrapped it fully and looked at it as a colleague.

The outermost ring was engraved with months. The next ring, degrees. The third carried star positions—but wrong ones, she realized, shifted systematically, the way a map is shifted when someone wants only the right reader to find the right places. At the center, beneath a glass cover gone cloudy with age, hung the needle: a sliver of magnetized steel balanced on a point finer than a prayer.

She rotated the month ring to the current date. She set the degree ring to the declination offset. Nothing happened, obviously—it wasn't magic, it was machinery—but the needle swung once, lazily, and settled pointing north-northwest.

Toward the Escarpment.

Toward a specific bearing, she thought, if she did the correction the shifted stars implied, that matched no summit, no town, no anything on any map she owned. Just empty high country. A blank spot on the survey, which in her experience was never actually blank. Blank spots were where people hid things.

"It's a compass," Tomas said.

"It's a *lie*," Freya corrected, and heard the wonder creep into her voice despite herself. "It's a compass that pretends to be an orrery so that anyone who steals it reads the stars instead of the needle. My grandmother hid a key inside a clockwork universe." She shook her head slowly. "God, she must've been insufferable. I would've liked her so much."

Tomas laughed—real laughter, tired and warm—and for a moment the grain elevator and the debt and the smiling assassin named Rook all receded, and it was just the two of them and a dead woman's cleverness glowing brass-bright in the firelight.

Then Freya did the other arithmetic. The one she was built for.

Six hours' drive to the foothills. Early snows due within the window the models kept tightening. Rook's team, wherever it was, almost certainly closer than six hours. And somewhere under all that ice and stone, a vault full of proof that a nation had existed, waiting on a needle's bearing.

"This isn't treasure," she said quietly, closing the ledger. "You understand that, right? Whatever's down there, it's not going to pay the debt. It's not going to buy us out. It's going to make us *responsible*. To Mirela's grandfather's ghost, to every *lost* in this book, to a country that doesn't exist anymore except in boxes like this one." She met Tomas's eyes. "We could still sink it."

"We could," Tomas agreed. He picked up his tin of beans, considered it, and set it down again. "But you're already calculating the route, aren't you."

Freya looked at the needle. North-northwest. Steady as a heartbeat.

"Bearing two-nine-seven," she admitted. "Allowing for terrain shadow. We'd want to ford the Alda before the melt comes down tomorrow afternoon, then take the ridge line after dark."

"There it is." He lay back on his bedroll with the air of a man surrendering gracefully. "Wake me at three."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She told herself she was keeping watch, which was technically true, but mostly she sat with the ledger open on her knees and read the names, all of them, front to back, because it seemed suddenly obscene that they should have waited this long for one complete reader. Vasari, Ilonka. Brenner, Oskar. Halvett registry. *Lost. Lost. Lost.* And near the end, in that forward-leaning hand: *Dunmore, Alba,* and below it, added later in different ink, smaller, almost shy:

*Line continues. Unconfirmed. Pray for the girl.*

Freya closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

"Prayer received," she murmured. "Though I'd have preferred money."

That was when Tomas sat bolt upright in his bedroll, head cocked, and held up one finger—their oldest signal, older than the chest, older than the partnership's formal vocabulary. *Listen.*

Far down the valley, maybe two miles, maybe less, an engine turned over. Then a second one. Headlights bloomed briefly against the tree line where the gravel road bent, sweeping the low cloud like searchlights, and went dark again—deliberately dark, which was worse.

Freya was already moving, folding the ledger into its oilcloth, wrapping the orrery, sliding the chest beneath the false floor panel they'd built into the truck bed back when the cargo was merely expensive sensors. Her hands were steady now. Fear, properly dosed, was just focus with better marketing.

"Three o'clock came early," Tomas whispered, pulling the tent stakes.

"Yeah." Freya slid behind the wheel and let the engine wake at idle, quiet as breathing. Ahead of them the dark road climbed toward the foothills, toward the ford, toward bearing two-nine-seven and whatever her grandmother had died guarding. Behind them, two engines idled in the black, patient as creditors.

"Invisibility was always an illusion," she said, more to herself than to him, and put the truck in gear. "Might as well be visible going somewhere."

The headlights behind them flicked on.
