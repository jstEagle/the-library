## 6. Running Water

Tomas had a theory about three o'clock in the morning. It was the hour the world forgot to pretend. No birdsong, no traffic, no neighbor's dog performing its nightly grievances—just the raw machinery of things ticking along underneath, indifferent to anyone watching. Which was exactly why Freya picked it for breaking camp. If you had to run, run when the world wasn't looking.

He stood in the cold with a thermos of coffee he'd already regretted drinking and watched her work by red-lensed headlamp, moving around the truck like a surgeon who'd grown up in the operating theater and never quite left.

"Hand me the tarp clips," she said, not turning around.

"You said you didn't need them."

"I said that an hour ago. I've since revised my position. It's called science."

He fished the clips out of the toolbox and handed them over. Her fingers were quick, sure, almost cheerful in a way she only got when things were going badly enough that competence was the only comfort left. She was rigging the campsite to read as abandoned—not fled, abandoned. The fire pit would look days old by morning; she'd banked it with ash from three separate burns. A torn map weighted under a stone, deliberately the wrong map, one he'd drawn himself full of plausible nonsense. The sensor mast leaned against a tree at the angle of something left behind in haste or boredom.

"And if they buy it?" Tomas asked.

"Then Rook's second team spends two days combing the flats south of here while we're over the ford and into the hills." She cinched a strap. "And if they don't buy it, we find out how good their tracker is."

"Which is it? Optimism or a plan?"

"With me those are the same thing."

It was, annoyingly, true.

* * *

They rolled out at ten past three with the headlights off until the track met the county road, Tomas navigating by the pale seam of sky between the ridgelines and the smell of the river coming up on their left. The chest rode wedged behind his seat, wrapped in a wool blanket, lashed down like cargo that might object to the journey. He'd checked the lashings four times. Five, if you counted the check he did without admitting it.

Freya drove the way she did everything—fast, precise, and with the air of someone who had already gamed out every way this could go wrong and found most of them survivable.

"You're quiet," she said.

"I'm navigating."

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Navigating has more hand gestures."

He looked out at the dark water sliding past beyond the trees. "I keep thinking about Rook's face," he said. "When she smiled at us. Nobody smiles like that unless they've already done the math."

"The math being?"

"That we're worth more caught than chased. Chasing costs money. Catching pays." He turned the coffee cup in his hands. "She gave us forty-eight hours, Freya. Generously. And she'll spend thirty-six of them making sure we think we're winning."

Freya was quiet for a kilometer. Then: "You could still get out. Drop at the crossroads, take the bus money, be in Vellmark by Thursday with a new name and no weather in your life."

"You ask me that every time we run."

"And every time I have to hear the answer out loud so I know it's still true."

"It's still true." He said it plainly, because plainness was the only currency she trusted. "Besides, who else is going to tell you when your optimism and your plan stop being the same thing?"

The corner of her mouth moved. In the dark, that counted as laughter.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and thin over the river country, and with it came the sound that ended the argument about whether they were winning: a single engine, far back along the county road, running steady and unhurried.

Tomas heard it first—he always heard engines first, a leftover from a childhood spent listening for trucks that meant trouble—and held up a hand. Freya killed the headlights they weren't using anyway and eased off the road onto a farm track, threading between fence posts, and they sat in the shadow of a hay barn while the engine sound grew, passed, and faded north of them.

"Wrong direction," Tomas breathed.

"No," Freya said slowly. "Right direction. Ours, twelve hours ago." She stared through the windshield at nothing. "They're not following our trail. They're walking it backward from where we slept. Which means somebody read the camp."

"The ash?"

"The ash was perfect. The tire tracks weren't. I backed the rig into that clearing and I backed it out again—two passes over the same ground reads different than one." She hit the wheel once, lightly, a tap of pure self-annoyance. "Amateur hour. That's what happens when you let the pilot do the driving."

"So they know which way we went."

"They know which way we *were*. There's a difference, and the difference is called the river." She put the truck in gear. "There's a ford eight kilometers upstream. Old crossing, pre-bridge, the kind the maps stopped marking when the bridges went in. Water's high with the flood but dropping. We cross there, and every track we've laid ends at a river that eats evidence for breakfast."

"And if the water's higher than you think?"

"Then we learn something about ourselves."

* * *

The ford announced itself before they saw it—a broad hush in the river's voice where the current spread shallow over gravel, willows leaning in from both banks like conspirators. Except today, after the flood, "shallow" was doing heroic work. The water ran brown and muscular, carrying twigs, a section of fence rail, one extremely committed duck riding the current sideways with an expression Tomas chose to interpret as confidence.

Freya got out and walked the edge, reading the water the way other people read documents. Tomas watched her do it and felt the familiar mix of admiration and dread. Admiration, because she was genuinely the best he'd ever seen at this. Dread, because the best he'd ever seen at this was currently squinting at a brown flood and nodding to herself.

"Gravel bar runs most of the way across," she reported. "Current's maybe knee-deep on the bar, waist-deep in the channel past midstream. Truck can do it if we keep momentum and don't stop for anything."

"Don't stop for anything," Tomas repeated. "Noted. Writing it down."

"Your jokes are a coping mechanism."

"Your driving is a coping mechanism. We all cope."

They rigged the chest double—blanket, then dry bag, then lashed to the cargo rack as high as it would sit. Tomas insisted on the third layer. Freya rolled her eyes and helped him tie it.

"If we lose the orrery," she said quietly, working the knot, "we're just two people in debt running from people with better vehicles."

"I know."

"And if we lose the ledger—"

"We're not going to talk about losing the ledger."

"—then Mirela's price is unpayable and Halvett forgets us by Friday. So don't stall in the channel."

"Wasn't planning to."

"Everyone plans not to. That's why rivers have reputations."

She waded in first, on foot, probing the gravel bar with a pole, and Tomas followed the truck in behind her at her signal, low gear, steady throttle, water climbing the doors in a brown sheet. For thirty seconds it was almost boring—the kind of boring he could have loved forever. Gravel humming under the tires, current shouldering the chassis, Freya out ahead hip-deep and gesturing left, left, straight.

Then the channel.

The bottom fell away the way river bottoms do, without negotiation, and the truck lurched off the bar into water suddenly at the hood, then the windshield, the engine note dropping into a drowning gargle that Tomas felt in his sternum. The current took the tail and swung it. Somewhere behind his seat the chest shifted against its lashings with a groan of wet rope.

"Tomas." Freya's voice, flat and clear, from somewhere above the waterline. "Momentum. Do not lift."

"I'm not lifting!"

"You're lifting with your *soul*, give it fuel—"

He gave it fuel. The engine coughed, swallowed, coughed again, and for one long gray moment the whole world was brown water pressing at the glass and the sound of his own heartbeat filing a formal complaint. Then the front wheels found gravel, the rear followed, and the truck hauled itself up the far bank trailing weed and dignity, stalling at the top with a shudder like a dog shaking off.

Silence. The river kept talking behind them, unbothered.

Tomas turned around.

The chest was there. Lashed, bagged, blanketed, tilted at fifteen degrees and dripping, but there. He put his hand on it anyway, the way you touch a sleeping child to confirm breathing.

"Ledger's dry," he reported. His voice came out steadier than he'd budgeted for. "Orrery's dry. My boots are a complete loss, but the nation of Karstia survives to see another morning."

Freya climbed in, soaked to the ribs, hair plastered down, grinning like a woman who'd just beaten the house. "Knee-deep," she said. "I said knee-deep."

"You said knee-deep on the *bar*. The channel was a surprise to everyone, including the river."

"Take the win, Tomas."

"I'm taking the win. I'm also noting for the record that 'don't stall' was your instruction, and you stalled."

"The truck stalled. I was merely present." She wrung out a sleeve over the door and started the engine, which caught on the second try with a sound like a complaint being withdrawn. Behind them, on the far bank, the willows hid the crossing completely. No tracks led away from it. The river had eaten their trail whole, just as promised, and asked nothing in return except several years of Tomas's life.

* * *

They made the hills by late afternoon, climbing off the floodplain onto a shelf of pine and broken limestone where the road narrowed to a cart track and the cart track narrowed to a suggestion. Freya found a hollow below a ridge, backed the truck in under a lean of rock, and declared it home. Tomas built a fire small enough to hide in a coffee tin and heated the only food the day allowed, which was beans, which were, in fairness, hot.

He was on second watch when he heard it—not an engine this time, but a light. Far back and below, down where the valley darkened toward the river, a flashlight swept twice across a clearing and went still. Then, minutes later, another, farther east. Methodical. Spaced. Men walking a line abreast through country that ate lines for breakfast.

He woke Freya without touching her, the way they'd trained themselves to—just her name, once, at the volume of a thought.

She was up instantly, binoculars up, and for a long time neither of them breathed.

"Two lights," she murmured. "Probably four men. They're quartering the valley mouth." A pause. "They haven't crossed the river yet. They're looking for the crossing."

"The river bought us a day."

"The river bought us a night." She lowered the binoculars, and in the firelight her face did something complicated—part satisfaction, part arithmetic. "Come dawn they'll find the ford. Old crossings aren't secret, Tomas, they're just unfashionable. After that it's our tracks on this side, and this side is all mountain."

"Six days of mountain," Tomas said. "If the snow holds off."

"If the snow holds off." She looked north, where the Escarpment stood up against the last light, and even from here, even at this distance, he could see the white on it. Too much white. Too early. He'd been telling himself it was cloud.

Freya reached over and turned the fire down to a coal. "Sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we climb."

He lay back against the tire and pulled the blanket to his chin, and just before sleep took him he heard her say, very quietly, to herself or to the mountain or possibly to the chest:

"Hold together. All three of us."

Above the ridge, the first flakes came down fine and silent, and none of them saw them fall.
