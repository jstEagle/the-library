## 7. The Town That Forgot Itself

Halvett announced itself the way a liar announces himself—too smoothly, with too much confidence.

We came down out of the hills at dusk with the truck running on fumes and Tomas running on stubbornness, and there it was in the valley below: a scatter of slate roofs and chimney smoke, a river running through it the color of old pewter, and above the town, rising out of a terraced hillside like the spine of some buried animal, a wall of cut stone that no farmer had built and no farmer had ever once mentioned building.

"Look at that masonry," Tomas said, slowing so I could see. "That's Karstian work. Has to be. The coursing, the way they keyed the blocks—"

"Tomas."

"I'm just saying."

"You're just saying it out loud, in a town we're about to walk into, where the last thing we need is a navigator who points at ruins and names them." I rubbed my eyes. Two days of running water and back roads had left me with a headache that lived behind my right eye and paid rent there. "We need fuel, food, and forty-eight hours where nobody looks at us twice. That's the whole list."

"And the chest?"

"The chest stays under the tarp and the tarp stays boring."

He grunted, which from Tomas was a full paragraph of disagreement, and took the switchback down into town.

* * *

Halvett had two streets, a fuel pump that predated the war it sat beside, and an inn called the Grey Goose that had clearly been called something else once. You could see the ghost of the old name under the paint on the signboard—two words, scraped and repainted, the second one stubbornly legible if the light hit it right. *Kestrel.* My chest went tight in a way I decided not to examine.

The innkeeper was a broad woman named Petra with forearms like dock rope and the specific hospitality of someone who had decided, years ago, exactly how much of herself she was willing to sell. She took our cash without counting it, which I appreciated, and asked no questions, which I appreciated more, and then asked one anyway.

"Passing through?"

"Storm work," I said, which was even true. "We read weather. There's a client wants data off the high passes before the snow closes them."

"Early snow this year," Petra said. "Old people say the mountain's restless." She said it the way you'd mention a neighbor's bad hip—factual, faintly dismissive, and with the undertone that the old people were always saying something.

"Old people say a lot of things."

"In Halvett they've learned to say them quietly." She set down our stew—mutton, root vegetables, bread dense enough to use as a wheel chock—and her eyes went, just for a half second, to the window, where the last light was going off the top of that cut-stone wall. "Rooms are at the end of the hall. Don't prop the door. And if anyone asks, you're surveyors for the highway authority."

"Are we?"

"You are if you'd like to keep eating here."

I liked her immediately. I liked her the way you like a locked door that at least has a lock.

* * *

Tomas slept. I didn't, which was standard. I sat by the window of our room with the curtain open a hand's width and watched Halvett do its evening arithmetic—lamplight by lamplight, smoke by smoke—and I counted the things that didn't add up.

The town was built on ruins. Not near them, not beside them. *On* them. You could see it once you knew to look: the church sat on a foundation too big for it, its bell tower stitched onto an older tower like a coat sewn over a uniform. The low walls along the river were dressed stone below and rough brick above, the way you'd build if you'd been handed a city's bones and told to make a village out of them. And everywhere, in door lintels and bridge abutments and the step of the well, the same keyed masonry Tomas had named from the ridge.

Karstian. All of it. And nobody in Halvett, I'd have bet the truck, had said that word in forty years.

I'd grown up around forgetting. My mother used to say that erasure wasn't a single act, it was maintenance—somebody had to repaint that sign every few years, somebody had to correct the child who said the old name, somebody had to look slightly puzzled, every single time, for a generation. Forgetting was a job. Somebody in this town was still employed.

The chest sat under the tarp in the truck bed, and I caught myself thinking about it the way you think about a sleeping animal in the room with you. The ledger with its columns of names. The letter addressed to *the Dunmore line*, in handwriting that had made my hands go cold because I'd seen that handwriting before, or its ghost of it, on the flyleaf of a book my grandmother had left me and I had lost in a border crossing in a year I didn't talk about.

Down in the common room, a door opened and closed. Voices. I didn't move to look. I'd learned a long time ago that the safest thing to be in a town like this was furniture.

* * *

In the morning I went looking for diesel and found the town's memory instead.

The fuel situation was grim—Petra's pump worked but the tank was half water—so a boy of maybe fourteen directed me, with the elaborate boredom of his profession, to a yard behind the chandlery where a man named Oskar sold fuel, batteries, and, judging by the inventory, opinions. Oskar was seventy if he was a day, built like a fence post, and he watched me the whole time he filled my jerry cans, which I didn't mind. Being watched is only a problem when you have something to hide, and I had a chest full of it.

"Highway survey," I said, before he could ask.

"Of course." He screwed the cap down. "You'll want to look at the old road, then. Up the escarpment. Before your time they'd have shown you on a map."

"Before my time?"

"Maps change." He said it lightly, the way you'd say *weather changes*, and something in the lightness was so rehearsed, so worn smooth from use, that it stopped me like a hand on the chest.

"Oskar. Do you know what a double-headed eagle looks like?"

The pump handle stopped.

It was only a second—less. He finished the fill, capped the second can, and turned to me with a face that had been professionally arranged into mildness, the way a shopkeeper arranges fruit. "Can't say I do. You'll be wanting the road up the north side, if you're surveying. The south road's washed out." And he walked back into his chandlery and shut the door, and the door had a lock on it, and I understood that I had just watched a man spend a lifetime of discipline to avoid nodding.

I stood in his yard with two jerry cans and a hammering heart, and I thought: *they didn't forget. They were made to forget, and some of them are still doing the making.*

* * *

Tomas found me at the inn, white around the mouth, and pulled me into the back hall where the woodpile was.

"Freya. There's a woman asking after us."

"Rook?"

"No. Younger. Says she's a schoolteacher." He lowered his voice to almost nothing. "She came to Petra, asked for the weather surveyors, and then—Freya—she drew it. On the back of a receipt. The seal. The double eagle. Drew it from memory and slid it across the bar and asked Petra if anyone passing through had come in carrying one."

My stomach did something complicated. "And Petra?"

"Petra burned the receipt in the stove and told her she was a foolish girl and there was no such thing. And then Petra came and told me the woman's name is Mirela Voss, and her grandfather was the town archivist, before there was no archive, and that she's been asking after strangers with old papers for six years and everyone in town pretends she's simple." Tomas ran a hand through his hair. "And then Petra said, and I quote, 'if you're going to meet her, meet her at the mill ruin above the orchard, and don't be seen, because I've fed you and I'd rather not have to stop.'"

We looked at each other.

"She knows," I said. "Petra knows what we're carrying."

"Petra knows what *everyone's* carrying. That's the job, isn't it? That's what you said forgetting is." He almost smiled. "Maintenance."

* * *

The mill ruin above the orchard was Karstian too, of course—everything here was, the whole town a palimpsest written over and written over until the first hand was nearly invisible—but the mill had resisted better than most. Arched and vaulted, its wheel long gone, its channel still holding a thread of water that murmured through the floor like a conversation the room was tired of having.

Mirela Voss was waiting on the sill of the old sluice gate, and she was not what I'd expected, which was a furtive conspirator. She was maybe thirty, with ink on her fingers and a schoolteacher's posture, and she stood up when we came in the way people stood up in old photographs—formally, deliberately, like a gesture inherited from people who had reasons for it.

"You're the surveyors," she said. Her eyes went over Tomas and landed on me and stayed. "You're not surveyors."

"We read weather."

"For money."

"For money," I agreed.

"And before that?" She had the directness of someone who had been called foolish so many times that she'd stopped bothering with approach angles. "Before that. What were you?"

And there it was, the question I'd spent fifteen years making sure nobody could ask, let alone answer. I felt Tomas go still beside me.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"Sit down," she said. "Or don't. But I'm going to talk, because I've been talking to a town that doesn't listen for six years and I've gotten very efficient at it." She reached into her satchel and brought out a book—a real book, old, its cover cracked, wrapped in oilcloth—and set it on the sill between us like a card played face-up. "My grandfather was the archivist of Halvett. Not the *town* archivist—the *Karstian* archivist, for this district, one of nine. When the Annexation came, the orders said the records were to be surrendered for cataloguing. He surrendered forty crates of ledgers and kept this one, and eleven others, and he spent the rest of his life being a schoolteacher and burying his real work under a false floor."

She unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside was a ledger, and the moment I saw the binding my mouth went dry, because I knew that binding. I had a chest in a truck bed with its twin in it.

"He died in the spring," Mirela said. "And I've been reading what he left, and most of it is lists—names, holdings, shipments, the boring machinery of a country that existed—and some of it is this." She opened the ledger to a marked page. "Custodian registries. The families who kept the archive's keys, before the Annexation. Nine districts, nine lines. And look."

She turned the book toward me.

I looked. I had known what would be there—some part of me had known since the river, since the letter, since the handwriting on the flyleaf of my grandmother's lost book—and knowing it did nothing at all to blunt it. There in the column, in the same hand, in the same ink: *Dunmore. Custodial line, western district. Status: dispersed.*

"Dispersed," Mirela said softly. "Not extinguished. My grandfather underlined that word three times. He spent his last ten years writing letters to every dispersed line he could trace. Most never answered. I think most never got the letters." Her eyes came up to mine. "But something answered you, didn't it. Something found you. Because you looked at that page like a person looks at their own name, and I've watched enough faces to know the difference."

The water murmured in its channel. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Say I have something," I said carefully. "Say a river gave it to us, which is the truth, which sounds insane. What exactly are you offering?"

"Help. The real kind. My grandfather's notes include the routes—the passes, the stations, the vault approaches, everything the archive's custodians were meant to know if the country ever needed to be put back together. He drew maps that don't exist anywhere official. I can read the old script, the survey shorthand, all of it. You have a key—" her chin tipped toward the truck, and I realized she'd seen it, or guessed, or simply reasoned it the way I would have, "—and no map. I have a map and no key. This is not a complicated equation, Ms. Dunmore."

I hadn't given her the name. Tomas hadn't given her the name. The ledger had.

"And the price," Tomas said quietly. "There's always a price."

Mirela didn't flinch. I liked that about her, and I distrusted how much I liked it.

"I want one thing," she said. "When you find whatever it is you find—the vault, the archive, the proof—I want a single document brought back here. One. Anything. A page, a photograph, a shipping manifest, I don't care. Something with Karstia's name on it that was *in* the ground, that came *out* of the ground, that I can hold up in this town and say: this was real, and you all knew it was real, and you let them make you lie about it for forty years."

"That's not a price," I said. "That's a mission."

"It's my grandfather's mission. I'm just the executor of an estate." She wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth with quick, precise movements. "You should understand something about Halvett, since you'll be sleeping in it tonight. Nobody here forgot by accident. After the Annexation, the men who'd run the district were given a choice at a public meeting in the Grey Goose's back room: cooperate and keep your houses, or remember and lose them. Some remembered. Their names are in my grandfather's ledger under a different heading, and there's a reason the Karstian section of the churchyard has no stones. The rest chose to forget. Out loud, every day, on purpose, in front of their children. Forgetting was the price of the town surviving, and they paid it, and they've been paying it so long they've convinced themselves it was never a choice at all." She shouldered her satchel. "I'm not angry at them. I want to be clear about that. I just think a debt that big shouldn't get to be invisible. And I think you, of all people, understand invisible debts."

The word *debt* went through me like a probe finding a nerve. She couldn't have known about the Syndicate. She couldn't have. But the word landed anyway, in the exact place it was aimed.

"Think about it," Mirela said, moving toward the orchard door. "But think fast. Because I read weather too, in my way—grandfather's almanacs, the old records, forty years of them—and the snow this year is coming down the escarpment three weeks early. And I don't think you have as much time as you think you do."

She paused at the door, half in the light.

"One more thing, Ms. Dunmore. Yesterday a car came through town. Two men, very polite, very well dressed. They asked at the chandlery about surveyors. Oskar told them nothing, which cost him something—you could see it cost him. But they bought fuel, and they're parked at the north end of the valley, and they have a radio antenna up." She looked at me, and for the first time the schoolteacher's composure cracked, just a hairline, and underneath it was something scared and steady and very young. "They're not here for the chest. They're here to make sure nobody in this valley ever talks about it again. So whatever you decide—decide it tonight."

* * *

Tomas and I walked back down through the orchard in the last of the light, and the apples hung there, small and sour and unbothered, and I thought about how many towns in the world were Halvett, and how many of them I'd driven through without once looking at the walls.

"She's right about the time," Tomas said finally. "I've been watching the peaks. There's a cap on the north ridge that wasn't there yesterday."

"I know."

"And Freya—the men at the north end of the valley. If Rook's second team found the ford, they found the road. It's not a question of if."

"I know, Tomas."

We came out at the inn's back gate and I stopped, hand on the latch, and looked up at the escarpment where it went up dark against the last violet of the sky, and somewhere up there was a vault, and a dead woman's station, and a country that had been erased so thoroughly that its own survivors had to pretend to be orphans.

"Tomorrow at dawn," I said. "We take her map."

Tomas nodded. Then he frowned. "Freya—did you tell her your name?"

I hadn't. We went inside, and Petra met us in the hall with a face like a closed shutter, and said, very quietly, "The two men from the north end just called the Grey Goose asking for a room. They'll be here by ten." She set a key in my palm and folded my fingers over it. "Back stairs. My truck's behind the woodshed. I never learned to read, so I never wrote down that you were ever here—and I'd thank you to return the favor."

I was beginning to think Halvett's forgetting was the most expensive memory in the world.

And then, from the common room, warm and unhurried and unmistakable, came the sound of a woman laughing at the innkeeper's joke—a low, easy, professional laugh I had heard across three states and two border crossings, and hoped never to hear again.

Ilse Rook had arrived.
