## 8. Rook's Arithmetic

Ilse Rook had a rule about hotel rooms: never sleep in one twice, and never unpack more than the kettle. She was breaking the second half of the rule in Halvett's only establishment above a feed store, the Hotel Meridian, which had a meridian painted on its lobby floor in 1971 and had been letting it fade ever since. Her kettle sat on the dresser. Her boots sat beside it, laces threaded through the top eyelets so they'd pull on fast. Her pistol sat inside the kettle, which was not regulation but was tidy, and tidiness was the closest thing Ilse had to a religion.

She was doing arithmetic.

Not the kind with numbers, exactly—she'd been good at numbers once, in a previous life, in a government ministry that no longer existed under a name she no longer used. This was the other kind. The kind where you laid out everything you knew on the bed like laundry and tried to see what matched.

Item one: the Dunmore woman had a chest with the Karstian double-eagle on it. Item two: she hadn't sold it, hadn't photographed it for a quick payout, hadn't done any of the things a drowning woman does with treasure. She'd run. Item three: the Syndicate had known about the chest for—Rook checked her notes, though she didn't need to—thirty-one years. Item four: nobody spends thirty-one years and, by her rough estimate, four hundred thousand in operational costs chasing a box of heirlooms.

So it wasn't the chest. It was what the chest opened.

She sat on the bed with her back against the headboard and her notebook on her knees, and she did what she always did before a report: she told herself the truth first, so the lies she was about to tell would have somewhere solid to stand.

The truth was this. Ilse Rook collected debts. That was the job title, and for eleven years she'd worn it comfortably, the way you wear a coat that fits. Debts were clean. A man owed money, she found the man, the man paid or arranged to pay, and everyone went home with their teeth. She had rules about teeth. She had never once broken a bone she didn't have to, and she'd only ever had to twice, and both times she'd sent flowers, which the recipients had found, on reflection, more unsettling than the bones.

But this wasn't a debt. This was a hunt, and she'd known it was a hunt since Vane's office first handed her the Dunmore file with its thirty-year paper trail of dead ends. Debt collectors don't keep files that long. Debt collectors don't care if the debtor finds something. The Syndicate cared. The Syndicate had cared enough to have her watch the river country all spring, waiting for the floods to give something up, which meant the Syndicate had known the floods might give something up, which meant the Syndicate knew more about a dead nation's paperwork than any lending house had a right to.

And there was the arithmetic that actually mattered, the sum she'd been carrying around for three days like a stone in her boot: if the Syndicate wanted the chest this badly, then the chest was worth more than the debt. And if it was worth more than the debt, then the debt was never going to be the price. The debt was the hook. You don't pay a hook. You cut it out of your mouth, or you get reeled in.

Freya Dunmore, Rook had to admit, was cutting well. The woman drove like a mathematician and hid like someone who'd practiced, and she'd read the trap in the Hotel Meridian lobby faster than most. Rook had watched her face do it—the little flicker, the recalculation, the smile that got politer by exactly one degree. Rook respected that. You could learn a lot about a person from the degree of their polite.

The problem was that respect didn't pay, and neither, she was beginning to suspect, did this job. Not in the currency she'd been paid in all her life, which was the simple one: do the work, keep your hands clean enough, go home.

* * *

The call came at nine, which meant it came at nine, because her superior, a man called Ferenc Vane the younger—nephew, not son, and sensitive about it—believed punctuality was a form of dominance. Rook set her notebook face-down on the bed, took the phone off the dresser, and stood at the window while it rang. Halvett at night was four streetlamps and a lot of dark with mountains in it. Somewhere out there, the Dunmore woman's truck was doing what trucks do in Halvett after dark, which was nothing, convincingly.

"Report," said Ferenc Vane, in the tone of a man who had never once been curious, only thorough.

"They have the chest. They've opened it. The woman's grandmother is in the ledger—custodian class, which our own records confirm. They know what the chest is for now, or they're close. They went to ground in Halvett, made contact with a local, and moved on before my team could close. They're heading for the high passes."

A pause. Rook watched a moth batter itself against the streetlamp below, with what she felt was understandable commitment.

"The passes will snow," Vane said.

"Weeks early, yes. The models say they'll make it if they leave now and don't stop."

"Then you'll stop them."

"I can stop them," Rook said. "That's not the same as catching them, and it's not the same as the thing you actually want, which I'd like to discuss."

"Discuss," said Vane, the way other men said *proceed*, and Rook took a breath and laid it out, because she'd decided on the walk back from the feed store that she would say it once, plainly, and then never have to wonder.

"The archive under the Escarpment. I've read the old ministry fragments, the ones we bought in '09. It's not treasure down there. It's records. Treasury records. Correspondence. The engineering assessments on the border levees—the ones that failed the winter of the Annexation, that drowned the valley districts, that made the population manageable. If those documents are what the fragments say they are, then the Annexation wasn't a tragedy. It was a project. Somebody signed off on the levees, and somebody else paid for the signatures, and both sets of somebodies have grandchildren, and those grandchildren have names I recognize from the Syndicate's own client list."

She let that sit. Through the window, the moth gave up on the lamp and dropped into the dark.

"You're describing history," Vane said.

"I'm describing leverage," Rook said. "Theirs. The archive is only dangerous to us if we treat it as a thing to be seized, because a seized archive gets read. The moment we put hands on it, we've authenticated it. Every document we carry out is a document a tribunal can hold up. The safest thing the Syndicate can do with that vault is lose the address."

Silence, long enough that she checked the signal bar out of professional habit. Full bars. He was just thinking, or letting her think he was.

"Your assessment of the risk is noted," Vane said finally, and something in his voice had changed temperature, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere you can't see. "The Syndicate's position is unchanged. The chest and its contents are to be recovered intact. The debtors are to be—"

He stopped. Rook waited. She was good at waiting; it was most of her job.

"The debtors are to be handled," he said, "and the operation is to leave no witnesses. That includes the local contact in Halvett. And it includes, Ilse—" and here he used her first name, which he did perhaps twice a year, always before the part that cost, "—it includes the debtors themselves. The debt was the instrument. The debt is closed. Effective tonight, the Dunmore woman and her associate are not assets to be recovered. They are liabilities to be removed."

Rook looked at her boots by the kettle. She thought about the two bones she'd broken in eleven years, and the flowers.

"There's a girl in Halvett," she said. "Archivist's granddaughter. Nineteen, maybe twenty. She recognized the seal because her grandfather kept a photograph under his floorboards for fifty years. That's her entire crime. A photograph and a floorboard."

"Then she's had a long time to make peace with her family's choices," Vane said, "as we all have. No witnesses, Ilse. The order comes from above me, and I'll tell you what I tell you every year at the audit: the people above me are not sentimental, and they are not patient, and the tribunal convenes in the spring. Whatever is in that vault cannot be in a courtroom in the spring. Do you understand the shape of your instructions?"

"I understand the shape," Rook said.

"Good. And Ilse—if you find the arithmetic of this assignment disagreeable, you may of course resign. There's a form."

There was always a form. She'd seen the form. The form was how the Syndicate found out where you lived.

"I don't resign," Rook said. "I finish."

"I know," Vane said, and hung up, satisfied, which was the most dangerous thing about him: he was never wrong about her, only about what that meant.

Rook stood at the window a while longer. Below, the streetlamp buzzed and the moth was gone, and she thought: that's the whole instruction, isn't it. No witnesses. Not *recover the archive*, not *protect the clients*—no witnesses, because the clients had finally said the quiet part into a phone, and a phone was a witness too, in its way, and Ferenc Vane the younger had just made her the last one who'd heard it.

She took out her notebook and turned to a fresh page, because that was the ritual, that was the religion: write down the truth first, so the lies have somewhere to stand. She wrote: *Order received. No witnesses. Includes Dunmore, associate, local contact.* She looked at it for a long moment. Then, underneath, in the small tidy hand she used for things she did not intend to show anyone, she wrote a second line, which was not a report to anyone but herself:

*Item: a debt is a number. This is not a number.*

She closed the notebook, took the pistol out of the kettle, and began, methodically, to clean it—because whatever she decided to be tomorrow, tonight she was still Ilse Rook, and Ilse Rook kept her equipment in order. Outside, far to the north, over the black shoulder of the Escarpment, the first snow of the season was already falling on a pass that two debtors and a dead woman's brass machine were racing toward, and Rook found herself, for the first time in eleven years, hoping the weather would hold just long enough to complicate everything.

She noticed the hope. She filed it under *item*, and kept cleaning the gun.
