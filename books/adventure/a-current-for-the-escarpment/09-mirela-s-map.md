## 9. Mirela's Map

Tomas had learned long ago that the most dangerous people in the world were not the ones who wanted something from you. It was the ones who wanted something *for* you.

Mirela was that kind of dangerous.

She'd fed them breakfast—actual eggs, actual bread, actual coffee that hadn't come out of a tin—and now she sat across the kitchen table from them with her grandfather's atlas open between her hands like a priest about to read banns. The house smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender and, faintly, of the cellar below, where Tomas suspected half of Halvett's forbidden memory was stacked in crates marked FLOUR.

"The route is real," Mirela said. "My grandfather walked it eleven times. Smuggled people out during the Annexation, smuggled documents back in after. He called it the Shepherd's Stair." She tapped a pencil line on the map—a thin, confident stroke that climbed the Escarpment's eastern face through country no road surveyor had ever bothered to insult. "Three days up if the weather holds. Four if it doesn't. Five if you're idiots."

"Then plan for five," Freya said.

Tomas hid his smile in his coffee. That was Freya all over: she'd rather budget for her own stupidity than pretend it didn't exist. It was one of the hundred small things he liked about her, filed away in the part of himself he never mentioned out loud, because mentioning things out loud was how you got people killed or, worse, attached.

Mirela looked at Freya the way a chess player looks at an opponent who has just moved a pawn somewhere interesting. "You're not going to ask me why I'm helping you."

"I assumed you'd tell us when it cost us something," Freya said. "That's usually how it goes."

"Freya," Tomas said mildly.

"What? I'm being polite. I haven't checked the exits once since we sat down."

"That's true," Tomas told Mirela. "For her, this is basically a hug."

* * *

The map itself, when Mirela finally turned it around, was a marvel. Not printed—drawn, in a draftsman's hand so fine it might have been etched with a needle. The Shepherd's Stair wound up through a sequence of places that appeared on no modern chart: the Hollow Bridge, the Weeping Ledges, a shelf of stone called the Choir because, according to a marginal note in faded brown ink, *the wind sings here and it is rude to interrupt*. Water sources were marked with tiny blue crosses. Campsites were marked with even tinier tents, each one annotated with the year it had been used and, in one case, the phrase *good berries, bad company*.

"Your grandfather drew this?" Tomas asked.

"My grandfather *lived* this. He drew it for the next person who'd need it. He just didn't know who that would be." Mirela's fingers rested on the margin, light as a bird's. "He waited forty years for someone to carry it. He died waiting. So you'll forgive me if I'm not entirely casual about the handoff."

Freya said nothing, but Tomas watched her do the thing she did—the silent arithmetic, weighing every kindness against its eventual invoice. He'd seen her run that calculation against weather data, against fuel gauges, against the Vane Syndicate's interest rates. She ran it against people too, which was the sad part, and the sensible part, and the part of her he understood best without liking.

"So what's the price?" Freya asked.

And there it was. Mirela didn't flinch. She'd been waiting for the question the way you wait for thunder after lightning.

"One document," she said. "From the vault. Anything—a photograph, a page, a recording, a child's drawing, I don't care. Something that proves Karstia existed. Something I can hold." She folded her hands. "Bring it back down the mountain, and the map is yours. Free and clear. No debt, no interest, no Syndicate arithmetic."

The kitchen went quiet enough that Tomas could hear the clock on the mantel, ticking with the smugness of a clock that has never owed anyone anything.

"You want us to go back down," Freya said. "After. Through Rook's people. Through whatever's left of the passes."

"I want you to come back at all," Mirela said. "Most people who take my grandfather's map won't. I've made peace with that. But I'm asking anyway."

"Mirela—" Tomas started.

"No promises," Freya said.

It came out flat and fast, before Mirela had even finished, and Tomas felt it land in the room like a dropped wrench. Mirela's face didn't change, exactly. It just went still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"No promises," she repeated.

"I don't make promises I can't keep," Freya said. "I especially don't make them to people who feed me eggs. It's a bad combination. You get sentimental, you take risks you shouldn't, and then somebody dies holding a piece of paper they died for and I have to live with that too." She pushed back from the table. "We'll try. That's the honest version. We'll try, and trying might be worth nothing, and I'd rather tell you that now than dress it up in a promise and let you find out later."

"Mirela," Tomas said again, because someone had to be the weather balloon here, rising gently into the pressure zone before everybody exploded. "Could I see the rest of the map? There's a notation near the Choir I can't read."

It wasn't true. The notation was perfectly legible. But Mirela leaned over the map gratefully, grateful for the excuse, and Freya stood by the window with her arms crossed, looking out at Halvett's crooked rooftops as if the town had personally disappointed her, which, to be fair, towns generally had.

* * *

Later, while Mirela was in the cellar fetching a spare set of the maps—she kept copies, she said, because fire took the first set in '61 and she'd sworn never to let paper be the only witness again—Tomas found Freya on the back step, splitting a stick of kindling with her knife for no practical reason whatsoever.

"You could have said yes," he said.

"I could have said lots of things." The knife flashed. The stick surrendered. "Yes is cheap. Yes is what you say when you want everyone to stop looking at you."

"She's asking us to carry one piece of paper down a mountain. One. Out of thousands."

"And how many pieces of paper do you think we'll be carrying when Rook's people are behind us and the snow's ahead of us and every gram matters?" Freya didn't look at him. She looked at the mountains, where the morning haze had burned off to reveal the high passes wearing their new white caps like men who'd put on formalwear early for a funeral. "Every extra ounce is a decision. Every promise is weight. I've spent ten years learning to carry nothing I can't drop."

"You carried me."

The knife stopped mid-stroke.

"That was different," she said.

"How?"

"You were already there. I didn't choose you. You were just... ambient. Like weather." She resumed splitting, faster now. "You don't promise the weather you'll come back for it."

Tomas considered several responses to this, ranked them by likelihood of getting him stabbed, and selected the third-least dangerous one. "You know that's the loneliest sentence I've ever heard you say, and I once heard you describe your relationship with your truck as 'a professional arrangement.'"

That got him the look. The real one, the one with the crack in it, the one she showed maybe twice a year and always by accident. Then the cellar door banged open and Mirela came up the steps with an oilcloth bundle under each arm, and the look was gone, packed away, and Freya was Freya again: all business, all edges, a woman built like a storm shelter.

But Tomas had seen the crack. He filed it away with the other hundred small things.

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and it was the kind of afternoon Tomas would remember later, in the bad days ahead, as the last easy hours. Mirela taught them the route landmark by landmark, her finger tracing the Shepherd's Stair while her voice supplied what ink couldn't: which scree slopes sang before they slid, which stream crossings lied about their depth in spring melt, which stretch of the Weeping Ledges could be crossed only before ten in the morning, when frost still held the rock together like a nervous chaperone.

"Here." She stopped at a blank space on the map, a day's march below the summit, where her grandfather had written nothing at all. "This is the part he wouldn't draw."

"Why?" Freya asked.

"Because it changes. A crevasse field under the last headwall. He used to say you couldn't map a thing that rearranges itself out of spite." She glanced up. "He also said that anyone crossing it should apologize to the mountain first. He was a very superstitious man for someone who claimed to believe in nothing but paper."

"I like him already," Tomas said. "Dead men with opinions are the best company."

By dusk the truck was repacked, the fuel cans lashed, the orrery wrapped in three layers of wool and riding in the cab between them like a sleeping relative nobody wanted to wake. The ledger stayed in Tomas's pack, against his spine, where he'd been keeping it since the col—though neither Freya nor Mirela needed to know exactly how much comfort he took from that.

At the door, Mirela pressed the oilcloth bundle into Tomas's hands. Both copies. Her grandfather's original and the spare.

"Both?" Tomas said. "If we lose one—"

"If you lose one, you lose both, because you'll be dead, and then it hardly matters." She said it cheerfully, the way border people say terrible things, and then her voice dropped and lost its cheer entirely. "Tomas. The document. I know she won't promise. I watched her not promise. It was very impressive—not promising is clearly a skill she's practiced." She held his eyes. "But you'll bring something back. Won't you."

And there it was. The other kind of dangerous. The kind that didn't threaten you, didn't invoice you, just quietly handed you their whole heart and stepped back and trusted you not to drop it.

Tomas thought about lying. It would have been kinder, probably. It would certainly have been easier. But he'd spent the whole day watching Freya refuse to dress things up, and annoyingly, horribly, some of it had rubbed off.

"I'll try," he said. "Same as her. Trying might be worth nothing."

Mirela nodded slowly. "Your grandfather would have liked you," she said, and then frowned at herself. "My grandfather. Sorry. I've been talking to his map so long I've started introducing people to him."

"He sounds like he'd have enjoyed that," Tomas said.

* * *

They rolled out of Halvett at first light, the truck loaded, the map secured in a waterproof sleeve inside Tomas's jacket, directly over the ledger, so that he was essentially a filing cabinet with legs. Freya drove. The town shrank in the mirror—crooked roofs, lavender smoke, one girl standing at the edge of the road with her hand raised, not waving exactly, just raised, like a flag of a country that technically no longer existed.

Freya watched her in the mirror for a long time.

"You think I was wrong," she said finally. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't accusatory. It was just Freya, doing inventory.

"I think you were honest," Tomas said. "I think honesty and wrongness are cousins who see each other at holidays."

"Hm." The tires hummed. The Escarpment grew in the windshield, white-capped and indifferent. "When I was a kid," she said, apparently to the road, "my mother told me that a promise is a debt you volunteer for. And look what volunteering did to this family." Her knuckles whitened on the wheel, then eased. "But I'll tell you what I *will* promise, since you're sitting there radiating disapproval like a small warm stove."

"I would never radiate."

"You're radiating right now. I promise you this: if there's a document in that vault that proves Karstia existed, and I am physically capable of carrying it, I will not leave it behind. Not for Mirela. Not for her grandfather. For the woman in Station Kestrel's logs, whoever she turns out to be—for everyone who recorded data nobody read." She exhaled. "That's not a promise to come back. Coming back depends on things I can't control. But leaving something behind when I could carry it—that's a choice. And I'm telling you now what I'll choose."

Tomas looked at her profile against the growing mountains—the jaw set, the eyes doing that storm-reading squint she didn't even know she did anymore—and felt the familiar ache that lived somewhere between admiration and worry, the ache that was, as far as he could tell, simply what it cost to love Freya Dunmore.

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said," he said, "and it was about archival retrieval."

"Don't ruin it."

"Too late. I'm putting it on a plaque."

Ahead, the road began to climb, and the first switchback revealed what the haze had hidden: fresh tracks in the new snow on the far ridge. Vehicle tracks. Two vehicles, moving fast, heading for the same passes they were.

Freya saw them at the same moment he did. The truck slowed. Neither of them spoke.

"Well," Tomas said quietly. "Looks like we're not the only ones who got a map."

"We got ours free," Freya said, and put the truck into gear, and the Shepherd's Stair rose above them like a question the mountain had been waiting forty years to ask.
