## 10. First Snowline

The mountain told us before the models did.

I'd been watching the Escarpment through the windshield for two hours, one elbow out the window of the truck, the other hand holding coffee that had gone the temperature of regret, and I'd seen it first as a wrongness in the light. The peaks had gone from blue to white at the edges, the way a bruise announces itself before the pain. By the time we stopped at the fuel depot in Kessel's Bend, the satellite feed on my laptop had caught up, and the model output was unambiguous in the way model output only is when it's delivering bad news.

"Six days," I said.

Tomas leaned over my shoulder, reading the pressure charts upside down, which was his habit and my irritation. "Six days until what, exactly? Say it plainly. I find the weather-speak makes it worse."

"Six days until the snowline drops below the pass and stays there. After that, every route over the Escarpment closes for the season. Not 'gets hard.' Closes." I turned the laptop so he could see the snowfall accumulation bands, the pretty gradient of white marching down the elevation contours like an invading army with excellent cartographers. "Early snow. Three weeks ahead of the median. Somebody up there has a grudge."

"Somebody up there has always had a grudge. You just usually find it profitable." He straightened, rubbed the back of his neck. "Six days to climb a mountain that sensible people take two weeks to climb. With a truck. With our truck, which is held together by prayer and the specific kind of duct tape you steal from gas stations."

"I don't steal it. I liberate it. There's a moral distinction."

"Is there."

"There's a moral distinction," I confirmed, and closed the laptop before he could ask the next question, which would have been *and what happens if we don't make it in six days*, and which I did not have an answer for that wasn't *we winter in a border town full of people who've forgotten how to remember things, waiting for Ilse Rook to find us*, and I wasn't ready to say that out loud. Saying things out loud makes them real. I'd built a whole life on that principle.

The fuel depot man came out to top off our tanks, a broad, sunburnt fellow who squinted at the Escarpment the way farmers squint at livestock they don't trust. "Early for that," he said, nodding at the white. "My grandfather used to say when the mountain puts its hat on before the equinox, it's telling you something."

"Mountains talk to your grandfather?"

"Everything talked to my grandfather. Mostly complaints." He finished the tank, wiped his hands on a rag older than both of us. "You two headed up?"

"Passing through," Tomas said, smooth as river water, and I loved him a little for it. We were not passing through. We were fleeing a syndicate enforcer with excellent boots and worse intentions, carrying a brass orrery that behaved like a compass with opinions and a ledger of dead people that included my grandmother. But *passing through* was the dialect of the road, and Tomas spoke it fluently.

We drove north. The Escarpment grew.

*

I want to explain something about mountains that storm chasers understand and other people don't, because it matters to what happened next.

A mountain is weather's favorite instrument. It takes the smooth, indifferent movement of air across a landscape and forces it to make decisions — up or around, over or through — and every one of those decisions produces drama. Lenticular clouds stack on the windward faces like plates. Wind shear gets organized into something you could set a watch by, if your watch were made of terror. The first time I'd chased a storm along the foothills of the Escarpment, years ago, in the brief season when I still chased storms for science instead of for debt payments, I'd watched a supercell wrap itself around a peak like it was trying to buy the mountain a drink, and I'd understood, in a way that never left me, that the high country doesn't have weather. It has *intent*.

By the third day of driving, the mountain's intent had a color, and the color was white.

We'd left the lowlands behind in stages — the river country with its drowned fields and its willows still holding last spring's flood debris like old gossip, then the hill country where the farms got stonier and the dogs got louder, then the pine country where the road narrowed and the radio dissolved into static. The truck complained the whole way. Her name is Bess, and she is a research rig built from the salvaged skeleton of a storm-intercept vehicle, and she has half a sensor array since the lightning took the other half, and she drinks fuel like a syndicate enforcer drinks other people's patience. But she climbs. I'll give her that. She climbs the way I do — badly, stubbornly, with a lot of noise.

We camped that third night in a saddle below the tree line, and I got out the models again because I couldn't sleep, which was becoming a professional condition.

The six days had become five. The snow bands had moved down eight hundred meters in forty-eight hours. I ran the numbers three times, using different initialization data, hoping one of the runs would be kind. Models are like people that way — you ask them the same question enough times and eventually one of them tells you what you want to hear, and you have to be disciplined enough to know that the kind one is lying.

"Freya." Tomas's voice from the tent. "Come to bed or come to a decision, but stop doing that thing with the keyboard. It sounds like rain on a tin roof and it's making me anxious."

"The snowline's dropped again."

A pause. The zipper, and then his head emerging into the cold, hair flattened on one side, face creased from the sleeping bag. Even half-awake, he had the particular patience of a man who has spent years watching a woman argue with atmosphere. "How much?"

"Eight hundred meters in two days."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the mountain's not just getting snow early. The pattern's locked. Cold air pooling on the plateau, moisture feeding in from the northwest — it's a machine now, and machines like this don't stall, they *build*." I looked up. Above us, the peaks had put their hats on, all the way down to the rock, and the stars above them had that hard, polished look they get when the air between is dry and cold and honest. "Five days, Tomas. Maybe four, if the moisture feed strengthens, and it's strengthening."

He sat down next to me on the tailgate. For a while neither of us said anything, which was its own kind of conversation. We'd gotten good at those. Years of partnership will do that — you develop a whole language out of silences, and the silences get more articulate the worse things get.

"Rook's behind us," he said finally. "A day, maybe two."

"A day. She doesn't waste time."

"And ahead of us is a mountain that's closing like a shop." He blew out a breath that hung in the air like a small ghost. "So we're in the middle of a closing door."

"That's about the shape of it, yes."

"And your plan is?"

"My plan," I said, "is to be on the far side before it shuts."

He looked at me. In the starlight his face was mostly shadow and one raised eyebrow, and that eyebrow said everything a paragraph could have. *You and your plans. Your plans have a survival rate, Freya. What is it. Be honest.*

"It's a good plan," I said.

The eyebrow remained.

"It's the *only* plan," I amended, "which in my experience is the strongest kind."

That got a laugh out of him, short and reluctant, breath-smoke in the cold. He bumped his shoulder against mine, which in Tomas was a full-throated declaration of loyalty, and went back to the tent, and I sat a while longer watching the white line on the mountain and thinking about doors.

Here is the thing about the middle of a closing door. Everyone thinks the danger is the door. The door is not the danger. The danger is what's chasing you through it, and what you become when you realize there's no way back — because a person with a retreat is a person making choices, and a person without one is a person making a *commitment*, and those are very different animals.

I didn't know yet how different. I was about to find out.

* * *

The bridge was gone on the morning of the fourth day.

We came around the shoulder of a ridge with the Kestrel River — the same river, I couldn't help thinking, that had given us the chest, though it was a different creature up here, fast and green-white and furious — and the road bent down toward the crossing at a place the map called Vasser's Ford, and the map was wrong, because Vasser's Ford was now Vasser's Absence.

The old timber-and-truss bridge, the one Mirela's grandfather's route had counted on, the one that had stood there through every flood the lowlands could throw at it, was a black smear of char on both banks and a scatter of burned pilings in the water. The fire was fresh. I could smell it from two hundred meters out — diesel and pitch smoke, sharp as a reprimand.

Tomas brought the truck to a stop and we both just sat there for a moment, looking at it.

"They burned it," he said. Not a question. He had that flat tone he got when he was keeping his voice away from his temper, like a man carrying something hot with both hands.

"Rook's second team." I got the binoculars and swept the far bank, the treeline, the switchbacks above. Empty, but empty in the way that costs money to arrange. "It's her style. She doesn't chase. She *herds*. Cut the retreat, narrow the options, keep us moving in the direction she wants." I lowered the binoculars. "She's not trying to catch us on this side of the mountain. She's trying to make sure we can only come out one way. Her way."

"Which means she knows the routes."

"She knows the routes." I said it evenly, but something cold moved through me when I said it, because there was only one way Rook knew the routes that well, and that was if the Syndicate had been studying the Escarpment crossings for a long time. Decades. As long as they'd been hunting whatever the chest had named us heirs to. The mountain wasn't just a mountain. It was a board, and somebody had set it up a long time ago, and we were the pieces that had learned to read.

Tomas got out and walked down to the bank, and I watched him crouch there, studying the water, the burned stumps, the far side. He came back with river mud on his knees and an expression I recognized. It was the same expression he'd worn pulling the chest out of the willow roots, back when this whole thing started — the look of a man whose conscience has overruled his survival instinct and is prepared to live with the paperwork.

"There's a ford," he said. "Downstream, maybe a kilometer, where the river braids out over gravel. It's deep in the channels but there's a line through. Bess could make it."

"Bess could *drown*."

"Bess could make it," he repeated, with the mild immovability of a man who has already decided and is merely informing me. "Or we go back, and we spend the winter in Kessel's Bend, and Rook finds us in a town with one road out. Your call. It's always your call."

I looked at the burned bridge. I looked at the mountain, wearing its snow like a deadline. I looked at the river, running cold and fast with the melt off the early storms, carrying pieces of winter down from the heights to show us what was coming.

The mountain was the enemy. I'd been telling myself that for days. But standing there at the burned crossing, with Rook behind us and the snowline dropping above us, I felt the other truth of it, the one chasers know: the mountain was also the only thing on this earth that didn't care about the Vane Syndicate. It would bury their bridge-burners same as it would bury us. It had no alliances. It had weather, and weather, for all its cruelty, is at least honest. Rook's smile was less honest than a whiteout. I'd take the mountain's cruelty over the Syndicate's kindness any day of the week.

"The ford," I said. "But I drive. Your fording lines are optimistic. You navigate like a man who trusts rivers, and rivers have never once deserved it."

"Everyone needs a faith," Tomas said, and climbed in.

* * *

The crossing took forty minutes and cost me ten years.

I won't describe all of it. There are things a person does with a truck in a braided mountain river that are between her and the river and God, and I have a professional reputation to think of. I'll say this much: the water came over the hood twice, the second channel tried its level best to swing our tail downstream into a hole you could have drowned a cathedral in, and at the worst of it — Bess slewed sideways, the current shoving, Tomas out of his door up to his chest holding a tow strap to a boulder because he is either the bravest man I know or has never once done the math — at the worst of it, I heard myself laughing.

Actually laughing. In the water. With the orrery wrapped in oilcloth and strapped down behind my seat, with the ledger in its waterproof case against my ribs, with the snowline four days from sealing us in or out.

Tomas hauled the strap taut, and Bess found her feet, and we came up the far bank in a spray of gravel and river, and he stood there dripping, hair plastered to his skull, looking at me like I'd lost my mind.

"You're laughing."

"I'm laughing."

"The truck nearly drowned. The *orrery* nearly drowned. You're laughing."

I checked the strap on the oilcloth bundle. Dry. I checked the ledger case. Dry. And the laugh was still in me, bubbling up from somewhere I didn't have a map for, and I thought: this is what it's like, isn't it. This is what people feel like when they've stopped being able to go back. It's not despair. It's not courage, either, whatever the books say. It's lighter than both. It's the strange, giddy lightness of a woman who has been running for so long that the running has finally become the whole world, and there's nothing left behind her to be afraid of losing, because the bridge is burned, and she didn't even burn it.

"Adrenaline," I told him. "It's a documented phenomenon."

"It's a *worrying* phenomenon." But he was almost smiling as he wrung out his shirt, and when we loaded back in and started up the switchbacks into the pine country, climbing into the white, there was something in the cab that hadn't been there in the lowlands. I want to call it hope, but it wasn't quite. It was more like *direction*. The difference between fleeing and going, which is the whole difference, maybe, between a life and a run.

The snow started at four thousand meters. Fat, slow flakes at first, the kind that fall when the air is too cold to be in a hurry, and then thicker, and then the world beyond the windshield went soft and white and the wipers began their hopeless metronome.

"Four days," I said.

"Three and a half, the way it's coming down." Tomas had the map out, Mirela's grandfather's route marked in faded pencil, the smuggler's line over the Escarpment that the official maps had never admitted to. "There's a station on this route. Old one. Marked here —" his finger tapped a small symbol — "Kestrel something. Abandoned since the Annexation, if it's even standing. We could shelter there if the storm catches us."

"Stations like that are standing longer than towns. Nobody's around to tear them down." I said it lightly. Something about the mark on the map itched at me — the orrery had been twitching in its case for two days, its needle swinging whenever we hit altitude, and I hadn't told Tomas yet, because I hadn't figured out yet what it was pointing at, and I don't share my instruments until they've learned some manners.

Above us, through a tear in the cloud, the summit showed for maybe ten seconds: white to the rock, wind pluming off the crest like smoke from a signal fire. The mountain, telling anyone who could read it exactly what it intended.

I could read it. I'd spent my whole life learning to read skies that wanted to kill me, and here was the largest one yet, and for the first time I could remember, I wasn't reading it to get away.

I was reading it to get through.

"Bess," I said, patting the dash, "up we go."

The snow thickened. The road climbed. And somewhere behind us, down in the white noise of the storm, I knew Ilse Rook was watching our taillights and smiling, and somewhere ahead — off-route, off-map, at a dead station whose name was a bird that used to mean something — the orrery's needle swung in its case like a finger, pointing.

Pointing up. Pointing on.

The door was closing. We were going through it.
