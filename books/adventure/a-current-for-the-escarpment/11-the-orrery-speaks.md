## 11. The Orrery Speaks

The truck died at 3,400 meters, halfway up a switchback that Mirela's map had promised was a road and the mountain had since repossessed.

Tomas felt it before he heard it—the engine's note flattening, losing its argument with the thin air, and then a rattle like dice in a tin cup, and then nothing but wind and the tick of cooling metal. He sat with his hands on the wheel for a moment, as if the situation might be negotiable if he just held on long enough.

"Fuel pump," Freya said from the passenger seat. She said it the way other people said *it's raining*. "Or it wants to be. Give it a minute."

He gave it a minute. Then another. The mountain gave them nothing back except more wind and a view of the valley they'd climbed out of, which looked, from this height, like something spilled.

"Well," Tomas said. "I've had worse Tuesdays."

"It's Wednesday."

"That explains it."

They got out. The cold up here was a different animal than the valley cold—not just colder but *hungrier*, the kind that found the gap between your collar and your neck within seconds and moved in without paying rent. Tomas shrugged his pack higher and watched Freya circle the truck once, her breath streaming behind her like a scarf she'd knitted out of worry.

She'd been quiet for two days. Not the good kind of quiet, either—not the focused silence she wore when she was working a storm, reading data the way he read contour lines—but the other kind, the one that came after Station Kestrel's logs. He'd caught her reading them again last night by headlamp, the dead scientist's careful handwriting, page after page of measurements taken for no one. *Barometric pressure, 14 March. No one will ever read this. Barometric pressure, 15 March.*

Tomas had pretended to sleep. It was becoming a specialty.

"Walk it or fix it?" he asked.

"Fix it first. Walking gets worse before it gets better." She popped the hood and disappeared into the engine's shadow, and he heard her muttering to the truck in the low, affectionate scolding voice she saved for machines and never for people. He'd noticed that years ago. He'd decided, around the same time, not to take it personally.

While she worked, he unloaded the chest.

It rode in the truck bed now, wrapped in a tarp and strapped down like cargo that might make a break for it—which, given everything, wasn't an unreasonable fear. He'd carried it up three switchbacks on his back when the ford nearly took it, and his shoulders still filed complaints every morning. But there was something about having it close that he'd stopped questioning. Like a dog that follows you room to room. You stop asking why. You just leave the door open.

He set it on a flat rock and unwrapped it, mostly to check for water damage, mostly because his hands wanted something to do.

The orrery sat inside where it always sat, nested in wool gone gray with age. Brass rings within brass rings, a small sun at the center, planets frozen mid-orbit—and at its heart, that needle. They'd assumed it was decorative. A compass-shaped flourish on a machine built to model heavens that no longer existed, in a country that no longer existed, made by hands that were almost certainly no longer anything at all.

"Freya," he called. "Come look at this."

"If it's the needle, I already know it points north."

"It doesn't."

That got her out from under the hood. She came over wiping her hands on a rag, leaving grease stripes down her thigh like war paint applied by someone in a hurry, and leaned over the orrery.

The needle pointed east-southeast. Firmly. Unambiguously. Toward a wall of rock and ice that contained, as far as Tomas knew, absolutely nothing.

"Maybe your compass is broken," Freya said, but her voice had changed. He knew that change. It was the sound of a hypothesis being born.

"My compass is fine." He pulled it out and held both side by side. His needle swung north with the bored confidence of an instrument that had done this ten thousand times. The orrery's needle ignored it completely, holding its line toward the rock wall like a man pointing at something only he could see.

Freya crouched. She didn't touch it at first—she did that thing she did, hovering her palm near an instrument without contacting it, as if machines could feel attention the way cats could. Then she turned the outermost ring slowly, and the whole assembly responded: gears Tomas hadn't known existed engaged with a whisper of resistance, and the inner rings rotated against each other, planets sliding along their tracks, until the mechanism settled into a new configuration with a click he felt in his teeth.

The needle didn't move.

"Huh," Freya said.

"'Huh' is not reassuring."

"'Huh' is the most honest word in science." She was already digging in her pack. She came out with the magnetometer—one of the few sensors that had survived the lightning strike back on the flats, scorched along one edge but functional, a survivor like everything else they owned. She walked a slow arc around the orrery, watching the readout.

"Tomas. What's our elevation?"

"Thirty-four hundred. Why?"

"And what's the local magnetic variation up here?"

"I don't—I don't know, some number, why?"

"Because the needle isn't pointing at north." She looked up at him, and despite the exhaustion carved into her face, despite the bruise still yellowing along her ribs from the col, her eyes had lit in a way he hadn't seen since before Halvett. Since before Rook. Since maybe before the river. "It's pointing at an anomaly."

* * *

They spent an hour proving it, because Freya did not believe things; she *verified* them, the way other people ate—regularly and with total commitment.

She carried the magnetometer in widening circles around their campsite, calling out readings while Tomas logged them on paper because the laptop battery was a resource now, budgeted like ammunition. The numbers told a story: a smooth gradient across the slope, and then—a hundred meters east-southeast, toward the rock wall—a spike. Small. Localized. The kind of signature a large mass of ferrous material makes when it's buried, or sheltered, or otherwise hiding.

"There's iron up there," Freya said. "A lot of it. Under that ridge."

"A meteorite?"

"Meteorites don't come with buildings." She pointed, and he followed her finger to where the ridge broke against the sky—and saw, for the first time, what the flat light had hidden. A structure. Low, angular, half-swallowed by scree and fifty years of weather. An antenna mast, snapped, leaning against the slope like a drunk against a lamppost. A roofline.

"A station," Tomas said slowly.

"A dead one." Freya turned back to the orrery, and her expression did something complicated. "Mirela's map ends at the summit route. The vault is supposed to be under the summit. That's what the ledger says, that's what everything says." She tapped the brass ring. "But this thing isn't pointing at the summit. It's pointing *off-route*. At a ruin nobody's map mentions."

"So the chest is lost?"

"No." She said it with sudden certainty, and it startled him. "No, look—" She pulled the ledger from the chest, flipped to the pages she'd marked, the custodians' names in columns going back generations. Dunmore-Karsti appearing like a heartbeat in the rhythm of strangers. "Every custodian signed here. Every one. And look at the marginalia—coordinates, partial ones, corrections. This wasn't just a registry. It was a *survey log*. Whoever carried this chest was mapping something." She looked up. "The orrery isn't a model of the sky, Tomas. Or it's not *only* that. It's a survey instrument. The needle finds magnetic anomalies because the vault was built inside one—the Karstians hid it inside a geological feature so strong it would mask any metal detector. And this—" she touched the needle, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, "—this is tuned to find its siblings."

Tomas looked at the needle. Still east-southeast. Still patient.

"You're saying the chest wants us to go there."

"I'm saying the chest was *designed* so that anyone carrying it could find the rest of the network." She laughed—a short, disbelieving sound with no humor in it, only wonder wearing humor's coat. "Karstia didn't just hide an archive. They hid a *system*. Stations. Waypoints. And they gave the key legs." She closed the ledger. "The chest doesn't want to be found, Tomas. It wants to find."

* * *

They argued about it, because arguing was how they prayed.

"We have six days," Tomas said, pacing the length of the campsite, which took four strides. "Six days before the snowline drops and every pass becomes a coffin with scenery. The summit route is there—" he pointed south, where the col waited, "—and that thing—" he pointed east, at the ruin, "—is a detour into a building that has been dead since before I was born."

"The summit route may be watched."

"May be."

"Rook knows the ledger. Rook knows the vault is under the summit. Where would you wait, if you were her?"

He hated that she was right. He hated it in the specific, familiar way of a man who had spent years losing arguments to someone who was usually right. "And if we go poke around a ruin and burn a day doing it, and the snow comes early—"

"Then we die on a mountain instead of in a ditch, and honestly, Tomas, I've priced both options." But she said it tiredly, without the usual blade in it, and he saw that underneath the calculation she was afraid. Not of the mountain. Of the answer the ruin might hold—or the question it would force.

He thought about Station Kestrel's logs. About the scientist who had stayed. About Freya reading those pages by headlamp with her face doing something he'd never seen it do, and saying nothing, and him pretending to sleep, and both of them carrying it alone because that was the arrangement, that was the treaty, that was how they survived.

"Tomas?" she said.

He realized he'd been quiet too long. "I'm weighing it."

"You've been weighing it for a full minute. I can hear the scales."

"One more thing on the scale, then." He crouched by the chest. "You said the custodians mapped the network. Your grandmother's name is in that book. Which means somewhere in that ruin—or whatever's under it—there might be more than instruments. There might be *her*. Her handwriting. Her route. What she knew." He met Freya's eyes. "You've been running from this name your whole life. Here's a building full of it. Are you really going to walk past?"

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Wind pulled at her hair. Somewhere above them, ice cracked in the sunlight, a sound like a knuckle popping in a cathedral.

"When did you get wise?" she said finally.

"About the same time you got stubborn. It was a difficult year for everyone."

* * *

They left the truck with the hood up and a wheel chocked wrong, the universal language of *abandoned, nothing here, move along*, and climbed.

The ruin resolved as they approached: a weather station, older than Kestrel, built into the ridge's lee side so the mountain itself shielded it. The door had been steel once. It hung open now, warped, exhaling a smell of cold iron and old paper and something faintly organic—mold, or memory.

Inside: desks overturned. Instruments rusted to sculptures. And on the walls, everywhere, pinned and taped and nailed, *maps*.

Dozens of them. Hand-drawn. The Escarpment rendered in obsessive detail—every pass, every chute, every glacier tongue—and overlaid with symbols Tomas didn't recognize until Freya made a sound beside him and he understood she did.

"Survey markers," she whispered. "The whole network. There are *stations* all through these mountains. Kestrel. This place. More." Her hand hovered over a symbol inked near the summit. "And there. The vault."

The orrery, in his arms, shifted its needle.

Not much. A degree, maybe two. But enough. Enough that Freya saw it, and went very still, and then crossed the ruined room to the far wall where a final map hung alone, larger than the others, drawn in a steadier hand. In its corner, in faded ink, a signature:

*E. Dunmore-Karsti.*

His partner stood in front of her grandmother's handwriting for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough as gravel.

"She drew the route off this ridge. Northeast. Through the dead station network." Freya traced the line with one finger, not touching the paper. "It's longer. Harder. But it never crosses the col." She turned to him, and the exhaustion on her face had transmuted into something else entirely—something bright and terrified and alive. "Rook is waiting at the col for us to come to the vault. My grandmother just gave us another way in."

Behind them, in the doorway, the wind changed direction—and brought with it, faint and wrong, the smell of woodsmoke.

Someone was on the mountain below.
