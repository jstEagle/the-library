## 12. Station Kestrel

The needle had been pointing them uphill for two days, and Freya had stopped pretending she didn't believe it.

That was the strange part—not the orrery itself, which she'd made her peace with somewhere around the second switchback, but the fact that she believed it. Freya Dunmore did not believe in instruments she hadn't calibrated herself. She didn't believe in dead women's letters, sealed nations, or brass spheres that swung toward things they had no business sensing. She believed in barometric pressure and the smell of hail before it fell and the specific way Tomas said *maybe* when he meant *no*.

And yet here she was, following a needle.

"It's not a needle," Tomas said from behind her, reading her silence the way he always did. "It's a magnetometer with delusions of theater."

"I know what it is."

"You were staring at it like it was going to apologize."

She huffed a laugh that cost more breath than she wanted to spend. The air up here had thinned into something you had to negotiate with. Below them the valley had folded away into haze, and above—above was the thing that kept her climbing: a ridgeline black against a sky the color of old pewter, and on the ridgeline, just where the orrery insisted, a shape.

A tower. A stubby, wind-scoured tower with a guy-wire mast leaning off it like a broken umbrella rib.

"Station Kestrel," she said, because saying it out loud made it real, and real things could be dealt with.

"That's what the logs called it." Tomas caught up beside her, breathing in careful, rationed pulls. He'd been doing that since the col—the fight, the chute, the whole ugly arithmetic of losing the orrery for six hours and getting it back crusted in another man's blood. He didn't talk about it. He just breathed like a man who'd decided to keep doing it on purpose. "The scientist. Vessna. She logged from here for eleven years."

"Eleven years at four thousand meters."

"Somebody has to be the person who stays."

Freya looked at him. He was watching the tower, not her, and his face had that settled quality it got when he'd already decided something and was waiting for the world to catch up.

They reached the station as the light went long and copper. It was smaller than it had looked from below—a single concrete pod half-buried against the ridge, the tower beside it, everything furred with lichen the color of dried blood. The door was steel, painted once, and it opened on the third shoulder-shove with a groan that echoed off the rocks like something waking up annoyed.

Inside: dust, dark, and cold that had been waiting patiently for years, keeping the room like a held note.

Then Tomas found the breaker panel, and then the solar array on the south slope—tilted, snow-crusted, absurdly still connected—and then, with a crackle and a stutter, one overhead lamp came on, buzzing like a trapped fly, and Station Kestrel lit up around them.

"Oh," Tomas said softly.

*Oh* was right.

The room was a time capsule with a workbench. Charts pinned to walls, curling at the edges. A cot with a blanket folded so precisely it looked like an argument for order in an unordered world. Jars of pencils. A kettle. And everywhere—every flat surface, every shelf, every drawer they pulled—paper. Observation logs in a small, disciplined hand. Barographs. A wall calendar from a year that no longer officially existed, its months named in Karstian, each day marked with tiny weather glyphs: sun, cloud, the little spiral that meant wind, the asterisk that meant snow.

"She stayed," Freya said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. Like someone else's.

"Through all of it," Tomas said. He'd found the logbooks themselves now, a run of them on the shelf, spines numbered in sequence. Volume after volume after volume. Eleven years of weather recorded by a woman alone on a mountain while her country was deleted beneath her.

Freya picked up the last book. Its cover was soft with handling. She opened it to the final entry and read:

*October 14. Wind NW 40 gusting 65. Clear. Visibility unlimited. Recorded all standard observations. No relief arrived. No transmission acknowledged. I have begun recording for the archive rather than the network. The distinction, I find, changes nothing about how carefully one measures.*

Below that, in the same hand, slightly larger, as if the writer had needed to see it clearly herself:

*Someone will read this. Data wants a reader. That is its nature. Until then I will be its keeper, and being a keeper is also a kind of work.*

Freya set the book down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Tomas."

"Yeah."

"Read the last entry."

He read it. She watched his face do what faces do—first the confusion, then the understanding, then the thing after understanding, which was worse. He closed the book gently and looked at her over the top of it.

"Freya."

"Don't."

"You haven't slept properly in—"

"*Don't.*"

But the room wouldn't let it go. That was the trouble with rooms like this one; they were built by people who paid attention, and attention soaked into the walls. Everywhere she looked there was evidence of a mind refusing to stop working: corrections in margins, a chart where someone had extrapolated a storm track years past any reason to care, a tin cup holding a single pencil sharpened down to a stub, saved anyway. Saved anyway. Eleven years of saving anyway.

Freya sat down on the cot because her legs informed her they were done taking requests, and the blanket was right there, and the blanket smelled faintly of cedar and decades.

Tomas put the kettle on. Of course he did. There was a spirit stove and a dented pot and water from a cistern that still, impossibly, worked, and within ten minutes the room smelled of boiled snow and the particular comfort of a man who processed grief through beverages.

"She was my age," Freya said. "Look at the first log. Look at the dates. She took this posting at twenty-six."

Tomas handed her a tin mug. "You don't know that."

"The handwriting does that thing mine does. Slanting worse when the pressure drops." She turned the first volume toward him, open to the earliest page. "Vessna Aran. Twenty-six, newly certified, *grateful for the assignment and the quiet.*" Freya read the words aloud and heard them land in her own chest like stones dropped down a well. Grateful for the quiet. She had written those exact sentiments in a dozen motel registers across a dozen flatland counties, under names that weren't quite hers.

"And then the Annexation came," Tomas said, "and the network went silent, and everyone told her to come down."

"Did they?" Freya flipped forward through the years. "Or did nobody remember she was up here? Because those are different tragedies, and I want to know which one we're looking at."

They dug for the answer together, and it was worse than either option. There had been orders—she found them, carbon copies tucked into a folder labeled in three languages, the last official correspondence Station Kestrel ever received: *Personnel are instructed to withdraw pending administrative reorganization.* Dated two weeks after the Annexation. And beneath it, in Vessna's hand, a response that had apparently never been sent, drafted and redrafted on the same page until the paper wore thin:

*I am the reorganization.*

Freya laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, and she pressed her fist against her mouth to stop the rest of it coming out after.

"Hey." Tomas crouched in front of her, mug steaming in both hands. "Talk to me."

"If I talk I'll say it, and if I say it, it'll be true."

"It's already true. That's how true works."

She looked at him. His face was tired and kind and completely without pity, which was the only kind of face she could have borne in that moment.

"She stayed eleven years," Freya said, "recording data no one would ever read. And she knew. She *knew*, and she kept measuring anyway, because—" her voice snagged— "because being a keeper is also a kind of work. That's not devotion, Tomas. That's what happens to people who convince themselves that staying invisible is the same thing as mattering."

The lamp buzzed. Outside, the wind tested the guy-wires and found them wanting.

"You think that's you," Tomas said. Not a question.

"I think I've spent nine years making sure no one could find me, and calling it strategy. I think I never stayed anywhere longer than a season because a season isn't long enough to owe anyone anything. I thought I was surviving." She wrapped both hands around the mug because they were shaking and tin conducts heat and heat is honest. "She survived too. Look how well it worked out. They found her skeleton in that chair, Tomas. I guarantee you they found her in the chair, pencil in her hand, because God forbid she die between observations."

Tomas didn't flinch. He'd learned, over years, that flinching at Freya only encouraged her.

"Okay," he said. "Say that's true. Say invisibility is just dying slowly with better scenery. What's the alternative?"

"The alternative is what's in the vault." She said it before she could stop herself, and once it was out, it stood there in the lamplight, enormous. "Thousands of documents. A whole nation's proof of itself. And every instinct I have says sell it, disappear, buy our lives back with it and never look at another map again. And every hour in this room says—" she gestured helplessly at the shelves, the charts, the stub of pencil saved in its cup— "says that if I do that, I'm just Vessna Aran with a truck instead of a tower. Recording everything. Reading nothing. Keeping data for no reader."

The wind rose. Somewhere above them, loose metal chattered against the tower like teeth.

Tomas sat down beside her on the cot, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was how he argued when arguing with words seemed inefficient.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I don't think she died for nothing. I think she died knowing exactly what she was doing, which is more than most people get. And I think—" he hesitated, choosing, which he almost never did— "I think the difference between you and her is that you still have a choice. She ran out of choices in October of the Annexation year. You've got six days of weather window and a decision. That's not a tragedy, Freya. That's just a life, arriving late."

Freya stared into her mug. The tea was terrible. Everything up here was terrible—the cold, the altitude, the odds—and yet some traitorous part of her chest had gone quiet for the first time since the river, since the seal, since the letter with her family name on it.

"She would have liked you," Freya said finally. "Terrible tea, stubborn opinions. Very Karstian, probably."

"High praise."

"Don't let it go to your head."

They worked through the evening because work was the only prayer either of them trusted. Tomas photographed the logs with the rig's last functioning camera, page by page, muttering coordinates and dates like a rosary. Freya read. She read eleven years of weather in a country that had stopped existing, and somewhere around year four she stopped seeing it as a mirror and started seeing it as a message, because Vessna Aran had gotten clever in her isolation—had started encoding things in her observation notes, small anomalies flagged in the margins. Pressure readings that matched nothing atmospheric. Magnetic deviations, dated and timed, always clustered along one bearing.

The same bearing the orrery pointed.

"She wasn't just keeping records," Freya said, sitting back, heart going hard. "She was surveying. All those years. She mapped the approach to the vault from up here, and she hid it inside the weather data because she knew someday someone who read weather would come looking."

Tomas lowered the camera. "You're sure?"

"Tomas. I've spent my whole life reading other people's storms. This is a storm. It's just made of paper." She tapped the margin notation, the little spiral glyph that meant wind everywhere else in these logs—but here, in this one column, appearing on clear days with impossible regularity. "It's a signal. She left a signal for whoever was paying attention."

Outside, the light had failed entirely, and the temperature with it. Through the frost-feathered window, the stars over the Escarpment burned with the pitiless clarity of high altitude, and below them, far down the dark flank of the mountain, Freya saw something that stopped her breath.

Lights. Moving lights. Two of them, maybe three, descending the glacier route they'd taken two days ago—steady, deliberate, spaced like professionals.

Tomas followed her gaze. The humor drained out of his face by degrees, replaced by the flat calm he wore into bad terrain.

"How fast can you read?" he asked.

Freya was already pulling volumes off the shelf. "Wake me if the mountain falls on us."

* * *

They worked through the night by lamplight and fear, photographing pages, cross-referencing the encoded bearings against Mirela's grandfather's route, and building—line by line, glyph by glyph—a picture neither of them had expected: Vessna Aran hadn't just surveyed the vault's approach. In her final winter, her handwriting deteriorating, her entries shortening, she had recorded something else. A secondary entrance. A back door into the ice, unmarked on any map, known only to her.

On the last page of the last log, dated three days before the entries simply stopped, she had written a single line that Freya read aloud twice to make sure she wasn't inventing it:

*If you are reading this, you are the reader I waited for. Take the north bearing. Do not trust the summit approach—they will watch the summit. And tell them Karstia measured its own weather to the end.*

Below the lamplight, the moving lights on the glacier had stopped. Holding position. Waiting for morning, and for them.

Freya closed the log and looked at Tomas across the little circle of light, and felt—for the first time in nine years—not like a woman running, but like a woman carrying something.

"We leave before dawn," she said. "North bearing."

Tomas nodded once. Then, quietly: "She'd have liked that."

"Don't let it go to your head," Freya said, and began packing.
