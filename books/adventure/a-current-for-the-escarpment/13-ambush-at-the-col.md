## 13. Ambush at the Col

The col announced itself the way bad news always does: quietly, then all at once.

Tomas had been watching it for an hour from the shoulder of the ridge—a saddle of grey scree between two shoulders of rock, maybe two hundred meters of exposed crossing, the only sane way through. Everything else around it was either a cliff or a glacier that ate people. So of course that was where Rook would be waiting. If he'd been planning the ambush himself, he'd have picked the col. He had, in fact, picked it in his head three days ago, and had then spent three days hoping Freya wouldn't think of it, because Freya thought of everything, and so did Ilse Rook, and that was the problem with being chased by someone who thought like you did.

"Col's clean," Freya said, lowering the binoculars.

"You sure?"

"I'm sure the *col's* clean." She handed the binoculars back and rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. She had a sunburn starting across her nose and a cut on her forearm from two days ago that she kept pretending wasn't there. "The approach isn't. See the boulder field on the west flank? Half those rocks have fresh scuff on the uphill faces. Somebody climbed up there this morning and didn't care who knew it."

"Or a mountain goat with poor manners."

"Mountain goats don't smoke." She nodded toward a faint thread of blue-grey drifting off the rocks, so thin it might have been nothing, might have been imagination. It was neither. "Rook's people are patient. They'll let us commit to the crossing and then close the trap from both ends. It's what I'd do."

"It's what you *did* do," Tomas said, "in the Karoo, when those insurance men were after the rig."

"That was different. Those men were amateurs with a pickup truck and a grudge." Freya shouldered her pack and looked at the col the way she looked at a storm cell she'd decided to drive into anyway. "Rook is a professional with a helicopter budget and a mandate."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a very cheerful person. Ask anyone who's ever owed me money."

They were at four thousand meters and the air had the thin, glassy quality of altitude—every breath a little transaction, every step a negotiation. The orrery rode in Tomas's pack, wrapped in the spare sleeping bag, and he could feel it there the way you feel a cracked rib: not painful exactly, but present, insistent, reminding you that it mattered. Since the dead station it had taken to twitching its needle at odd moments, and Freya had gone quiet in a way that worried him more than shouting would have. She'd spent the last day and a half recalculating. He knew the signs. She did it the way other people prayed.

"Option two," he said. "We go around. South ridge, drop low, cross the glacier tongue at night."

"Option two costs us a day and a half, and we don't have a day and a half. The snowline's already at the second bench." She pointed up-slope, to where the grey rock gave way to white with no apology. "That's three weeks early. The passes seal, we're on the wrong side of them, and Rook knows it. She's not chasing us anymore, Tomas. She's herding us. The col is the door and she's standing behind it."

"So we knock politely."

"We knock *first*." And there it was—the thing about Freya that had kept him employed and occasionally alive for six years. Most people, cornered, got smaller. Freya got *specific*. She was already unpacking the flare gun, the last two flares, a coil of rope, and the emergency beacon she'd been saving for a genuine catastrophe, on the theory that a catastrophe you caused yourself was still a catastrophe. "They expect a couple of debt-runners to bolt or beg. Nobody expects us to come through the front door at a dead sprint making noise like the end of the world."

"That's because it's a terrible plan."

"It's a *cheap* plan. Terrible and cheap are different things." She pressed the flare gun into his hand. "You take the west flank, up through the boulders, and light one off above their position. They'll shift to cover the flare—that's instinct, you can't train it out—and while they're looking up, we cross. Straight through the middle, fast, no stopping. If anyone's still standing in our way when we get there, I'll handle the talking."

"Your talking involves elbows."

"My talking is *very persuasive*."

* * *

It went wrong in the way plans go wrong, which is to say: immediately, and then all at once.

The boulder field was worse than it looked from below—the rocks were the size of trucks and balanced like drunkards, and Tomas spent the first ten minutes of the climb convinced he was going to bring the whole hillside down on his own head. He climbed anyway, because the alternative was standing in a valley with Ilse Rook, and he'd rather be crushed by geology. At least geology didn't smile at you first.

From the high rocks he could see them: four figures, spread along the col's eastern lip in the textbook pattern—two visible, two not, the not-ones positioned where the terrain funneled anyone crossing into a kill-sack of scree and open ground. Rook herself stood a little apart, hands in her pockets, watching the approach trail with the relaxed posture of a woman waiting for a bus. She'd even brought a thermos. Tomas had a brief, unworthy fantasy of rolling a boulder onto the thermos specifically.

He braced the flare gun against a rock, aimed well above and left of the two visible figures, and fired.

The flare went up with a sound like a goose being insulted, burst red, and drifted. And the trap sprang—not on Freya, on *him*. The two hidden figures hadn't been watching the col at all. They'd been watching the west flank, because of course they had, because Rook had read the same play Tomas had, and the flare was not a distraction to her, it was a *confirmation*. A voice shouted below him, flat and professional, and two climbers started up through the boulders with the unhurried confidence of people who had done this before and been paid well for it.

Tomas did the arithmetic he hated: Freya was already crossing, alone, into four people. The flare had bought nothing. He had maybe ninety seconds before the climbers reached him, and the only asset he had was the thing in his pack.

So he did what he'd been dreading since Halvett. He took out the ledger.

It was heavier than paper had any right to be—Karstian ledger-board, oilcloth-wrapped, the kind of thing built to survive a river and a war and a grandmother's attic. He'd read it cover to cover in the whiteout, every name, every custodian's mark, and he understood now why the Syndicate wanted it more than the orrery. The orrery opened a door. The ledger *was* the country. He wrapped it in his spare shirt, then in the waterproof map case, then, after a moment's thought, in the foil emergency blanket, and looked around for somewhere a determined woman with a helicopter budget would never think to look.

There wasn't one. There was never one. That was the lie of hiding places.

So he didn't hide it. He *buried* it—three feet down in the gravel of a meltwater channel, under a flat stone, and then he did the thing that made his hands shake: he took the orrery out of his pack, left it wrapped in the sleeping bag on top of the stone, and covered the whole arrangement with loose scree until only the brass rim of the sleeping-bag bundle showed, the way gear looks when someone has dropped it in a panic.

A decoy. The orrery as bait, the ledger as the prize nobody would know to dig for. It was the worst trade he'd ever made and the best, both at once, and he was still deciding which when the first climber came over the rocks ten meters away and said, pleasantly, "Morning. Don't."

* * *

They took the orrery. Of course they took the orrery—the sleeping bag, the brass, the needle that pointed at secrets. One of them, a big man with a beard full of ice, turned it over in his hands like a fisherman judging a catch and said, "Huh. It's warm."

"It's been in a sleeping bag," Tomas said.

"It's *warm*," the man said again, and looked at it a little differently after that, which gave Tomas a small, vicious satisfaction.

They marched him down to the col with his hands laced behind his head, and the whole way he was doing a second, quieter arithmetic: where was Freya, and why hadn't the trap closed on her? He got his answer when he reached the saddle and saw her—twenty meters out on the open scree, down on one knee with her hands up, and Rook standing over her, and between them, glinting on the stones, a scatter of blood. Not much blood. Enough.

"Tomás," Rook said, without turning around. "Good morning. You found the col. I left the light on for you."

"You left four men on it."

"I left *two* men on it. The other two were a contingency. I find contingencies are the only honest part of any plan." She finally turned, and she was smiling the way she smiled, which was to say warmly, which was to say not at all. She looked tired, he noticed. There were new lines around her eyes and she was holding her left arm slightly stiff, and he thought: *she's been climbing at altitude for a week too, and she's ten years older than us, and she's doing this on orders she doesn't like.* It was hard to hate someone you could see limping. He hated her anyway, on principle, but it took effort now.

"Freya's cut her leg," Rook said. "Fell on the scree when my colleague got enthusiastic. It's not deep. I'd like to bandage it. She'd rather I didn't touch her, which is her right, and also her problem, because she's bleeding on a mountain."

"Let me," Tomas said.

"That's the spirit." Rook stepped back and gestured with two fingers, and one of her people—a young one, freckled, who kept glancing at the orrery like it might bite—tossed Tomas the med kit. Freya's leg was a long shallow tear from knee to mid-calf, ugly but honest, the kind of cut that bled dramatically and meant little. Tomas cleaned it and taped it while she stared at the middle distance with her jaw set, and under his breath, so only she could hear, he said, "The ledger's safe. Buried. They got the orrery and they think it's the prize."

Her eyes flicked to him—just once, a whole conversation in a millimeter of movement—and then back to the distance. *You gave up the orrery?* the look said. *You buried the ledger under it?* And then, slowly, the jaw unclenched a degree, which from Freya was a standing ovation.

"Here's the situation," Rook said, crouching down to their level, which she did deliberately, he knew, because it made her seem reasonable, and because it put her below the ridgeline and out of the wind, and Rook never did anything for only one reason. "My orders changed this morning. I'm not to bring you in. I'm to bring in the instrument and confirm the destruction of any documents. You two are—" she paused, and for a moment the smile went somewhere else, somewhere tired, "—you two are a line item I'd rather not itemize. So. Walk away. Down the south side, back to Halvett, back to your truck, back to your small dishonest lives. The debt dies with the chest. Everyone goes home. That's the generous version, and I'm only offering it once, because I'm standing on a col at four thousand meters and my knees are filing complaints."

"And if we don't take the generous version?" Freya asked.

"Then the version after this one is written by people who enjoy their work." Rook stood, and nodded at the bearded man, who set the orrery down on a flat rock between them, wrapped and gleaming, like a hostage displayed at a window. "The instrument comes with me. That part isn't negotiable. The rest is."

And that was when Freya laughed.

Tomas had known her through storms, bankruptcies, one wedding neither of them mentioned anymore, and a memorable incident involving a stolen weather balloon, and he had never heard her laugh like that—short, sharp, genuinely delighted, the laugh of someone who has just seen the answer to a puzzle. Rook's eyebrows went up a careful centimeter.

"You want to know what's funny?" Freya said.

"I genuinely do."

"You're holding a very beautiful, very expensive piece of brass that my grandmother's people built to find one specific place on this earth. And you're standing on it." She pointed down, at the scree, at the col, at the meltwater channels threading through it. "The needle's been twitching for two days because we've been walking toward it. You didn't need the orrery to find the vault, Ilse. You needed it to *recognize* it. And you're standing on the one spot in a hundred kilometers where it would point straight down." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile, and Tomas fell a little bit in love with her all over again, in the strictly professional way he had long ago learned to file and not act upon. "So take it. Take it to your clients. And every night for the rest of your life, it'll point at the ground under your feet, and they'll dig, and they'll find gravel, and they'll dig somewhere else, and they'll find more gravel. You didn't seize the key. You seized a compass with one address, and you're standing on the address."

It was a bluff. Tomas knew it was a bluff, because he'd buried the orrery himself an hour ago, and the thing Rook's man was holding had never pointed at anything in its life except the general direction of Freya's ambitions. But Rook didn't know that. Rook's people didn't know that. And he watched the doubt move through them like weather—the bearded man glancing down at the bundle, the freckled one looking at his boots, Rook herself going very still in the way of a woman recalculating at speed.

"You're lying," Rook said.

"Probably," Freya agreed pleasantly. "Want to bet your career on which part?"

* * *

What saved them was not the bluff, in the end. What saved them was the mountain, and a map drawn by an old man in a border town who had spent his life memorizing a country that no longer existed.

Because while Rook was recalculating, the wind changed.

Tomas felt it before he understood it—a pressure shift, a flattening of the air, the specific hush that Freya had taught him to fear more than any sound. Freya felt it too. Her head came up like a bird dog's, and she looked west, at the cloud deck that had been sitting on the far peaks all morning, and Tomas watched her read it the way other people read a letter from a lawyer.

"Katabatic," she said quietly. "Cold air off the icefield. It's going to come down that glacier chute in about four minutes and it's going to come down like a freight train. Sixty, seventy knots, maybe more, with spindrift behind it." She looked at Rook. "Your helicopter's on the east side. It won't fly in that. Your people are roped for rock, not wind. And that chute—" she pointed south, at the steep glacier tongue that fell away from the col in a long white throat, the route every sane person avoided, the route Mirela's grandfather had drawn in pencil with a note that translated, roughly, to *only in emergency, only downhill, only if you are not afraid*— "that chute is about to become the fastest road off this mountain that anyone's ever seen. If you're on it when the wind hits, you ride. If you're not, you're up here in a whiteout with a brass compass that points at gravel."

"You're bluffing about that too," Rook said. But her voice had changed. She was a professional, and professionals know when the other professional has stopped performing.

"Check my eyes," Freya said. "I've never once lied about weather. It's the only thing I've never lied about."

And that, God help them, was true, and Rook knew it was true, and Tomas watched the whole future turn on that single fact: that a woman who lied about everything had built her one incorruptible truth out of wind, and it held.

"Four minutes," Rook said.

"Three and a half now."

Rook looked at her people. She looked at the orrery. She looked, for one unguarded second, at Freya, and something passed between them that Tomas would spend a long time not asking about—something that looked less like hatred than like recognition, two women who had both learned to survive by calculating, each seeing the cost of the other's arithmetic.

"Go," Rook said. "Take the chute. If you're lying about the wind, my people will find you in the whiteout, and we'll finish this conversation somewhere warmer." She turned to the bearded man. "Pull back to the east bench. Move."

They moved. And Freya grabbed Tomas's arm and said, "Rope. Now. And Tomás—" her voice cracked, just once, just a hair, and he saw how white she'd gone around the mouth, and how much of that was the leg and how much was the rest of it, "—if I'm wrong about the wind, I'm sorry about everything."

"You're not wrong about the wind."

"I know. I just like saying sorry while it's still optional."

They clipped in, pointed themselves down the throat of the glacier, and jumped into the white.

* * *

The chute was exactly what Mirela's grandfather had promised and nothing like what Tomas had hoped. It was a half-kilometer of steep blue ice and old snow, banked on both sides like a luge run built by a glacier with a grudge, and the wind arrived while they were still in the top third of it and hit them the way debt hits—suddenly, totally, with interest. Sixty knots, then seventy, a horizontal river of air and ice-crystals that erased the sky, erased the mountain, erased everything except the rope between them and the next three meters of slope. Freya went first because her weight was lower on the injured leg and because she was Freya; Tomas came second, paying out rope, driving his ice axe in on the pattern she'd drilled into him years ago—*plant, sit, slide, plant*—while the world screamed and the cold went through every layer he owned as if the layers were a rumor.

Twice Freya fell. The second time, the leg gave out entirely and she slid ten meters on her hip before the rope caught her, and Tomas held the anchor with his whole life in his shoulders and hauled her back onto her feet, and she stood up swearing with a fluency that would have made her grandmother proud, and they kept going, because stopping was death and going was only probably death, and probably was the best odds they'd been offered in a week.

They came out of the chute at the bottom like two rag dolls spat from a machine, tumbling onto the gentler ice of the glacier tongue, and the wind passed over them and went on down-valley to ruin someone else's afternoon, and the quiet that followed was so total that Tomas could hear his own heart and Freya's ragged breathing and, distantly, the creak and settle of the ice, which he chose to interpret as applause.

Freya lay on her back on the glacier, chest heaving, leg bleeding through the tape, one glove gone, frost in her eyelashes, and started to laugh again—that same sharp, disbelieving laugh.

"We lost the orrery," she said.

"We lost the orrery."

"Rook has the orrery."

"Rook has the orrery."

"And we're alive, on the wrong side of the mountain, with no instrument, no map past this point, a leg that's going to need real stitches, and—" she stopped. Sat up. Looked at him with an expression he'd learned to be afraid of, because it was the expression she wore when she was about to be honest. "And the ledger. You buried the ledger, Tomás. Under the orrery. On a col that Rook's people are standing on."

"Buried under a flat stone in a meltwater channel, wrapped in foil, with the orrery on top as a decoy. They'll dig for the orrery, find it, and never check what's under it. Nobody checks what's under a thing they've already won." He paused. "Also I may have mentioned to the big one that the brass was warm, so they'll treat it carefully. You're welcome."

She stared at him. The wind moaned somewhere up the chute. And then she said, very quietly, "You gave up the key to save the country."

"I gave up the key because the key was replaceable and the country wasn't. That's not the same thing, and I want it noted that I know it's not the same thing, and that I'm going to feel clever about it for exactly one more hour before I start feeling terrified."

"What happens in an hour?"

"In an hour," Tomas said, "we have to go back up and dig it out before they do. And Freya—I don't know how we do that. I genuinely don't. The map ended at the col. Mirela's grandfather's route ends at the col. Everything past this point is a country that isn't on any map, and we just slid out of it the wrong way."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket and pulled out the thing she'd been carrying since Halvett without telling him—the folded page of Karstian field notes from Station Kestrel, the dead scientist's last log, water-stained and soft as cloth—and smoothed it on her knee. Her finger stopped on a line near the bottom, a line he'd seen her read a dozen times and never explain.

"Kestrel wrote it down," Freya said. "'The vault does not open from above. It opens from below, where the ice is oldest. When the orrery fails, follow the meltwater. The water remembers the way in.'" She looked up at him, and her eyes were bright and scared and more alive than he'd seen them since the river. "Tomás. Where did you bury the ledger?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Thought about the meltwater channel, the gravel, the flat stone, the cold clean water that ran over it every afternoon when the sun hit the icefields.

"In a stream," he said.

Freya smiled. It was not entirely a reassuring smile. It was, in fact, the smile she wore when driving into storms.

"Then," she said, "we go back up."
