## 14. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive so much as descend on them like a lid being screwed onto the world.

Freya had seen it building for six hours—had named it, catalogued it, given it the grudging respect she gave all weather worth its salt—and still, when it came over the shoulder of the mountain, it came faster than her models said it should. The Escarpment had a habit of that. It took your data and laughed at it.

"Tomas!" she shouted into the wind, though he was ten feet away and had been roped to her since noon. "Left! There's a hollow in the rock face—left!"

"I'm going left because you're pulling me left," he shouted back, which was fair.

The hollow was barely a hollow—a seam where two slabs of granite leaned against each other like tired drunks, with a floor of packed snow and a ceiling low enough that Tomas, who was not a tall man but was taller than she was, would spend two days apologizing to his spine. It faced away from the wind. In the arithmetic of survival on the Escarpment in early winter, that made it a palace.

They dug it out with the shovel and the flat of the ice axe and their hands, and by the time the light went—not dusk, just the storm eating the sun whole—they had something that could charitably be called a snow cave and uncharitably be called a grave with amenities.

* * *

Fuel first. That was the rule, and Freya held to rules when everything else went sideways, because rules were load-bearing.

"Two canisters," she said, holding them up in the light of the headlamp. "We've got two."

Tomas looked at them the way a man looks at a diagnosis. "And how long does two last?"

"If we're careful? Two days."

"And if we're not careful?"

"One very cozy evening."

She almost smiled. That was the thing about Tomas—underneath the caution, underneath the conscience that weighed more than the rest of him combined, there was this dry little vein of humor that surfaced exactly when things were worst, like water finding a crack. She'd relied on it more than she'd ever admitted.

"So we're careful," he said.

"We're careful." She set one canister aside—the emergency, the do-not-touch, the one they'd both pretend didn't exist—and lit the other on its lowest flame. The stove hissed. Snow went into the pot. In twenty minutes they'd have water, and water was life, and life was what you kept doing when you'd run out of reasons and had to settle for momentum.

Outside, the wind found a note and held it, a long flat scream against the rock, the kind of sound that got inside your skull and rearranged the furniture. Freya had slept through hurricanes. This was different. Hurricanes moved. This thing had planted itself on the mountain like it owned the deed.

"How's the ribs?" Tomas asked.

"Ribs are a social construct."

"That's not an answer."

"They hurt," she admitted. "Less than yesterday. The bruising is going green, which means it's going, which means I win."

"You fell down a glacier chute."

"I descended a glacier chute with great style and only minor structural consequences."

He shook his head, but she caught the smile before he turned away to tend the stove. Small victories. You banked them like fuel.

* * *

The first day passed the way days pass in holes: slowly, and then all at once.

There wasn't much to do. They melted snow. They ate half a ration each and agreed without discussion to call it a full one. Freya checked the weather by instinct—pressing her palm flat against the stone at the cave mouth every few hours, reading the vibration of the wind through the rock the way her grandmother had supposedly read tea leaves, except her grandmother had been reading barometric pressure and calling it magic, which was the family business in a nutshell.

The orrery sat between them in its nest of pack cloth, brass arms folded like a sleeping insect. Since Station Kestrel it had stopped being a curiosity and become a presence. Its needle still pointed off-route, toward nothing either of them could name, and neither of them mentioned it, because mentioning it meant deciding whether to follow it, and deciding that required fuel they were currently melting snow with.

Tomas slept. Freya watched the ceiling of the cave, which was blue-white and rippled like the surface of a frozen lake seen from below, and thought about ceilings she had known. Motel ceilings. Truck cab ceilings. The canvas ceiling of a tent in Nebraska with hail hammering it like applause from an audience that wanted her dead.

Somewhere in there she slept too, badly, and dreamed about her mother's kitchen, which was odd, because she hadn't thought about that kitchen in years, and in the dream the window over the sink showed a sky with the wrong clouds in it—Karstian clouds, she understood somehow, clouds from a country that no longer existed, and her mother was humming, and Freya woke up with wet eyes and blamed the altitude.

* * *

The second day, the fuel ran out.

Not all of it. The emergency canister remained, sacred and untouched, and they both looked at it across the cave the way you look at a relative you're not speaking to. But the first canister sputtered and died around midday—or what they guessed was midday; the storm had abolished time along with everything else—and after that the cold came in earnest, the patient kind, the kind that doesn't attack, just waits, and takes a degree an hour, and lets you watch it go.

"We light the second one tonight," Freya said. "Low. Just enough to take the edge off and melt drinking water. Then we walk out tomorrow regardless of what the sky's doing."

"And if the sky's still doing this?"

"Then we walk out anyway and complain about it."

Tomas fed the stove its last cup of snow and watched it melt with the concentration of a man defusing something. His hands were steady. His face was doing something complicated.

"Freya."

"Mm."

"Why do you keep moving?"

She looked at him. He didn't look back—he kept his eyes on the pot, on the small silver skin of water forming over the snow—but she knew him well enough now to know the question hadn't come out of nowhere. It had been sitting in him for weeks. Maybe since the river. Maybe longer. Maybe since the first season they'd worked together, when she'd told him she never stayed anywhere past autumn and he'd asked why and she'd said *rent's cheaper when you don't sign anything*, and he'd accepted the lie with a politeness she should have recognized as patience.

"What kind of question is that?" she said.

"The kind you answer at altitude. Everybody answers one true thing at altitude. It's a rule."

"Says who?"

"Says me. I just made it up. Altitude law."

Despite everything—the cold in her boots, the ache in her ribs, the wind outside sounding like the end of the world rehearsing—she huffed something that was nearly a laugh. "Altitude law."

"Take your time. We've got nothing but."

The pot began to murmur. The stove flame bent and steadied. Outside, the storm screamed its one long note.

And Freya Dunmore, who had built a whole life out of not answering questions, found that the cold had gotten under her defenses somewhere, or the altitude had, or the exhaustion, or the strange blue light of the cave, or the fact that Tomas had pulled a chest out of a river against her explicit instruction and been right to do it, and that she owed him a debt no ledger had ever recorded.

"My parents didn't die in an accident," she said.

The pot murmured. Tomas went very still.

"They told people it was a road collapse. A bridge, actually—the Vraka bridge, spring flood, terrible thing, happens all the time in river country. And bridges do collapse. But theirs collapsed three weeks after my father testified at a land commission hearing. Three weeks. He'd stood up in a room full of men with Syndicate money in their coats and said out loud that the Annexation surveys were falsified. That the Karstian holdings hadn't been legally transferred, they'd been laundered. He had documents." Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She'd expected it to break. Flat was worse, somehow. Flat was the sound of a thing carried too long. "He had documents, and my mother begged him to burn them, and he wouldn't, because he was that kind of Dunmore. The kind who thinks the truth is armor."

"It isn't," Tomas said quietly.

"No. It's a flare. You light it and everyone sees you, including the people shooting."

The stove hissed. Somewhere above them, tons of snow shifted and settled with a groan like a sleeping animal.

"After the bridge, I was nineteen. An aunt took me in—my mother's sister, who'd already changed her name twice and taught me to do the same before I understood why. Change the name, change the town, change the trade. Never file anything. Never sign anything. Never be the person the record points to." Freya wrapped her arms around her knees, ribs protesting, and stared at the flame. "And I got good at it, Tomas. Do you understand? I didn't just survive it. I got *good*. I became weather. Weather doesn't have a surname. Weather shows up, does damage, moves on. Nobody serves papers on a thunderstorm."

"Is that what you think you do? Damage?"

"I know what I don't do. I don't stay. Staying is how they find you. Staying is how my father ended up under a bridge with his documents scattered in the floodwater like confetti." She heard her own voice climb and dragged it back down. "Every season I leave, some part of me thinks: *there. Paid another installment.* Like penance. Like if I stay invisible long enough, I square some account. Except the account never squares, because the account isn't real. Nobody's counting. My parents are dead either way. Moving doesn't bring them back. It just—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It just means I never have to be anywhere when it hurts."

The silence afterward was enormous. The wind filled it, and then the wind couldn't fill it anymore, and the silence came back bigger.

Tomas reached over and turned the stove off. The flame died, and the cold rushed in like it had been waiting politely at the door, but he didn't seem to care. He took off one glove and put his bare hand over hers where it gripped her knee.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what? That wasn't a gift, that was a hemorrhage."

"For trusting me with it." He paused. "I have to tell you something. And I want to tell it now, while you're the version of you that just said all that, because if I wait until you're rested you'll build the walls back up and I'll lose my nerve, and then we'll carry it up the rest of this mountain like ballast."

Freya's stomach dropped. Cold, and not from the cave. "Tomas."

"The night we pulled the chest from the river. Before I brought it to camp—I opened it. I read the letter. 'To the Dunmore line.' I read it standing in the willows with my hands freezing, and then I closed the chest and carried it to you and let you think we'd discovered it together." His voice didn't waver, but his hand did, just slightly, on hers. "I told myself I was protecting you. That if you knew your name was in it from the first minute, you'd bolt—burn it, sink it, drive us both into a canyon trying to make it never have existed. And maybe that was even true. But that's not the same as honesty, is it. Protecting you from a choice isn't protection. It's just making your choices for you. Which is what the Syndicate does. Which is what the people who killed your father did."

The cave was very dark now, with the stove off. Only the faint grey glow at the mouth, the storm's idea of daylight.

"When?" she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

"The river. First night."

"Two months." She pulled her hand out from under his. "Two months, Tomas. Rook searched our camp. People died at the col. I broke four ribs going down a glacier chute, and you knew—from the *first night*—that this thing was addressed to me. To my family. By name."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me now. In a hole. With no fuel."

"With no fuel," he agreed. "Because tomorrow we might die on that ridge, and I decided I'd rather you be furious at me up here than grieve me down there with it still unsaid." He took a breath. "Also, altitude law. One true thing per day. You went first."

She wanted to hit him. She genuinely, physically wanted to hit him, and she was fairly sure that with the ribs the way they were she'd do herself more damage than she'd do him, which was probably why he'd chosen this moment, the absolute—

No. No, he hadn't chosen it. He'd said so. He'd lost his nerve everywhere else and the cave had done what caves do, stripped things down to studs. That was almost worse. That meant he'd been carrying it up every switchback, every ford, every mile of Mirela's route, silent as ballast, exactly as he'd said.

"You idiot," she said, and her voice cracked clean in half on the second word.

"I know."

"You absolute—" She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "I spent two months thinking the chest found us. Randomly. Fate, luck, the river coughing it up. And it wasn't random. It was *mine*. It was always mine, and you knew, and I—" The grief came up then, finally, the real grief, not the flat recitation but the live wire of it: her father under the bridge, her mother's begging, the aunt changing names like bed linens, and herself, nineteen years old, learning to become weather. All of it arriving at once in a blue-lit hole in a mountain, with a man who had lied to her for the best possible reasons, which were the most unforgivable kind.

She cried the way she imagined she'd cry if she ever let herself: badly, gracelessly, with her ribs screaming, into her sleeve, while the storm outside held its one long note and Tomas sat beside her not touching her, not saying *it's okay*, because it wasn't okay, just present, the way he'd been present through every disaster she'd dragged him into, steady as a tripod leg.

It took a long time. The storm didn't mind. Storms never do.

When it was wrung out of her—when she was empty in the good way, the scoured way—she lowered her hands and looked at him. His face in the grey light was older than she'd ever let herself notice.

"The letter," she said hoarsely. "'To the Dunmore line.' What else did it say?"

Tomas was quiet a moment. "It said the bearer inherits an obligation, not a treasure. It said the archive belongs to everyone Karstia lost, and the heir is only its custodian—first among witnesses, it called it. And it said—" he hesitated, then finished, "it said the line must not run again. Not hide again. That hiding is how nations die. Not conquest. Hiding."

Freya looked at the orrery in its nest of cloth, its brass needle patient as a compass rose, pointing off toward whatever it had been pointing at since Station Kestrel.

"First among witnesses," she repeated.

"That's the phrase."

She thought about her father standing up in that room full of bought men. About the scientist at Kestrel, logging data no one would ever read, staying anyway. About Mirela's grandfather smuggling maps. About a whole country that had survived forty years the only way it could—by hiding—and what it cost, and who was still paying it.

"Tomorrow," she said, "when this storm breaks, we're not going down. We're going where the needle points."

Tomas nodded slowly. "Even though—"

"Even though. I'm done running installments." She reached over and squeezed his hand, hard, once—forgiveness delivered the only way she knew how to deliver it, in freight rather than words. "But Tomas? If you ever keep a secret from me again, I will bury you in a snow cave so deep they'll find you in the spring tribunal."

"Noted," he said. "For the record, I hate secrets. I'm terrible at them. As demonstrated."

"As demonstrated," she agreed, and for the first time in two days—in two months, maybe in nineteen years—she lay back against the cold stone and closed her eyes without listening for the wind.

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of snow, the storm screamed on. But far beneath the scream, so faint she might have imagined it, the mountain made a different sound: a deep, shifting groan, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Or waking up.
