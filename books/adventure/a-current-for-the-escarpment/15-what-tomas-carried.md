## 15. What Tomas Carried

The storm had moved off eastward sometime in the night, taking its thunder with it, and what remained was the kind of morning Tomas had learned to distrust on principle: too blue, too still, the Escarpment standing up ahead of them like a held breath. Snow on the high ridges. Six days, the models said. Maybe five.

He sat on the tailgate of the rig with two enamel mugs and a pot of coffee that had been reheated so many times it had developed what he could only describe as a personality. Freya was still in the cab, asleep sitting up, her forehead against the wheel, her breath fogging the glass in small, even pulses. She'd taken the first watch and then the second, and when he'd told her to sleep she'd said *I'm fine* in the voice that meant she was not fine, and then she'd slept anyway, mid-sentence, like a lamp going out.

Tomas looked at the mugs. Looked at her. Looked at the ledger, which was wrapped in a wool sock inside a dry bag inside his duffel, because he had not trusted it to any of the obvious places since the col, since Rook's lieutenant had put a boot in Freya's ribs and taken the orrery and left them with nothing but each other and a glacier chute and, as it turned out, a book of dead names.

And here was the thing. Here was the thing he had been carrying since the river, since before the river, since the moment he'd knelt in the willow mud with his hands on wet brass fittings and felt the whole shape of his life tilt.

He had read the letter first.

Not the ledger. Not the orrery, which had taken weeks and a geomagnetic storm and Freya's particular genius to even begin to understand. The letter. *To the Dunmore line.* He had read it by headlamp in the cab while Freya was still down at the waterline, arguing with the flood about the sensor array, and he had understood in the space of one paragraph that everything was about to change, and he had folded it back along its creases and put it in the chest and closed the lid and said, when she climbed up, *Nothing. Just flood junk. A chest, though. Might be worth something.*

He had lied to her. Gently. For her own good, he'd told himself, the way all liars tell themselves, the way his mother had told herself about the money she didn't mention and the relatives she didn't name. And the lie had grown a spine and started walking, and now it was standing on the Escarpment with them, five days from the vault, and Freya had confessed her grief to him in a snow cave two nights ago — her parents, the Karstian ties, the penance of never stopping — and she had done it believing he'd learned it all from the ledger, same as she had, same week, same shock.

She didn't know he'd known from the first hour. That he'd had months to get used to the idea while she was still being ambushed by it. That when she'd opened the ledger in the light of the camp stove and found her grandmother's name and gone white as the snowline, he had sat across from her and poured her a drink and said *take your time* with the calm of a man who had already taken his.

The coffee steamed. The morning waited. Somewhere below them, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

Tomas poured the second mug, because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't wringing themselves, and went and knocked on the cab door with his knuckle, twice, soft.

"Freya. Coffee that has achieved self-awareness."

A groan. The door opened. She unfolded herself out of the driver's seat in stages, like a folding rule, and took the mug without looking at it, which was how he knew she was still in the snow cave in her head, still down in the dark with the things she'd said.

"Thanks." She drank. Made a face. "It's gotten worse."

"It's evolving."

They stood at the tailgate together, shoulder to shoulder, looking at the mountain. It was the kind of silence they were good at, the comfortable kind, the kind they'd built over six years of sharing a rig the size of a generous closet. And that was exactly why it was unbearable. Because the comfortable kind was built on habit, and habit, it turned out, was just a lie you'd stopped noticing.

"Tomas." She said it to the mountain, not to him. "About what I said. In the cave. The — all of it."

"You don't have to—"

"I do, actually." She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "I've been thinking about it all morning, and I need to say one thing, and then we're going to drive, because we have five days and a mountain and a syndicate with our orrery. The thing is—" she stopped, restarted. "The thing is, I feel *lighter*. Which is obscene. My parents are dead because of who we are, and I feel lighter, and I don't know what to do with that."

"You feel lighter," Tomas said, "because you've been carrying it alone for twenty years and you just put it down for the first time."

"Yes." Her jaw worked. "And that's the other thing. Because I put it down *with you*. And you—" She stopped. And Tomas watched it happen, watched the thought arrive in her face like weather coming over the escarpment, watched her go from tired to still to very, very quiet.

"You knew," she said.

"Freya—"

"You *knew*." Not loud. That was the terrible part. Her voice didn't rise at all. It went flat and precise, the way it did when she was reading pressure gradients, when she was doing arithmetic on their survival. "In the cave. When I told you about my parents. When I told you about the penance, when I cried on you, Tomas, I cried *on you* — you already knew. You'd known since—"

"Since the river."

The word *river* landed between them like a dropped tool.

She set her mug down on the tailgate. Very carefully. The way you set something down when the alternative is throwing it.

"Say that again," she said.

"The letter. *To the Dunmore line.* I read it the first night. You were down at the water pulling sensors out of the flood, and I opened the chest, and the letter was on top, and I read it." His voice was doing something embarrassing, so he made it stop. "And then I put it back and I told you it was probably junk."

For a long moment she said nothing at all. The bird below them made its hinge sound again. Somewhere on the ridge, wind moved through snow without moving the snow, a white ghost of itself.

"Get your bag," Freya said.

"Freya—"

"Get your bag, Tomas. We're not driving anywhere until you tell me all of it, every piece, from the first night to right now, and we're going to stand here in the cold and you're going to do it while I look at you." She picked up her mug again and drank, grimly, like a woman fortifying a garrison. "I want you to have the full experience of my face while you do it."

* * *

So he told her.

He told it in order, because order was the only dignity left to him. The river first: the willow roots, the chest snagged like a sleeping animal, the mud to his knees, her voice from the bank saying *leave it, we don't pick up strange things, that's rule one* — and him, kneeling there, feeling the brass fittings under his hands and hearing a door open somewhere in himself that he'd been leaning against his whole life.

"I know rule one," he said. "I wrote half of rule one. Curiosity won. It does that. It's the whole reason you and I are in this business instead of sensible ones."

"Flattery later. Keep going."

The letter. He gave it back to her as nearly word for word as he could, because she deserved it exact, and he watched her mouth move slightly as he spoke, following along, checking him against her own memory of the ledger's implications, her grandmother's name, the custodians, the vault. He told her how he'd sat in the cab with the headlamp and read it three times, and how by the third time his hands had stopped shaking and started thinking, and what they were thinking was: *she will read this and she will stop running, and the running is the only thing keeping her alive.*

"You have to understand what I knew," he said. "Not about Karstia. About you. I knew you hadn't stayed anywhere longer than a season in six years. I knew you paid the Syndicate in storm data the way other people pay in blood, a little at a time, forever. I knew that the moment you read *Dunmore* on Karstian paper you were going to have to decide who you were, and I knew — I *knew*, Freya — that deciding things was the one thing you'd built your whole life to avoid."

"And so you decided for me."

"Yes." It came out flat and ugly and true. "And I'd like to tell you I've been lying awake about it every night since, but I haven't. I've been sleeping fine, which is its own confession. I told myself it was protection. I told myself I'd tell you when the time was right. And then the time was never right, and then Rook was at our fire smiling at us, and then the ledger, and then it had been weeks, and by then telling you felt less like honesty and more like admitting I'd gotten away with something."

"Because you had."

"Because I had."

Freya looked at him for a long time. The wind came up the slope and moved her hair and she didn't fix it, which from Freya was a sign of catastrophic distraction.

"The cave," she said. "Everything I said in the cave. You sat there and let me hand you all of it, and you already had it."

"I let you—"

"You *let* me. Don't you dare soften it. You made a decision about what I could handle, and then you made another one, and another, and every single one of them was *for me* and *without me*, and that is—" Her voice finally cracked, and he flinched, and she steadied it by force. "That is what my parents' world did to people. Decided for them. Quietly. Kindly, even, some of them thought. For their own good. And then a whole country was gone from the maps and everyone who'd decided for everyone else said they'd meant well."

The words went into him like a splinter going in deep, clean, no blood until later.

"I'm not them," he said.

"No," she said. "You're not. That's the only reason we're having this conversation instead of me driving away with the ledger and the rig and half the coffee." She looked down at her hands. "You're not them. But you were doing the same move. The same *shape* of move. And I need you to see the shape, Tomas, because I've been doing it too. My whole life is the same shape. Deciding for other people what they can't know, can't carry, can't be — deciding for *myself*, year after year, that visibility would kill me, without ever once testing it."

The morning had warmed by a degree or two. It didn't feel warmer.

"Why tell me now?" she asked, and it wasn't an accusation, it was an actual question, and that was somehow worse. "Why not carry it over the pass? You're good at carrying things. It's practically your job."

Tomas looked at the mountain. Then at her.

"Because you told me the truth in the cave," he said. "You handed me the worst of you, no armor, and you did it because you'd decided we were past armor. And I sat there accepting it while wearing mine. And I realized that if I went over that pass with this still in my pocket, then everything we built up there would be standing on the same lie as everything behind us. And I don't—" He stopped. Started again, smaller. "I don't want a partnership built on habit, Freya. Habit is what you have with a truck. I want the thing where you can put it down with me. Both of us. All of it. Or there's no point to any of this."

* * *

She was quiet for so long that he started doing the thing he did when he couldn't bear a silence, which was narrate it, and he caught himself just in time and drank his terrible coffee instead.

When she finally spoke, it was slow, like she was testing ice.

"I'm furious," she said.

"Okay."

"I'm furious, and I want you to know that the fury isn't going anywhere. It's going to be in the truck with us. It's going to be in the tent. It's going to be there at the vault, sitting in the corner, arms crossed." She held up a hand before he could speak. "But I've spent twenty years being furious at the dead, and I can tell you from experience that it's a terrible use of fuel. And the arithmetic is this: you lied to protect me, which was wrong, and you did it out of love, which doesn't excuse it but does explain it, and then you told me the truth when telling it cost you everything comfortable between us. That's not nothing. That's actually the hardest version of honesty there is — the kind you volunteer when you could have gotten away with it forever."

"I could have," Tomas said quietly. "That's the worst part. You'd never have known."

"I know." She almost smiled, and it was a battered, crooked, real thing. "You're annoyingly trustworthy in every way except the one that mattered."

"That does sound like me."

"And here's what I keep coming back to." She turned to face him fully, and he braced. "In the cave, I told you things I've never told anyone. And it was — it was the first time in twenty years that being known didn't feel like being hunted. And you were the reason it felt safe. So the lie and the safety came from the same place, and I don't know how to separate them, and maybe I can't, and maybe that's what forgiveness actually is. Not separating them. Just deciding the whole mess is worth more than the wound."

Tomas found that his eyes were doing something. He looked at the mountain until they stopped.

"Freya Dunmore," he said, when his voice worked, "that is the most generous thing anyone has ever said to me, and I would like to note for the record that I also brought coffee."

"You brought coffee that has achieved sentience and turned hostile."

"Character-building coffee."

She laughed. It was short and wet and real, and it broke something loose in his chest that had been set in place since the river, since the willow roots, since the first lie. She stepped in and put her forehead against his shoulder, the way she did maybe twice a year, and he stood very still and let her, and neither of them said anything for a while, because some repairs don't need commentary, they just need to hold weight.

When she pulled back, her face had changed. The grief was still in it — that wasn't going anywhere either, and they both knew it now, and there was a strange relief in that too, in grief being allowed to just *be there* instead of being smuggled — but under it was the other Freya, the storm-reader, the one who could look at a sky full of ruin and find the current running through it.

"New rule one," she said. "No more deciding alone what the other one can carry."

"New rule one," he agreed. "It goes above *don't pick up strange things*."

"Nothing goes above *don't pick up strange things*." She drained her mug and grimaced. "We just make an exception for chests. And for partners who confess before the pass instead of after." She was already moving, rolling up the tarp, her hands busy the way her hands always were when her heart was settling. "Five days. Rook has our orrery, which means she has a key she doesn't know how to read, and we have a ledger, a route, and a mountain that owes us a favor after that chute. Get the truck warm. I want the ridge line before the wind comes up."

Tomas went to the cab. He opened his duffel to stow the mugs, and his hand found the dry bag, and the wool sock, and the ledger inside it — and stopped.

Because there was something else in the sock now. There had been since Halvett, since Mirela had pressed it into his palm at the door of her grandfather's house with a look that said *you'll know when*. A folded scrap of paper, old, soft as cloth, in a hand that wasn't Mirela's and wasn't her grandfather's and wasn't anyone he'd ever met.

He'd been carrying it for two days. Telling himself the time wasn't right.

Tomas stood in the cold morning with the paper in his fist and the new rule one sitting bright and untested in the air behind him, and he turned around, and he called across the campsite in a voice that came out steadier than he had any right to expect:

"Freya. Before the ridge line. There's one more thing."

She looked up from the tarp, eyebrows rising, and waited.
