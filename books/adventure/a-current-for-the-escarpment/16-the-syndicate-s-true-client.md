## 16. The Syndicate's True Client

Ilse Rook had a theory about bad news: it always arrived dressed as good news.

The message came through at 6:14 in the morning, while she was standing outside the Halvett waystation drinking coffee that tasted like it had been filtered through a boot and watching the snowline creep down the Escarpment like something patient and hungry. The courier drone was Syndicate issue—matte grey, no markings, the kind of machine that existed officially nowhere and unofficially everywhere—and it dropped the sealed case into her gloved hand with the gentle courtesy of a bird delivering something dead.

"Priority One," the drone chirped. "Eyes only. Acknowledge receipt."

"Acknowledged," Rook said, and the drone climbed away into the grey morning, mission accomplished, presumably to go bother someone else's breakfast.

She waited until she was alone in the waystation's back room before she opened it. This was not paranoia. Paranoia was for people who hadn't yet learned what happened to field officers who read their mail carelessly. This was simply hygiene.

Inside the case: a single data slate, biolocked to her thumbprint, and a handwritten note on cream paper that made her stomach do something complicated.

*Verhoef wants confirmation of asset recovery by end of week. No further extraction windows after first major snowfall. Handle the remainder per standing protocol.*

Rook read it twice.

*Handle the remainder.*

There were four people who knew about the chest. Two of them were Dunmore and her navigator, currently somewhere above the tree line with a head start she was no longer certain she'd been permitted to close. One of them was her lieutenant, Deverell, who was currently downstairs eating the waystation out of dried sausage and complaining about his boots. And one of them was Ilse Rook herself, holding a note that used the word *remainder* the way a butcher used the word *trimmings*.

She sat down on the bunk, which complained under her, and pulled up the slate.

The encryption took ninety seconds to peel. That was the first wrong thing. Priority One traffic from Verhoef's office usually came wrapped in layers you needed an afternoon and a grudge to get through. Ninety seconds meant either sloppiness or a message that somebody wanted found. Rook had been in this business long enough to know that both possibilities were worse than they sounded.

The file contained two documents.

The first was a routing order—the operational instruction she'd been expecting. Recover the orrery. Recover the ledger. Terminate exposure. Standard language, standard coldness, the Syndicate's house style, which treated human beings as line items and violence as a rounding error. She'd signed off on orders phrased exactly this way eleven times in nine years. She could probably still recite three of them from memory, which was not a thing she thought about during daylight hours.

The second document was not standard anything.

It was correspondence—actual correspondence, names attached, signatures at the bottom—from the Syndicate's client of record. Not Vane. Vane was a name on a door, a logo on invoices, a fiction maintained so that the actual money could stay invisible. Rook had known this the way you know your employer's books are cooked: abstractly, professionally, without ever being handed the recipe.

Now she was holding the recipe.

The client was a trust. The trust was administered by a family. And the family name was one she recognized from history modules she'd taken twenty years ago and never expected to use: Aldermere-Visser. The architects of the Annexation. The men whose signatures had erased Karstia from every atlas on the continent in a single bureaucratic afternoon—a nation deleted with the stroke of a pen, its archives seized, its records scattered, its name declared a clerical error.

The correspondence was brief and businesslike. It thanked the Syndicate for its discretion over the years. It noted, with what Rook could only describe as a lawyer's version of nervousness, that a restitution tribunal had been convened in the lowlands, scheduled for spring, with international observers and subpoena power. It noted further that the continued existence of any authentic Karstian archive would constitute, quote, "an existential legal liability." It requested, therefore, that all recovered materials be destroyed rather than delivered, and that destruction be certified.

Destroyed rather than delivered.

Rook set the slate down on the bunk beside her and looked at it for a long moment, the way you look at a dog that has just bitten someone for the first time.

Nine years. Nine years of recoveries, extractions, negotiations, and the occasional regrettable necessity, and in all that time she had operated on a simple professional understanding: the Syndicate acquired things for people who wanted them. Priceless things. Dangerous things. Things that got locked in vaults or sold to collectors or traded between governments. The merchandise changed hands; it did not burn. Burning was for amateurs and fanatics. Burning destroyed value, and value was the entire point.

But this wasn't about value. She understood that now, reading the Aldermere-Visser letters a third time with the particular clarity that arrives around six-thirty in the morning when you haven't slept properly and the coffee was terrible. This wasn't acquisition. This was evidence management. Somewhere in the lowlands, lawyers were sharpening pencils, and a family that had gotten rich deleting a country was paying the Syndicate—not to find the proof that Karstia had existed, but to make sure no one else ever did.

She thought about the chest in the river willows. She thought about the ledger full of names. She'd skimmed it once, early on, before Dunmore went to ground with it—rows of custodians, generations of them, ordinary people who had agreed to keep a nation's memory alive in cellars and attics and the backs of their minds, knowing what it would cost them if they were caught. She remembered thinking, at the time, that it read less like an inventory than a congregation.

And she remembered the letter addressed to *the Dunmore line*, and how she'd filed that away as leverage, because leverage was how she thought, and had not let herself wonder what kind of family spent seventy years passing down an obligation like a relay baton through fire.

*Handle the remainder.*

Deverell knocked—two raps, his knock, the one that meant *I have food and opinions*. She closed the slate.

"Come."

He came in carrying two bowls of something the waystation cook insisted was stew, and he set one on the bunk beside her and lowered himself onto the room's only chair, which groaned in solidarity with the bunk. Deverell was a large man built along the lines of a well-fed wardrobe, with a face that suggested he'd been designed for kindness and then repurposed.

"You look," he said, "like a woman who's read her mail."

"I've read my mail."

"The boots thing stands, by the way. I want it minuted that I raised the boots issue before we went to altitude and was told, and I quote, 'they'll do.'"

"They'll do," Rook said absently.

"They absolutely will not do. There's a hole in the left one you could post a letter through. If I lose toes to frostbite, I'm invoicing the Syndicate, and I want you to know I've already drafted the invoice. It's very funny. There's a section for emotional damages."

"Deverell."

He stopped, spoon halfway to his mouth. In nine years she had learned every register of his voice, and he had learned hers, and he heard whatever it was in hers now, because he put the spoon down.

"What is it?"

She considered, for perhaps four seconds, telling him nothing. Keeping it in the vault where she kept everything else—the things she'd done, the things she'd signed, the faces she occasionally saw in the space between waking and sleep. Four seconds. Then she slid the slate across the bunk to him.

"Read the second file."

He read it the way he read everything, slowly, lips moving slightly, a habit that should have disqualified him from intelligence work and somehow never had. She watched his face change. It didn't collapse or harden; it simply went quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important dies in the next building.

"They want it burned," he said finally.

"They want it burned and certified."

"But the archive—that's—" He gestured vaguely, a big man reaching for a word too large for his hands. "That's a whole country. That's everything they wrote down. Everything they kept. You don't burn that. That's not a *thing*, that's—" He gave up on the abstraction and landed on the concrete, which was where Deverell always landed. "There are photographs in there. Probably. Letters. People's handwriting."

"People's handwriting," Rook agreed.

"And the Dunmore woman. And her partner. *Remainder.*" He said the word like it tasted bad. "That's us, isn't it? We're the remainder. Or we're the ones doing the remaining."

"That depends entirely on our performance review."

He looked at her. She looked at the window, where the Escarpment stood up against the sky with its new white cap, beautiful in the way that large indifferent things are beautiful.

"Say something, boss."

"I'm saying something. I'm looking at a mountain."

"You're stalling."

"I am *thinking*," Rook said, "which looks identical from the outside, and I'd thank you not to confuse them in front of the staff."

But he was right, and they both knew it, and the thing she was actually thinking was this:

For nine years, Ilse Rook had told herself a story about her work. It was a good story, well-constructed, load-bearing. The story went: *I recover property. The property belongs to people who can afford me. What those people do with it afterward is not my department.* It was the same story every person in her line of work told, refined over centuries, polished smooth as a river stone, and it worked precisely because you never, ever looked underneath it.

She was looking underneath it now. Underneath was a spring tribunal, and a family named Aldermere-Visser, and a nation that had been not conquered but *cancelled*—struck from the record so thoroughly that children born in Halvett grew up denying the old names of their own streets. And underneath that was a woman named Freya Dunmore, drowning in debt, running weather rigs across flatland for syndicate money, who had spent her whole life being invisible because visibility had killed her parents—and whom Rook had been hunting across half a mountain range to deliver, along with a nation's entire memory, into a furnace.

*No witnesses,* the earlier order had said. She'd read it without flinching. She flinched now, remembering not flinching.

"Boss," Deverell said quietly. "What do we do?"

It was, she reflected, the first time in nine years he had asked her that question in a voice that genuinely didn't know the answer. Usually he asked it rhetorically, the way you ask *what's for dinner*. This was different. This was a man standing on a fault line, feeling the ground move, asking whether the building was still safe.

And here was the trouble, the real trouble, the trouble she'd carry up the mountain with her if she carried anything: she didn't know. Loyalty wasn't a switch; it was a habit, worn deep as a footpath. Nine years of habit said *follow the order, certify the burn, collect the fee, tell yourself the story again tonight until it holds*. But habits, she was beginning to understand, could be broken by a single piece of paper if the paper was honest enough.

A family wanted a country's memory burned before the world could look at it. A woman and her partner were climbing toward that memory right now, through snow, on a route some archivist's granddaughter had sold them, carrying a brass instrument that apparently *wanted* to be found. And Ilse Rook, who had never hesitated in her professional life, was sitting on a bunk in a waystation smelling of boiled mutton, hesitating.

"We do our jobs," she said at last, and watched Deverell's shoulders drop half an inch. "We go up the mountain tomorrow, first light. We follow the route. We find them."

"And then?"

She looked at the slate. At the cream-colored note with its butcher's vocabulary. At the snowline, lower this evening than it had been that morning.

"And then," Rook said, "we'll see what the weather does."

Deverell blinked at her. "That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed, standing, pulling her coat off the hook. "It isn't. It's the most honest thing I've said in nine years, and I suggest you get used to the sensation, because I have a feeling there's more of it coming."

Outside, the wind had turned. It came down off the Escarpment carrying the smell of snow and old stone, and it pressed against the waystation walls like a hand testing a door, and Ilse Rook stood in it for a long moment, letting it push at her, thinking about fault lines—geological and otherwise—and how the ground beneath a person only ever seemed solid right up until the moment it decided, all at once, to move.

Tomorrow they climbed. Whatever she was going to be when she reached the top—loyal soldier, reluctant witness, or something with no name yet—she would find out on the ice.

Above her, far up in the white silence, two small figures were already moving toward the col, unaware that the woman following them had just stopped being certain whose side the mountain was on.
