## 17. The Last Ascent

Mirela's map ended at a pencil line drawn across the shoulder of the mountain, and beneath it, in her grandfather's cramped hand, a single word: *Kraj.* End.

Tomas held the paper up against the wind so I could see it, and the wind took that moment to try to peel him off the mountainside like a sticker from a jar.

"That's it?" he shouted. "That's all we get? One word?"

"It's more than most maps give you," I shouted back. "Most maps don't even admit this mountain exists."

He looked at me the way he always did when I said something technically true and emotionally useless, which is to say he looked at me like family. Then he folded the paper into his inner pocket—the one closest to his heart, because Tomas had a sense of drama he'd deny under oath—and clipped his carabiner to the anchor line.

Above us, the Escarpment's final wall rose four hundred meters of black rock and blue ice, and threading through it, visible even to eyes that hadn't spent ten years learning to read the sky, was the shear.

I want to be clear about what wind shear is, because people think it means *windy*. Windy is weather. Shear is argument. It's two currents of air moving at different speeds or directions, stacked on top of each other like guests at a wedding who hate each other, and everything caught between them gets torn. Clouds shred themselves into rags. Birds turn around. On the ridge above us, spindrift was lifting off the icefield in long horizontal banners—not falling snow, not blowing snow, but snow being *smuggled* sideways by a current it couldn't argue with.

Ten years ago, I would have turned back. Five years ago, I would have turned back but hated myself for it. Two years ago, standing in a flooded river bottom holding a chest with my grandmother's nation inside it, I wouldn't have had a choice at all—because Rook's team was behind us, the bridge behind *that* was smoke, and the only direction left was up.

But here's the thing nobody tells you about running: you get good at it. You get so good at it that one day you look at a wall of sheared cloud and you don't see an obstacle. You see a schedule. A structure. A thing with seams.

"Freya." Tomas's voice, closer now, pitched under the wind rather than through it. "Talk to me. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you're doing math and the math is winning."

I almost laughed. The cold made laughing expensive—it cost heat you couldn't spare—but I paid it anyway, because some things are worth the exchange rate.

"The shear's layered," I said. "Three bands. Low current's coming off the western valley, maybe forty knots, steady. Middle band's the jet remnant, west-northwest, faster, and it's pulsing—see how the banners on the ridge go long, then short, then long? That's a rhythm. Maybe ninety seconds on, thirty off."

"And the top?"

I squinted up at the summit cap, where the clouds were doing something I'd only ever seen over supercells in flat country: rotating. Slowly. Politely. Like a door deciding whether to open.

"The top is the top," I said. "It'll do what it wants. But if we climb in the gaps between pulses—if we move when the banners shorten—we're not fighting the mountain. We're reading it."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. When I glanced at him, he had that expression he got during river fords and other near-death experiences, the one that meant he was about to say something kind and cutting at the same time.

"You know what you just described," he said. "That's what you used to do when we were chasing. Except backwards."

"How do you mean?"

"When we chased storms, you read them to stay ahead of them. To keep moving before they arrived." He tapped his temple. "Now you're reading one to arrive."

I didn't have an answer for that, mostly because it was true, and true things said out loud have a way of rearranging your insides. So I checked his harness instead, tugged the knots the way he'd taught me years ago—*trust but verify, Freya, trust is a feeling, verification is a skill*—and pointed at the first seam in the rock.

"Move on my mark," I said. "When the banners drop."

* * *

We climbed the way I imagine smugglers climb, which is to say in stolen moments, in the pauses between attention.

The first hundred meters were honest work—rock, ice, the ordinary hostility of altitude. Tomas went first, placing anchors, and I followed, clearing the line, and we moved in the rhythm I'd called: ninety seconds of stillness while the shear screamed overhead, thirty seconds of motion when it exhaled. It was like climbing inside a heartbeat. The whole mountain pulsed around us, and we lived in its diastole.

Somewhere around the second pitch, my hands started remembering things my brain had filed away as useless. The way pressure drops feel in your teeth before a downdraft. The smell of ozone that isn't lightning yet, just lightning thinking about it. My grandmother's voice, absurdly, saying something in Karstian I didn't understand anymore but whose *tone* I knew—the tone she'd used checking the sky over the orchard, the tone that meant *we have time, but not much.*

I'd spent ten years using those instincts to leave places. Reading a sky to know when to fold the tents, when to skip town ahead of a creditor or a flood or a season. Every skill I had was a skill of departure. And now here I was, four hundred meters of vertical ice below a vault containing everything my family had ever been, using those same instincts to *arrive*, and the strangeness of it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"Banner's dropping," Tomas called down. "Mark."

I moved.

* * *

The trouble came at three hundred meters, because trouble keeps a schedule too, and its schedule never once has coincided with mine.

The middle band shifted. Not dramatically—that was the problem. If it had shifted dramatically, I'd have felt it the way you feel a door slam. Instead it rotated fifteen degrees, quiet as a liar, and suddenly the pulse I'd been timing wasn't a pulse anymore. It was two pulses, interleaved, and the gap I'd been climbing through began to close like a book.

I was on the ice field when I noticed—a broad blue slope, forty degrees, beautiful and featureless—and the spindrift banners above me went long and stayed long, and stayed long, and did not shorten.

"Tomas." My voice came out flatter than I wanted. "The rhythm's gone."

"How gone?"

"Gone gone. The middle band's turning. We've got—" I watched the ridge, counting, watching the ragged clouds tear and re-tear themselves. "Maybe four minutes before the shear comes down to us. Maybe less. The surface layer's going to hit this slope at sixty knots and everything not anchored becomes a kite."

"I'm a very bad kite," Tomas said. "No aerodynamics whatsoever."

"Tomas."

"I'm anchoring. Talk me through it—you're the one who reads these things. Where does it come down first?"

I looked up. This was the part—the part where ten years of running either meant something or didn't. Because here was the truth I'd never said out loud, not to him, not to anyone: I hadn't survived a decade of storms by being brave. I'd survived by knowing where storms *didn't want to go*. Every escape, every midnight departure, every burned bridge behind me—I'd always found the seam. The low ground the flood ignored. The county road the cell split around. The gap.

"West side of the slope," I said. "See the rock rib, the dark stripe? The incoming flow's going to funnel down the center of the ice field—it'll follow the fall line, it always follows the fall line—but the rib breaks the flow. There'll be turbulence behind it, ugly, but slower. Ugly and slow beats fast and dead."

"So we cross left."

"We cross left, fast, before the pulse reaches the rib. And Tomas—" I grabbed the rope, felt him take my weight through the anchor, felt the whole simple grammar of it: *I hold you, you hold me, neither of us is alone on this mountain.* "If we get separated, you go to the rib. You don't wait. You don't look for me. The vault doesn't need both of us. It needs one of us alive and stubborn."

He was quiet for exactly one second, which for Tomas is a speech.

"Noted," he said. "And now I'm going to ignore it, because you've been giving me instructions I'm allowed to ignore since the day we met, and honestly it's the foundation of our whole partnership."

Then the shear came down.

* * *

I have been inside a supercell's inflow. I have stood close enough to a tornado to hear it chewing. None of it prepared me for the specific insult of sixty-knot air arriving horizontally at eight thousand feet, because at least a tornado is honest about wanting to kill you. The shear just picked up the entire surface of the mountain and threw it at us sideways, a white freight train made of our own footprints' snow.

We crossed anyway. Forty meters of sloped ice with the world erased, Tomas ahead of me on the rope, both of us bent nearly double, driving ice tools in blind and trusting the placement, trusting each other, trusting the arithmetic I'd done in my head because there was nothing else to trust. Once, the wind lifted my boots clean off the slope and I hung from the rope for three full seconds, weightless, ridiculous, a middle-aged woman being dangled by her best friend over a blue void—and then it set me down again, almost gently, like it had just been checking.

The rib took us in. Behind its black spine of rock the roar dropped to something merely apocalyptic, and we stood in the lee, gasping, plastered white, looking like a pair of badly made snowmen who had opinions.

Tomas pulled his balaclava down. His eyebrows were entirely frozen, which gave him the expression of a skeptical caterpillar.

"Reading it to arrive," he said. "You absolute maniac. It worked."

"Don't celebrate yet." But I was grinning, I realized. Actually grinning, cheeks cracking, at eight thousand feet in a wind that had just tried to post me to another country. "One pitch left. The summit cap's still rotating, but it's slow—look at it. That's not a storm organizing. That's a storm leaving. We've got a window, and the window closes at dusk whether we're through it or not."

"Mirela's map says *kraj*," Tomas offered.

"Mirela's grandfather never climbed this high. He drew the end of his knowledge, not the end of the route." I looked up at the last pitch—the headwall, steep, black, threaded with old fixed lines someone had placed decades ago and the mountain had half-forgotten. "From here, we're the map."

* * *

The last pitch took two hours and cost me everything I had, and I mean that in the good way.

There's a particular clarity that arrives near the top of a hard climb, when your body has no surplus left for anything but the next move, and the mind—freed of its usual committee meetings—goes strangely quiet and strangely generous. Somewhere around the third bolt of ancient fixed line, my hands moving on autopilot, my breath timed to the rope, I stopped being afraid.

Not brave. Brave implies a choice. Just—unafraid, the way you're unafraid of a room you finally understand.

Because here is what I understood, hanging on a rusted line driven into Karstian rock by hands I'd never meet: every year I ran, I thought I was fleeing. The debts, the Syndicate, the name I didn't use, the parents whose grave I visited in a different surname's coat. I thought fear was the engine. But fear doesn't hold a rhythm for ninety seconds and thirty. Fear doesn't find the seam. Fear doesn't cross a slope in a whiteout because it did the math and trusted the math and trusted the man on the other end of the rope.

What I'd been calling flight was practice. Ten years of it. Every escape had been a rehearsal for this ascent, and I hadn't known, and nobody had told me, and I laughed again—expensive, worth it—into the screaming wind, because the joke was on me and it was a good joke and I'd finally gotten it.

"Freya!" Tomas, below me, anchored, watching my progress with his whole face. "Status!"

"I'm fine!" I called down. "Better than fine! I think I've been training for this my whole life without meaning to!"

"That's either profound or hypoxia!"

"Both! Probably both!"

* * *

We topped out at 4:47 in the afternoon, with the light going amber and long, and the summit plateau opened before us like a held breath finally released.

It wasn't a peak, exactly. It was a shoulder of the Escarpment, a broad shelf of wind-scoured ice ringed by higher ridgelines, and in its center, according to the orrery—which had begun, in the last hour, to tick. Audibly. Through its case. Through my pack. Like a clock that had spent eighty years waiting for the right longitude and had finally, politely, cleared its throat.

Tomas set the anchors and belayed me onto the flat, and then we stood together in the gold light, steam pouring off us, the whole lowlands spread out below in haze and distance, and the orrery ticking away in my pack like a small brass heart.

"Off-route," Tomas said quietly. He nodded toward the eastern edge of the plateau, where the ice dipped into a shadowed declivity between two ridges. "That's where the needle pointed. Not the summit. The station."

Station Kestrel. The dead station. The place the chest wanted to be found—or so I'd started believing somewhere around the second pitch, and believing it had felt less like superstition than like reading a chart.

I knelt and opened the pack. The orrery lay in its nest of wool, brass arms trembling, its central needle swung hard east and steady as a compass at the pole. And etched into the frost on its base—frost that shouldn't have formed a pattern, frost that had no business forming *letters*—was a single word in Karstian script.

I couldn't read it. But I knew its shape. I'd seen it on the chest, on the ledger, on my grandmother's letter, and Mirela had sounded it out for me once in Halvett's back room, her hand shaking slightly:

*Doma.*

Home.

Tomas crouched beside me. For a long moment neither of us spoke. Below us the shadows were filling the valleys like water filling a basin, and somewhere down there, Ilse Rook was climbing with a recovered orrery's twin and orders she no longer believed in, and somewhere above us the summit cap turned its slow, polite rotation, and the window was closing, and none of it mattered as much as the small brass machine pointing east, saying the only word it knew.

"Well," Tomas said at last, and his voice was rough. "Shall we go home, then?"

I stood. I shouldered the pack. And the last of the fear—the last of ten years of it—went out of me like heat out of an open door, replaced by something older and heavier and infinitely more useful.

"Yes," I said. "Before the light goes. Move fast, watch the ice—and Tomas?"

"Yeah?"

"If anything's waiting for us down there besides answers—" I checked the rope, tugged his knots, trust but verify, always. "I'm glad it's you it's waiting with."

He grinned, frozen eyebrows and all. "Same," he said. "Now let's go meet your grandmother's ghost."

The orrery ticked. The light failed. We walked east into the blue shadow of the plateau, toward Station Kestrel, toward whatever had waited eighty years in the ice for someone stubborn enough to arrive.
