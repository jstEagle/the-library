## 18. The Vault Beneath the Ice

Tomas had carried a lot of things in his life—a duffel bag of other men's sins across two borders, a broken wrist through a blizzard, Freya's fury for three days without once setting it down—but he had never carried a nation. That was the thought that kept circling him as they climbed the last pitch toward the fissure, the orrery wrapped in his spare shirt and strapped to his back like a sleeping child.

Freya climbed ahead of him, her injured arm bound tight against her ribs, using her good hand and both boots with a stubbornness that would have been admirable if it weren't going to get her killed. The storm two days ago had taken something out of her—taken something out of both of them, honestly—but it had also, in the strange arithmetic of mountains, given something back. She moved differently now. Not like a woman running. Like a woman arriving.

"Left side," she called down, voice half-swallowed by wind. "The needle said left side of the twin spires. Tomas, tell me the needle said left side."

"The needle said left side," he confirmed, though privately he'd been having doubts about the needle ever since it had started pointing at things with the serene confidence of a compass that had read a book about compasses.

The fissure announced itself before they saw it—a breath of warmer air, smelling faintly of stone and old paper, rising out of the mountain like the building itself was exhaling. Then the ice parted around a seam no wider than a door, and there it was: a crack in the escarpment, sheltered by an overhang of blue glacier, invisible from below, invisible from above, visible only to someone standing exactly where a dead Karstian surveyor had decided, decades ago, that someone should stand.

"Okay," Tomas said quietly. "That's unsettling."

"That's engineering," Freya said, but her voice had gone soft in a way he'd learned to distrust, because it was the voice she used when she was frightened and refusing to say so.

They'd taken the orrery off Rook's fallen lieutenant three days and one lifetime ago. The man—Voss, Rook had called him, in a voice that held something Tomas didn't want to examine too closely—had gone into a crevasse with most of their dignity and all of their spare rope, and the brass instrument had come out of his pack scuffed but whole. Since then it had behaved less like a machine and more like a member of the party. It hummed near the fault lines. It warmed in Tomas's hands at night when the temperature dropped, which he chose to attribute to residual heat and not to anything the chest's dead woman might have wanted them to feel.

At the fissure's mouth, set into the rock, was a socket. Round. Brass-rimmed. Exactly the diameter of the orrery's base, because of course it was.

Freya looked at Tomas. Tomas looked at Freya. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the spires with a sound like a long inhale.

"You want to do the honors?" she asked.

"It's your inheritance."

"It's *our* inheritance, and I've been doing the honors since chapter one. Your turn."

He unslung the orrery, and its weight surprised him—had always surprised him, heavier than brass had any right to be, as though it were packed not just with gears but with consequence. He knelt in the snow at the mouth of the mountain and set it into the socket, and for a moment nothing happened, and he felt the specific embarrassment of a man who has just knelt in the snow for nothing.

Then the needle swung. Once, twice, and settled pointing straight down.

The mountain answered.

It was not dramatic. That was the thing Tomas would remember afterward, trying to describe it to people who hadn't been there—people who would eventually exist, people who would read about this in documents Tomas was about to spend the afternoon weeping over. It was not dramatic. There was a sound like a lock the size of a valley turning over, felt more in the boots than the ears, and the ice at the back of the fissure shivered, and a seam opened where no seam had been, and warm air rolled out carrying the smell of paper and lamp oil and, faintly, impossibly, lavender.

"Somebody's been down there," Freya whispered. "Recently."

"Somebody's been *keeping* it," Tomas said. "There's a difference."

* * *

The passage went down in a spiral, cut into the living rock, with niches every few meters holding stubs of candles and, in one, a tin cup and a folded note in Karstian that Freya read aloud in a voice that wobbled on the second word: *"If you have come this far, you are either a friend or a very thorough thief. Both are welcome. The friends will understand why."*

"She had a sense of humor," Tomas said.

"She had a *vault*," Freya said. "The humor is a bonus."

The spiral opened without warning into a chamber, and Tomas stopped, and Freya stopped, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing in either of their vocabularies that fit.

The room was the size of a chapel and organized like a library and lit, when Freya found the switch and the old generator caught with a cough and a roar, like something between a cathedral and a very well-run pantry. Shelves rose to the ceiling on every wall—wooden shelves, hand-built, each one labeled in careful Karstian script. And on the shelves: documents. Thousands of them. Bundled correspondence tied with string. Photographs in sleeves, thousands of photographs, faces looking out at them from a country that no longer appeared on maps—weddings, harvests, schoolrooms, a man laughing in front of a shop with a sign in a language Tomas had never seen written but somehow recognized the shape of, the way you recognize a relative's profile in a stranger's face.

There were recordings—wax cylinders and early tape reels, hundreds of them, labeled by hand. There were maps. There were ledgers. There was a child's drawing pinned to one shelf, a house with too many windows and a sun with a smile, and beneath it in pencil: *our house, before.*

Freya walked forward slowly, the way you walk in a museum, or a church, or the apartment of someone who has just died. She stopped at the nearest shelf and read the label and made a sound that Tomas had never heard her make—not a sob, not a laugh, something between, the sound a person makes when the ground proves solid after all.

"Vital records," she read. "Births, marriages, deaths. 1871 through the Annexation." She turned to the next shelf. "Land registries. Court transcripts." Next. "The parliamentary record. Complete. They took the parliament, Tomas. They burned the capital's archive. This is—" She stopped. Started again. "This is the only copy."

Tomas had drifted to the photographs. He didn't know why that was where his feet took him, except that faces have always made more sense to him than paper. A woman in a baker's apron, flour on her cheek, squinting into sunlight. Two boys on a bicycle built for one. An old man holding a fish the size of his torso, proud as a general. An entire town assembled on a church steps, and every single person looking at the camera as if the camera were a promise.

"They knew," he said. His voice came out rough. "Whoever built this. They knew what was coming. They didn't hide the gold, Freya. They didn't hide the leaders or the weapons. They hid the *evidence*. They hid the fact that people lived here."

"Because you can kill a country twice," Freya said quietly. "Once with soldiers. Once with maps."

Tomas picked up a photograph at random—a girl on a dock, maybe ten years old, holding up a fish that was clearly her first, grinning with the whole force of her face. On the back, in pencil: *Mirela, age 9, Lake Ostro. Grandfather says I held it wrong.* He stared at it for a long time.

"Freya."

She came over. She read it. She went very still.

"Mirela," she said. "Halvett. The archivist's granddaughter."

"The archivist's *son's* daughter. Her grandfather's the one who kept the route." Tomas turned the photograph over again, as if the pencil might rearrange itself. "Freya, her family didn't just remember Karstia. Her family *filed* it."

* * *

They worked for hours. There was no other reasonable response to a room like this; you worked. Freya moved down the shelves cataloguing aloud, her voice steadying with each label, the researcher in her rising up through the exhaustion like a plant through snow. Tomas carried boxes to the center table and opened them and read the first page of things and had to put them down and walk a little and come back.

It was on one of these walks that he found the recording booth—a corner of the chamber with a microphone, a reel-to-reel, and a stack of tapes labeled in the same careful hand as everything else, but these labels were different. Not categories. Names. Hundreds of names, each one a tape.

He threaded one at random and pressed play.

A man's voice filled the chamber, old and accented, speaking Karstian, and then—clearly for the benefit of whoever might someday listen—in careful, learned English:

*"My name is Anton Brez. I was a schoolteacher in Vell for thirty-one years. I am recording this so that someone, someday, knows that we were here. Not what was done to us. That we were here. There is a difference, and I have learned it the hard way, and it has cost me everything, and I would pay it again."*

Tomas stopped the tape. He found he couldn't listen to more of it standing up. He sat down on a crate of maps and listened to Anton Brez talk about his students for eleven minutes, and when it ended he sat there in the hum of the generator and understood, finally and completely, what Freya had been trying to say at the col, what she'd been circling since the river:

This was never an inheritance. You don't inherit a thing like this. A thing like this is a *custody*. It comes with obligations attached, the way a river comes with banks. The Dunmores hadn't been left a treasure. They'd been left a *duty*, and the letter in the chest had been not a bequest but a summons.

Freya found him there. She didn't say anything at first. She just sat down next to him on the crate, shoulder to shoulder, and looked out at the shelves, at the thousands of lives filed in careful order by people who had known they were about to become a rumor.

"My grandmother's name is in the custodian ledger," Freya said at last. "I kept thinking—mine, ours, the Dunmore line. I kept thinking it was about *us*." She laughed, wet and unsteady. "It was never about us. We're just the next pair of hands. The chest didn't come to us because we're special. It came to us because the river moved and we were the ones who didn't walk away."

"Speaking of hands," Tomas said, "we should talk about what happens now. Because I've been doing arithmetic, and I'm not good at much, but I'm good at that. We can't carry this out. There's ten tons of paper on these shelves. We can't leave it, because Rook knows about the station, and Rook knows about the col, and it won't take her people long to work out where the needle was pointing. And we can't—"

He stopped. Because Freya had gone still in a new way, a way he recognized from the river, from the moment before she decided something.

"The tribunal," she said. "Mirela said there's a restitution tribunal convening in spring. In the lowlands. With jurisdiction." She stood up, and Tomas watched the last of the fugitive go out of her posture, replaced by something he'd only ever seen in her eyes when she was reading a storm: total, terrifying clarity. "We don't move the archive. We seal it. We make it so the mountain keeps it better than we could—and then we bring back the people with the legal authority to open it. Witnesses. Judges. Cameras, if we can get them."

"That means going back down. Through Rook. Through the passes. Through—"

"Through everything. Yes." She was already moving toward the shelves, pulling specific documents—the parliamentary record, the land registries, a sampling of Brez's tapes—with the efficiency of a woman who had spent her whole life deciding what to save from a storm. "We take proof. Enough proof to bring them back with shovels and subpoenas. The rest stays with the mountain."

"The mountain," Tomas said, "has been a mixed ally this week."

"The mountain," Freya said, threading a tape into her pack with hands that only shook a little, "has kept this room for eighty years. The mountain is the most trustworthy party involved."

He laughed. It surprised him, the laugh, coming up out of him sideways, and then she laughed too, and for a moment they were just two exhausted people sitting in a nation's attic, laughing at the size of what they'd found, because the alternative was crying and they'd done their ration of that in the snow cave and it hadn't fixed anything.

* * *

It was Freya who heard it first—of course it was, her ears were instruments the Syndicate had never managed to repossess. She went quiet mid-sentence, head tilted, and Tomas felt the laugh die in his own chest as he listened.

Boots. On the spiral stair. More than one pair. Moving carefully, the way people move who have been trained to move carefully and have decided, just now, to stop bothering.

Freya killed the generator. The chamber went dark except for one emergency lamp they'd found, and in its small circle Tomas saw her look at him, and he saw what was in her face, and it wasn't fear. It was the look of a woman doing final calculations on a storm that had finally arrived.

"How many?" she breathed.

"Three. Maybe four. Heavy on the left foot—mountain boots, not patrol issue."

"Rook's?"

"Rook doesn't do patrol issue."

The footsteps stopped. Somewhere above them, at the mouth of the fissure, a voice called down into the dark—muffled by stone, but clear enough in its one word, the one word that made Freya grab his arm and Tomas's whole body go cold.

It was his own name.

And then, after a pause long enough to hold a whole erased country, a second word, in a woman's voice he knew:

"*Tomas. It's Rook. We need to talk about my orders.*"
