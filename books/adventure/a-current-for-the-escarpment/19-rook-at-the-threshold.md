## 19. Rook at the Threshold

The vault breathed cold.

That was the first thing Freya noticed when the awe wore off enough for her to notice anything at all. The chamber beneath the ice held its own weather—a slow exhalation of air that had been sealed away since before she was born, carrying the smell of paper and lamp oil and something faintly floral, like a drawer of dried lavender in a house where no one had lived for thirty years.

Tomas stood hip-deep in documents. Literally hip-deep; he'd waded into the shelving rows like a man crossing a river, hands out, touching spines and tin boxes and rolled maps as if to confirm they were solid. "Freya," he kept saying, in different tones each time, like a man trying out words in a language he was only just learning. "Freya. There are *recordings*. Wax cylinders. Someone catalogued them by season."

"Spring through autumn, 1938 through '41." She'd found the index within ten minutes, because indexes were her native country. "The last entry is winter. It just says: *continuing.*"

Tomas went quiet at that. He picked up one of the cylinders—wrapped in waxed cloth, labeled in a careful hand—and held it the way you'd hold a sleeping animal.

Freya let herself have ninety more seconds of it. Ninety seconds of standing inside proof that Karstia had existed, had counted its rainfall and its weddings and its bad harvests, had bothered to write it all down knowing—knowing, by the end—that no one would ever read it. Then she made herself do the arithmetic she always did, because arithmetic had kept her alive this long.

One entrance. One fissure, barely wide enough for the orrery's case. Two of them, exhausted, half a ration tin between them. And somewhere below them on the mountain, three people from the Vane Syndicate who had lost them two days ago and would not stay lost.

"We can't move any of it," she said. "Not even one box. If we carry anything out, we're carrying a target, and we're slower than we've ever been."

"I know." Tomas set the cylinder down with exaggerated gentleness, as though it might resent being put back. "I hate it, but I know."

"So we seal it. We mark it. Mirela's coordinates, the station logs, everything—we write it down in three copies, and we—" 

The light changed.

It wasn't dramatic. That was what her body understood before her mind did: the grey glow coming down the fissure simply shifted, as if a cloud had crossed the sun, except they were underground and there was no sun. Freya turned toward the entrance and saw a silhouette standing in it, blocking the pale daylight, and every muscle she owned decided at once that running was an excellent idea and also impossible.

"Don't," said Ilse Rook.

* * *

She came down into the chamber the way she did everything—efficiently, without wasted motion, boots finding the ice-slick rock as if she'd rehearsed it. Which, Freya supposed, she probably had. Rook carried a pistol low against her thigh, not raised, not holstered. Present. A punctuation mark waiting for its sentence.

Her lieutenant wasn't with her. Neither was the other one—the big quiet man whose name Freya had never learned and whose face she'd last seen at the bottom of a glacier chute, groaning philosophically about his ankle.

"Alone?" Freya asked.

"For now." Rook's voice echoed oddly in the vault, flattened by all that paper. Paper ate sound the way it ate time. "My team is at the col. I told them I was scouting the approach." A pause, precise as a ledger line. "That was eleven hours ago."

Tomas had drifted, without appearing to drift, until he stood between Rook and the deepest row of shelves. Freya loved him a little for it, and also wanted to tell him that if it came to shooting, his body was not going to stop a bullet any better than his principles would.

"You found it," Rook said. Her eyes moved across the chamber—not covetously, Freya noticed. Inventorily. Counting exits, counting weight, counting the thing itself the way you'd count a bridge you'd been ordered to blow. "The Syndicate has spent forty years and, conservatively, nine million looking for this room. My predecessor spent nineteen of those years. He died in Halvett of a heart attack, still convinced it was under the church." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite humor, but adjacent to it. "It was never under the church."

"No," Freya said. "It was under the weather. Where nobody sensible looks."

"And you found it by accident, following a flood, because your partner can't leave strange things alone." Rook looked at Tomas then, and there was something almost warm in it, the way there is warmth in a stove you're deciding whether to throw someone into. "You pulled a chest out of a willow root because it looked lonely."

"It did look lonely," Tomas said, unrepentant.

"That," said Rook, "is either the stupidest or the most important decision anyone has made in this entire affair. I haven't decided which."

The gun stayed low. Freya watched it the way she watched a sky full of energy—tracking the pressure, reading the small tells. Rook's weight was on her heels. Her breathing was even but shallow, the breathing of someone who hadn't slept. Her left glove had a tear along the thumb, freshly sewn with black thread, crooked. Ilse Rook, who pressed her shirts, had sewn her own glove badly.

People falling apart showed it in the seams first. Freya had learned that about storms and about herself, and now, apparently, about syndicate enforcers.

"Your orders," Freya said. Not a question.

"My orders are to secure the archive and eliminate loose ends." Rook said it flatly, the way you read a shipping manifest. Then, because she was Ilse Rook and precision was her religion, she added: "*Loose ends* is doing a great deal of work in that sentence. It includes the documents. All of them. Fire does not distinguish between a witness and a witness's paperwork."

"They want it burned." Freya felt it settle over her, cold and clarifying, like the moment a storm cell finally organizes on radar. "Not stolen. Burned. Because a stolen archive can resurface. A burned one can't testify."

Rook said nothing, which was its own confession.

"The tribunal convenes in spring," Freya went on, taking one step sideways—not toward Rook, not away, just sideways, keeping the geometry honest. "And there are people—old people, heirs, whatever they are—who need every scrap of proof that the Annexation was engineered to be ash before anyone can hold it up in a courtroom. And the Vane Syndicate, which I have personally financed with eleven years of my life and roughly the value of a small island, turns out to be their broom."

"You knew?" Tomas asked quietly.

"I suspected the debt was ugly. I didn't know it was *historical*." She looked back at Rook. "Did you?"

For a long moment the only sound was the vault breathing its lavender-and-paper breath.

"I signed on eight years ago," Rook said at last. "Debt recovery. Asset location. I told myself it was a job with sharp edges, and the sharp edges were other people's choices, not mine. I was very good at telling myself things." She turned the pistol slowly in her grip—not aiming it anywhere new, just turning it, like a woman worrying a ring. "Then my employers started using words like *no witnesses*, and sending them in writing, and expecting me to burn a room full of rain gauges and wedding records and—" her eyes caught on the shelf nearest her, on a child's school notebook, its cover stamped with a double eagle, "—and *homework*. And I found that the version of me who could do that was a version I had also been telling myself stories about."

"So don't," Freya said.

Rook's gaze came up, sharp. "Don't what?"

"Don't be her. Look—I'm not going to stand here and pretend I'm better than you. I've spent eleven years making myself invisible so I'd never have to answer for anything. I owe these people money I can't pay. I ran from my own name until a river handed it back to me in a chest. I am the last person alive qualified to give moral lectures." She took a breath. "But here's the difference between us, Rook, and I think you already know it. Your employers gave you exactly two options and called them both duty. I'm giving you a third."

"Which is?"

"Witness."

The word dropped into the cold air and sat there.

"You've seen the archive," Freya said. "You know what's in it. You know what your employers are. In spring there's a tribunal, and tribunals run on testimony the way engines run on fuel, and right now the only person positioned to testify about forty years of the Syndicate's hunt—the ledgers, the orders, the *no witnesses* memos—is standing in this room holding a gun. You can be the executioner they hired. Or you can be the witness they didn't. Those are the doors. There's no third one where you walk out of here clean, because nobody walks out of anything clean. But one of them has a future attached."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you shoot us, you burn forty years of a nation's existence, and you spend the rest of your life being very good at telling yourself things." Freya shrugged, and was surprised to find the shrug was almost steady. "I've tried it. The rent on that particular apartment is higher than you think."

* * *

What happened next, Freya would remember afterward not as a decision but as a sound.

Because underneath them—underneath the vault, underneath the mountain, far down in the bones of the Escarpment—there came a noise like the world clearing its throat.

Both women froze. Tomas's head came up from the index box he'd been clutching.

"That," he said, "is not good."

They climbed to the fissure together, gun forgotten or at least deprioritized, and looked out at the upper face of the pass. The early snow that had chased them for six days lay thick on the slopes above—too thick, too fast, sitting on old ice the way debt sits on a life. And the wind had come around. Freya read it instantly, the way she read everything: southwest, warming, loading the slope, and beneath their feet that deep subsonic mutter of a mountain deciding something.

"Slope above the col is at critical load," she said. Her voice came out calm, which surprised her, because inside she was doing the opposite of calm. "When it goes, it'll take the whole bowl. Everything below the cornice line."

"Including the col," Rook said. "Where my team is."

"Yes."

Rook closed her eyes for exactly one second. When she opened them, she was already someone slightly different—or perhaps, Freya thought, someone who had stopped pretending to be someone else.

"Kessler and Brandt," Rook said. "My team. Whatever else they are, they're mine." She keyed her radio, got static, tried again, got a thin crackle and half a word. She looked at the sky, then at the slope, then at Freya, and made the fastest arithmetic of her life. "They won't make the descent in time unless someone meets them at the chute and moves them off the fall line. That someone cannot be me, because someone also has to seal this entrance, and I'm the only one of us three who knows how the Syndicate seals things so that other Syndicate people can't find them again."

Freya stared at her. "You're asking us to save the people who burned a bridge behind us and shot at us at the col."

"I'm telling you what needs doing," Rook said. "Who does it is above my pay grade. Which, as of approximately four minutes ago, is nothing."

Tomas was already pulling rope off his shoulder. Of course he was. "The chute Mirela mapped comes out two hundred meters above the col," he said. "If I go now, and they're alive, I can get them moving east before it cuts loose."

"If you go now," Freya said, "you might not come back, and I—" She stopped. Swallowed the rest of the sentence, the way she'd swallowed versions of it for eleven years. Then, deliberately, she unsent it. "Come back. That's the whole order. Come back."

"Wasn't planning otherwise." He squeezed her arm once, hard, and was gone into the fissure's grey light, moving downhill with the easy long stride of a man who had spent his whole life walking toward things other people walked away from.

* * *

Ilse Rook holstered her pistol.

She did it without ceremony, without a speech, without looking at Freya—which was, Freya understood, the point. Some surrenders could only happen if nobody watched them land. She knelt at the vault's entrance and began unpacking charges from her pack: shaped, minimal, the kind that brought rock down rather than blowing it open.

"Seismic charges will collapse the fissure mouth," she said, all business again, but a different business. "The Syndicate's survey teams read collapse as geology. They've walked past a dozen sealed sites in forty years because they trusted the mountain to lie for them." She glanced up. "The mountain's about to lie very convincingly indeed."

"And in spring? When the tribunal needs this opened?"

"Then it gets opened properly, by engineers, with witnesses, with cameras." Rook seated a charge against the rock with the tenderness of a mason. "A vault that takes effort to reopen is a vault worth reopening. A vault that's ash is nothing forever." She worked in silence a moment, then added, quieter: "There's a document in there. Shelf nine, blue box, marked *Annexation Order, draft*. My grandfather's signature is on a related requisition. I'd like to be present when it's read aloud. Publicly."

Freya looked at her—at the crooked thread in the glove, at the set of her shoulders—and felt something shift in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name.

"Shelf nine," she said. "Blue box. I'll remember."

Above them, the mountain cleared its throat again, longer this time, and somewhere downslope a whistle sounded—Tomas, three sharp notes, the signal they'd agreed meant *found them, moving, hurry.*

Rook thumbed the detonator's safety cover and looked at Freya across the mouth of the vault, and for the first time since Halvett, since the smile and the offer and the trap, she smiled. It was a small thing, and tired, and real.

"When this comes down," she said, "the Syndicate will assume the mountain did their work for them. It'll buy us until spring. After that—" she nodded at the buried nation beneath their boots, "—after that, the current changes direction."

Freya put her hand over Rook's on the detonator.

"Together, then," she said. "On three."

Far below, the col erupted into white.
