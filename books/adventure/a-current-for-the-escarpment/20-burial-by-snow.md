## 20. Burial by Snow

The sound came before the mountain moved.

Tomas had spent enough years around storms to know that the loudest things in nature rarely announce themselves honestly. Lightning lies about its distance. Tornadoes hum like a refrigerator. But an avalanche — an avalanche clears its throat first, a deep percussive *whump* somewhere above them, like the Escarpment shifting its weight in a chair it had occupied for ten thousand years and intended to occupy for ten thousand more.

"Down," Freya said, and they were already down.

They had made it perhaps four hundred meters past the vault's entrance when the slope let go. Tomas pressed himself against a granite rib of rock, Freya's shoulder jammed hard into his, her breath coming fast and white in the thin air. Above them the mountainside unzipped. Snow that had sat patient since the last ice age decided, all at once, that it had somewhere to be.

It did not roar so much as *arrive* — a freight train made of everything soft in the world, pouring down the couloir they'd climbed that morning, swallowing the switchbacks, swallowing the col where Rook's lieutenant had fallen, swallowing the narrow throat of the pass itself.

Then it was over, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Tomas lifted his head. Where the pass had been there was now a smooth white rampart, fifty meters high at least, sculpted into clean curves by wind already getting to work on it. The entrance to the chamber — the fissure, the brass mechanism, the thousands of documents sleeping inside — was gone. Not destroyed. *Buried.*

Buried under forty feet of consolidated snowpack on a north-facing slope at three thousand meters, where nothing would melt until June.

"Huh," he said, because eloquence had abandoned him somewhere around the second ice age.

Freya was laughing. It took him a moment to recognize it as laughter and not hyperventilation, because she was still flat on her stomach in the scree and her laugh came out muffled, half-hysterical, shaking through her ribs into his arm.

"It's safe," she said. "Tomas. It's *safe*. We just watched the best security system in Europe install itself."

"The mountain did our job for us."

"The mountain did Rook's job for her, is what you mean, except she's standing right behind us, so."

She was. Ilse Rook stood ten meters downslope, weapon holstered — holstered, which was its own kind of miracle — with snow crusted in her gray hair and an expression Tomas could only describe as a woman watching her entire professional life get buried along with the pass. Her remaining team member, Voss, was nowhere in sight, which meant either he was dead in the debris or he'd had the good sense to be elsewhere when the mountain filed its paperwork.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The wind filled it, keening off the new rampart, carrying that particular high-altitude loneliness that Tomas had always found less frightening than clarifying. Up here you couldn't pretend the world was bigger than it was. Up here the world was exactly as big as the next ridge, and everything else was weather.

"So," Rook said finally. Her voice was hoarse. "That's that, then."

"That's that," Freya agreed.

"I have orders to retrieve or destroy the archive. The archive is now under several hundred tons of snow. I cannot retrieve it. Destroying it would require—" she gestured vaguely at the rampart, at the Escarpment, at the general concept of geology— "more than I brought."

"You brought two men and a helicopter pad's worth of arrogance," Freya said.

"One man. And I resent the characterization of my confidence as arrogance. It was *budgeted*." Rook sat down on a rock. Actually sat down, the way exhausted people do, without ceremony, her elbows on her knees. For the first time since Halvett she looked less like an enforcer and more like what Tomas suspected she'd been before the Syndicate got its hooks in: a field researcher who'd taken the wrong contract twenty years ago and never found the exit. "Do you know what my orders actually say? Verbatim?"

"Enlighten us," Freya said, not moving from where she crouched, ready to move if Rook's hand went anywhere near the holster.

"'No witnesses.'" Rook laughed, short and bitter, a sound the wind immediately stole. "That's it. That's the whole operational directive. No witnesses. Do you know how many witnesses I've been over the years? I'm very good at being no witnesses." She looked up at them, and her eyes were red-rimmed with cold or something older. "I stood in that chamber. I saw the photographs. Children in schoolyards. A whole country's tax records, wedding registers, a woman's recipe box, for God's sake. Someone catalogued a *recipe box*, Dunmore. You don't destroy a recipe box unless you're afraid of what a people might cook."

Tomas felt something loosen in his chest. He'd been carrying a knot there since the col, since the fight, since Freya went down with a gash across her forearm and he'd put himself between her and Voss with a climbing axe and no plan whatsoever. The knot didn't untie entirely. But it loosened.

"You hesitated," he said. "At the threshold. I saw it."

"I did not hesitate. I performed a tactical assessment." Rook pulled off a glove and pressed her bare hand against the rock, as if checking whether the mountain was real. "The assessment concluded that my employers are not the Syndicate. The Syndicate is a tool. My employers are the grandchildren of the men who signed the Annexation papers, and they are terrified of a tribunal convening in April, and they sent me up this mountain to make sure that a nation stays dead until they can finish killing it legally." She flexed her fingers. "I've done a great many terrible things for people who paid well. This is the first time they've asked me to do something unforgivable *cheaply*."

"Spring," Freya said quietly. "June melt. Four months."

"Five, if the snowpack consolidates deep. Which it will. North face, heavy loading season, this storm isn't finished." Rook glanced up at the sky with something approaching respect. "Your forecast said six days to the passes. You were wrong, by the way. You had five. If you'd been slower we'd all be dead in the couloir instead of having this charming conversation."

"My forecasts are never wrong," Freya said. "They're occasionally *early*."

And there it was — the first real laugh any of them had managed in days, ragged and surprised, Tomas's own joining before he could stop it. It echoed off the rampart and came back smaller, and somehow that was right. Grief and relief sharing a room, taking turns.

* * *

They descended together, which was the strangest part. Not together-together — Rook kept twenty meters of separation and her hand nowhere near her weapon but also nowhere far from it, and trust between a debt collector and her debtors does not bloom overnight, whatever the novels say. But they picked the same line down through the rocks, and when Tomas slipped on verglas above the icefall, it was Rook who called the warning, and when Rook's boot punched through a snow bridge, it was Freya who had the rope taut before anyone asked.

By the time they reached the lower bivouac — the shelf where they'd cached the tent and the last of the fuel — the light had gone the color of pewter and the temperature had dropped like a stone down a well. They got the tent up in the kind of efficient silence that only develops between people who have recently tried to kill each other and decided, provisionally, to postpone.

Inside, Freya's arm needed seeing to. The gash from the col had opened again on the descent, and Tomas cleaned and taped it while she held a candle stub steady and narrated his technique with the gentle cruelty of someone who has bandaged more wounds than she's received.

"A little to the left. No, my left. Are you wrapping a forearm or gift-wrapping a ham?"

"I'm improvising. There's a difference between a medic and a navigator with opinions."

"There is, and the difference is about four centimeters of tape."

Rook watched from the tent door, saying nothing, holding the candle when Freya's hands shook. When Tomas tied off the last knot, she spoke to the flame rather than to either of them.

"I can carry testimony."

Freya went still. Tomas set down the tape.

"I was in the chamber," Rook said. "I saw the archive. I know what my employers ordered, and I know why, and I know names — theirs, and the names of everyone they've bought since the Annexation. Judges. Surveyors. Two archivists who 'retired' inconveniently. I kept records, Dunmore. Twenty years in collections teaches you habits. I have a ledger of my own." She almost smiled. "Everyone in this story keeps ledgers. It's practically a national trait, and none of us are even Karstian."

"They'll hunt you," Tomas said. "You know that. Defectors don't get severance."

"I've read their books, boy. I wrote some of the chapters." She blew out the candle stub, and in the sudden dark her voice was level and tired and, for the first time, free. "In spring, when the melt opens the pass, there will need to be witnesses. Real ones. Archivists, jurists, someone from the tribunal with a camera and a spine. Until then, the mountain holds the evidence better than any vault your ancestors built — no offense to your ancestors. And I hold the rest." A pause. "If you'll have me."

Freya was quiet long enough that Tomas could hear the wind reorganizing itself outside, probing the tent seams, finding them sufficient.

"Why?" Freya asked finally. "Give me the real answer, not the tactical one."

The dark made honesty cheaper. Tomas understood that; he'd used it himself, once, badly, in a river camp with a letter he should have shown her sooner.

"Because I spent thirty years making people disappear," Rook said. "Debts, mostly. Debts and debts' consequences. And I told myself it was arithmetic — that numbers don't bleed. Then I watched a woman catalogue a recipe box knowing no one would ever read it, and I watched *you* climb a mountain to keep her company, and I thought: someone has to carry the other ledger. The one with the names of the people who did the disappearing." The tent fabric snapped in a gust. "Arithmetic doesn't bleed. But somebody always bleeds for it. It should be the one who did the counting."

* * *

Later, when Rook had taken first watch outside with the radio scanning frequencies she clearly knew too well, Tomas lay beside Freya in the dark and listened to the mountain settle. New snowpack groans as it compacts — deep, tectonic sighs, the sound of weight becoming permanence. Somewhere above them, under all that white, a nation's memory slept in the dark with its recipe box, safer tonight than it had been in eighty years of open air.

"Strange," Freya murmured. She was on her back, her bandaged arm laid carefully across her chest like a relic. "All winter I ran from the thing that finally saved us."

"The mountain?"

"Mm. Every storm I ever chased, I treated like an enemy with a schedule. Something to intercept, to survive, to profit from before it turned on me." Her voice was drowsy, unguarded, the voice she only had at altitude when exhaustion stripped the irony off everything. "But it wasn't turning on us today. It was closing a door. Standing guard." A pause. "My grandmother used to say the Karstians never feared the mountains. They feared the lowlands. Said the valleys were where people forgot things, and the heights were where they remembered."

"She sounds like she'd have liked Mirela."

"She'd have hired Mirela." Freya's breath evened out. Just before sleep took her she added, barely audible: "Stopping costs less than it used to."

Tomas stared at the ceiling of the tent, at the candle-shadow shapes, and let those words land where they wanted to. All these years he'd read her restlessness as fear — and it had been fear, partly, the penance she'd finally confessed to in the snow cave, the invisibility she'd worn like hair shirt. But sitting here, with the pass sealed and the enemy converted and the debt to the Vanes hanging by a thread that a spring tribunal would soon cut or knot forever, he understood she'd been saying something else. That movement wasn't the opposite of safety anymore. That for the first time since the river gave up its chest, they were moving *toward* something instead of merely away.

He slept, and dreamed of willow roots, and woke to Rook's hand on the tent flap and a look on her face that ended the night's gentleness like a slammed door.

"Radio," she said. "Voss is alive. He reached the Syndicate an hour ago." She held up the handset, and her voice was flat and cold and absolutely certain. "And they've just chartered a helicopter out of Gradska. They're not waiting for spring, Dunmore. They're sending demolition charges — and they'll be here tomorrow."

Outside, the wind had died completely, and the sky over the Escarpment was clearing toward a dawn so bright and beautiful it looked like a promise the mountain fully intended to help them keep.
