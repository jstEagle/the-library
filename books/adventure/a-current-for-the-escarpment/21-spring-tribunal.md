## 21. Spring Tribunal

The lowlands in April smelled like cut grass and rain that had somewhere better to be. Freya noticed this from the courthouse steps because it was the first thing in months she'd noticed without calculating what it might cost her.

She stood on the wide stone landing with Tomas beside her and watched people climb past them toward the doors—clerks with armfuls of paper, an old woman in a coat too heavy for the season, a cluster of journalists whose cameras hung at their sides like holstered weapons they hadn't decided to draw. Nobody looked at her twice. That used to be the whole point of her life. Now it felt like being unrecognized at your own wedding.

"You're doing the breathing thing," Tomas said.

"I'm always doing the breathing thing."

"You're doing it *loudly*."

She laughed, which surprised her more than anything else about the morning. The sound came out rusty, like an engine turning over after a winter in the barn.

Inside, the tribunal chamber was nothing like she'd imagined. She'd pictured something Karstian—dark wood, double eagles carved into the rafters, gravity pressing down from every corner. Instead it was a converted municipal hall with water-stained plaster and folding chairs added to accommodate the overflow, and the overflow itself: three hundred people, maybe more, spilling into the aisles. Descendants of custodians. Archivists who had spent careers being told the archive didn't exist. A delegation from Halvett, including Mirela, who wore her grandfather's smuggled route map framed under glass on a strap around her neck like a medal, because apparently that was how you carried a promise you intended to keep in public.

Freya's name was on the docket. Not *Dunmore*. Not the alias she'd used for the syndicate's paperwork, not the name on the truck registration or the weather-service contracts or the lease she'd broken in four provinces in six years.

*Dunmore-Karsti.*

The clerk read it aloud during roll call and Freya felt it land in her chest like a barometric drop—the pressure change before weather arrives. She had spent so long treating her own name as a liability, something to be spelled differently on every document, that hearing it spoken plainly, in a room full of witnesses, felt almost indecent. Like walking outside without a coat.

Tomas squeezed her wrist once and let go. He knew better than to hold on. He'd learned that in a snow cave with dwindling fuel, when holding on was the only thing left and therefore worth rationing.

* * *

The first documents surfaced publicly at ten minutes past nine, and they surfaced the way floodwater surfaces—quietly, then all at once.

The presiding commissioner, a small round woman named Ondrej with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried manner of someone who had waited thirty years for this morning and refused to be rushed by anyone else's urgency, began with a photograph. It was passed along the counsel tables, then held up to the chamber, then projected onto a screen that had been wheeled in overnight and still bore a price sticker on its corner.

A schoolhouse. Karstian script over the door. Children in two rows, squinting into sun, dated eleven days before the Annexation.

"Exhibit one," Commissioner Ondrej said. "A school. Photographed by a teacher who remained at her post through the occupation and recorded, in her final log, the following sentence." She put on her glasses. "'They can take the country off the map. They cannot take it out of my classroom.'"

The room did not cheer. Freya had expected cheering—she'd rehearsed for cheering the way you rehearse for lightning, braced and counting seconds. Instead there was silence, the specific silence of three hundred people each privately confirming that they had heard correctly, that the words were real, that the erasure could now be spoken of in the past tense. Then a woman in the third row began to cry without any effort to hide it, and nobody looked away from her, and that was its own kind of verdict.

Freya sat with her hands flat on the table and thought about Station Kestrel. The scientist who had stayed through the Annexation recording data no one would ever read. *No one would ever read* had turned out to be wrong, and the wrongness of it was the entire point of this room. Every log kept against hope was evidence now. Every act of stubborn record-keeping by people who chose memory over safety had compounded interest for sixty years and come due this morning, in a municipal hall with a price sticker on the screen.

Tomas leaned toward her. "You're grinning."

"Am I?"

"Like a woman watching a supercell finally do exactly what her model said it would."

"Then I'm grinning appropriately."

* * *

They called her at half past eleven.

Walking to the witness stand took eleven steps and Freya counted every one, because counting was what she did instead of panicking, and because eleven steps was short enough to survive even if her legs stopped cooperating at step seven. They didn't. Her legs, unlike the rest of her, had never once doubted her.

Commissioner Ondrej asked her to state her name for the record.

"Freya Dunmore-Karsti."

She said it slowly. Not for emphasis—for accuracy. She wanted the stenographer to get every syllable, wanted it entered into a public document that would outlive her, wanted the name to exist in ink somewhere other than a ledger recovered from a river. Somewhere official. Somewhere that couldn't be flooded away or burned behind a retreating column or scrubbed from an atlas by men in clean offices.

"And you are testifying today as heir to the Karstian custodial line."

"That's what the ledger says." She paused. "I'll be honest with the commission—I didn't know what I was heir to until October. I thought I was heir to a debt. That's mostly what I thought I was heir to, for years. My family's name came with obligations I didn't understand and costs I understood very well. My parents paid them." Her voice caught on the word *paid*, and she let it catch rather than smoothing it over, because she had spent years smoothing things over and look where that had gotten everyone. "My parents died because of who we were. So I became no one. I moved every season. I spelled my name differently on forms. I got very good at weather and very bad at staying."

Ondrej peered at her over the glasses. "And now?"

"Now I've seen the vault." She heard herself say the word *vault* and almost smiled at how inadequate it was—a fissure-sheltered chamber beneath the ice, thousands of documents, photographs, recordings, a nation's entire testimony packed against the cold like seeds against winter. "It isn't treasure. I want the record to show that. Everyone keeps saying *treasure* and *inheritance* like it's gold bars. It's homework. It's sixty years of homework that a lot of dead people did on our behalf, and the assignment is very simple: don't lose it again."

Someone in the gallery said *amen*, quietly, and Ondrej allowed it with a small nod, as if amen were procedurally admissible.

The cross-examination, such as it was, came from counsel representing parties Freya thought of privately as *the clean offices*. Their lawyer was a tall man with a voice like a filing cabinet and a brief full of objections he deployed the way Ilse Rook had once deployed smiles—professionally, warmly, and as instruments of leverage.

He established, gently, that Freya had operated for years under financial obligation to the Vane Syndicate.

"I did," Freya agreed.

"That the Syndicate extended considerable credit to you."

"They extended credit the way a riptide extends an invitation to swim."

A ripple through the gallery. Ondrej's mouth twitched and was brought back under control.

The lawyer pressed on: that she had been, at various points, in possession of Syndicate property; that her testimony regarding the Syndicate's conduct should be weighed against her outstanding debts; that a debtor might say many things to void a debt.

Freya considered him for a moment. She thought about storms—about how the amateurs watched the cloud and the professionals watched the pressure, because the cloud was theater and the pressure was truth.

"Sir, I'd like to answer that properly," she said. "Yes. I owed the Vane Syndicate money. A great deal. Enough that for six years every decision I made had their arithmetic underneath it, the way bedrock sits under topsoil. If I wanted to lie to this commission, voiding that debt would be the single best reason I've ever had to lie." She let that sit. "So here is my testimony anyway, free of charge: the Vane Syndicate hunted this archive for decades. Not for profit—for destruction. Their client needed the Annexation's paper trail erased before a tribunal like this one could convene. They sent armed teams after two storm chasers and a chest. One of their enforcers—" she paused, choosing carefully, because some things deserved care even now, "—one of their enforcers eventually decided the orders were worse than the consequences of disobeying them. Her sworn statement is already in evidence. Ask her, if you doubt me. But don't ask me whether I'm lying to escape a debt. Ask yourself why a Syndicate that forgave nothing in sixty years would forgive mine."

The filing-cabinet voice had no follow-up question ready, which Freya found deeply satisfying in a way she planned to savor for weeks.

* * *

The debt was voided at four o'clock, in a paragraph.

That was the astonishing part—not the outcome but the format. Years of her life compressed into a clause: *all obligations arising between the claimant and the Vane Syndicate are hereby declared null, having been contracted under duress and in furtherance of acts enumerated under Sections 4 through 9.* A paragraph. She had carried that debt across the Escarpment, through whiteouts and ambushes and a snow cave where she'd confessed things she'd never said aloud, and it turned out to weigh exactly as much as ink on a page, which is to say it weighed nothing at all once the right people read it.

She walked out of the chamber into the late-afternoon light feeling lighter by more than the paragraph accounted for, and stood on the steps trying to locate the difference in her body. It took her a minute. Then she realized: she wasn't scanning. For six years she had scanned every street, every parking lot, every rearview mirror, running the constant background calculation of exits and sightlines. The calculation had simply stopped running, the way a rig stops humming when you finally shut it down, leaving a silence so unfamiliar it registered as noise.

"There she is," Tomas said, coming down the steps with his jacket over his shoulder. "The most visible woman in the lowlands."

"Don't joke. I'm having an identity crisis on a courthouse staircase."

"Good. You've earned a nice conventional one. Most people have theirs at university."

Mirela found them at the bottom of the steps. She was carrying the framed map and a document folder tied with string, and she had the expression of someone about to conduct a transaction and enjoying it immensely.

"One document," Mirela said, holding out the folder. "Proving Karstia existed. As promised. Exhibit forty-one, if you're keeping count—the commission certified copies this afternoon. My grandfather's route map stays with me, obviously. We agreed on one document, and I am a businesswoman."

Freya took the folder. Inside was a birth certificate—Karstian issue, pre-Annexation, bearing a name and a town and an official stamp that had been illegal to own for sixty years and real the entire time. Mirela's grandmother's.

"This is yours," Freya said. "This was always yours. You shouldn't have needed to trade for it."

"No," Mirela agreed cheerfully. "But I'm not going to turn down the fact that I didn't. Grandfather always said the mountain gives you nothing you didn't pay for—it just sometimes lets you choose the currency." She tucked the frame back under her arm. "There's a gathering tonight. Half of Halvett drove down for it. There will be food, and singing, and at least one man who will insist on telling you the story of the ford where you nearly drowned the orrery, except in his version you will be much more heroic and the river much deeper. You should come. Being visible, I'm told, includes the boring parts."

"We'll come," Freya said.

Tomas raised an eyebrow at her. *We?*

"We," Freya confirmed. "I'm practicing. Staying. Showing up. All the skills I skipped." She looked up at the sky out of habit—old habits being the last to die—and found only ordinary spring clouds, drifting east without ambition, promising nothing worse than evening rain. No supercell anywhere on the horizon. For the first time in her adult life, the absence of a storm felt less like a lull than like permission.

She was still standing there, holding a grandmother's birth certificate and learning how to breathe without calculating, when a courier pushed through the courthouse doors and asked, politely, for Dunmore-Karsti.

"That's me," Freya said, and discovered she liked the way it sounded.

The courier handed over an envelope, thick, official, stamped with a seal she didn't recognize—though the paper stock, she noted with a professional's eye for provenance, was old. Very old. The kind of paper that had been waiting somewhere patient for a long time.

Inside, a single sheet, written in careful Karstian script, addressed to *the custodian of the archive, with thanks, from those who kept the light at Kestrel.*

There was a postscript. And the postscript contained coordinates.

Tomas read it over her shoulder and went very quiet. "That's not the vault," he said finally. "Those coordinates aren't under the ice at all."

"No," Freya said, feeling the old current stir somewhere under her ribs—not dread this time, not flight, but the specific electric attention of a woman who has just spotted rotation on the far horizon. "It's a second station. And according to this, we're not the only ones who know."
