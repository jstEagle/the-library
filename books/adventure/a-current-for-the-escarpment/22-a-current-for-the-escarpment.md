## 22. A Current for the Escarpment

The road into Halvett had not changed, which was the first surprise. Freya had expected the town to look different now that she knew what it was hiding—the Karstian bones under the plaster, the old names under the new ones. But Halvett kept its face turned the same way it always had: a crooked main street, a church with a steeple patched in three generations of tin, and the mountains rising behind it like a held breath.

"You're staring," Tomas said from the passenger seat.

"I'm appreciating."

"You're staring *appreciatively*. There's a difference, and it's mostly that you do it at things you intend to take apart."

She laughed, and it came out easier than she expected. Four months of testimony, depositions, and lawyers who spoke in footnotes had worn grooves in her; but apparently the grooves ran down to something lighter than she'd been carrying before. She pulled the truck up outside Mirela's house—the archivist's granddaughter's narrow stone place with the blue door—and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, not quite ready to be done arriving.

Inside a padded case on the back seat lay the document. A single page, sealed in archival plastic by a tribunal clerk who had handled it like a sleeping animal: a 1931 census record from the Karstian district of Halvett, listing forty-one households, including one *Dunmore-Karsti*, occupation *weather observer, Station Kestrel*. Her great-grandmother. The woman who had stayed through everything, recording data no one would ever read, until someone finally did.

Mirela opened the door before Freya knocked. She was smaller than Freya remembered, or maybe Freya had gotten bigger somewhere along the way—harder to say which direction that particular measurement ran.

"You found it," Mirela said. Not a question. Her eyes went past them both to the truck, as if the document might be visible through the window glass.

"We found it." Freya lifted the case. "And the tribunal released copies to descendants of the named custodians. Which means—" she paused, because she had rehearsed this part on the drive over and it still snagged in her throat, "—which means there are more coming. Photographs. Recordings. The whole archive, catalogued by spring's end. Your grandfather's route is in the acknowledgments. I made sure of it."

Mirela took the case with both hands, the way you hold something that might be heavier than it looks. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and Freya watched a woman stand very still in a doorway while eighty years rearranged themselves behind her eyes.

"Come in," Mirela said finally, voice only slightly unsteady. "I made coffee. It's terrible. My grandfather used to say bad coffee was the price of good company, and he was wrong about most things but right about that."

* * *

They spent two days in Halvett, which was one day longer than Freya had planned and exactly as long as Tomas had predicted. The town did not suddenly remember itself—Freya had learned enough by now to know that forgetting, once chosen, doesn't reverse like a tide. But small things surfaced. An old man at the café hummed a tune he couldn't name and got defensive when Mirela identified it as a Karstian harvest song. A woman brought out a christening gown with a double-eagle embroidered at the hem, worked so small you'd miss it unless you knew to look. "My mother always said it was just a bird," the woman said, turning it over. "Just a bird," Mirela agreed gently, "that happens to have two heads."

On the second evening, Mirela walked them to the edge of town where the ruins started—the low walls the locals called "the old foundations" with the same tone they used for weather, impersonal and permanent. She stopped at a stretch of stone and pressed her palm flat against it.

"My grandfather drew his map because he believed someone would need it," she said. "He never said who. He just kept it oiled and folded and ready, like an umbrella." She looked at Freya. "You were the umbrella person."

"I was the person running through the rain."

"Same thing, some days." Mirela smiled. "The tribunal asked me if I wanted restitution. Land, compensation, a formal apology with a wax seal. I told them what I wanted instead: a plaque. Small. On this wall. With the old name of this street on it. Let people argue about it for fifty years. Arguing means remembering."

Tomas, who had been quiet in the way he got quiet when something mattered, said, "They'll argue."

"Good," said Mirela. "Halvett could use the practice."

* * *

They left on a morning with high thin cloud and a wind out of the southwest that Freya read the way other people read headlines. Storm season was six weeks off, but the atmosphere already had opinions. She stood by the truck in the café parking lot, watching the cumulus build over the far ridgeline with the slow confidence of a thing that intended to keep its appointment, and felt the old pull—that current she'd spent years mistaking for fear.

Tomas loaded the last of the gear without being asked. He'd gotten good at knowing which silences meant *help me move* and which meant *let me stand here a minute*. This one, he judged correctly, was the standing kind.

"Route?" he asked eventually.

She'd spread the maps across the bed the night before, and they'd argued pleasantly about it the way they now argued about most things—with the underlying assumption that whatever they decided, they'd decided together. The plan was simple and, for the first time in her adult life, unforced. North and east, following the spring instability line where dry air met moist, chasing supercells for paying clients who now included meteorological institutes rather than debt collectors. Then, in June, back to the Escarpment—not to flee over it but to work alongside the survey teams cataloguing the vault's contents, because the archive needed weather data from the surrounding stations, and because somebody had to teach the young researchers how to read a sky that was thinking about killing them.

"And after that?" Tomas had asked.

"After that we see what the next season wants."

It was not a plan, precisely. It was a direction. She was learning the difference, slowly, the way you learn a new instrument—badly at first, then with occasional grace.

Ilse Rook had sent a letter in March. An actual letter, on paper, which was somehow the most Ilse Rook thing imaginable. It contained no apology—Freya hadn't expected one—but it contained something adjacent: a full accounting of the Syndicate's operations against them, sworn testimony, delivered voluntarily to the tribunal. At the bottom, beneath her signature, Rook had written a single line: *The arithmetic was wrong. I've recalculated.* Freya had read it four times and then put it in the glovebox, where important things lived. She suspected she and Rook would never be friends. She suspected they might, someday, share a drink and mean it. Both suspicions could coexist. That was another thing she was learning.

* * *

The truck cleared the last of Halvett's rooftops and opened onto the long flat south of town, the road running straight as a ruled line toward weather that hadn't happened yet. Freya drove with the window cracked, letting the cool air move through, and Tomas navigated without navigating—there was nothing to navigate yet, just miles, but he liked to narrate the country anyway, pointing out hawks and fence lines and the particular shade of green that meant the river table was high.

"Do you ever think about stopping?" he asked around mile thirty. "Like, actually stopping. A house. A porch. A barometer that stays calibrated because nobody's rattling it across three counties every week."

She considered it honestly, which was new. For years the question had been rhetorical—a door painted on a wall. Now it was just a question.

"Sometimes," she said. "But then I think about my great-grandmother at Station Kestrel. Recording readings into a void for eleven years. And I think—she wasn't waiting for anyone to read them. She just thought the record should exist. That somebody, someday, would want to know what the sky did up there while the world was busy looking away." Freya tapped the wheel. "I used to think moving was my penance. Run fast enough and the past can't catch you. Turns out the past caught me anyway, and it turned out to be—"

"A chest full of homework?"

"—an inheritance," she said, laughing. "Yes. Also homework. Enormous amounts of homework. Have you seen the catalogue request forms? There are *eleven*."

"There are twelve. They added one while we were in Halvett."

"Don't tell me that. I'm having a nice morning."

They drove on. The clouds over the eastern ridge had organized themselves into something worth watching—still distant, still harmless, but with the beginnings of rotation written in their shoulders. Freya's hands itched for the instruments. Not the salvaged array, though half of it had survived and the rest had been replaced with tribunal money that she'd accepted, after some argument with herself, as wages rather than charity. New instruments. Calibrated. Hers.

"Pull over at the next rise," she said. "I want to get a reading before it builds."

"You want to chase it."

"It's sixty miles out and drifting northeast. I want to *observe* it. Chasing would be irresponsible."

"You once drove us into a hail core because, quote, 'the data was right there.'"

"The data *was* right there."

"We lost a windshield."

"We gained perspective."

Tomas shook his head, but he was smiling, and when the rise came he didn't even pretend to hesitate—just slowed and pulled onto the gravel shoulder like a man who had long ago made peace with the fact that his life's work was assisting a woman's conversation with the sky.

Freya climbed out and set up the portable mast, and while the sensors woke and began their patient counting, she leaned against the warm flank of the truck and watched the storm assemble itself on the horizon. It moved the way it always moved—unhurried, immense, indifferent to everything except its own physics. Once, that indifference had felt like a verdict. *The world does not care where you go or why; keep running.* Now it felt more like company. Something else out here doing its work without apology, without hiding, without asking permission.

Tomas handed her a thermos cup of terrible gas-station coffee. They drank it standing side by side, two small figures on a big plain, and the wind came across the grass in long silver strokes, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"Sixty miles," Tomas said. "We could beat it to the county line."

"We could," Freya agreed.

She looked at the road, and at the storm, and at the man beside her who had pulled a chest out of a river against her better judgment and thereby dismantled and rebuilt her entire life. Stopping, she thought, would be possible now. That was the strange gift of it all. The door she'd painted on the wall had become a real door, hinged and handle and all—and because it was real, she could leave it open without anything chasing her through it.

She drained the coffee, rolled the cup into the trash bag, and swung back up behind the wheel.

The engine turned over. The storm waited, patient as arithmetic, sixty miles and a whole season away.

Freya put the truck in gear and pointed it east—not fleeing, not falling, just going, the way water goes, finding its level, finding its course. Behind them Halvett shrank to a smudge of rooftops and a blue door; ahead the land opened like a page.

Stopping costs less than it used to.
