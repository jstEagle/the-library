## 1. The Lowest Tide in Living Memory

The sea went out on a Tuesday, and it did not come back the way it should have.

Zephyrine Underhill noticed it first as a wrongness in the sound — the gulls had moved. For eleven years he had worked this coast under other men's names, hauling crab pots and scrubbing decks for skippers who never asked where he'd come from, and in all that time he had learned to read the water the way other men read weather. The gulls told you things before the tide did. They had been feeding on the flats past the kelp line since dawn, which meant the ebb was running long, running strange, pulling back beyond its usual boundary like a dog straining a chain.

By midmorning the whole harbor had stopped working to watch it.

"Look at that," said Tam Breck, the skipper Zephyrine crewed for, a squat man with hands like knotted rope. He stood at the top of the slipway with his pipe unlit, which was how you knew something had truly unsettled him. "Look at *that*."

Zephyrine looked. The bay had emptied itself. Where the water should have been lapping at the sea wall there was now a quarter mile of exposed seabed, gleaming brown and silver, threaded with channels that shone like spilled mercury. Old Wenna Corrie, who was ninety-one and claimed to remember the great storm of her grandmother's day, came down on her grandson's arm and stood staring for a long time without saying anything at all, which frightened people more than any exclamation would have.

"I've seen low tides," she said finally. "I've seen the water go out so far you could walk to the beacon rock dry-shod. I've never seen the sea *leave*."

There was laughter at that, nervous laughter, the kind people make when they want to believe the world is still ordinary. But nobody went back to work. Boats sat aground in their moorings, listing gently on the mud, and their owners stood around them like mourners. Children ran out onto the flats and were called back, then let run again because there seemed no harm in it. Someone found a whale rib half-buried in the sand and someone else found an anchor older than the town, and by afternoon the exposed seabed had become a fairground, a curiosity, a thing to be walked over and remarked upon.

Zephyrine walked it too, but not for curiosities.

He kept to the edge of things, as he always did — anonymous in his oilskins, hands in pockets, a boat-hand among boat-hands, a man whose face gave nothing away because he had spent a decade teaching it to give nothing away. He walked the tideline where the water had retreated farthest, past the mussel beds and the black ribs of some ancient wreck the town had known about forever, and he told himself he was only stretching his legs, only curious the way anyone would be curious, only following the crowd.

That was the lie he carried with him down the flats: *I'm just walking.*

The cliffs at the north end of the bay were called the Hollow Teeth, and beneath them the water had always been deep and always been cold, cold enough that even the bravest swimmers kept clear. There was a legend attached to them, one every child in the town learned before they learned their letters: that there was a cave under the Teeth, and that the cave charged admission. Admission of what, depended on who told it. A boat here, a fisherman there. In the oldest versions it took whole ships, and the sea kept them, and the sea did not give refunds.

Zephyrine had heard the story his first week on this coast, from a deckhand with a lazy eye, and had felt the words go through him like a fishhook. He had never asked about it again. He had never gone near the Hollow Teeth at any tide, high or low, in eleven years. Some doors you do not stand in front of, because standing in front of them is halfway to knocking.

But the sea had gone out farther than living memory, and the door was standing open whether he knocked or not.

He saw it from two hundred yards and stopped as if he'd hit glass. Below the cliff base, in a bowl of naked rock that had not seen sunlight since before the town existed, there was a mouth. Black, arched, tall as a church door, rimmed with weed that hung from it in dead brown curtains, dripping. The retreating water lay in pools before it, mirror-still, holding the sky upside down. It looked less like a cave than like a throat — something the cliff had swallowed long ago and was now, briefly, showing.

Around him the townsfolk were exclaiming, photographing, daring each other closer. Zephyrine heard none of it. His heart had begun doing something slow and heavy, a bell tolling underwater.

*Turn around,* he told himself. *Walk back. You've got pots to scrub.*

He turned around. He got perhaps forty paces.

Then the light shifted — a cloud sliding off the sun, or the angle changing as he moved — and something inside the cave mouth caught the glare and threw it back, and Zephyrine Underhill stopped again, and this time he did not turn around, because what he had seen was wood. Not driftwood. Not the gray, gnawed, anonymous timber the sea scatters everywhere along this coast. This was pale, clean-edged, dressed wood, laid in curves, and he knew those curves. Any man who had worked boats knew them. But these particular curves he knew better than he wanted to.

Carvel planking. Oak, by the color of it where the weed had peeled back. And along the visible strake, half-swallowed by ten years of barnacle and shadow, a line of lettering too eroded to read — but the *shape* of the lettering, the length of the word, the way the final letters climbed slightly as if the painter's hand had hurried —

He had spent ten years not thinking about a ship. He could still spell her name in the dark.

"No," he said aloud, to himself, to the flats, to nobody.

A boy nearby looked up. "You say something, mister?"

"Nothing." His voice came out sanded flat. "Watch your footing. Ground drops off past that ridge."

The boy grinned and promptly went to test exactly that, and Zephyrine left him to it and stood with the wind coming off the emptied sea and tried to think like a man with nothing behind him. That was the discipline. That was the whole discipline of his life now: to be a man with nothing behind him. No mountain. No pass. No inquiry. No name worth printing. A boat-hand named whatever the skipper felt like calling him that week — Tam Breck called him Zeal, short for nothing, and Zephyrine answered to it the way a dog answers to any whistle blown kindly enough.

But the ship was behind him. The ship had always been behind him, these eleven years, riding at anchor in the dark of his skull, and now the sea had gone out farther than living memory and dragged her into the light.

Because it was her. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The Meridian Star had been a salvage vessel, hundred and forty feet of postwar steel-and-oak hybrid, and the section showing in the cave mouth was her bow — the flared stem, the hawse hole choked with weed, the scrollwork at the rail that the yard at Falmouth had given her as a vanity. Salvage ships didn't get scrollwork. Her owner had insisted. Zephyrine had read about it, years ago, in the only account of the sinking the papers had ever printed — three paragraphs, page nine, *vessel lost with all hands in heavy weather off the western coast; cause attributed to navigational error.*

Navigational error. That was the phrase. Four syllables of it had landed on him like a verdict, though the Meridian Star had sunk two years before Kestrel Pass, two years before his own ruin, in waters he'd never sailed. He hadn't understood then why the phrase mattered. He understood now, standing on a seabed that should have been ocean, that the ledgers she carried — the survey ledgers, the falsified soundings, the bribed charts — were the same ledgers, the same company, the same rot that had later reached up the mountains and taken his name.

Halloran Marine. The name was a stone in his boot he had learned to walk on.

*She sank in deep water,* he thought. *Forty fathoms, off the shelf. She cannot be here. She cannot be under my cliffs.*

And yet the scrollwork caught the light again, patient as a held breath.

The tide would turn eventually. Old Wenna had said the sea had never gone out this far, and seas that do unprecedented things tend to do the returning unprecedentedly too. Whatever was in that cave would drown again within hours, maybe sooner, and the door would shut, and the legend would keep charging its admission, and Zephyrine could walk back up the slipway and scrub Tam Breck's pots and remain, safely, permanently, a man with nothing behind him.

He turned around a second time.

This time he made it a hundred paces up the flats before his feet simply quit. He stood there in the wind with the town at his back and the cave at his front, and he understood, with the weary clarity of a man watching a landslide begin, that he had already decided. He had probably decided eleven years ago. All this anonymity, all this careful nothing — it had not buried the past. It had only stored it, the way the sea stores wrecks, waiting for one impossible tide to bring it up gasping into the light.

*Dread,* he corrected himself. *What you're feeling is dread. Name it properly.*

But it wasn't. Or it wasn't only. Underneath the dread, foul and unwelcome as a fish head in a net, there was something else — a quickening, a lean, the old mountaineer's hunger for what lies past the next ridge. Curiosity, wearing dread's coat, waving dread's flag. He knew the difference. He had spent a career reading clients who mistook fear for excitement, and every one of them had followed him anyway, and some of them had died of it.

The sun was dropping toward the horizon, and the light in the cave mouth went from gray to gold, and the gold touched the drowned lettering along the bow and made it legible for one clean moment, one gift or one cruelty:

...RIDIAN S...

Zephyrine's breath went out of him.

Behind him, up the flats, the town was lighting its windows. Ahead, the first thin sheets of the returning tide were sliding across the seabed, silent as spilled oil, filling the channels, climbing the rocks, reaching for the cave with the unhurried confidence of something that has collected debts for a thousand years.

He looked at the water coming in. He looked at the black arch. He calculated — automatically, helplessly, the old habit — the distance, the ground, the time. Two hundred yards of seabed, firm going until the weed line, then slick rock. Twenty minutes out, twenty minutes back, if the tide behaved. If the tide behaved like no tide in living memory had ever behaved.

Tam Breck would be wondering where his boat-hand had got to. Tam Breck would grumble, and dock his pay, and ask no questions, because asking questions was not done between men who lived off other men's forgetfulness.

"They'll say the cave took me," Zephyrine murmured. "Charged admission."

The sea slid another yard up the rock.

He started walking toward it. He told himself he was only going to look — only to stand at the mouth, only to confirm what he already knew, only to see with his own eyes the thing he had spent ten years refusing to see. He told himself he would take nothing, change nothing, tell no one. He told himself the story every man tells himself at the threshold of a door he should not open.

The weed curtains dripped as he passed beneath them, cold as fingers. Inside, the dark smelled of stone and salt and something older, something like tar and iron and long-ago fire. And deeper in, where the last of the daylight barely reached, the wreck of the Meridian Star lay heeled over in the hollow of the cave, intact, whole, preserved like a body in ice — and lashed to her deck, glinting dully under a skin of barnacles, a brass strongbox stamped with a crest he would have recognized anywhere, even blindfolded, even at the bottom of the sea.

Halloran Marine.

Above him, faint on the evening air, came the clang of the harbor bell announcing the turn of the tide — and, thinner still, carried down from the cliff road, the sound of a car engine stopping, and a door closing, and footsteps that did not move on.
