## 3. Blacklisted

Ten years is long enough to forget the sound of your own name spoken kindly.

Zephyrine Underhill remembered it anyway, the way a body remembers a broken bone before rain. It came back to him on fog nights, on the coast, in the pauses between waves—*Underhill, Zephyrine, certified alpine and high-altitude guide, license number 4471-K*—stamped into the laminated card he had carried for eleven years and surrendered on an afternoon in March, in a room with a wet carpet and a radiator that ticked like something counting down.

But memory is a dishonest guide. It takes you by the arm and leads you up the mountain again whether you want to go or not. So he went.

* * *

Kestrel Pass, ten years earlier. Late September, the season turning its collar up against winter.

He had been thirty-one, and good—truly good, not the word other people used about him but the word he allowed himself only in the privacy of his own pulse. He could read a snowpack the way sailors read water. He could feel a storm three days out in the ache of his back teeth. The Kestrel expedition was a plum assignment: a private client, six days, high traverse, a summit bid if the window held. The client was Edmund Marchetti, a surveyor, a man of fifty with a laugh like a door swinging open and a habit of asking questions no one else thought to ask.

"You're the one who reads the mountain," Marchetti had said at the trailhead, shaking his hand with both of his own, warm and dry. "So I'll believe you over the paperwork. Paperwork's just the mountain's first draft."

Zephyrine had laughed. He would spend ten years wishing he hadn't.

The first three days were clean. Blue mornings, firm snow, the kind of cold that makes the blood sing. Marchetti was a strong walker for his age and a better talker, filling the hours with stories about his daughter—Isla, fourteen, who wanted to be a cartographer, who drew maps of places that didn't exist yet. "She says the world's got blank spaces left," Marchetti said, "and somebody ought to fill them in honestly." He said *honestly* the way some men say *God*, and Zephyrine, who had grown up around guides who treated clients as freight, felt something loosen in his chest. This was why he did the work. Not the summits. The people.

The fourth day, the mountain changed its mind.

It came out of the west without ceremony—no towering anvil cloud, no theatrical darkening, just a thinning of the light and a wind that arrived all at once, as if a door had been flung open at the top of the world. By noon it was snowing sideways. By two, the wind was doing things to the snow that snow should not do, lifting it off the slopes in long white arms and laying it down again as something new, something with no history, no layers, no memory. Zephyrine stood at the col and felt his back teeth sing.

"Turnaround's now," he said. "This isn't a storm we wait out. This is the front edge of three days of it, and we're above the treeline with a warming trend underneath. The whole face below us is going to load up."

Marchetti looked at the sky, then at him, and nodded without argument. That was the thing Zephyrine would carry longest—not a client who pushed on, not hubris, not a man who ignored the guide's warning. A client who trusted him completely. *I'll believe you over the paperwork.*

They turned. And the mountain, which had seemed merely hostile, became something else entirely.

* * *

The descent is the part he cannot make linear, even now. Memory gives it to him in fragments, the way a wreck gives up its cargo: a piece at a time, out of order, sharp-edged.

The whiteout. The compass he checked four times in ten minutes because the world had been erased down to a circle three meters wide and he no longer trusted his own legs to tell him level from slope.

The radio. He remembered the radio with a clarity that felt like cruelty—the way it crackled, the way the base station's voice came through in pieces, *—front moving faster than—shelter at the—* and then nothing but static like sand pouring through the world.

The slope. He remembered testing it. He remembered the column test he dug at the shoulder below the col, the one that sheared clean at thirty centimeters, and the decision he made because of it—the long way down, the ridge route, two extra hours in the teeth of the storm but on ground that would hold.

And he remembered the logbook.

That was the detail that came back to him last, years afterward, and when it did it arrived like a blade sliding between ribs. The logbook was company property—Halcyon Survey & Expedition Services, his employer of six years, the firm that had brokered the Kestrel contract. Every guide kept one. Weather observations, snowpack notes, decisions made and the reasons for them. It was supposed to be the guide's protection: a record that you had done the work, read the signs, made the calls a reasonable professional would make. Zephyrine's was meticulous. He had written the column test in it himself, hunched in the wind with his mittens between his knees, the page stiffening as the ink cooled. *Shear at 30 cm. Ridge route. No go on the face.*

He had left the logbook at base camp, in the expedition chest, because you did not carry what you did not need into a storm.

When he came down the mountain—when they carried him down, half-frozen, with Marchetti's body in a litter behind him and three days of storm in his lungs—someone had already been through the chest.

He didn't know it then. Grief doesn't leave room for suspicion. For three days they had fought the mountain, and on the second night, crossing a slope that even the ridge route could not make safe, the mountain had taken its price. A slide, small by avalanche standards, enormous by human ones—a slab no bigger than a tennis court, moving faster than a falling man could think. Zephyrine had been on the windward edge of it. Marchetti had been three meters behind, roped to him, and the rope had held, and that was the cruelty of it: the rope held, and it pulled Zephyrine back down the moving slope toward the man at the end of it, and they had both gone into the white together, and when Zephyrine dug himself out—minutes, only minutes, but minutes are a country you cannot cross twice—he dug in the wrong place first.

He dug where the rope's memory told him the man should be. He was wrong by twenty meters. The slide had rotated. By the time he found Marchetti, the cold had.

They told him later that no guide alive could have done better. They told him that in the inquiry room, too, at first.

* * *

The inquiry was held in a conference room in the Halcyon offices, which should have been the first warning. There was a table the length of a small boat, and a carafe of water nobody poured, and five men in jackets who asked him questions with the cadence of men who already had the answers typed up.

The company's incident report was presented on the second day. Zephyrine, exhausted, still coughing frost out of his chest, listened to a man he had never met read aloud from *his* logbook—the logbook he had left in the expedition chest—and heard a different expedition described. In this version there had been no column test. In this version there had been no decision to take the ridge route. In this version, Zephyrine Underhill had assessed the slope as stable, had elected to descend directly, and had noted—these words were read into the record with the flat ceremony of a verdict—*"conditions acceptable, proceeding."*

"That's not what I wrote," Zephyrine said.

"Mr. Underhill, the logbook is quite clear."

"That's not my entry. I wrote the shear at thirty centimeters. I wrote *no go on the face*. I wrote the ridge route. My handwriting is in that book for six days and that entry isn't mine."

There was a silence with a specific texture, the silence of men waiting for a thing to finish happening. Then the chairman, a gray man with a gray voice, said: "The logbook was recovered from the expedition chest at base camp. There is no indication of tampering. Is there anything further?"

There was a great deal further. There were the weather records he'd kept. There was the radio log from base camp, which would show the storm warning received at 11:40 and his acknowledgment at 11:43. There was Marchetti's own field journal, which Zephyrine had seen him writing in every evening, and which the family had, he learned later, been asked to surrender and then told had never been recovered.

But here is what he understood only afterward, in the long slow education of disgrace: an inquiry is not a search for truth. It is a search for a version of events that everyone can live with. Halcyon Survey had a contract with the regional authority worth more than a decade of expeditions. Halcyon Survey had insurance, and insurers do not pay out on expeditions whose own guides documented a known hazard and turned back anyway—no, insurers pay on *accidents*, and accidents require a responsible party, and a responsible party was sitting right there with frost scars on his cheeks and no lawyer and no idea that the logbook in front of him had been rewritten in a hand that imitated his own well enough for men who had never seen him write.

He gave them everything. He drew them the route. He described the column test until his voice cracked. And on the final day, the chairman read the finding aloud in a voice like a man reading a shipping manifest: the guide had failed in his professional duty of assessment and care; the client's death was attributable to guide error; the matter was closed.

Two months later, the certification revocations arrived within a week of each other—the regional authority first, then the neighboring country's, the two bodies sharing a database and, he suspected, a phone call. *License 4471-K, revoked. Ineligible for reapplication.* No appeal process was printed on the letter, because there wasn't one. There was only a line at the bottom, in smaller type: *Halcyon Survey & Expedition Services has cooperated fully with this review.*

He read that line forty times. It was the closest thing to a confession he would be given for ten years.

* * *

The years afterward had a shape he came to know too well. The shape of a door closing in every room he entered.

There was the hearing he attended where Marchetti's widow would not look at him—no, that was not quite true; she looked at him once, and her looking was worse than not looking, because in it was not hatred but a question, and he had no answer that her heart could hold. *Did you kill my husband?* And the honest answer—*the mountain did, and then men did, and I have been dying of it ever since*—was not an answer at all.

There was the daughter. Isla, fourteen, who had wanted to be a cartographer, who had wanted the world's blank spaces filled in *honestly*. He had sent one letter, in the first winter, and received no reply, and told himself that silence was the kindest thing she could give him.

There was the slow migration downward, altitude by altitude, profession by profession, from the high country to the hills to the coast, until he was a boat-hand in a fog-bound harbor where nobody knew his face and he had learned to answer to nothing at all. He worked with his hands and slept with his back to walls and kept one rule without exception: he never went near a mountain again. The sea was kinder. The sea buried its evidence.

And yet. And yet, in all those years, one ember refused to go out, and it was this: the logbook existed. The false entry existed. Forgeries are written by hands, and hands belong to people, and people keep records—companies above all keep records, obsessively, in triplicate, because companies trust paper more than they trust men. Somewhere in Halcyon's files, or in the files of whoever had held Halcyon's leash, there were the true logs, the real weather records, the original radio transcripts, perhaps even Marchetti's field journal, which had never been recovered because it had never been lost. Somewhere there was paper that told the truth.

He had stopped saying this aloud years ago. People who say such things are called bitter, and then they are called worse. But he had never stopped believing it, the way a man never stops believing in the country of his birth.

* * *

Now, on the fog-bound coast, in the years of anonymity, the ember had banked itself down to almost nothing. Almost.

He thought of it the night after the cave, sitting in the dark of his room above the harbor with the photograph he had taken—taken nothing else, only the photograph—lying face-down on the table beside his lamp. The brass strongbox he had seen in the wreck's hold, stamped with the survey company's mark, rose behind his eyes every time he closed them. He had told himself it was coincidence. He had told himself the sea gives back strange things.

But he had spent ten years learning what men do with paper when the paper is dangerous. They sink it. They burn it. And when they cannot burn it, they weight it and give it to the water, because the water keeps what men cannot afford to keep, and men believe—men have always believed—that the sea does not give back.

Zephyrine turned the photograph over and looked at the stamp on the strongbox for a long time, the lamp hissing, the fog pressing against the window like a hand.

The logbook had been rewritten once. He knew the handwriting now. He would know it anywhere.

And if the sea had kept the true ledgers all these years—ten years, ten winters, ten springs of tides turning over their pages—then the water had been holding the one testimony no inquiry had ever heard. His. The mountain's. The truth with the ink still on it.

He sat until the lamp burned low, and outside, far below, the tide began its long pull back out of the bay, farther than any old-timer could remember, drawing the sea off the cliffs like a sheet drawn off a body—exposing, in the black rock below, the cave mouth that the old men said charged admission.

He had paid once already. He had paid ten years.

He stood, and took up his lamp, and went down toward the water.
