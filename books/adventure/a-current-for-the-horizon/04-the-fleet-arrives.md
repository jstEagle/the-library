## 4. The Fleet Arrives

They came on the morning tide, three of them, riding in out of the fog like a verdict.

I was mending nets on the quay when the first hull broke the gray line of the horizon—a working vessel, broad-beamed and ugly with purpose, her crane arm folded against the swells like a mantis foreleg. No pleasure craft moves that way. No fishing boat carries that much steel. I set down my netting needle and watched her come, and something in my chest went cold and quiet, the way it does before a storm you've been pretending not to smell.

The second ship followed an hour behind. The third came at noon.

Three salvage vessels do not anchor in a bay like ours by accident. Our harbor holds eleven working boats, a cannery that runs half the year, and a church whose bell hasn't rung true since the war. There is nothing here worth salvaging—nothing, at least, above the waterline.

The ships took up stations in a triangle across the bay, precise as surveyors' pins, and ran up their colors. I knew those colors before I could read their names. Deep blue field, white chevron, a gold star at the point where the chevron breaks. Halloran Marine. The same mark that had been stamped into every falsified logbook, every revoked certification, every letter that ended my life ten years ago and signed itself *with regret*.

The netting needle slipped from my fingers and clattered on the stones. Old Tammas, who mends crab pots beside me most mornings and has never once asked my name, glanced up.

"Big money," he said, nodding at the ships. "Somebody's lost something out there."

"Somebody always is," I said.

My voice held. I'll give myself that much. Inside, I was already running through the arithmetic of ruin: the cave, the tide, the photograph in my coat pocket, the silhouette on the cliff road. Three ships. A company that had spent a decade making sure one man stayed buried. They hadn't come for salvage. They'd come because someone had seen me come down off that cliff in the dark, and somewhere in Halloran's ledgers of favors and threats, my shape had matched a name they thought was dead.

* * *

By evening the town had transformed. The Anchor's Rest had more strangers in it than locals, men in oilskins too new to be honest, drinking with the careful economy of people being paid by the day. A tender shuttled between the ships and the quay, ferrying crates marked FRAGILE and a woman who stood at the bow with her hands in her pockets, watching the water as though she were reading it.

I kept to the shadows of the fish shed and watched her step ashore. She was not what I expected. Salvage chiefs, in my limited experience, are either pirates in pressed shirts or bureaucrats with salt in their blood. This one moved like neither. She was perhaps forty, lean, weathered past caring about it, with a face that seemed assembled entirely from decisions already made. She spoke briefly to the harbormaster—I saw him bend toward her, eager, the way dogs bend toward anyone holding food—and then walked the length of the quay alone, unhurried, looking at everything.

She stopped at the notice board outside the harbormaster's office, and I watched her pin a single sheet of paper there, square the corners with two fingers, and walk away without looking back.

I waited until the light failed and the quay emptied. Then I crossed over and read it.

**REWARD**

Halloran Marine Salvage Division offers a substantial reward for information regarding unauthorized diving activity in the waters adjacent to Gullwrack Cliffs. All reports treated confidentially. Inquire aboard M/V *Iron Meridian*, or at the harbormaster's office.

Below it, a number. Below the number, the blue-and-white chevron and the gold star.

*Unauthorized diving.* Not treasure hunting. Not salvage speculation. Unauthorized diving—as if they already knew diving had happened, and only needed to know by whom. As if the question weren't *what* but *who*, and the answer had a price tag that would buy a fisherman a new boat, a widow a new life, a desperate man a decade back.

I read the notice three times, committing it to memory, then walked away along the darkened quay with my collar up and my heart going like a bilge pump.

* * *

That night I sat in my room above the boatshed with the lamp turned low and laid it all out the way I used to lay out route plans on Kestrel Pass—every fact on the table, weighted, ordered, interrogated.

Fact: the cave had opened on the lowest tide in living memory. Fact: I had entered it once, taken nothing but a photograph, and left the strongbox where it sat bolted to its dead captain's safe. Fact: someone had seen me on the cliff road. Fact: within four days, three Halloran vessels had crossed the horizon and dropped anchor with the confidence of an army arriving at coordinates.

Conclusion: they knew the wreck was there. They had known—or suspected—for longer than I had. And my silhouette on that road had told them the one thing they feared: that someone else knew too.

I turned the photograph over in my hands again, though I no longer needed to look at it. The Meridian Star, listing gently in the amber light of my lamp, her name still legible on the stern, the hold hatch sealed with chain and padlock gone green with forty years of salt. A salvage ship sent to recover evidence of her own company's crimes, sunk under circumstances the inquiry never properly examined, carrying in her belly the ledgers that would have hanged Halloran Marine in open court—and instead had hung me, by proxy, in absentia, in every licensing office in two countries.

Ten years I had told myself the sea had taken that evidence beyond any reach. Ten years the sea had been keeping it for me, patient as a debt collector, waiting for a tide low enough to make delivery.

And now the tide had come, and so had they.

* * *

I slept badly, which is to say I slept the way I always sleep—in shallow water, surfacing at every sound. Somewhere past midnight I heard the tender engine again, soft against the pilings, and looked out through the gap in the curtain in time to see two figures cross the quay below my window. One was the harbormaster. The other carried a lantern, and the lantern light caught the white chevron on his shoulder.

They stopped at the notice board. The harbormaster added a second sheet beneath the first. Then they walked on, and the lantern swung away toward the office, and I stood in the cold with my breath fogging the glass, understanding that the town itself had just been bought out from under us.

In the morning I went down early, before the fishermen, and read the new notice. It was shorter than the first.

**NOTICE TO ALL PERSONS**

The waters adjacent to Gullwrack Cliffs are closed to all private vessel traffic effective immediately, by order of the Harbor Authority, pending commercial survey operations. Any vessel entering the exclusion zone will be subject to seizure. Any person entering the water within the zone will be subject to prosecution.

The Harbor Authority. Which is to say, the harbormaster. Who last autumn had borrowed money from a lender in the city whose name, if you pulled the thread far enough, would almost certainly lead back to a blue flag with a gold star.

They had fenced the sea. My sea—the stretch of cold gray water I'd worked anonymously for six years, hauling pots and mending nets and being nobody, safely nobody, invisibly nobody. Now it was a restricted site, patrolled by spotter boats, and every soul in this town knew there was reward money floating on it like oil on water.

Old Tammas found me at the rail, staring out at the ships.

"You know anything about that diving they're on about?" he asked. He said it mildly, the way old men say dangerous things, testing the air.

"No," I said.

"Didn't think so." He wound wire around a pot frame with fingers knuckled thick as coral. "Fella came through yesterday asking questions, though. Young fellow, city clothes. Asking who'd been down to the cliffs at night lately." He tied off the wire, bit it clean. "Told him I keep my eyes on my pots and my mouth on my business. Some of the others weren't so particular."

"Who wasn't?"

He gave me a look then—not unkind, but level, the look of a man who has survived forty years of hard seasons by knowing exactly how much to know. "You're new here six years, friend, and I've never asked your business. I'm not starting today. But I'll tell you what I told my boys when they were young: when the big boats come in flying colors like that, little boats get swamped. Best thing a little boat can do is be somewhere else."

"I can't be somewhere else."

"No," he said, and went back to his wire. "Didn't figure you could."

* * *

Through the afternoon I watched the fleet work, and watching taught me things.

The *Iron Meridian*—the chief's ship, by right of the woman who commanded her—held station at the triangle's apex, closest to the cliffs. The other two, *Corvane* and *Salt Warrant*, ran grid patterns across the bay with instruments slung from their davits: side-scan sonar, magnetometers, the slow patient technology of finding things that don't want to be found. Their spotter boats crisscrossed the exclusion line with searchlights even in daylight, sweeping the base of Gullwrack Cliffs in long deliberate strokes.

They were mapping the approach. That was the part that made my hands go cold. Anyone can find a wreck with enough gear and enough time. But the cave under Gullwrack doesn't open onto open water—it opens through a drowned passage that floods and empties with the tide, a throat of current and rock that will swallow a diver who enters at the wrong hour or the wrong stage. I had felt that current on my one visit, dragging at my fins like a hand deciding whether to keep me. The fleet had divers, permits, cutting equipment, and money without bottom. What they didn't have—yet—was the timing. The approach. The knowledge of when the throat opens and when it closes.

For now, that knowledge lived in exactly two places: my head, and whatever notes the Meridian Star's own crew had logged forty years ago and sealed inside her with the rest of their secrets.

Which meant the race was real, and it had already started, and I was losing.

A man with a boat and a plan can beat a corporation to a door that opens six times. But a man being hunted—who can't rent gear without a name, can't buy cutting torches without questions, can't put to sea without a spotter boat logging his heading—that man isn't racing anymore. He's running.

* * *

At dusk the woman came ashore again.

I was stacking crab pots at the far end of the quay, doing my best impression of a man with no thoughts in his head, and she passed within ten feet of me without a glance. But at the head of the ramp she stopped, and stood looking out at the cliffs for a long moment—the same stretch of black rock where I'd climbed down nine nights ago with a lamp and a photograph's worth of hope—and then she turned and looked directly at me.

Not at the pots. At me. Two seconds, maybe three, in the failing light, her face unreadable as ballast stone. Then she walked on toward the Anchor's Rest, and I stood among my crab pots with my pulse hammering in my ears, certain of two things and unable to decide which frightened me more.

Either she had recognized my silhouette from a description given by whoever had seen me on the cliff road.

Or she hadn't recognized me at all—and had simply looked at every man on that quay the way she'd looked at me, filing faces, hunting without a net.

I finished the stack. I walked home the long way, along the shingle, in the dark, listening to the fleet's generators humming across the water like a hive deciding something. Above the boatshed I lit no lamp. I sat by the window with the photograph face-down on my knee and watched the spotter boats sweep their lights along the base of Gullwrack Cliffs, patient as tides, and I understood that the anonymous life I had built—six years of mended nets and no name, the small gray peace I'd traded my whole history for—was over. They had posted a price on a stranger's silhouette, and somewhere in this town, tonight, some desperate or careless or simply hungry neighbor was going to look at me across a barroom table and see a season's wages standing in oilskins.

The hunt had begun. And I had six tides, a sealed safe, no gear, no ally, and no way to reach the one thing that could save me without walking straight into the arms of the company that had spent a decade making sure I never surfaced.

Then, near midnight, a knock at the stair door. Soft. Twice.

I went down with a marlinspike in my fist and opened the door on a woman I didn't know—young, travel-worn, rain-beaded, with eyes that had my own history in them—and the first words out of her mouth stopped my heart like a hand around it.

"You're the guide who lost my father on Kestrel Pass."
