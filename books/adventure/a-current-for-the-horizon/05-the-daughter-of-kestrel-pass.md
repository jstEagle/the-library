## 5. The Daughter of Kestrel Pass

Isla Marchetti had learned to read men the way her father had taught her to read weather: by what they did with their hands when the truth got close.

The harbormaster's hands were busy. They straightened papers that didn't need straightening, smoothed a chart already flat, tapped twice on the counter as though keeping time to some private, nervous music. He hadn't looked at her since she'd said her father's name.

"Marchetti," he repeated. "Emilio Marchetti. That's a long time ago. Long way from here."

"Ten years," Isla said. "And four hundred miles. But grief keeps poor records."

He laughed at that — a short, startled bark — and then seemed sorry for it, and his hands went back to the papers. Outside the harbor office window, the fog was doing its slow work on the bay, erasing the moored boats one by one until only their mastheads floated, disembodied, like a fleet of ghosts waiting for orders. Somewhere out in that gray, three salvage vessels sat at anchor, their running lights burning pale through the murk. Halloran Marine colors. She'd checked the registry twice from the ferry, because she hadn't trusted herself to believe it.

"You've come about the cave," the harbormaster said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation either, but it landed somewhere between the two.

"I've come about a lot of things." She set her father's tide tables on the counter — the real ones, photocopied twice and hidden in two countries, the originals in a bank vault in Genoa. "But yes. The cave."

"There's no cave."

"The lowest tide in living memory says otherwise. Half this town watched the sea walk backward off those cliffs last week. There's a black mouth under the headland that every child here has been told never to enter, because it *charges admission*." She let that sit. "That's not the kind of story a place forgets just because a salvage fleet shows up asking people not to remember it."

His hands stopped moving. For a moment — half a second, maybe less — something moved behind his eyes that might have been pity, or might have been warning, and then it was gone behind the professional blankness of a man paid to know nothing.

"Miss Marchetti," he said carefully, "you should talk to the fleet's people if you want information about diving. Dree Voss, chief of the *Iron Meridian*. They have permits. I don't involve myself in—"

"In anything," Isla finished. "Yes. I noticed how thoroughly you don't involve yourself."

She took back the tide tables, folded them into her coat, and left him to his papers. The bell over the door gave a small, cheap sound, like a coin dropped in a church plate.

Outside, the town smelled of diesel and kelp and woodsmoke, the particular perfume of a coast that lived by the water but had long ago stopped trusting it. Gulls argued over something unspeakable on the breakwater. A boy of perhaps twelve sat on a bollard mending a net with quick, bitter fingers, watching her with the frank appraisal of someone who'd already learned that strangers were either money or trouble, and usually both.

She'd been in towns like this before. Ten years of them. Fishing villages on two coasts, mountain towns in three countries, archive rooms and inquiry chambers and the long fluorescent corridors of maritime courts where clerks shuffled her father's death between folders like a card they didn't want to draw. She knew how these places worked. They kept their dead close and their questions closer, and they could smell an outsider's purpose through three layers of rain gear.

Her father had died in a place like this too — not this town, but its twin, up in the mountains where the air went thin and honest. Kestrel Pass. The name still tasted like iron filings on her tongue.

*Emilio Marchetti, client, lost in whiteout conditions during descent. Guide error cited as primary cause.*

Guide error. Two words that had unmade her family, that had turned her mother quiet and then absent and then gone entirely, that had put Isla at nineteen behind a desk in a shipping office, learning ledgers instead of mountains, learning that paper could be made to lie as fluently as any witness.

She'd spent ten years pulling his records apart. Route plans, equipment manifests, weather service logs, the guide's own filed itinerary versus the itinerary actually walked. Piece by piece, year by year, she'd built a picture that didn't match the official one — and the gap between them had a shape, and the shape had a name, though she hadn't known whose name it was until three weeks ago, when a survey company's internal memo surfaced in a bankruptcy filing and she'd read the words *Halloran Marine* attached to a date she knew better than her own birthday.

Halloran Marine. Whose salvage ships were sitting in this fog right now, three days after an unprecedented tide exposed a cave everyone here swore didn't exist.

Coincidence was a luxury Isla Marchetti had stopped affording herself around year six.

* * *

She found the tavern by following the sound of argument — the low, territorial grumble of locals discussing outsiders, which carried further than church bells in a place like this. Inside, the room was low-beamed and yellow-lit, thick with peat smoke and the smell of fried fish, and conversation dipped when she entered the way water dips around a stone.

She ordered a beer she didn't want and took the corner table with her back to the wall, and she listened.

"—Voss posted another notice at the chandlery. Fifty pounds for word of unauthorized diving, they're saying. Fifty!" A fisherman with forearms like hawser rope shook his head. "Fifty pounds to inform on your neighbor. What do they think we are?"

"Richer than we were yesterday," someone said, and there was laughter without much joy in it.

"They're not fishing the wreck, mind. Whatever's down there, they want it quiet. You see how they run their spotter boats? Tight as a poacher. My cousin asked one of their deckhands outright, bought him a pint, and the lad went gray as cod and wouldn't say a word past 'survey work.'"

"Survey work." An older woman at the fire snorted. "In a cave that's been sealed since before my grandmother drowned. Surveying what?"

"Whatever the tide showed them," said a voice near the window, quieter than the rest, and Isla felt the room change around it — not louder, but denser, the way air goes dense before a storm. "Tide showed them plenty."

She turned.

He was sitting apart from the others, the way a man sits who has been apart so long it's become the shape of his body. Mid-forties, maybe, though the years had worked on him unevenly — deep lines around the eyes, a gray coming into dark hair, hands wrapped around a mug of tea he didn't seem to be drinking. Boat-hand's clothes, salt-stained, a deckhand's canvas jacket gone soft with wear. But he held himself wrong for a boat-hand. Too still. Too watchful. He sat like a man on a ledge.

And she knew his face.

Not from life — from photographs, from inquiry transcripts, from a decade of nightmares with excellent production values. Younger in the pictures, broader in the shoulder, standing in a courtroom corridor with his certification case number pinned to his lapel and an expression she had spent ten years cataloguing: guilt, or grief, or the flawless performance of both.

Zephyrine Underhill. The guide of Kestrel Pass. The man who had walked her father up a mountain and walked down alone.

The room went on talking around them, but Isla heard none of it. Her blood had developed its own weather system, a pressure drop, a rising wind. Ten years of ledgers and letters and dead ends, and here was the center of it all drinking tea in a corner like any other anonymous man, close enough to touch.

He was looking at her now. He'd registered her attention the way animals register a rifle — instantly, without turning his head fully, his weight shifting almost imperceptibly toward the door. And then his eyes found hers, and she watched recognition move across his face like a slow tide going out, exposing everything.

He knew her too. Of course he did. She had her father's face; everyone had told her so all her life, and she had hated it, and now, watching this man go pale by degrees in the yellow light, she was suddenly, viciously glad of it.

She stood. Crossed the room. Pulled out the chair across from him without asking, and sat.

"Mr. Underhill," she said.

"Don't," he said quietly. "Not that name. Not here."

"What should I call you? The boat-hand name? The one you've been hiding behind while the rest of us buried our dead?" She kept her voice low, conversational, pleasant even — she'd learned that trick from her mother, who could flay a man with a smile. "Because I've been calling you something for ten years, and it isn't printable in a public house."

His jaw tightened. Up close, the courtroom photograph hadn't lied about his eyes — they were the color of slate after rain, and they were tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix. "You're Emilio's daughter."

"Myra," she said. "My name is Myra. Isn't that the point of a new name? You'd know."

"I didn't choose—" He stopped. Breathed. Started again, slower. "You shouldn't be here."

"No," she agreed. "I shouldn't. Neither should three Halloran Marine salvage ships. Neither should you, frankly, given that your presence in this town is the single most improbable coincidence I've encountered in a decade of improbable coincidences. So let's skip past the part where we pretend this is chance." She leaned forward. "You knew the tide would show something. Or you knew what it showed the moment you saw it. Which was it?"

For a long moment he said nothing. The fire cracked. Someone fed coins to a jukebox in the corner and thought better of it. And Zephyrine Underhill looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse — not the guilt she'd rehearsed against, not defiance, but something rawer, like a man hearing a knock at a door he'd bricked up long ago.

"You've been reading files," he said finally. "Ten years of files. Tell me what you think happened on Kestrel Pass."

"You tell me. You were there. I've only got the paperwork — the falsified route plan, the weather log your company altered, the inquiry that asked exactly zero questions about any of it. I've got a dead father and a report that blames the one man whose testimony nobody bothered to take properly." She heard her own voice fraying and hated it, steadied it. "So no, Mr. Underhill, I don't need you to tell me what happened. I need you to tell me why you let it stand."

Something flickered across his face — pain, fast and involuntary, like a nerve struck. "Let it *stand*?"

"You walked away. Two countries revoked your certification, and you vanished, and the report became the truth because nobody contested it. My father has been *guide error* for ten years. His name is a footnote in somebody else's negligence. And you—" her voice did fray then, badly, and she let it, "—you drink tea."

The silence between them stretched until it sang.

"You think I haven't stood in front of that report?" he said at last, very quietly. "Every day. Every single day for ten years. You think there's a version of me that slept through it?" He set the mug down with exaggerated care, the way a man does when his hands want to do something else. "Your father trusted me. I took him up that mountain. I brought the storm down on us by pushing the schedule, and I lost him in the whiteout forty meters below the saddle, and I dug for him until my hands were meat, and they pulled me off that pass half-dead and put me in a room full of lawyers who'd already written the ending." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "So don't tell me what I drank, Miss Marchetti. You weren't there."

"And whose fault was the schedule?" she shot back. "Yours. Your own deposition says you advanced the summit push against protocol. The company's logs were forged, fine — I believe you, I've *proven* that — but the decision to climb was yours, and my father followed you because he believed guides don't make fatal mistakes, and you let him believe it."

"Yes." Just the one word. No defense attached. And that undid her more than any argument could have, because she'd spent ten years imagining this confrontation, and in every version he fought her, and fighting she knew how to survive. This — this man sitting in his own wreckage without flinching — left her nowhere to put all the anger she'd carried, and it started sliding through her fingers like meltwater.

"He wrote you a letter," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Before the expedition. I found it in his effects. He told you about me — that I'd just gotten my first survey contract, that he was climbing Kestrel partly to pay for it. He said—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He said he trusted you with his life, and that was the same thing as trusting you with mine, because everything he earned came home to me."

Underhill closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he didn't bother hiding it, and that was its own kind of unbearable.

"I know," he said. "He told me. On the approach, the first night. He talked about you for an hour." A breath. "It was the best hour of that trip. I've thought about it every day since, and I have never once decided whether that makes it better or worse."

The jukebox finally chose a song, something old and mournful, and the room's noise swelled back around them, and Isla sat in the wreckage of ten years' certainty and found that underneath the anger there had always been this: not hatred. Hunger. She had wanted, more than vindication, more than apology, simply to sit across from someone else who remembered him alive.

"He was afraid of the descent," she said. "He told me once he was fine going up, it was coming down that scared him. Something about how you can't see where you're going, only where you've been."

"He said that." Underhill's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Second night, at the high camp. Word for word."

They sat with that. The fire settled. Outside the fogged window, far out in the bay, the lights of the salvage fleet burned steady and patient as a held breath.

Then Isla straightened, and the hunger folded itself away, and the ledger-keeper came back up, because there was work to do and only so many tides to do it in.

"Halloran Marine is anchored in your bay," she said. "The same company whose survey memo ties them to Kestrel Pass. The same company whose founder signed off on the falsified logs. They're here for whatever the tide uncovered, and I'd bet everything I have left that it connects to my father's expedition — and to you." She slid her hand into her coat and laid the tide tables on the table between them, unfolded, weighted with her palm. "Six more accessible tides before the spring seals that cave. I know the numbers. I suspect you know the currents. And I think we both know that neither of us walks out of this with what we came for alone."

Underhill looked down at the tables. Then at her. And in his slate-colored eyes she watched the same arithmetic turn over — hope and fear dividing the same sum — before he said, quietly:

"You'd trust me with that? After everything?"

"No," Isla Marchetti said. "But I'd trust your hatred of Halloran. For now, that's the same thing."

Across the tavern, unnoticed by either of them, the dockside boy with the mended net slipped out the door and into the fog, moving fast, toward the harbor office — and the fifty pounds posted on the chandlery wall.
