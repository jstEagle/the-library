## 6. Six Tides

*Isla*

The harbormaster's office smelled of tar and old coffee, and Isla Marchetti had been sitting in its doorway for an hour pretending to read her father's tide tables while she watched the boat-hand with the careful hands.

He worked the fuel dock like a man who wanted to be furniture. That was the tell. Honest men moved through a job; guilty men, or hunted men, became part of it. He'd been hauling lines and scrubbing decks for three days now, and in three days he hadn't once looked at the sea the way the other hands did—with boredom, or appetite. He looked at it the way you look at a door you know is locked and are not sure you want opened.

She'd spent ten years learning to read documents. Ledgers, survey reports, weather logs, the inquiry transcript she'd bought secondhand from a clerk who shouldn't have sold it. Documents didn't lie so much as they went quiet in certain places, and you learned to hear the silence. People were harder. But this man—tall, gray at the temples, moving with that particular economy of someone trained to count every motion against the cold—this man was a document too, and half of him was redacted.

Zephyrine Underhill. She was almost sure. The photographs from the inquiry were twenty years old now, or close enough—ten years old, she corrected herself, ten years since Kestrel Pass, and the man in them had been younger by a whole lifetime. But the jaw was the same. The way he stood slightly off-balance even on flat ground, as if the world were always a slope to him.

Her father had trusted that man with his life. And then the mountain had taken her father, and two governments had decided it was the guide's fault, and Isla had grown up with a name that meant *the girl whose father died under Underhill's rope*.

She closed the tide tables.

The tables themselves had been the strange part. Her father had been meticulous—a surveyor's habit, everything logged, everything cross-referenced—and among his effects there had been a notebook of tidal calculations for a coast he had never, as far as any record showed, visited. Dates going back years. Predictions of extreme low water at a place called Carrick Reach, annotated in his small precise hand: *cave mouth, west cliff, access window*. The last entry was dated nine days before he boarded the plane for Kestrel Pass.

He had known about the cave. He had come here—or sent someone here, or planned to come—and whatever he'd expected to find in it had been worth calculating tides for, months in advance, while he prepared for a mountain expedition on another continent.

Isla had followed the numbers across an ocean and down a fog-bound coast, and now she sat watching the man her father had trusted, waiting for him to do something incriminating enough to justify what she was about to do.

He did it at dusk. He untied a skiff—not his own, one of the boats he tended—and slipped out along the cliff shadow toward the west headland, where the sea had pulled back three days ago farther than anyone could remember, and where a black cave mouth had surfaced out of legend like something rising from a dream.

She followed in a rented dinghy with the engine muffled, and she thought, absurdly, of her father saying: *The water keeps what the land can't afford to lose.*

* * *

*Zephyrine*

He heard the second engine before he saw the second boat, which was the only reason he didn't panic.

Ten years of living below the notice of other people had taught him to listen past the first sound to the sound underneath it—the gull behind the gull, the engine behind the engine. Someone was trailing him through the dusk with a muffled motor and more patience than sense. He let the skiff drift in the lee of the headland and waited, and when the dinghy ghosted around the rocks he said, quietly, into the dark:

"If you're from Halloran, you're wasting your time. If you're not, you're wasting your life."

A long silence. Then a hand-lamp clicked on, low, hooded, pointed at her own face rather than his—an old trick, blinding yourself to see nothing else. The light showed a woman perhaps thirty, sharp-featured, salt wind already in her hair despite having clearly arrived recently, holding a battered notebook against her chest like a shield.

"I'm not from anyone," she said. "I'm from Kestrel Pass."

The name went through him like a spike through the boot sole. He kept his voice level, because level was all he had left. "Nobody's from Kestrel Pass. It's a hole in the world where people stop being."

"My father stopped being there." She killed the lamp. In the dark he heard her breathing, controlled, deliberate—someone who had rehearsed this conversation for years and was furious to find herself shaking anyway. "Marco Marchetti. Client four of six. The inquiry says your rope placement failed on the traverse below the col."

"The inquiry," Zephyrine said, "says a great many things."

"So does my father's last letter." A pause, weighted, offered. "Which I haven't shown you yet. Because I want to see your face when I do."

He should have rowed away then. Every instinct honed over a decade of survival said: *strangers bearing names from your worst night are bait, or bombs.* Instead he found himself shipping the oars, letting the skiff drift, listening to the water suck at the cave mouth fifty yards off where the wreck of the Meridian Star lay sealed in the dark like a held breath.

"Say your piece," he said.

"You've been inside." It wasn't a question. "Three nights ago. I watched you come down the cliff path at two in the morning with wet cuffs and something in your pocket. I've been watching this coast for a week. You're the only person who moves like they already know the approach currents."

"And if I have been inside?"

"Then you've seen the strongbox." Her voice cracked, just once, on the word. "Brass. Stamped with the Halcyon Survey Company crest. My father's employer. The company whose logs the inquiry believed over yours without checking a single entry."

The fog was coming in off the water in slow gray ropes. Somewhere behind them, far out in the bay, lights burned on the salvage fleet that had anchored that morning—three vessels flying colors he knew better than his own scars. He thought about Dree Voss's posted reward, the neat printed notices: *INFORMATION REGARDING UNAUTHORIZED DIVING.* He thought about the silhouette someone had seen on the cliff road, and how many days he might have left being nobody.

"What do you want, Marchetti?"

"The ledgers. The truth. My father's name cleared alongside yours—" she said it like it cost her, "—if it comes to that. And I want to know why you took nothing but a photograph."

He stared at her. The fog swallowed the fleet lights, swallowed the headland, until there was only the skiff and the woman and the cave breathing somewhere in the dark.

"How do you know what I took?"

"Because I know what's in that box," Isla said, "and I know what isn't. Sit still. I'm going to show you my father's tide tables, and then we're going to talk about how much time we have, which is less than either of us wants to say out loud."

* * *

They beached the boats in a fold of rock below the cliff path, and she spread the notebook open on a flat stone, weighting the pages with two shells and his knife. The lamplight made her father's handwriting float up off the paper, small and exact as stitching.

"These aren't guesses," she said. "Marco Marchetti didn't guess. Harmonic analysis, perigean cycles, the storm-surge residual from the winter gales—it's the same method the hydrographic office uses, done by hand, done better. He predicted this year's extreme lows to within centimeters. Look at the annotations."

Zephyrine looked. He had spent his life reading mountains—the grammar of ridgelines, the tense of snowpack—and he recognized the same fluency here, turned on water. Column after column of dates and heights, and beside each one, in that patient hand: *access window, 40 min. Cave mouth, west cliff. Do not attempt on ebb against swell.*

"He came here," Zephyrine said slowly. "Before Kestrel Pass."

"He was here. There's a guesthouse receipt in my mother's things, dated that week. Whatever he learned in that cave—or expected to learn—he flew straight from this coast to that mountain." Her finger stopped on the final entry, and her voice went flat in the way voices go flat when feeling would otherwise drown them. "And here's what matters. Count forward from today."

He counted. The moon phases marched down the margin in her father's neat notation, and the access windows shrank as the spring tides approached and then—

"They stop."

"Spring tides seal it. The neap range won't drop the water below the sill of the cave mouth. Once this cycle closes, that door is shut for four months minimum—probably longer, if the winter surge stacks sand across the entrance the way it did in his data from '09." She looked up at him, and the lamplight caught something hard and bright in her face. "Six more windows. Six tides between now and the seal. After that, whoever holds that box holds it until summer, and Halloran Marine holds salvage permits for these exact coordinates—I checked, under three shell companies, because I assumed they'd check too."

Zephyrine crouched over the notebook and felt the arithmetic settle onto him like ballast. Six tides. On the other side of the bay, three ships rode their anchor lights, and aboard them were divers, cutting gear, winches, lawyers, and money—the kind of money that didn't need luck because it purchased probability outright. Against that: one disgraced guide, one surveyor's daughter, a rented dinghy, and a channel full of currents that charts drew wrong.

"They'll find it anyway," he said. "Sonar doesn't need a low tide. Give them a week of calm water and they'll put divers through the outer passage regardless of what the moon is doing."

"The outer passage kills divers." She said it simply. "That's the legend, isn't it? The cave charges admission. Your old-timers' stories—the ones I've been buying drinks for all week—say the current through the throat runs four knots on the flood and reverses without warning. Their divers will try it with permits and rebreathers and a safety officer, and the cave will take its fee, and Voss will lose people, and she'll keep sending them, because her employer doesn't accept *no* from geology." She paused. "You've met the currents. Haven't you? Three nights ago, in the dark, alone, without a permit or a safety officer or anyone knowing where you were."

It wasn't a question either. This woman asked no questions; she laid facts on stones and waited for you to pick them up.

"Yes," he said. "I've met them."

"Then you're the only person on this coast who knows the way in alive." She closed the notebook and put her hand flat on it, and for a moment the hardness flickered and he saw underneath it: a girl who had been ten years old when the mountain widowed her, who had spent her entire adult life pulling threads out of a lie, who had crossed an ocean on the strength of a dead man's arithmetic. "I don't trust you, Underhill. Ten years ago I would have told you my father trusted you, and look what that bought him. But I need what you know, and you need what I have—which is the timeline, the research, and the only complete copy of the documents that prove what Halloran did to both our names. So."

She extended her hand across the stone, over the tide tables, over her father's patient predictions of doors opening and closing in the sea.

"Alliance," she said. "Uneasy, temporary, and dissolved the moment either of us lies to the other. Those are my terms."

He looked at her hand. He thought about the photograph in his coat lining—his crew, young and laughing, taken the season before Kestrel Pass, the faces of people whose families still received envelopes every quarter from a company that had never once admitted why. He thought about the ledgers in the strongbox, and what publication would mean for those families, and what silence would mean for his own name, and how he had not yet let himself think it all the way through.

He thought about the fleet lights burning through the fog, patient as lice.

"Your father's letter," he said instead of taking her hand. "You said you'd show me. I want to see it before I shake anything."

Something moved through her expression—respect, maybe, or disappointment, or the recognition of a fellow creature who had also learned never to accept terms unread.

"At first light," she said. "There's a room above the chandlery. And Underhill—" she was already gathering the notebook, already turning away, already armored again, "—six tides. We burn the first one tomorrow night. Try not to spend tonight getting recognized."

He watched her climb the cliff path with the lamp hooded, a small fierce light ascending into the fog, and he understood that his decade of anonymity had just ended—not with the fleet's arrival, but with this handshake he hadn't yet given.

Far out in the bay, a bell buoy tolled through the murk, counting the water like a miser.

Six tides. He took her hand's ghost out of the air at last, alone, and went to find out whether he remembered how to sleep before a climb.
