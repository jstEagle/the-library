## 7. What the Water Keeps

The tide did not so much go out as lose interest.

Isla Marchetti stood at the top of the cliff path at half past four in the morning, watching the sea withdraw from the bay like a guest slipping away from a party it had never wanted to attend. The water peeled back in long silver sheets, exposing ribbed sand and weed-slick rock, and beneath the black shoulder of Gannet Head the cave mouth opened—first a seam of darkness, then a doorway, then a throat. The air coming out of it smelled of iron and old salt and something faintly sweet, like fruit rotting in a locked room.

Beside her, Zephyrine Underhill checked his watch against the tide table she'd spread across her knee, and said nothing. He was a man who treated silence as equipment, something packed deliberately for the journey. She had learned that about him in three days of uneasy alliance: he rationed words the way other men rationed rope.

"Window's four hours," he said finally. "Maybe five if the swell stays down."

"And the fleet?"

"Two spotter boats out since dawn yesterday. They run a loop from the point to the shoal marker and back." He nodded toward the gray line where sea met sky. "Voss is thorough. Thorough people are predictable."

Isla folded the tide tables along their worn creases—the same creases her father's thumb had made, ten years ago, when he'd planned an expedition of his own—and tried not to think about how much of this felt like standing at the base of Kestrel Pass again. Different mountain. Same weather inside her chest.

They went down with the water.

* * *

The approach current was everything Zephyrine had promised and worse than she'd imagined. They paddled the borrowed skiff through a channel no wider than a country lane, where the ebb ran against them like a river deciding its course, and Isla pulled until her shoulders burned and her breath came in ragged white plumes. Zephyrine read the water the way she read documents—noting the seams, the eddies, the places where the surface wrinkled over something hidden below.

"Left of the kelp," he said. "Trust the kelp. It grows where the rock isn't going to kill you."

She trusted the kelp.

Inside the cave, the sound changed. The slap and suck of open water gave way to a hollow resonance, the sea breathing through stone, and when they shipped the paddles the silence that followed was so complete Isla could hear her own pulse in her ears. Their headlamps swept across walls furred with pale anemones, across ledges crusted with barnacles as thick as armor, and then—

There.

The Meridian Star lay on her side in the cave's inner chamber, settled into the sand like a sleeping animal. Forty feet of salvage vessel, hull green-black with ten years of growth, her deck crane collapsed across the shingle like a broken arm. Her name was still legible on the stern, gold leaf gone dull but stubborn: MERIDIAN STAR, PORT CARRICK.

Isla's light found the hold hatch. Sealed. Bolted from outside, a chain threaded through the dogs, rusted into a single mass. Whatever was in there had been put away carefully, by someone who intended it to stay put.

"That's not storm damage," Zephyrine said quietly. His beam traced the chain. "That's deliberate. Someone sealed that hold before she went down—or after."

"After," Isla said. "My father's records say the Meridian Star made an unscheduled stop two days before she sank. Cargo manifest amended by hand. Halloran Marine's own survey company signed off on it." She kept her voice flat, clinical, because the alternative was letting it shake. "You don't amend a manifest by hand unless you're hiding what you're carrying."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment, lamplight carving his face out of the dark—deep lines around the eyes, a scar through one eyebrow she hadn't noticed before, or hadn't let herself notice. Then he turned back to the wreck.

"We're not cutting that today," he said. "No gear for it. Today we map. Every obstruction, every collapse risk, every route in and out. If we get one clean window later, we don't waste it learning the layout."

So they mapped. It was slow, cold work, wading thigh-deep through the flooded inner chamber while the tide clocked itself away somewhere beyond the cave mouth. Isla measured; Zephyrine recorded, scratching numbers onto a slate with a grease pencil because paper drowned. He moved through the wreck with a strange familiarity, ducking under the crane arm without looking, stepping over a deck cleat as if he'd stepped over it a hundred times.

"You've been here," she said. Not a question.

"Once." He didn't elaborate. His lamp swung toward a drift of debris against the port bulkhead—rope, canvas, a wooden crate gone soft as bread. "Night after I first saw the cave. I took nothing."

"Liar," she said, and immediately regretted it, because his whole body went still, the stillness of a man who has been accused correctly too many times. But then he reached into his jacket—dry, somehow, tucked against his chest where the water couldn't reach—and held out a photograph.

A crew of eleven men on the Meridian Star's foredeck, squinting into sun, arms slung over shoulders. On the back, in pencil: *Last day out. All hands. — T. Corrigan, capt.*

"I took this," he said. "Because I'm going to give it back. To whoever's left."

Isla took the photograph from him with both hands, the way you take something breakable, or holy. Eleven faces. She looked for her father among them and knew she wouldn't find him—he'd never set foot on the Meridian Star—but grief doesn't consult facts. For a moment every drowned man in the picture wore her father's smile.

Then the light came.

It came through the cave mouth first—a long blade of white sweeping across the chamber wall, bright enough to throw their shadows up into the vaulted dark above. Then the sound followed: engines, muffled by stone but unmistakable, idling low and patient just outside.

Zephyrine killed both lamps with two clicks.

"Spotter," he breathed. "Early. Voss moved her schedule."

The blade of light swung back across the wall, closer now, probing. Somewhere beyond the rocks a boat was nosing toward the entrance, and Isla's mind supplied the image her eyes couldn't have: divers' flags, a launch, the silhouette of Dree Voss at the rail with binoculars. The outer chamber, the one they'd already crossed, would be lit up like a stage within minutes.

"There's a shelf," Zephyrine whispered. "Portside, above the waterline, behind the crane housing. Climb. Now. Don't splash."

They climbed. The crane housing was a lattice of corroded steel, and Isla went up it with her heart hammering against her teeth, finding handholds by feel, the photograph pressed flat between her jacket and the metal where no water could take it. The shelf was barely wide enough for two, a natural pocket in the cave wall screened by a curtain of hanging kelp and the wreck's own superstructure. They folded themselves into it like letters into envelopes.

And they waited.

* * *

Waiting in the dark is its own kind of drowning.

The spotter boat's searchlight came all the way in. Through gaps in the kelp curtain Isla watched the outer chamber turn white, then gold, then white again as the beam hunted across sand and stone. Two shapes entered the water from the launch—divers, fins kicking up phosphorescent ghosts—and began working the outer cave methodically, left to right, the way you'd search a room for a lost coin.

"They're mapping too," Zephyrine murmured, so close to her ear his breath stirred the wet hair at her temple. "Voss doesn't waste tides either."

"How many can she field?"

"Eight divers, maybe ten. Two boats with hard-hat rigs for heavy salvage." A pause. "Cutting gear."

The word landed in Isla's stomach like a dropped anchor. Cutting gear meant the chain on the hold hatch meant the ledgers meant everything—her father's name, Zephyrine's name, the truth that had been lying in the dark for ten years getting loaded onto a Halloran barge and carried away forever.

The divers probed the outer passage for the better part of two hours. Isla's legs cramped. Cold worked its way through her wetsuit in slow increments, a tax collected joint by joint. At some point her hand found Zephyrine's forearm in the dark—not deliberately, just her fingers seeking warmth and finding it—and he didn't pull away, and neither did she, and they stayed like that, two strangers welded together by circumstance and seawater, listening to the enemy breathe through regulators forty feet away.

One diver surfaced near the wreck, close enough that Isla could hear the scrape of his tank valve, see the glint of his mask as he swept his light along the hull. The beam passed over the hold hatch. Paused.

Moved on.

"He saw the chain," Zephyrine breathed. "He'll report it. We've got one tide, maybe two, before they come back with torches."

The divers withdrew. The launch reversed out of the cave mouth with exaggerated care, its wake slapping the walls in diminishing echoes, and then the light was gone and the dark rushed back in like water filling a boot print.

Neither of them moved for a long time.

* * *

They didn't dare cross the outer chamber yet. Instead Zephyrine led her up and out—a crack in the cave's upper wall, a chimney of stone that opened onto the roof of the headland itself, where wind hit them like a wall and the sky was turning the bruised purple of early morning. Below, wrapped around a sea stack a quarter mile offshore, they sheltered in the skiff's lee while the spotter boat completed its loop, its running lights stitching the gray water.

The sea stack was a fist of granite rising thirty feet out of the swell, crowned with guano-white ledges where cormorants hung their wings out to dry. They tied the skiff in its shadow and sat on the rocks with their backs to the stone, sharing a thermos of coffee that had gone lukewarm, watching the fleet's second boat join the first on the horizon like wolves conferring.

"My father wrote me a letter," Isla said.

She hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way water comes through a cracked hull—all at once, and everywhere.

Zephyrine turned his head. Up close, in the flat morning light, he looked older than his years and tired past them, a man built out of weather and waiting.

"The last one. Posted the day before he flew out to meet your expedition." She wrapped both hands around the thermos cup. "I was nineteen. He wrote to me the way he wrote to everyone—half expedition logistics, half terrible jokes about airline food. And then at the end, a paragraph that didn't fit either. He said if anything happened to him on the mountain, I shouldn't accept the company's account of it. He said—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it, "—he said *Halloran's books are cooked, sweetheart, and if I go quiet, that's why.*"

The wind filled the silence. Out on the water, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"I spent six months hating you," Isla went on, because now that the crack had opened she couldn't stop the flood. "You were the guide. You were the man who walked my father up Kestrel Pass and walked down without him. I read the inquiry transcript until I could recite it. Your rope log. Your weather calls. The decision to push for the summit window." She looked at him straight on. "And then I started pulling my father's financial records apart, because grief makes you obsessive and I had nothing else, and I found the payments. Halloran Marine paid the inquiry board's travel expenses. Paid the expert witness. Paid for the transcript printing. Ten years of a man's life destroyed, and it cost them less than a new survey vessel."

Zephyrine said nothing. His jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing on something too large to swallow.

"I know you were framed," she said. "I've known for two years. That's why I came here. Not for the ledgers—for the letter. My father's last letter names Halloran, and I needed to know if anyone else in the world had proof, or if it was just me and a dead man's handwriting against a company with a fleet."

The cormorants shifted on their ledges above them. Far out, the spotter boat turned at the shoal marker and began its long return.

"Why didn't you say any of this," Zephyrine said at last, and his voice was rough, unpolished, like stone broken fresh, "three days ago. On the dock. When you looked at me like you wanted me dead."

"Because I did want you dead. Part of me still does, some mornings." She said it plainly, because he deserved plainness; he'd been fed ten years of lies dressed as procedure. "Grief doesn't update its files just because the evidence changes. But I watched you read the tide tables, and I watched you trust the kelp, and I watched you keep a photograph you stole from a wreck so you could return it to a widow someday, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought, this is either the best liar I've ever met, or the most ruined innocent man on this coast. And I decided to bet on ruined."

Something moved through his face then—too fast and too complicated to name, grief and relief and fury all trying to use the same door. He looked out at the horizon for a long moment, and when he spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"Ten years," he said. "Ten years I assumed everyone who read that transcript believed it. Because believing it was easier. Because I believed parts of it myself, some nights." He shook his head slowly. "Your father wrote that letter and posted it and got on a plane knowing what might happen. And nobody ever found it, because he sent it to a nineteen-year-old girl instead of a newspaper."

"He sent it to someone who'd fight," Isla said. "Newspapers settle. Daughters don't."

That earned her something almost like a smile—it crossed his face and got halfway before the habit of ten years dragged it back down. But it had been there. She'd seen it.

"So," she said. "The chain on the hold. Voss's cutters. One tide, maybe two." She held out her hand, palm up, an offer as formal and old-fashioned as a treaty. "Partners. Properly, this time. No more dockside accusations, no more secrets in jackets. Everything we know, we know together."

Zephyrine looked at her hand. Then, instead of shaking it, he reached into his jacket again and laid something in her palm: a small brass key, green with age, its bow stamped with the same survey-company crest as the strongbox he'd described to her two nights ago.

"Took this the same night as the photograph," he said. "From the captain's cabin. There's a strongbox bolted beside the safe in the hold—I couldn't move it alone, and I wasn't sure I wanted to know what was inside. Now I'm sure." He closed her fingers over the key with his own, his hands steady despite the cold. "Everything we know, we know together. Starting with that."

Isla curled her fist around the key and felt its weight—two ounces of brass carrying ten years of ruin—and understood that trust, like the tide, does not return all at once. It comes back in inches, over hours, leaving foam and wreckage and, if you're lucky, something worth keeping.

Out on the water, the spotter boat altered course. Not its usual loop—something new, a line drawn straight toward the Serrated Reach, toward the channel only Zephyrine could read.

He saw it at the same moment she did. She felt him go rigid beside her.

"She's not searching anymore," he said. "She's guarding the way in."

Which meant Voss knew exactly where they'd have to come from—which meant someone had talked, or someone was about to—and the next tide was in eighteen hours, and the key in Isla's fist suddenly weighed as much as the sea itself.
