## 8. The Informant

The boy came to me the way fog comes to a harbor—not all at once, but in layers, until you realized he'd been there all along.

I'd seen him before, of course. You see everyone on a working dock, and after three weeks of hauling crates for men who never asked my name, I had become one of those things that is seen without being noticed: a broad-shouldered boat-hand with salt-bleached hair and a face people's eyes slid off like rain off oilskin. The boy was different. He watched. He watched the way gulls watch a fish-cleaning table—patient, opportunistic, with that flat arithmetic of hunger behind the eyes.

His name, I learned from the crab-pot menders who tolerated his errands, was Tam Pell. Fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and sharp chin, with a voice that hadn't decided yet whether it wanted to be a man's. His father had drowned off the Serrated Reach when Tam was small, which made him either the most tragic boy in Carrickmoor or the best-informed, depending on which dockside philosopher you asked. He ran messages. He sold gossip. He knew which boats were carrying what and which skippers owed which harbormaster which favors, and he sold it all by the scrap, cheap, to anyone who bought.

On the morning it happened, I was mending net in the lee of the fish shed, my hands working the twine while my mind worked the tide tables Isla had copied out in her precise, furious hand. Six tides, she'd said. We'd spent one already—the failed attempt, the long cold hours wedged inside the sea stack while Voss's divers probed the outer cave like fingers feeling for a coin in a pocket. Five left. And somewhere across the gray water, three ships rode at anchor with Halloran Marine's colors at their gaffs, patient as sharks.

"Mister." The voice came low, from beside the stacked creels where no one else stood.

I didn't look up right away. That's the first rule of being nobody: never show that you heard your own name, even when it isn't your name.

"Mister," Tam said again, closer now, and then, with the delicacy of a pickpocket laying a watch on a table: "I know who you are."

The twine went on moving through my fingers. In, over, under, draw tight. My heart did something complicated and quiet, the way water does under ice.

"Do you," I said.

"You're the one goes out at night. You and her. The Marchetti woman." He crouched down beside me, all knees and angles, close enough that I could smell woodsmoke and herring brine on him. "Out past the point, in a boat with no lights. Two nights back, and once before that. I seen you."

"Boys see a lot of things," I said. "Most of them aren't true."

"This one is." He glanced toward the harbor office, quick and practiced, a bird checking for hawks. "And the woman from the *Vigilance*—the salvage fleet—she's been asking. Posting coin for it, too. Coin for anything about unauthorized diving, about night boats, about strangers." He let that sit between us like bait in clear water. "Five pounds just for a name. More for where you're going."

So there it was. Voss had cast her net wide, and the net was made of money, and money in a poor harbor catches everything smaller than itself. I kept my eyes on the netting. In, over, under, draw tight.

"And you thought," I said, "you'd come tell me first. Out of kindness."

"I thought," Tam said, with a grin that showed too many teeth for its size, "I'd come tell you first because whoever pays last pays most. The fleet woman offered five. Maybe you'll offer six."

There it was, then. Plain as a gutted fish. The boy wasn't selling me protection; he was selling me an auction, with himself as auctioneer, and the bidding would go on until one of us stopped. I set down the netting slowly and looked at him properly for the first time. Up close his bravado had seams in it. His hands, wrapped around his own knees, were chapped raw at the knuckles, and there was a bruise going yellow along his jaw that he'd turned away from the light without seeming to think about it. Somebody had hit him recently. Somebody bigger.

"How much do you actually need?" I asked.

The question wrong-footed him. I watched it land, watched the merchant fall away for half a second and the child look out—hungry, tired, fourteen years old and running messages in a harbor full of strangers' ships.

"What?"

"To eat. To live. Whatever it is you need the five pounds for." I kept my voice level, uninterested, the way you talk to a spooked animal or a frightened client on a bad pitch of rock. "Because if it's five, I can't help you, and neither can she, and you'll sell me to Voss and be hungry again by Thursday. But if it's something real—if someone's leaning on you—" I nodded, very slightly, at the yellowed bruise. "Then maybe we should talk about who's doing the leaning."

The boy's face closed like a door. But doors that close fast are doors that hide rooms, and I had spent ten years learning to read locked things.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"No," I agreed. "You wouldn't."

* * *

I found Isla that afternoon in the back room above the chandlery she'd rented under a name that wasn't hers, spread over with charts the way other tables are spread with cards. She looked up when I came in, and whatever she saw in my face made her put down her pencil.

"We have a problem," I said. "Or possibly an opportunity. They wear the same coat."

I told her about Tam Pell. She listened the way she did everything—completely, without blinking, her whole attention a physical weight—and when I finished she was quiet for a long moment, looking at the chart of the bay as though the boy might be hiding somewhere in the contour lines.

"He's the harbormaster's errand boy," she said at last. "I've seen him carrying papers to the *Vigilance*. Official-looking ones. Harbor schedules, pilotage notes."

"That's what worries me. A boy like that doesn't get access to official papers out of charm. Someone gives them to him. Which means someone wants them carried—which means Voss has a friend ashore." I pulled the chart toward me and tapped the harbor office, a small square of ink at the head of the quay. "Harbormaster Grell. Ten years ago he signed the permit that let Halloran's survey ships work this coast without local oversight. Everyone's forgotten that. I haven't."

"So the boy sells information to both sides," Isla said, "and the man who owns the boy owns the channel into the harbor." Her jaw tightened. "Then we cut the boy loose. We don't tell him anything. We move without him knowing."

"No." I surprised myself with how quickly it came. "That's exactly what Voss expects. A hunted man hides. A hunted man goes silent, changes his route, stops trusting everyone—and every change he makes tells you where he was." I traced the coastline with one finger, from the cliffs down to the black slash of the cave mouth, hidden now beneath twelve feet of cold green water. "We don't cut him loose. We turn him."

"Turn him." Isla said it flatly, testing the weight. "He's a child."

"He's a child who's been hit by someone and sent to sell me. That's not a spy, Isla. That's a lever. And levers work both ways." I met her eyes. "You've spent ten years pulling your father's records apart. You know how these machines work. Halloran doesn't buy loyalty—it buys fear, and fear comes cheaper in bulk. Give the boy something better than fear and he'll carry whatever we want straight into Voss's cabin."

"And what do we have that's better than fear?"

I looked at the chart, at the deep-water channel marked in blue that every pilot in the fleet would read as gospel, and felt the old mountain instinct stir in me—that cold, clean pleasure of seeing a route where other men saw only rock.

"A lie," I said. "A beautiful one."

* * *

We built it that evening, carefully, the way you build anything meant to bear weight. Isla drew new markings on an old chart—one of her father's, worn soft at the folds, the kind of document that carries its own credibility. We aged the ink with tea. We folded it wrong, refolded it, rubbed the creases with wax until they looked like they'd lived in a pocket for weeks. On it, in a hand not quite mine and not quite anyone's, we marked a passage through the outer shoals—a false approach to the cave, plausible enough to sound like hard-won local knowledge, wrong enough to put any boat that trusted it squarely onto the teeth of the Carrick Bar at half tide.

Then I went to find the boy.

Tam Pell was waiting for me, as I'd known he would be, in the alley behind the net loft where the lamplight didn't reach. He tried to keep the merchant's grin on, but it kept slipping, and underneath it his face was pale.

"Well?" he said. "Have you got an offer? Because the fleet woman's offer doesn't sit still. It grows."

"It does," I said. "That's how she works. Dree Voss buys cheap and sells dear, and what she sells is people." I stepped closer, and I let him see me look at the bruise on his jaw, deliberately, the way you'd point at a mark on a chart. "Who gave you that?"

"None of your—"

"Grell," I said. "The harbormaster. He found out you'd been sniffing around the fleet's business on your own account, and he reminded you whose boy you are. Am I close?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere beyond the alley, the tide was turning, that vast slow breath the sea takes twice a day, and I waited through it the way I'd learned to wait out storms on exposed faces—still, patient, letting the weather make the first mistake.

"He says what I hear belongs to him," Tam said finally, and his voice had gone small and sullen and young. "Everything on his dock. Everything in his harbor. He says if I sell outside, he'll have me pressed onto a trawler crew so far north I won't see land for two years." His chin came up, defiant and miserable at once. "So. Have you got an offer or not?"

"Yes," I said. "Here it is. Not money."

His face fell, and I talked over the falling.

"Money runs out. Money gets you found. What I'm offering is the thing Grell actually took from you, which is ownership. Of yourself." I held out the folded chart. "You take this to Voss tonight. You tell her you stole it from the Marchetti woman's room—you tell her you're clever, and greedy, and that you'll bring more. Let her believe she's bought you. Because from tonight, you're not Grell's boy and you're not Voss's boy. You're ours, and here's why that's better than five pounds: in a month, when this is over, there will be people on this coast who owe you more than money. People who remember. The kind of people who can get a bright lad a berth on a proper vessel, with papers, with pay, with a future that isn't running messages through alleys for a man who hits him."

It was a good speech. I'd have believed it myself, once, before I learned how rarely such futures arrive. But the boy was staring at the chart in my hand, and hunger does its own arithmetic, and sometimes the difference between a lie and a promise is only whether the person making it intends to try.

"Why should I trust you?" he whispered.

"Because I'm the one taking the risk," I said. "Grell owns this harbor. Voss owns three ships. All I own is a boat with no lights and a grave's worth of trouble. If you betray me, you lose nothing. If you stand with me, you're betting on the only side in this harbor that isn't buying." I pushed the chart into his hands. "Besides. Your father drowned on the Serrated Reach, didn't he? Running the channel honest, in bad water, because somebody ashore cut corners on the pilotage fees."

I saw it strike home, the way a piton strikes home—hard, deep, holding. Ten years of grief, and nobody had ever told the boy his father's death had a shape, a cause, a name worth hating.

"The ledgers in that cave," I said quietly, "name the men who cut those corners. Not just mine. All of them. Every wreck this coast was fed to, for profit." That much was true, or true enough; Isla's father's letter had hinted at rot that went back decades. "You wouldn't be selling me out by helping us, Tam. You'd be collecting your father's debt."

The boy looked down at the chart in his hands. His thumbs moved over the fold lines, reading it the way dockside children read everything—by touch, by feel, by the language of paper and crease.

"If Voss finds out it's false," he said, "she'll—"

"She won't find out. That's the whole art of it." I put my hand briefly on his shoulder, and felt him flinch and then, grudgingly, not flinch. "Tonight. Her launch comes to the quay steps at nine. Be on it."

* * *

He was. I watched from the dark end of the quay, muffled in a borrowed coat, as the small boat slid out across the black water toward the anchored fleet, one lantern swinging at its bow like a slow pendulum counting down the hours. The boy sat stiffly upright among the sailors, clutching his stolen prize, and I felt the strange double weight of what I'd done—pride and sickness in the same hand, like a tool with two edges.

We had turned him. The lie was planted. By tomorrow morning, Dree Voss would believe she owned the eyes we wanted her to own, and her divers would spend their next window probing shoals that would chew her tender to splinters if she pushed too far, too fast.

A small victory. I held it up to the light and examined it, the way you examine a hold you've placed in questionable rock, and that was when I saw the flaw.

It came to me standing there in the cold, watching the lantern shrink toward the fleet, and it arrived with the particular chill of a rope coming slack when you thought it was anchored. Tam would deliver the chart. Tam would play his part. But Tam reported to Grell—that was the architecture of the whole arrangement, the reason the boy had access, the reason the bruise existed. Nothing moved through that harbor office without crossing the harbormaster's desk. And if Grell read the boy's reports before they went out—if he compared our false approach against the real charts, against ten years of his own quiet knowledge of these waters—

Grell had signed permits for Halloran a decade ago. Grell's loyalty had been bought before I ever came to this coast, before the cave ever opened, perhaps before Isla's father ever boarded that expedition. He wasn't Voss's informant. Voss was his instrument.

Which meant the false coordinates might reach her deck already annotated. Already corrected. Already turned back upon us like a compass needle reversed.

The lantern reached the *Vigilance* and went out.

Across the black water, three ships lay at anchor with their riding lights burning, and behind them the cliffs stood blind and enormous, keeping the cave, keeping the dead, keeping the truth in its sealed brass box. Five tides left. And somewhere in the warm-lit harbor office at the head of the quay, a man who had been paid to forget things was very probably remembering.
