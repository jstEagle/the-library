## 9. Dead Reckoning

The chart was older than either of us.

Zephyrine unrolled it across the table in the back room of Maren's net loft with the reverence of a man handling a shroud, weighting the corners with a brass compass, a coil of waxed twine, and a stone that might once have been a doorstop. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, soft as cloth at the folds, and someone—decades ago, by the hand—had inked the coastline of the Serrated Reach in a careful, confident line that no modern survey would dare repeat: *Here be teeth.*

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

"Same place I got everything worth having." He didn't look up. "A place nobody wanted it."

I had been in this room three nights running now, and each night the fog outside had come a little closer to the windows, as if the sea were leaning in to listen. The loft smelled of tar and hemp and old rain, of the particular salt-sweetness of rope that has lived its whole life wet. A single lamp swung on a hook above us, and every time the building shuddered under a gust off the water, our shadows leaned and righted themselves like sailors.

I should have been afraid. I was, a little. But mostly I was awake in a way I hadn't been in ten years—the way you're awake on a summit ridge at three in the morning, when the cold has burned away everything except the next step.

"Show me again," I said. "The Reach."

He set his finger on the channel between the outer shoals and the cliff base—a ragged corridor maybe two hundred meters wide on the chart, though he'd told me twice that the chart lied, that at low water it narrowed to less than half that, and that the water inside it moved like something with an opinion.

"The flood tide comes up the coast from the south," he said, tracing it. "Hits the headland here—" his nail tapped a promontory drawn with hatching like scar tissue "—and splits. Half goes out to sea, half goes into the Reach. Inside, the channel bends twice around these stacks, and where it bends, it speeds up. First bend is nothing. Second one—" He paused, and I watched him go somewhere else for a moment, some other coast, some other year. "The second one is where the water decides if it likes you."

"And you can read it."

"I can read water." He said it without pride, the way another man might say *I can breathe*. "Current leaves signatures. Ridges on the surface, lines of foam, the way kelp lies down. On a mountain it's wind loading on a slope, hollow sounds under your boots, the way snow grains shift. It's all the same language. Most people just never learn to listen."

I thought of my father then—not the way I usually did, which was as a photograph going gray at the edges, but as a man who had hired a guide because he trusted exactly this: that somewhere there were people who could read a mountain the way Zephyrine read water. That trust had killed him. Or so the inquiry had decided, in a room smelling of pipe smoke and institutional carpet, while men from Halloran's survey company sat in the gallery with their ties knotted neat as nooses.

"I want to see the ledgers published," I said.

It came out flat, and I meant it to. We had been circling this for two days, the way weather circles before it commits. Every time we planned the run—the boat, the tides, the cutting gear we still had to find—I could feel the argument waiting underneath the plans like a rock under new snow. Tonight I put my boot on it deliberately.

Zephyrine's hands went still over the chart.

"You've seen what's in that list," I went on. "Nineteen families. Nineteen envelopes of hush money, paid quarterly, for ten years, out of a company account labeled *contingencies*. My father is dead because those men falsified their logs, and they bought nineteen families' silence to keep it buried." I heard my own voice climb and made myself bring it down. "Publication clears you. It names the founder. It names the inquiry officials who took the payments. It gives my father back his name—which is the only inheritance he left me."

"It takes away theirs," Zephyrine said quietly.

"Their *what*?"

"The money." He finally looked up, and the lamplight caught the gray in his beard, the network of fine scars at his hairline that I had started, against my will, to be able to read like a map of everywhere he'd ever been. "You think those families are living well on it? I've seen the amounts. It's rent money. It's boots for the kids and coal for the winter. Widow Corrigan on the north shore—her husband was bosun on the *Meridian Star*. She's got a son in a wheelchair since a worksite fall. You publish those ledgers, and yes, the payments stop, because the moment they're public, every one of those families becomes evidence. And the company lawyers will claw back what they can, and the town will know exactly which houses have been taking Halloran's coin, and those children will grow up as the kids whose mothers sold their fathers' memory." His voice never rose. That was the worst of it. He spoke about ruin the way he spoke about currents—as something he had already measured. "I lived through being the name everyone spat. I wouldn't hand that to nineteen more people. Not even for my own name back."

"So you'd stay condemned." I stood up. The chair scraped; the lamp swung; our shadows reeled. "Ten years, Zeph. Ten years you've been a ghost, working other men's boats under other men's names, and you're telling me you'll do ten more—thirty more—rather than let the truth cost anyone anything?"

"I'm telling you justice that's paid for with other people's lives isn't justice. It's just a bigger version of what Halloran did. They spent those families to protect themselves. You want to spend them to protect me."

"That is not the same thing and you know it."

"It's close enough that it should keep you up at night."

I turned away from him, to the window, because he was right and I hated it. The fog had reached the glass now; beyond it the harbor lights burned in halos, and somewhere out in that blindness the salvage fleet lay at anchor, Voss's ships riding their lights like patient animals. Six tides, she'd said—or rather, Isla's arithmetic had said it, and Voss's divers had confirmed it by their persistence. The cave would open five more times before the spring tides sealed it away until summer. And after tonight's gale warning, maybe fewer than that.

"My father wrote me a letter," I said to the window. "You know that. The last one. He named Halloran in it—he knew what they were doing on that pass, he'd found the falsified readings himself, and he was coming down the mountain to make it public when the storm took him." I turned back. "He wasn't a man who could be bought, Zeph. He died trying to publish. And you want to honor him by burying the proof?"

"He died trying to do right by people," Zephyrine said. "The publishing was the method, not the point."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The lamp flame bent in a draft from under the door, and the whole room seemed to hold its breath with us.

Then Zephyrine did something I hadn't seen him do. He sat down slowly, rubbed both hands over his face, and when they came away he looked, for the first time since I'd known him, simply tired—not guarded-tired, not performing competence, but the bone-deep exhaustion of a man who has been carrying a weight so long he's forgotten it isn't part of his skeleton.

"Do you know what the inquiry asked me?" he said. "Eleven hours of testimony, and the question that decided everything was: *Why did you descend the east couloir when your own judgment flagged avalanche risk?* And I told them the truth—that your father asked to, that the client's wishes carry weight, that I judged the slope stable within margins, that the storm arrived six hours ahead of every forecast. And they read out Halloran's log, which said the forecast had warned us, which said I'd been advised against the route, which was all lies—and the lies had signatures on them, and my judgment was only my word." He looked at his hands. "My word against paper. Paper always wins. So now you're asking me to bet nineteen families' livelihoods on paper winning again. This time our paper. But it's the same wager, Isla. It's the same table."

"And if we don't sit down at it," I said, "the house keeps everything. Forever."

He didn't answer. Outside, the wind found a loose sheet of tin somewhere along the waterfront and worried it, a slow metallic groan like something large turning over in its sleep.

* * *

We worked through the argument by working through the plan, which is how sailors and mountaineers settle most things too large for words.

The Serrated Reach could only be run on the last hour of the ebb, when the current slackened enough to give a small boat a fighting chance but the water was still deep enough over the inner shoals. That gave them—gave *us*—a window of perhaps forty minutes, at night, in fog, through a channel with two blind bends and a seabed the charts had abandoned as a bad job. Any other pilot would refuse. Any other pilot would be right to.

But Zephyrine had spent three winters here, running mail and fish crates through weather that kept honest boats tied up, and he had learned the Reach the way he claimed to learn everything: by listening to it fail other people first.

"There's a ledge," he said, marking it with the graphite stub in a series of short, hard strokes. "Second bend, eastern wall, just below high-water line. If the current's lying down, there's an eddy behind it big enough to hold a dinghy. We wait there for the turn of tide, then ride the last of the ebb into the cave mouth. Coming out, we do it in reverse on the flood—but the flood runs harder, so we'll have maybe twenty minutes, not forty."

"And if the current isn't lying down?"

"Then we sit on the ledge until it is, or until the tide turns against us and we spend the night on a shelf of rock the size of a kitchen table." He glanced up. "Bring blankets."

I almost laughed. It startled me, the laugh—it felt foreign in my chest, like a coin found in the pocket of a dead man's coat. Ten years I had carried my father's death like ballast, and here was this ruined man drawing me a route through the dark with a pencil stub, telling me to bring blankets, and some part of me that I'd thought drowned was kicking back toward the light.

"And the fleet?" I asked. "Voss has spotters on the headland. The harbormaster's boy told us her divers are rigging for a night attempt of their own."

"They'll try the outer passage," Zephyrine said. "It's the obvious line—wider, straighter, and it kills you slower, which fools people into thinking it's safer. Voss is good, but she's reading charts and a fathometer. Charts don't tell you the second bend backs up on itself when the swell's from the northeast. Fathometers don't tell you anything fast enough to matter at the speed that water moves." He rolled the pencil between his fingers. "She'll lose a diver or nearly lose one, and she'll pull back, and while she's pulling back, we'll be inside."

"You sound certain."

"I sound like a man who's checked his knots." But he smiled—one-sided, brief, gone again—and I understood that certainty, for him, was not the absence of fear but a decision made daily, hourly, the way you decide to keep breathing.

We divided the rest of it like survivors dividing rations. I would get the cutting gear—a borrowed oxy-acetylene rig from a farm-wrecking yard two valleys inland, hauled over in Maren's oyster punt under nets, because no harbor yard would rent torches to two people clearly planning something. Zephyrine would take the boat itself, a sixteen-foot gaff-rigged tender named, with a grimness I only later appreciated, the *Second Chance*, and he would run her solo through the bends while I handled the line and the light. He would not hear otherwise.

"One pair of hands on the tiller," he said. "Mine. Because in white water you don't discuss, you don't vote—you read and you act, and the reading has to live in one set of nerves. You'll be on the bow with the searchlight and the heaving line, and when I call a mark, you hit it. No questions mid-channel."

"No questions mid-channel," I agreed. "And afterward? When we have the safe open and the ledgers in our hands?" I held his eyes. "What then, Zeph? Because that's not a channel. That one we have to talk in."

He was quiet for a moment. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere below us in the loft, Maren's dog turned over in its sleep, tags clinking.

"Then we talk," he said at last. "And I'll tell you now what I'll tell you then: I mean to send copies to the maritime court, sealed, with the confession letter—the founder's confession, the inquiry officials' names. That much goes on record, because that much costs the families nothing and gives us everything else. And the payment lists, the account numbers, the quarterly schedules—" his jaw worked "—those burn. Or they go in a bank vault until every last child on that list is grown and gone, whichever comes first."

"And my father's name sits in that vault with them. Yours too."

"For a while longer."

"*A while*." I shook my head. "You've already served ten years of *a while*. When does it end? Who decides?"

"The same person who decided when it started," he said, and there was something in his face then that stopped me—something that wasn't stubbornness at all but grief, old and worn smooth as beach glass. "Me. I decided to take those clients up the pass, Isla. My signature is on the route card next to your father's. Whatever Halloran forged afterward, the fact underneath is mine: I made the call, and the call got him killed. Clearing my name doesn't clear that. It never will. So if the price of keeping nineteen families fed is that I stay the man who lost Marchetti on Kestrel Pass—" he spread his hands, empty, "—that's a price I can pay. I've been paying it. I know the exchange rate."

I wanted to scream at him. I wanted to shake him. And underneath both of those, worse, I wanted to weep, because I recognized the arithmetic. I had been doing it myself for a decade—every unopened file, every interview declined, every year I worked a job beneath my training because pursuing the truth about Halloran meant becoming *that woman*, the daughter who wouldn't let it go, and some cowardly part of me had called the silence protection when it was only fear wearing a coat.

"You really believe it," I said. "That you owe them that."

"I believe debts are real," he said. "Everything else is negotiable."

* * *

We finished the plan as the fog thickened to milk against the windows, and I copied the channel marks into my notebook in my own hand—*first bend, keep the broken water on your left shoulder; second bend, the eddy behind the ledge; the cave opens on the last third of the ebb, green light on the northern wall means depth, brown means teeth*—because a good climber writes down the route even when the guide knows it by heart. Redundancy keeps you alive. My father taught me that. One of the last useful things he taught me before the mountain took the rest.

Zephyrine stood at the table rolling the old chart closed, and for a moment, watching his hands move—careful, economical, unhurried—I saw what Isla-of-three-days-ago would never have believed: the guide he must have been before. The one clients trusted with their lives and gave, apparently, gladly. The one my father chose.

"First bend," I said. "Broken water on the left shoulder."

He looked up, surprised.

"If we're doing this," I said, "then I'm learning it properly. All of it. I'm not cargo, Zeph. I'm crew."

Something moved through his expression too fast to name—something that might, in a different life, in a different decade, have been gratitude, or relief, or the beginning of trust. He nodded once.

"Crew," he said.

I was at the door, pulling my collar up against the fog, when he spoke again, quieter.

"Isla. The list of families. Corrigan's boy—the one in the wheelchair." He hesitated, and I realized he was choosing, carefully, whether to say the thing he'd decided to say. "There's a name on that list I didn't expect. Third page. You should look at it before we run the Reach. Before you decide how far you're willing to go."

"What name?"

He just looked at me, and the lamp swung, and our shadows leaned apart across the floorboards like two people standing on opposite banks of something deep and moving.

I opened the notebook to the copied pages of the ledger list, ran my finger down the third page—and stopped.

The handwriting swam. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the fog, out on the black water, a ship's bell rang once, twice, and went silent, and I understood that whatever I had thought this was—a rescue, a reckoning, a simple race between us and the fleet—it had just become something else entirely.

Because there, on Halloran's private list of silenced families, in the column marked *relationship to crew*, beside a name I had known my whole life—

—was the word *daughter*.
