## 10. Tide Three

The third tide came in on a Tuesday, grey and businesslike, without the theatrics of the first two. Isla had the tables spread across the floor of her rented room above the chandlery, weighted at the corners with coffee mugs and a brass compass that had belonged to her father, and she traced the curve of it with one finger like she was reading a scar.

"Window's four hours," she said. "Maybe four and a half if the swell stays down. After that the cave floods to the roof and we're swimming with everything we came for."

"And everything we didn't come for," I said.

She looked up at me across the lamplight. We had not yet learned how to be in a small room together without the past sitting between us like a third person, but we were learning. That was something. Ten days ago she'd stood on the harbour wall and told me my name was a lie I'd built out of other people's grief, and now she was handing me her father's compass to hold while she rolled the charts. Grief does strange arithmetic. It can subtract a decade and add an ally in the same breath.

We went out at slack water before dawn, running dark through the Serrated Reach with the engine at half-throttle and no lights but the green smear of the compass card. The Reach lived up to its name even in calm — the current ran against us in long, muscular surges, and twice the hull shuddered as we crossed water that was fighting over some submerged argument of rock. I steered by feel more than chart. Mountains taught you that. A ridgeline is just a wave of stone; a wave is just a ridge of water. You read the wrinkles. You read where the force piles up and where it folds away, and you put yourself in the folds.

Isla sat beside me with her hood up, watching my hands on the wheel rather than the water ahead.

"You've done this before," she said. Not a question.

"I've done worse in the dark."

"With clients?"

"With clients who paid me to get them somewhere everyone said they couldn't go." I kept my eyes forward. "That was the job. Before."

She let it sit. She was getting better at letting things sit, which I suspected cost her more than it cost me. Her kind of grief wanted answers the way a wound wants air. Mine had learned to breathe underwater.

* * *

The cave took us differently by day than it had by night. In darkness it had been a throat, all echo and pressure. In the flat grey light filtering down through the entrance — a ragged oval maybe eight metres across, ten metres up from the seabed shelf — it was a cathedral. The water inside was that impossible colour you only find in flooded places, a green-gold like light through bottle glass, and our exhaled bubbles rose in silver strings toward a ceiling furred with white plumose anemones that opened and closed like slow breathing.

The Meridian Star lay on her starboard side along the cave's far wall, exactly where she'd been for eleven years, and exactly where I'd found her on that first trespassing visit. Seeing her again was like meeting someone you'd dreamed about and finding them unchanged, which is its own kind of unsettling. Salvage ships are working vessels, blunt and practical, and this one had gone down with her work half-done: the crane arm still swung out over the collapsed deck, trailing cable like a snapped fishing line, and her hold hatch gaped where the sea had peeled it back in the first storm after the sinking.

Isla dropped through the hatch ahead of me, fins kicking, her torch beam cutting a clean cone through the murk. Inside the hull the visibility dropped to a few metres — the sea keeps its houses dusty — and the world contracted to torchlight and the sound of our own regulators, that rhythmic wet breathing that makes every diver sound like they're sleeping.

The strongbox sat where I'd left it, aft of amidships, half-buried under a drift of silt and collapsed shelving. Brass, or what had been brass — eleven years underwater had turned it the colour of old bone, furred green at the seams, the stamp of the survey company still legible on the lid: HALLOWAY & VOSS GEODETIC SURVEYS. My company. The letters that had signed off on falsified route logs, that had hung the Kestrel Pass disaster around my neck and walked away whistling.

I got both hands on it and lifted.

It moved six inches and stopped hard, with a sound I felt through my gloves more than heard — a deep, structural clank, the sound of one heavy thing refusing to part from another heavier thing.

Isla's torch swung over. She saw my face through the mask and swam closer, and together we swept the silt back with our gloves until the shape of it emerged: the strongbox wasn't free-standing at all. It was bolted — four bolts, each thick as my thumb — through its base into the lid of a ship's safe, a proper iron safe, the captain's safe, riveted plates and a dial worn smooth, the whole assembly lashed to a frame member that had once held the chart table. Someone aboard the Meridian Star had wanted those ledgers to stay with the ship's most guarded possession. Someone had trusted the safe more than the sea.

Or less, I thought. Maybe they'd trusted nothing, and bolted the truth to the heaviest thing they had.

Isla pulled a folding spanner from her thigh pocket — she'd come prepared, because Isla Marchetti came prepared for everything except the things that actually happened — and got it onto the first bolt nut. The nut turned a quarter-turn and then simply spun, corroded to lace, the threads gone to orange dust. She tried the second. Same story. Eleven years of seawater had eaten the fastenings into decoration.

She looked at me and shook her head, and behind the mask her eyes did the thing they did when she was furious at the ocean specifically.

*Back,* I signalled. *Air check.* We were forty minutes in with maybe twenty-five of bottom time left, and the tide window would start closing whether we liked it or not. The sea doesn't negotiate. I'd spent ten years learning that lesson and it had never once offered a refund.

But before I turned for the hatch, my torch caught on something in the debris field aft — a tangle of line, pale against the black silt, snagged over a broken locker door.

I knew that rope.

You know your own gear the way you know your own handwriting. This was 9-mil static line, the kind we used for fixed anchors on glacier travel, and it was spliced in a particular way — a double fisherman's with a long tail whipped in red cord — because that was how I spliced it, because a client named Aldous Marchetti had once laughed at my knots and said, *Zeph, you whip everything like you're afraid the mountain will notice if you're sloppy.*

My hands stopped working. My regulator hissed. For a moment there was nothing in the world but that pale coil in the torchlight, eleven years drowned, impossibly far from any mountain.

It couldn't be mine. That was the thought, arriving clean and reasonable: ropes don't migrate. Rope lost on Kestrel Pass — lost the night the storm took him — stayed on Kestrel Pass, three hundred kilometres inland, two thousand metres up. Rope did not walk to the coast and stow away aboard a salvage ship and sink with it into a cave beneath the cliffs of a fog-bound nowhere.

And yet.

I swam to it. My gloves shook, and I noticed the shaking with the detached interest of a man watching weather happen to someone else. I lifted the coil free of the locker. It came up trailing silt in slow ribbons, and the core was stiff, salt-fused, dead weight — but the splice was intact, and the whipping was red, and when I turned it over my mark was there, burned into the rope end with a soldering iron in a bunkhouse nine years before the pass: **Z.U.** Two letters, small and crooked, in a hand I'd have known anywhere because it was mine.

There was no version of the world where this made sense. There was only the fact of it, cold and wet and real in my glove, heavier than rope has any right to be.

Isla touched my shoulder. She'd seen my face. She pointed at the rope, then at me, her question visible even through glass: *Yours?*

I nodded. And because there was no way to explain, and no air budget for trying, I clipped it to my harness ring — the way you clip a client's fallen axe to your own pack, the way you carry what people lose — and I kicked for the hatch, and the Meridian Star let us go the way the sea lets go of everything eventually: without comment.

* * *

We surfaced in the cave's air pocket beneath the entrance arch, hanging on to the anchor line in the grey-green half-light, masks pushed up, breathing the cold mineral smell of the place. Above us the oval of sky showed high cloud moving fast. Below us the wreck waited with its secret welded shut.

"It's bolted to the safe," Isla said. She said it flatly, the way you state a diagnosis. "Which means we need cutting gear. Which means—"

"A hydraulic cutter, or a burning bar. Neither of which fits in a dive bag, and neither of which you buy in a town with one harbormaster and a fleet of Halloran boats anchored in the bay."

"So we steal one."

"We borrow one," I said, "from people who owe us nothing, without being seen, during a gale." I spat water. "Yes. Essentially we steal one."

She almost smiled. It was the closest we'd come to humour, and it sat between us for a moment like a bird that might fly off if either of us moved.

Then her eyes went to the rope on my harness, dripping between us, coiled against my chest like something rescued rather than recovered.

"You want to tell me about that now?"

I looked at it. The red whipping had faded to pink. The burned letters were still legible, stubborn little scars in the fibre. Ten years I had built my life on the premise that Kestrel Pass was behind me — sealed, sunken, done — and here was a piece of it floating in a sea cave, having travelled further without me than I'd ever managed with it.

"No," I said honestly. "But I will."

I told her about the expedition. Not the inquiry version — the real one. Six clients, a late-season window, a storm that came in twelve hours ahead of every forecast. The fixed lines I'd placed myself, whipped in red cord. The traverse below the serac where the snow bridge failed. How I'd gotten five people down and one man hadn't come back, and how the company's logs — the logs Halloway & Voss filed with the certification boards — showed a route approval and a weather briefing that had never happened, dated three days before the climb, signatures that weren't mine on pages I'd never seen. How the inquiry had asked me one question — *why did you proceed* — and never asked the question that mattered, which was *who wrote these documents*, because the men who could answer it were the men paying for the inquiry.

"The ledgers in that box," I said, "are the survey company's financial records. Every payment, every contract, every falsified log bought and filed. If they're in there, they say who signed my name."

"And the rope?" Isla asked quietly. "How does your rope end up in a shipwreck?"

"I don't know." I turned the coil over in the water. "I lost it on the pass. Upper section, near the serac. I remember the exact spot. I've remembered the exact spot for ten years, because losing gear on a mountain is how people die, and I've never forgiven myself for losing anything anywhere."

The cave breathed around us. Somewhere below, in the flooded corridors of the Meridian Star, the rest of the past waited with its bolts rusted shut.

"Eleven years ago," Isla said slowly, and I watched her do the arithmetic I was refusing to do, "the Meridian Star was doing salvage survey work up the coast. Before it sank. Dad mentioned it once — there was a dispute about a contract, Halloran versus the geodetic people. What if—" She stopped. Started again. "What if the ship called at the mountain region? What if someone aboard collected lost equipment? Guides' gear gets catalogued after accidents. It gets sold, sometimes. It gets *salvaged*, Zephyrine. That's literally the word for it."

Salvaged. My rope, dragged off a mountainside by strangers, tagged and traded and loaded aboard a ship that then carried it down into the dark — so that the only piece of my old life I'd ever get back was waiting inside the wreck of the thing that ruined me. The sea had taken eleven years to arrange the joke, but it had arranged it thoroughly.

"There's something else," I said. "The safe. The ledgers aren't loose in a strongbox. They're bolted to the captain's safe. Somebody on that ship wanted them protected from the crew. From *anyone*. That's not paperwork, Isla. That's leverage. Or a confession somebody meant to survive the sinking."

"Or meant to drown," she said.

Above us the sky-darkened oval was darkening further, the cloud coming in low off the open water in a way I recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd that you hoped you'd never see again. Weather. Real weather, not coastal drizzle — a front with weight behind it, rolling in ahead of schedule, eating our window one tide at a time.

I felt the change in the water before I saw it on the surface: a long, low pulse through the cave, the sea clearing its throat. The tide was turning early. Or the storm was pushing it.

"We're out of time today," I said, and pulled us up the anchor line toward the boat.

* * *

We ran for home ahead of the front, the Reach throwing white horses even in the channel's lee, and I understood, watching the first squall curtain slide across the headland, that the arithmetic had changed. Four accessible tides remained on Isla's tables. The weather would kill at least two of them. That left perhaps two chances — maybe one — to come back with tools capable of opening a safe that had kept its secrets through a decade of drowning.

And somewhere out in the grey, three Halloran vessels rode their anchors, and Dree Voss's divers were running their own tables, and they had cutting gear already aboard.

Isla stood at the bow with her hood snapping, watching the storm come, and shouted back to me over the wind:

"If they cut that safe open before we do—"

"They won't know it's there," I said. But even as I said it, I remembered the boy on the dock with his quick eyes and his quick prices, and the harbormaster's new paint, and the way the fleet's spotter boat had lingered, that morning, just outside the Reach — patient as a creditor.

The rain hit us a mile from the harbour, sudden and sideways, and through it the navigation lights of the salvage fleet burned steady and green and red, three sets of eyes watching the water we had to cross.

I had my rope back. Now I needed the truth back too, and the sea was running out of patience for both of us.
