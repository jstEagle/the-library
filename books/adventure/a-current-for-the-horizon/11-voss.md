## 11. Voss

Dree Voss had drowned three ships in her life and raised eleven, and she could tell you which of the two felt more like murder.

She stood at the rail of the *Iron Meridian*—a name she'd chosen herself, a small private joke that no one aboard understood—and watched the fog come off the water in slow gray ropes. Below her, the bay lay flat as poured pewter, and beneath the pewter, somewhere past the black teeth of the outer shoals, the cave waited with its cargo of paper. Paper worth more than every hull she'd ever salvaged combined. Paper that had brought her north through two hundred miles of cold water on the personal word of a dying man.

"Chief." Pell, her second, came up the companionway with the radio handset still warm in his fist. "Harbormaster again. Says he's got confirmation on the route they'll take through the Reach."

Voss didn't turn around. "He said that yesterday too."

"He's got a name this time. A boy who runs messages on the dock. Says the guide—the disgraced one, Underhill—has been asking after tide windows."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Zephyrine Underhill. She'd read the file on the crossing south: inquiry transcripts, revoked certifications, the photograph of a man standing in the rain outside a hearing room in Invercarrick, thirty-four years old and already finished. Halloran Marine's legal department had kept that file the way some men keep trophies. She'd assumed it was sentiment. Now she wondered if it was inventory.

"And the daughter?" she said.

"The Marchetti woman. They're seen together now. The harbormaster thinks—" Pell hesitated, because Pell was a careful man and knew his chief hated being handed conclusions like coins from a beggar. "He thinks they're planning to dive it themselves."

Voss finally turned. Pell was forty, weathered to the color of old rope, honest in the way of men who had nothing left to lie about. She trusted him the way she trusted her own hands.

"They're civilians," she said.

"They're competitors."

"They're a schoolteacher and a man who carries other people's luggage up mountains for a living." She heard the edge in her own voice and didn't soften it. "They don't have cutting gear. They don't have permits. They have a rowboat and a grudge."

Pell looked at her for a long moment. "You've met them, then."

"I've read them." She turned back to the water. "There's a difference."

* * *

The call from the founder came at dusk, as it always did, routed through three relays so that his voice arrived stripped and thin, like something carried a long way by wind.

"Dree." Edmond Halloran pronounced her name carefully these days, the way men handle glass when their hands start to shake. "Tell me about the tide."

"Fourth window opens tomorrow, nineteen-forty. Five hours of access if the swell stays under a meter. It won't. I'm giving it until the sixth before we put divers in the inner passage."

"You said the fourth."

"I said the fourth was possible." She walked to the chart table as she spoke, out of habit, though he couldn't see her. Her finger found the bay, the cave, the serrated line of the outer rocks where the current ran like a millrace. "Edmond. I need to say something, and I need you to hear all of it before you answer."

A silence on the line. In the background, faintly, the wet rattle of his breathing—the sound that had gotten worse every week since February, the sound of a man drowning slowly on dry land in a house above the very harbor his company had built.

"Say it," he said.

"There are two people on that coast trying to reach the wreck ahead of us. One is the daughter of Marcus Marchetti. One is the guide your company buried ten years ago." She laid her palm flat on the chart, over the cave, as if she could hold the whole problem down. "Your instructions were to secure the ledgers discreetly. Discreet has a definition, Edmond. What your people are describing now—what the harbormaster is offering to help with—is making sure two civilians are underwater when our team comes through the passage. That's not discreet. That's a body count."

Another silence, longer. When Halloran spoke again, his voice had changed—not softer, but older, the voice of the man he'd been before the money set like concrete around him.

"Do you know what's in those ledgers, Dree?"

"I know what you told me."

"Then let me tell you what you weren't told." A cough, wet and long, and then the voice returned scraped thin. "In ninety-eight we ran a survey on the Kestrel Pass approach road. The ice data was wrong. Not mistaken—wrong. My chief engineer falsified the seasonal readings because correcting them would have cost us the contract, and the contract was worth four million, and I signed off on the survey without reading it because I was in Zurich closing a merger and I liked the number. Marchetti hired a guide anyway because Marchetti was the kind of man who checked everything himself. He found the discrepancy. He was going to report it. And then the storm came, and the pass came down, and my lawyers looked at the wreckage and saw an opportunity instead of a grave."

"The inquiry," Voss said quietly.

"We bought the inquiry. Three officials. Small payments, clean channels. The guide's logs were altered to show he'd overridden the client's safety concerns—when in fact the client had overridden *his*, because the guide wanted to wait out the storm and Marchetti had a plane to catch and a daughter graduating in June." The breath rattled. "Isla Marchetti. She'd have been nineteen. Do you know what I think about, Dree? I think about a girl in a graduation dress waiting for a father who was already dead, and I think about how I slept fine that night. That's what those ledgers prove. Not the disaster. The sleeping."

Voss closed her eyes. Through the porthole behind the chart table, the last light was going off the water, and the fog was thickening into something you could lean against.

"So burn it," she said. "That's what you're paying me for. But you pay me to raise things, Edmond. Not to put them down. If those two go into that cave and only one comes out—if either of them goes in and doesn't come out—that's not salvage anymore. That's a different crime, and I'm not built for it, and neither, whatever you've told yourself, are you."

"I'm dying, Dree."

"I know."

"Then understand me." His voice gathered itself, and for a moment she heard the founder in it, the young man who'd built a fleet out of a single leaky tug. "I will be dead in four months. Maybe six. Everything I am will fit in a paragraph in the business pages. The only thing I can still choose is what gets buried with me. Those ledgers are the difference between a paragraph and an obituary. Between leaving my granddaughter a company or a crime scene. You want me to weigh that against two strangers? I have weighed it. Every night since February. I keep arriving at the same arithmetic, and I hate myself for the sum, but I arrive at it."

Voss stood very still. Somewhere below decks, the diesel generator throbbed like a second heart.

"And the families?" she said. "The crew of the Meridian Star. The ones still taking your money every quarter."

This time the silence went on so long she checked the handset to see if the line had died.

"You know about that," Halloran said at last, and there was something new in his voice—not anger. Relief, almost. As if a weight had shifted from him onto her and he was grateful to put it down. "Yes. Eleven families. Wives, sons, one mother in Carrick who's ninety-one. Fourteen years of quiet deposits. It was meant to be kindness. It became a leash. Publish those ledgers in full and the payments stop being charity and become evidence. Half those households live on that money, Dree. You'd be clearing my conscience and emptying theirs in the same stroke."

"I know," Voss said. "I read the disbursement schedule you sent."

"Then you understand why it can't simply come out. Why it has to come out *shaped*." A pause. "Which is why I need someone with a code. Yours is strange, Dree, but it's real. You don't drown people. So don't drown them. Be cleverer than the sea instead. That's always been your gift."

She ended the call without saying goodbye, which he expected, and stood at the chart table while the cabin darkened around her, thinking about arithmetic.

* * *

She dreamed, that night, of the *Corvane*.

It was the third ship she'd drowned, twenty-two years ago, and the only one she'd done deliberately—a fraud-riddled hulk, uninsured cargo of nothing, scuttled in deep water off the Azores on the owner's instruction, and she'd taken the job because the fee was triple and she'd told herself the sea swallows worse every day. She still dreamed of the way the hull sounded going down. Not a crash. A sigh. Forty meters of steel exhaling its air in one long silver rush, and then the dark taking it, polite as a hotel closing for the season.

She woke at 0400 with the dream still on her and went up to the wheelhouse. The fog had lifted; stars over the bay, hard and close. The cliffs stood black against them, and at the base of the cliffs, invisible, the cave.

Pell was already there, coffee in hand, watching the shore through glasses.

"She's out there," he said. "The Marchetti woman. Walking the cliff path with a lamp. Been at it an hour."

Voss took the glasses. Found the small moving point of light against the black shoulder of the headland, steady, unhurried, a woman measuring the ground the way her father must have measured ice. Voss thought of a graduation dress. Of a letter written by a dying man confessing to a murder committed with paperwork.

"What do you figure she wants?" Pell asked.

"The same thing I do," Voss said. "The truth. She just hasn't figured out yet what it costs." She lowered the glasses. "Nobody ever does until the invoice arrives."

Below them, far down in the dark, the tide was already turning, beginning its patient work of opening and closing the door in the cliff. Six windows, the tables said. Then the spring tides would seal it for months, and whatever was inside would belong to whoever got there first—or to no one, which was the outcome Voss was starting, quietly, treacherously, to hope for.

"Pell," she said. "When the harbormaster calls again—and he will—you tell him the Iron Meridian pays for information, not for arrangements. If anyone suggests otherwise, they can swim out here and make the offer to my face."

"And if the founder asks why we're slow?"

Voss watched the little lamp move along the cliff path, northward, toward the lighthouse, and thought about codes: the ones you chose and the ones that chose you.

"Then I'll tell him myself," she said. "Get some sleep. We dive on the fifth window, and God help whichever of us is standing closest when the paper comes up."

Out on the black water, unseen, a small boat with no lights slipped along the base of the cliffs, heading for the cave mouth, and Dree Voss—who had spent her life reading water—felt the shape of the next six days settle over her like the fog coming back in, and knew, with a salvager's certainty, that something down there was going to surface whether anyone wanted it to or not.
