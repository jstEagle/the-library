## 12. Storm Glass

The storm glass had been Isla's father's—a slender brass cylinder of camphor and alcohol that he'd carried on every expedition, screwed to the dash of whatever boat or truck or tent table happened to be bearing him toward the next horizon. She'd inherited it along with his tide tables, his charts, and the particular stubbornness that had gotten him killed. For ten years it had hung in the window of her flat in Portsmouth, doing nothing but slowly crystallizing whenever winter came, pretty and useless as a paperweight.

Tonight it was a fistful of white feathers.

She held it up to the lamplight of Zephyrine's cottage and watched the crystals swirl, thickening even as she looked, like breath fogging glass from the inside. Her father had taught her to read it when she was nine: *Feathers mean foul. If it goes solid, girl, you get off the water and you don't argue with it.*

"It's falling fast," she said.

Zephyrine didn't answer at once. He stood at the window with his back to her, watching the harbor through a gap in the salt-hazed glass, and Isla had learned over the past three days that his silences were not empty—they were load-bearing, holding up something heavy while he decided whether to set it down. The lamp threw his shadow long across the floorboards, up the wall, into the rafters where dried kelp hung from some earlier life of the house.

"How fast?" he said finally.

"Fast enough." She set the storm glass on the table between them, next to the chart they'd been arguing over for an hour—the Serrated Reach, inked in her own hand with the current arrows her father had drawn thirty years ago, when he'd come through this coast on the survey that had, in the end, been Halloran's first lie. "I called the weather office in Falmouth from the phone box by the chandlery. They're saying a deep low, tracking northeast, making landfall tomorrow evening. Gale force eight, maybe nine on this stretch before it's done."

"And the tides?"

"That's the thing." She spread her hands over the tables. "Tide four is tomorrow morning—too rough already, the leading swell will be in. Tide five is tomorrow night, dead center of the gale. Undivable. Not dangerous-undivable. *Impossible*-undivable. After that, spring tides seal the cave until April."

Zephyrine turned from the window. In the lamplight his face looked older than it had on the cliff road the day they'd met, all suspicion and hostility; older than it had in the sea stack, too, when she'd told him about her father's letter and watched ten years of guilt rearrange itself around a new shape. Now there was something else in it. She recognized it because she'd seen it in mirrors during the worst months after Kestrel Pass—that arithmetic people do when they're counting what's left.

"So it's tonight," he said.

"Tonight. Tide three." She tapped the column of figures. "Which we already used. We reached the safe—we know exactly where it is, bolted to the captain's strongbox, forty feet inside the Meridian Star's hold. But we don't have cutting gear, and now we have one tide instead of two to get it, cut it free, and get out."

"One tide," he repeated. He came to the table and stood over the chart, and Isla watched him do the thing he did—read water the way other men read print. His finger moved down the channel of the Serrated Reach, tracing the serpentine gap between the teeth of rock that guarded the cave's approach, and stopped where the arrows crossed.

"The leading edge of the storm will be here by midnight," he said. "Southeast wind building. That means the Reach will be running against us on the flood—it always runs against you on the flood, but with a gale behind it, it'll be standing waves in the narrows. White water over rock we can't see."

"Then we go at slack."

"Slack in a building sea isn't slack. It's a held breath. You get maybe twenty minutes where the water forgets which way it's going, and then it remembers all at once." He looked up at her. "You're asking me to take a boat through a channel I've run twice in my life, both times in fair weather, into a cave we can only enter for ninety minutes, to cut loose a safe that defeated us last time, and be out again before the sea remembers us. With a storm arriving like an unpaid debt."

"I'm not asking," Isla said. "There's no one to ask. There's just what's true."

* * *

What was true, she thought later, standing at the sink washing the teapot neither of them had drunk from, was that they had lost their margin somewhere without noticing it go.

Three days ago they'd had six tides and a secret. Then the fleet had come—three gray hulls flying Halloran Marine's colors, cranes folded like sleeping insects—and Dree Voss had nailed a notice to the harbormaster's board offering five hundred pounds for information about unauthorized diving, and the secret had begun to leak. Then the spotter boats. Then the failed dive at tide three, hiding in the sea stack's shadow while Voss's divers probed the outer passage with lamps that swept the water like searchlights. Then the boy, Tam Yelland, fourteen years old and hungry-eyed, who had tried to sell them and been turned instead—but whose handler, they'd learned this afternoon, was Harbormaster Clegg himself, and Clegg's loyalty had been bought so long ago it had probably grown into the grain of him.

It was Clegg who had ended the argument before it started. Isla had gone down to the harbor office at four o'clock to check the mooring registration on the boat they'd borrowed—her idea, careful, legitimate—and had heard, through the thin partition, Clegg on the telephone. His voice had the particular smoothness of a man reading from a script he'd been paid to memorize.

*"Serrated Reach, aye. Night of the third, if they've any sense left. Slack water around half eleven. You'll want your divers ready early—these two won't come at it like professionals. One of them's a woman."*

A pause. Then, lower, almost fond: *"Miss Voss, I've been paid to know things about this harbor for twenty years. Knowing things is the whole of my trade."*

Isla had stood in the cold outside the office with her heart going like a bilge pump and understood, with terrible clarity, that the map of their last chance had just been sold. Every hour they waited made Voss better prepared. Tomorrow the Reach would have a picket boat in it. Tonight it might already.

She'd walked back up the cliff path in the rising wind and told Zephyrine all of it, and he had listened without moving, and then he had gone to the window, and then he had said nothing for a long time, and the silence had held up its weight.

Now she dried her hands and said, "We could still walk away."

He turned. "Could we?"

"We could let it go. Let Voss have the ledgers. Halloran buries them, the founder dies rich and unindicted, and you stay—" She stopped, because the word she was going to say was *condemned*, and she had promised herself she wouldn't use it as a weapon anymore. Not since the sea stack. Not since she'd told him about the letter, her father's last, written in a shaking hand in a tent at eight thousand meters: *If anything happens to me, look at the logs. Look at who wrote them. Halloran knew the ice bridge was rotten and sent us anyway, and someone will need to prove it.*

"Stay what?" Zephyrine asked quietly. "Say it. You've earned the word."

"No." She shook her head. "That's the old argument. I'm not making it. I'm saying we could survive losing this. We've both survived worse. But the crew families can't survive the ledgers being buried, because buried is what they were bought to be. Eleven families, Zephyrine. Taking hush money month after month for ten years because the alternative was nothing. If Voss gets that safe, they never learn why their fathers died, and they keep taking the money that buys their silence, and it goes on forever."

The wind chose that moment to arrive properly. It came off the sea in a long gust that rattled the cottage windows and set the dried kelp swinging in the rafters like hanged men, and somewhere out in the dark the buoy bell at the harbor mouth began its urgent, arrhythmic tolling.

Zephyrine crossed to the hearth and crouched before it. There was no fire—there hadn't been since she'd known him; the man lived like the house was a place he was only passing through—but he crouched there anyway, staring into the black grate, and when he spoke his voice was low, almost to himself.

"When I was blacklisted, I went through a period—I don't know what to call it. A year, maybe two. I kept thinking if I could just find the piece of paper, the proof, everything would reverse. Like the inquiry was a lock and the ledgers were the key. Clear my name, get my certification back, walk back up a mountain someday with my name on the permit." He turned his head, firelight-less firelight in his eyes. "But that was never quite true either, was it? Because the moment I saw that strongbox in the wreck, I didn't think about my name. I thought about the Marchetti report. I thought about what your mother read in the papers. I thought about eleven families being paid to swallow their dead."

"My father would have—" Isla began, and stopped, because she'd said this sentence before, in the sea stack, and she wasn't sure she had the right to keep spending it.

"He would have burned the whole company down," Zephyrine finished for her. "Yes. And he'd have felt every family it scorched. That was the thing about Elias Marchetti. Everyone at the inquiry talked about how thorough he was, how careful. Nobody mentioned that he used to carry sweets for porters' children." Something moved across his face that was not quite a smile. "I only met him once. Did you know that? The briefing in Kathmandu. He asked me more questions about the route than about the cost. Asked whether the icefall doctors were paid fairly. Ten minutes in, I remember thinking: this client is going to be trouble, the good kind."

Isla's throat tightened. She had spent ten years reconstructing her father from documents—expedition permits, insurance filings, the letter—and here was a man who had known the living version of him for ten minutes and remembered the sweets.

"Then we go tonight," she said.

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the storm assembled itself beyond the headland.

Zephyrine laid out gear on the floorboards with the economy of a man who had packed for a living once: two hundred feet of line coiled in flat figure-eights, a slate and soapstone pencil, the borrowed boat's keys, flares, a hand-bearing compass, the hacksaw and cold chisel they'd scavenged from the chandlery along with a battery angle grinder that Isla had paid for with the last of the money her mother's estate had left her, wrapped in oilcloth against the spray. He tested everything twice. He made her test everything once herself, watching, correcting her grip on the grinder's trigger, showing her how to cut a bolted plate by starting at the corner and letting the wheel walk.

"You'll be cutting," he said. "Not me."

She looked up. "You're the diver."

"I'm the diver. Which means I'm the one who'll be half-frozen and breathing hard when we reach the safe. Your hands are steadier and you've practiced. Besides—" he almost smiled, "—if the fleet comes through the outer passage while we're inside, one of us needs to be worth something in a fight, and it was never going to be me with a saw."

Outside, the first spit of rain came against the windows like thrown gravel. The barometer—if the storm glass needed confirming—had fallen through every mark her father had ever penciled on its brass collar. Isla went to the door and opened it a crack, and the wind pushed it wide and came into the room smelling of iodine and iron, and far out past the harbor mouth she could see the leading edge of the gale as a darkness darker than the night, a wall of moving water and cloud with lightning working inside it like thoughts.

"Half eleven slack," she said into the wind. "It's nearly nine now. The Reach is forty minutes' run if the sea lets us have it."

"Then we leave within the hour." Zephyrine came to stand beside her in the doorway, and for a moment they both just looked at the coming weather, two people about to step into the mouth of something enormous. "Isla. There's a thing you should hear before we're committed, because after tonight there's no version of this where we both walk away clean."

She waited.

"If the Reach beats us—if we lose the boat in the narrows, if the cave floods before we're out—the story they'll tell is that two fools drowned chasing salvage. Voss will make sure of it. No one will ever connect it to Halloran, and the ledgers stay down there, and the families keep their blood money, and you die being wrong about your father, and I die being right about myself and unable to prove it." He said it flatly, without drama, the way a guide reads out a route's hazards—not to frighten, but because a client deserves the truth before the rope goes on. "I've made peace with dying badly once already. It's the dying *wrongly* I can't stomach. So if it comes to that—if it comes to choosing between getting you out and getting the safe out—you swim, and I cut the line."

"No," Isla said.

"Isla—"

"*No.*" She turned to face him fully, rain beginning to streak the doorway between them. "Ten years ago a guide made a decision on a mountain about who got saved, and it destroyed him, and everyone let him believe it was his fault because it was easier than looking at the logs. I am not going to be another person you sacrifice yourself for to settle an old account. We go in together and we come out together, or we don't come out, and if you try to cut any line on me I swear to God I will find the ledgers myself and publish every page including the parts that hurt."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind and the buoy bell and the rain finding its rhythm on the cliffs. Then Zephyrine laughed—a short, startled sound, rusty, as though the mechanism hadn't turned in a decade.

"Elias Marchetti's daughter," he said. "He argued with the expedition doctor the same way. About altitude. He won, too."

"He usually did."

Zephyrine picked up the coil of line and slung it over his shoulder, then the oilcloth bundle with the grinder, then took the storm glass from the table and held it out to her. The crystals had gone nearly solid, a white blizzard sealed in brass and glass.

"Your father's rule," he said. "Get off the water and don't argue with it."

Isla closed her hand around the warm brass. "My father also wrote in the margin of that same page: *rules are for weather you didn't choose.*"

She put the storm glass in her pocket, where it lay against her ribs like a small cold heart, and together they stepped out into the wind and pulled the door shut behind them, and the cottage went dark, and below them in the black harbor the borrowed boat tugged at its mooring like a dog that already knew.

By half past ten the rain had become horizontal and the Serrated Reach was waiting for them, white-toothed in the lightning's stutter, and somewhere between the harbor mouth and the narrows—though they would not learn it until it was almost too late—a gray picket boat with Halloran's colors at its mast had already found the channel, and Dree Voss was watching the water with binoculars and a patience like stone.
