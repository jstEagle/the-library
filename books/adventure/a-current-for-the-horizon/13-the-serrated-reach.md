## 13. The Serrated Reach

The storm did not arrive so much as send its heralds ahead of it. By dusk the wind had come around to the southeast and begun to carry that wet-iron smell I knew from a decade of high places—the smell of weather still over the horizon but already committed. The barometer on Isla's borrowed boat had fallen all afternoon in slow, deliberate ticks, like a man descending stairs he does not want to reach the bottom of.

We left the harbor at slack water under a sky the color of wet slate, running dark, no lights, because lights could be read at three miles by anyone with glass and a grudge, and Dree Voss had both.

"You're certain about this channel." It was not quite a question. Isla stood braced at the wheel beside me, her father's tide tables folded into the pocket of her oilskin like a scripture she no longer fully believed in. "The charts show nothing but reef."

"The charts are right," I said. "That's the problem. They show the reef. They don't show the way through it."

I had been studying the Serrated Reach for two days—not on paper, because there was no paper, but the old way, the way you study a mountain before you climb it. From the headland above Carrick Point, with the sun low and then with the moon low, watching how the water moved over the teeth. A channel is not a thing; it is a behavior. The rock underneath is only half the truth. The other half is what the sea does when it meets the rock—the standing waves, the slicks where current runs smooth over deep water, the V-shaped seams of chop that point, always, toward the gap. On Kestrel Pass we had called them snow bridges and wind scours, but they were the same language. Water and stone arguing, and if you listened long enough, the argument told you where the door was.

No chart held that. No chart could. Charts were written by surveyors who came in daylight, in fair weather, and asked the sea politely. The Serrated Reach answered only to people who watched it lie.

"Tell me again," Isla said.

I told her again. Three miles of broken ground between the outer shoals and the deep water off the cave cliffs, a channel nowhere wider than forty meters, and in the middle of it a place the fishermen called the Grinder—a constriction where the ebb funneling through two rock spires could stand up into waves taller than our mast even on a moderate day. Tonight, with the gale's leading edge stacking wind against ebb, the Grinder would be a saw blade.

"We go through on the last of the flood," I said. "Not slack. Slack is a myth in there—the current never fully stops, it just changes its mind. We ride the tail of the flood, which will be fighting the wind instead of joining it. Two forces opposed cancel each other out. Mostly."

"Mostly."

"I've never taken a boat through it."

She looked at me then, and in the dim red glow of the compass light her face was very still. "You said—"

"I said I'd get us through. I didn't say I'd done it before." I kept my eyes forward, on the black line of the outer shoals rising out of the dark like a buried spine. "On the mountain, nobody takes a route they've climbed before. Not really. Every storm changes it, every season, every year. You take the route as it is tonight or you don't take it at all. What you carry is the method. Reading. Listening. Knowing which silences mean danger and which ones mean depth."

"And if your reading is wrong?"

"Then we'll know very quickly," I said, "and I'll be wrong in front of an audience."

Something happened to her mouth that was almost a smile. It was the first one in two days, since the harbormaster's betrayal had taught us that our route lived now in Voss's pocket too, and speed was all we had left. Behind us, far back along the coast, I imagined I could see the riding lights of the salvage fleet, patient as vultures on a fence. They knew the general water. They did not know the door. But they would find it eventually, and tonight was the fourth tide, and after tonight the storm would close the book for two days, and after that there would be one page left.

* * *

The first hour was the easy kind of hard. Rain came in bands, soft at first, then with weight behind it, and the wind built from a shove to a sustained pressure that leaned on the boat like a hand flat against her shoulder. I took us wide of the marked channel, out past the last buoy, until the shore was a rumor behind us and the open sea worked beneath us in long confused swells. Then I turned south, and turned again, and began to thread.

"Watch the water, not the rocks," I told her. "The rocks will lie to you in the dark. The water can't help telling the truth."

She learned fast—I'll say that for her, and I have said little enough good about anything for ten years. Within twenty minutes she could pick out the difference between the smooth black tongues where current ran deep and the pale, boiling patches where it broke over stone. She stopped flinching at every white crest and started reading the shapes of them: the crescents that meant a shallow tooth, the long unbroken lines that meant safe passage, the standing wave—that stationary, snarling hump—that meant rock directly beneath, holding the sea up like a fist through cloth.

"There." Her arm shot out. "Left of that standing wave—is that a seam?"

It was. A clean lane of darker water, maybe thirty feet wide, running between two fields of broken white. I altered course without answering, and she understood that silence too, and something passed between us that had never passed between me and any client in fifteen years of guiding: the understanding that neither of us needed to be reassured, only to be accurate.

The rain thickened. The wind found its full voice, a sound like the whole coast being dragged across a whetstone. And then we came to the entrance of the Reach itself, and I understood why no fisherman who valued his hull went near it, and why the charts gave up.

In the dark, with the storm's breath on it, the Serrated Reach did not look like water. It looked like the surface of some other planet seen from orbit—white fire everywhere, chaos stitched with sudden black calm, and threading through all of it, faint as veins in marble, the seams. My seams. The ones I had memorized from the headland in two days of watching, now transformed by darkness and violence into something that bore only a family resemblance to what I'd studied.

A client once asked me, on a bad day high on Kestrel Pass, whether I was ever afraid. I told him fear was a tool, like an ice axe—you didn't leave it behind, but you didn't let it lead. Standing in that cockpit with the Reach roaring fifty meters ahead, I took out my fear and looked at it honestly. It was large. Larger than it had been on any mountain, because mountains do not move, and this did, ceaselessly, and I had perhaps ninety seconds of decisions ahead of me and no second attempt.

"Engine to idle," I said. "Hands ready. When I call a turn, it's already late, so don't wait for me to finish the word."

Isla killed the engine to a mutter. And I did the thing I had done ten thousand times at the base of routes worse than this one: I stopped looking at the whole and started seeing the parts. One seam at a time. One gate at a time. The next thirty seconds, and only the next thirty seconds. The rest of the night existed somewhere else entirely.

"Now," I said, and put the bow into the first gate.

* * *

What follows, I remember in pieces, the way survivors of avalanches remember—in flashes separated by white.

The first third of the Reach was ferocious but honest. The seams ran where I had promised they would run, and though the standing waves were twice what I'd estimated, they were visible, and visible danger is navigable danger. We threaded four gates in six minutes, the boat slewing and plunging, spray coming aboard in solid slaps, Isla's hands steady on the wheel when I handed it to her for the straight stretches so I could stand forward and read. Once a wave lifted the stern and slewed us sideways and for one full second we were broadside to a field of breaking water, and I called the turn early—against every instinct—and the bow swung through just as the sea collapsed where we should have been.

Then the channel bent east, and the honesty ended.

The bend was the trap I had feared from the headland and hoped I had misjudged. The flood tide, funneled around the eastern spire, set hard across the channel at the exact point of the turn, and where it met the ebb eddying off the northern teeth it stood up into the Grinder. Even in the dark I could hear it before I could see it—a sound I had heard once before, years ago, standing below the icefall on the north face of something with a name nobody uses anymore: the sound of enormous weight moving over enormous force, grinding, groaning, alive.

"We can't—" Isla began.

"We can't go back," I said. "The seas astern are worse than these. There's a pocket. Look—"

And there it was, or I believed there it was: a slick of almost-calm water tucked in the lee of the eastern spire, a room in the middle of the avalanche, maybe sixty feet across. Beyond it, the Grinder barred the channel like a wall of churning bone. But the charts—her father's annotations, the soundings some forgotten surveyor had taken a century ago—showed deep water on the far side of the spire itself. Not through the Grinder. Around it, hugging the rock so closely that the fenders would scream, in water that the standing waves could not quite reach because the spire's own turbulence made a dead zone at its foot.

It was a mountain move. It was the kind of line you pick on a face when the obvious route has avalanched out and the only option left is the one that looks impossible and isn't, because the very feature that makes it look impossible is what protects it. Climbers know this. Sailors, mostly, do not.

"It's thirty feet off that rock," Isla said, reading the water exactly as I was. "Maybe less."

"Maybe less," I agreed. "The turbulence collar will hold if the spire goes all the way down. If it doesn't—if there's an overhang—we're on it."

"How do you know it goes down?"

"I don't. That's what makes it a guide's decision instead of a chart's." I put my hand over hers on the wheel. "I'll call the line. You steer. Your hands are faster than mine and I need my eyes. Whatever happens, whatever I shout, you hold the line I give you until I give you another. Can you do that?"

The rain ran down her face and she did not wipe it away. "My father trusted you," she said. "Everyone said he was wrong to. Show me why he wasn't."

I throttled up, and we went at the wall of the Grinder's edge, and at the last possible moment I called hard to starboard, and we turned inside the spire's shadow into water so close, so violently alive on either side of us, that I could feel the suction of the great waves pulling at the hull like hands testing our weight. The rock rose off our port beam, black and streaming, close enough that the backwash off it slapped the deck. Below us, unseen, the spire either plunged clean into the dark or it did not, and for eleven seconds—which I counted, because counting is what guides do when there is nothing else left to do with their minds—we were committed to an answer we could not see.

The fenders screamed once against stone. Isla held the line. She did not deviate a degree, not when the bow tried to blow off, not when a wave's shoulder shoved us toward the rock, not when I shouted the final correction with my voice torn half away by wind. She held it the way her father must have climbed—exactly, completely, without negotiation.

And then the spire fell astern, and the Grinder's roar dropped behind us like a door closing, and we were through, sliding out into the long deep swell beyond the Reach with the cave cliffs a mile ahead and the storm's first true gusts arriving overhead like outriders.

Neither of us spoke for a while. There was nothing that needed saying that the breathing hadn't already said.

* * *

We anchored in the lee of the cliff in water that heaved but held, and waited for the tide window, and drank tea from a thermos with shaking hands that we pretended were steady. The lightning had begun out at sea, silent sheets of it deep inside the cloud, illuminating the whole coast in stroboscopic flashes—once, in one of those flashes, I saw Isla looking at me across the cockpit with an expression I had not been on the receiving end of in ten years.

"What?" I said.

"That's what he saw," she said. "In you. On the Pass. Before." She looked down at her cup. "They asked me, at the inquiry, whether I blamed you. I was nineteen. I said yes, because grief needs a shape and they offered me yours." The lightning came again, and in its light her eyes were very bright. "I'm not taking it back tonight. I'm just saying I finally understand what my father paid for, that day. He wasn't paying for a guide. He was paying for someone who reads the unreadable."

"Your father read people better than I ever read water," I said. "He hired me when every other client had walked. Don't make me a legend, Isla. I got us through a channel. I couldn't keep him alive on a mountain, and I've spent ten years not knowing whether that was the weather or me."

"It was Halloran," she said simply. "We both know it now. That's why we're here."

Ahead of us, in the gaps between the lightning, the cliff loomed, and beneath it, hidden under tons of cold Atlantic, the Meridian Star waited with its sealed hold and its brass-bound truth. The tide tables said the water would begin to drop within the hour. The storm said it would try to kill us while we worked.

Through the rain, far out in the black, I caught it—one flash of white navigation lights where no fishing boat had any business being, holding station off the seaward side of the bay. Station-keeping. Watching. Waiting for someone to lead them to the door.

Voss had found the neighborhood. She still didn't have the address.

But she had all night, and so did we, and only one of us could afford to be wrong.
