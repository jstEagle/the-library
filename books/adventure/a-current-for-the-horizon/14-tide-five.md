## 14. Tide Five

*Zephyrine*

The water was already in the cave when they went down to it.

That was the thing about tide five—there was no pretending anymore, no leisurely wading across a floor of wet stone while the sea stood politely aside. The Meridian Star's resting place had become a half-drowned cathedral, and the sea was coming home to it, inch by patient inch, the way a creditor comes for a debt. Zephyrine crouched at the lip of the entrance with his lamp held low and watched the black water slide between the rocks below him, filling channels that had been dry as bone two tides ago.

"Forty minutes of slack," Isla said beside him, checking her father's tables by lamplight. Her voice was steady, but he could hear the arithmetic underneath it, the same cold counting he was doing himself. "Maybe fifty if the swell stays down. After that the current turns and this becomes a throat."

"Then we don't dawdle." He tested the cutting rig one last time—the borrowed hydraulic packer, salvaged from a derelict fishing boat and coaxed back to life over three days of Isla's stubborn tinkering. It weighed forty pounds on land. Underwater it would weigh nothing and fight like something alive. "You stay at the strongbox line. I cut. We do not separate, we do not improvise, and the moment I say *up*, you go up. No argument."

"No argument," she agreed, which from Isla Marchetti was less a promise than a treaty signed under duress.

He looked at her a moment longer than he needed to. In the lamplight her face was pale and set, and there was salt spray caught in the loose hair at her temples, and he thought—not for the first time, but with a new and unwelcome clarity—that Marcus Marchetti's daughter should never have come within a mile of this water. That he should have found some way to leave her ashore. That she would have climbed into the boat anyway, because she was her father's daughter, and Marchettis did not wait on shore for other people to do the necessary things.

"Zeph," she said quietly. He hadn't told her he hated the shortening of his name; she'd simply started using it somewhere around the Serrated Reach, and he'd discovered he didn't hate it at all. "Whatever's in there—we get it, or we don't. Either way we surface."

"Aye," he said, because it was what she needed to hear, and because lying to yourself is just another kind of ballast until the storm comes.

They went down together into the green-black cold.

* * *

*Isla*

The wreck was different underwater. On the low tides, when they'd walked its tilted decks in the drained hush of the cave, the Meridian Star had been a ruin—a thing to be read, evidence, an argument made of rusted steel and silt. Submerged, with her lamps throwing trembling coins of light along the hull, she became a ship again. A drowned thing still holding its shape, still keeping its dead, and Isla felt her throat tighten around the regulator mouthpiece as they swam the length of the starboard rail toward the hold where the safe waited.

Her father had died on a mountain, not here. She reminded herself of that. But grief does not keep good records; it files everything under the same name.

The captain's cabin came up out of the murk like a memory surfacing. The safe sat where it had sat for ten years, bolted through the deck plates, furred with white anemones that waved gently in the surge like a congregation agreeing with a sermon. The strongbox they'd reached on tide three was lashed to it still—brass gone green, stamped with the survey company's mark, the mark that had signed away Zephyrine's life with a falsified logbook and a shrug.

He was already working. The packer roared against the bolts in a sound she felt in her teeth more than heard, and the water around them shivered with it. She held the light steady on the mounting plate and watched the first bolt shear, then the second, and thought about how strange it was that the whole architecture of her life—her mother's silence, her own decade of obsession, the ledgers that might exist or might be silt—now hung on whether a machine older than she was could chew through corroded steel before the ocean finished turning its key.

Third bolt. Fourth. The safe shifted with a groan she felt through her gloves, a long metallic complaint, like something being woken who did not wish to be.

And then the light flickered.

Not her lamp. His—the beam he'd angled down the outer passage, their tripwire against exactly this, swept once, twice, and went dark.

She turned. Down the length of the flooded corridor, far off, where the cave mouth swallowed all light, something moved. Not fish. Not current. Lights—three of them, maybe four, bobbing, disciplined, closing.

The fleet's second team. Through the outer passage, the way Voss's divers had been probing for days. They'd found the throat, and the throat had let them in.

Zephyrine saw it a heartbeat after she did. She watched his whole body change in the water—watched ten years of caution collapse into something harder. He pointed: *safe*. Then at the lights: *them*. Then he put both hands back on the packer and pulled the trigger, and the fifth bolt screamed.

* * *

*Zephyrine*

There was no time to be clever. Cleverness was a luxury of people whose enemies were not swimming toward them through a flooding tunnel.

The last bolt parted and the safe came free all at once, too fast, tearing loose of the anemone crust with a sound like a door kicked open, and the sudden buoyancy of it lifted them both. Forty pounds of steel became a kite. Zephyrine got his arms around it and kicked for the inner passage, and Isla fell in beside him with her shoulder under the opposite edge, and together they hauled their prize through the corridor while behind them the other team's lights bloomed brighter, spreading, searching.

The passage narrowed. It had been tight on the walk-in tides; now, half-drowned, it was a slot of surging green with a ceiling that breathed—down with each swell, up with each retreat—and the safe filled most of it. He wedged it through a pinch point and felt the steel kiss rock on both sides, and pushed, and Isla pushed, and the sea pushed back, and for three full seconds nothing moved except his heartbeat, which was trying to exit through his ears.

Then it ground free, and they were through, and the entrance chamber opened around them gray and vast, and the light at the cave mouth was wrong.

It should have been silver-gray evening. It was churning white.

The swell had come up while they were inside. Of course it had. Tide five was not going to die quietly; tide five intended to take the furniture with it. The cave mouth that had been a doorway was now a lung, inhaling and exhaling, and each exhale dragged the water level down six feet and each inhale brought it back with interest, and hanging in that breathing space was the only road out.

He looked at Isla. She looked at him. Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the water wasn't already saying better.

*Up on the exhale,* he signaled. *Ride it out.*

Behind them, deeper in, the fleet divers' lights reached the pinch point and stopped. He heard—felt—their frustration as vibration, a hammering on rock, muffled curses in the water. They were big men with commercial gear, and the slot that had barely passed the safe would not pass them quickly. It bought them minutes. Minutes were going to have to be enough.

The sea exhaled. Zephyrine kicked for the surface with the safe hugged to his chest like a child, and the falling water took them, and for one glorious moment they were riding a waterfall in reverse, shooting out through the cave mouth into open gray air—

—and the sea inhaled.

He'd misjudged it. Or the sea had lied; with this coast those were often the same thing. The returning surge caught them ten feet outside the mouth and slammed them back inward, and the world became noise. He lost the light. He lost which way was up, briefly, badly—the oldest terror there is, the body's animal certainty inverted—and then his shoulder hit stone and the safe hit stone above him and the ceiling of the passage, weakened by ten years of salt and the day's pounding swell, let go.

He heard it go. A deep structural groan, then a crack like the mountain speaking, and then the beam came down.

Later, he would piece it together in fragments: the weight of timber striking his back, driving him down onto the sloping rock; the regulator torn sideways from his mouth; the safe grinding away from his arms into the dark; cold water pouring into his open mouth with a violence that had no malice in it, only appetite. The beam pinned him across the shoulders and hips, and beneath him the surge was already rising again, and above him the surface was a rumor, a gray shimmer getting farther away.

Ten years ago, on Kestrel Pass, he had lain under a cornice of snow and listened to the mountain decide, and the mountain had decided against Marcus Marchetti, and Zephyrine had lived with the arithmetic of that ever since. Now the sea was deciding, and he understood with perfect, drowning clarity that the sea kept no ledgers at all.

Something struck the beam. Once. Twice. A third time, with fury.

Hands found his collar. Small hands, gloved, impossibly strong. Isla's face appeared in the murk inches from his, lit by her headlamp, her eyes wide and furious and absolutely unafraid, and she got her shoulder under the beam's edge and levered, and the timber ground up two inches, three, not enough, nowhere near enough, and the water was at his chin now, and she did not stop.

She braced her boots against the rock and threw her whole body into it, a woman lifting a roof beam with her spine, and he heard her scream through the water, a raw bubble-wrapped roar, and the beam rose four inches, five, and he wriggled. Ribs screaming. Lungs past screaming into some silent burning country beyond. He wriggled like a man escaping his own grave, because that was precisely what he was doing, and then her grip slipped on the wet wood and the beam dropped and caught his ankle—

—and the surge lifted it.

The sea, having decided, changed its mind. The exhale came early, hard, sucking the water out from under the beam so fast it dropped its own weight, and Isla hauled, and Zephyrine came free into open water with his boot half torn off and his lungs full of fire, and her arm locked around his chest, and she kicked.

They broke the surface together in the open gray chaos outside the cave, and the first breath he took hurt so much he nearly wished he hadn't, and the second one was the sweetest of his entire ruined life.

Isla had the safe's towline already clipped to her harness. Of course she did. He coughed seawater and laughed, or sobbed, or some amphibious thing between the two, and she dragged him and the prize both toward the rocks where they'd left the tender, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left in either of them that wasn't being spent on breathing.

* * *

They came ashore on the shelf below the cliff path like wreckage themselves—two shapes crawling out of the surge, hauling a barnacled strongbox between them, collapsing on wet stone. The sky was the color of dishwater. The wind had teeth in it now, the gale's outriders arriving ahead of the main body, and every wave that hit the shelf threw spray twenty feet up the rock.

For a long time neither of them moved. Then Isla rolled onto her back, ripped her hood back, and stared at the sky, chest heaving.

"You," Zephyrine managed, "are the single most—" He stopped. Started again. "That beam."

"Don't." She held up a hand without looking at him. "Don't make it a speech. I've got a whole decade of speeches saved up for you and none of them are nice."

He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and she sat up and pounded his back with more force than sympathy required, and that was when they both noticed the silence of the anchorage.

Their boat—the little patched-together workboat that had carried them through the Serrated Reach in the dark, that had been their one thread back to the world—was not where they'd left it.

It was moving. Out in the bay, running lights on, standing away toward the fleet anchorage with the easy confidence of a vessel under new management, and standing on its stern in a yellow foul-weather suit, one hand raised in something that was not quite a wave and not quite an apology, was Dree Voss.

Beside Voss, small and miserable and unable to look toward the shore, stood the harbormaster's boy.

Isla was on her feet before Zephyrine could speak, fists clenched, shouting words the wind tore apart and scattered. Zephyrine stayed where he was, on his knees on the wet rock with the safe between them, and watched the boat shrink, and felt the old familiar shape of it settle over him like a coat he'd worn for ten years: the loss, the blame, the closed door.

Except.

Except this time the ledger was in his hands. This time the proof sat on the rock between himself and the woman who'd pulled him out from under a falling world, salt-crusted and dented and real. Voss had taken their boat, but Voss had not taken this, and Voss—Voss had raised her hand. Not a taunt. He'd seen enough taunts in his life to know the difference.

"That wasn't a threat," he said slowly.

Isla stopped shouting. "What?"

"She's telling us where to find her." He got to his feet, joints reporting their grievances one by one, and looked at the safe, and then at the darkening horizon where the fleet rode at anchor like a row of drawn knives. "She could have sunk us both in that cave and reported an accident. Instead she took the boat and waved goodbye." He turned to Isla, and the wind shoved at them both, and somewhere behind them the sea was sealing the cave for another cycle, indifferent as law. "Dree Voss wants to talk."

Isla looked at the safe. Then at him. Her hair was plastered to her face and her hands were shaking—with cold, with adrenaline, with the aftermath of lifting a roof beam off a drowning man—and her eyes, when they met his, were already doing the arithmetic.

"Then we'd better decide," she said, "what we're willing to trade."
