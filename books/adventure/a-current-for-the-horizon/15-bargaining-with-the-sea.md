## 15. Bargaining with the Sea

The storm had not yet arrived, but its outriders were already on the water—long gray lines of squall marching in from the southwest like a fleet of their own, and the wind had that torn-edge sound I knew from Kestrel Pass, the sound the mountain made an hour before it tried to kill you.

We climbed.

There was no other word for it. The cliff path above the cave mouth was barely a path at all—a seam of broken shale and old sheep track that the sea had been unpicking for centuries, slick with spray, angled like a roof. Isla went first because she was lighter and because I wanted my hands free to catch her if the rock let go. She climbed well. Better than well. Somewhere in ten years of pulling her father's records apart she had learned to move through difficult country without wasting a single motion, and watching her was like watching a letter arrive that I'd been afraid to open.

The safe rode on my back in a harness we'd improvised from the boat's mooring lines and two of Voss's own lifting slings, which felt like a small theft and a larger joke. It weighed as much as a drowned man. Every step it shifted against my spine with a dull iron knock, like a heart beating out of time, and every step I thought: *this is what you carried off the mountain too, Zeph. Just smaller then. Just a body instead of a box.*

Halfway up, the shale moved under me.

Not much. A shelf the size of a door, letting go with a sigh, sliding a foot before it caught. But enough that the safe swung left and nearly took me with it, and for three full seconds I hung there by one hand with the whole gray ocean turning slowly beneath me, thinking very clearly and very calmly: *not like this. Not after all of it, not with the thing finally in my hands.*

Then Isla's arm came down through the spray like a branch in a flood, and I took it, and we stood together on the ledge while the sea chewed up the fallen stone below us.

"Your rope work," she said, when she could breathe again, "is a war crime."

"It's salvage," I said. "It's supposed to look distressed."

She laughed—one short, sharp note, gone almost before it started—and we kept climbing.

* * *

The top of the cliff was gorse and old grass bent flat by wind, and beyond it the coast road ran empty toward Carrick Light, and between us and everything—the town, the harbor, the fleet at anchor, the whole machinery of Halloran Marine—there was nothing. No boat. The *Kestrel's Wake*, Isla's borrowed launch, sat half a mile offshore where we'd left her, riding her anchor in the rising chop, unreachable and useless. And somewhere behind us, in the darkening water below the cliffs, the fleet's second dive team would be surfacing into an empty cave, finding our cut marks still warm, and Dree Voss would be doing arithmetic.

I did the same arithmetic. It came out badly.

"They'll have boats around the point within the hour," I said. "They'll watch the road. They'll watch the harbor."

"So we walk north," Isla said. "Ten miles of coast to the next slipway. In this wind. With that." She nodded at the safe, which sat between us in the grass like something beached and dying.

"We can't carry it ten miles. We can barely carry it ten yards."

"Then we leave it."

I looked at her. Ten years of grief and stubbornness looked back at me, wind-whipped, salt-crusted, absolutely unwilling.

"No," I said.

"No," she agreed, and that was the whole negotiation, and somehow it settled the matter more firmly than any argument could have.

We dragged it into the hollow of an old turf-cutting, out of the worst of the wind, and crouched over it like conspirators over a grave. The padlock Voss's divers had fitted to the hasp was nothing—Isla broke it with a wrecking bar from the boat kit in four minutes flat—but the safe itself was another country entirely. Its dial gleamed dully under the bruised sky, patient as a magistrate.

"I've watched men crack safes in films," Isla said. "They always use a stethoscope."

"Do you have a stethoscope?"

"I have a wrench and a deep disappointment in cinema."

In the end it wasn't skill that opened it. It was the storm. The leading edge of the gale came up the cliff face in a single long shove, flattening the gorse, driving spray forty feet inland, and the safe—old, salt-seized, its hinges corroded by a decade underwater—shuddered in its frame. Something inside it shifted with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Isla heard it the same moment I did. We looked at each other, and then at the hinge side, and I put the wrecking bar against the upper hinge plate and hit it once, twice, three times, each blow swallowed by the wind, until the plate tore loose with a shriek of metal that no one in the world heard but us.

The door fell open like a mouth.

* * *

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were the ledgers.

Seven of them. Bound in gray board, their corners furred with mold, their spines stamped in faded gilt: *HALLORAN MARINE SALVAGE & SURVEY — ACCOUNTS.* Beneath them lay a bundle of letters tied with legal tape, and beneath those, flat as a slab, a single typed list on onionskin paper, so old it had gone the color of tea.

We didn't speak. The wind spoke for both of us, shoving at our shoulders, trying to read over them.

Isla lifted the first ledger onto her knees and opened it, and I watched her face do something complicated—watched hope and horror arrive at the same door and neither give way. Her finger moved down columns of figures. Her lips moved slightly, counting.

"These are the survey costs," she said quietly. "For the Kestrel Pass route. Dated eight months *before* the expedition." She turned pages faster. "Zeph. They surveyed the pass. They knew the ice bridge was failing. There's a memo here—'structural degradation, eastern crossing, recommend reroute or delay'—and it's signed, and it's dated, and they sent my father across it anyway because delaying cost money and the contract had a penalty clause."

The wind took her hair across her face and she didn't bother to push it back.

"There's more," I said, because my voice worked even if nothing else did. "Look at the accounts. Look at who got paid."

She found it herself, a few pages on. Disbursements. Round sums, recurring, coded only by initials—and beside three sets of initials, in a later hand, the words that turned my stomach even after ten years of knowing the shape of the truth: *inquiry. inquiry. inquiry.*

"The officials," Isla said. "The men who ran the hearing. They were on the payroll before my father's body was even found."

"Not found," I said. "Recovered. Eight months later. I carried him down myself." I hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, flat as the sea. "And they sat there in that room with his boots on the table between us and read their falsified logs, and I sat there and couldn't prove a single thing, because they'd bought everyone whose signature mattered."

Isla closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. Then she untied the legal tape and drew out the last document—the one that stopped us both.

It was a letter. Typed, unsigned, but initialed in ink gone brown: *E.H.*

Edmund Halloran. The founder himself. The name on the ships, the name on the fleet in our bay, the name that had been carved into the harbor wall since before either of us was born.

Isla read it aloud, because some things need a voice or they aren't real. It was a confession, plain and cowardly and complete. It named the falsified logs. It named the bribed inquiry officials by full name and office. It named the penalty clause and the decision made in a boardroom over lunch while a client and his guide walked into a known deathtrap. And near the end, in a paragraph that seemed written by a different, younger hand, it said:

*I have paid these families for their silence because their silence protects me, and I tell myself it also protects them, and some nights I almost believe it.*

Below the letter lay the list.

Twenty-two names. Crew families—salvage crew who had served aboard Halloran vessels, widows, grown children, an old bosun's mother. Beside each name, a figure. A quarterly figure. Still being paid. Still being paid *now*, ten years on, by a company that needed those twenty-two households quiet more than it needed new hulls.

I sat back on my heels in the flattened grass and the wind pushed at me and I let it, because if I didn't lean into something I was going to come apart.

"There it is," I said.

"There it is," Isla said.

* * *

We might have sat there until dark, except the dark was coming whether we sat or not, and it was bringing friends.

I laid the documents out on the oilcloth in order—ledgers, memo, disbursements, letter, list—and looked at them the way I used to look at a mountain route from below, reading the line, looking for the way through. And the way through split in front of me like a channel around a reef, two channels, and only one boat.

"Publication," Isla said, before I said anything. "All of it. Everything. The ledgers, the letter, the names. Let the papers have it. Let the inquiry reopen. Let Edmund Halloran die in a courtroom instead of a bed."

"And the list?" I asked.

Her jaw tightened. "What about it?"

"Twenty-two families, Isla. Quarterly payments. You publish the ledgers, you publish the proof that the silence money exists—which you'd have to, because it's evidence, because it proves the cover-up—and the day it prints, Halloran's lawyers stop every payment by close of business. Legally they *have* to; continuing them after exposure is admission. Those people have built lives on that money. Kids in school. Mortgages. An old woman in—" I checked the list, though I knew it by heart now, "—in Porthaven, eighty-one years old, whose son died on a Halloran hull in '09. What does she live on when the checks stop? Apologies?"

"That's not your debt," Isla said sharply. "That's *their* debt. That's the man who wrote that letter. He made them dependent on their own grief—"

"Yes. He did. And if we burn it all down in one headline, he wins anyway, because the ones he bought stay bought and the ones he ruined get ruined twice, and I—" I stopped. The wind filled the pause.

"You what?" Isla asked, quieter.

"I get my name back." I said it and immediately wished I hadn't, because saying it aloud showed me exactly what it was: small, and hungry, and mine. "Ten years, Isla. Blacklisted in two countries. Every harbor I've worked under a false name, every client who hired me and never knew they were being guided by the man who 'lost Marchetti on Kestrel Pass.' I could be *me* again. I could stand in a room and say my own name out loud." I looked at the ledgers glowing pale in the dying light. "God help me, I want it. I've wanted it so long I'd forgotten it was want and not just breathing."

Isla was silent a long moment. Below us the sea had gone the color of gunmetal, and the first true rain arrived, fine and horizontal, needling our faces.

"My father," she said at last, "kept a rule. He told me once, when I was small, and I thought it was just a thing fathers say. *When the choice is between yourself and someone with less, take less.*" She picked up the list of twenty-two names and held it in both hands, sheltering it from the rain with her body. "He crossed a failing ice bridge because a delay would have cost him a penalty he couldn't afford, and because the company told him it was safe, and because he trusted the world to be roughly honest. He took less his whole life. He took the least there was to take." Her voice cracked and steadied. "If I publish everything and twenty-two families lose the only justice they'll ever see—money isn't justice, Zeph, but it's the only shape they were ever offered—then I'm spending their lives to buy yours back. And his rule says no. His rule says *no*."

"And if we bury it," I said, "Halloran dies rich and warm, and I stay dead. Officially. Forever. Every harbor, every hire, every year left to me—I spend them under a name that isn't mine, carrying a thing I didn't do."

"Yes," Isla said simply. "That's the bargain. That's what the sea's charging."

I looked at the documents a long time. The rain thickened. Somewhere south, faint under the wind, I thought I heard an engine—not certain, not yet, but the fleet would be moving, and the light was going, and whatever we decided we would decide it wet and cold and soon.

Ten years ago I had stood in an inquiry room and been unable to prove a single thing. Now I held the proof in a waterproof wrap on a clifftop, and the proof asked a price I hadn't seen printed anywhere.

"Then we don't publish," I heard myself say. "Not the ledgers. Not the list. We use the letter—the confession alone—to force the inquiry reopened. Names of the officials, the falsified logs, enough to clear the record. But the hush payments stay sealed. We go to the families ourselves, one at a time, and we tell *them* the truth and let them choose what to do with it. Their grief. Their choice. Not the newspapers'."

Isla stared at me. "You'd give up your name. For them. For people you've never met."

"For people my ruin helped pay for," I said. "Seems only fair I keep paying."

Something moved across her face that I couldn't read in that light—grief and respect and something warmer underneath, banked like a stove overnight. She reached out and put her hand flat on the oilcloth, over the ledgers, over the letter, over the list, as if taking an oath on all of it at once.

"Together, then," she said. "Your plan. My father's rule."

"Together."

And that was when the engine sound resolved itself out of the wind for certain—close now, rounding the northern headland, running without lights—and a white searchlight beam swept up the cliff face below us, slow as a lighthouse, patient as a tide, and began to climb.

Isla killed the last of our lantern with her palm.

"Voss," she breathed.

"No," I said, watching the beam crawl toward our hollow, watching the rain turn it into a solid column of ghost-light. "Worse. That's not a salvage boat's searchlight. That's a police tender."

The beam reached the lip of the hollow and stopped, full on the rain, full on the flattened grass, full on the two of us and the open safe between us—and a voice came up over a loudhailer, distorted by wind, unmistakable all the same, calling a name I hadn't answered to in ten years.

"*Zephyrine Underhill!* Stay where you are!"

Isla's hand found mine in the dark and gripped it, and the ledgers lay between us and the light like a heart finally exposed, still beating.
