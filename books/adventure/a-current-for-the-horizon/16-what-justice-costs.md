## 16. What Justice Costs

The wind had changed while we argued with the sea, and now it was arguing with us.

Isla Marchetti sat with her back against a shelf of granite that smelled of salt and old birdlime, the confession letter open across her knees, and she could not make herself read it a second time. Once had been enough to burn it into her. The paper was warped from its years underwater, the ink bloomed brown at the edges like frost damage, but the words were clear enough—clearer than anything had been in ten years.

*The logs were altered before the inquiry convened. I was instructed by E. Halloran personally. Payments were made to the following parties...*

And then the names. Three of them. Men who had sat behind the long table at the inquiry in Threlkeld and listened to her mother weep in the public gallery, and whose signatures were already bought and paid for before Zephyrine Underhill ever walked into that room.

Below them, the cliffs dropped two hundred feet to a sea gone suddenly gentle, as if it too were waiting to hear what they would decide. The safe lay open between them, its door hanging like a jaw. Inside: the ledgers, bound in oilcloth, dry as bone. And beside them, the list.

She picked up the list again because she couldn't help it. Eleven families. Eleven lines of handwriting, each with a sum and a date, stretching back a decade. The widow of the *Meridian Star*'s engineer had been paid quarterly for ten years. The son of the deckhand Okafor—she remembered his name from her father's crew manifest, the boy who'd been nineteen when the ship went down—had his apprenticeship fees marked *paid, per arrangement*. A house in Millhaven. A pension for a sister with failing eyes.

Blood money, if you wanted to call it that. But it was also rent. It was also medicine. It was also a girl's schooling that would simply stop, mid-sentence, if the ledgers went into the light.

"You're doing the arithmetic," Zephyrine said.

He was standing at the cliff edge with his back to her, the way he seemed to do everything—as if facing away made the thing easier to say. The gale's leading edge was still combing through his hair, though the worst of the storm had spent itself seaward hours ago. He hadn't slept. Neither of them had. There was a cut above his eye from the beam that had nearly killed him inside the cave, and the bruise around it had gone the color of deep water.

"I'm doing more than arithmetic," Isla said. "I'm doing what you apparently can't. I'm counting people."

"Then count me too." He turned. His voice was quiet, which somehow made it worse. "One more line on your list. Ten years of my life. My certification. My name. Every door in two countries closed." He came closer, and she saw that his hands were shaking—not with cold, though there was plenty of that, but with something he was trying very hard to hold still. "You have the proof, Isla. You've had it in your hands for an hour. Say it out loud. Tell me what you're thinking of doing."

She looked down at the ledgers rather than at him. "I'm thinking," she said slowly, "that eleven families eat because of these payments."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have."

* * *

They had come off the water half-drowned, hauling the safe up a fisherman's stair cut into the cliff below Carrick Light, and for the first hour there had been no argument at all—only the animal business of survival. Wringing out clothes. Sharing the flask of brandy from the lighthouse keeper's emergency cache, which they'd broken into without apology. Watching the horizon for Voss's boats.

It was only when their hands stopped shaking that the thing between them finally stood up and demanded to be looked at.

"The ledgers get published," Zephyrine said now. "All of them. Every page."

"And the list?"

"The list gets published too."

"Then you sentence eleven households to poverty so your name comes clean." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, her mother's voice, the one her mother had used with the inquiry officials, the one that had frightened everyone including herself. "That's the trade you're proposing. Your vindication for their livelihoods. Say it plainly if that's what you want."

"I am saying it plainly."

"No." She stood up, and the wind took her hair across her face and she didn't bother with it. "You're not. Because if you were saying it plainly you'd be able to look at the list while you said it. Look at it, Zephyrine. Look at the Okafor line. That boy is in his third year of marine engineering school. Do you know what happens to a scholarship that arrives *per arrangement* when the arrangement becomes front-page news? He doesn't get angry. He doesn't get justice. He just gets pulled out of his life."

"He gets the truth."

"The truth doesn't pay tuition!" The words cracked out of her louder than she meant, and somewhere below them a gull lifted off the cliff face in alarm. "I have lived on truth for ten years. Truth was all I had after they buried my father under your name. I built my whole life out of it—every archive, every shipping record, every sleepless night with my father's letters. And do you know what truth got me? Nothing. Truth changes nothing unless someone with power decides to act on it, and the men with power are the ones on your confession letter."

Zephyrine flinched. She saw it land—the mention of the letter, of her father—and something moved through his face like weather.

"Your father," he said, and stopped. Started again. "Your father trusted me to bring him down off Kestrel Pass alive. Whatever else is true, whatever those ledgers say about Halloran, that part is mine. I tied him into that rope. I read that storm wrong. So don't stand there and tell me I haven't paid, Isla. I've paid for ten years in a currency you can't even see from where you're standing."

"Then take it back!" she said. "Take your name back! That's what this is about, isn't it? Not the families, not the truth—*you*. For once in your gray, hidden, fog-bound little life, you want something for yourself, and you hate it, and you're going to do it anyway, and you want me to make it righteous for you!"

The words hung in the wind between them, cruel and exact, and she knew even as they left her mouth that some of them were unfair—that he had carried her out of a flooding cave an hour ago, that he had held the cutting torch steady while the water climbed his chest—but she was past fairness. Grief had been waiting ten years for a door, and he had left it open.

He didn't shout back. That was the terrible thing about him; he never shouted back. He just stood there in the dying light with the bruises rising on his face, and she watched him turn her words over and find the true ones among the cruel ones, and accept them all.

"Yes," he said finally. "Yes. I want it. God help me, I want my name back." His voice broke on the last word, quietly, like ice giving way. "I have told myself for ten years that I didn't care. That anonymity was a kind of freedom. That the mountain took my reputation and gave me back the fog and I should be grateful for the trade. And every word of it was a lie I told so I could get up in the morning." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them. "So yes. I argue for myself. For once. And I hate every second of it, and I will hate it more tomorrow, and I am asking you anyway."

* * *

The light failed by degrees, the way it does on that coast—not a sunset but a slow drowning of the sky, gray into darker gray, until the Carrick Light began its rotation above them and swept its white arm across the clifftop every fourteen seconds. Fourteen seconds of visibility. Fourteen seconds of darkness. Isla found herself timing her thoughts to it, the way sailors time their strokes.

They sat on either side of the open safe like two people keeping vigil over a body.

She read her father's letter again. The third reading was the one that undid her—not the accusations, she'd known those in her bones for a decade, but the postscript, written in his smaller hand, the hand he used for things he wasn't sure he should say:

*I. — If you are reading this, then I did not come home, and I am sorry, and I need you to know that whatever happened on that mountain, I never once believed it was the guide's fault. I watched him read that sky for three days. He read it better than any man I've hired. If they blame him, it is because someone needed a place to put the blame, and guides are cheaper than companies.*

She pressed the paper flat against her sternum and let herself cry, briefly, silently, the way she'd learned to cry in archives and courthouse galleries where crying was a weakness to be exploited. Zephyrine pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

When she could speak again, she said: "My father would have chosen the families."

Zephyrine looked over.

"If he were sitting here with this safe open in front of him—if it were his name buried instead of yours—he would have burned the list before he let one of those children lose their schooling." She laughed, and it came out broken. "He used to say that a man's name is the cheapest thing he owns. He said it the week before he hired you. He said, *Isla, if I ever die inconveniently, mind you don't let anyone make a monument out of me. Monuments are expensive, and somebody always sends the bill to the wrong person.*"

"He sounds like a man I would have liked to know."

"You did know him. For six days." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Six days, and he trusted you with his life, and he was right to. That's the joke of it, isn't it? The inquiry took everything from you based on six days, and the truth of those six days was that you were the best guide he ever hired."

The Light swept past. Darkness. Light again.

"So what do we do?" Zephyrine asked. It was not a rhetorical question. She could hear how much it cost him—the surrender in it, the man who had just argued for himself for the first time in a decade already folding his own case shut. "Tell me what we do, Isla. Because I'll tell you honestly: if you tell me publish it all, I'll publish it. And if you tell me bury the list, I'll—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I'll spend the rest of my life wondering what my name would have been worth."

"That's not fair," she whispered. "You can't put that on me."

"Neither of us has ever played fair," he said. "We've been unfair to each other since the day you walked into the Salt & Anchor and looked at me like I was the reason your father was dead. We might as well be unfair together."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she gathered the ledgers into her lap, all that weight of paper, all those columns of figures that were really columns of lives—payments made, payments promised, silence purchased at quarterly intervals—and she thought about her father's hands, which had been good hands, careful hands, hands that had taught her to splice line and read charts and never, ever spend another person's money to buy your own comfort.

"We hold the ledgers," she said at last. "We give the court the confession letter—it names Halloran, it names the bribed officials, it clears you, and it doesn't touch a single family on that list. The letter is enough to reopen the inquiry. Voss can testify to what she was hired to do. You get your name back." Her throat tightened. "And the list stays sealed. The payments continue. The families keep their lives. And nobody ever knows what it cost."

Zephyrine was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "The founder walks free?"

"For now. Until the families' children are grown, until the payments aren't load-bearing anymore. Then maybe the ledgers see daylight. Maybe I'm an old woman and I hand them to some journalist and let history sort it out." She shrugged, and felt the whole weight of the decade shift on her shoulders. "Justice delayed isn't justice. I know that. I've known it since I was sixteen years old, sitting in that gallery. But justice *purchased with other people's ruin* isn't justice either. My father taught me that too. He just taught it to me too late for it to be easy."

Zephyrine reached across the safe and touched the edge of the ledger stack—did not take it, just touched it, the way you'd touch a coffin lid. "Ten years," he said softly. "I traded ten years for this moment, and the moment turns out to be me choosing to stay lost." He exhaled, long and unsteady. "You're right, though. God help me, you're right. It's the first time anyone's been right *at* me in a decade and I can't even enjoy it."

"Enjoy it later," Isla said. "When you have a later."

And that was when she saw it—the thing she'd been refusing to see since the light began its turning. Down the cliff path, far below, where the fisherman's stair met the coast road: a lantern. Moving. Steady. Climbing.

Not wandering. Climbing. Toward the lighthouse.

"Zephyrine." Her voice went thin. "Someone's coming."

He was on his feet before she finished, all the exhaustion burned off him in an instant, and he followed her gaze down to the climbing light. Above them, the great lamp of Carrick swept its arm across the dark, and in that brief white flood Isla saw the road clearly for one full second—

—and saw, behind the lantern, the shapes of four more figures, moving fast, fanning out across the clifftop the way salvagers fan out when they're closing a net.

"Voss," Zephyrine breathed. "She didn't wait for morning."

The Light turned. Darkness fell. And somewhere below them, boots rang on iron stair-rungs, coming up.
