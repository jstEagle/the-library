## 17. The Trap at Carrick Light

The storm had spent itself by dawn, but the sea hadn't forgiven anyone yet. It came in long, bruised swells, gray-green and heavy, slamming the base of Carrick Point with a sound like a door being shut over and over. The lighthouse stood above it all on its black thumb of rock, white paint peeling in long ribbons, its lamp long since automated and its keeper's cottage gutted by salt and years. Gulls owned the place now. They wheeled and screamed around the gallery rail as if they'd been paid to.

Zephyrine climbed the last stretch of the cliff path with the oilskin-wrapped safe contents pressed against his chest, and every step felt like walking into a mouth.

They'd come here because there was nowhere else. The harbor was Voss's. The road south ran through the village, where the harbormaster's eyes were everywhere and his loyalty had been bought months ago. The cliffs north were sheer for six miles. Isla had said *the lighthouse* the way you'd say *high ground*—some instinct older than either of them—and he'd agreed because agreeing was easier than admitting how few choices remained. One boat. Theirs. And Dree Voss had it, moored in the lee below the point with two of her crew aboard, patient as tide.

"She'll have seen us coming," Isla said beside him. Her hair was still stiff with salt from the cave, her hands bandaged where the beam had caught her. She climbed without complaint, which he was learning was simply who she was. "She let us get here."

"I know."

"That doesn't worry you?"

"It worries me plenty." He paused at the top, where the path widened into cracked flagstones and a rusted iron gate hung open on one hinge. "But worried is what I've got. Might as well spend it somewhere with a view."

Isla almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked past him at the lighthouse and stopped smiling altogether.

Dree Voss was sitting on the cottage doorstep.

She wasn't armed—or rather, she carried no weapon visibly, which from her amounted to the same courtesy. She wore the same oilskins as always, dark green gone black with spray, and her gray-streaked hair was tied back wet. A thermos sat beside her, steam curling off the cup in her hand. She looked less like a woman who had cornered them than one who had arrived early for an appointment.

"Mr. Underhill." She said it evenly, no triumph in it, and that frightened him more than shouting would have. "Miss Marchetti. You look terrible."

"We've been swimming," Isla said.

"In February. Yes." Voss rose, unhurried, joints cracking like ship's rigging. She nodded toward the sea below, where their stolen tender—the one thing standing between them and thirty miles of coast—bobbed at its mooring under the watch of her crewman. "You've had a productive week. I'll give you that. Nobody's gotten into that cave twice in a hundred years, and you've done it three times between you. I've read the charts. I don't understand how you did it. I suspect you don't entirely either."

"Instinct," Zephyrine said.

"Instinct." She tasted the word. "That's what they took your license for, isn't it? Instinct. Reading weather nobody else could read, and being wrong once." She sipped from the cup. "Except you weren't wrong, were you? We both know that now. I've known it since the second day, when I pulled your file and saw the shape of it. Falsified logs make a particular kind of mess. I've cleaned up enough company disasters to recognize the handwriting."

Isla shifted beside him. "Then why are you still doing their work?"

"Because I'm paid to." Voss said it flatly, without shame, and then, quieter: "And because until this week I told myself paper was paper. Ledgers, manifests, insurance claims. Salvage men don't ask what's written in the boxes we raise. We ask what they weigh." She set down the cup and looked out at the water. "My employer is dying. Did you know that? Halloran himself. Not the company—the man. Eighty-one years old, lungs full of the sea he never sailed, and terrified of what follows him into the ground. He wants those ledgers drowned or burned or buried in his own mausoleum, whatever keeps them from the courts. He has wanted it for ten years. I am the last person he trusts to want it more quietly than he does."

"And you do?" Zephyrine asked.

Voss turned and looked at him straight on, and up close her eyes were the pale washed-out blue of old chart paper, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"No," she said. "Not anymore."

* * *

The wind came around with the morning, swinging northeast, carrying the smell of kelp and cold iron. They stood in the shelter of the cottage wall while Voss laid it out—not as a threat, though everything about her was a threat held politely in check, but as a ledger of her own.

"Here's how it stands. I have your boat. I have four divers, two vessels, and a radio. I have a client who will pay me enough to retire if I bring him ash and ashes, and who will ruin me if I bring him nothing. You have documents that can hang him and half the inquiry board, and no way off this rock except me." She spread her hands. "So. My offer. Give me the ledgers—all of them—and I put you both ashore at Penharrow by nightfall. Unharmed, unseen, back to whatever lives you were living before that tide went out. That's generous. That's more than generous. That's me spending my reputation to buy you out of a hole."

"And Halloran gets to die clean," Isla said.

"Halloran gets to die," Voss said. "Clean or dirty is between him and whatever he believes in. I'm not a court, Miss Marchetti. I'm a woman with boats."

"You're a woman with a choice."

"Aye." Something moved behind the pale eyes—there and gone, like a fish under ice. "There's always that."

Zephyrine had been quiet through all of it, holding the oilskin bundle against his chest, feeling the corners of the ledgers through the canvas like the edges of a bone he'd swallowed. Ten years he had imagined this moment—imagined clearing his name, imagined the word *exonerated*, imagined walking back into a certification office and watching some clerk's face change as he signed. He had built whole nights out of that imagining, out on the fog coast, alone with the gulls and the foghorn. It had kept him alive the way a splinter keeps a wound alive.

And now the offer was on the table. His name, his life back, traded away for silence, and all he had to do was hand over the bundle and walk down to the boat.

He thought of the list they'd found folded inside the confession letter. Eleven families. Eleven envelopes, sent quarterly, marked only with initials. The widow in Penharrow who still left a lamp burning. The son in the merchant academy whose tuition was paid by a ghost. Ten years of hush money, and the moment the ledgers went public, every payment stopped, every family dragged into the inquiry as accomplices or fools, their names printed next to Halloran's in columns they never asked to stand in.

"Your offer's missing something," Zephyrine said.

Voss raised an eyebrow. "Is it."

"The letter." He worked the oilskin loose just far enough to draw out the confession—a single sheet, brittle now, Halloran's own hand, naming himself, naming the falsified logs, naming the three inquiry officials whose signatures had been bought before Kestrel Pass ever went wrong. "This one page. This goes to the maritime court. Not the ledgers. Not the payment lists. Just this."

Voss stared at it. For a long moment the only sound was the sea working the rocks below and the gulls arguing overhead.

"You understand what you're offering," she said slowly. "That letter convicts him. It also opens the inquiry. And an opened inquiry means the ledgers get subpoenaed eventually—every page, every name."

"Not necessarily. Confession entered, sentence passed, case closed for want of further public interest. Courts are lazy. Boards are lazier. If the letter lands with the right registrar and the families' names stay out of the record—" He stopped. Heard himself. Heard exactly what he was doing: engineering justice so that it touched everyone but him. "The inquiry reopens. My name comes up. It gets cleared or it doesn't. But it happens in a courtroom, on the record, with my name attached—not in a newspaper, not with the crew's families strung up alongside the man who bought them."

"And you trust me to deliver it?" Voss said. "A salvager. On Halloran's payroll as of Tuesday."

"No," Zephyrine said. "I don't trust you to deliver it. I trust you to testify."

The wind dropped, just for a breath, the way it does before it changes its mind.

"You'd swear it," he went on. "Before the court. What you found in the wreck. What your client ordered. What you were told to do to two civilians on this coast this week—I know what the order was, Voss. I've spent ten years around men who drown things. I know what 'handle it quietly' sounds like from the inside." He held the letter out across the space between them, over the cracked flagstones, into the wind. "You carry this in yourself. Your hands, your oath, your testimony. Not a courier. Not a dead drop. You stand up in that courtroom and say *I was hired to bury this*. And then whatever happens to me, whatever happens to the ledgers—you'll have done one clean thing in a dirty trade, and it'll be yours for the rest of your life. Nobody retires from salvage with clean hands. But you could retire with one."

Voss didn't take the letter. She looked at it the way sailors look at weather they've been pretending isn't coming.

"You're asking me to burn my career."

"I'm asking you to spend it," Zephyrine said. "There's a difference. Burning is waste. Spending buys something."

Below them, the tender knocked against its mooring buoy, patient, indifferent. One of Voss's crewmen lit a cigarette on the rocks and the smoke flattened in the wind.

"And if I say no?" Voss asked. "If I take the bundle off you and put you both ashore anyway, like the generous woman I claimed to be five minutes ago?"

"Then you'll go home rich," Zephyrine said, "and in about eighteen months a dying man's estate lawyers will find out what you did or didn't do with a court document, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering which of them is coming for you. Or—" he shrugged, "—you keep the ledgers, Halloran dies peaceful, eleven families lose their income inside a year, and a guide who was already ruined stays ruined. Everybody gets what they paid for. The sea's happy. The sea always is."

Isla made a small sound beside him—not quite a laugh. He glanced at her. She was watching Voss with an expression he couldn't read, something fierce and sorrowful at once, and he realized she was seeing her father in this somehow, in the argument, in the arithmetic of lives against names. He'd ask her later. If there was a later.

Voss walked to the edge of the flagstones. Stood looking down at the water for a long time, her back to them, oilskins snapping in the wind. When she turned around, her face had settled into something older and harder and, strangely, lighter.

"My father ran a pilot boat," she said, as if continuing a conversation they hadn't been having. "Forty years. Never lost a soul. When he died they found a drawer full of letters from widows thanking him, and not one penny saved, and he was the happiest broke man I ever knew." She wiped spray from her face with the heel of her hand. "I used to think that was weakness. Sentiment. Bad arithmetic." She looked at the letter in Zephyrine's outstretched hand. "I'm fifty-seven years old, Mr. Underhill. I've raised wrecks on four coasts and I have never once raised anything that made the sea give back what it took. Until this week. Two civilians in a borrowed boat did what my fleet couldn't." 

She reached out and took the letter.

Her fingers closed around it carefully, the way you handle something recovered from deep water—something that has changed by being brought up into the light.

"One condition," she said. "The ledgers stay sealed until the verdict. No leaks, no copies, no clever registrar games. If the court needs them, they get them properly, subpoenaed, with protections for the families written in. You hold them. Both of you. And if either of you sells a page to a newspaper before that gavel falls, I will personally see to it that the rest of your lives become very short and very damp. Are we understood?"

"Understood," Isla said.

Zephyrine nodded. It cost him—he felt it leave him like blood, the last use of the ledgers as leverage, the last card—and it cost him less than he'd expected. "Understood."

Voss tucked the letter inside her oilskins, against her chest, the same place he'd been carrying it. Then she jerked her chin toward the path down to the water.

"Get in the boat," she said. "Both of you. There's one more tide tomorrow that reaches that cave, and I have orders to stop you from using it." A pause. The pale eyes met his, and something in them had shifted—some weight set down, some other weight taken up. "I intend to disobey those orders. So you'd better be quick, and you'd better be quiet, because after tomorrow the sea closes that door for the season—and God knows what it charges to open it again."

She turned and started down the cliff path toward her crewman and his cigarette, leaving Zephyrine standing in the wind with empty hands and a heart going like a hammer, and Isla's voice low beside him:

"What do we tell the families? About tomorrow?"

He watched Voss descend, small and steady against the enormous gray water, carrying another man's confession against her chest like a coal she meant to deliver still burning.

"We tell them," he said, "to be ready to receive something."
