## 18. Last Tide

**ZEPHYRINE**

The tide came back for them at dawn, the way it always did—patient, indifferent, filling the cave's throat with green light and cold water until the Meridian Star floated again inside her stone coffin, lifting on the swell like a sleeper turning over.

Zephyrine stood in the tender a hundred yards off the cliff line and watched the water climb the black rock, and he thought: *this is the last time.* The almanac said so plainly enough. After tonight's tide the spring series would close, and the cave would seal itself behind forty feet of sea for months, maybe forever—the winter storms would bring down half that cliff face before it opened again, if it ever did. The sea had charged its admission. It was collecting now.

"You're doing the thing where you stare at nothing," Isla said beside him.

"I'm staring at everything."

"Same thing." She pulled her gloves tighter. They had bought new ones yesterday, proper dive gloves, and she'd held them in her lap the whole way back from the chandler like they were something breakable. "Say it out loud instead. Whatever it is."

He looked at her. Ten days ago she'd been a stranger with her father's eyes and her father's stubbornness, and now she read him the way he read water—which was to say better than anyone alive and more uncomfortably than he liked.

"We're not going for the ledgers," he said. "We're going for them." He nodded toward the shore, toward the town still gray and sleeping under its fog, where eleven families would be waking up without knowing what today might bring them. "The personal effects. Whatever's left in the crew quarters. Watches. Letters. Rings. Photographs. Things that were never evidence and never treasure and never worth anything to Halloran Marine or Dree Voss or any court in either country."

Isla was quiet a moment. "Her father's watch," she said. "If it's there."

"If it's there."

"And you're sure Voss won't stop us."

He wasn't sure of anything about Dree Voss except that she hadn't taken their boat when she could have, hadn't reported the safe's contents when she'd had every reason to, and had stood at Carrick Light with the confession letter in her hands looking like a woman holding something hot. He said none of this. What he said was: "She gave us the tide window. She didn't have to."

"She also has three ships and thirty men."

"Yes," Zephyrine said, and started the engine. "So let's not be late."

* * *

The cave took them in the way it always had, which was reluctantly—a squeeze of current at the mouth, a hand of cold closing around the hull, then the sudden hush of the inner chamber where the water lay flat as poured glass and the air smelled of salt and old iron and something older, mineral and patient, like the inside of the earth holding its breath.

Their lights swept the chamber walls. The wreck sat exactly where they'd left her, the Meridian Star listing gently against the shelf rock, her name still legible along the stern in flaking gilt letters, *MERIDIAN STAR — PORT CARRICK*, as if the ship herself wanted to be remembered properly. Ten years underwater and she'd kept her dignity better than most men keep theirs above it.

They dove the crew quarters first because Isla insisted and because Zephyrine had already decided to agree before she asked.

It was a small compartment aft, four bunks, and the water in it was thick with drifting silt that turned their lights into columns of gold smoke. Time moved strangely down here. Every motion had to be slow, deliberate, a courtesy to the dead. Zephyrine worked his way along the lower bunk and found, wedged between the frame and the hull plating, a canvas duffel gone stiff as board, its strap rotted through. Inside: a wool sweater fused into one solid mass, a tin cup, and a photograph in a waterproof sleeve—a woman laughing on a dock, squinting against sun, holding up a fish the size of her forearm. On the back, in pencil faded almost to nothing: *Marta, August. Beat me again.*

Marta Okafor. Second engineer. Zephyrine remembered the name from the inquiry transcripts, remembered how often it appeared and how little anyone had listened to it.

"I've got boots," Isla's voice came through the comm, muffled and strange. "Small ones. And—" a pause, long enough that he turned—and there she was in the amber murk, holding up a child's shoe, leather gone the color of driftwood, no bigger than his palm. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that the shoe didn't say better.

They worked. That was the whole of it, the next hour: two people moving through a drowned room, gathering what the sea had kept and never needed. A pocket watch, stopped at 4:17, its chain fused to a belt buckle. A rosary. A harmonica, its reeds furred with soft white growth, and when Isla pressed one key out of some instinct neither of them mentioned afterward, it gave a single bent note that traveled through the water like a bird through a closed window. A bundle of letters tied with fishing line, the ink long dissolved but the paper's creases still holding the shape of hands that had folded them. A wedding ring in a matchbox. Two more rings worn on fingers that had been fingers once, and those they left, because some things belong where they are.

Zephyrine found his own ghost last. Tucked in the captain's footlocker, beneath charts he'd helped draw in another life: a length of red-and-white braided rope, coiled neat as a sleeping snake. His guide rope. Kestrel Pass rope, mountain rope, rope that should have ended its life on scree ten thousand feet above any ocean—and here it was, impossibly, carried by currents and coincidence into the one place on earth he'd been hiding from himself. He'd found it on Tide Three and told himself it meant nothing. Holding it now, weightless in the water, he admitted what it meant. Nothing stays lost. Everything comes around. The sea and the mountain had conspired to tell him so, and he was done pretending not to hear.

He put the rope in the bag with the rest. Some things you return too.

* * *

**ISLA**

They surfaced into the inner chamber as the tide began its slow withdrawal, and Isla pulled off her mask and breathed the cave's cold iron air and felt, for the first time in ten years, that her lungs were working all the way down.

Eleven families. She counted the bags twice, then a third time, because counting was something her hands could do while her heart caught up. Eleven duffels and satchels and oilcloth bundles, laid side by side on the flat rock shelf where the water couldn't reach, each one tagged in Zephyrine's blocky print with a name and a berth number. He'd known the manifest by heart. Of course he had. He'd lived inside that disaster so long it had become a kind of address.

"My father's effects aren't here," she said. Not an accusation. Just the fact, set down between them.

"He wasn't crew." Zephyrine wrung out his hair, gray-streaked now, plastered flat. "Clients berthed forward. That section collapsed in the sinking—I saw it go on Tide Three. I'm sorry, Isla. If there was anything of his, the sea's keeping it."

She nodded. And found, examining the feeling carefully, that it wasn't grief exactly—or not only. Her father had spent his last hour, according to every reconstruction she'd ever built, doing what he always did: asking questions, writing things down, refusing to leave a ship that was taking on water because the ledgers weren't secured yet. He'd died holding the truth. She'd spent ten years trying to pry it out of the world's hands. Now it sat in a lighthouse strongbox waiting for a maritime court, and what remained for her was this—smaller, softer work. Boots and photographs and a child's shoe carried up into daylight.

"This is the part he'd have liked best," she said. "Not the inquiry. This."

Zephyrine looked at her across the bags, and in the cave's dim green light his face did something unguarded. "You think?"

"I know. He used to say salvage was just grief with paperwork." She laughed, and it came out wet. "He'd have hated Halloran for what they did to you. He'd have hated them for the ledgers. But this—" she touched the nearest bag, Marta Okafor's, with the photograph of the laughing woman inside, "—this he'd have carried up the cliff himself."

Above them, faint through the cave mouth, came the sound of engines. Not one engine. Several, idling in formation, the way ships idle when they're waiting for orders.

Isla went still. Zephyrine was already moving to the lip of the inner pool, shading his light, peering out through the arch at the bay beyond.

"They're not coming in," he said slowly. "They're just... sitting there. Between us and open water."

"The harbor launch too. And that's—" She squinted. "That's Voss's gig. She's out there herself."

Zephyrine checked his watch against the tide tables chalked inside his forearm—a habit from diving, ink and salt, the numbers riding his skin like a tattoo. "We've got ninety minutes of ebb before the mouth gets ugly. We load the tender, we run the gap, and we find out what Dree Voss has decided to be today."

"And if she decided wrong?"

He was quiet a moment. Out in the bay, the fleet's lights burned in a ragged crescent, and somewhere among them a radio crackled with voices too far away to parse.

"Then we'll learn what my father learned on Kestrel Pass," Isla answered for him, before he could. "That the people who give the orders rarely come down to see what they've ordered." She picked up two bags, one in each hand, and felt their absurd lightness. Whole lives, and they weighed less than groceries. "Come on. Let's not keep the sea waiting. She hates that."

* * *

**ZEPHYRINE**

The tender cleared the cave mouth into a bay gone strange with evening—flat calm, fog burning off in long rags, and the salvage fleet arranged across the water like a jury that had already deliberated.

Three ships. The *Corvus* nearest, floodlights on, divers' platform lowered. The harbor launch standing off to seaward. And dead ahead, squarely in the channel, the black hull of Voss's own vessel, her gig bobbing alongside it, Dree Voss visible at the rail with binoculars raised—not at them, Zephyrine noticed, but past them, at the cave.

He cut the engine and let the tender drift. No sense rowing into a verdict.

"Zeph," Isla murmured. "The *Corvus*'s deck crane is rigged."

He saw it. Rigged and manned, lines taut, ready to deploy a net or worse. Thirty men on that deck, and every one of them watching the little tender with its cargo of other people's memories.

Voss's gig puttered out alone. She came alongside without hailing, close enough that Zephyrine could see she was dressed for sea, not for ceremony, and that her face had the particular flatness of a woman carrying a decision she hadn't finished making.

"You're loaded," she said. Not a question.

"We're loaded," Zephyrine said.

"Crew effects." Her eyes moved over the tagged bags, and something flickered there—recognition, maybe, of names she'd salvaged wrecks beside for twenty years. "Eleven of them."

"Twelve bags. One man's effects split across two."

Voss looked at the cave, then at the fleet, then at him. When she spoke again her voice was low, pitched for the three of them and the water. "An hour ago I got a direct order. Founder's office, relayed through counsel. Intercept the tender. Recover all materials. Detain both of you pending legal action." She said it the way she'd say *wind's backing, barometer's falling*—weather report, not threat. "They know what you took from the safe. They know what's in the letter. They're scared, Underhill. Scared men with money make stupid orders."

"And?" Isla said.

Voss was silent long enough that the water spoke instead, slapping the hulls, patient.

"And I've sunk things for clients my whole career," she said. "Ships, mostly. Once or twice reputations. Never asked what was in the boxes, because not asking was the job." She turned and looked full at Zephyrine, and he saw that her eyes were the exact gray-green of deep water over sand. "But I watched your father's letter get read aloud at Carrick Light, girl, and I watched eleven names on a silence ledger, and I've decided I'm done being the sea's errand runner for men who drown paper instead of facing it."

She straightened. Raised her voice—not shouting, but carrying, the voice of a woman accustomed to being heard over weather. "Stand down the crane!" she called across the water, and the word went from ship to ship like a current. "*Corvus*, hold station. Nobody touches that tender. Anybody asks, the tide window closed early and the cave's undivable. Log it however you like—I'll sign it."

On the *Corvus*'s deck, a pause, and then the crane lines went slack, one by one, like fists unclenching.

"You'll lose the contract," Zephyrine said quietly. "Maybe the company."

"I'll lose a client who wanted me to drown two civilians for stationery." Voss shrugged, and it was the shrug of someone setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying. "Founder's dying anyway. Let him die wondering whether the sea keeps receipts." She glanced at the bags again. "Where will you take them?"

"Door to door," Isla said. "Starting tomorrow. Marta Okafor's sister lives in Port Carrick. She gets the photograph first."

Voss nodded once, slow, like a helm answering. Then she did something Zephyrine didn't expect: she reached into her coat and set a small object on the tender's gunwale. A brass compass, old, dented, its face clouded but its needle swinging true.

"Off the *Meridian Star*'s bridge wing. Recovered it myself, eight years ago, before I knew what she was." She pushed it toward them. "Bridge crew's effects. I never knew whose. Maybe nobody's. Maybe it's the ship's own." Her jaw worked. "Take it up with the rest. Let somebody claim it or let the sea have it back. Either way it stops being mine."

Then she backed the gig away, and stood at her rail, and watched them go—not blocking the channel anymore, but opening it, the whole fleet falling aside like water around a stone.

Zephyrine started the engine. As the tender swung for home, the sun broke fully through the burning fog and lit the bay gold, and behind them the cave mouth drank the last of the light, sealing itself for the season, keeping whatever it still kept.

Isla's hand found his shoulder. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing left to say that the bags didn't say, sitting between them in the bottom of the boat—twelve bundles of ordinary, irreplaceable things, going home.

Tomorrow they'd knock on doors. Tomorrow the names would become faces, the faces would become griefs shared instead of carried alone, and somewhere in that arithmetic Zephyrine's own name might finally begin to mean something other than what the inquiry had made of it. The reopened hearing was set for Thursday. Voss would testify. The letter would speak.

But tonight—tonight there was only the run home across golden water, the compass needle steady on the gunwale between them, pointing not north but simply *true*, and the fleet standing down behind them in perfect, silent, mutinous order, letting the sea give back what it had taken.

Port Carrick's lights came up ahead through the clearing fog, and Zephyrine realized his hands were shaking, and that for the first time in ten years it wasn't fear.
