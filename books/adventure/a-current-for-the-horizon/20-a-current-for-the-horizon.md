## 20. A Current for the Horizon

The news came to Carrick Head the way news always came there—late, salt-stained, and secondhand. The boy Tam brought it up the cliff path at a run, waving a folded newspaper like a gull chasing a fish boat, and by the time he reached me he was breathless enough that I made him sit on the wall before I let him speak.

"They got him," he said. "Old man Halloran. They took him off his own boat."

I took the paper from him and read it twice, standing in the wind with my back to the sea, because some part of me still didn't trust the water not to take things back.

FOUNDER OF HALLORAN MARINE ARRESTED IN SALVAGE FRAUD CASE, said the headline. Beneath it, in smaller type: *Reopened maritime inquiry hears testimony of salvage master Dree Voss; documents recovered from wreck of the Meridian Star implicate company founder Edmund Halloran in falsification of survey records, bribery of inquiry officials, and concealment of navigational hazards leading to the loss of eleven lives.*

Eleven lives. Ten years ago they had counted only one—Marcus Marchetti, lost on Kestrel Pass—and laid him at my feet like a stone.

Tam watched me read. He was fourteen now, or near enough, and he'd learned in the past months to read faces almost as well as tide tables. "That's you, isn't it," he said. Not a question. "In the story. The unnamed source."

"There's no name there," I said.

"No," he agreed. "But Voss wouldn't have talked without somebody making her. And Isla Marchetti came here asking about caves." He shrugged, the way boys do when they've said more than they meant to. "Harbormaster's been walking around like a man waiting to be arrested himself. Thought you'd want to know."

He went back down the path, and I stood a while longer with the paper snapping in the wind, feeling something I hadn't felt in ten years and couldn't immediately name. It wasn't joy. Joy is loud. This was quieter—a loosening, like ice going out of a river, the current moving underneath again after a long freeze.

*Unnamed in every report.* Exactly as planned. The ledgers stayed sealed in Isla's keeping; the hush payments stayed flowing to eleven families who had already paid more than money could measure; and the confession letter, delivered through Voss's sworn testimony, had done its work without ever showing my handwriting to the world. Edmund Halloran would stand trial. The inquiry would be reopened. And Zephyrine Underhill would remain what he had been for a decade: a disgraced guide, an anonymous boat-hand, a rumor on a fog-bound coast.

It was what I'd chosen. I held onto that as the morning wore on, because the feeling underneath kept trying to become something else—something that tasted, uncomfortably, like grief.

* * *

I went down to the harbor that afternoon, partly out of habit and partly because I couldn't stay away. The fleet was breaking anchor.

All three vessels of Halloran Marine sat low in the gray water, their salvage cranes folded like sleeping birds, their colors struck. Word had come down from the company's lawyers before it came down from any court: no more diving, no more claims, no presence in waters that were about to become very public and very litigated. Dree Voss's flagship—the *Kestrel*, someone had told me she'd renamed her years ago, though I'd never asked why—was first to raise her hook.

I stood at the end of the breakwater where the tourists didn't come, in my oilskins with my collar up, and watched.

Voss appeared on her stern rail as they warped out. She was too far to make out her expression, but I knew her stance by now—the feet planted wide, the hands loose, a woman reading water the way I once read mountains. She looked toward the breakwater. Toward me, or toward the place where I stood; at that distance there was no difference, and we had never needed one.

She raised one hand. Not a wave. Something older than a wave—an acknowledgment between people who have stood on opposite sides of a thing and come out the other side of it changed. I raised mine.

Then the *Kestrel* swung her bow to the open sea, and the fog took her the way the fog takes everything here: gradually, then completely.

I might have gone home after that. Instead I walked the length of the quay, and there, tied at the inner mooring where the fishing boats clustered thickest, sat a tender I knew. Fifteen feet of clinker-built cedar, gunwales scarred from a decade of hard service, a small outboard lashed under canvas against the rain. Painted on the transom, freshly done by someone who cared about letters: *HORIZON*.

There was no note. There didn't need to be. Voss had never once said a kind word to me that I could recall, and this was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in ten years. A boat means passage. A boat means you're expected to keep moving. A boat means *I know what you are, and it isn't nothing.*

I put my hand flat on the transom and left it there until the wood warmed.

* * *

Isla found me at dusk on the headland above the cave. Spring tides had come early that year, and the sea had already drawn its curtain across the black mouth beneath the cliffs; you'd never know anything was down there but dark water and legend. She stood looking at it anyway, hands in the pockets of her coat, hair whipping loose in the wind.

"You saw the paper," she said.

"Tam brought it up."

"And?"

"And Halloran's arrested. Eleven families will get their names in the record instead of a check in the post. The inquiry reopens next month." I turned to look at her. "And nobody knows it was us."

"That was the deal."

"That was the deal," I agreed.

We stood in silence while the light went out of the sky in long bands of copper and slate. Below us the sea worked at the cliff base, patient as it had always been, keeping its admission.

"I keep thinking about my father," Isla said at last. "Ten years I spent pulling his records apart, and I think somewhere in the middle of it I stopped grieving and started prosecuting. Like if I found the truth hard enough, fast enough, loudly enough, I'd get him back." She shook her head. "You got the ledgers out and gave them away. I'd have published every page. I'd have burned the world down to clear your name, Zephyrine, and you just—" She gestured at the sea, at the town, at all of it. "Handed it over. Quietly."

"It wasn't quiet," I said. "It cost."

"I know." Her voice gentled. "I watched it cost. That's what I can't stop thinking about. You argued for yourself once—one time, at the cliffs, with the safe open between us—and then you chose them anyway. My father would have done the same. I know that now. I hated knowing it, for about a week."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to write to the families. Not about the payments—they'll keep those, and they'll never learn where they came from. Just letters. Telling them the truth about how their people died, that it wasn't weather and it wasn't chance, and that someone finally answered for it." She looked at me sideways. "They'll ask who. Everyone will ask who."

"Let them ask."

"You're impossible."

"I'm a boat-hand," I said. "I have a reputation to maintain."

She laughed—short and surprised, the way she laughed maybe three times a season—and then it caught in her throat and became something else, and she turned her face to the sea so I wouldn't see it, which was considerate of her, and transparent, which was very Isla.

"The tender," she said after a while. "Voss's tender. I heard."

"HORIZON," I said. "She has a sense of humor after all."

"She has a sense of *debt*," Isla corrected. "There's a difference. Come on. Tam says the Anchor's doing mussels tonight and the harbormaster is buying, out of terror and gratitude in equal parts, and I intend to eat until he regrets both."

She started down the path. I lingered a moment longer, looking at the place where the cave mouth slept under the swell, and thought about tides—how they go out and how they come back, how nothing the sea takes stays taken forever, and how a current, given time, will carry even the heaviest thing toward the horizon.

Then I followed her down, toward the lights of the town, toward mussels and a boy named Tam and a woman who had every reason to hate me and had chosen otherwise, and the wind at my back pushed gently, the way currents do, as if the whole coast had decided at last to let me go.

Below, unseen, the sea closed over the Meridian Star, and kept what it keeps, and gave back what it gives.
