## 1. The Debt of Optimists

The cell went up over the eastern flats at 2:14 in the afternoon, and by 2:20 I had already done the arithmetic that would ruin my evening.

That's the thing about chasing for money instead of love: you stop watching the sky like a lover and start watching it like a ledger. A storm is just a line item with a rotation. Every minute it holds together is a minute the Calloway syndicate's sensors don't have to guess at, and every verified reading I feed their network pays down a number that has been sitting on my chest for three years like a sleeping animal I'm afraid to wake.

I did the math the way I always do, one hand on the wheel, one eye on the mesocyclone blooming out of the haze like a bruise in reverse. Fuel to intercept: forty kilometers of dirt road and whatever the flats did to my suspension, call it sixty credits. Time on station before the cell either roped out or ate itself: maybe ninety minutes if the shear held. Bounty per confirmed sensor packet with ground-truth verification: two hundred and ten. Which meant today was worth, at absolute best, four hundred credits against a balance of sixty-one thousand.

Sixty-one thousand minus four hundred is still sixty-one thousand, if you squint. That's what compound interest does to optimism. It doesn't kill it. It just makes it do laps.

"Come on, sweetheart," I told the storm, because talking to weather is free and therapy isn't. "Hold your shape."

She held. Of course she held. The flats east of Kettering Junction in late dry season are the most reliable storm factory in the territory — hot air off the scrub, moisture bleeding up from the coast two hundred klicks south, and nothing but dead-level ground for the convection to stand on while it decides what kind of monster it wants to be. This one wanted to be the pretty kind: a barber-pole updraft, crisp anvil, a tail cloud crawling in from the northeast like something sniffing for a door left open.

I've chased storms since I was nineteen years old, which means I've spent more hours under supercells than under roofs. People think storm chasers are adrenaline junkies. Some are. The good ones are accountants. We count seconds between flashes, kilometers between us and the hook, liters of fuel between us and the next town with a pump that works. The sky rewards precision and punishes romance, and I learned that lesson at a price I'm still paying off. Literally. Sixty-one thousand credits' worth.

The road out to the flats is called the Meridian Cut, though there's no meridian anywhere near it and the only thing it ever cut was the tires of people who drove it too fast after rain. Dry season meant it was hardpan and washboard, and I took it at speed anyway because the cell was building fast and the syndicate's contract paid double for first-verified data inside the first hour of tornadogenesis. Calloway Weather Services, the invoice said. As if a syndicate that moved guns and opium through half the coastal territories needed a weather division for anything besides laundering its conscience.

They'd bought my debt off a bank in Vellport two years ago, along with a few hundred other accounts, and they'd discovered quickly that a woman who can read a sky can also be read like an instrument. Fly the truck where we tell you. Verify what our satellites can't see. Don't ask what the data's for. In exchange: manageable payments, generous hazard bonuses, and the permanent low hum of knowing that the people who own your paper could fold it into a plane and sail it into your throat.

Four hundred credits today. Maybe five-fifty if the tornado was photogenic enough for their media arm. Eleven thousand short of freedom, no matter how many beautiful monsters I fed them.

I know what you're thinking, because everyone thinks it: take a real job. Sell the truck. Walk away. But the truck is the only asset I own outright, and a real job pays thirty credits a day, and thirty credits a day against sixty-one thousand is not a plan, it's a eulogy with extra steps. So you chase. You chase until the chasing is the only thing you're good at, and then you discover that being good at the only thing you're good at is its own kind of trap, and you keep driving, because the alternative is stopping, and stopped is how they catch you.

* * *

By three o'clock the cell had organized enough to make the radio worth listening to, and by quarter past it had made the road worth respecting.

The wall cloud dropped out of the base like a jaw unhinging, ragged and turning, and the inflow came across the flats so strong it stood the scrub grass flat and pushed the truck sideways a half-meter when I slowed to look. Dust rose off the ground in a beaded curtain, feeding up into the circulation, and somewhere in all that gray mess a funnel was deciding whether to be born. My hands were doing their work without me — throttle, wheel, eyes flicking between the mirror and the sky — and some detached part of me noted, as it always does, that this was the only time I felt competent anymore. Behind the wheel of a truck pointed at a killer, alone, I was exactly as good as I believed I was. Everywhere else, the numbers disagreed.

I parked on high ground off the cut, nose angled for a fast exit, and got the sensor array running. Ground-truth verification meant standing outside with a handheld unit while the satellite pass overhead matched my readings to theirs. Two minutes of exposure. The syndicate didn't pay for bravery; they paid for confirmation. Bravery was just what the confirmation cost.

The wind hit me like a crowd shoving through a door. Sand stung my forearms. Above me the whole sky was rotating now, slow and enormous, the way glaciers move or empires fall — too big to see happening, undeniable a minute later. The handheld chirped. Pressure falling like a stone down a well. I keyed the readings back to the network and watched the little progress bar crawl, and thought, not for the first time: four hundred credits. Four hundred credits for standing under the mouth of God.

Then the light changed.

Chasers talk about the green. Everyone talks about the green. What nobody warns you about is the white — the half-second before a strike when the world goes overexposed and silent, like the sky drawing breath. I got the white at 3:31 and fourteen seconds, and I did the only correct thing, which was nothing, because there is nothing to do. You cannot outrun a decision the atmosphere has already made. You can only be somewhere else when it makes the next one.

The bolt came down close enough that I heard it arrive before I heard the thunder — a crack like the world's largest whip taking the world's largest hide, and then the pressure wave slapped me flat against the truck's flank and the smell of ozone flooded everything, sharp and chemical, the smell of the air being briefly wrong. The hair on my arms lifted and lay down again. Somewhere behind me, where I'd been standing ninety seconds earlier, the hardpan had a new scar in it, forked and black, steaming faintly in the rain that had just begun to fall.

I don't remember getting into the cab. That's the honest truth. One moment I was pressed against the door frame with my ears ringing like struck bells, and the next I was inside with the engine running and both hands strangling the wheel, shaking so hard the gearshift chattered in its slot. Rain hammered the roof. On the dash screen, the network pinged: PACKET VERIFIED. BOUNTY PENDING. 210 CR.

Two hundred and ten credits. I sat in the truck and laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, while the storm walked away across the flats trailing its skirts of rain, indifferent as a queen.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about nearly dying: it's boring afterward. The body handles the emergency with perfect professionalism and then presents you with the bill later, itemized, in tremors. I sat in that truck for twenty minutes while the cell collapsed into rain and the flats turned the color of wet slate, and I shook, and I ran the numbers again because the numbers were the only thing in the world that would hold still.

Sixty-one thousand, two hundred and forty. That was the principal as of this morning's statement. Interest accrued daily at a rate I'd stopped reading because reading it felt like picking at a wound. Today's haul, once the bounty cleared: six hundred and thirty, counting the bonus packet from the second sensor pass. New balance: sixty thousand, six hundred and ten.

Eleven thousand short. That was the figure I carried everywhere, folded small, like a photograph of someone I owed money to. Eleven thousand and I was free — free meaning no longer property of the Calloway syndicate, free meaning able to point the truck in any direction including away, free meaning the word might mean something again. Eleven thousand was four more months of this. Four more months of standing under the mouth of God for two hundred and ten credits a packet, assuming the sky kept cooperating and my luck kept holding, and today my luck had held by approximately ninety seconds and fourteen meters.

I did that math too. Fourteen meters. The strike had come down fourteen meters from where I'd been standing. I knew because I paced it afterward, in the rain, like an idiot, because a chaser measures things, it's the disease, we measure everything, distance and pressure and debt and the exact span between ourselves and the thing that almost killed us. Fourteen meters. If I'd fumbled the handheld case for another ten seconds —

I didn't finish the sentence. You learn not to finish those sentences. Finishing them is how chasers end up selling insurance in Vellport, flinching at summer thunder.

The rain thinned. Out west, where the sun was going down behind the last ragged edge of the anvil, the light came through gold and green and gorgeous, the way it always does after a cell dies, the sky showing off its forgiveness like it never meant any of it. I got out and checked the scar in the hardpan. Forked, black, already cooling. Fourteen meters, give or take a pace.

"You missed," I told the sky.

The sky declined to comment. It usually does. That's the arrangement.

* * *

I drove back toward Kettering Junction with the windows cracked and the wet wind coming through, smelling of creosote and dust and that lingering electrical sharpness. The fuel depot on the edge of town glowed in the dusk like the last lit window in a dark house, and I pulled in mostly because the tank was low and partly because I wanted to be around another human being and would rather have died than admit it, even to myself. Especially to myself.

The depot man — old Ferro, missing two fingers and most of his curiosity — rang me up without looking at the sky once, which I've always admired about him. People who live out here treat weather like a neighbor they don't borrow sugar from. Acknowledge it, don't engage it.

"Cell go past you?" he asked.

"Went past everybody," I said. "That's generally the ambition."

He grunted, which from Ferro is a full conversation. I was counting out credits for fuel — actual physical credits, because the syndicate's account draws scrutiny at depots and I liked keeping at least one transaction a week that wasn't theirs — when I noticed the other vehicle.

It was parked at the far end of the apron, an expedition rig, the heavy kind with roof racks and jerry cans and the particular battered patience of something that has crossed a lot of empty country. A man stood beside it, leaning on the hood like the hood was holding him up. Old, gaunt, sun-cured to the color of saddle leather, with the stillness of somebody conserving something he didn't have much of left. He was watching me with an attention that had weight to it, the way some men watch weather and some men watch people, and he was doing the second thing.

I've been looked at by a lot of men in a lot of depots. Most of them look at the truck first and the woman second, and both looks are appraisals. This one wasn't. This one looked at me like I was a door he'd been walking toward for a long way, and I felt it on my spine before I consciously saw him at all.

"Friend of yours?" Ferro asked.

"No," I said, and handed him the credits, and told myself the prickle at the back of my neck was residual static from the lightning, nothing else, just the day's electricity draining out of me slow.

But the old man pushed off the hood and started across the apron toward me, unhurried, deliberate, each step placed like he was crossing a river on stones, and I stood there with the fuel nozzle in my hand and the receipt curling damp in my pocket — six hundred and thirty credits richer than yesterday, eleven thousand short of free, fourteen meters from a story I'd never tell anyone — and I thought, with the tired clarity that comes after the shaking stops:

Whatever this is, I can't afford it.

He stopped an arm's length away, tipped his head at the dying light and the wet black road going east, and said, "You're the one who chases them."
