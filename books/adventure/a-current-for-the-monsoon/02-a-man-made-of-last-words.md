## 2. A Man Made of Last Words

The fuel depot at Kilometer 114 was the kind of place that existed only because two roads crossed there and nobody had gotten around to fixing either one. A rusted canopy, four pumps of which two worked, a cinder-block office with a fan that turned like it was apologizing for itself. I'd stopped there maybe forty times over six years, and every time it looked a little more like something the country was slowly digesting.

I was topping off the tank and doing arithmetic I didn't want to do when he came out of the shade on the far side of the building.

I noticed him the way you notice weather. Old man, walking with the deliberate care of someone whose body has become a negotiation. Canvas hat gone soft as cloth from years of sweat and sun. He moved along the wall with one hand trailing the concrete, like he needed the building to vouch for him.

I went back to my numbers. Eleven thousand, three hundred and some change. That was the distance between me and a life where Calloway's name wasn't stapled to mine. The storm cell I'd chased the day before had paid out nine hundred after fuel, after the tracker lease, after the cut the syndicate took for the privilege of owing them money. Nine hundred against eleven thousand. At that rate I'd be free sometime around the heat death of the sun.

"You're the storm woman," he said.

I looked up. He'd come around the truck without me hearing him, which should have worried me more than it did. Up close he was worse than the silhouette suggested—skin like paper left out in rain, eyes sunk back in his skull but bright, terribly bright, the way lamps are bright right before the generator dies.

"Depends who's asking."

"Elias Wren." He said it like it should mean something. It didn't, or I pretended it didn't, and something flickered across his face that told me he'd watched that pretense before. "You chase cells across the flats. Solo. Nobody else is stupid enough to run the northern basin alone this time of year."

"Flattery will get you nowhere."

"It's not flattery. It's reconnaissance." He put his hand flat on my fender, steadying himself or claiming territory, hard to say. "I need a driver."

"Fuel's inside. So's a bus schedule."

"I don't need a bus. I need someone who can read sky." His voice had a rasp in it, like a file drawn slow across wood. "Dry season ends in five weeks. Maybe less, this year. I need to be somewhere before the first rain closes the roads, and I can't drive myself anymore."

"I'm not a taxi."

"No," he said, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him because it was patient. "You're a woman eleven thousand short of getting out from under Calloway. And I'm a man offering to pay cash."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about debt: it doesn't make you reckless. It makes you *available*. There's a difference. Reckless people choose their risks. Debt just stands behind you at every crossroads, breathing on your neck, whispering that any door is better than standing still.

I walked him into the shade of the canopy and bought him a warm soda I couldn't afford, because some habits of decency survive even when the reasons for them don't. He drank half of it and set it down with the care of a man conserving everything now, including gestures.

"How do you know my number?" I asked.

"Calloway keeps books. Books have margins. Margins have notes." He tapped his temple. "I spent thirty years prospecting ground they wanted. You learn where the bodies are filed."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." He reached inside his jacket—it took him a while; his arm didn't want to cooperate—and brought out an envelope soft with handling. From the envelope, a sheet of paper, folded twice, worn along the creases like cloth.

It was a map. Hand-drawn, ink faded to the brown of old blood, on paper that had been folded and unfolded so many times the fibers were breaking. But whoever drew it had drawn it well. Contours, washes, a rail spur marked in dashed line, bearings written small and precise in a hand that had clearly done this for a living once. In the upper corner, coordinates. In the lower corner, a small X above a dry wash, and beneath it, in the same careful script: *the place.*

"What am I looking at?"

"A promise," Wren said. "Mine to you, if you make yours to me."

"I don't make promises to strangers with maps."

"Then let's not be strangers." He settled back against the pump housing like a man planning to be there a while. "Seven years ago I walked out of the northern basin carrying nothing but this map and a debt of my own. I've been meaning to go back ever since. Meaning is cheap. Bodies are expensive. Now mine's called in the loan." He said it flatly, without drama, the way men report weather. "The doctors at the mission gave me a season. The dry season, if I'm lucky. They were very precise about that, mission doctors. They like their ironies."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Be useful." He tapped the map. "Take me there before the rains. That's all. One trip, one old man, one place. You name a price."

"And what's there?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the fan in the office window turned its apologetic circles. Somewhere down the road a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, or at the particular nothing that dogs find at the edge of towns like this.

"Ground," he said finally. "Ground I buried something in seven years ago, and I mean to dig it up before I'm buried myself. You don't need to know more than that."

"Everybody says that. Nobody means it."

"I mean it the way dying men mean things, Ms. Iversen. Which is to say: completely, and too late to matter."

I should have said no then. Cleanly, quickly, the way you decline a drink at ten in the morning. Instead I heard myself ask the question that has ruined better women than me:

"How much?"

* * *

He named a figure that made my ears ring. Not enough to clear the whole debt—but enough to cut it by more than half, enough to change the arithmetic from impossible to merely brutal. Cash, paid to Calloway directly, with a cut to me for expenses. He had the money. Prospecting, he said, though he didn't smile when he said it, and I've learned that the truth usually travels without a smile.

"One condition," I said. "No questions. I drive, I keep us alive, I don't ask what you buried and you don't ask why I owe what I owe. We're two strangers sharing a cab."

Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise exactly. Recognition. Like I'd offered him a courtesy he'd already budgeted for.

"Agreed," he said. Too fast. Too easily. The word came out of him smooth as water off waxed canvas, and somewhere in the back of my head a small voice noted that men who agree too easily are either honest or rehearsed, and the second kind are much more common.

But the small voice was tired, and the number was large, and the dry season was burning down like a fuse.

"I'll need a day," I said. "To check the truck, check the routes."

"Take two." He began folding the map back into its creases, and his hands shook, and I saw how much of his remaining strength the simple act cost him. "I'll be here. I don't go far these days." He paused, envelope halfway into his jacket, and looked at me with those lamp-dying eyes. "One more thing, Ms. Iversen. If a young woman comes asking—a cartographer, dark hair, my jaw on her face—you haven't seen me."

"Why?"

"Because she'll ask questions you've promised not to answer." He pushed off the pump housing and stood, swaying slightly, a tree deciding against falling. "And because some answers are heavier than the people who carry them. I wouldn't wish mine on her. Or on you."

He walked away along the wall, hand trailing the concrete, and the building kept vouching for him until the corner took him.

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat in the cab of my truck at the depot's edge with the windows cracked and the map open on the passenger seat under a flashlight, telling myself I was studying the route.

The route was good. That was the problem. Whoever drew it knew the northern basin the way I knew the backs of my own hands—the low crossing at the basalt shelf, the spring that runs even in drought, the stretch of rail spur high enough to beat the flash floods. This was not a treasure map. Treasure maps are drawn by hopeful men. This was drawn by a surveyor, someone who measured the world because measuring it was how he loved it, and that frightened me more than any X could have.

Because here's the fine print, the part I read only after midnight, only after the flashlight had died and I was sitting in the dark doing the thing I do instead of sleeping:

Nobody pays that kind of money to retrieve ground.

Prospectors bury gold. Dying men bury other things. And a man who walks out of the basin carrying a map and a "debt of his own"—his words, chosen with a precision that should have warned me—who pays half my ransom to be carried back to the scene of whatever he owed it on? That's not a job. That's a confession looking for a road.

I thought about calling it off. I actually got as far as composing the sentence—*Mr. Wren, I've reconsidered*—and imagining his face receiving it, patient, unsurprised, the face of a man who had already accounted for my conscience the way he'd accounted for my debts.

Then I did my own accounting. Eleven thousand, three hundred and change. The interest that accrued while I slept, a tax on my existence levied by men in offices I'd never see. The bounty chases getting thinner every season as the syndicate squeezed the independents out. The math that never changed no matter how many storms I chased, because that was the design, that was the whole elegant design: you could run forever, but you could never arrive.

Wren's offer wasn't charity. I knew that even then, sitting in the dark. Charity would have been small and deniable. This was a purchase—he was buying a driver, yes, but also a witness, or a shield, or a pair of hands that hadn't yet decided what they were willing to carry. Temptation had arrived dressed as charity, and I was poor enough to open the door, and we both knew it, and he had been polite enough not to say it aloud.

That was the worst part. Not the money. The politeness. The way he'd folded the map, the way he'd named his price like a gentleman, the way he'd said *you name a price* and then quietly named it himself. A man made of last words, spending them carefully, and every one of them a hook dressed as a favor.

Dawn came up gray and hot over the flats. The depot fan resumed its apology. I started the truck because starting things is easier than deciding about them, and drove the two hundred meters back to the canopy, where Elias Wren sat waiting on an overturned crate with his bag between his feet, as if he'd known—of course he'd known—that I'd come back before I did.

"Well?" he said.

I looked at the old man and the bag and the map in my jacket pocket, heavy as a stone, light as a bill.

"Get in," I said. "Before I change my mind."

He smiled that patient smile, and behind us, far off across the flats, a column of dust stood up straight in the still air, like something coming, though nothing was visible yet, and nothing, I told myself, would be.
