## 3. Terms Measured in Rain

I slept badly, which was standard, but I slept badly *productively*, which was new.

The map stayed on the passenger seat all night where I'd left it, folded into its eighths like a letter nobody wanted to open in public. Every time the depot lights buzzed and shifted, the creases caught them and threw a little pale line across the truck's ceiling, so that even with my eyes closed I could feel it up there, patient as a creditor.

Around three in the morning I gave up on sleep and read it properly by dome light. It was good work, actually. That annoyed me. The linework was confident, the elevations penciled in a surveyor's shorthand I half remembered from a year I'd spent running supplies for a geological outfit before I learned that geology pays worse than weather. Whoever drew this had spent real time in the monsoon country — you could tell by the way he'd marked water. Amateurs mark roads. People who've survived out there mark water first, roads second, and everything else not at all.

There was a name in the corner, small: *E.W., 7th Survey Co.* And a date seven years old. And beneath it, in fresher ink, a single sentence that had nothing to do with cartography:

*For whoever is desperate enough.*

"Well," I said to the empty cab, "flattery will get you everywhere."

* * *

Elias Wren was at the fuel depot by eight, sitting on the same bench as if he'd never left it, as if benches were where men like him went to photosynthesize. He'd shaved. Badly. There was a stripe of blood dried under his jaw where the razor had lost an argument, and his shirt had been buttoned one hole off, so the whole man sat slightly askew, like a picture hung in a hurry.

"You read it," he said. Not a question.

"I looked at it."

"Same thing, for a woman who slept in her truck." He patted the bench beside him. I stayed standing. He nodded like I'd passed something. "Terms, then."

"Terms," I agreed. "You talk first."

He talked. I'll give him this: he didn't waste my time with biography. No dying-man speech, no trembling hand held up to the light. He laid it out flat, the way you'd lay out tools before a job.

"I pay your syndicate what you owe. Directly, through a lawyer in Corvane, so there's no trail through me. Eleven thousand, four hundred, whatever the number is when they finish adding their interest."

"Twelve even, by now."

"Twelve even." He said it without blinking, which told me either he had the money or he was a very good liar, and I was starting to suspect both. "You keep a cut of whatever we find. Ten percent."

"Fifteen."

"Ten," he said, "because whatever we find, you're going to want to be rid of fast, and fast costs money. Fifteen makes you greedy. Greedy makes you slow."

I hated him a little for that, mostly because he was right. "Ten. Plus expenses. Fuel, food, repairs, all of it comes off the top before your cut or mine."

"Done."

"And—" I held up a finger. This was the part I'd rehearsed at three in the morning, staring at the ceiling while the map threw its pale line around the cab. "No questions. You don't ask about my debts, my history, why a storm chaser owes Calloway money. I don't ask why a prospector with a bad heart wants to go back to country he marked seven years ago and never once went back to. We drive, we dig, we split, we're done."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. There was something moving behind his face, some calculation or maybe just tiredness settling deeper, and then he said the words I should have heard as a warning siren and instead heard as a door opening:

"Agreed. No questions."

Too easy. Even then, some animal part of me knew it was too easy. Nobody agrees to *no questions* that fast unless the questions are worse than the silence. But I was standing in the dry-season sun with twelve thousand dollars of rope around my neck, and a man was offering to pay a lawyer to cut it, and the animal part of me was small and easily outvoted.

"One more thing," Wren said. He reached into his jacket and produced a paper, folded once, and a pen. "In writing. For both our protections."

"A contract."

"A contract." He almost smiled. "I spent thirty years watching men shake hands over claims and then shoot each other over the interpretation. Paper's kinder."

I took the paper. It was short, which I liked; contracts that run long are hiding something, but contracts that run short are usually just honest. Payment of debt via counsel in Corvane. Ten percent of recovered value. Expenses deducted from gross. Duration: until the end of the dry season, no extension. And then the clause, right there in the middle, plain as a fence post:

*Neither party shall inquire into the past dealings, obligations, or motives of the other, nor disclose the terms or existence of this agreement to any third party without mutual consent.*

"There it is," I said. "No questions, in writing."

"You asked for it."

"I did." I turned the page over, found nothing on the back, turned it again. "And if I break it?"

"Then the deal breaks," Wren said simply. "And you're back where you started, except poorer in time, and time's the one thing the season won't sell back to you."

I should have asked him then. Right there, pen uncapped, I should have said: *Why does the duration matter? Why the dry season? What happens when the rain comes?* Three questions. That's all it would have taken, and every one of them was permitted under the clause because none of them were about his past — they were about the future, and the future, technically, belonged to both of us.

But asking questions meant wanting answers, and wanting answers meant admitting the whole arrangement was strange, and admitting it was strange meant risking the only exit I'd been offered in two years. So I did what desperate people do, which is mistake speed for safety.

I signed.

My signature came out steadier than I expected. Wren countersigned beneath it, his hand shaking just enough to make the letters double-struck, and I watched that tremor and felt the familiar cold arithmetic settle in my chest: this man might genuinely die out there, and I would be the one holding the shovel, the wheel, or the bag, depending on how it went.

"The lawyer's name is Ferro," Wren said, folding his copy away. "She'll contact your syndicate today. By tonight, Mr.—"

"Calloway's people don't use names with me. They use numbers."

"By tonight, your number will be zero." He stood up off the bench in stages, like a crane lifting something too heavy for it. "We leave tomorrow. Early. Before the heat."

"Tomorrow?" I blinked. "I need to provision. Route-plan. The southern tracks are—"

"You have today," he said. "I've had seven years. I'm not spending another night in this town."

He walked off toward the road, that slightly crooked, carefully buttoned man, and I sat down on the warm bench where he'd been and put my face in my hands and laughed, once, without much humor in it.

Twelve thousand dollars. Gone. Just like that. Two years of bounties and storm-chase contracts and selling data to insurance adjusters who paid late and argued always — erased by a stranger's fountain pen in the space of a morning.

Any sensible person would have asked what it cost.

* * *

I spent the day provisioning, and I want to be honest about the mood I did it in, because the mood was the trap.

Relief is a drug, and I was high on it. Every case of water I loaded into the truck bed, I loaded lighter. Every fuel can, every spare belt, every tin of the awful compressed biscuits that taste like sweetened chalk — I stacked them humming. When the parts shop charged me triple for a radiator hose because the owner could see I needed it today, I paid it grinning. Twelve thousand dollars of weight, gone off my shoulders. I'd have paid triple for anything that day.

That's the thing about relief nobody tells you: it doesn't just lift the weight. It lifts your judgment along with it. A person running from a fire will run straight into a river without checking how deep it is, and she'll call it luck when she doesn't drown, and she'll do it again tomorrow.

By late afternoon I'd planned the route twice — once conservatively, once honestly — and the honest version ran through flood country the whole way, following the dry washes north while they were still dry, racing the season. Doable. Tight. The kind of route that only works if nothing goes wrong, which is the kind of route I specialized in, because my life had been that route for years.

I was rechecking the tie-downs at dusk when the radio crackled.

"Number nine." The voice was smooth as poured concrete. Okafor. Of course it was Okafor; the syndicate sent Okafor whenever money moved in amounts that made other enforcers nervous. "You've been busy today. Ferro, Corvane. Interesting firm. Very discreet."

"Morning paperwork," I said. "Debt settlement. You should be happy."

"Oh, I'm delighted. Delighted." A pause with texture in it, the sound of a man smiling at nothing. "It's just that settlements usually come *from* you, number nine, not *to* you. Money flowing uphill. Toward dead country, no less — Ferro's office files out of the northern circuit. Curious routing."

"My business."

"It is," he agreed pleasantly. "Your business. That's what makes it interesting. Business is all we ever look at." Another pause, longer. Somewhere behind his voice I could hear a toll gate clanking, trucks idling. "Drive safe, number nine. The season's turning. Roads get unreliable. Men on unreliable roads sometimes need... assistance. We're very helpful."

He signed off without saying goodbye. I stood in the dark with the handset in my hand and told myself it was routine — that Okafor talked like that to everyone, that curiosity wasn't suspicion, that a settled debt was a settled debt no matter whose lawyer moved it.

I believed myself for about four minutes, which was long enough to finish the tie-downs.

* * *

I didn't sleep at the depot. I drove six miles out and camped in a culvert cut, engine off, doors locked, and lay in the cab watching the stars do their indifferent rotation and thinking about the clause.

*Neither party shall inquire.* Fine. Symmetrical. Fair, even.

*Nor disclose the terms or existence of this agreement to any third party without mutual consent.*

I turned that one over for a long time. Because here was the shape of it, once you looked: if anyone asked me why I was heading north with a dying prospector, I couldn't say. If the syndicate asked — and Okafor had just asked, in his way — I couldn't say. If a magistrate asked, if a doctor asked, if Nadia—

Where had that name come from? I hadn't thought about the daughter yet. I made myself think about her now, lying in the dark: somewhere out there was a woman Elias Wren had walked away from seven years ago, and women like that had a way of showing up at fuel depots and supply towns exactly when they weren't wanted. If she showed up, and she asked me what I was doing with her father, I would have to lie to her face. That was in the contract. Mutual consent or nothing.

"No questions," I said aloud to the windshield. "Both directions."

It sounded less like protection out here, in the culvert dark, than it had on the bench. On the bench it had sounded like a wall between me and his secrets. Out here it sounded like a wall between me and everyone — and I noticed, with the particular clarity that arrives around two in the morning, that walls don't care which side of them you're standing on.

The map was still in the door pocket. I didn't take it out. I knew what was on it: contour lines, washes, water marks, a collapsed something-or-other in the high country north of the Serpentine flats. What I didn't know was what had happened there seven years ago, and I had signed away my right to find out, and the man who knew was paying me handsomely to stay ignorant.

Best deal of my life. I fell asleep telling myself that, and if the telling took longer than usual, well. The stars were pretty.

* * *

We rolled out at first light, Wren in the passenger seat with his bag on his knees and his eyes already on the north, and the last thing he said before the town fell away behind us was cheerful, almost boyish, and it stayed with me the way a splinter stays:

"Good weather for driving," he said, squinting at the cloudless sky. "Enjoy it. It's the last honest sky we'll see for a while."

I laughed, because it sounded like old-prospector poetry, the kind of thing men say to hear themselves say it.

It wasn't until much later — after the green throat of the country had swallowed us, after the river, after the fire, after I'd learned exactly what *no questions* was built to protect — that I understood he hadn't been talking about the monsoon at all.

He'd been talking about me.
