## 4. The Cartographer's Daughter

I learned to read maps before I learned to read words, which explains a great deal about how I've lived my life. Maps are honest in a way people aren't. A map will tell you the bridge is out. A map will never look at you across a fuel depot table and pretend the last seven years didn't happen.

So when I wanted to find my father, I didn't ask around. I mapped him.

It wasn't hard. Elias Wren had three habits: he bought diesel at depots that still pumped by hand, he paid cash for anything under fifty, and he always, always traveled toward old survey lines like a moth flying backward into the flame that had already burned him once. Cross-reference the depot ledgers — cartographers have friends in unglamorous places — and you get a dotted line moving northeast toward the monsoon country, one truck, two men, leaving on the last week of the dry season because of course he was. My father had spent seventy-one years treating weather forecasts as personal challenges.

The last supply town before the green country was called Meridian Flats, which was optimistic naming if I'd ever seen it. One street, two bars, a general store whose shelves held more rope than food, and a fuel depot where the owner knew me by reputation if not by name. I arrived two days ahead of them, rented a room above the store, and did what any reasonable estranged daughter would do.

I waited. And I planned what I would say.

That's the thing about me and my father — we're both preparers. He surveyed every route twice before driving it. I draft conversations like legal documents. In my head, over those two days, I said everything. I asked him about Mother's letters. I asked him why he burned them. I asked him why a man who claimed to love his family had answered grief by becoming a locked room. I rehearsed until the words wore smooth as river stones.

Then the truck came up the street at six in the morning, trailing dust, and every word I'd prepared evaporated like it had never existed.

* * *

There were three of them. My father in the passenger seat, looking like a man assembled from spare parts and stubbornness, thinner than the photograph I kept refusing to throw away. A hired driver I didn't know, big hands, bored eyes. And in the driver's seat — because the big man was riding cargo — a woman who stepped down from the cab with the particular economy of motion that told me she'd been in and out of vehicles in bad weather her whole life.

Beatrix Iversen. I'd done my homework. Storm chaser, syndicate-adjacent, the kind of name that showed up in bounty notices and nowhere else. The kind of woman who got hired when the job required someone willing to drive into weather that killed people for a living.

She looked at me the way you look at a washout on a road you were counting on.

"You're blocking the pump," she said.

"I'm blocking your expedition." I had my folder open on the hood of their truck before she could object — my own maps, my own annotations, seven years of knowing this country better than anyone alive. "You're heading into the upper basin in the last week of the dry season with no one who's actually been past the toll bridge since the rail line closed. Your route follows the old 7th Survey benchmarks, which means it follows terrain that hasn't been properly mapped since before the flood control works went in. You need me."

"We don't," Beatrix said.

"She's right, though," said the big man, leaning out of the cargo bed. "Those benchmarks are—"

"Nobody asked, Tomas."

My father hadn't moved. He stood on the far side of the truck with his hand resting on the doorframe, watching me the way you watch weather building on a horizon — assessing, measuring, deciding whether to run or stand.

"Hello, Dad," I said, because someone had to say it first, and I'd be damned if it cost me more than it cost him.

"Nadia." Just my name. Seven years compressed into two syllables, delivered flat as a contour line.

"I want a seat on the convoy."

"No," Beatrix said again, faster this time, and I realized she wasn't just refusing me — she was refusing the entire shape of what my presence meant. She'd signed on to drive a dying man somewhere private. I was a witness. Worse, I was family.

"It's not your convoy," I said. "It's his."

"He's paying. That makes it mine."

"That makes it yours?" I turned to my father. "Is that what money does now? Does it buy you a daughter-shaped hole in the passenger list?"

"Nadia—" my father started.

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't do the voice. Don't do the tired-old-man routine. I've had seven years to imagine this conversation and in none of the versions do you get to be quiet at me."

For a moment — one long, electric moment — something moved behind his face. Not guilt exactly. Something older and more geological than guilt, the way fault lines are older than earthquakes. Then it settled, and he was just an old man in a dusty shirt, and I hated how much that hurt.

Beatrix watched all of this with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. I recognized it, later, as the look of someone calculating whether a problem could be driven around or had to be driven through.

"There's a supply run at eight," she said finally, to no one in particular. "We leave at nine. This isn't a democracy."

* * *

I spent the hours between six and nine doing what I do best: making myself indispensable in writing.

By seven I'd left a copy of my basin annotations under the wiper of their truck. By eight I'd cornered Tomas at the store and walked him through the three places where the old survey line crossed channels that flash-flood without warning — dry gravel at dawn, waist-deep water by noon if there's rain in the high country, and there is always rain in the high country this time of year, even when the flats are baking. By half past eight I'd bought my own supplies, topped off my own vehicle, and parked it squarely behind theirs, so that leaving without me meant leaving without their rear axle's best friend.

Petty? Absolutely. Cartography attracts a certain personality type. We are people who believe the world can be fixed if only it's measured correctly, and we are frequently insufferable about it.

Beatrix found me at the depot counter at ten to nine, drinking coffee that tasted like it had been filtered through a boot.

"The annotations are good," she admitted, sitting down across from me without being invited. She had a way of occupying space that suggested she considered furniture a temporary arrangement. "Better than good. Where'd you learn the high country?"

"My mother surveyed it. Before the accident."

Something flickered across her face — recognition, maybe, of the word *accident* and all its freight. "Elias mentioned a survey background in the family."

"He mention which survey?"

"No questions asked," she said, and there it was — not smugness but something wearier, a woman reciting terms she'd agreed to and was already regretting. "That was the deal. His money, his silence, my driving."

"And you're a woman who keeps deals."

"I'm a woman who's alive because she keeps deals." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the counter, some private ritual. "Look. Nadia. I don't know what happened between you two and I genuinely don't want to know. But I've taken contracts with families in the back seat before, and here's what happens: day three, somebody starts crying. Day five, somebody starts lying. Day seven, I'm refereeing a divorce in a river crossing while trying not to drown. I don't referee. I drive."

"That's very tidy."

"Tidy keeps people breathing."

"And what does it keep out?"

She looked at me then — really looked, the way you check a gauge you suspect is lying to you — and for a second I thought I'd landed something. Then the shutter came down, smooth as a slide rule, and she was all business again.

"Nine o'clock," she said. "We're leaving. With or without your truck parked behind ours."

"With," I said. "Obviously. I'm coming."

"That's not—"

"Dad!" I called across the depot, loud enough to turn heads, because I am not above weaponizing a public. My father stood by the truck, and I watched him weigh me — the same weighing he'd done at dawn, the same arithmetic, except now the numbers had changed because I'd made them change. "Tell your driver I'm navigating or I'm following. Your call. But one of those options ends with me finding out where you're going anyway, and you know I will. You taught me the benchmarks yourself. Every campsite you ever loved, I can find blind."

He didn't answer immediately. He looked at Beatrix instead — and I want to be precise about this, because it mattered, though neither of us understood it yet. He didn't look at me and decide. He looked at Beatrix, and whatever passed between them was silent, brief, and completely illegible to me. A whole negotiation conducted in the space of a breath. Permission granted, or a warning issued, or a debt called in — I couldn't tell. Neither, from her expression, could she.

"Let her ride," my father said.

Beatrix exhaled through her nose. "Fine. Back seat. You carry your own weight, you don't touch the radio, and when I say stop, you stop."

"When you say stop, I'll tell you if stopping is a mistake," I said. "That's the deal. Take it or spend the next three weeks wondering what I'd have flagged."

Another look between them. This one longer. Whatever my father was saying to her with his eyes, it had layers, and I felt — sharply, uncomfortably — like a stranger reading a letter written in my own family's handwriting.

"Deal," Beatrix said, and stuck out her hand, and I shook it, and her grip was dry and strong and told me nothing except that she'd rather have been anywhere else.

* * *

We loaded at nine-fifteen. Tomas took the cargo bed with the drums and crates; I took the back seat of the cab with my satchel of maps wedged between my feet like ballast. My father climbed in beside me, and for the length of one terrible minute we sat thigh to thigh in a truck cab that smelled of diesel and old canvas, both of us staring forward, both of us pretending the other was scenery.

"You look like her," he said at last, to the windshield.

"Don't," I said. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. But definitely not yet."

He nodded slowly, accepting the terms, and I understood that this — this mutual treaty of postponement — was the most honest conversation we'd had since I left. Two cartographers agreeing to leave a region unmapped for now. Some territory you survey too soon and get everyone killed.

Beatrix started the engine. Through the rear window I watched Meridian Flats shrink behind us, the store, the bars, the boot-flavored coffee, all of it folding away into heat shimmer. Ahead, the paved road ran another forty kilometers and then simply stopped, the way roads do at the edge of the green country, giving up the way roads do, as if the land itself had filed an objection.

I unfolded my basin map across my knees. My father glanced at it. I angled it away — petty, yes, but I'd earned pettiness — and marked our position with a pencil dot.

Seven years of distance, I thought, and it turns out the shortest route between us runs straight through flash-flood country in the worst week of the year. Of course it does. Of course.

Beside me, my father took a battered journal from inside his jacket, checked that it was there the way other men check a wallet, and put it away again. He thought I didn't see. He thought the angle hid it.

I've been reading my father's habits since before I could read words.

I saw.

And forty kilometers ahead, where the pavement ended and the green throat of the monsoon country began, the first clouds of the season were stacking up over the high country like a debt coming due.
