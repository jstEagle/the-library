## 5. Into the Green Throat

The paved road ended at a cattle grid and a sign that had been shot so many times it read more like braille than language. Beyond it, the track unspooled into green country—acacia scrub giving way to taller things, the kind of vegetation that only exists where water has opinions.

"Officially," I said, killing the engine at the grid, "this is where I'd tell you the expedition is over. Unofficially, this is where the money starts."

Elias Wren sat in the passenger seat of my truck with his hands folded on the map case in his lap like a man attending his own funeral and pleased with the turnout. Behind us, Nadia's Land Rover idled, her face visible in the rearview as a pale smudge of disapproval. She'd been driving behind me for two days without speaking to either of us, which for a cartographer was practically a love letter.

"You've done this before," Elias said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've driven into places before." I got out and walked the edge of the track, reading the ground the way other people read weather reports. The soil was dark, heavy, holding last week's rain like a grudge. "Driving out is the part people skip in the brochures."

He smiled at that, or something adjacent to smiling—the movement of a man whose face had gotten rusty at it. Seven years ago, according to the fuel depot gossip I'd paid for in cheap coffee, Elias Wren had walked out of the monsoon country alone, half-dead, and rich-adjacent. He'd spent the years since buying rounds he never drank and staring at maps of country he refused to revisit. Now he was paying me a sum that made my accountant weep with gratitude to go back.

I didn't ask why. That had been the deal. No questions asked—a clause I'd signed with the relief of a drowning woman grabbing a rope, not noticing the rope was attached to an anchor.

Nadia's door slammed behind me. She came up the track with her surveyor's stride, a rolled chart under one arm like a baton she intended to use on someone.

"The road forks in four kilometers," she said. "The eastern branch follows the wash. Your map shows the western one."

"My map doesn't show anything," I said. "My map is a rumor with contour lines."

"It's wrong."

"It's *old*. There's a difference, and the difference is usually water." I nodded at the sky, where clouds were assembling themselves over the high country with the unhurried confidence of creditors. "That system's been building since dawn. When it lets go, every wash in this district becomes a river, and every river becomes a liar about where it used to be."

"So take the eastern fork."

"The eastern fork crosses the wash twice."

"Then we wait it out."

"We don't have time to wait it out." I said it flatly, and watched her hear what I didn't say: her father's clock was running. The dry season ends when it ends, and Elias Wren's doctors had given him until roughly the same date, as if the sky and his body had coordinated.

Nadia looked past me at the truck, at her father's small silhouette behind the glass. Something moved through her face—grief, anger, and the specific exhaustion of loving someone who communicates exclusively in silences.

"Fine," she said. "Western fork. But when your rumor of a road turns out to be a swamp, I'm saying I told you so in three projections."

"Cartographers," I said, climbing back into the cab. "They always want the world to hold still."

She heard that. I meant her to. It was crueler than it needed to be, and I filed the cruelty away under *things Beatrix does instead of being afraid*, a folder that was getting thick enough to need its own filing cabinet.

* * *

The western fork held for six hours, which in my experience is exactly long enough to make you trust it.

We climbed through country that kept changing its mind—savanna, then thornveld, then stands of fever trees whose bark glowed yellow-green in the truck's headlights like something radioactive and beautiful. The air thickened. Insects hit the windscreen in soft suicides. Behind me, Nadia's headlights swung patiently through my dust, and ahead of me the track did that thing dirt tracks do in monsoon country: it narrowed, softened, and started making suggestions rather than promises.

At four in the afternoon, the sky finally cashed the check it had been writing all day.

It didn't start so much as arrive—one moment the light was bruised gold, the next the world was white noise and water, rain coming down so hard the wipers became decorative. I dropped to second gear and felt for the track through the wheel, the way you feel for a handrail on stairs in the dark.

"Beatrix." Elias's voice from the passenger seat. Calm. Too calm. "The wash."

I saw it a half-second later: a brown seam across the track ahead where there should have been none, water moving with that smooth, muscular indifference that flash floods have—no rapids, no drama, just volume and intent. A river that hadn't existed at breakfast.

I stopped with the front wheels a meter from the edge and sat there while the rain drummed the roof like applause from an audience that wanted more.

"That wasn't on the map," Elias said.

"Nothing ever is." I got out into the downpour, because some measurements you can't take from inside glass.

The flood was maybe forty meters across and moving fast—not torrent-fast, but freight-train-slow, the kind of current that doesn't look dangerous right up until it takes your legs. Depth unknown. The far bank was invisible; the rain had erased the world past thirty meters. Somewhere under that brown water was the track, and somewhere along the track was the low point where the wash crossed it, and the question—the whole expensive, stupid, beautiful question—was whether the crossing was knee-deep gravel or chest-deep silt.

Behind me, doors opened. Nadia came around the truck with a measuring line already in her hands, because of course she had a measuring line, because of course the daughter of Elias Wren would carry instruments into a flood.

"Don't," I said.

"It's my job."

"Your job is to draw places after they've stopped trying to kill you."

"And yours?" She tied off the line and waded in to her knees before I could stop her, feeding the weighted end downstream, reading the current against her own shins with her head bent against the rain. I stood on the bank hating her—for the recklessness, for the competence, for the fact that watching her do it did something to my chest I declined to examine—and then she turned and shouted over the water:

"Waist-deep at the channel! Gravel bottom, but it's *moving*! If we cross, we cross now, before this rises!"

I looked upstream. The rain on the high country had hours left in it. Every minute we debated, the river got a vote.

"Back!" I shouted. "Everyone back!"

She fought her way to the bank, streaming, furious, alive. "There's a window—"

"There's a window until there isn't, and then there's a widow." I was already moving, throwing tarps over the cargo, my hands doing the arithmetic of weight and buoyancy while my mouth ran the briefing. "We lighten everything. Fuel cans lashed to the roof rack—they float if we lose them. Winch cable to the highest tree on the far side if we can get one anchored. We cross one vehicle at a time, mine first, angled downstream, low gear, steady throttle. Nobody stops in the channel. Not for anything. You stop, you're a rock in a river, and rivers win arguments with rocks eventually."

"And if you get swept?" Nadia asked.

"Then you've got a winch cable and a decision to make." I met her eyes. Rain ran between us like a curtain neither of us could afford to acknowledge. "I'm good at this, Wren. Let me be good at it."

Something crossed her face that wasn't agreement. It was closer to grief wearing agreement's coat.

* * *

My crossing went fine, which is how these things set you up.

The truck wallowed through the channel with the current shoving at the flank like a drunk leaning on a friend, and I came out the far side with water sloshing in the footwells and my heart performing percussion, and I anchored the winch cable to a fig tree with roots deep enough to have survived several geological eras, and I waved Nadia forward, and I thought: *see? Competence. The margin holds.*

Nadia drove better than I expected—steady, patient, exactly the throttle discipline I'd asked for. She was halfway across, in the deepest part of the channel, when the high country arrived.

You feel a surge before you see it: a change in the push against the vehicle, the water rising a handspan in seconds, the sound of the river going from talking to shouting. I saw her taillights dip. Saw the current take the Land Rover's stern and swing it, gentle as a fairground ride, ten degrees downstream. Saw her correct. Saw the correction load the winch cable, which went taut with a sound like a struck cable-drum, and then—

The anchor held. The tree held. What failed was the cable's fairlead on her bumper, a piece of stamped steel worth perhaps four dollars, which tore free with a crack I heard over the rain and went spinning off downstream like a fishhook lost to something enormous.

The Land Rover swung broadside.

I have been in maybe two hundred crossings, rescues, chases, and disasters, and I will tell you the truth about all of them: there is a moment, right at the center, when time goes syrup-thick and you can see every branch of what happens next, and you choose one, and you spend the rest of your life finding out whether you chose right.

I chose the river.

Later, I would reconstruct it in pieces: the winch cable re-rigged to my own bumper in ninety seconds that took a year. The harness I threw together from a tow strap because there was no time for anything proper. Nadia standing on the roof of her swaying vehicle, water at the windows, refusing—with a stubbornness I recognized like a mirror—to leave the charts lashed inside. Me shouting at her to clip in, to jump for the line, to *let the maps drown*, and her voice coming back thin over the roar: "They're the only copies of the old surveys—"

"They're paper!"

"They're *his*!"

And then the surge took the Land Rover's wheels off the gravel, and there was no more debate, only physics.

I went in on the cable, and the river hit me like a debt collector—impersonal, total, interested only in what I owed it. The water was warm and violent and full of debris. I caught the strap to the roof rack on the second try. Nadia's hand found my wrist in the brown chaos, and I got the carabiner closed on her harness with fingers that no longer belonged to me, and then the winch took us both, meter by meter, dragging us through the flood toward the bank while the Land Rover went sideways off the submerged track and rolled once, slowly, almost politely, and vanished into the murk with everything she'd carried.

We came out on the gravel like something the river had tasted and rejected. I lay there gasping. She lay beside me, coughing brown water, one hand still locked on my jacket as if letting go might restart the drowning.

Across the channel, on the far bank, Elias Wren stood in the rain beside my truck, motionless, watching.

He didn't shout. He didn't come to the water's edge. He stood with his hands at his sides and his face doing nothing at all, and I understood, in the cold spreading through my chest, that I was looking at a man who had watched a river take people before—who knew exactly what that posture of helplessness cost, because he'd worn it once on a night he'd never described to anyone, and had been wearing it internally ever since.

Then the moment passed, and he was just an old sick man on a bank, and I was a woman who had nearly killed his daughter, and the rain came down on all of us equally, the way rain does, having no interest in our stories.

* * *

We made camp on the high bank because there was nowhere else and no light left to drive by. The Land Rover was gone—charts, spare parts, Nadia's instruments, seven years of whatever she'd been carrying in that vehicle along with her luggage. My truck had taken water in the diffs and coughed grey smoke for twenty minutes before settling into a sulky, damaged idle. The convoy was now one vehicle, one driver, one passenger who paid the bills, and one soaked, silent cartographer sitting as close to the fire as she could get without catching.

I couldn't stop shaking. Not from cold—or not only from cold—but from the specific tremor that comes after you've spent yourself completely and the body presents the invoice. I wrapped my hands around a tin mug of tea I had no intention of drinking and watched the steam and hated myself with great efficiency.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about self-sufficiency: it works beautifully right up until it doesn't, and the moment it fails, you discover it was never strength at all. It was just loneliness with good equipment. I had built a whole life on the premise that if I was skilled enough, prepared enough, hard enough, I would never need anyone—and tonight the river had reached into that life and turned it over like a pocket, and what fell out was: a dead woman's charts, a broken truck, and a stranger's hand on my wrist that I still couldn't stop feeling.

Nadia broke the silence first, because of course she did. She was her father's daughter even in estrangement.

"The surveys," she said, to the fire. "Do you know what was in them?"

"No questions," I said. "That was the deal."

"You nearly died in that river for cargo you won't ask about."

"I nearly died in that river for *you*, which was considerably stupider."

She turned and looked at me then, firelight and exhaustion stripping her down to something raw and young. "Why?"

And I had no answer that wasn't a confession, so I gave her the only currency I had left. "Because you wouldn't let go of the charts, and someone had to be stupid enough to match you."

A sound escaped her—half laugh, half sob, gone too fast to name. She pulled her knees up and stared into the flames, and I stared at her, and across the fire Elias Wren sat with his journal open on his knees, pen moving, writing by firelight with the concentration of a man transcribing a verdict.

He caught me looking. He closed the journal. And in the morning, when we limped east toward the toll bridge at Kambui Crossing with one truck, one map, and a silence in the cab wide enough to drive a convoy through, I noticed that he'd burned something in the fire overnight, and that the ashes were stiff with ink.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight, the river talked to itself in the dark below our camp, patient as a creditor, and I lay awake listening to it and understanding, for the first time in eleven thousand dollars' worth of debt, that some things you can't cross alone—and that I had just, catastrophically, willingly, become someone who knew that.
