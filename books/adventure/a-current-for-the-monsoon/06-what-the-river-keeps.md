## 6. What the River Keeps

The river took my boots. Both of them, in sequence, with the unhurried confidence of a creditor who knows exactly what you owe and when you'll pay it. I watched the left one go first—turning over once in the brown water like a small animal righting itself—and then the right, and then I stopped watching because Beatrix's hand closed around my wrist and hauled me toward the far bank with a grip that suggested she'd done arithmetic about my weight versus the current and hadn't liked the answer.

Now I sat by a fire in wet clothes that smelled of silt and diesel, wearing one of her spare shirts because mine had gone into the wash bucket along with my dignity, and I was trying to decide how much of myself to dry out along with the laundry.

My father was asleep in the truck cab. Or pretending to be. At seventy-one, Elias Wren had perfected the art of sleeping in ways that let him hear everything, a skill he'd presumably learned in mining camps where men argued about claims with dynamite instead of lawyers. His breathing came through the cracked window in long, deliberate intervals. Too even. A man genuinely asleep doesn't breathe in paragraphs.

Beatrix fed the fire a piece of broken pallet she'd pried off the abandoned crossing shed, and the flames went up green and hissing with creosote, and for a while neither of us said anything, which is its own kind of conversation.

"You almost died back there," she said finally.

"I almost drowned," I corrected. "Dying takes longer. Drowning was very efficient."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." I pulled my knees up under the oversized shirt. "You want to know what I was thinking? Underwater. Third time the current flipped me."

She didn't say yes, but she put another stick on the fire, which from Beatrix Iversen was the same as saying yes, go on, I'm listening, don't make it weird.

"I was thinking about a map," I said. "A map I drew when I was nine. Of our yard, in Copperfield Flats. My mother helped me with the scale bar. She said a map without a scale is just a picture of somewhere else." I turned my hands toward the flames. They were still shaking. Not fear, exactly. The river had wrung something out of me and my body hadn't decided what to replace it with yet. "That's the last thing we did together. Before she got sick. And I thought—if this is how it ends, at least it ends with cartography. There are worse last thoughts. Most people get inventory regrets. Did I lock the door. Did I send the email."

"What did your mother die of?"

"Everyone asks that first." I smiled, and it came out crooked, so I aimed it at the fire instead of at her. "Not what killed her. What happened *after*. That's the better question. That's the one nobody asks."

Beatrix waited. She was good at waiting—I'd noticed that about her within an hour of meeting her, the way she could let silence sit there like a loaded tool and not pick it up until the moment it would do the most work. Storm chasers, I supposed, spent their lives reading pressure drops. She read people the same way. Watch the barometer. Wait for the wind to change direction before it changes speed.

"After she died," I said, "my father burned her letters."

The fire popped. Somewhere downriver, water moved through the channel we'd crossed, patient, indifferent, keeping whatever it kept.

"All of them?" Beatrix asked.

"Every letter she ever wrote him. Thirty years of correspondence. She wrote him constantly—he'd be out on survey contracts for months at a time, and she wrote him these long, funny, furious letters about the garden and the neighbors and me and the price of everything. He kept them all. Boxes of them. In the shed behind the house, under the tarp with the good rope." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "Three weeks after the funeral, I came home from school and the burn barrel was going and he was feeding it, page by page, very carefully, like he was returning library books. I asked him what he was doing and he said, 'Some things aren't meant to be archives.' Which is a strange sentence for a man whose entire career was making sure things *were* archives. Survey records. Core samples. Claim filings. His whole life was paper that proved where he'd been and what he'd found."

"And he burned hers."

"He burned hers." I heard my own voice flatten out, which happens when I'm angry—the anger goes somewhere subterranean and comes up as flatness, as precision. "I never forgave him. I want to be honest about that, since we're being honest by a fire, which is apparently a thing people do. Seven years. I've seen him maybe four times. Weddings, funerals, one disastrous Christmas where he gave me a drafting set like that could fix anything. You can't buy back thirty years of someone's handwriting with a Rotring pen set."

"Why do you think he burned them?"

I looked at him then, at the truck cab, at the shape of him behind the fogged glass. "Grief does strange accounting," I said. "Some people keep everything. Some people need the past to weigh nothing. He'd just come back from the survey contract that made him—well, made him comfortable. Rich-adjacent. The accident, they called it. The 7th Survey Company lost six people in a rockfall, and my father walked out of it alive and with a partnership stake, and three weeks later he was burning my mother's voice out of existence." I shook my head. "I used to think it was grief. Now I think grief was just the part I could see. Grief is what you tell your daughter. Whatever it actually was, it had a shape he wouldn't show anyone."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. When I glanced over, she was looking into the fire with an expression I couldn't file anywhere—not pity, not discomfort exactly. Something more like recognition. The look of a woman hearing a story and privately comparing it against a ledger of her own.

"Your turn," I said.

"My turn for what?"

"Whatever it is you're not saying. You've been carrying something since the fuel depot. You hold your shoulders like there's a pack on them even when there isn't." I tipped my head. "I draw maps for a living, Beatrix. I notice terrain."

Something moved across her face—fast, gone before I could name it. "Terrain," she repeated. "Right." She picked up a stone and turned it over in her fingers, considering it like a client considering a bid. "Everybody's got a river they're standing next to," she said. "Doesn't mean you have to swim it tonight."

"That's not a no."

"It's not a yes either. It's—" She exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh. "It's a woman who owes money telling a woman who owes answers that debts are boring. You'd hate mine. Mine has interest rates."

"Try me. I find other people's obligations fascinating. It's the only way I can stand my own."

"Another night." She said it lightly, but she said it, and I understood two things at once: that she meant it, and that she was lying about something adjacent to it. The second thing arrived not as a thought but as a cartographer's instinct—that sense you get when contour lines almost close but not quite, when the map promises a summit the ground refuses to confirm. There was a hollow space in Beatrix Iversen's account of herself, a depression the contours bent around but never filled in. I filed it. I didn't press. Pressing a flash flood doesn't make it move faster; it just makes sure it takes you with it.

"Fine," I said. "Another night. But I'm keeping a ledger."

"Of course you are. You're a Wren."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing." She smiled at the fire. "Your father keeps ledgers too. I've seen his field notes. Man writes down how many grams of rice he eats."

"That tracks." I pulled the shirt tighter around my knees. "He once itemized a family argument. Actual columns. 'Points raised,' 'points conceded,' 'unresolved.' I found it when I was fourteen. He'd given himself a score."

"Who won?"

"The argument or the scoring?"

"Both."

"He won the scoring. I won the argument. Neither of us has ever acknowledged this."

Beatrix laughed—a real one, short and surprised out of her, the kind that escapes before the accountant in her can stop it. And I felt something shift in my chest that I distrusted immediately and completely, because I had not driven two thousand kilometers to sit by a fire and feel warm toward a stranger. I had come for answers. I had come because a dying man's expedition was the first crack in seven years of my father's silence, and cracks are how light gets in, and also how water gets in, and I knew enough about both to respect them.

But she'd pulled me out of a river today. Her hand around my wrist, her whole body braced against the current, and afterward she hadn't made a speech about it. Hadn't even mentioned it. Just handed me a towel from the truck kit and told me the water was colder than it looked, as if that were the remarkable thing.

"Why storm chasing?" I asked. "Of all the ways to be broke and brave."

"Broke and brave." She weighed the phrase. "That's generous. Mostly it's broke and competent, which pays worse." She fed the fire again. "I like weather you can see coming. Everything else in life pretends to surprise you. A cell on the flats gives you forty minutes' warning and then does exactly what it said it would. There's an honesty in that I find—" she searched, "—restful."

"Restful. You chase supercells for rest."

"Compared to debt collectors? Yes. Debt collectors never show you their rotation."

I laughed despite myself, and across the fire our eyes met, and held a half-second longer than the joke required, and I looked away first, which I noted with professional irritation, because I always look away first and I resent every instance of it.

* * *

Later—much later, when the fire had gone down to a red argument among the coals and the frogs along the bank had organized themselves into something like a choir—I lay in the truck bed under a tarp and listened to the river.

It sounded different at night. Bigger. During the crossing it had been an obstacle, a problem with a solution involving tire placement and prayer. Now it was just itself, moving in the dark, carrying things. Silt, branches, somebody's fence post, the topsoil of somebody's field. A river is a ledger too, I thought. Everything that ever fell in, downstream, eventually. Nothing destroyed, only relocated.

I thought about my mother's letters going up in smoke, and whether ash counted as relocation. I thought about my father breathing in paragraphs behind the cab glass. I thought about the way Beatrix had said *another night*—the specific weight of it, the way she'd looked at the fire instead of at me, the way her hand had gone briefly to the inside pocket of her jacket, touching something flat and folded, an unconscious gesture I'd now seen twice and hadn't mentioned either time.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said by the fire: I hadn't joined this expedition only for answers about my mother. I'd joined it because my father had sent me money for the first time in seven years—no note, just a wire transfer and coordinates—and Nadia Wren did not accept mysterious coordinates. She followed them. And following them had led me to a fuel depot, a convoy, and a storm chaser who flinched whenever my father said the words *no questions*.

No questions. Who agrees to no questions that easily? Only someone who already knows the questions would be dangerous.

The river kept moving. The coals ticked as they cooled. Behind the cab glass, my father's breathing changed—finally uneven, finally human, the performance of sleep giving way to the real thing sometime in the small hours, though I couldn't swear to when.

And I lay there adding it up, the way I add up everything: the map he wouldn't show me, the pocket Beatrix kept touching, the word *accident* doing more work than any single word should have to do, and the fact that a man who built his life on paper that proves where you've been had burned the only paper that proved who my mother was.

Tomorrow we'd cross into the green country proper, where the roads ended and the survey grids went blank. Wherever my father was taking us, it was somewhere he needed witnesses and couldn't ask for them outright.

I closed my eyes and let the river sound fill the dark, keeping what it kept, and I made myself a quiet promise over the noise:

Before this expedition ended, I was going to read everyone's fine print—including hers.
