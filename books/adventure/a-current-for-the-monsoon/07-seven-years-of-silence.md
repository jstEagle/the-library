## 7. Seven Years of Silence

The journal is a fool's object. I want to say that plainly, before anything else, because everything else I have to say will be dressed up in surveyor's language and coordinates and the dry grammar of a man who spent forty years measuring ground instead of standing on it honestly.

It is a fool's object: a school exercise book, water-swollen once and dried crooked, its cover soft as old cloth. I bought it in Kessara Junction for two coppers the week after it happened, because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Seven years of entries in pencil, because ink runs when a book gets wet, and I had already learned what water does to the things you're trying to keep.

I keep it in the oilskin roll with the map. Beatrix thinks the map is the cargo. Nadia — if she ever lets herself think about me at all, which she mostly doesn't, which is fair — probably thinks the same. A dying man wants one last look at where he buried his fortune. That's a story people can carry without dropping it.

Nobody asks to see the rest of the roll. People are polite about other men's luggage. It's one of the few mercies left in the world, and I've built my whole late career on it.

Tonight we're camped above the second river crossing, high enough that the mosquitoes are only a rumor. Beatrix is out at the trucks doing whatever storm chasers do to trucks at night — some liturgy of grease and suspicion. Nadia is at the fire with her drafting tools, drawing our route again, though she's drawn it three times already and it hasn't changed. She draws the way her mother used to hum. Not for the product. For the sound it makes inside her.

And I am sitting up against a wheel with this book open on my knees, pretending to sleep, writing the thing I swore seven years ago I would never write down anywhere a living person might find it.

Here is why I'm writing it now: the cough has changed. Anyone who's been around dying knows there are stages to it, like river levels. There's the cough that's a nuisance. There's the cough that's a weather report. And then there's the cough that's a schedule. Mine crossed over somewhere outside Vellamark. The doctor there didn't say anything. He just looked at me the way you look at a gauge reading you've seen before, and wrote a prescription for something that tastes like it's mainly kindness.

So. Before the schedule runs out, the account should exist somewhere besides my skull. Not to be read — God willing, not to be read by either of them while I can still stand between them and it. But written. Because there's a difference between a secret and a stone, and I've been carrying mine so long I've forgotten which it is. Maybe the weight changes if you set it down, even in pencil, even in a book nobody opens.

Let's find out.

* * *

*From the journal of Elias Wren, entry dated the 14th of the Dry Month, seven years past:*

*Camp 4 established. Grid corrected for the declination error in the Junction office tables — third time this season. Note for the final report: the Junction tables are wrong by a degree and a half east and somebody should be made to answer for it.*

*Merrick says the eastern wash shows color. I told him color isn't gold. He said he knows, he's not an idiot, and then he panned three colors out of the sample bag while I was telling him. Good man. Insufferable.*

*Company strength: nine. Morale: high. Water: adequate. Complaints: none worth recording.*

That was the last easy entry. You'll notice how much of it is complaint-as-affection — Merrick being insufferable, the Junction tables being wrong. That's how I wrote then. I thought precision was a form of care. If I recorded the world accurately enough, I believed, nothing in it could be lost.

I was thirty-four years old and I knew nothing whatsoever about loss.

* * *

The 7th Survey Company was nine souls, and I could name them still, all nine, in the order they slept around the fire, which is the order I say them in when I can't sleep, which is most nights now:

Merrick Osei, senior prospector, who found the color in the eastern wash and laughed like a landslide when he did.

Adaeze Okonkwo — no relation to the magistrate, though I checked, later, with a shame I won't describe — our instrument woman, who could shoot a sun angle faster than any man in the district and who kept a photograph of her sister's children folded inside her hatband.

The Ferro brothers, Tomas and Ruy, packers, who argued constantly about everything except each other's backs, which they watched with a devotion most brothers never achieve.

Samuel Idowu, cook, whose beans were a crime and whose coffee was a sacrament, and who was saving his wages to buy his mother a house with a roof that didn't sing in the wind.

Peter Lassiter, junior surveyor, nineteen years old, who had joined us straight out of the Junction school and asked questions the way rain falls — steadily, everywhere, whether or not you wanted it.

Hana Bright, our doctor, who had come out from the coast for reasons she never gave and treated every cut and fever in three settlements' worth of people who couldn't pay, and never once mentioned it.

And Callum Reeve. Company backer. He didn't sleep around the fire — he had a tent, and a cot, and a case of the good whiskey — but I include him in the count because he was one of the nine, and because leaving him off the list would be the last lie I'd ever tell, and I'm done with those. Almost done.

Reeve was charming the way deep water is calm. He'd put up the capital for the survey, taken his percentage of the claim rights as his fee, standard arrangement, and he was generous in small visible ways — extra tobacco, a round of drinks at the Junction, a new tarp for Samuel's kitchen fly. Generosity is cheap when you intend to collect it back with interest.

I liked him. That's the part I can't sand down no matter how many times I go over it. I *liked* him. He listened when I talked about geology. He remembered Adaeze's sister's children's names. When Peter Lassiter got a fever in the second week, Reeve sat up with him half the night reading aloud from a cheap adventure novel, doing all the voices badly, until Hana chased him off and the boy slept.

You want your monsters to be monsters. You want to look back across seven years and see the fangs showing from the first day. But Callum Reeve was funny, and attentive, and brave in the ordinary ways — he waded the flooded crossing with the rest of us, he carried his share up the scree. Whatever was wrong with him wasn't wrong yet, or wasn't wrong in ways a man could see. Or — and this is the version I believe on the bad nights — it was wrong in ways I saw and explained away, because the eastern wash was rich, richer than anything I'd touched in twenty years of prospecting, and I wanted the survey to succeed more than I wanted to look too hard at the man holding our purse strings.

There were signs. I'll list them, since this is meant to be an account and not a confession — although let's be honest, it's a confession wearing an account's clothes.

He asked, twice, casually, exactly how the claim registration worked. Who held the original documents. Where they'd be filed. Whether the company seal or the individual marks governed priority if there was ever a dispute.

He asked about the crew. Not their names — he knew their names — but their situations. Who had family in the district. Who was from the coast. Who would be missed quickly and who would be missed slowly.

I remember that question landing on me like a cold drop off a ledge. *Who would be missed slowly.* And I remember explaining it away, because we were four days' walk from anywhere and the man was just making conversation, and men making conversation around a fire ask careless things, don't they? Don't they?

They don't. Not like that. Not twice.

* * *

*Entry dated the 19th of the Dry Month:*

*Eastern wash confirmed. Sampling trench 3 returned better than a gram to the pan average — I won't write the figure properly even here, in case this book is ever lost. Suffice it to say the 7th Survey has found what we came for, and found it in quantity, and every man and woman on this crew has earned their percentage ten times over.*

*Merrick wants to push a full week of sampling before we break camp. Reeve argues for moving on the claim registration immediately — file first, sample after. Adaeze backed Merrick. I split the difference, which is what I do, which is what I've always done, and which I am beginning to suspect is not wisdom but cowardice with a theodolite.*

*Reeve took the decision poorly. Said something under his breath I didn't catch. Samuel heard it. He wouldn't tell me what.*

I should have gone to Samuel that night and made him tell me. I should have gone to Reeve and said, plainly, *what did you mean by that.* I did neither. I banked the fire and went to sleep in the middle of my own crew, nine people strong, feeling like a rich man.

Four days later eight of those people were dead and I was the ninth, and the difference between us was that I'd gone down to the river to re-shoot a benchmark at dawn, alone, because splitting the difference had left the day's work ambiguous and ambiguity itches in me worse than malaria.

Ambiguity saved my life. Remember that, if you take anything from this book: my worst habit is the only reason I lived to develop better ones.

* * *

I heard the blast from the river. Everyone within twenty miles heard it; it echoed off the western wall twice. I told myself it was Reeve clearing rock for the trail works. We used powder for that. It was normal. I stood in the shallows with my instrument on its tripod and I told myself it was normal for the length of time it takes a man to walk half a mile upstream, and then I stopped telling myself things and ran.

What I found — no. I'm not going to render it. Not here, not in any book, not in any confession a priest or a magistrate or my own daughter could drag out of me. There are eight people who deserve to be remembered as they were around a fire, arguing about beans and declination tables, and I will not make them into a scene. The magistrate's record will contain the technical findings. Let the technical findings be enough.

What I will record is this: it was not an accident. Powder stores don't detonate themselves, and they especially don't detonate themselves in a pattern that happens to kill every witness to a claim dispute and leave the claim documents — which had been in Adaeze's instrument chest, which had been moved, I noticed even through the horror, *moved* — recoverable and intact.

And Callum Reeve was gone. His tent, his cot, his whiskey case, his mule. Gone before the echo finished.

I did what a surveyor does. I surveyed it. God forgive me, I walked the site with my notebook and I measured and I sketched and I noted azimuths and distances and the position of every piece of evidence, because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and because some animal part of me understood, even then, that the measurements were the only weapon I would ever have. You can't fight a man like Reeve with grief. Grief is what he'd expect you to drown in. But numbers don't grieve. Numbers just sit there, being true, waiting.

Then I buried them. All eight. It took me two days. I dug until my hands split, and Hana — Hana who was gone, who was one of the ones I was digging for — Hana's kit had a needle and thread and I sewed my palms closed on the second night and kept digging. Samuel last, because he'd have wanted to serve the others first. I said words over them. I don't remember what words. I think I recited the declination correction for the Junction tables, over and over, because it was the only prayer I had that I knew by heart.

Then I took the evidence — the site notes, the photographs Hana had insisted we take of the sampling trenches for the company record, the duplicate claim registrations with all nine marks including mine, and the page from Reeve's own ledger that I found snagged on brush a quarter mile downstream, in his handwriting, itemizing powder drawn "for trail works" in an amount that would have cleared a canyon — and I sealed it in an ammunition tin with wax, and I carried it eleven days out of the green country, alone, sick, half-mad, and I buried it where no register would ever look, and I drew the map.

And then I did the thing that has defined every day since.

I said nothing.

* * *

People will ask — Nadia will ask, someday, if she ever reads this, and she'll have the right to ask it louder than anyone — *why.* Why didn't I go to the magistrates. Why didn't I stand up in the Junction and name him. Why did I let Callum Reeve walk into Calloway House with Adaeze's claim in his pocket and build an empire on top of eight graves.

Here are the answers I gave myself, in order, over the years. None of them survive daylight. I record them anyway, because a confession that curates itself is just another performance.

One: who would believe me? A lone survivor, no bodies produced — I'd buried them in wild country, unphotographed, unverified — versus the respected backer of a legitimate survey. My word against his ledger. And I'd seen, by then, how far his ledgers reached. The inquest was opened and closed in nine days. Accidental detonation, improper storage, tragic. The Junction paper printed a paragraph. Reeve sent flowers to the memorial and a month's wages to each bereaved family, and I watched Samuel Idowu's mother accept the envelope with both hands because a roof that doesn't sing in the wind doesn't come free, and I understood that the money was the muzzle, and it fit everyone, and it fit me best of all.

Two: I told myself I'd wait. Gather more. Build a case that couldn't be dismissed. This is the answer I wore longest, because it has the shape of virtue. But waiting is a room, and I furnished it comfortably, and I stayed seven years.

Three — the true one, the one underneath: I was afraid. Not of dying. Of the arithmetic. Eight families fed by Calloway wages now, indirectly, distantly, the way the mountain feeds the spring. Suppliers, carters, clerks, whole towns. If I pulled the thread, the sweater came apart, and everyone wearing it would feel the cold, and they would know my name as the man who pulled it. Cowardice dressed as mercy. I've had seven years to learn the difference and mostly I have.

And four — the one that shames me more than all the others combined, because it's the one that cost someone else: I told myself that silence protected Nadia. Her mother was already two years gone by then; the letters — Adaeze's letters, my wife's letters, the ones I burned — they were full of her own griefs and her own accusations, none of them about this, but I couldn't separate the papers anymore, couldn't bear the archive of us sitting in a box while I sat on a grave. So I burned them, and told my daughter it was tidying, and watched her look at me the way you look at a stranger, and I chose that wound over the truth because the truth had a body count.

A man can ruin a child with protection. Nobody warns you. They warn you about everything else.

* * *

Present day. The fire's down to coals. Nadia rolled into her bedroll an hour ago, facing away from camp the way she always does, presenting me the back of her head like a bill for services never rendered. Beatrix came in from the trucks smelling of diesel and stood over me for a moment — I felt her there, deciding whether I was asleep — and then she sat down at the fire's edge and looked into the coals for a long time with an expression I recognized, because I've worn it in mirrors for seven years. The look of a person running arithmetic they don't like.

She thinks I don't notice things. Dying men are furniture to the healthy, mostly. But I notice. I noticed her reading the fine print of her own desperation the night she signed on, and I noticed that she read it fast, the way you skim a contract you've already decided to sign, and I have been carrying a small cold weight of guilt about that ever since, because I counted on it. I picked a woman in debt specifically because a woman in debt doesn't ask questions, and I wrote *no questions asked* into the terms myself, and I told myself it was mercy — hers and mine — and it is, partly, but it's also a lock, and I'm the one who turned the key.

Because here's what she doesn't know, lying there calculating her eleven thousand: the tin in the adit isn't a fortune. It's a verdict. And the road we're driving goes through country where Calloway money moves, and Calloway money has eyes, and I have watched Mr. Okafor's particular breed of courtesy operate before, at a distance, through other men's disasters. A convoy heading into dead country with a dying prospector and a hand-drawn map is a strange enough shape that someone at the syndicate will eventually tilt their head at it. Strange shapes get followed.

When that happens, Beatrix will lie for us. She's good at it; I've heard her do it on the radio, smooth as poured concrete, and I've hated how grateful I was. She'll lie because she's paid to, and then she'll lie for free, and somewhere in between those two kinds of lying she'll start wanting to know what she's lying *about*, because that's the tax on a conscience, and she has one, however hard she's tried to pawn it.

And when she asks — when the *no questions* clause finally comes due and she breaks it, and she will, because she signed it too easily herself and easy signatures itch — when she stands in front of me with the map in one hand and the question in the other, I will have to decide, in real time, with my lungs failing and my daughter twenty feet away pretending to sleep, how much truth a dying man owes the people carrying him.

I close the book. Oilskin first, then the cord, then the roll, then the roll back into the false bottom of the map case, which sits by my feet where a sleeping old man's property belongs. The coals tick as they cool. Somewhere out in the dark, the river we crossed today is talking to itself, patient, the way water is, the way debt is, the way truth is — it just keeps moving toward the low places, and it arrives when it arrives, and no one alive can order it otherwise.

Tomorrow we cross the toll bridge at Karsten's Ferry. I know the bridge; I helped survey the replacement span twenty years ago, back when I still believed measurement was a form of care. And I know who holds the toll concession these days, and whose money is behind the concession, and I have been watching the eastern horizon all evening for dust that moves with purpose.

Beatrix thinks the hard part of this journey is the water.

The water, child, is the easy part. Water only wants to drown you.

Wait until you meet the men on the bridge.
