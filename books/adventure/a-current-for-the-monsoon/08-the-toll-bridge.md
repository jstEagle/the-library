## 8. The Toll Bridge

The Kestrel River crossing had a name on the map—Marrow's Toll—and a reputation that traveled further than the river itself. The bridge was iron-boned and rust-skinned, built by some mining consortium back when the monsoon country still pretended to be tame, and the toll was collected by a man named Dree who charged whatever he thought your face could afford. I'd crossed twice before. Both times I'd smiled like a woman with nothing in her truck bed worth taxing.

This time there were three black SUVs parked under the tamarind shade on the far bank, arranged with the casual geometry of men who owned the ground they stood on.

I saw them from two kilometers out, cresting the last rise, and my stomach did the thing it does—not a drop, exactly, more like a slow tilt, the way a ship lists when it's taking on water somewhere you can't see.

"That's Calloway," Nadia said from the passenger seat. She'd been navigating off a survey map so old the river had moved twice since it was printed, and she said it flatly, the way she said most things, as if emotion were a rounding error she refused to carry.

"It's a car company," Elias said from the back.

"It's three cars," she said, "parked like a checkpoint."

Elias didn't answer. In the rearview I caught him folding something—paper, small, practiced—and putting it somewhere inside his coat that his coat did not officially have. Seven years of practice will teach a man pockets the tailor never dreamed of.

"Options," I said, because saying options out loud is how I keep my hands steady. "One: we turn around and lose a day finding another crossing, which puts us behind the dry season clock and tells whoever those gentlemen are that we've got something to hide. Two: we drive through and smile."

"Three," Nadia said, "we find out what they want before we're inside arm's reach of them."

"We are inside arm's reach of them," I said. "We have been since Wren signed my contract with syndicate money. That's the arithmetic nobody wants to do at the signing table."

Elias coughed—the wet, structural kind that had gotten worse since the high country—and said, "Drive through and smile. It's worked for you your whole life, Ms. Iversen. I'd hate to see you change strategy this far from shore."

There are days I despise being known.

* * *

Dree's toll booth was a shipping container with a window cut into it and a hand-painted sign reading TOLL — PRICES NEGOTIABLE, which was honest advertising if nothing else. The SUVs were parked thirty meters past it, and the men leaning against them watched us come with the unhurried attention of herons watching fish.

I rolled to a stop at the window. Dree leaned out, took one look past me at the SUVs, and suddenly found urgent business with his ledger.

"Toll's waived today," he murmured. "Compliments of the gentlemen behind you."

Which meant they'd paid him to wave people through slowly. Which meant they wanted time to look.

I paid him anyway—habit, and a superstition about owing anyone anything—and rolled forward at a crawl I hoped read as mechanical trouble rather than reluctance.

The man who stepped away from the middle SUV was tall, gray at the temples, dressed in linen the color of sand that had given up trying to be white. He wore no visible weapon, which in this country is either poverty or confidence, and everything about him said confidence. He smiled like a man greeting an old friend whose name he absolutely knew and wanted you to sweat over whether he knew it.

"Beatrix Iversen." He said it warmly, tasting each syllable. "The storm chaser. I've heard your name in rooms where names get eaten. Mr. Okafor." He extended a hand.

I took it. His grip was dry and precise, a handshake calibrated to communicate that he could crush things but had chosen, for now, not to.

"Mr. Okafor," I said. "I don't know you."

"No one knows me. That's rather the point of me." He gestured at the convoy, at the sky, at the whole green throat of the country ahead of us, as if all of it were laid out for his inspection. "You'll forgive the ambush. We tried to catch you in Marrow's Ford but you'd already gone to ground. You travel like weather, Ms. Iversen. My compliments."

Behind me, I heard the second vehicle—our supply truck, driven by Okonjo the younger, who'd joined us at the last town and whose loyalty was a fixed cost I'd already budgeted—pull up and stop. Doors stayed shut. Good instincts.

"What can I do for Calloway?" I asked. "If this is about the bounty cell I chased in the flats, I filed the paperwork. Your office sat on it for six weeks."

"This isn't about weather." Okafor's smile deepened by a degree, the way ice deepens. "It's about money. Specifically, a disbursement—Calloway funds, drawn against a private account, paid to one B. Iversen, expedition services. A large sum. And the route of that expenditure—" he consulted a leather folio he hadn't been holding a moment ago, a magician's prop, "—runs directly into the dead country. The flood country. Where, as every schoolchild knows, there is nothing left to find and no one left to pay."

"Prospecting," I said. "Old claims. Some of them predate the company's surveys. There's gold in ground nobody's bothered to re-check."

"There is," he agreed pleasantly. "And there are also ghosts. The company has a history with that particular stretch of country. A sensitive history. When Calloway money starts walking toward it, certain people upstairs develop insomnia." He closed the folio. "So. Who hired you?"

Here it was. The moment the chapter turns.

I want to be honest about what happened in the next four seconds, because I've spent enough nights staring at canvas ceilings replaying them. There was a version of me—a younger one, maybe two years younger, maybe only one—who would have hesitated. Would have looked at Elias in the mirror, or at Nadia beside me, and let the hesitation show. That version might have told a clumsy truth or a clumsy lie, and either way Mr. Okafor would have smelled blood in the water and the story ends differently, worse, earlier.

But that version of me had been sanded off by years of debt. Years of smiling at creditors. Years of lying to men with ledgers about where I'd been and what I'd found and whether the storm cell I chased had really been worth the fuel. I had become, without ever deciding to become it, a very good liar. It's the one skill the Calloway syndicate gave me free of charge, and standing there in the heat with Okafor's pleasant eyes on mine, I hated it the way you hate a scar you earned doing something stupid.

"Elias Wren," I said. "Retired prospector. Dying, if you must know—he hired me to take him to a claim he staked seven years ago, before his health went. He wants to stand on his own ground once before the ground stands on him." I shrugged, and made the shrug do double duty: bored, transparent, slightly offended at being stopped over sentiment. "He paid through the syndicate because his accounts are frozen pending probate. Ask your own finance office. They cleared the transfer."

Every word true. Every word arranged like furniture in a room designed to make you look at the wrong wall. That's the craft of it—you don't invent lies, you curate truths. Nobody teaches you this. You learn it across tables from people who own pieces of your life.

Okafor studied me. Not aggressively—he was far too good for aggression—but with the patient, affectionate attention of a man reading a document he suspected had a forged seal somewhere, and enjoying the search.

"Wren," he said. "The name rings a faint bell. Survey work?"

"Independent. He kept to himself."

"They usually do, the ones who keep to themselves. It's the ones who talk who worry us." He turned his head, casually, and looked past my shoulder at our truck. At Elias, who had opened his door and lowered himself to the gravel with the deliberate slowness of a man conserving breath, and was now standing in the open, hat in hand, looking frail and sun-cured and entirely harmless.

"Mr. Wren," Okafor called. "A pleasure. I understand you're taking a sentimental journey."

Elias smiled. It was a good performance—I'll grant him that, dying man that he was, liar that he'd apparently been for seven years. He looked like every old prospector I'd ever seen shuffling through a fuel depot: used up, harmless, nostalgic.

"Just want to see my claim again," Elias said, voice like gravel poured slow. "Bury a few things. Say a few words. Won't be long."

"Bury a few things," Okafor repeated softly. Something moved behind his eyes—not suspicion exactly. Inventory. "Well. The country keeps what we give it. That's rather its charm." He turned back to me and produced, from the folio, a card. Heavy stock. No title, just a telephone number embossed in silver. "Ms. Iversen. The company values its contractors. If your client's health deteriorates—if the trip becomes more than you contracted for—call that number. We're generous with people who keep us informed. Generosity is cheaper than surprises."

"I don't inform on clients," I said. "It's bad for repeat business."

"Of course it is." He tucked the folio away. "Then consider it an offer of assistance. Roads wash out this time of year. Vehicles fail. Men fail faster." His gaze flicked, briefly, to Elias—measuring him, I realized, the way you measure a load limit on a bridge. "The dry season is ending, Ms. Iversen. Whatever he means to bury, he should bury it soon. Before the rain makes everything honest."

I felt that sentence go in under my ribs and sit there.

"Safe travels," Okafor said, and stepped back, and the herons resumed their fishing.

* * *

We crossed the bridge in silence. Dree waved us through without meeting my eyes; whatever the gentlemen had paid him, it evidently didn't cover conversation. On the far side the road degraded immediately into the red clay and elephant grass that marked the border of the flood country proper, and I put eight kilometers between us and the SUVs before I trusted myself to speak.

"He knows," Nadia said.

"He suspects," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is whether we're alive next week."

"He said 'bury.' He heard my father say 'bury' and he filed it." She twisted in her seat to face the back. "What did you bury out there?"

"Nadia." Elias's voice had gone quiet in the way that quiet things go when they're dangerous. "Not here."

"Not anywhere! That's been the arrangement for seven years, hasn't it? Not anywhere, not ever, not even—" She stopped herself. Her jaw worked. Through the windshield I watched the road and did not watch either of them, which is a skill of its own, and one I hated having.

"He's a symptom," I said finally. "Okafor's a symptom. The disease is upstream—it always is. Somebody upstairs got insomnia, which means somebody upstairs already knows money is moving, which means somebody upstairs already had a reason to care. We didn't start this. We're driving through the middle of it."

"And what happens," Nadia said, turning back around, "when the middle of it decides to close?"

Neither of us answered. Ahead, the road bent toward the river lowlands, and I could see the light changing over the water—that bruised, metallic quality the sky gets when the dry season starts counting down its final weeks. Somewhere beyond the horizon, the first cells of the monsoon were assembling themselves out of ocean heat, patient as debt.

* * *

We camped that night on a shelf of high ground above the Kestrel's tributaries, close enough to hear the water working at its banks. Okonjo cooked. Nadia cleaned her instruments with a cloth and a focus that was clearly a form of prayer. Elias sat apart, as he always did, and I saw him take out the journal—the oilcloth-wrapped thing I'd glimpsed twice and never mentioned—and hold it over the fire's edge, as if considering.

He didn't burn it. He put it away and caught me watching, and neither of us pretended otherwise, and that shared silence was somehow more intimate than anything I'd said aloud in months.

Later, Nadia came and sat on the truck's running board beside me while I checked tie-downs I'd already checked.

"You lied well," she said.

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment." She looked at the fire, not at me. "My father spent seven years being lied *about*. Survey accident, the papers said. Bad gas pocket, the company said. Everyone said something smooth and everyone believed it because smooth is easier than true." Her hands stilled on the cloth. "When you lie that well, Beatrix, I can't tell which side of it you're on."

I had an answer ready. I always have an answer ready; it's the affliction Okafor spotted in under a minute. But sitting there in the dark with the river talking to itself below us, I found I didn't want to use it. So I said nothing, and she waited, and the nothing stretched between us like a wire.

In the morning, the tributary we needed to cross had come up half a meter overnight—early snowmelt from the high country, or the season shifting early, or the country simply deciding to test us. The ford was gone. The old rail spur upstream was the only way through, and the rail spur meant elevation, exposure, and a day lost we didn't have.

And when I climbed the rise to scout it, I saw, far back along our own track, a single point of dust moving at a patient speed.

Not following. Pacing us.

The dry season was ending, Okafor had said. Before the rain makes everything honest.

I got back in the truck and told them we'd take the spur, and I drove, and I did not tell them about the dust.
