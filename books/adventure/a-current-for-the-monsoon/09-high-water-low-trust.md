## 9. High Water, Low Trust

My father taught me to read water before he taught me to read words. He'd stand at the edge of a current and say, *Tell me what it's doing*, and I'd have to answer in specifics—gradient, load, the color that meant clay upstream, the smell that meant rain on a plateau we couldn't see. It was the closest thing to tenderness he knew how to offer: knowledge instead of hugs. I took it and ran, which is a thing you can do with knowledge in a way you can't do with hugs.

So when the river came up over the ford road like something rising from sleep, I was the one who called it.

"That's not a crossing anymore," I said, standing on the hood of Beatrix's truck with my boots planted on the roof rack, because apparently that was a thing storm chasers just did, stood on vehicles, as if gravity were a rumor. "That's a river remembering it used to be bigger."

Beatrix squinted at it the way she squinted at everything, like the world was an invoice she intended to dispute. "How long?"

"Hours. Maybe less if there's more cloud sitting on the headwaters." I climbed down. "We need high ground that isn't going anywhere."

"Rail spur," my father said from the back seat.

I hadn't heard him speak in two days. His voice had gone thin, a wire pulled too tight, but it still carried that old surveyor's certainty—the tone of a man who had already walked the ground in his head. "Two miles east. Cut into the bluff. The old Monsoon Line bed. Ballast doesn't flood."

"You've been out here before," Beatrix said. Not a question. She'd been not-asking questions this whole trip with the discipline of someone who'd been paid for it, and every not-question was starting to pile up between us like unopened mail.

"I've been most places," Elias said, and closed his eyes, and that was the end of the conversation, because that was how conversations ended with my father: not with agreement but with exhaustion, yours or his, whichever arrived first.

* * *

The spur was exactly where he said it would be, which unsettled me more than if it hadn't been. Seven years of silence between us, seven years of me building a life out of maps of places he'd never been, and the man could still navigate my skepticism blindfolded. The rail bed ran along the bluff on a shelf of crushed stone, ties rotted to black lace, rails long since pulled for scrap except for occasional rusted stubs pointing nowhere, like exclamation marks over sentences nobody finished.

Beatrix got both trucks up onto the ballast with a precision that annoyed me because I admired it. She didn't celebrate. She just checked the tie-downs twice and then sat on her tailgate eating cold rice out of a pot and watching the river come up through the trees below us, brown and muscular, carrying whole branches, a fence post, something that might have been a canoe or might have been a door.

"We're pinned for a day at least," she said. "Maybe two."

"Good," my father said.

Nobody asked him why. That was the other thing about this expedition—we'd all gotten very good at not asking him things. Beatrix because she'd signed a contract that paid her not to. Me because I'd spent seven years learning that asking my father questions was like drilling into rock: enormous effort, small core samples, and sometimes the drill came back bent.

But I watched him that evening, and watching is its own kind of asking.

He sat apart, the way he always sat apart, on a flat stone near the fire with his journal open on his knees. He'd kept it hidden the whole trip—wrapped in oilcloth, tucked inside his bedroll, handled the way other men handle money or medicine. I'd told myself it was none of my business. I'd told myself a lot of things were none of my business lately; it was practically a second career.

The wind shifted. A gust came off the river carrying spray and the green rot-smell of drowned country, and it flipped the corner of a page, and my father moved to smooth it—and then stopped. Looked around. Looked at the fire.

And tore the page out.

He fed it to the flames the way you'd feed a dangerous animal something to keep it quiet: quickly, without looking directly at it. Then another page. Then three more, torn as a block, folded once, pushed down into the coals where they caught with a soft whump and curled into black lace that rose on the heat and came apart above our heads.

I don't know what I said. Something. My voice came out of me the way water comes out of a cracked pipe—under pressure, in the wrong place. I crossed the camp in maybe six steps and put my hand flat on the journal, on the remaining pages, and my father looked up at me with eyes that were, for once, entirely unguarded, and what was in them wasn't guilt exactly. It was arithmetic. He was calculating whether he could stop me, and I could see the sum coming out against him.

"Nadia." My name in his mouth sounded like a station call on a dead line.

"What are you burning?"

"Old notes."

"Don't." I held the journal down. "Don't do the thing where you give me half a sentence and expect me to build a cathedral out of it. What are you burning?"

Behind me I felt Beatrix go very still by the fire. She'd been pretending not to watch; now she was pretending harder, which is a distinct skill and she has it at a professional level.

Elias closed his hand over mine on the journal. His grip was weaker than it should have been—I felt that, catalogued it, hated that I catalogued it—and he didn't pull the book away. He just held on, like we were two people holding the same rope over a drop.

"Some things are mine to carry," he said.

"You burned Mom's letters." The words came out flat and fast, seven years compressed into a single detonation. "You sat in the kitchen and burned her letters and when I asked you why, you said some things are mine to carry. So forgive me if that sentence doesn't close the account."

His face did something complicated. Grief will do that to a face—run four expressions at once and crash them into each other. "This isn't that."

"No. This is exactly that. You're doing it again. Whatever's in this book, it's about her, or it's about the survey, or it's about whatever happened out here that turned you into a man who burns paper instead of talking—" I stopped, because my voice had gone up into a register I didn't like, a register that belonged to a nineteen-year-old slamming a door, and I was thirty-one and standing in monsoon country holding my dying father's journal over a fire, and I made myself lower it. Made myself say, quietly: "What happened to the survey crew, Dad?"

The river filled the silence. It had a lot to say and no interest in any of us.

"The 7th Survey Company was eleven people," he said finally, in the voice he used for technical matters, coordinates and gradients, the voice I'd grown up understanding meant *do not interrupt*. "It was a good crew. Best claim assessment work I ever saw. And they died in a collapse at the Adeline workings, and I survived it, and everything else—" his thumb moved on the journal cover, back and forth, a tiny repetitive motion like a man smoothing a wrinkle that wouldn't smooth, "—everything else is in there, and it isn't yours yet."

"*Yet.*"

"It isn't yours yet," he repeated, and there was something under the repetition, some weight he was setting down carefully on the word, and I understood with a cold sliding feeling that he was telling me the truth as precisely as he knew how. Not yet. Which meant eventually. Which meant soon, maybe, given the gray of his skin and the way he'd started sleeping sitting up so the coughing wouldn't drown him lying down.

"Why not now?" I asked. "What could possibly be worth waiting for? Who are you protecting?"

He looked past me then—not avoiding my eyes, I realized, but looking at something behind me. At Beatrix, who had become extremely interested in the rice pot. And I watched my father make a decision, and I watched it cost him, the way decisions visibly cost him now, each one a coin counted out of a dwindling purse.

"Her," he said. "I'm protecting her. From the part of this that isn't hers either. Don't let her read the rest, Nadia. When it comes time—and it's coming—she'll want to carry it because she thinks that's what she's for. Carrying things for people who pay her. Don't let her read the rest until I'm done deciding what she owes."

"That's not your choice to make for her."

"No," he agreed, terribly gently. "It isn't. But it's mine to make for me, while I still can. There's a difference, and you of all people know the difference, because you left home to learn it."

That landed the way he always landed things—no raised voice, no cruelty, just a fact placed on the table like a specimen. I let go of the journal. My hand had gone numb anyway.

He wrapped it back in the oilcloth with those careful hands, surgeon's hands on a prospector, and put it away, and the fire ate the last of the torn pages, and I stood there feeling the loyalty split inside me along a seam I hadn't known was there. On one side: my father, dying, keeping his own counsel, entitled to it maybe, entitled to his silences the way any man is entitled to his wounds. On the other side: Beatrix, who had driven us through a flooded country on a promise and a payment, and who deserved to know what she was hauling besides an old man and his secrets. And underneath both, cold and patient as groundwater: the question I'd actually come to ask, the one I'd dressed up as closure and reconciliation. *What did you do out here, Dad? What did the survey buy you, and what did it cost everyone else?*

Because rich-adjacent men don't burn their dead wives' letters over ordinary grief. I'd known that at nineteen. I'd just hoped I was wrong.

* * *

I took first watch, which surprised everyone including me. Beatrix argued—said I'd been driving, said she didn't need much sleep, said it in that clipped way she had that was either generosity or control and I genuinely could not tell which, and maybe neither could she. I told her I slept badly next to rivers. It was even true.

She banked the fire and rolled into her bedroll ten feet away, close enough to hear, far enough to pretend otherwise. My father's breathing had found the wet, rattling rhythm that had become the soundtrack of the trip, and the river below talked over it, and somewhere upstream thunder was working a cell across the plateau like a man working a stubborn bolt.

Around midnight, Beatrix spoke into the dark without preamble, the way she did, as if we'd been mid-conversation for hours.

"He's burning pages and you're not sleeping and nobody's asking anybody anything. That's the deal, right? That's what 'no questions' looks like from the inside."

I should have lied. I considered it. But lying to Beatrix Iversen felt like falsifying a map—you could do it, but you'd be the one lost later.

"That's the deal," I said.

"And you signed on anyway."

"I signed on for answers. Turns out answers are also a currency, and he's hoarding."

A long pause. Rain started somewhere above us, hitting the canopy in a slow scatter, not committed yet. "For what it's worth," she said, "he watches you when you're navigating. Like he's checking his own work against yours. I don't think he does that because he doubts you."

"No," I said. "I think he does it because I'm the last thing he surveyed that he's proud of."

She didn't laugh. I felt her turn that over in the dark, taking it seriously, which was somehow worse than laughter and better than sympathy.

Then, quieter, almost to herself: "Eleven people. He said eleven."

"You were listening."

"I listen for a living." A pause with weather in it. "Nadia, what survey company loses eleven people and pays out enough to make a man rich-adjacent? Insurance doesn't run like that. Claims don't run like that."

I stared at the fire's red eye and felt the ground shift under a question I hadn't let myself ask out loud in seven years, asked now by a stranger in the dark, casually, the way you'd note a discrepancy in a legend key.

"I don't know," I said. "Yet."

Above us, the rain found its conviction and came down hard, drumming the rail bed, filling the dark with static. And two miles south, where the ford had been, the river kept rising, patient and total, closing every road behind us but the one my father had chosen—which was, I was beginning to understand, the only kind of choice he'd ever really offered anyone.
