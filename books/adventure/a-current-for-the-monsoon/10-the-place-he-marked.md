## 10. The Place He Marked

The coordinates put us on a shelf of rock above a dry wash, forty kilometers past the last thing anyone would generously call a road, and the mine adit—if you could call a collapsed mouth of timber and rubble an adit—sat in the hillside like a wound that had scabbed over and then been forgotten about.

I killed the engine and let the silence come down. Out here the silence has weight. It presses on the truck roof like a hand checking whether you're still there.

"Is that it?" Nadia asked from the passenger seat. She had her father's map spread across her knees, her own pencil annotations crawling up the margins in that precise cartographer's hand—elevations, bearings, little question marks where Elias's ink had gone vague. She'd been annotating for three days straight, as if enough measurement might make the man himself legible.

"That's what the map says," I said. "Maps have been wrong before."

"Not his," she said, and there was something in her voice I couldn't file anywhere—not loyalty exactly, not forgiveness. Something still under construction.

Elias was in the back seat with the satchel of medical supplies we'd stopped pretending he didn't need. He'd lost six kilos since the fuel depot. His skin had gone the color of the dust outside, which felt less like a symptom than a gradual agreement between the man and the country. But when I looked at him in the mirror, his eyes were open and fixed on the adit with an expression I'd only ever seen on people looking at graves they'd dug themselves.

"That's it," he said. "That's the place."

* * *

We made camp below the wash, out of the wind, because even in the dry season this country gets an evening wind that comes off the high ground carrying grit and the smell of stone heating all day and now giving it back. I set the tent up for Elias first. He protested, of course. Dying men protest everything; it's how they prove they're still holding the pen on their own story.

"I can sleep in the truck," he said.

"You'll sleep where I put you," I said, "because you're paying me to make decisions, and that's the one product I sell."

He almost smiled. "You should charge more."

"Don't tell my creditors that."

Nadia built the fire while I checked the truck over—axles, fuel line, the slow leak in the rear left tire I'd been nursing since the river crossing. Habit. When your whole life is vehicles and weather, maintenance is a form of prayer. You check the things you can check because the things you can't check will kill you regardless.

By full dark Elias was propped against a bedroll with a tin cup of broth he had no intention of finishing, and Nadia sat across the fire from him with her knees drawn up, and I sat where I always sat—slightly apart, facing the dark, because a storm chaser learns early that the interesting weather comes from behind you.

"You've been here before," Nadia said. Not a question.

"Once." Elias turned the cup in his hands. "Seven years ago. Nighttime, then too."

"What was it?"

"A survey camp. The 7th Company. They were mapping the upper tributaries." He paused long enough that I turned around. "I was their guide."

Nadia's pencil hand went still. Even across the fire I could see her doing the arithmetic—the dates, the years, the gap in her father's life shaped exactly like seven years and a burned box of letters.

"And after?" she asked.

"After, I left."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have tonight."

I should have said something. That's the job, technically—keeping the convoy functional, keeping the client alive, keeping the peace. Instead I fed the fire and watched the sparks go up and thought about nothing, which is a skill, and a cowardly one.

Later, when Elias slept—and his sleep had developed a wet, rattling quality I liked about as much as I liked anything these days—Nadia came and stood beside me at the edge of the firelight.

"He knows I know something," she said.

"Everyone knows everyone knows something. It's a convoy, not a monastery."

"Beatrix." She used my name like a pry bar. "You read the map before I did. You negotiated with him before I found you. Whatever he's carrying, you knew first."

I looked at her. Firelight does flattering things to most faces and honest things to hers—she wasn't angry, exactly. She was braced. A woman who measures distance for a living, standing as close to me as she'd stood to anyone in weeks, asking me to give her a bearing.

"When he's ready," I said, "he'll tell you himself. And if I tell you first, I take that from him. It's the only thing he's got left that's actually his."

She held my eyes for a long moment. Then she nodded once, sharp, and went to her tent, and I sat alone with the fire and the sound of a dying man breathing twenty feet away, and I told myself the lie I'd been telling since the fuel depot: that no questions asked meant no answers owed.

* * *

Morning came gray and hot, the sky the color of old zinc. We climbed to the adit together, slowly, because Elias now needed a shoulder on anything steeper than a staircase and I was the shoulder. Nadia walked ahead with the map, marking our route, and I tried not to notice how each step cost him, how he rationed his breath like a man who'd learned the exact size of his remaining account.

At the collapsed entrance he stopped and put his palm flat against the timbers. They were black with age, some of them splintered where the rock had come down.

"Seventy-one men," he said quietly. To the wood, maybe. Maybe to me. "Company roster said seventy-two. They never corrected it."

My spine did something cold. "Elias—"

"Not today." He took his hand back. "Today we dig."

The collapse hadn't sealed the adit completely. There was a crawl space along the eastern wall where the rubble had settled into a kind of corridor—two feet wide, maybe thirty meters deep, choked with rock and rotted timber. Elias knelt at the mouth of it and pointed with a hand that trembled unless he pinned it against his knee.

"The cache is at the far end. Behind a false wall of rubble. There's a marker—a stake with a brass tag, 7-C-9."

"You're not going in there," I said.

"I'm not asking to." He looked up at me, and his eyes were terribly clear. Clearer than they'd been in days, as if the nearness of the thing had temporarily outweighed the dying. "I'm asking you to. Nadia stays out here with me."

"Why does she stay out here?"

"Because what's in there isn't hers yet." And then, softer: "And because if it comes down while you're inside, I don't want her watching."

That sentence went through me like a drill bit. I looked at the crawl space. Thirty meters of unsupported rubble in seismically bored country, in a mine that had already collapsed once, taking—apparently—some portion of its crew with it. Every instinct I owned filed a formal objection.

"Fine print," I said. "'No questions asked.' This counts as a question."

"This counts as the reason," Elias said.

* * *

I went in because I'd signed, and because a promise made to a dying man in exchange for debt relief is still a promise, and because—though I'd have bitten through my own tongue before saying it aloud—I wanted to know. Seven days of driving toward a dot on a map buys a certain hunger.

The crawl was worse than it looked. The air tasted of stone dust and old rot, and twice the ceiling shifted with a sound like a throat clearing, and both times I lay flat in the dark with my heart going like a winch motor and waited to find out whether this was the day. Storm chasers talk about fear like it's weather—you drive into it, you drive out, you log it. Nobody talks about the fear that sits on your back in a hole in the ground with no exit strategy except backward, and makes you think about your mother, of all people, and the boat she almost bought in '09.

The stake was where he said. Brass tag, green with age: **7-C-9**. Behind it, the rubble gave way to a cavity, and in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth and tarped under slabs of slate, a metal ammunition case, a canvas roll, and a bundle of documents bound with wire.

I didn't open them there. Some part of me understood, lying in the dark with my arms full of another man's seven years, that whatever was in those papers was heavier than gold and I should carry it out before I learned its weight.

When I backed out into the daylight, blinking, Elias was sitting against a boulder with Nadia holding a cup of water to his lips, and the look on his face when he saw the case in my arms was the look of a man setting down something he'd carried so long he'd grown around it.

"Thank you," he said. Just that. Just thank you, in the voice of a man who knew it wasn't nearly enough and had decided to say it anyway.

* * *

The radio crackled at four in the afternoon.

I was under the truck at the time, dealing with the rear tire properly instead of prayerfully, and the voice that came out of the handset—Nadia had answered it—was warm, unhurried, and familiar in the way a knife is familiar.

"—convoy Bravo-Kilo, do you copy? Mr. Okafor sends his regards and a routine inquiry as to your position and cargo. Over."

I slid out from under the chassis with grease on my forearms and my stomach already composing the lie. Okafor's men. Of course. Calloway money moving toward dead country had finally gotten curious enough to send voices ahead of bodies.

Nadia held the handset out to me, eyebrows up. *Your client, your problem,* said the eyebrows.

I took it. "Bravo-Kilo copies. Go ahead."

"There you are, Ms. Iversen." The voice smiled audibly. "Routine check. Syndicate policy—all funded expeditions report position weekly. You're overdue by nine days."

"Weather delays. River crossings. Your dispatch office has the amended schedule."

"Mm. And your position?"

I looked north, at the adit, invisible from here behind the shoulder of the hill. I looked at Elias, asleep or pretending it, propped against the truck's wheel well with the ammunition case sitting beside him like a sleeping dog nobody wanted to wake.

"Thirty klicks south of the toll bridge," I said. "Heading back. Client's health turned. We're running for the mission clinic at San Cristóbal."

A pause. Long enough to be a message itself.

"That's unfortunate. Mr. Calloway values Mr. Wren's wellbeing. We'll want to assist. Perhaps an escort to meet you—"

"Not necessary. Roads are bad. Extra vehicles just churn them up. I'll have him at the clinic in two days."

Another pause. Somewhere on the other end, a pen clicked.

"Two days," the voice repeated pleasantly. "We'll hold you to that, Ms. Iversen. Mr. Okafor says a woman with your record keeps her word. Over and out."

I set the handset down. My hands were steady. That was the worst part—they were always steady now, steadier than they'd been when I was honest.

Nadia was watching me. "Two days," she said.

"Two days."

"San Cristóbal is four days' drive from here."

"Three and a half if I break every rule I have."

"And if we don't go to San Cristóbal?"

I crouched by the wheel well and started cleaning the grease off my hands with a rag, because it's easier to think with your hands busy and easier to lie with your face aimed elsewhere. "Then I've lied to armed men about the position of a dying witness carrying evidence against my creditors, and when they figure it out—and they will, because Okafor doesn't lose arguments with geography—we're a dot in flood country with no roads worth the name and a head start measured in hours."

"So we go to the clinic."

"We go wherever Elias tells us to go," I said. "It's his cargo. It's his grave if it goes wrong. I promised him the place, and we're at the place, and as far as I'm concerned my contract—" I stopped. Because Elias had opened his eyes, and he was looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse and didn't enjoy trying to.

"One more day," he said.

"Elias—"

"The papers need sorting. Sealing. There are names in them—names of families who were paid off, names of men who helped dig the hole. If they reach the wrong hands half-sorted, they hurt the wrong people." He pushed himself upright, and it cost him visibly, and he kept talking through it. "One more day. Then we run."

"And the clinic?" Nadia asked. "The lie?"

"The clinic is real," Elias said. "Eventually. Everything ends at a clinic, one way or another." He almost laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and Nadia was up and holding the water cup before I could move, and I watched the two of them—seven years of silence between them, and her hands steadier around the cup than his were around anything—and felt the weight settle onto my spine where it had apparently decided to live.

One more day. One more lie. The interest compounding daily, same as the debt.

* * *

That night I sat up with the rifle across my knees and the fire banked low, and I listened to the country breathe. Off to the southeast, past the ridge, there was heat lightning without thunder—dry storms, all show, no rain. The season was turning. I could feel it in the way the air had begun to carry weight again, that faint electric pressure that means somewhere north of the horizon the monsoon is loading its gun.

In two days, Okafor's men would either meet a truck at a mission clinic or they wouldn't. In two days, Elias Wren would either be a client or a corpse or both. And somewhere in a canvas roll and an ammunition case ten feet from where I sat was enough truth to buy my freedom ten times over—or to spend it all on a magistrate whose gratitude paid no debts I owed.

I checked the rifle. Checked the sky. Checked the fire.

Behind me, in the dark, Elias began to cough again, and did not stop for a long time.
