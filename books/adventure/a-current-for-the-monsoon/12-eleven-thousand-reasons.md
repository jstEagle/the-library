## 12. Eleven Thousand Reasons

The satchel weighed four pounds, maybe five. I know because I held it in both hands for the better part of an hour, standing in the lee of the truck with the dry wash hissing below me, trying to guess its weight the way you'd guess the weight of a thing you were thinking about throwing away.

Four pounds of paper. Photographs gone amber at the edges. A confession signed in a hand that shook so badly the signature looked like a seismograph reading. And somewhere out there, a man named Okafor who would pay — I did the arithmetic the way other people pray, automatically, without deciding to — call it a hundred and ten thousand for the privilege of feeding it into a stove.

Eleven thousand was my number. Eleven thousand was the gap between me and a life where the phone ringing at three in the morning meant weather instead of money. A hundred and ten thousand was a life where the phone never rang at all, because I'd have bought a house so far from any road that nobody who wanted something from me could find it.

I set the satchel on the tailgate. I opened it. I told myself I was checking that the contents had survived the climb, which was even true, the way a lie is true when you rehearse it enough times on the walk over.

* * *

Let me tell you how the arithmetic actually works, because the arithmetic is the whole story, and I've never told it to anyone. Not Nadia. Not Elias, who was sleeping off the climb with his lungs rattling like a truck on a corrugated road. Not the magistrate I'd never met, whose name I'd only learned four hours ago and whose integrity was, as far as I knew, a rumor told by a dying man.

Item one: the debt. Calloway didn't lend money the way banks do, with paperwork and patience. Calloway lent money the way weather happens to you. My truck — my rig, my radar, my whole rolling argument that I was good at something — sat under a lien the size of a small country's budget. Every bounty I chased went through their hands first, and their hands were shaped like funnels. Eleven thousand was what remained after seven years of chasing cells across the flats for a syndicate that owned the sky I drove under. Eleven thousand, and the dry season had maybe three weeks left in it, and the monsoon country doesn't pay bounties to people who drown.

Item two: the offer I hadn't been made yet but could write myself. Okafor had caught us at the toll bridge with the easy charm of a man who'd never once needed to raise his voice. He'd asked questions with the texture of small talk. He'd let me lie to him and I'd had the distinct, sickening sense that he'd enjoyed it — the way a chess player enjoys watching you hang your own queen. A man like that doesn't need the satchel. A man like that needs the *absence* of the satchel, and Calloway money has never once failed to find the exact price of an absence. Founder's name on the confession. Founder's name on the warrants that would follow. You don't lowball a number like that. You don't even negotiate. You pay what makes the problem stop existing.

Item three, the item I kept trying to demote to a footnote: Elias Wren was dying. Not dying the way people say it at funerals — dying the way a storm cell dies, visibly, structurally, with the whole shape of it coming apart in real time. He'd climbed to that adit on legs that had no business carrying him and come back down looking like a man who'd paid for the view with everything in his account. He had maybe a week. Maybe less. And dead men, I'm sorry to report, are terrible at collecting on promises. Dead men can't testify that Beatrix Iversen swore an oath in a mine shaft. Dead men leave satchels that are, legally speaking, four pounds of unowned paper.

That was the thought. That was the thought I had, alone, with the wash hissing and the light going copper over the ridge, and I want to be honest about it because I've spent my whole life being honest about everything except the things that matter: for about ninety seconds, the thought didn't disgust me.

It felt like relief.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about desperation: it doesn't look like a man in an alley with a knife. It looks like competence. It looks like a woman doing calculations at a tailgate with a pen she keeps behind her ear, running numbers she's run a thousand times, except this time the numbers have a different shape and she tells herself it's just due diligence. Just knowing her options. Just being thorough, because thorough is what she is, thorough is the one thing she's ever been.

I did the math three times. I want you to understand that. Three times, standing there, with the satchel open and the confession on top like a lid on a well.

Run one: sell to Calloway. One hundred ten, minus the eleven I owed, minus whatever Okafor skimmed for the privilege of transacting with me — call it ten percent, he struck me as a ten-percent man — and I walked away with eighty-eight thousand and a permanent subscription to looking over my shoulder, because men like Okafor don't burn the evidence and then forget the woman who carried it. You become the loose end. Loose ends get tied. But eighty-eight thousand buys a long way to run.

Run two: deliver to the magistrate. Okonkwo's gratitude, Elias had said, as if gratitude were a currency with an exchange rate. Maybe the magistrate could shake the lien — a warrant against Calloway's founder would freeze a lot of assets, maybe including the paper my truck was stapled to. Maybe. Law moves like groundwater, slow and in the dark and not necessarily in your direction. Meanwhile Okafor finds out, and Okafor's alternative to the envelope was never stated but was perfectly legible anyway. I'd seen the men he traveled with. They carried themselves like furniture that could walk.

Run three: the one I didn't want to write down even in my head. Sell to Calloway, take the eighty-eight, and don't run. Stay. Buy the debt twice over, keep the truck, keep the road, keep the version of my life where I was free because I'd paid for it — never mind what I'd paid *with*. Nadia would never know. Elias would be dead before the ink dried. The confession named a founder who was already a monument, a man with buildings named after him, and monuments don't fall over because one dying prospector kept receipts. The world would go on exactly as it was, minus four pounds of paper, and I would go on exactly as I was, plus eighty-eight thousand reasons to never think about it again.

Three runs. Three clean columns. And the terrible thing — the thing I'm setting down here because this chapter of my life deserves a witness, even if the witness is just me — is that run three didn't lose on the merits. It lost on a technicality. It lost because I imagined Nadia's face, and my imagination turned out to have a conscience my checkbook didn't.

* * *

I closed the satchel. I sat on the tailgate. The wash had stopped hissing — the water in it was so low it moved in whispers now, the dry season eating the river a finger-width a day, and somewhere downstream that same river was rising toward the mission town where we'd take Elias if his lungs gave out before his legs did. Everything out here was the same water, just at different points in its accounting.

Nadia was twenty meters up the slope, at the fire, re-inking her survey of the wash with the particular concentration she brought to maps and to nothing else — the concentration of a woman drawing borders around a country she didn't live in anymore. She'd barely spoken to her father since the adit. She didn't know what was in the satchel. She knew there was a satchel, and she knew her father had gone into a hole in the ground with me and come out lighter by something that wasn't ore, and she was doing what she always did with the distances she couldn't close: she was measuring them.

If I sold it, she'd never know that either. That was the seduction of it. Not the money — the money was just the number the seduction wore. The seduction was the erasure. One clean transaction and the whole rotten week un-happened: the adit, the confession, the photographs of men who'd been alive on a Tuesday and were a crater by Wednesday, the seven years Elias had carried it all in a box in a hole in the ground because he couldn't carry it in his chest anymore. I could make it un-happened. I was the only person positioned to make it un-happened, and Calloway would pay a premium for exactly that position, and wasn't that — I remember the thought arriving with its little bow tie on — wasn't that just the market working? Wasn't that just supply meeting demand, a woman with a satchel meeting an organization with a stove?

I got up. I walked to the edge of the wash. I looked at the water, which was barely a water anymore, more of a rumor the river had left behind, and I thought about the crossing at the flooded ford a week back, the one that nearly took Nadia, the one where Elias stood on the bank and watched and said nothing — and I realized I'd been reading that silence wrong. I'd thought he was a hard man watching a hard thing. He was a guilty man watching the river do what rivers do to things people bury. Everything out here comes back down. The water takes the mountain apart a grain at a time and delivers it to the sea, and the sea doesn't negotiate, and the sea doesn't take ten percent.

I came back to the tailgate. I put the satchel in the cab, under the seat, under the blanket I kept there for the radar gear. Then I sat back down and did the honest thing, which was to notice that I had not decided anything. I had moved an object four feet. The arithmetic was still in me, all three columns of it, and it would still be in me tomorrow, and the day after, right up until the moment I put it in front of Okonkwo or put it in front of Okafor, and I would never tell a living soul which way it had leaned for ninety seconds on a copper evening in the dry wash country. Some debts you pay in money. This one I was going to pay in silence, and silence, it turns out, is the one currency I've never been able to counterfeit.

* * *

The light went. I made coffee I didn't drink. Nadia came down the slope at full dark and stood at the tailgate, and for a second I thought — the way you think anything at that hour, with the arithmetic still humming in you like a live wire — *she knows.* But she just looked at the truck, and then at me, and said, "He's asking for you."

Elias was propped against his bedroll with his back against the wheel of the trailer, because lying flat made the fluid win. His face in the firelight was the map of a country that had been surveyed to death. He took my wrist. His grip was nothing. It was like being held by a current of air.

"You did the math," he said. Not a question. A man who'd spent seven years not telling anyone anything develops an ear for the things people don't say.

"I looked at the options," I said, which was true, and which was the most cowardly sentence I'd spoken in a week, and I've spoken some cowardly ones.

"Good," he said. "You should always look. Looking's free." He coughed, a long wet business, and when it cleared his eyes had gone bright and fixed, the way they'd gone in the adit. "The looking doesn't matter, Miss Iversen. It's the last thing you do that counts. Make sure the last thing is the right thing, and the looking stays between you and God."

He closed his eyes. I sat there with his hand still on my wrist, light as a moth, and understood that he had just forgiven me for something I hadn't done yet, and that the forgiveness was heavier than the satchel.

I didn't sleep. Around three, the radio on the dash crackled with the kind of static that means weather building over the high country — the season's first cells, still two days out, maybe three. And around four, from up the slope, Nadia's voice went up an octave, sharp with a fear I'd only heard from her once before, at a flooded ford.

I was moving before I'd finished deciding to, which was, when I thought about it later, the first honest thing I'd done all day.
