## 13. The Long Way Down

My father had a theory about mountains, which he shared with me exactly twice in my childhood and never once in the seven years after: that the way down kills more people than the way up, because going up you're still hungry, and hunger keeps you honest about your limits. Coming down, you're satisfied. You've already won. You start spending strength you don't have.

He said this on the descent from a ridge neither of us should have been on, my mother furious in the valley below, and he said it the way he said most true things — quietly, to the landscape, as if the landscape had asked.

He collapsed a little after noon on the fourth day down, in the scree field below the saddle, and I understood immediately that he had known. That he had known for some time. That the whole expedition — the map, the money, the stubborn arithmetic of a dying man's schedule — had been a race between two clocks, his body and the dry season, and that his body had won by losing.

"El," Beatrix said, which was the first time I'd heard anyone shorten his name, and the familiarity of it hit me sideways. She was already kneeling, two fingers at his throat, her other hand peeling back his eyelid like she'd done this before, more than once, in worse places. "El. Stay with the truck, Nadia."

"There is no truck."

"Then stay with the truck anyway."

He was conscious. That was the terrible part — he was conscious and apologetic, muttering about the grade, about how he'd misjudged the scree, as if he'd merely miscalculated and not, as was obvious to anyone with eyes, been running on fumes and calcium deposits for a week. His lips were the color of the slate we were standing on. His hands, when I took them, were cold in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"We need a stretcher," I said. "We need—"

"We need the mission clinic at San Rafael," Beatrix said. "Fourteen kilometers. There's a road at the bottom of this wash that the mission keeps graded. Ivo can bring the second vehicle up." She was already rigging the tarp into a litter with the economy of someone who had done this, too, and I felt the old familiar sting of it — the realization, arriving again and again like a bill, that Beatrix Iversen had a whole life I'd never seen, a whole competence, and that she measured out information about herself the way my father measured out truth: in thimbles, and only when the alternative was worse.

We carried him down. It took three hours. He weighed almost nothing, which is its own kind of horror, a man who had been a wall of shoulders in my earliest memories reduced to a bundle of sticks and apology. He talked the whole way, in fragments. Survey bearings. The names of creeks that hadn't existed when I was born. Once, clearly, he said my mother's name, and then said, "No — the other ledger, the blue one," and said nothing else for twenty minutes.

At the bottom of the wash, the road appeared like a promise kept, and Ivo came up it in the supply truck at a speed that would have gotten him arrested in any country with functioning law, which, out here, was a category of country that included this one only on paper.

* * *

The mission at San Rafael was a church, a dormitory, a water tower, and a clinic with four beds, run by a nun named Sister Bernadette who had forearms like a dockworker and the bedside manner of a customs official. She took one look at my father, said "heart, probably, and everything else," and started an IV with the tenderness of someone racking a shotgun.

"He'll live," she told me later, in the corridor that smelled of carbolic and dust. "For a definition of live. His heart is a bad tenant. He's been evicting himself for years." She looked at me over her glasses. "You're the daughter?"

"I'm the daughter."

"He never mentions a daughter."

"He never mentions anything. That was our arrangement."

Something moved behind her face — not pity, exactly. Recognition, maybe. She was too professional to offer it and I was too proud to take it, so we stood in the corridor in a stalemate of mutual restraint until Beatrix came out of the ward and the moment dissolved.

My father slept for eighteen hours. When he woke, he asked for me first.

I want to record that accurately, because later I would spend a long time being angry, and it's important to be angry about the right things. He asked for me first. He held my hand with a grip that had maybe a quarter of its old strength and still felt like the strongest thing in the room, and he said, "Nadia. You came."

"You asked."

"You didn't have to come."

"No," I said. "You didn't have to burn her letters, either. We've both been very free about it."

He closed his eyes. I thought, with a spike of guilt, that I'd tired him, and leaned forward to take it back, and he said, "Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Take it back. Don't take any of it back. You're angry and you're right to be angry and I'm not going to fix it in the time I have, which is less than I've let anyone believe." His eyes opened, and they were clear in a way that frightened me more than the collapse had. Dying men get a terrible lucidity. It's like watching someone spend their last coin. "I'm not going to ask you for anything. I want you to hear that. I'm not going to ask you to forgive me, or to stay, or to carry what I'm carrying. I've spent seven years asking people for things and it's how I got here. So: nothing. I ask you for nothing. You're free."

"That's not — you don't get to unilaterally—"

"Nadia." He smiled, and it was my father's smile, the one from the ridge, the one aimed at the landscape. "You were always the one who read the fine print. I'm telling you what's in it."

I sat with him until he slept again, furious in a way that had no purchase, because he had done the one thing I couldn't argue with. He had asked me for nothing. He had handed me my freedom like a man handing back a tool he'd borrowed, and I sat there holding it and discovering that freedom, unasked-for, weighs more than obligation.

* * *

He asked Beatrix for everything.

I know because I was outside the door. I know because the mission clinic's walls were board and tin and Iversen's voice, when it finally came, was pitched for the corridor as much as the bed, whether she knew it or not.

"You'll take it to Okonkwo," my father said. Not a question. I heard the satchel's buckle — that small brass sound I'd been hearing for two weeks without knowing what it guarded.

"That was the deal," Beatrix said. "Delivery before the dry season ends."

"No. The deal was you'd carry it. Carrying and delivering are different verbs, Ms. Iversen, and I've watched you conjugate both. I want you to say it plain. You'll put it in his hands. Not a copy. Not a summary. Not a negotiated settlement where the truth comes out looking like something else. His hands."

A silence long enough to count your own heartbeats in. I counted eleven.

"His hands," Beatrix said.

"And if I don't make it—"

"You'll make it."

"Nadia doesn't know what it is."

Another silence. This one had a different texture, and I stood in the corridor with my hand flat against the warm tin and felt, before I understood it, the shape of the thing underneath.

"She'll know," Beatrix said. "When it's done. She'll know everything."

"She'll hate you."

"She might."

"She should," my father said, and there was something in his voice that was almost humor, the gallows kind, the kind that runs in families like eye color. "You're the one holding the bag. That's how it works. The messenger gets the arrows. I got to be the coward at leisure."

"You weren't a coward, El."

"I was a survivor," he said, "which is cowardice with better marketing," and then his voice dropped below the register of the tin, and whatever he said next, he said only to her, and I only heard the shape of it — a rising question, her low answer, his silence, and then, distinctly, terribly, my father crying. Not loudly. The way men of his generation cried, which was like a pipe leaking inside a wall: you didn't see it, you heard it, and you had to decide whether to open the wall.

I opened the wall.

Beatrix was standing at the foot of the bed with her arms folded, and my father's eyes were shut, and neither of them acknowledged me, which was its own acknowledgment. The satchel sat on the blanket between his hands, and Beatrix's hand was resting on it, lightly, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you weren't sure was tame.

"Nadia," my father said, without opening his eyes. "The corridor has poor acoustics. I'm sorry."

"How much," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to, "doesn't she know that you do?"

Beatrix looked at me then. And I want to be fair to her, because in the weeks since I have had occasion to be fair to her at length, and fairness was never my strongest subject — I inherited my mother's gift for the map and my father's gift for the omission, and between the two of them I can draw you any territory except my own. But in that moment Beatrix Iversen did not lie to me. She didn't tell the truth either. She did the thing that I have since learned is the hardest thing in her considerable repertoire: she stood still and let me look at her and draw my own conclusions, and my conclusion was that she knew everything, and that everything was heavier than gold, and that my father had chosen a stranger to carry it because a stranger could put it down.

"Ask him," Beatrix said quietly. "Not me. Him."

"I'm asking you."

"Then I'm not answering. Some things you only get to hear once, and you should hear them from the source." She picked up the satchel, and — I'll remember this — she held it out to me first. Held it out, handle toward me, an offer, a test, I don't know which. "Do you want to carry it a while? See how it sits?"

I looked at the satchel. Old canvas, brass buckles, the kind of bag surveyors used before my time, the kind my mother's company had used, and something went off in me like a flashbulb — a photograph of our hallway, the year I was nine, this exact bag on a hook by the door, my mother's handwriting on the tag.

"No," I said.

"No," my father agreed from the bed, eyes still closed. "She doesn't. Not yet. That's the whole point of the yet."

* * *

Sister Bernadette found me in the chapel at dusk, which was not a chapel so much as a room with a cross and excellent acoustics, and sat down beside me on the pew with the sigh of a woman whose knees had opinions.

"Your father is sleeping. The driver is fixing the truck's fan belt, which does not need fixing, because he is a man who prays with his hands. The other one—" she tilted her head toward the corridor, toward the ward, "—is on the roof."

"The roof?"

"Storm watcher, she says. There's a cell building over the eastern escarpment. She wanted to watch it come in." Bernadette folded her hands. "I have buried forty people on this hill, Miss Wren. Farmers, miners, smugglers, two magistrates, one bishop. You learn to hear the confessions people make without words. Your father has been making his to that woman since he could no longer make them to himself. Whatever is in that bag, she did not ask for it. People who ask for burdens are rare, and people who accept them without asking are rarer, and I have met perhaps three in my life."

"She's in debt," I said. "To the Calloway people. I did the arithmetic off her fuel receipts and her bounty payouts. She's drowning. Whatever my father's paying her, it isn't enough to matter, and whatever's in that bag is worth enough to matter to the wrong people, and she's standing on a roof watching a storm instead of running."

"Yes," said the nun. "I noticed that too."

We sat with it. Outside, the light went the color of old brass, and the first far thunder came off the escarpment like something clearing its throat.

"He asked you for nothing," Bernadette said. It wasn't a question; the walls had told her too, or my face had. "And her for everything. Do you understand why?"

"Because I'd have said no."

"Because you'd have said yes," she said, "and he couldn't have borne it. It's different. Saying no to your child is the one cruelty a man will refuse even to save himself. Saying yes to a stranger costs him only his shame, and he's been carrying that so long he's built a house in it." She stood, knees cracking like green wood. "Come. Eat something. Grief is a labor, and laborers who skip meals fall off roofs."

"I'm not grieving yet."

"No," she agreed. "You're rehearsing. It's worse."

* * *

I went up to the roof after dark, because of course I did. I am my mother's daughter and my mother never once left a map unread, and a roof in storm country at dusk is a map of a kind.

Beatrix was at the north edge, binoculars down, watching the cell walk across the escarpment — a big one, organized, the anvil sheared flat by shear, lightning ticking inside it like a faulty fuse. She didn't startle when I came up beside her. I don't think Beatrix startles. I think she files the startle away for later, where it can't inconvenience anyone.

"You knew," I said. "Before the adit. You knew what he was carrying, or you guessed, and you took the job anyway, and you let me walk into this blind."

"Yes," she said.

"That's it? Yes?"

"You want the speech where I explain myself? I don't have one. He offered me money I needed for a job I didn't ask questions about, and I signed a contract with a clause I didn't read, and the clause was you." She said it flat, no apology in it, and I realized that was the most respectful thing she could have done. Beatrix Iversen did not insult me with comfort. "He didn't tell me what was in the satchel until the adit. But I knew it wasn't gold by the second week. Nobody pays a syndicate's rates and hires a storm chaser and burns journal pages at midnight over gold. Gold you dig up and carry out and spend. This — whatever this is — he wanted carried somewhere specific, to someone specific, by someone specific. I just didn't know the someone was a magistrate and the something was a grave."

"A grave," I repeated.

She was quiet for a moment. Below us, the mission lights made a small yellow island, and beyond it the dark came down off the hills in sheets as the storm front arrived, still miles out, still all sound and no rain.

"Your father was the seventh man of a seven-man crew," Beatrix said. "I'll say that much, because you'll get the rest from him or from Okonkwo's paperwork, and either way it'll hit you like a truck, and maybe knowing the shape of the truck helps. The other six didn't come home. He did. And what's in the satchel is the reason, and the reason has a name, and the name is on a building in the capital." She turned and looked at me, and the lightning lit her from behind so her face was all shadow, which felt accurate. "He made me promise to deliver it. He made you promise nothing. I've spent the last hour trying to decide which of us he loves more, and I've decided that's the wrong question. He did for each of us the only kind thing he had left. Don't waste it being angry at the packaging."

"And if I want to be angry at the packaging anyway?"

"Get in line," Beatrix said. "I've been angry at him since the toll bridge. It's a good anger. It keeps your arms strong for the carrying." She lifted the binoculars, then lowered them. "Nadia. There's something else. I'd tell you I didn't want to tell you, but you'd know I was lying, so: Okafor's people are on the roads south of here. Ivo heard it from a fuel runner at midday. They're not looking for your father anymore. They're looking for the satchel. And they know what clinic he's in."

The thunder arrived then, the front edge of it, a long rolling concussion that I felt in my sternum, and under it, faintly, the mission's generator stuttered and caught.

"So we move him," I said.

"So we move him," Beatrix agreed. "Tomorrow, before first light, the back way, the one that isn't on your father's maps because I made it up myself. And Nadia — when we run, you run with us. Not because he asked. Because I'm asking, and I'm told I only do that once."

Below us, in the ward, my father slept the sleep of a man whose ledgers were finally balanced — except the last entry, the big one, still open, the ink not yet dry. I stood on the roof with a woman I had known for three weeks and trusted further than I could account for, and watched the storm come on like a verdict, and understood that whatever my father had buried in that mine, the dangerous part wasn't what was in the satchel.

It was that now there were two of us who knew it existed.
