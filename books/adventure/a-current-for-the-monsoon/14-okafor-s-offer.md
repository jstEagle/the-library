## 14. Okafor's Offer

The mission clinic smelled of carbolic and old paint, and Elias Wren was dying in room four with the same stubbornness he'd used to stay alive for seven years after he should have stopped.

I'd been sitting in the corridor outside his door for three hours, watching a ceiling fan turn in circles it clearly regretted, when I decided I needed air. That's what I told myself, anyway. What I actually needed was distance—from the satchel under my chair, from Nadia's red-rimmed eyes, from the arithmetic that had started running in my head somewhere on the mountain descent and hadn't stopped since.

Eleven thousand had been my number for two years. Eleven thousand and I was free of Calloway. And now I was sitting on something the syndicate would pay ten times that to make disappear, and I hadn't told a soul how many times I'd turned that fact over in the dark like a stone looking for its good side.

I stepped out into the parking lot because the alternative was thinking honestly, and I wasn't ready.

The lot was gravel and heat shimmer, a handful of vehicles baking under a sky the color of unpolished tin. Dry season, late afternoon, the light going long and amber across the mission wall. Somewhere behind me a generator coughed. Somewhere ahead of me, leaning against my truck as if he owned it—and in a way he did—stood Mr. Okafor.

I should explain about Okafor. He was Calloway's problem-solver, which meant he was everyone else's problem. A big man gone soft at the edges the way dangerous men go soft, with reading glasses he wore on a chain around his neck so people would underestimate him, and hands that had never once been underestimated by anyone who'd felt them. He dressed like a man attending a funeral he was pleased about: grey suit, grey shirt, no tie, because ties were for men who needed help looking serious.

"Miss Iversen." He didn't push off the truck. He just stood there, comfortable, the way a man stands when he knows you can't leave without walking past him. "You look tired."

"I look like I've spent two weeks in flood country, Mr. Okafor. Same thing."

"It is the same thing," he agreed pleasantly. "That's why I said it."

I stopped maybe ten feet away, near enough to be polite, far enough to make a point I couldn't have named. My keys were in my pocket. His hand was resting on my hood. The geometry of the situation was not subtle.

"Heard you had some excitement upriver," he said. "Flash floods. A crossing went bad. Then old man Wren goes down on the descent and here you all are, tucked up in a church clinic like orphans." He smiled. Okafor's smiles were architectural—they held things up. "Calloway pays your retainer, Miss Iversen. We like to know where our money sleeps."

"My money sleeps where I tell it to."

"Mm." He took off his glasses, breathed on a lens, polished it with a handkerchief that appeared from nowhere. It was theater. Everything Okafor did was theater, and the genius of it was that the performance never once contradicted the threat underneath. "Elias Wren. You know, I looked him up when the wire came through. Seven years ago this man walks out of the green country half-dead, and Calloway—generous company that we are—settles a claim on him. Quietly. No fuss. Most men would call that charity."

"Most men would call it hush money."

Okafor put his glasses back on and looked at me over the top of them, which was somehow worse than through them. "And there it is," he said softly. "There's the question, isn't it? What does Elias Wren know that was worth seven years of quiet?"

The generator coughed again. A lizard made a run across the gravel between us, thought better of it, reversed.

"I'm a driver," I said. "He hired a driver. I drive."

"You're a storm chaser with a debt," Okafor said. "Let's not insult each other. Insulting each other is for people who haven't read each other's files." He pushed off the truck then, finally, and came toward me with his hands loose at his sides, an ambling, friendly walk that I had heard described by two separate witnesses who both used the word *unhurried* and both shuddered saying it. "Here's what I know. Wren is dying. Wren hired you—the woman who owes us eleven thousand—to take him somewhere. And now you're standing in a parking lot looking like a woman doing sums she doesn't like."

"And what do you do, Mr. Okafor, when you see a woman doing sums?"

"I offer her a calculator."

He produced the envelope from inside his jacket. Not dramatically. That was the thing about Okafor—he never did anything dramatically. He handed you ruin the way a bank teller hands you a withdrawal slip, and somehow you ended up feeling grateful for the efficiency.

I didn't take it. "What is it?"

"It's a number." He held it out anyway, patient, letting it hang in the space between us. "Open it later if you're shy. But I'll say the figure aloud, because I want you to hear it in a human voice and not just read it in ink. Twenty-six thousand."

The number landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. Twenty-six thousand. More than double what I owed. Enough to clear Calloway and still have enough left over to buy back the truck outright, replace every tire, and drive until the maps ran out. Enough to be a free woman by nightfall.

"For what?" I asked, though we both knew.

"The satchel. Whatever's in it. Photographs, papers, whatever the old man buried out there—I don't know and I genuinely don't want to know. I want it burned, and I want confirmation it's burned, and then I want you to forget Elias Wren ever learned to write." He smiled again, warmer this time, a man offering a favor. "In exchange, your account with Calloway closes tonight. Zeroed. And the difference is yours, cash, no questions—which I understand is a term you have personal experience appreciating."

"No questions," I repeated. "That's how Wren bought me too."

"Yes," Okafor said. "We noticed. It's a company phrase. We're rather proud of it."

A wind had come up off the flats, carrying dust and the faint mineral smell of a season waiting to break. The first cells were building out past the escarpment—I could feel it in my teeth the way I always could, pressure stacking up like debt. Somewhere inside the clinic, Nadia was holding her father's hand and asking him questions he was too weak to answer, and I was standing in a parking lot being offered twenty-six thousand reasons to make sure she never got any.

"Mr. Okafor," I said. "Why are you being polite?"

His smile flickered—not much, a hairline crack in the architecture—and reassembled itself. "Because politeness is cheaper than the alternative, and I am, above all things, a cost-conscious man."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." He folded his hands in front of him, glasses catching the low sun, twin orange coins. "But since you ask for honesty, here is the rest of it. If the number doesn't move you, then tomorrow the number becomes different. Not bigger. Smaller. Because what happens next, Miss Iversen, is that the conversation stops being about money and starts being about consequences, and I have found that most people prefer money. Money ends. Consequences—" he spread his hands, a small apology to the universe "—consequences compound."

"There it is," I said. "The interest rate."

"There it is," he agreed, delighted, as if I'd gotten a joke right. "You always did understand the terms."

I looked at the envelope. I want to be honest here, because the whole point of this chapter of my life—and I've had time to reread it—is that I was not a good woman making a hard choice. I was a tired woman holding a bag of money in her eyes and weighing it against a dead man's wish and a living woman's grief, and the scale did not sit level. For a long moment—longer than I'll ever admit to Nadia, longer than I admitted to myself at the time—the only thing that kept my hand at my side was pure, stupid inertia. The physics of it. My arm simply declined to rise, the way a stalled engine declines to turn over, and while I sat there refusing to start, I thought: *twenty-six thousand. Nadia would never have to know. Elias will be dead by Friday either way. Dead men don't audit.*

That was the voice. That was the exact voice, and I'm writing it down because leaving it out would be a lie, and I've decided—late, expensively—that I'm finished lying in my own head.

"Take your time," Okafor said gently. "I mean that sincerely. Deliberation is a virtue. But I'll need your answer by morning. The dry season ends soon, and the company has... seasonal obligations."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that when the rains come, the roads close, and after the roads close, certain conversations become impossible to have." He tucked the envelope into my jacket pocket himself—he was that close before I registered him moving, that fast, that practiced—and patted it flat over my heart like a doctor checking for a beat. "Consider it a deposit on your decision. Either way, it spends the same."

"That's not how deposits work."

"It's exactly how ours work." He stepped back, straightened his cuffs, became once more entirely a grey-suited gentleman at a funeral he was pleased about. "Give my regards to the old man. And to his daughter—she has his eyes, I'm told. Stubborn eyes. The whole family is stubborn; it's practically a geological feature." He walked past me toward a black sedan idling at the lot's edge, then paused, and without turning around said: "One more thing, Miss Iversen. Free of charge, because I like you. Magistrate Okonkwo is an honest man. Do you know what happens to honest men in this province?"

"They get promoted," I said. "Eventually. Slowly. After everyone who isn't honest is dead."

Okafor laughed—a real laugh, brief and genuine, the worst sound I'd heard all day. "They get buried," he said. "Slowly. Before everyone who isn't honest is dead. Think about whose funeral you're dressing for." And then he was in the sedan, and the sedan was dust, and I was alone in a parking lot with an envelope over my heart and a satchel full of a dead crew's names thirty feet away through a clinic wall.

I stood there until the sun touched the escarpment.

Then I took out the envelope and opened it, because apparently I wanted to know exactly how heavy the stone was before I decided whether to carry it or throw it. Banded notes, crisp, the kind that come from banks and not from pockets. I counted it twice, mechanically, the way I check tie-downs before a storm run. Twenty-six thousand, give or take the courtesy rounding.

Enough to erase me. Enough to remake me. Enough to buy the version of Beatrix Iversen who never has to calculate anything again, who drives wherever she likes, who owes nobody, who could take Nadia Wren anywhere in the world she wanted to go and pay for the fuel with money that didn't smell like anything at all.

That last thought was the dirtiest one, and I knew it, and I had it anyway. That's the thing about temptation—it doesn't knock. It moves in and helps with the dishes.

Behind me, the clinic door banged open. Nadia stood in the doorway backlit by the corridor's yellow light, her father's journal clutched against her chest, and even across forty feet of gravel I could see that whatever was happening inside room four had changed the shape of her face.

"Beatrix," she called. "He's awake. He's asking for you." A pause, and then, quieter, in a voice I felt in my spine: "Both of us. He says there's something he should have told us seven years ago."

I looked down at the envelope in my hand. Then at the clinic door. Then at the sky, where the first thunderhead of the season was stacking itself up over the escarpment like a verdict being written in real time.

I put the envelope in my pocket instead of the bin, which tells you everything about where I actually stood—and walked inside to find out what the truth was going to cost me now.
