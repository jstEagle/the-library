## 16. What Elias Wanted

I have spent seven years rehearsing this conversation in the privacy of my own skull, and I want it on the record that every rehearsal went better than the live performance.

In rehearsal, I was eloquent. In rehearsal, Nadia sat down without being asked, and the light through the mission clinic's one good window fell kindly across her face, and I said what needed saying in an order that made sense, and she wept exactly as much as a daughter ought to weep and no more, and forgiveness arrived like a scheduled train.

In reality, she stood in the doorway with her arms crossed for four full minutes before she came to the bed, and the light was wrong, and my mouth tasted like the copper they'd been feeding me intravenously, and the first thing I managed to say out loud—the first words of my great confession, the sum total of seventy-one years—was:

"You're standing in the drip line."

There is a gutter above the clinic window that the brothers never fixed. When it rains—and it had begun, that evening, a thin preliminary drizzle, the monsoon clearing its throat—a bead of water forms at the gutter's seam and lets go directly onto the tile where a person might stand if they were hovering at the edge of a room, deciding whether to enter it.

She looked up at the ceiling. She looked down at me. "You're dying," she said, "and you're worried about the plumbing."

"I've been dying for two years, sweetheart. The plumbing has been broken since the mission was built. One of us can still be fixed."

That got me half a smile, which in the currency of Nadia Wren is a fortune. She dragged the plastic chair over with a screech that made the nurse down the hall click her tongue, and she sat, and she put her hands in her lap the way she used to when she was nine years old and waiting to hear whether she was in trouble.

She was not in trouble. That was the whole point. That was the thing I had to make her understand before the morning, because I could feel the morning coming the way old men feel weather in their joints, except in my case the weather was final.

"Beatrix told you some of it," I said.

"She told me there are records. That you kept them. That they prove something about Calloway." Her jaw worked. "She wouldn't say what."

"No," I said. "She wouldn't. That was my doing. I made her promise not to carry it for me." I took a breath, and it whistled going in, which was new and undignified. "Because it isn't hers to carry. It never should have been mine either. But it is mine, all of it, and if anyone is going to hand you a stone this heavy, it's going to be the man who put it in his own pack."

* * *

Let me set the scene properly, because Nadia inherited my love of maps and maps are useless without context.

The year Nadia was eleven—I'll come back to what she was doing that year; I'll come back to it because I have to—the Seventh Survey Company filed a claim on a stretch of high country east of the Verge: copper, mostly, with a vein of gold running through it like a signature. Seven men and one woman. Good people. Tomás Okafor-Leyva, who sang off-key. Ruth Mbeki, who could read rock strata the way Nadia reads contour lines. A kid named Prewitt, nineteen, who'd lied about his age to get on the crew and wrote letters home that made the rest of us feel like poets by comparison.

The claim was contested. Calloway—the founder himself, not the syndicate his name became, though there was never much difference between the man and the machine—filed a counterclaim built on paperwork so clean it could only have been laundered. The case went to arbitration. The arbitration went nowhere. And then, on the fourth of October, seven years ago this past dry season, the Seventh Survey Company went into a mine adit on their own claim to assess it for the hearing, and somebody sealed the entrance behind them with forty pounds of commercial explosive.

Ruth was outside. She'd gone to check a benchmark. She heard it. She dug for six hours with her hands before the secondary collapse convinced her body what her mind refused to know.

The official finding was structural failure. Unstable country. Tragic. The arbitration lapsed with the claimants dead, and Calloway took the ground, and the ground has been paying him ever since in gold and silence.

I was not in the adit.

I want that understood, and I want it understood immediately, because everything else I am about to confess only makes sense against that single fact: I was not in the adit. I was three miles south, alone, having left the camp the night before after an argument with Tomás about survey methodology that seems, from this distance, like the stupidest argument ever conducted between two human beings. I sulked. I walked off to cool down. And while I was sulking three miles away with my pride keeping me warm, eight of my crewmates were buried alive under forty pounds of somebody else's decision.

Survivor's guilt is a phrase people use to mean they feel bad. What it actually means is this: you spend the rest of your life conducting an audit you can never complete, trying to find the entry in your own ledger that explains why the column marked *you* came out balanced when everyone else's came up short. I audited for seven years. I found nothing. There was nothing to find. That is the cruelest part—that the guilt is arithmetically unjustifiable and completely non-negotiable.

But here is the part I have not said aloud to any living soul, and here is why Beatrix was forbidden to be my messenger: knowing what happened was not enough for me. Knowing passively, the way a bystander knows, would have been bearable. Instead I did something worse.

I investigated.

* * *

Nadia hadn't moved while I laid out the bones of it. Her hands stayed in her lap. But her thumbs were pressing against each other hard enough to blanch, and I recognized the gesture because it was her mother's gesture, Amara's, the way she'd hold herself together during my long silences, which were, I now understand, the visible portion of this same audit.

"You knew them," Nadia said quietly. "The survey crew. You knew them before."

"They were my crew. I was the senior geologist on the Seventh Survey." I watched that land. Watched her do the arithmetic—seven years ago, the accident, the same seven years as our silence, and my God, the coincidence wasn't a coincidence at all, it was the load-bearing wall of our whole estrangement and neither of us had known it. "Your mother knew too. Not everything. But she knew I'd been on the crew, and she knew what the money was that started making us comfortable, and she asked me—one time, only once—whether I'd looked into it. And I lied to her. I said the collapse was an accident and looking into it would be grave-robbing."

"And then you burned her letters."

"After she died. Yes." My voice cracked on the second word and I let it. There was no dignity left to protect; I'd checked. "Because she'd written to her sister about the argument I'd had with Tomás. About how I'd walked off. About how I'd been lucky. And I couldn't—" I stopped. Started again. "Every letter she wrote was a mirror, and I couldn't stand what was in them anymore. So I burned them. Which was its own crime, and you're entitled to hate me for it separately. Don't bundle it in with the rest. Itemize."

A ghost of a laugh went through her, wet and unwilling. "Itemize," she repeated. "You're itemizing your sins."

"I'm a surveyor. We itemize. It's how we keep from getting lost."

* * *

So: the investigation. Here is how a dying man spends seven years and most of a modest fortune.

I went back to the adit site twice, once in the second year and once in the fifth, both times dressed as a lone prospector, which in that country is a costume nobody questions because hope is commoner than sense. The second time I found what the first trip had taught me to look for: detonator wire fragments, fused into the talus, of a gauge and manufacture that matched Calloway's own mining operations and matched nothing registered to the Survey. I found the blasting cap tin, rusted to lace, stamped with a lot number. I spent fourteen months tracing that lot number through three shell companies and one very frightened former quarry clerk who eventually traded me the sales ledger page for enough money to move his family somewhere the syndicate's memory didn't reach.

And then, in the sixth year, I found Ruth Mbeki's field journal. Her sister had kept it in a trunk, unable to read it and unable to throw it away, and when I showed her the detonator evidence she gave it to me with both hands and said, "Finish it, then. Finish it or stop carrying it." Ruth had been outside, remember. Ruth had seen, in the last light, a vehicle on the ridge road. She'd noted the make, the color, the direction, and the time, in her tidy surveyor's hand, because noting things was simply what she did, right up until the world declined to be noted accurately anymore.

Photographs. Affidavits—the clerk's, the sister's, a retired constable's who'd been paid to lose a report. A copy of the arbitration file with Calloway's forged provenance documents side by side with the Survey's originals. And finally, last winter, when I understood the cough was not a cough, I wrote out a signed confession of my own—not of the crime, I didn't commit the crime, but of the knowledge, the years, the burned letters, the money I'd taken and spent and never once asked where it came from. I signed it. I witnessed it. I put it in a waterproof satchel with everything else and I buried it in a mine adit above a dry wash, because I am a sentimental old man and I wanted it hidden in the kind of place that had already taken everything from me once.

"Why there?" Nadia asked. "Why not a bank? Why not just give it to someone?"

"Because banks burn, sweetheart. People change their minds. Dead drops get found." I tapped my own chest, gently, over the heart, where the monitor leads ran. "The only safe place for a thing like that is inside a story nobody wants to hear. A dying prospector dragging two strangers into flood country on a treasure hunt—who follows that story to its end? Nobody. Nobody until now."

* * *

She cried then. Not the way she'd cried at fourteen, loudly, furiously, throwing things. Quietly, the way adults cry, which is worse to witness, because adults cry like people doing accounting.

I let her. I'd earned the silence and anyway I wasn't finished, and the unfinished part was the part that mattered most.

"There's a thing you need to understand about Beatrix," I said, when she could hear me again. "I hired a debt-ridden storm chaser because I needed someone desperate enough to say yes and competent enough to get me there alive. That was the calculation, and it was a cold one, and I won't pretend otherwise. But somewhere on the road I stopped calculating and started hoping, and I want you to know that whatever she does next—whatever she's tempted to do, and she will be tempted, because Mr. Okafor will find her and he will make an offer with real numbers in it—none of that is a verdict on her character. I chose her *because* she's the kind of woman who owes eleven thousand dollars to dangerous men. I don't get to act surprised that the debt followed her onto my expedition."

"That's not fair to her," Nadia said. "Or to you."

"Fairness is for arbitrators. I'm done with arbitration." I reached out and she took my hand, and her grip was stronger than I expected, and I held on like a man holding a benchmark in a wind. "Here is what I want. Not what I need—what I *want*, and I've earned one want, I've checked the ledger. The records go to Magistrate Okonkwo. Not sold. Not leaked. Delivered, by hand, to the one judge in this territory whose kitchen table I've eaten at and whose price list I've read, and the price list is blank. He can't fix everything. Justice moves slower than money; I've had seven years to learn that. But it moves, and it moves in one direction, and that's more than I can say for anything else I've loved."

"And you?" she asked. "What happens to you?"

"I happen at dawn, if the pattern holds. The night nurse says I'm on schedule." I smiled at her, and it cost me, and I paid. "Don't fight the schedule, Nadia. I've read the surveys. This country's flooding."

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't joke."

"It's the only tool I brought that still works." I squeezed her hand. "Listen. The last thing. The thing I actually called you in here for, underneath all the confession. I am not asking you to forgive me. Do you hear me? I am refusing absolution. Whatever you feel in five minutes or five years, it doesn't have to be forgiveness—it can be anger, it can be nothing, it can be a filing error. You don't owe me a clean ending. I spent seven years believing I owed everyone one, and it nearly kept me from doing the one thing that mattered. So take the records, take the truth, take your mother's name back from the fire—and leave the ledger open. Some accounts aren't meant to balance. They're meant to be carried forward."

She bent over my hand then, and I felt her tears on my knuckles, and I did not ask her to stop, and I did not ask her to stay, and I did not ask her anything at all, because asking was finished.

* * *

Beatrix came in sometime after midnight. I heard her arguing softly with the night nurse in the corridor—she has a voice that negotiates with doors—and then she was standing at the foot of the bed with her hat in her hands like a man in church, which was funny, because I'd have bet money she'd never been in one.

"Nadia's asleep in the chair in the hall," she said. "Mostly asleep. She's pretending, so you can talk freely."

"She's pretending because I asked her to rest and she wanted to honor the request without obeying it. She gets that from her mother." I studied Beatrix for a moment. She looked wrung out and lit from inside, both at once, like a lamp with a frayed cord. "Okafor contacted you."

It wasn't a question and she didn't treat it as one. "This afternoon. Parking lot. Envelope." Her chin came up. "I didn't take it."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're standing there hating yourself instead of driving south with a full tank." I let that sit. "Beatrix. Come closer. My voice is going."

She came. I took her wrist—her pulse was fast, always fast, a woman living slightly ahead of herself—and I said what I had left to say, quickly, before the morphine fog rolled back in.

"The envelope will still be there tomorrow. And the day after. Temptation doesn't expire; that's what makes it temptation. But Okonkwo's window closes with the dry season, and the dry season closes in days. Whatever you decide about yourself, decide it *after* the records are on his table. Promise me the order of operations. Justice first, judgment after. Even self-judgment. Especially self-judgment."

Her eyes were bright and furious and scared. "And if I fail?"

"Then you'll fail in excellent company. I failed for seven years." I patted her wrist and released it. "Now go wake my daughter. Tell her the stars are out. She'll want to see them. She gets that from me."

* * *

They came back together, the two of them, and stood at the window a while with the curtain pushed aside, and I listened to them breathe and did not join the conversation because it wasn't mine anymore. Somewhere out in the dark the first real cells of the monsoon were stacking up over the hills, and Beatrix—storm chaser to the marrow—named them aloud for Nadia the way other people name constellations, and Nadia laughed once, small and surprised, and I thought: *there*. There it is. That's the current. That's the thing I buried the map inside of, without ever quite admitting it. Not the gold, not even the truth. That.

I slept. Or something adjacent to sleep.

At 4:40 in the morning I woke clear—completely, cruelly clear, the way the dying sometimes get one last hour of perfect visibility—and Nadia was awake in the chair beside me, watching, because of course she was; she'd inherited that too.

"Elias," she said. Not *Dad*. I noticed, and I let it go, and I blessed her for the honesty of it.

"Third drawer of the bedside table," I said. "Under the Bible the brothers put there, which I never opened, which seemed rude to move."

She pulled out the envelope. Inside was a photograph—the whole Seventh Survey Company, squinting into the sun on the day we staked the claim, eight faces and mine, all of us young and certain the ground loved us back. On the back, in my handwriting, the names. All of them. Including Ruth. Including the boy Prewitt.

"For the memorial," I said. "When it's over. Read the names out loud. Names are the only monuments that work."

"I will."

"And Nadia—" The dawn was starting, gray at the window's edge, the gutter dripping its slow bead. "The records. Beatrix has them. Trust her with them. Not because she's trustworthy—though she is, more than she believes—but because trusting her is the last gift I can give you both, and I intend to spend it."

Outside, far off, thunder cleared its throat.

"Go on," I told her, and smiled, and meant it. "Beat the rain home."

She leaned down and kissed my forehead, and I closed my eyes, and the audit, at long last, closed itself.
