## 17. Running the Dry Season

The mission clinic had a map of the province on the wall of its waiting room, laminated and ten years out of date, and I stood in front of it for an hour on the second night after Elias died, doing what I do best: reading weather in flat things.

Nadia found me there with a marker in my hand and three roads already crossed out.

"You're planning," she said.

"I'm grieving," I said. "It just looks like planning."

She looked at the map. She looked at my crossings-out. She took the marker from me without asking and drew a line that made my stomach drop—not south along the highway where every roadblock Okafor had would be sitting fat and patient, but east, into the green throat, into the flood country we'd barely crawled out of once already, following the river basins up toward the high pass and only then cutting down to Okonkwo's jurisdiction like a thief leaving through the kitchen window.

"That's not a road," I said.

"It's a drainage," she said. "It was a rail spur before the washout of '09. My father surveyed it." Her voice did something small and complicated on *my father*, and I let it do its thing without staring at it. We'd buried Elias two mornings ago under a tree the mission gardener said was older than the town, and neither of us had cried yet, which meant both of us were carrying it somewhere structural, somewhere load-bearing, and the only way through was to keep moving until the weight redistributed or the building fell.

"Okafor will have men on the highway," she said. "Every fuel depot, every toll bridge, every one-road town between here and the capital. He knows you. He knows you think in roads."

"He knows I think in weather," I said. "That's different."

I put my finger on the map, on the empty quarter northeast of the spur, where the cartographers had run out of confidence and just printed hatching. "First cells are coming up from the gulf. Early this year. The dry season doesn't end on a calendar—it ends when the first storm cell decides it has, and everything after that is improvisation. Okafor's men are syndicate men. Syndicate men drive trucks on roads because roads are assets and assets are predictable. Nobody sane drives into the leading edge of a monsoon front."

"Are you sane?"

"Not even slightly. That's the qualification."

* * *

We spent the next day turning my truck into something it was never designed to be and Nadia turned her father's old survey maps into something they were never designed to be either: a route.

The satchel sat between us on the clinic's back step while we worked, that canvas bag with its dead man's confession inside, and I'll admit I kept positioning myself so it was closer to me than to her. Not because I didn't trust her. Because I didn't trust the world, and the world had been winning lately.

"Say it out loud," Nadia said, not looking up from the map she'd weighted flat with four stones. "The plan. All of it. If you keep it in your head it stays a fantasy."

"Fine." I counted on my fingers. "Tonight we load. Before dawn we go north on the mission track—dirt, no checkpoints, nobody watches a road that floods twice a season. We follow it to the old spur junction. From there we're on Elias's drainage, heading east-northeast through the basin country. Two days if nothing goes wrong."

"And when something goes wrong?"

"Then we're already where the rain is. First cells hit the highlands tomorrow night, maybe the night after. Every radio, every satellite feed, every syndicate man with a phone is going to be watching radar and praying their own roof holds. A storm chaser in monsoon season is invisible. It's the one time of year the sky does my lying for me."

Nadia traced the drainage line again, slower. "There's a crossing here." She tapped a place where her pencil hovered over a gap in the contour lines like a dentist hovering over a bad tooth. "The spur crosses the Mkwawa branch. In the dry season it's ankle-deep. In the wet—"

"In the wet it's a different river wearing the same name."

"So we don't cross it."

"We cross it *before* the wet. That's the whole trick. We have to outrun the front to the high ground, then let the front catch us and cover us while we come down the far side. Too early and Okafor's men are still watching clear skies. Too late and the Mkwawa eats the spur and us with it." I looked at her. "There's a window about six hours wide. Maybe eight. Your father's maps give us the elevation, your head gives us the terrain, and my barometer gives us the timing. Three instruments. One reading."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "He used to talk like that. About surveying. Like it was music and he was just transcribing."

"I never heard him talk like that."

"No," she said. "You got the version that had already lost the argument with himself." She rolled the map, tied it with a bootlace, and handed it to me—which I understood, correctly, as a larger gesture than it appeared. "Don't lose it."

* * *

We left at 4 a.m., headlights off, running on moonlight and the mission track's forgiving ruts. The satchel rode in the cab between us, seatbelted in like a child, which neither of us commented on because commenting would have required admitting how much of our future was riding in sixty-year-old canvas with a rotted strap.

For the first hour Nadia navigated by memory and starlight, calling turns before I needed them—"left at the termite mound, then the track forks, take the right fork even though it looks worse"—and I drove the way I drive, which is to say I listened to the truck more than to anything else, feeling for the moment the suspension stopped complaining and started negotiating.

"You know what's funny," she said around dawn, when the sky went the color of dishwater and the basins started showing themselves, long gray flats stitched with dry channels. "Seven years I hated him for burning those letters. Seven years I built this whole architecture of who he was out of that one fact. And now I know why, and the architecture won't come down. It just stands there, wrong, and I walk past it every day."

"Grief keeps whatever shape you built it in," I said. "Doesn't care if the facts change."

"When did yours get built?"

I drove for a while. "My mother used to say optimism was a debt you pay forward. I thought it was a joke. Then I borrowed against a future I didn't have yet, and Calloway held the paper, and here we are." I shrugged, which is hard to do convincingly at seventy kilometers an hour on a track that wanted to kill me. "Mine's built out of arithmetic. Eleven thousand reasons. Except now the eleven thousand is drowned at the bottom of a flood channel with my truck's twin, so I suppose I'm building new architecture too."

"What's it shaped like?"

I glanced at her. She was looking at the map, not at me, the way she did when a question mattered—measuring distance to avoid feeling it, except this time she'd aimed the question straight down the middle of the distance and dared it to move.

"Ask me when we're across the Mkwawa," I said.

* * *

The spur junction was exactly where Elias's maps said it would be, which was the first time in seven years his work had been where anyone said it would be, according to Nadia, and she said it quietly, touching the junction mark on the map with one finger like a woman touching a scar.

The old rail bed ran east through country that got greener and less apologetic about it. Air like a wet towel. The barometer had been falling since midnight, slow and steady, the way a promise falls.

By noon we could see the weather.

Not rain—the front itself, stacked up over the highlands to the northeast, a bruise-colored rampart with lightning working inside it like something trying to get out. Any other week of the year I'd have been driving toward it for money. Today I was driving toward it for my life and carrying a dead man's confession, and honestly the emotional register wasn't that different. Storms don't care what you're fleeing. That's the whole contract.

"Cell's tracking southwest," I said, checking the feed on the battered handheld—the one luxury I'd kept through all the debt years, because a chaser without radar is just a person in a fast car. "If it holds, it hits the highlands tonight and the basin tomorrow morning. We need to be at the Mkwawa crossing by tomorrow noon at the latest. Preferably earlier."

"Preferably," Nadia repeated, in the tone of someone filing the word away for later use as evidence.

Behind us, far back along the spur, a dust plume. Small. Patient. I'd been watching mirrors long enough to know the difference between a plume that's going somewhere and a plume that's following. This one was following, keeping just inside visual range, close enough to say *we see you*, far enough to say *we don't need to hurry*.

"Nadia."

"I see it." She didn't look up from the map. "One vehicle. Maybe two behind the rise."

"Okafor doesn't waste men."

"No. He sends one truck to herd and keeps the rest on the roads for when the herd breaks." She finally looked up, and there it was—the thing I'd been falling for since a fuel depot and a dying man's handshake, the thing that had no business surviving a flood, a clinic, a funeral, and a fight, but had. "So we stop being on a road."

I grinned, and it felt strange on my face, like a coat returned from storage. "Cartographer after my own heart."

* * *

We lost them at dusk, and we lost them the way only two women who trusted each other's madness could.

The spur bent north toward a ridge, and any sane pursuer would assume we stayed on it, because the ridge was high ground and high ground was survival. Instead, at the bend, I killed the lights and dropped off the rail bed onto a cattle track Nadia had found that afternoon—a thread of a thing, half washed away, winding down into the basin proper, into the low country that every instinct I owned screamed to avoid with a storm coming.

"Low ground before the rain," she said, reading my face. "Because the rain isn't here yet. We sleep in the dry channel tonight and climb out before first light. He can't follow in the dark, and by the time he can, we're gone and the front's here and every track in this basin is a river."

"It's a terrible plan."

"It's your plan. You told me it yourself, the night at the clinic. *Nobody sane drives into the leading edge.*"

"I also said I wasn't sane."

"Consistency," she said, "is for people with retirement plans."

We slept in shifts in the channel bed, the truck tucked under a leaning fig tree, the satchel under my jacket under my arm like the world's most incriminating pillow. Above us the stars went out one district at a time as the front's anvil cloud slid over, and the air pressure dropped until my ears popped and the fig leaves hung limp, waiting.

At 3 a.m. I woke Nadia, and we climbed.

* * *

The Mkwawa branch, at dawn, was still a river in name only—a braided ribbon of pools and gravel bars, maybe forty meters wide, shin-deep in its deepest thread. Behind us and to the northeast, the front had arrived in the highlands overnight; we could hear it, a soundless-seeming rolling that was actually very loud thunder flattened by distance, and the eastern sky was the color of a healing bruise.

"Six hours," I said, wading the crossing to test it—up to mid-shin, cold, quick, honest. "Maybe five. After that this becomes uncrossable and we're committed to the far side anyway, so really the question is whether we cross now on purpose or later by accident."

"On purpose," Nadia said. "Always on purpose. That's the whole religion."

We crossed. The truck took it in low gear without drama, water sheeting off the axles, and on the far bank we stopped and I got out and looked back the way we'd come, at the whole basin lying open under the dying dry season, and I felt something I hadn't felt in seven years of chasing: not the chase-high, not the relief of a near miss, but something quieter. Competence, maybe. Or company.

Nadia stood beside me, mapping the rising cloud line with her eyes, and I realized she was doing what I was doing—reading the same sky, arriving at the same numbers—and that we didn't need to say any of it out loud anymore. Somewhere between a flooded crossing and a burned journal and a grave under an old fig tree, we'd become a single instrument with two dials.

"Front makes the highlands by dark," she said. "Covers our descent tomorrow. There's a track off the ridge on the far side, here—" her finger moved, sure, unhurried, "—that drops into the Okonkwo's district through the tea country. No tolls. No syndicate. Just mud."

"Mud I can do."

"Mud," she agreed, "is your native language."

And that was when the radio, which had been static and silence for two days, cleared its throat.

A voice, affable, unhurried, terribly familiar. *"Miss Iversen. You're a difficult woman to find and an easy one to predict. Enjoy the high ground while it lasts."*

Nadia looked at me. I looked at the sky, where the first real rain of the season was still an hour and a mountain away, and understood that Mr. Okafor had known about the Mkwawa crossing all along—and that whatever waited for us on the far side of the ridge, it wasn't mud and tea.
