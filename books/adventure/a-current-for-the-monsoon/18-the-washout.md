## 18. The Washout

There's a particular sound a road makes when it stops being a road. Not the dramatic crack of a bridge giving way—that's for the movies, all thunder and slow motion. No. It's quieter than that. A kind of absence under your tires, a hollowness in the engine note, like the ground itself has stopped returning your calls.

I heard it forty seconds before I saw why.

"Brace," I said, and stood on the brakes.

The truck slewed in the mud, and through the windshield, in the gray-green half-light of a monsoon sky doing its best impression of a closed fist, I watched the Kessel River Bridge exist and not exist at the same time. The approaches were still there—two concrete stubs reaching toward each other across eighty meters of churning brown water like the severed ends of a handshake. Between them, nothing. The middle span had gone downstream sometime in the last six hours, and the river had already forgotten it had ever carried anything heavier than rain.

Behind us, three kilometers back and closing, Okafor's men.

"Well," Nadia said from the passenger seat, in the tone of a cartographer who has just watched her life's work invalidated by weather. "That's inconvenient."

"That's one word for it."

I threw the truck into reverse and backed us off the crumbling lip of the approach until the front tires sat on solid ground, or what passed for it this time of year—which was to say mud with ambitions. In the mirror, dust. Not our dust. The dry-season kind, raised by tires on hardpan, which meant vehicles moving fast, which meant engines that hadn't been drowning in floodwater all morning, which meant Okafor had found the one road south we didn't think he knew about.

"He anticipated us," Nadia said. It wasn't a question. She was unfolding her field maps across the dash with hands that wanted very badly to shake and were being forbidden to by sheer professional discipline. "He let us commit to the spur. He knew the bridge was out."

"Of course he did." I killed the engine, because fuel was fuel and we might need every liter of it later, and because sitting in an idling truck felt like being a lit match in a room full of what we were. "Okafor doesn't chase people. He herds them. We're not running from him, Nadia. We've been running *along* his fence line since dawn."

Through the rain-streaked glass I took inventory of my kingdom: one dead-end approach, eighty meters of flood channel moving at a speed I'd estimate as somewhere between fast and fatally fast, a far bank of red clay rising into scrub country that eventually became the mission road, and, if you looked closely at the near bank, about thirty meters downstream—

"There," I said.

Nadia followed my finger. "That's a tree."

"That's a tree leaning over the channel at maybe forty degrees, with its crown in the water. Which makes it either a bridge or a headstone, depending entirely on execution."

She looked at me the way she'd looked at me crossing the Green Throat, back when we were strangers pretending to tolerate each other—the look of a woman recalculating whether the person next to her was an asset or a liability. I'd gotten used to that look. Somewhere in the last two weeks I'd started finding it almost flattering.

"You want to cross on a tree."

"I want to cross on a tree that is currently holding position against a current that wants it gone. That's more than the bridge can say."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about debt: it isn't a number. Eleven thousand, twenty-two thousand, whatever the ledger said this week—it was never really money. Money is just how the Calloway syndicate kept score. The actual debt was structural. It lived in the load-bearing walls of my life. Every route I planned, every job I took, every lie I told at toll bridges—I'd built my whole architecture around it, and I'd done it so gradually that I'd mistaken the cage for a skeleton.

Which is why, standing in the rain beside a dying truck with a satchel of evidence that could hang a dynasty and three kilometers of enforcers behind me, my first instinct wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. What's the truck worth? What will Okafor pay for the satchel? What does a bullet cost these days?

I'd like to tell you I'd stopped making those calculations after the clinic parking lot, after Nadia stripped me bare in a mission hallway and I admitted everything—the temptation, the envelope, the eleven thousand reasons. I'd like to say the math had left me.

It hadn't. But it had changed denominators. Because now when I ran the numbers, Nadia was in them, and Elias was in them—not the man, anymore, but the promise, the weight of his hand on mine in that clinic bed asking me for everything—and the sum kept coming out wrong no matter how I arranged it. There was no configuration where I sold the satchel and still got to be the person Nadia had decided, against all evidence, I might be.

So: the tree.

We worked fast. Rain has a way of clarifying priorities. I dragged the winch cable out of the truck bed while Nadia scouted the trunk—she moved along it like she was surveying it, testing bark, reading grain, and I realized she was doing exactly that, mapping its failure points the way she mapped everything, looking for the places it would betray us before they did.

"The root plate's lifting on the far side," she called back. "Every surge, it shifts another few centimeters. We have maybe—" she glanced upstream at the water, calculating volume and velocity the way other people check their watches, "—twenty minutes before it lets go. Less if that cell upstream dumps."

"Then we spend fifteen of them working and keep five for crying about it."

I rigged the winch anchor to a boulder above the bank—a real anchor, tested with everything the truck had, because a false anchor is just a way of choosing which of us drowns first. Then the plan, spoken aloud so it couldn't quietly mutate into something worse:

"Nadia goes first. Satchel strapped to her chest, carabiner clipped to the cable, she traverses hand over hand down the trunk to the fork where it dips toward the water, then drops to the far bank where the crown touches clay. I come second. Then we cut the cable and walk south until the mission road finds us."

"And the truck?"

"The truck stays here and becomes a very expensive distraction." I patted the hood. Rain drummed off it. "Okafor's men arrive, find fresh tracks pointing south past the washout, and do what any sensible hunter does—they look for a crossing. Downstream there's a ford at Kilometer Nine, bad but passable in a four-by-four. They'll burn forty minutes convincing themselves we went that way, because the alternative is admitting two women crossed a flood channel on a dead tree, and nobody plans for that."

"You're leaving them the truck."

"I'm leaving them the *idea* of the truck." I cinched the strap across Nadia's shoulders, over the satchel, checking it twice. My hands were steady. I noted that with some surprise, the way you notice a stranger's good manners. "Everything else in it is replaceable. Winch, tools, my mother's radio, seven years of storm logs." I hesitated over the logbook, then left it in the glovebox. Sentiment is heavy, and we were traveling light. "Let them have it. Consider it a tip."

Nadia caught my wrist. Rain ran off her chin. "Beatrix."

"I know."

"You don't have to—"

"I absolutely do have to, and we don't have time for the speech where you argue and I pretend to consider it." I clipped her harness to the cable. "You go first because you're lighter and better on rope and because if I go first and something breaks, you'll freeze, and if you go first and something breaks, I'll be too angry to freeze. Anger crosses rivers. I've seen it done."

"That's the worst rationale I've ever heard."

"It's also true. Go."

* * *

She went.

I'll tell you honestly: watching her step onto that trunk was the longest ninety seconds of my life, and I once spent four hours in a hailstorm listening to my own truck get progressively destroyed. The rain came sideways now, the upstream cell arriving right on schedule, rude as ever. Nadia moved down the lean of the tree with the cable singing taut above the water, small and deliberate, a figure in a woodcut. At the fork—the low point, where the current reached up and grabbed the lower branches, shredding them with a sound like tearing canvas—she stopped. The whole tree groaned. The root plate shifted; I saw it lift, a dark plate of earth the size of a dinner table peeling up out of the far bank like a lid coming off.

"Nadia!" I didn't mean to shout. Shouting costs breath and breath costs grip. But she was hanging over the worst of the channel now, and the water below her was moving house-sized volumes of brown violence, and there are things a person should not have to watch the person they love do.

She dropped.

The crown hit the clay bank, flexed, held. She rolled clear of the branches, came up on her feet, and turned—satchel intact, face pale as paper, one arm wrapped where a branch had opened it—and gave me a thumbs-up with the specific ferocity of someone who intends to yell at me extensively at a later date.

My turn.

I want to report that I crossed with grace. I want to tell you my mind was clear, that I thought noble thoughts about justice and second chances. What actually happened is this: I got three steps onto the trunk, the surge hit, and the world tilted. The tree rolled under me—forty degrees becoming sixty, the whole lever of it swinging on its failing roots—and suddenly I was hanging from the cable over the channel with my boots kicking air and the water reaching up for me with the patience of something that has drowned better people than me.

And there, dangling, spinning slowly, I made my peace with the arithmetic.

Because here's the thing about the debt: it was downstream. All of it. The syndicate, the ledger, the bounty chasing, the version of myself who could lie smoothly at toll bridges and call it survival—all of it belonged to the near bank, the bank I was leaving, the bank where Okafor's dust was growing on the horizon. And the far bank had Nadia on it, and a magistrate's kitchen at the end of a long walk, and a list of names from the 7th Survey Company that deserved to be read aloud somewhere other than a burned journal.

The choice wasn't between the truck and the tree. It had never been. It was between the woman I'd been and the one holding the rope.

I climbed.

Hand over hand, down the lean, the bark slick as soap, the current roaring up at me like a congregation. At the fork the tree bucked again—hard, final, the sound of roots letting go in stages—and I let go with it, dropping the last two meters as the crown slammed the clay and the entire trunk began, with terrible dignity, to slide.

I hit the bank rolling. Nadia hauled me up by the harness. Behind us, the tree surrendered to the river all at once—lifted, swung, and folded into the flood like a pen signing its name, gone downstream in seconds, taking the crossing with it.

We stood there, soaked, bleeding a little, breathing hard, and watched the water close over the place where our escape route had been.

"You cut the cable?" she shouted.

"Didn't have time!"

"So Okafor gets the winch too?"

"Okafor gets the whole truck, probably." I looked back across the channel. On the far approach, tiny with distance, headlights had appeared—three vehicles, fanning out, stopping at the broken bridge. Even from here I could picture Okafor stepping out, adjusting his cuffs, regarding the flood with the expression of a man whose invitation has been declined. He'd find the tracks. He'd send men to Kilometer Nine. And by the time they'd forded it and driven the loop around—two hours, maybe three—we'd be a rumor on the mission road.

But that wasn't what made me laugh, standing there in the rain with my ribs aching. What made me laugh was simpler than tactics.

"They'll search the truck," I said. "They'll find the logbook. Seven years of storm data, every chase, every near miss, every entry in my handwriting." I wiped rain out of my eyes. "Okafor's going to sit in that cab and read my whole life, and the last entry's going to be coordinates for a washed-out bridge, and he is going to know—*know*—that I chose this. That I drowned my own escape route on purpose."

Nadia stared at me. Then, slowly, she started laughing too, the unhinged kind, the kind that lives next door to crying. "He's going to take it personally."

"He's going to take it *professionally*, which is worse." I shouldered the satchel—Elias's cargo, the 7th Survey's testimony, the only thing on either bank that mattered now—and turned south. "Come on. Magistrate Okonkwo's kitchen is nine kilometers that way, and I promised a dying man we'd deliver before the dry season ended."

"The dry season ended an hour ago," Nadia said, falling in beside me. "Look up."

I looked up. The sky had finally finished clenching, and the rain was coming down straight and honest and endless, the first real rain of the season, the kind that doesn't tease. It fell on the flood channel behind us, on the drowned truck, on the red clay road ahead, on both of us equally, asking nothing.

"Then we're on schedule," I said, and we walked.
