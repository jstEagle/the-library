## 19. Magistrate Okonkwo's Kitchen

The favor Beatrix never cashed turned out to be a woman named Ferro, who ran a fish-smoking operation on the edge of the delta that was, by every visible metric, a fish-smoking operation. By every invisible metric it was a post office for people the post office had forgotten.

We came to her at dusk, two days out of the washout, walking the last three kilometers because Beatrix wouldn't let the truck—any truck, even one Ferro's people lent us—come within sight of the compound. My feet were blisters wearing boots. The satchel rode against my chest in a sling I'd made from a torn shirt sleeve, and I'd stopped noticing its weight the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat, which is to say I noticed it constantly and would have panicked if I didn't.

"Ferro owes me," Beatrix said, for the fourth time since the bridge.

"From what?"

"A thing I did."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer." She walked with her head down and her shoulders rolled forward, the posture of a woman trying to be weatherproof. "I did a thing. She owed me. I never collected. That's the entire architecture of our relationship, Nadia. Don't gild it."

I wanted to ask what the thing was. I'd spent three weeks learning when to leave Beatrix's silences alone and when to dig, and this one had the texture of load-bearing silence—the kind that holds up a roof. So I left it.

Ferro met us at the smokehouse door, a broad woman in an apron stiff with salt, holding a cleaver with the casual authority of someone who had never once needed to use it as anything but a cleaver. She looked at Beatrix for a long moment. Then at me. Then at the satchel, because of course she looked at the satchel; everyone looked at the satchel; I had begun to think of it as a third passenger.

"You look like hell," Ferro said.

"I've been told I'm weathered," Beatrix said. "It's a professional aesthetic."

"You look like hell that's been rained on." Ferro stepped aside. "Come in before someone sees you looking like it."

* * *

Her kitchen was the back room of the smokehouse, and it was the first room I'd stood in since my father's house that smelled like food instead of diesel. There was a pot of something dark on a wood stove, a table scarred white with knife marks, and a radio playing low, tuned to a station that mostly played static with occasional acts of God. Ferro fed us without being asked—fish and rice and a pepper sauce that made my eyes water and my ribs ache from laughing, actually laughing, at something Beatrix said about a toll collector we'd bribed with a spare tire.

Then Beatrix set down her spoon and said, "I need a door to Okonkwo."

Ferro's ladle stopped moving. "You need what?"

"The magistrate. The real one. Not the one in the capital who does ribbon cuttings. The one in the river district who still signs his own warrants."

"I know who he is." Ferro put the ladle down with great care, the way you put down a thing you might later throw. "Beatrix. What is in that bag?"

And here was the moment I learned something about the smuggler's code, or maybe just about Ferro: she didn't ask twice, and she didn't ask loudly, but she asked, and she waited, and the kitchen got very quiet except for the static-radio and the wood stove ticking.

Beatrix looked at me. It was the first time she'd handed me a decision like that—not a navigation call, not a route through flood country, but the question of how much truth a stranger could hold. I found I liked the weight of it more than I expected.

"Evidence," I said. "That Calloway murdered a survey crew seven years ago and stole their claim. Signed confession. Original records. Photographs." I heard my father's voice under mine, the way he'd taught me to read coordinates aloud, flat and exact, so there could be no mistake. "My father was the only survivor. He carried it until three days ago. He's dead now."

Ferro sat back. She looked older suddenly, or maybe just less armored. "Calloway," she said, like a woman testing whether a floorboard would take her weight. "You're carrying Calloway's death warrant in a shirt sleeve."

"Yes," I said.

"And you want my door."

"I want your door," Beatrix said, "and I want it tonight, and I want it quiet, because there are men on the roads south of here whose employer has a very strong interest in this bag never arriving anywhere."

Ferro was silent long enough that the radio filled it—a burst of music, gone again. Then she stood, took the pot off the fire, and said, "Okonkwo eats dinner at eight. He eats alone. He doesn't trust his cooks, which is wise of him, because two of them report to people I could name. But his housekeeper buys her peppers from me." She untied her apron. "So. You'll come as my delivery girls. And Beatrix—if this goes wrong, if this touches my operation, we are square no longer. Do you understand? Whatever I owed you dies at his doorstep."

"Understood," Beatrix said.

"One more thing." Ferro paused at the door, cleaver still in hand, though I think she'd forgotten it. "Okonkwo is honest. That's rarer than you think and worth less than you hope. Honesty is a man standing in a river telling the water to go around him. Sometimes it does. Mostly it doesn't." She looked at the satchel. "Don't expect the water to part just because you're right."

* * *

Magistrate Okonkwo's house was smaller than I'd imagined, which sounds like a strange thing to notice, but I had spent the walk over building him out of my father's stories—a tower of a man, a fortress—and what we got was a low bungalow behind a wall, with a garden gone slightly wild and a single lamp burning in a window. The housekeeper admitted us through the kitchen door, took Ferro's basket of peppers, and vanished. And then we were standing in a magistrate's kitchen at half past eight, dripping river mud on clean tile, waiting for a man to finish eating.

He came in wiping his hands on a cloth, and he was not a tower. He was a compact, gray-templed man in a shirt buttoned to the throat despite the heat, with reading glasses pushed up into his hair and the eyes of someone who had spent thirty years listening to liars and had gotten very good at hearing the true parts anyway.

"Ferro," he said. "You've never once delivered my peppers yourself."

"No, sir."

"So." He looked at us. "Who is dying, and who killed them?"

It was so precisely the right question that for a second nobody answered. Then Beatrix said, "Neither yet, sir. If we're fast."

He pulled out a chair. "Sit. Both of you. Start at the beginning, and don't start with the bag. Start with why you're both standing like the bag might explode."

I started. I don't know why me—I think because Beatrix had already decided it should be, had handed me the satchel's story somewhere back on the river and never taken it back. I told him about my father, the 7th Survey Company, the letters burned, the map drawn in a dying man's hand. I told him about the adit above the dry wash, the tin box, the confession signed in ink gone brown. I told him about the clinic and the dawn. I did not tell him about the envelope Okafor left in the parking lot, and I felt Beatrix not-telling-it beside me, a shared silence so complete it was almost a third voice in the room.

Okonkwo listened without interrupting, which I have since learned is the rarest skill in any government. When I finished, he held out his hand—not for the satchel, but palm up, patient. "May I?"

Beatrix unbuckled the strap and passed it across.

He went through it the way I imagine he went through everything: slowly, completely, reading every page as if each were the only page. The photographs he held up to the lamp. The confession he read twice. At the survey records—the claim maps, my father's neat annotations, the numbers that proved the Calloway claim and the 7th Survey's claim were the same ground—he stopped for a long time, and I watched his jaw work like a man chewing something bitter.

"These names," he said finally. "I knew one of them. Osaze Bright. He clerked for me before he went into field survey. Good handwriting." He set the papers down and aligned their edges with a small, precise violence. "They told us he drowned."

"He did not drown," I said.

"No," Okonkwo said. "Evidently he was murdered by men who then became rich enough to have his drowning officially confirmed." He took off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Do you know what happens tomorrow morning when I file this?"

"Warrants," Beatrix said.

"Warrants. Which go to a police commissioner who plays cards with a Calloway director every Thursday. Which are executed by officers whose salaries Calloway's logistics contracts supplement. Which arrive at Calloway House approximately four hours after Calloway's lawyers do." He put the glasses back on and looked at us over them. "I am not telling you this to discourage you. I am telling you because you have carried this thing through flood country and I will not insult you with theater. This is the beginning of something, not the end of it. Justice moves slower than syndicates. A syndicate can move money at the speed of light. Justice moves at the speed of paper, and paper has to be served, and served again, and appealed."

"Then why take it?" I asked. It came out harder than I meant. "If it's just going to sit in a drawer while they buy everyone—"

"Because drawers are not all alike." He gathered the papers with both hands, squaring them, and I understood that this gesture was the closest he would come to ceremony. "There are drawers in the capital that open for money. There are drawers here that open for nothing, because I keep the key and I sleep badly and I have buried too many friends to be bought by men who dug the graves. Your father chose this drawer, Miss Wren, when he chose to carry that box for seven years instead of selling it. He understood exactly what you are angry about. He carried it anyway. That is what carrying means—it means knowing the current is against you and swimming regardless, because the alternative is to be carried."

The kitchen was quiet. On the stove, the housekeeper's kettle began, very softly, to sing.

"There will be a preliminary inquiry within the week," Okonkwo said. "I will need testimony. Yours—" he nodded at me "—and yours." He looked at Beatrix, and something in the look made me glance sideways at her. She had gone very still, the stillness she wore like armor. "Yours especially, I think. You know these roads. You know how Calloway's money moves through them. I suspect you know Mr. Okafor by more than reputation."

"I know him," Beatrix said evenly.

"Then you'll testify."

It wasn't quite a question. Beatrix was quiet for three full seconds, which from her is a geological age. "I'll testify," she said. "Provided the inquiry protects the witnesses first and the process second. I've seen process used as a weapon, sir. I'd rather not donate anyone else to it."

Okonkwo almost smiled. "Spoken like a woman who has been on the wrong end of a subpoena."

"Spoken like a woman who has issued a few."

He stood, and we stood, and he shook our hands—one at a time, properly, looking at each of us as if filing our faces somewhere permanent. At the door he paused.

"The founder's name is on these pages," he said. "By Friday it will be on a warrant. After that, everything depends on speed and luck, and I control only one of those." He opened the kitchen door onto the dark garden. "Go home by different routes. Don't celebrate where anyone can see you. And Miss Wren—" he touched the satchel, lightly, the way you'd touch a headstone "—when they read the crew's names aloud someday, and they will, make sure they read all of them. Including your father's. Survivors count too. They pay the same price, only in installments."

* * *

Ferro walked us out through the smoking sheds, past racks of fish gleaming amber in the lamplight, and said nothing until the wall was behind us and the delta road lay ahead, black and wet.

"Well?" she said.

"We delivered," Beatrix said.

"You delivered a piece of paper to a man with a pen. In this country that's not a victory, it's an opening bid." But she was smiling, crookedly, in the lamplight spilling from the shed. "Still. Opening bids win sometimes. Come back inside. You'll eat, you'll sleep in the loft, and tomorrow you'll disappear, because by noon there will be men asking after two women and a bag, and I'd rather they ask after my fish."

We ate again, though I wasn't hungry, and slept in the loft above the smokehouse on sacks that smelled of salt and oak, and I lay awake listening to Beatrix breathe beside me and thinking about currents. My father had spent seven years swimming against one and called it carrying. Okonkwo stood in his and told the water to go around. And Beatrix—

Beatrix was asleep the way soldiers sleep, flat and total, one hand curled near her hip where the satchel used to hang. Tomorrow she would have to decide what she was. Testimony would put her name on a document that Calloway could read, and Calloway had already offered her a number once, and numbers were the only language she'd ever trusted.

I reached over and laced my fingers through hers. She didn't wake, but her hand closed around mine, deliberate even in sleep, and I thought: whatever she decides, I want to be in the room when she decides it.

Outside, far off over the flats, thunder cleared its throat. The season was turning. And somewhere south of us, on a road we'd have to take in the morning, I would have bet the satchel itself that Mr. Okafor was already making his next offer.
