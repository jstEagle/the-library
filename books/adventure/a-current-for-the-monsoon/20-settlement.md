## 20. Settlement

The message came the way the worst news always does in this country: politely, and by radio.

I was at the mission clinic's fuel pump, doing arithmetic with a siphon hose and a jerry can, when Okafor's voice crackled through on the channel I'd been monitoring out of habit and dread in equal measure. Not his men. Him. Direct.

"Miss Iversen. You're a difficult woman to find when you don't want to be found."

"You're a persistent man to keep looking," I said, "when your employer is about to be indicted."

A pause. On Okafor, a pause was a paragraph. "May I buy you lunch?"

"Absolutely not."

"Dinner, then."

"Okafor."

"A drink. One drink, in a public place of your choosing, with witnesses of your choosing. I'll come unarmed. You have my word."

"Your word is worth about as much as Calloway stock right now."

Another pause, longer, and then — and this was the thing about him that had kept me alive at the toll bridge, that had let me lie to his face and walk away — he laughed. A real one, low and unbothered, like a man watching weather roll in from a porch he owned.

"Fair," he said. "Then no drinks. Come to the depot at Kilometer Forty-One tomorrow at noon. Or don't. But if you don't, Miss Iversen, you should know that the conversation we need to have will happen anyway, just with less courtesy and more people. I'd rather it be the two of us and a table."

I looked at the jerry can. I looked at the clinic windows, where Nadia sat with her father's effects spread across a cot like an archaeologist cataloguing a dig site — which, in a way, she was. I thought about every road south being watched, every bridge a choke point, every day the dry season burning down toward its last inch of wick.

"Noon," I said. "Kilometer Forty-One. And if I see so much as a sidearm—"

"You'll drive off, and I'll deserve it." He signed off with something almost like warmth. That was the most frightening part of Okafor, and I'd never decided whether he knew it.

* * *

Kilometer Forty-One was a fuel depot that had died sometime in the last decade and been half-resurrected by vultures and commerce. There was a canopy with three pumps, two of them ornamental, and a cinderblock office where a woman named Mrs. Adeyemi sold diesel, warm soda, and gossip at prices that adjusted for the buyer's desperation. I'd bought from her before. She knew me. More importantly, she knew everyone who came and went within forty kilometers, and she owed me a favor from the year I'd pulled her nephew's truck out of a wash before the flash flood took it.

That favor was why I chose her depot. When I rolled in at eleven-thirty, she was sweeping a porch that didn't need sweeping, and there were two of Okafor's men leaning against a grey Land Cruiser looking bored in the specific way of men told to be visible but not useful.

"They came an hour ago," Mrs. Adeyemi said without looking up from her broom. "Ordered nothing. Sat in the heat like lizards." Her eyes flicked up, quick and dry. "You want I should leave the back door open?"

"I want you to sweep very loudly near the window."

She almost smiled. "For you? I'll sweep like a hurricane."

Okafor was already seated at the plastic table under the canopy when I walked over, and I'll give him this: he'd dressed down for the occasion. No suit. Just a linen shirt open at the collar and reading glasses pushed up on his head, so that he looked less like a syndicate enforcer and more like a retired schoolteacher on holiday, which was exactly the kind of disguise Okafor wore best because it wasn't a disguise at all. It was just another true face among the many he kept.

There were two bottles of soda on the table and two glasses. He'd brought ice. Actual ice, out here, in a cooler, which cost more than my weekly food budget and said everything about how seriously he took this meeting.

"Miss Iversen." He gestured at the chair opposite. "Thank you for coming."

"You said witnesses of my choosing." I nodded at the two men by the Cruiser. "They're not mine."

"They're not armed. Check if you like. They're here because after this week, I don't trust empty roads either." He said it lightly, but something moved behind his eyes when he said *this week*, and I filed it away.

I sat. I poured the soda myself, both glasses, keeping my hands busy and my eyes on his hands, which stayed flat and visible on the table like a man demonstrating he had no cards left.

"You look tired," he said.

"You look unemployed."

He smiled at that, genuinely, and took off the reading glasses and folded them. "You've heard, then."

"I've heard rumors. Rumors travel faster than warrants."

"Warrants are coming. Real ones. Okonkwo filed yesterday." He said the magistrate's name the way you'd say the name of a storm cell you'd been tracking for days and had finally stopped hoping would turn. "The board met Tuesday night. Emergency session. By Wednesday morning, Mr. Calloway's founder had retained four lawyers and lost two board seats, and by Thursday—" he spread his hands, elegant, helpless, a conjurer showing the trick had failed "—by Thursday, I was informed that my department's methods 'no longer reflected the values of the company.' My contracts are terminated. My men are being paid off quietly. My expense accounts, closed."

"And yet here you sit, buying ice."

"Here I sit." He picked up his glass, considered it, set it down again without drinking. "Do you know what happens to a man like me when the company disavows him, Miss Iversen? Nothing dramatic. No bullet in a parking lot — though I understand you expected one, and I'm sorry the clinic's security camera made such a theatrical moment of our last conversation. No. What happens is paperwork. My name comes off accounts. Doors that opened for me stop opening. The people I protected for eleven years discover they were protecting themselves all along, and I was merely convenient." He turned the glass a quarter-turn on the table, precise as a man adjusting an instrument. "I have spent my career being the reason people feared Calloway. Now I am simply a man with excellent contacts, a modest severance, and a great deal of knowledge about where certain bodies — financial and otherwise — are buried."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It's a résumé."

I laughed before I could stop myself. He inclined his head, accepting it like a tip.

"Here's what I've learned this month," he went on, and now the lightness drained out of his voice, leaving something older and flatter underneath. "I learned it watching you, actually, which irritates me more than the board's memo did. I learned that loyalty to an institution is a loan the institution never intends to repay. I gave Calloway eleven years. I collected debts for them that I was told were legitimate, and when I found out some weren't, I was told the exceptions were temporary, and when I noticed the exceptions had a pattern, I was promoted — which is how companies buy silence, Miss Iversen, they promote it until the salary does the work conscience used to do." He finally drank, a small measured sip. "And then the day comes when the pattern surfaces anyway, because it always surfaces, because men like Elias Wren bury things that stay buried only until someone digs, and the company discovers that its conscience problem has a name and an address, and the name is mine."

"Elias Wren is dead."

"Yes." For a moment, just a moment, the flatness cracked and I saw something in Okafor's face that I hadn't expected and didn't enjoy finding: grief. Small, private, unwelcome to him. "I read the clinic's report. Heart, they say. I imagine seven years of carrying what he carried counts as a cardiac condition." He set the glass down. "I met him once, you know. Seven years ago. After the survey accident. I was sent to confirm there were no loose ends."

The table between us got very quiet. Mrs. Adeyemi's broom scraped rhythmically somewhere behind the cinderblock wall, loud as a heartbeat.

"There was one loose end," Okafor said. "Him. And I stood in a hotel bar in Meridian and watched an old prospector drink himself steady, and I reported back that he was nobody — a drunk, a ghost, a man whose story no one would believe. Which was even true, mostly. What I didn't report was that he looked at me across that bar with perfect sobriety behind his eyes and said, *'You'll be back for me someday. Everyone comes back.'*" Okafor exhaled through his nose. "He was right. You came back for him. I came back for him. We just wanted different things when we arrived."

"What do you want now, Okafor?"

"To offer you a job."

I stared at him. Of all the sentences I'd braced for across that table — threats, warnings, a last attempt at the satchel I no longer had because it was sitting in Magistrate Okonkwo's evidence room under seal — that one hadn't made the list.

"Don't make that face," he said. "It's a good offer. It's the only honest thing I have left to sell. Think about what you are, Miss Iversen. You're a woman who can read weather like scripture, cross country no sane driver attempts, lie to my face so smoothly I complimented you on it afterward, and — this is the part the board noticed, believe it or not — you can be trusted with something valuable. You had the Wren evidence in your hands for six days. Six days. Do you know what I'd have paid for those documents? What I *did* offer?" He tapped the table once. "And instead you drove them through flood country with my men on every road, nearly drowned yourself at a washed-out bridge, and delivered them to the one magistrate in three provinces who can't be bought. The board's lawyers are in a panic because they cannot model you. Neither can I. That's not an insult. In my former profession, unpredictability was a defect. In my next one, it's inventory."

"Your next one being what, exactly?"

"Recovery. Retrieval. Verification." He waved a hand, vague and expansive. "Call it due diligence for people who operate outside the comfortable fiction of courts. There are more Calloways in this world than the one that fired me, Miss Iversen. Every one of them needs someone who can go into bad country, find the truth, and come back with it intact. Someone who won't sell it to the highest bidder." His eyes were steady on mine. "Someone who already proved, at considerable personal cost, that she won't."

"And the debt?"

"Gone." He said it simply. "Not forgiven — repurchased. Your paper changed hands twice this week in the fire sale. I bought it Wednesday morning for eleven cents on the dollar, which tells you what the market thinks of Calloway receivables these days. As of noon today, you owe me nothing, because I've marked it paid in full. Consider it a signing bonus whether or not you sign."

I sat with that. Eleven thousand dollars of my life, eleven years of Okafor's, and Elias Wren's seven — all of it churned through the same machine, priced, discounted, settled. Somewhere in an office in Meridian, a clerk had stamped my freedom *PAID* without ever knowing my name.

"Why?" I asked. "Why buy it? Why any of this? You could still take the bullet route. You could still decide I'm a loose end."

"Because I'm done collecting loose ends," he said, "and starting to count them." He put the reading glasses back on, and just like that he was the affable terrifying man from the toll bridge again, except the terror had gone out of him and left only the affability, which was somehow worse. "Also, frankly, because you're the only person involved in this entire catastrophe who ended it better than she started it. Wren died. Calloway's founder is finished. I'm professionally deceased. You walked out of a flood channel with the truth in a satchel and a woman beside you who, I'm told, looks at you the way cartographers look at blank space." He stood, buttoning nothing, smoothing nothing, a man with no costume left to adjust. "I respect outcomes, Miss Iversen. Yours is the only clean one on the board."

"It's not clean."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But it's yours. That's rarer."

He slid an envelope across the table — not money, just a card with a radio frequency handwritten on it, and below it, in small neat print, a single sentence: *When you change your mind, and you will, ask for the man who bought your debt.*

"And if I never call?"

"Then I'll have bought the most expensive piece of paper of my career, and deserved it." He picked up his cooler, nodded to Mrs. Adeyemi's window — she'd swept herself into view, broom in hand, watching us with the frank interest of a woman pricing the whole scene for later retelling — and paused at the edge of the canopy.

"One more thing," he said, not turning around. "Okonkwo's warrants land Friday. Arrests Monday, if the courts move fast, and they won't, but the founder's passport will be seized by Thursday regardless, because Okonkwo is thorough. There will be hearings. Testimony. Names read aloud that haven't been spoken in seven years." He glanced back at me, and the grief was there again, unhidden this time, worn openly like a man who'd run out of pockets. "Wren's daughter will hear her mother's name in a courtroom. Make sure she knows, before that happens, that it was her father who made sure the name survived. Whatever else he was — whatever he burned, whatever he hid — he kept the truth alive in a hole in the ground for seven years so that a stranger with a truck could carry it out. That's not nothing, Miss Iversen. In my experience, it's everything."

Then he walked to the Land Cruiser, got in, and was gone down the dead-straight road in a long brown ribbon of dust, and the two bored men went with him, and the depot was suddenly just a depot again, hot and quiet and smelling of diesel.

Mrs. Adeyemi came out and collected the untouched second glass. "Trouble?" she asked.

"Settlement," I said.

"Hm." She looked down the empty road, then at me, with the shrewd unhurried assessment of a woman who had watched thirty years of travelers arrive as one thing and leave as another. "You look lighter. Debt settle too?"

"Paid in full."

"By him?"

"By the collapse of everything he served." I turned the card over in my fingers. Blank on the back. Even his generosity had fine print, and the fine print was just a frequency, waiting. "Funny thing about freedom, Mrs. Adeyemi. Turns out it can be purchased by accident."

"Nothing's accidental," she said, going back to her sweeping. "Man doesn't buy a debt he expects to collect. Man buys a debt he expects to matter."

* * *

Nadia was on the clinic's back steps when I returned, cross-legged, surrounded by her father's things: the journal with its burned pages, the brass compass worn smooth on one side where his thumb had lived, a photograph of the 7th Survey Company, eleven grinning people squinting into a sun none of them knew was their last. She'd been at it for hours. She looked up when I sat down beside her, and whatever she saw in my face made her set the compass down.

"Okafor?"

"Okafor."

"He offered you money."

"He offered me a job. The money was a side effect." I handed her the card. She read it, read it again, and handed it back with an expression I couldn't fully parse — part anger, part something adjacent to amusement.

"So the man who chased us across three provinces wants to hire the woman who outran him."

"When you say it like that, it sounds flattering."

"How else would you say it?"

"Predatory. Opportunistic. Deeply suspicious." I flicked the card against my knee. "He bought my debt, Nadia. Eleven thousand dollars, marked paid, no strings he'll admit to. I'm free."

"Are you?" She said it without cruelty, which was the only way she could have said it and still have me listen. She nodded at the card in my hand. "That's not freedom. That's a leash with a very long rope. He's patient. He bought your debt because he believes that eventually you'll need him — or want to — and either way, he owns the moment it happens."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned to face me fully, and I saw that she'd been crying earlier — the salt tracks were faint but present, dried in the heat — and that she'd cried, judging by the crumpled page in her lap, over the journal entry dated the night of the massacre. "Because my father spent seven years telling himself he was free too. He had his map, his distance, his no-questions-asked arrangement with a storm chaser. And none of it was freedom, Bea. It was just guilt with better logistics." She took a breath. "So if you're free, tell me what you're going to do with it. Right now. Out loud. Because if the answer is *wait*, or *figure it out*, or *keep the card in case* — then you're not free. You're just unemployed."

I looked at the card. Okafor's neat handwriting. *Ask for the man who bought your debt.* A door held open by a man who understood, better than anyone I'd ever met, that the cruelest traps are the ones you're grateful to enter.

Then I looked past her, at the mission clinic's noticeboard, where a government circular had been pinned that morning: *PUBLIC NOTICE — WARRANTS ISSUED IN THE MATTER OF CALLOWAY MINERAL CONCESSION 114. HEARINGS TO COMMENCE. DESCENDANTS AND CLAIMANTS OF THE SEVENTH SURVEY COMPANY ARE INVITED TO PRESENT DOCUMENTATION OF INTEREST.*

Descendants and claimants. Nadia's name was on that notice whether she'd read it yet or not. So were ten other names, belonging to families who'd spent seven years believing their people had died in an accident.

I tore the card in half. Then in half again. I dropped the pieces into the tin can Mrs. Adeyemi used for cigarette ends, where they landed on top of a week of other people's discarded habits.

"I'm going to help you resurvey Concession 114," I said. "Every boundary stone your father's crew drove. Every coordinate in their field books. Before the hearings start, so those families walk into that courtroom holding maps instead of grief." I stood, and the dry season wind came off the flats and pushed at us both like a hand on the back. "And then, when the first rain comes—"

The radio on my hip crackled. Okonkwo's clerk, brisk and formal: warrants confirmed, arrests scheduled, and one line at the end that made Nadia grab my arm —

"—and advise all parties: the founder's counsel has filed to contest jurisdiction. Expect delays. Expect pressure. Expect them to fight."

Nadia and I looked at each other over the tin can full of torn paper, and somewhere far south, thunder was already testing its voice against the horizon.

"Expect them to fight," she repeated softly. Then she picked up her father's compass, closed her hand around it, and smiled the first real smile I'd seen from her since the clinic. "Good thing we know the country better than they do."
