## 21. First Rain

The warrant came down on a Tuesday, which felt right. Justice, in my experience, doesn't arrive like lightning. It arrives like paperwork—slow, damp, and smelling faintly of someone else's coffee.

I was in the mission clinic's back office, cataloguing my father's effects into three boxes labeled by nobody in particular: *keep*, *burn*, and a third box I hadn't named yet because naming it would mean deciding what it was. His brass compass went in the first box. The half-burned journal went in the second, then moved to the third, then back to the second, until Beatrix leaned in the doorway and said, "You're going to wear a groove in that table."

"It's evidence," I said.

"It's grief with a cover on it."

She had a talent for saying true things in a tone that made you want to throw something at her. It was one of maybe six things I liked about her, and I'd stopped counting after six because the number kept embarrassing me.

The radio on the shelf crackled—the mission kept an old set for weather—and a voice read out the news the way voices do when they don't know what they're carrying: Magistrate Okonkwo's office had issued warrants in the matter of the 7th Survey Company. Augustus Calloway himself, founder, philanthropist, the man whose name was on two hospitals and a scholarship fund, taken into custody at his estate before dawn. No drama. No shootout. Just men in plain clothes and a magistrate who'd spent thirty years being unbribable, which turned out to be its own kind of weapon.

Beatrix went very still, the way she did when a storm cell changed direction.

"That's it," she said. "That's the whole thing. Seven years, and it takes one morning."

"Justice moves slower than syndicates," I said, quoting Okonkwo back at her. "But apparently it does move."

She looked at me then—not the glance she gave maps or storm fronts or exits, but the other look, the one she thought I didn't notice. "Your father would have wanted to see it."

"No," I said, and surprised myself with how steady it came out. "My father wanted to *not* be the reason it happened. Those are different things. He'd have settled for less and called it enough." I put the journal in the third box after all. "We're not settling."

* * *

The memorial was my idea, though I'll admit the idea arrived wearing Beatrix's coat. We'd been sitting on the clinic steps two nights later, watching heat lightning stitch the eastern sky, and she said, apropos of nothing, "You know their names?"

Whose names. Then I understood. The 7th Survey Company. Eleven men and two women who'd walked into a canyon in the dry season seven years ago and been reported as victims of a collapse—a geological accident, an act of God, a line item. My father had carried their names in his head like stones in a pack. He'd never once said them aloud to me.

"Some of them," I admitted. "The report listed them. Father kept the clipping."

"The report also said 'collapse,'" Beatrix said. "The report was written by the man who made the collapse." She turned her beer bottle slowly on the step. "Okonkwo will read them into the record eventually. Court takes months. Records get sealed. Names get filed." A pause, the length she always left for you to catch up. "Somebody should say them out loud first. Somewhere that isn't a filing cabinet."

So I organized it, because that's what cartographers do—we decide where things go.

I chose the overlook above the dry wash, near the mine adit. It took some doing: a ride from the mission truck, permission from a headman who remembered my father vaguely and me not at all, a promise to leave no structures behind. I made placards from plywood and paint marker, fourteen of them, because eleven men and two women is thirteen and I refused to round down, and the fourteenth was blank except for the words *AND THE ONE WHO CAME BACK*. That one was for my father. Whether it was accusation or absolution, I let the people who attended decide. I still haven't decided myself.

Word travels strangely in flood country. I expected a dozen people. Forty came. Then sixty. Surveyors' widows who'd never believed the official story. An old man who'd driven the supply mules for the 7th and recognized a name on a placard and sat down in front of it like it was a chair. Two junior clerks from the land registry, nervous in their good shirts, taking notes they probably shouldn't have been taking and clearly intending to keep anyway. A woman from the Calloway scholarship program who stood at the edge of the gathering for a long time and then, when I read the last name, sat down hard in the dust and stayed there.

Beatrix worked the perimeter the whole time—she can't not; I think silence in open country makes her itch—and I caught her more than once just listening, arms crossed, weight on her heels, wearing the expression of a woman auditing her own conscience and finding the arithmetic uncomfortable.

When it was done, when the names had been said aloud into the dry air and the wind had taken them wherever wind takes things, an old surveyor's widow took my hand in both of hers. Her grip was stronger than mine.

"He carried it alone all this time," she said, meaning my father. "That's not a burden a man should carry alone. But he carried it." She patted my knuckles. "You brought it home. That counts."

I said thank you. What I didn't say was that I hadn't brought anything home; my father had, dying, using a stranger with debts as a wheelbarrow. Grief makes you honest in directions you don't choose.

* * *

The rain came three days later, and it came the way monsoon rain comes—not as weather but as an announcement.

I'd been up since before dawn helping the mission sisters sandbag the low channel, and by mid-morning the air had gone the color of wet slate. You could smell it coming, that green mineral smell the country makes when it's about to drink. The birds went quiet in the wrong order. And then the western horizon simply stood up—a wall of gray-blue, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been late.

Beatrix was on the clinic roof with the anemometer when it hit, because of course she was. I climbed the ladder and found her standing in the open with her face tipped up, letting the first fat drops hit her like small applause.

"You're going to get soaked," I said.

"I'm going to get *paid*," she said. "Do you know how long I've chased these? Years. Thousands of miles. Always driving toward them, always arriving ten minutes after the leading edge, always measuring." She held out her hand, palm up, and a drop landed dead center. "This is the first one I've ever just stood in."

The cell reached us then, and the world went white-noise loud. Rain came down in ropes, in sheets, in the kind of volume that turns roads into rivers and rivers into arguments. Within minutes we were drenched past the dignity of caring, hair flat, clothes plastered, laughing at nothing—or I was laughing; Beatrix was doing the thing where her mouth almost cooperates with joy and then gets shy about it.

And then she wasn't laughing anymore. She was looking at me through the rain with an expression I'd only seen her wear once before, in the washout, in the half-second before she cut the truck loose and bet her life on the current.

"Nadia." The rain nearly ate her voice. "I got a call this morning. Halvorsen's outfit. They want a chase contract. Monsoon season, full circuit, top rate." She said it flatly, the way she recited numbers, which is how she says everything that matters. "It's more money than I've seen in one place since before Calloway."

"And?"

"And I told them I'd answer by Friday." She wiped rain off her face with the heel of her hand, a gesture so familiar now it hurt. "I've been trying to figure out why I'm stalling. I'm not a person who stalls. Stalling is how you die in this business." Another drop, another pause. "Then I figured it out. Every reason I ever had to leave this country was a debt. And as of Tuesday, I don't have any."

The rain hammered the tin roof. Below us, the channel was running brown and fast, carrying the dry season away piece by piece.

"So I'm asking," she said, and here her voice did something unpracticed, something without a fallback position, "what a person does with a season and no debts. Because I've never had one. I genuinely don't know the procedure."

I could have kissed her right there on the roof with the whole sky falling. Instead—I'm my father's daughter, after all; we save our declarations for difficult terrain—I took her hand and pointed south, past the mission, past the drowned road, to where the old claim boundaries ran along the ridge.

"There's a resurvey needs doing," I said. "Fourteen parcels. The heirs are scattered and the records are a mess and Okonkwo says it'll take a proper survey team most of a season." I squeezed her hand. "I need someone who can read weather. The pay is terrible. The company is questionable. There's a woman on the crew who lies beautifully and is learning, slowly, to stop."

Beatrix looked at the ridge, at the rain, at me. She measured it—I watched her measure it, distance and risk and return, the way she measures everything—and then, for the first time since I'd known her, she put the instruments down.

"Terrible pay," she said. "Questionable company." Her mouth finally cooperated with the joy. "Where do I sign?"

The rain answered for both of us, coming down harder, the first honest rain of the season, filling the wash, feeding the river, writing the whole country green. We stood on the roof until we were soaked through to the skin, and neither of us moved to go inside, because there was nowhere else either of us needed to be, and—for once in both our lives—we had run completely out of reasons to leave.
