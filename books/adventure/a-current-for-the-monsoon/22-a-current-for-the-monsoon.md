## 22. A Current for the Monsoon

The contract arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day the world had always chosen to remind me that I was for sale.

It came by courier this time instead of radio, printed on heavy stock with an embossed letterhead — Halvorsen Atmospheric Research, a name I recognized from the trade journals. They wanted a lead chaser for a three-season instrumentation program out of the eastern flats. Six figures. Equipment budget. A crew of four who would answer to me and only me. The kind of offer that, eight months ago, would have had me signing before the ink dried on their good intentions.

I read it twice at Nadia's kitchen table while she hunched over her drafting board across the room, correcting a contour line with the focused hostility of a woman arguing with the earth itself.

"Good news?" she asked without looking up.

"A job."

"You have a job."

"I have a *project*. This is a job. There's a difference, apparently. Who knew."

She set down her pen and came around to look. She read faster than I did — cartographers always do, trained to extract meaning from density — and when she finished, she looked at me with an expression I'd learned to dread slightly more than lightning: patient, waiting, giving me enough rope to either climb or hang myself.

"Well?" she said.

"It's six figures."

"I can read."

"It's the eastern flats. Three seasons of chasing cells across open country with nothing but instruments and my own excellent judgment."

"That does sound like your excellent judgment's natural habitat." She handed the letter back. "So what's stopping you?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question, the one I'd been circling for weeks the way I used to circle storm cells — at a respectful distance, taking measurements, refusing to commit to an intercept.

What was stopping me was that I'd finally done the arithmetic. Not the debt arithmetic — that was settled, drowned in a flood channel along with my truck and most of my former life. This was different math. This was the math of a Tuesday morning in a kitchen that smelled like coffee and pencil shavings, where a woman I loved was redrawing a stolen claim so it could be given back to the grandchildren of men who'd been murdered for it, and where I had somehow become the person who drove her to the archive every Thursday and pretended not to be proud of it.

Six figures versus that. It wasn't even close, and the fact that it wasn't even close was the strangest thing that had ever happened to me.

"The eastern flats are flat," I said. "That's the whole problem with them. Nothing to look at except weather."

"There's weather here too."

"There's weather here," I agreed. "Terrible weather. Unpredictable. Floods every road we care about."

"Almost like it's trying to keep us."

I put the letter face-down under the sugar tin, which is where important things went to be ignored in my family growing up, and kissed the top of her head, and said nothing else about it, and we both understood that the nothing was the answer.

* * *

The claim resurvey had started as Nadia's project and metastasized into something larger than either of us had planned, which is how all honest work goes. Magistrate Okonkwo's warrants had moved through the courts with the speed everyone warned us about — glacial, procedural, punctuated by motions and counter-motions filed by lawyers whose hourly rates could have bought my old truck outright. But they had moved. Calloway's founder was awaiting trial. The 7th Survey Company's records were now evidence rather than contraband. And somewhere in the machinery of all that, someone had realized that before the court could restore the claim to the heirs, somebody had to establish exactly what the claim contained — its boundaries, its workings, its value — using modern methods, on the record, beyond dispute.

Nadia volunteered before Okonkwo finished asking. I volunteered approximately four seconds later, mostly because I knew she'd do it alone otherwise, walking into abandoned workings with a theodolite and a stubbornness that made Elias Wren look cautious.

The heirs themselves were a quiet surprise. Seven years of silence had done what seven years of silence always does: scattered people, hardened grief into ordinary life. But the survey crew had families, and the families had kept photographs and grudges and, in one case, a grandmother named Adaeze Okafor — no relation to Mr. Okafor, a coincidence she found funnier than anyone else dared to — who showed up at our first community meeting with a ledger book older than the syndicate itself and a memory like a court stenographer.

"You're the one who carried the papers," she said to me, not as a question.

"I'm the one who didn't sell them," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed. "But you thought about selling them."

I looked at Nadia, who had, of course, told her everything, because Nadia believed in full disclosure the way I believed in spare tires.

"I thought about it for about nine hours," I admitted.

Adaeze nodded slowly, closing her ledger. "Nine hours is honest. A lie would've been faster." Then she poured me tea, and that was that. The families of murdered men, I was learning, had no patience for purity and endless patience for truth. It was the opposite of the syndicate's economy, and I was never going back.

* * *

We worked the claim in the weeks between the last dry heat and the first real rain, which meant we worked it in air like a held breath. The mine adit where Elias had hidden his cache was ours to document first — Nadia insisted, and I understood why without her having to say it. Her father had spent his last days in that collapsed throat of rock, trading his shame for her future, and she was going to survey every meter of it herself if it killed her, which it nearly did twice, and which I mention here only because I promised myself this account would include the parts where love looks less like poetry and more like hauling a cartographer out of a subsidence crack by the backpack straps while she yells that she's fine, she's *fine*, the readings aren't done.

The readings got done. We tagged the old workings, mapped the adits against Elias's journal coordinates, ran new boundary lines that matched the 7th Survey's original plats almost exactly — which was, Nadia pointed out with grim satisfaction, the whole point. Calloway hadn't just stolen a claim; they'd stolen a *map*, and maps were her language, and she was going to make the earth testify in it.

I handled the vehicles, the logistics, the weather. The rains were coming early this year — I could feel it in my knees and the barometer both — and we raced the season the way we'd once raced each other, two women and a borrowed Land Cruiser and a small caravan of heir-family volunteers who turned out to be better field hands than any professional crew I'd ever run.

Some evenings, after the generators went quiet, I'd sit outside my tent and look at the dark line of the wash below the adit and think about the man who'd died in the mission clinic at dawn, asking nothing of his daughter and everything of me. I still didn't know if I'd forgiven him for the "no questions asked" clause. I suspected that was a negotiation I'd be conducting for the rest of my life, in installments.

* * *

Halvorsen sent a follow-up letter. Then a man — an actual human recruiter, flown out to the provincial capital, who tracked me down at the archive office where Nadia was filing plates and cornered me in the hallway with a smile and a folder.

"Ms. Iversen. Your reputation precedes—"

"My reputation has multiple versions," I said. "Which one preceded me?"

He laughed like I'd been charming instead of accurate. He laid out the numbers again. He mentioned a signing bonus. He mentioned, shrewdly, that the program would put me back in the field full-time, doing the thing I was demonstrably the best at, and that a person could only sit still for so long before the stillness started to feel like a cage.

I let him finish. It seemed rude not to, and besides, I wanted to hear the whole argument so I could watch it collapse.

"Here's my problem," I said. "You're offering me miles. Three seasons of them. And I've spent my whole life measuring myself in miles — distance covered, storms intercepted, ground gained. It's a way of staying ahead of the fact that none of it accumulates. You cross ten thousand kilometers of nothing and you arrive exactly where you started, which is inside yourself, broke."

"That's very—" he began.

"I'm not finished. What I've got here is seasons. Wet season, dry season, planting, harvest, the memorial in October, the archive opening in November. Things that accumulate. Things you can be *inside of* instead of passing through." I shrugged. "You can't pay me enough to go back to being a current instead of a coastline."

He looked at me the way recruiters look at people who quote geography metaphors at them, which is to say with the polite horror of a man realizing the commission is walking away. He left the folder anyway. I gave it to Nadia, who used the back page to draft a boundary amendment, which I consider the highest honor that document will ever receive.

* * *

The first rain came on a Sunday, three weeks into the resurvey, while we were packing the last of the equipment at the lower camp.

I felt it coming the way I always did — pressure dropping, the light going strange and green, the birds shutting up all at once like a jury reaching a verdict. But this time I didn't reach for the radio or the keys. I stood in the middle of the camp and let it build, because some storms you chase and some storms you stand under, and I was finally, after forty-odd years, learning to tell the difference.

Nadia came out of the supply tent and read the sky off my face.

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes. Maybe fifteen. It'll hit the ridge first, come down the wash." I paused. "Everything's buttoned up. There's nothing left to do but get wet."

She considered this. Then she walked over, took my hand, and led me out past the last of the equipment caches to the edge of the bluff above the wash — the same wash I'd nearly drowned in getting her satchel-side, the same channel that had swallowed my truck and my debt and, God help me, most of my excuses.

The rain arrived the way it always does in monsoon country: not gradually but *declaratively*, a wall of water and sound rolling down the valley like something with intent. It hit us sideways. Within seconds we were soaked through, laughing, shouting things neither of us could hear, and I stood there in the first honest rain of the season with my arms out and my eyes closed and thought: *this is the direction. This is it. Not north, not south, not away. Down. Into.*

Below us, the dry wash woke up. Water found the channel, then filled it, then claimed its banks, running brown and fast and alive toward the lowlands, carrying silt and seed and the year's first promise of green. A current for the monsoon. Elias had written something like that once, in the margins of his journal, in the entry Nadia finally let me read — something about how a river doesn't choose its course, it just refuses to stop, and eventually the land agrees.

We stayed until the lightning got close enough to be rude, and then we ran for the tents hand in hand, and that night, in a camp full of sleeping volunteers and the drum of rain on canvas, I wrote the last entry in a logbook I'd started keeping since the flood:

*Truck: lost. Debt: drowned. Contract: declined. Claim: surveyed. Names: read aloud. Rain: arrived.*

*Position: here.*

*Heading: also here.*

Nadia stirred beside me and asked, half-asleep, what I was writing.

"Coordinates," I said.

"For where?"

I clicked the pen closed and lay back and listened to the water moving through the country below us, going wherever it was going, unhurried, unstoppable, home.

"Nowhere," I said. "For once, nowhere at all."
