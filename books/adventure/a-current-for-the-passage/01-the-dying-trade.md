## 1. The Dying Trade

The debt collectors had learned to work in pairs.

Felix Blackwood noticed this the way a man notices weather — not because he wanted to, but because it affected everything he did. One would knock at the front of whatever boarding house he'd found that week, loud and official, while the second waited by the back stairs with the patient air of a man who had done this four hundred times and expected to do it four hundred more. It was efficient. It was almost admirable. Felix had spent eleven years as a railroad engineer and he could appreciate good systems even when they were being used to squeeze him like a wet rag.

So when he heard the knock at the front door of Mrs. Pell's boarding house on the evening of the ninth of March, he did not go to the front door. He went out the kitchen window instead, which was less dignified than the back stairs but considerably faster, and he was three streets away and buying a sausage roll with his last forty cents before the man at the back stairs had finished lighting his pipe.

"Evening," said the sausage vendor, wrapping the roll in paper gone translucent with grease.

"Evening," said Felix. "Lovely night for walking."

"It's raining."

"Lovely night for walking quickly, then."

He ate the roll under the awning of a shuttered chandlery, watching the rain stitch silver threads down the lamplight on Cordwainer Street, and he thought — as he thought most evenings now — about how a man's life could be measured in what he used to have. He used to have a position with the Coastal & Northern Railway. He used to have a set of drawings in his own hand that engineers three provinces over asked to see. He used to have a name that opened doors instead of closing them, and a salary that arrived on time, and a future that stretched out ahead of him like fresh-laid track: straight, graded, going somewhere.

Then came the Verrall Junction collapse, and the inquiry, and the finding — *error of judgment, engineer Blackwood* — and the board's letter informing him that his services were no longer required, and the creditors arriving within the month like gulls after a fish cart.

He had argued, at the inquiry, that the junction design was sound and the survey data he'd been given was wrong. This was true. But the survey data had come from the office of a director named Harrowgate, and Harrowgate had friends on the board, and so the truth had been weighed against influence and found lighter. Felix had learned since then that truth is a currency, and like any currency its value depends entirely on who's doing the counting.

The rain eased. Felix pulled his collar up and walked, because walking was free and standing still invited conversation, and conversation these days tended toward sums owed.

* * *

He found Aldous Crane in the freight yard behind the old Stationmaster's Row, and later — much later — Felix would wonder whether the man had been waiting for him specifically or whether dying men simply gravitate toward the one person in a town too broke to run.

Felix had cut through the yards because it was shorter, and because the Coastal & Northern had closed this spur six years ago and there was something in him that still couldn't pass a length of dead track without wanting to walk it, the way a priest can't pass an empty church. The rails ran off into the dark, rusted orange at the joints, weeds shoulder-high between the ties. Somewhere a switch lamp still burned, forgotten by whoever was supposed to forget it.

The man was slumped against the signal box at the yard's edge, and at first Felix took him for a drunk, which was unkind and also wrong.

"Blackwood," the man said.

Felix stopped. "Do I know you?"

"No." The man tried to stand, failed, and settled for lifting his head, which cost him visibly. "Aldous Crane. I know you, though. Knew your work. Verrall Junction wasn't your fault."

Felix felt that sentence land somewhere under his ribs, in a spot he'd guarded carefully for two years. "You're misinformed."

"I'm informed better than you are." Crane coughed, and the cough was wet and long, and when he brought his hand away from his mouth there was blood on it, dark in the lamplight. Not the blood of a sick man, Felix thought. The blood of a hurt one. There was a stain spreading down the prospector's left side, low, where a coat might hide a wound from casual eyes. "I've been looking for you three days."

"Then you've wasted three days. I'm nobody's answer anymore. Ask the bank. Ask the railway. Ask the gentleman who'll be knocking at Mrs. Pell's front door around now."

"They'll wait." Crane's eyes were gray and steady and altogether too clear for a man leaking life into a freight yard. "This won't."

"What won't?"

Crane reached inside his coat, slowly, wincing, and brought out a package wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord. Even before he loosened the knot, Felix could smell the wax — beeswax and resin, the seal of a man who meant what he sealed. Inside the oilcloth lay a map, folded twice, and Felix's hands — traitor hands, engineer's hands — itched to unfold it.

"Don't open it here," Crane said. "Open it when I'm done talking, because after I'm done talking you'll want to argue, and I haven't got the time to win the argument."

"That," said Felix, "is the most honest opening statement anyone's made to me in two years."

"I'm dying. You've seen the blood; no point pretending otherwise. And I've got a daughter in Port Calloway who hasn't spoken to me in nine years, and a map she doesn't want, and a promise I need somebody to keep." He held the package out. His arm shook with the weight of it, which couldn't have been more than a pound. "Carry it to her. Isla Crane, Port Calloway, the house on Gullrow Lane with the blue door. That's all. Carry it, put it in her hands, tell her—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Tell her I'm sorry, and that the second annotation matters more than the first."

"The second annotation?"

"You'll understand when you read it. Or you won't, and she will. Either way it has to reach her before—" He counted silently, lips moving. "Six tides. It has to reach her inside six tides."

"Why six tides?"

"Because after six tides it's worth nothing, and men kill for things worth nothing every day, God help us." Crane's voice roughened. "Two of them already tried. Down past Marlow Bend. I got away with this and not much else."

Felix looked at the map, then at the man, then down the dark spine of the dead rail line, where the switch lamp burned its small stubborn flame for no train at all. Everything in him — two years of hard-earned caution, the whole careful architecture of a man trying to owe nothing to anybody — said *walk away*. Dying strangers in freight yards were other people's problems. He had debts enough without inheriting a stranger's promises.

But there was a thing about a dying man holding something out to you. There is no negotiation with that. No clever exit, no window to climb out of. A living man asking a favor you can refuse, and refusal costs only awkwardness. But a dying man — a dying man doesn't ask. He simply holds it out, and the world stands aside and watches what you do, and whatever you do becomes the sort of man you are, permanently, on the record, no appeals.

"I'm not an honorable man," Felix said. "I want that understood up front. I take jobs for money, and I don't have money, and I owe people who are actively looking for me. Whatever you think you know about my work at Verrall Junction—"

"I know the survey numbers were doctored," Crane said flatly. "I know whose initials were on the original sheets, because I carried half of them myself, twenty years ago, when Harrowgate was a junior clerk fudging grades for the Coastal & Northern and I was running ore samples along that same line." He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, gray around the edges. "You were ruined by a man's arithmetic, Mr. Blackwood. I'm offering you a chance to be saved by mine."

Felix stood very still. Rain dripped off the signal box roof between them, counting seconds.

"That's a cruel thing to say to a man," he said finally.

"It's the only coin I've got left. Take it or don't." Crane pushed the package toward him. "Swear it. Not to me — I'll be past caring by morning. Swear it to her. Isla Crane. Port Calloway. Six tides."

And here is the thing Felix would never quite explain afterward, not to Marshal Vane, not to Isla herself, not to the firelit circle of listeners who eventually made him tell it: he did not swear because it was right. He swore because the man's arm was shaking, and because the rain was cold, and because there was no version of himself — not the disgraced engineer, not the debtor, not even the bitter man he'd become — who could look at that outstretched hand and let it hang there until morning.

"I swear it," Felix said. "Isla Crane. Port Calloway. Six tides. On my name, such as it is."

"On your name," Crane repeated, and something in his face unknotted, some tension two decades deep letting go all at once. "That's worth more than you think. Names come back, Mr. Blackwood. Debts get paid in strange currencies. You mark me."

He gave Felix instructions in short, economical bursts — the daughter's trade (boatwright), her temperament ("stubborn as ballast iron; don't take offense, it's not personal, it's structural"), the road to Port Calloway ("not the coast road; the old line, the decommissioned one, if your maps are as good as your reputation"). Then he pressed a canvas satchel into Felix's hands alongside the map — inside it, a letter sealed with the same wax, a water-stained notebook, and eleven dollars and sixty cents in mixed coin.

"For the passage," Crane said. "It's not much."

"It's eleven dollars more than I had."

"There's the humor Harrowgate never managed." Crane laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough again, worse, and Felix knelt and held the man's shoulders and felt how little of him there was left holding together. When it passed, Crane gripped Felix's wrist with surprising strength.

"Second annotation," he whispered. "Tell her. Second annotation matters more than the first."

Then his grip slackened, and his breathing went shallow and tidal — Felix would think of it that way later, tidal, in and out, each wave a little further from shore — and by the time the yard bell rang the half hour, Aldous Crane was done with tides altogether.

* * *

Felix sat against the signal box for a long while, the map in his lap, the rain starting up again soft as an apology.

He should fetch someone. The watchman, a constable, Mrs. Pell's nephew who dug graves and was never short of work. He should report it, give a statement, answer questions — questions about who he was, what he was doing in a closed freight yard at midnight, why the dead man's blood was on his coat sleeve. Questions were the one commodity he had in surplus.

Instead he unfolded the map.

Just a corner. Just to see. The wax seal cracked under his thumb with a small sound like a knuckle, and lamplight fell across hand-drawn coastline, depth soundings in a fine draftsman's script, and — his breath caught — a stretch of submerged rail line marked in red ink, running out beneath the water toward a shape annotated in Crane's tight, precise hand. And below it, in different ink, older, blotted: a second annotation, cramped and ciphered, that made no sense at all.

Six tides, the dying man had said. Men kill for things worth nothing every day.

Somewhere across the dark yards, a train whistle sounded on the live line — long, low, heading south — and Felix Blackwood, disgraced, indebted, and newly sworn, folded the map into his coat, stood up, and began to walk toward Port Calloway.

Behind him, the forgotten switch lamp burned on, faithful to a schedule no one would ever keep.
