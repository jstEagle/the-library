## 2. Six Tides

Isla Crane had learned to measure grief the way other people measured weather. Some days it came in as a fog, soft and gray and everywhere at once. Other days it arrived like a squall off the Sound, sudden and mean, and you just had to stand there and let it soak you.

Today it was a squall.

She was elbow-deep in the bilge of a client's skiff when the knock came at the boatyard door — three raps, polite as a church visitor. Nobody polite came to the Saltmarsh yard. Polite people went to Harrow & Sons up the channel, with their brass fittings and their invoices on cream paper. People who came to Saltmarsh wanted something patched, cheap, and without questions.

"Miss Crane?" said the man in the doorway. He had the look of a man who had slept in his coat, and the coat had lost. "Felix Blackwood. I've — that is, I'm carrying something for you. From your father."

The wrench in her hand went cold against her palm. She hadn't touched a wrench in three days, which was how long it had been since the constable's boy had come down the lane with his hat in his hands and his news in his mouth.

"He's dead," Isla said. It wasn't a question. It had stopped being a question the moment she'd heard the man's boots on her floorboards. "Whatever he sent, you can give it to the constable. Or the tide. They both deal in things people throw away."

"He didn't send it," Blackwood said carefully. "He gave it to me. Last night. In person."

Isla set down the wrench. "In person," she repeated. "My father has been dead to me for nine years, Mr. Blackwood. If he's taken up delivering things in person now, he's improved considerably."

The man had the grace to look uncomfortable. He was younger than she'd first taken him for — thirty, maybe, with a railroad man's sunburn and a clerk's worry lines, a combination she'd have found funny under other circumstances. His coat was good once. His boots had been resoled twice.

"He's dead," Blackwood said plainly. "He died last night, in the back room of the Anchorline Hotel. I was there. I'm — I was the last person he spoke to."

The squall hit her then, right in the chest, the way it always did. Nine years of not-writing and not-reading and not-asking, and it turned out the body kept its own ledger anyway. She gripped the gunwale of the skiff until the varnish bit her fingers.

"Say what you came to say," she managed.

* * *

He told it badly, which she later decided was the only honest way to tell such a story. A dying man in a hotel room. A wax-sealed map pressed into his hands. A promise sworn before a stranger could even get the word *no* out of his mouth. Blackwood talked fast and looked at the floor and kept touching his breast pocket, where the map sat like a coal.

"And you took it," Isla said, when he'd run out of breath. "From a stranger. A dying stranger. And your first thought was to bring it to the daughter he hadn't spoken to since before I could vote."

"My first thought was to throw it in the harbor," Blackwood admitted. "But he'd already paid for my room. And he held my hand while he made me swear. It's difficult to throw something in the harbor when a man is holding your hand."

Despite everything — despite the fog and the squall and the nine years — something in Isla's chest twitched toward a laugh. Her father had been a man who paid for rooms. Who bought rounds for strangers. Who came home once with a parrot he'd won in a card game and no money for the rent. *Your father has a talent for finding people who need him,* her mother used to say, *and no talent at all for the people who already do.*

"Let me see it," she said.

* * *

They sat in the yard's lean-to, out of the drizzle, and Blackwood unwrapped the map with the care of a man defusing something. It was a chart — a proper hydrographic chart, the old survey style — of the waters off Port Calloway. Someone had marked a position in grease pencil, a cross with a depth notation beside it: *eleven fathoms at low slack.* Around the cross, in her father's cramped, slanting hand — a hand she'd have known anywhere, that she'd seen on birthday cards that never came and apology letters that never came either — ran a scrawl of annotations.

She read them twice before she could breathe properly.

*Wreck of the *Cormorant Queen*, lost '61. Cargo manifest in my ledger, God and the harbor master willing. Salvage rights under concession permit — see the harbor office, permit class four, expires with the spring tides. Six tides, Isla. I counted them wrong, sweetheart. I always counted wrong. Give this to my girl and tell her the numbers are honest even if I wasn't.*

"Six tides," Isla said aloud.

"From the spring tide three nights ago," Blackwood said. "I asked at the harbor office on my way here. The concession on that stretch of water — the whole Meridian line, they call it — lapses at the sixth tide. After that the zone reverts, and the Meridian Salvage Fleet files claim on everything in it. Everything." He paused. "There's a fleet at anchor off Calloway Point already. Three steamers and a barge. They've been dredging the channel for a week."

Isla stared at the grease-pencil cross. Eleven fathoms. She'd grown up on these waters; she could feel eleven fathoms in her knees the way other people felt weather. The *Cormorant Queen* was a story from her childhood — the packet that went down in the squall of '61 with a cargo of railway iron and, if the tavern talk was true, a strongbox of company gold that never appeared on any manifest. Salvage men had hunted it for a decade and found nothing but eelgrass and disappointment.

And her father had found it. Her father, who could find anything except his way home.

"He was going to do it himself," she said. "He was going to raise it himself and come to me with it. Like a present."

"I think he was going to come to you with it," Blackwood said, "regardless of how it went."

She wanted to throw that sentence back at him. She wanted to say *you didn't know him, you knew him for one night, you knew a dying man, and dying men say whatever the dying say.* But she had read the annotation twice, and the hand was her father's, and the *sweetheart* was her father's, and there is no cruelty quite so precise as the cruelty of a dead man being exactly the person you remembered.

"Six tides," she said again. "And the sixth tide is when?"

"Dawn. Day after tomorrow's tomorrow." Blackwood rubbed his jaw. "I did the arithmetic at the harbor office. Twice."

"Twice."

"I'm an engineer. Or I was. We count things twice."

* * *

She made him tea because there was nothing else to do with her hands. The yard kettle was crusted with salt and the tea was terrible, and Blackwood drank it like it was a duty he'd sworn to, which, she was beginning to understand, was simply how the man did things. He'd sworn a promise to a dying stranger and now he was going to see it through if it killed him, and he looked, sitting on her upturned dory with his terrible tea and his ruined coat, like a man fully prepared to be killed by it.

"Why you?" she asked. "Out of everyone on that coast. Why did he pick you?"

Blackwood looked into his cup for a long moment. "I owe money," he said finally. "A great deal of it, to people who are not patient. Your father found me at the Anchorline and bought my dinner, and I think — I think he saw it. What I was. A man who counts things twice and still ends up on the wrong side of the ledger." He shrugged, a small, defeated motion. "Maybe he figured a man like that wouldn't steal from him. Too busy stealing from himself."

Isla almost smiled. Almost.

"And what do you get out of delivering it?"

"Nothing. He made me swear. That's all. A dying man leaves no room for refusal, Miss Crane. You'd know that if you'd been there." He said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not asking you to mourn him. I'm not asking you to forgive him. I'm asking you to look at a map and tell me whether there's anything in it, because I've stared at it for a day and I can read the depths but not the man."

* * *

The letter came out of the map's oilskin wrapping at the bottom, folded small, addressed in that same slanting hand: *For Isla, if she'll read it. If she won't, burn it and think no worse of me than you already do.*

She read it on the walk down to the waterline, alone, with the drizzle coming in off the Sound and the gulls arguing over something dead on the flats.

It was four pages. It was not an apology — or rather, it was an apology the way her father did everything, sideways and too late and with the numbers written in the margins. He wrote about her mother, who had died believing the worst of him, and how he'd let her, because the truth was uglier. He wrote about the nine years, and the letters he'd drafted and never sent, and the one he'd sent and she'd returned unopened, which he said he'd deserved and had kept anyway, because it was the last thing with her handwriting on it. He wrote about the wreck — how he'd found it by accident, hunting something else, and how he'd spent three years and every coin he had buying the survey time and filing the concession, and how he'd told no one, not even the men he drank with, because *the last thing I ever did right, Isla, I wanted to do it alone, and then I wanted to do it for you.*

The last page was short.

*The permit dies with the sixth tide. I'm not going to see it. Go or don't go. But if you go, take Blackwood with you — he's the only man I've met in ten years who counts things twice, and I've counted them twice, and the numbers are honest. Whatever else your old man was, the numbers are honest.*

Isla stood at the waterline until the drizzle had soaked her through, and the squall in her chest did something she hadn't expected. It didn't pass. It *hardened*. It settled down into her ribs and set like concrete, and when she turned around, Felix Blackwood was standing a polite twenty paces off, holding the oilskin wrapping, looking like a man prepared to wait out a season.

"The sixth tide," she said. "Dawn. Day after tomorrow's tomorrow."

"That's the arithmetic," he said.

"Then we've lost a tide already, standing here drinking your terrible tea." She held out her hand for the map. "There's a handcar shed at the old junction above Saltmarsh. The Meridian line runs closed from there to Calloway — closed to everyone but the company, and the company's men are all at anchor off the Point." She was talking fast now, her father's fast, the fast that got him into trouble and, once, apparently, onto a wreck eleven fathoms down. "But my father kept survey charts of every decommissioned cut on that coast, and I know where he kept them, and Mr. Blackwood — you said you were a railroad man?"

"Disgraced," he said, "but yes."

"Good." Isla tucked the map inside her coat, against her heart, where it sat like a coal of its own. "Then you can tell me which of those old lines will still hold a handcar, and I'll tell you which of them my father said would flood. Between us we might just beat three steamers to eleven fathoms of cold salt water."

Blackwood stared at her. "You're coming."

"I'm coming." She turned toward the yard, toward the tool shed, toward the work. "And Mr. Blackwood — the next tea you drink in my company, you'll make it yourself."

Behind them, out on the gray water, the first gulls of the evening tide came in low over the flats, and somewhere beyond the point, three steamers rode at anchor, counting down.
