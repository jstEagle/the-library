## 3. Closed Lines

The map did not want to be read at night, and neither did Felix Blackwood.

He'd discovered this somewhere past the second mile of the old Calloway spur, pumping the handcar lever while Isla Crane sat opposite him with a storm lantern balanced on her knees and an expression that suggested she was still deciding whether he was a rescuer or a kidnapper with excellent paperwork. The wax-sealed map lay open between them on the car's bedplate, and every time the lantern swung, the ink seemed to move — coastlines breathing, soundings shifting like fish under ice.

"Your father drew this," Felix said, mostly to fill the dark.

"He drew everything." Isla's voice was flat as a rail end. "Menus. Weather. The backs of envelopes. He never once drew a letter home."

Felix decided not to argue with grief. He had learned, in his years of falling from grace, that grief argued back and always won.

The handcar was a relic he'd bought off a section gang for less than the price of its own scrap value, which even the creditors hadn't thought worth repossessing. It had a name stenciled on the frame — *Persistent* — in paint so faded it might have been an accusation or a compliment. It ran on muscle and stubbornness, two commodities Felix had in surplus and nothing else.

Ahead, the rails ran silver in the moonlight, straight into country no timetable remembered.

"You're certain this line goes through," Isla said. "It's not on any chart I've seen."

"That's rather the point." Felix let go of the lever long enough to tap his breast pocket, where a folded sheaf of papers crackled like dry leaves. "I spent eleven years surveying these mountains for the Coast & Cascade. Every grade, every culvert, every line they built and then abandoned when the money men lost their nerve. When they struck me off the rolls, they took my wages, my pension, and my good name." He allowed himself a thin smile. "They forgot to take what's up here."

He tapped his temple this time. Isla looked at him the way people look at a man who has just compared his own head favorably to a bank vault.

"The Meridian agents took the coast road," she said. "We saw their lamps at the last crossing."

"We saw one lamp. There will be more. Meridian doesn't send one man where six will do, and they don't send six where twelve looks more convincing." Felix resumed pumping. "But the coast road follows the shore, and the shore follows the tide, and the tide is the only clock we can't cheat. We're going inland."

"Inland to where?"

"To the Kestrel Junction cutoff. Closed nine years ago. Washed out, they said." He grinned into the dark, and was surprised to find the grin genuine. "I surveyed it myself. It washed out because they cheaped the drainage, not because the route's bad. And nobody — *nobody* — expects a handcar on the Kestrel cutoff."

* * *

Dawn found them at the junction, and the junction found them wanting.

Felix stood in the middle of the switchyard with his old survey maps spread across a rusted water tower's shadow, feeling something he hadn't felt since before the inquiry: the particular pleasure of knowing exactly where he was.

"There." He stabbed a finger at a penciled line. "Kestrel cutoff branches north here, climbs along the river shelf, and drops us at Grayling Siding by tomorrow night. From Grayling there's the old timber tramway down to tidewater — rails still in, if the ties haven't rotted, and if they have, we carry the car. Either way, we come at Port Calloway from behind, over ground Meridian's charts say doesn't exist."

"And your maps say it does."

"My maps say worse things than that." He folded them away. "My maps once said the Cascade trestle would fail inside four years. I put it in writing. They buried the report, the trestle failed, and somehow the failure landed on me." He heard his own voice sharpening and made himself stop. "Forgive me. Old grievances are like old rails. You trip on them when you're not looking."

Isla was watching the coast road below the yard, where the morning mist was peeling back off the water. "There," she said quietly.

Two wagons, maybe three miles off, crawling along the shore road. Even at that distance there was no mistaking the Meridian livery — white hulls, black trim, painted the color of a shark's ambition.

"They'll reach the junction in an hour," Isla said.

"An hour and a half, if the driver stops to water the horses, and drivers always stop to water the horses." Felix was already moving. "But we won't give them the courtesy of being here when they arrive."

Getting the handcar onto the cutoff branch was a quarter hour of swearing and leverage that Felix performed with a fluency that surprised them both. There was, he reflected as he threw the rusted switch — testing the points twice, because a man who had been blamed for one disaster developed habits — a particular joy in doing the thing you were actually good at. For three years he had been competent at nothing but failing upward into ruin. Now every joint of rail, every grade marker, every culvert he'd walked in his surveying days rose up in his memory like old friends who hadn't heard about the scandal.

"Hold the brake side," he told Isla. "When I say *lean*, lean. This grade lies about itself."

The handcar gathered speed down the cutoff's first descent, wheels singing on rails that hadn't carried weight in nine years, weeds whipping at their ankles, and Isla Crane — solemn, grieving, unconvinced Isla Crane — began, very slightly, to laugh.

"What?" Felix called over the clatter.

"Nothing. It's only—" She pushed harder on her lever. "You look *happy*. It's unsettling."

"I am unsettled by it myself," Felix admitted. "Don't tell anyone."

* * *

They made Grayling Siding by late afternoon of the second day, having outrun the junction entirely, and it was at Grayling that Felix discovered they had acquired a passenger.

He noticed it first as a weight distribution problem. The handcar handled differently on the climb out of the river shelf — a fraction heavy at the rear, a fraction slow on the return stroke. A lesser engineer would have blamed the grade. Felix, who had been blamed for enough grades to know them intimately, stopped the car on a straight stretch and walked to the back.

A pair of boots stuck out from under the tool locker. Above the boots, folded with impressive economy into the space between the locker and the ballast hopper, was a young man — all elbows and freckles, with hair the color of wet straw and the fixed, hopeful grin of someone who has been caught many times and has never once let it ruin his day.

"Afternoon," said the young man.

"Afternoon," said Felix. "You're stealing our handcar."

"No, sir. Your handcar is stealing *me*. I was asleep in that locker at Grayling before you ever arrived. Legally speaking, I'm cargo." He unfolded himself by degrees, like a carpenter's rule, and stood swaying on the moving car with the balance of someone who had slept in worse places at worse speeds. "Tam Wicks. At your service. I can cook, mend, splice, lie credibly, and I know every water stop between here and the coast."

"You know," Isla said, "most stowaways wait until they're caught to invent their qualifications."

"These aren't invented, miss, they're aspirational. Mostly true. The cooking part is entirely true." Tam had already, Felix noticed, begun tidying the tool locker without being asked, and doing it well. "And I'll work for passage. Fair warning: I'll also talk. Constantly. It's cheaper than most vices."

Felix should have put him off at the next crossover. A sensible man, a careful man, a man whose name was currently a synonym for catastrophe in three boardrooms, would have set the boy down with a biscuit and a blessing and pumped on alone.

Instead he heard himself say, "Can you read a signal lamp?"

"Green means go, red means stop, blue means somebody's under the car, and yellow means the signalman's got opinions."

"Then sit down before you fall down," Felix said, "and tell me how you came to be asleep in a tool locker at a siding that hasn't seen traffic in nine years."

Tam settled onto the bedplate, pulled his knees up, and gave them a grin that didn't quite reach his eyes. "That," he said cheerfully, "is a longer story than the ride to Calloway."

"Then start with the short version."

The short version, delivered between pumps of the lever with the wind pulling the words apart, was this: Tam Wicks, seventeen, orphaned by the same coastal fever that had emptied half the fishing villages, had drifted up the line doing odd work for whoever paid in food, and had recently done a week of odd work for some white-hulled gentlemen who paid in coin and asked questions about handcars heading inland.

Felix's pump stroke faltered, just once.

"White hulls," Isla repeated. "Black trim."

"That's the ones." Tam watched them both with eyes that were older than the rest of him. "I didn't tell them anything, mind. Didn't know anything to tell. But I reckoned if those gentlemen were paying good money to know who's traveling the closed lines, then whoever's traveling the closed lines might be worth knowing too. And possibly worth helping." He shrugged. "Or possibly I'm just a boy who likes trains. Both things can be true."

Felix exchanged a look with Isla. It was the first look they had shared that wasn't suspicion, and it contained an entire negotiation: *Do we trust him? Can we afford not to?*

"We'll try you," Felix said at last. "One condition. If anyone asks you questions again — anyone, in any livery — you come tell me before you answer them. Even if the answer seems harmless. Especially then."

"Yes, sir." And for just a moment, the grin dropped away entirely, and what was underneath it looked remarkably like relief. "Yes, sir. That I can do."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the old Kestrel signal box, a wooden skeleton standing sentinel over track that ended in alder brush thirty yards on. Tam produced a tin of tea, a sack of hardtack, and — from somewhere — three fresh trout, which he cooked over a small fire with a skill that went a long way toward redeeming the rest of his mysteries.

"Where did you learn to gut a fish like that?" Isla asked.

"Where I learned everything, miss. Being useful is the only inheritance some of us get." He handed her the best of the three trout without appearing to notice he'd done it, which Felix suspected was precisely the trick of it.

Later, with the fire burned to coals and Isla asleep wrapped in her father's coat, Tam sat across the embers turning a stick in his fingers.

"That map of yours," he said quietly. "The one in the oilskin sleeve. The gentleman in the white hull asked after a map. Said it belonged to his company." His stick paused. "Said whoever was carrying it stole it."

Felix fed a twig to the coals and watched it catch. "Did he."

"Said it loud, too. The way men do when they want a story spreading faster than they can chase it." Tam looked up, and his young face was dead serious. "I've been thieved from enough times to know what thieves sound like, Mr. Blackwood. That wasn't it. But I figured you'd want to know the story's out there ahead of us, walking the same roads."

Felix nodded slowly and looked north, where the abandoned rails vanished into the dark toward tidewater, toward Port Calloway, toward a wreck that Meridian wanted badly enough to buy the truth out from under it.

Six tides, the dying man had said. They had spent two.

"Sleep, Tam," Felix said. "Tomorrow we run the timber tramway, and it's the worst piece of track I ever surveyed."

Tam grinned and rolled into his coat. "Worst you ever surveyed? Then it must be the best there is, sir, 'cause you strike me as a man who surveys honest."

Felix lay back and stared at the stars and found, to his considerable surprise, that he was smiling — that for the first time in three years, disgrace and all, he was exactly where he was supposed to be, doing the one thing he knew how to do.

Which was, of course, precisely when the light appeared on the ridge above them. One flash. Two. Three. Deliberate. Patient. Answered, a moment later, from somewhere far down the valley.

Someone knew exactly where they were.
