## 4. The Marshal's Shadow

The river at Corben's Crossing was the color of strong tea and moving like it had somewhere important to be. Isla stood on the near bank while Felix argued with the ferryman, who wanted three silver for the crossing and was holding out for four.

"Three," Felix said, "and I'll throw in the story of how I once derailed a locomotive with a chicken."

The ferryman, a broad woman with forearms like dock rope, squinted at him. "Was the chicken all right?"

"The chicken was fine. The locomotive was never the same."

"Four," she said, "because that's worth hearing twice."

Isla turned away to hide her mouth. It kept doing that lately — twitching upward when it had no business doing so. She had known Felix Blackwood for four days now, and in those four days he had been swindled by a liveryman, out-negotiated by a child selling apples, and beaten at cards by Tam Wicks, who cheated so obviously it was almost a compliment. And yet here they were, still moving south, still ahead of whatever it was that made Felix check over his shoulder every time a door opened.

Her father's map rode against her ribs in an oilcloth sleeve, and she touched it now without meaning to, the way you touch a bruise.

*Daughter,* his letter had said. *If you're reading this, I ran out of tides.*

She had read it eleven times. Each time she arrived at the same sentence and each time it landed like a stone dropped down a well — a long fall, then nothing.

"Miss Crane." Felix appeared at her elbow, ferry settled, looking pleased with himself in the way of a man who has paid too much but won't admit it. "We're on. Tam's already aboard pretending to help."

Tam Wicks was indeed aboard, sitting on the ferry rail with his boots swinging, watching the water with the expression of a boy who had never once been trusted near anything valuable. Which, Isla reflected, was probably accurate.

The crossing took them in slow, rocking stages. The current pulled at the barge and the ferrywoman cursed it with the fluency of long acquaintance. Halfway across, Isla saw Felix go still.

He wasn't looking at the water. He was looking at the far bank, where a figure sat a horse among the alders, motionless, waiting the way stones wait.

"That," Felix said quietly, "is trouble."

"You say that about everything."

"And I'm always right eventually. It's my one reliable talent."

* * *

The trouble dismounted as they came ashore. She was tall, gray-eyed, wearing a marshal's coat that fit her like it had been cut for someone else and altered under protest, and she held up a tin star on a chain so there could be no confusion about what she was.

"Marshal Edith Vane," she said. "Felix Blackwood. Isla Crane. And—" her gaze slid to Tam, "—a young man I have arrested twice and released both times out of pity."

"Third time's the charm," Tam said cheerfully.

"I'm not arresting anyone. Yet." Vane produced a notebook, small and swollen with damp, and opened it against her knee. "Aldous Crane died nine days ago in a room above the Salt & Iron in Harrowgate. Stabbed once. Door locked from inside. His effects were gone before the constable got up the stairs — except for one thing left behind on purpose, which tells me whoever searched him didn't know what they were looking for, or found it and wanted me to think otherwise."

Isla felt the map grow hot against her side. "What do you want with us?"

"Answers." Vane looked at her directly, and there was something in the look that Isla hadn't expected — not suspicion exactly, or not only. Something more tired than that. "Your father came through my territory last spring. He was looking for a wreck off the Meridian shelf. He bought charts, asked questions, paid a boy to watch the harbor office. Men don't do that unless they've found something worth killing for. Nine days later, he's dead in a locked room."

"You think it was murder," Isla said.

"I think locked rooms are a courtesy murderers extend to themselves." Vane flipped a page. "And I think the last person known to have spoken with Aldous Crane was a disgraced railroad engineer who left Harrowgate the same night, carrying a bag, heading south."

Every eye went to Felix. He bore it with the dignity of a man accustomed to being the least respectable person in any room.

"For the record," he said, "he was dying already. Bad lung, bad blood. He told me so himself. There was no stabbing done by me, Marshal, I promise you. I lack the stomach. I once fainted at a tooth-pulling."

"Convenient."

"It's the most inconvenient thing about me."

Vane studied him for a long moment, then turned to Isla. "You. Did you know your father was alive? Before this?"

"No." The word came out harder than she intended. "He'd been gone eleven years. I knew him as a name on letters that stopped coming. Then a stranger arrives at my door with a sealed map and a story about promises, and two days later a marshal is asking whether that stranger killed him." She heard her own voice rise and couldn't stop it. "So no, Marshal. I don't know anything. That appears to be my defining condition."

Something shifted in Vane's face — a softening she tried to disguise as professional patience. "That's fair," she said. "It's more fairness than I usually manage."

* * *

They walked up from the bank together, because Vane would not discuss business in the open, and because Felix said the ferrywoman charged extra for loitering. They ended up in the shade of the toll shed, where Tam stationed himself ostentatiously within earshot until Vane told him to go count the ferry ropes, and Tam went, radiating the grievance of the unjustly excluded.

"Here's what I know," Vane said, low. "Crane's death was clean. Too clean. A dying prospector doesn't get knifed in a locked room by chance. Someone wanted the map and wanted no witnesses, and they were patient enough to wait until he was weak." She snapped the notebook shut. "There's a salvage concern called Meridian buying up concessions along this whole coast. Their agents ask questions in every port between here and Calloway. Questions about a map."

Felix's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. Isla caught it. She was learning to catch things about Felix Blackwood — mostly because he was terrible at hiding them.

"So we're being followed," she said.

"You're being hunted. There's a difference. Followed means they want to know where you're going. Hunted means they want what you're carrying and would prefer you stop existing at the moment of transfer." Vane let that sit. "Now. I could take you back to Harrowgate. Statements, inquests, three weeks of paperwork while the tide clock runs out and whatever your father found belongs to Meridian forever. Or—"

"Or?" Felix prompted.

"Or you keep moving, and you send word if you find anything. Evidence. A name. Anything that points past a dead man who can't defend himself." Vane's voice dropped further. "I've been a marshal eleven years, Mr. Blackwood. Do you know how many murders I've closed? Four. Do you know why? Because the guilty can afford lawyers and the dead cannot afford anything. This one I want."

"Why trust us?" Isla asked. "You said yourself you suspect him."

"I suspect everyone. It's the job." Vane almost smiled; it was a small, dry movement, like ice cracking. "But I watched you just now, Miss Crane. When I mentioned the map, you put your hand over your ribs like it hurt. People guard what matters to them. Thieves guard it differently — they check the exits. You checked your father's letter." She paused. "Also, Mr. Blackwood has been staring at my horse this entire conversation with naked longing, which no murderer does. Murderers stare at doors."

"It's a very good horse," Felix said, wounded.

"She's adequate. Don't change the subject."

* * *

Vane gave them until sundown and no more. She wrote something in her sodden notebook, tore the page out, and handed it to Isla: an address in Port Calloway, a telegraph code-word — *PASSAGE* — and instructions terse as a surgeon's knot.

"If you find proof of who killed your father, wire that word to this office and I will come so fast it'll bend the rails. If you find nothing, wire anyway. Silence makes me nervous, and when I'm nervous I ride fast in unpleasant directions."

"Understood," Isla said.

"One more thing." Vane mounted, then looked down at the three of them — the engineer, the daughter, the drifter — arranged on the bank like figures in a painting titled *Poor Prospects*. "Meridian's lead agent is a woman named Sorrel Dane. If she finds you, don't argue, don't bargain, and don't believe a single word she says, especially the kind ones. Especially those." She gathered the reins. "Kindness from people like that is bait with better handwriting."

She rode north along the bank, and Isla watched until the alders swallowed her.

"Well," said Tam, emerging from behind the toll shed where he had absolutely not been counting ropes. "She liked me. I could tell."

"She's arrested you twice."

"Familiarity breeds fondness."

* * *

They camped that night in a cutting above the old line, where the rails ran green with moss and the firelight made small warm circles of the dark. Felix cooked — badly, with great confidence — and Tam told a story about a bear and a church raffle that Isla was fairly sure had never happened, and gradually the day's fear wore down to something manageable, like a stone smoothed in a pocket.

But she did not sleep. She sat with her back against a tie pile and turned the map case over in her hands, and thought about her father's last nine days. Dying in a rented room. Writing a letter to a daughter who hadn't answered his last three. Choosing, out of everyone in Harrowgate, a bankrupt engineer with fainting spells and a talent for overpaying ferrymen.

Why him?

Because he was cheap? Because he was desperate enough to say yes? Or because Aldous Crane had seen something in Felix Blackwood that Isla, in four days, had only glimpsed sideways — the way he'd talked the ferrywoman down and then quietly given the difference back as a tip; the way he'd checked Isla's boots for rot that first morning without saying anything, just leaving a better pair outside her door.

Or maybe, said a colder voice, her father had simply been out of options, and dying men grab whatever drifts past.

Across the fire, Felix sat hunched over his old railroad maps, tracing lines with a pencil stub, muttering numbers. He glanced up and caught her watching.

"Can't sleep?"

"Thinking."

"Dangerous. I recommend cards instead. Tam cheats, but honestly, so does the universe."

Despite herself, her mouth did the thing again. She smothered it. "Marshal Vane thinks you might have killed my father."

"I know. I've been turning it over." He said it plainly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "For what it's worth, I wish I'd never met him. And I'm glad I did. Both at once. It's an uncomfortable way to live."

"He trusted you with everything he had left."

"Yes." Felix looked into the fire. "Which is either the best evidence of my character or the final proof of his bad judgment. We'll find out by Calloway."

He went back to his maps. Isla lay down and pulled her coat to her chin, and the map pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Somewhere out in the dark, far off toward the ridge, a light blinked. Once. Twice. Then darkness again.

Tam, half-asleep by the embers, opened one eye and closed it very slowly, and said nothing at all.
