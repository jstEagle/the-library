## 5. What Tam Knew

The trouble with lying, Tam Wicks had learned by the age of nineteen, was not the invention of the lie. Lies came easy as breathing. The trouble was the arithmetic afterward — every lie a debt, and debts compounded. He'd been carrying Meridian's coin in his boot for eleven days now, and it weighed more than the gold ever could.

He crouched at the edge of the campfire, turning a tin cup of chicory he had no intention of drinking, and watched Felix Blackwood oil the handcar's bearings by firelight. The man worked like someone apologizing to the machine. Every motion careful, almost tender, as if the handcar might forgive him for something.

*You don't know the half of what needs forgiving,* Tam thought.

"So," Felix said, without looking up. "You going to tell us how you really got aboard, or shall I keep pretending I believe the story about falling asleep in a crate?"

Isla Crane looked up sharply from her father's letter, which she'd read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. "You *knew* he was a stowaway?"

"I knew the crate was nailed from the inside," Felix said. "Stowaways nail themselves in. Passengers don't." He set down the oil can and finally looked at Tam, and his eyes were tired but not unkind. "Question is what kind."

Tam's mouth went dry. Here it was, then. The crossroads. He'd rehearsed this moment on the third tide, on the fourth, on every rattling mile of dead rail between the washout and here — rehearsed the lie, that is, the good one, the one where he was just a drifter who liked the look of their train. But Isla was watching him now with her father's gray eyes, and Aldous Crane's letter was soft in her hands, and somewhere behind Tam's ribs sat the memory of an old man bleeding into the gravel at Kestrel Junction while a woman with a voice like cold tea told Tam to follow the map and report back.

"Meridian sent me," he said.

The word landed like a dropped wrench. Isla went still. Felix didn't move at all, which was worse.

"Trackers," Tam went on, because stopping now would be drowning halfway across. "They hire drifters off the wharves when they want eyes on something. Cheap. Expendable." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's me. Expendable. They gave me a description of you, Mr. Blackwood, and a description of the girl, and instructions to wire ahead from any station with a working line. I was supposed to be three junctions behind you by now, telling them everything."

"And instead?" Isla said quietly.

"Instead I'm sitting at your fire drinking your chicory, which tastes like boiled rope, by the way, no offense—"

"Tam."

"—instead I ran." He pulled the Meridian scrip from his boot and tossed it into the fire before he could think better of it. It curled and flared, green ink going black. Eleven days of wages. It felt wonderful and terrible at once. "They paid me half up front. I took it because I owed people, and the people I owe don't accept apologies. And then I found out what happened to Mr. Crane, or part of it, and I—" His voice did something embarrassing. He pushed through it. "I couldn't be the one who led them to his daughter. Not after seeing him."

Felix fed another stick to the fire. "Seeing him where?"

And there it was. The second crossroads, right on the heels of the first, and Tam discovered he had nothing left in him but the truth, worn smooth as river stone from being turned over all night in his mind.

"Kestrel Junction. Nine days ago. I was coming off a freight car when I heard arguing by the concession office. An old prospector and a woman. She had a Meridian pin on her collar and a way of talking like she was reading terms and conditions aloud. Dane. Sorrel Dane. I only knew the name because everyone on the wharf knows it." He stared into the flames. "The old man wouldn't hand over a map. There was a scuffle. I didn't see the knife go in — I swear I didn't — but I saw her standing over him after, and I saw her wipe the blade on his coat like she was cleaning a fish. Then she saw me."

"You ran," Felix said.

"I ran." Tam's laugh cracked in the middle. "First smart thing I've done all year, maybe. Second smart thing was jumping your handcar instead of reporting. But I'll tell you what keeps me up nights, Mr. Blackwood: I stood there a full ten seconds before I ran. Ten seconds doing sums. Whether she'd pay me for silence." He looked up, and his eyes were burning. "I did the sums. That's who I am. I always do the sums. And I am so damned tired of it."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, the river muttered to itself about the storm two days past.

It was Isla who finally moved. She crossed the camp, sat down beside him on the log — close enough that he could see the letter folded in her pocket — and said, "My father trusted strangers. It killed him, probably. But he trusted them because he believed most people were one honest chance away from being decent." She held out her hand. "Consider this your chance. Spend it well."

Tam shook it. Her grip was stronger than he expected, and steadier than his own.

* * *

He should have slept well after that. Confession, they said, was good for the soul. What nobody mentioned was that confession only settled the old debts; it opened new lines of credit immediately. Because Tam hadn't told them everything. He hadn't told them about the signal schedule — flash twice for position, once for direction, dark for lost trail. He hadn't told them that if he missed his third check-in, Meridian wouldn't send trackers anymore. They'd send Dane.

And he hadn't told them the thing that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone: that he wasn't sure, even now, even after the fire and the handshake and Isla's steady gray eyes, that he was done doing sums.

He lay awake on his bedroll listening to the others breathe. Felix slept like a man keeping one ear on duty, which Tam suspected was how disgraced engineers slept everywhere. Isla slept curled around her father's letter like it was warm. And Tam lay flat on his back counting the stars through the pine branches and losing count on purpose, because counting reminded him of sums.

Around midnight he heard it.

Not a sound exactly. A light. A flicker high on the eastern ridge, above the tree line, where no honest traveler would be at midnight on a dead line with no town within twenty miles. Once. Twice. A pause. Twice again.

His blood went cold and then hot and then cold again. He knew that rhythm. He'd used that rhythm himself, twice, from hilltops, before the shame caught up with him and he'd started missing his check-ins on purpose.

Someone else was on Meridian's payroll. Someone who'd been closer than three junctions behind.

Tam sat up slowly, heart hammering, and looked across the dying fire at Felix Blackwood. The engineer's eyes were open. Had been open, Tam realized, the whole time.

"Tell me you saw that," Tam whispered.

"I saw it." Felix rose without a sound, all that tenderness toward machines gone hard and quick. He woke Isla with a hand on her shoulder, and when she understood — Tam watched it land in her face, watched the trust she'd handed him at the fire curdle into doubt — she looked straight at Tam.

"Who else knows we're here?" she asked.

And Tam Wicks, who had spent his whole life doing sums, found that the answer required none at all.

"Dane," he said. "She's not behind us anymore. She's above us."
