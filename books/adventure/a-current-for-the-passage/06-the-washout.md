## 6. The Washout

The storm announced itself the way an unwelcome relative does — a long, grumbling approach, then suddenly everywhere at once.

Felix felt it first in the rails. He had his palm flat against the cold steel of the old Kestrel Branch line, reading it the way other men read weather in their knees. There was a hum in the metal that hadn't been there an hour ago, a faint tremor running up from somewhere south.

"Storm's coming down the valley," he said. "Fast."

Tam looked up from where he'd been coaxing the handcar's chain back into cooperation. "You can tell that from *touching* it?"

"You can tell a lot from touching things. It's why I stopped shaking hands with creditors." Felix swung up onto the car. "Come on. There's a maintenance shed two miles ahead. We'll want walls tonight."

Isla climbed aboard without argument, which was itself remarkable. Three days ago she would have asked him to justify every mile. Now she simply braced herself against the frame and pulled her oilskin hood forward, and Felix tried not to feel too pleased about it. Trust, he was learning, was like a rail joint — you didn't notice it until it started to hold weight.

They made the shed as the first fat drops came down, big and warm and smelling of the sea forty miles off. The building had once housed section crews; now it housed a rusted stove, a colony of mice with strong opinions, and enough dry timber to keep a fire going. Tam got the stove lit while Felix stood in the doorway watching the rain come across the valley like a gray curtain being drawn by an impatient hand.

"The tunnel," Isla said quietly, beside him.

He nodded. The Kestrel Tunnel lay two miles beyond them, bored through a shoulder of granite half a century ago. His old railroad maps showed it sound as of the last survey — but surveys were done in fair weather by men who wanted their dinners.

"If the creek above it rises," she went on, "the crown could—"

"I know what it could do."

"I wasn't doubting you. I was asking if *you* knew."

Felix glanced sideways. There it was again — that dry edge to her, the one that reminded him uncomfortably of her father. Aldous Crane had used the same tone on him once, years ago, over a mislabeled shipment of bridge iron. *I wasn't doubting you, Blackwood. I was asking if you knew.* Some inheritances don't wait for probate.

"I know," he said. "And we'll know more at dawn."

* * *

Dawn told them everything they feared and none of what they hoped.

The rain had slackened to a steady drumming, but the creek that crossed the line a mile short of the tunnel had become something else entirely — a brown, muscular torrent hauling whole trees along like kindling. They walked the last mile because the ballast was underwater in places, and came around the final curve to find the tunnel mouth wearing a new expression: a fan of mud and shattered rock spilled out across the tracks, still trickling, still settling, as though the mountain were clearing its throat and might not be finished.

Tam whistled low. "That's a lot of mountain between us and Port Calloway."

"It's not the mountain I'm worried about." Felix crouched at the edge of the washout, studying the debris. The collapse had come from inside — a section of crown maybe thirty yards in, brought down by saturated ground. Clearing it would take a crew, powder, and a week they did not have. Two tides gone already. Four left after this one.

"So we go back," Isla said. "Around through Marrow Junction. Add a day."

"A day we may not have, and a junction Meridian will be watching, because it's the only sensible route." Felix straightened and turned, looking back down the valley the way they'd come. Then he looked up.

The trestle.

It stood a quarter-mile downstream where the creek cut through a gorge before joining the river proper — the old Kestrel viaduct, timber on stone piers, built to carry freight over water that was supposed to stay polite. In flood stage it would be madness. In flood stage it might also be the only way across the gorge to the far side, where a forgotten logging spur climbed out of the valley and hooked north toward the coast line.

"That's not sensible either," Isla said, following his gaze.

"No," Felix agreed. "But Dane isn't sensible either, and that's the point. Nobody sane crosses the Kestrel viaduct in flood. Which means nobody will be waiting for us there."

"Nobody sane," Tam repeated. "Just checking we're all clear on the terms."

* * *

Getting the handcar to the viaduct meant manhandling it along a flooded stretch of ballast, water sloshing over the ties, all three of them pushing and swearing while the creek tried its best to take their boots as souvenirs. By the time they reached the foot of the structure, Felix's arms were burning and his heart was doing arithmetic it didn't like.

Up close, the viaduct was worse than it had looked from a distance. The near span had lost part of its railing to some previous flood; the deck timbers glistened black with spray, and beneath them the creek roared through the gorge with a voice like a crowd angry about something. Spray leapt halfway up the piers. The whole structure shivered — not much, just enough to remind them it was wood, and old, and being asked a question it had never been built to answer.

"We walk it," Felix said. "Car goes last, on the flat section, once we've tested the spans. One at a time except for the car. Rope around our waists, tied off ahead."

"To what?" Tam asked. "There's nothing ahead."

"There'll be a pier every forty feet. We tie off to the piers as we go. Slow is smooth, smooth is fast."

"That's not a real saying."

"It is now. I just invented it, and I'm the engineer."

Isla was looking at the water below. Felix watched her do it — watched her measure the drop, the current, the white water smashing against the second pier — and saw her arrive at the same number he had. Falling in meant dying, probably quickly, possibly slowly, and no version of it was good.

"All right," she said.

They went.

The first span was tolerable. The second was where the wind found them, funneled up the gorge, shoving sideways with wet fists. Felix went first, rope trailing, testing each timber with his heel before committing his weight. Halfway across, a gust hit him broadside and lifted him half off the deck; he dropped to his knees and hugged a stringer and heard Tam behind him make a noise that was either a prayer or a very creative curse.

At the third pier, the trouble found them.

A log — a big one, the trunk of something that had once shaded a whole hillside — came down the creek and struck the pier below them. The impact traveled up through the structure like a hammer blow through a fence post. The deck lurched. And the section of railing Isla was holding gave way.

She didn't scream. That was the thing Felix remembered afterward, the thing that visited him at night for weeks afterward: she didn't scream. She simply went from standing to sliding, boots skidding on wet timber, body tipping toward the gap where the railing had been, toward the brown chaos below — and her hand shot out and caught the remaining post, and held, barely, her whole weight swinging out over the water on one arm.

Felix was moving before he decided to move. Later he would try to reconstruct the decision and find there wasn't one — just his body doing what thirty years of rails and bridges had trained it to do, which was to get to the broken place faster than sense could object. He dropped flat, hooked his leg around the surviving railing post, and slid out along the deck until his hand closed around her wrist.

Her arm was already trembling. The current below seemed to lean toward her, patient and enormous.

"I've got you," he shouted, over the water, over the wind. "Isla. Look at me. I have got you."

"I can't—" Her fingers were slipping on the wet post. "My grip—"

"Let go of the post. Both hands to me. Now."

She looked at him — really looked, the way you look at a thing when you're deciding whether your life belongs to it — and let go.

The jolt nearly took him off the deck. His leg wrenched against the post, tendons screaming, and for one long moment they were both hanging over the gorge, connected by his grip and nothing else, while the creek roared its appetite below. Then Felix hauled, inch by inch, arm burning, teeth gritted, until her knee found the deck and then her elbow and then she was up and crawling away from the edge, gasping, soaked to the bone, alive.

Tam reached them with the rope and they tied off properly this time, and the rest of the crossing passed in a blur of wet timber and counted breaths. When they finally stepped off the far abutment onto solid ground, Isla sat down right where she was, in the mud, and laughed — a shaky, disbelieving laugh that cracked in the middle.

"You didn't let go," she said.

"I'm aware. My arm filed a complaint."

"I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "On the mountain road, at the junction, I thought you were the kind of man who calculates. Who lets go when holding costs too much."

Felix sat down beside her in the mud, because standing suddenly seemed ambitious. "I am exactly that kind of man," he said. "That's rather my reputation. But there are costs and there are costs."

She looked at him for a long moment, rain streaming off her hood, and whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something. "My father trusted you," she said. Not a question anymore. A finding.

"He trusted me with a map. Let's see if I'm worth the rest of it."

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a logging camp on the far slope, under a roof that leaked philosophically rather than practically. Tam got a fire going in an old stone ring, and Isla dried her boots, and Felix lay awake listening to the creek still roaring in the gorge below, quieter now, its tantrum subsiding.

It was Tam who spotted it. Felix heard him go quiet mid-sentence, and followed his stare up the slope, to the ridgeline dark against the clouds.

A light. Small, steady, wrong. Not a campfire — a signal lamp, shuttered and opening, three flashes, pause, three flashes. Someone on the ridge was talking to someone else in the dark, and the direction of the conversation was unmistakable: down-valley, toward the washout, toward the route any pursuer would assume they'd taken.

Or toward the route they'd *have* taken, if a flooded viaduct hadn't persuaded them otherwise.

"Dane," Isla breathed.

"Or someone working for Dane." Felix kept his voice low. "Either way, they're looking for us on the wrong side of that gorge. We bought ourselves a night, maybe two." He stared at the light until it winked out, and felt the small, cold satisfaction of a gambler holding a card nobody knew he had.

Then, closer — much closer — a branch snapped in the trees below the camp.

All three of them went still. The fire popped. Somewhere down the slope, in the dripping dark, something moved again — deliberate, heavy, and heading their way.
