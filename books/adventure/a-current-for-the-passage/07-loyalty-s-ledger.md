## 7. Loyalty's Ledger

Tam Wicks had learned to count in a house where counting was survival — how many spoonfuls of gruel before his sister noticed hers was lighter, how many steps from the door to the coal bin before the cold got into your boots, how many coins short of the rent you could be before the landlord's fist came through the door. Counting was the first thing he'd ever been good at.

He was good at it still. He counted everything now: the miles on the handcar, the tides left on the permits, the number of times Isla Crane laughed in a day (three, on a good one; twice, since the washout), and the number of times Felix Blackwood looked at him sideways when he thought Tam wasn't watching.

That last number was going up.

* * *

It started small, the way debts always do. A scrap of paper in Tam's coat pocket, folded to the size of a thumbnail. On it, in pencil worn nearly to nothing: their last junction, and the hour they'd passed it. He'd written it at the river crossing, two nights back, when the fear had been sitting on his chest like a cat that wouldn't be shooed. He'd meant to burn it. He'd meant to burn it every night since.

Instead he kept writing them.

The system was simple, because Meridian liked simple. A chalk mark on the milepost — a short stroke for direction, a long one for speed. Dane's riders swept the line behind them; they'd been sweeping it since the first junction. Tam knew the sweep pattern because he'd helped design it, back when he'd been a clerk in the Meridian freight office in Calloway, back when "clerk" had seemed like a fine thing to be. Back before he'd borrowed against his own wages to cover a shortfall that turned out to be his supervisor's embezzlement, and discovered that in Meridian's ledger, an error in your column was a debt no honest wage could ever pay.

They didn't want money from Tam Wicks. They wanted the map.

And so, on the fourth tide, while Felix argued with a rusted switch stand and Isla walked the rails ahead humming something her father used to sing — Tam knew this because Aldous Crane had hummed it too, in the freight office, in the weeks before he died — Tam crouched by the milepost and drew his little strokes in the dust, and hated himself with a thoroughness that would have impressed a tax assessor.

"You're crouching like a man who's lost a coin," said Isla, from ten feet away.

Tam stood up so fast his knee cracked. "Checking the rail gauge," he said. "Felix says some of these old lines were laid narrow. You'd want to know before you put a handcar on them."

"Felix checked the gauge half a mile back."

"Double-checking," said Tam. "It's called rigor."

Isla looked at him the way she looked at weather coming in off the water — not hostile, just taking its measure. Then she shrugged and walked on, and Tam let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the river crossing.

The trouble with lying to good people, he reflected, was that they believed you. It made the whole enterprise feel unsporting.

* * *

That evening they camped in the lee of a cut bank, and Felix built a fire small enough to hide under a hat, which was a skill Tam decided he'd have to steal someday. They ate beans. Felix told a story about a stationmaster he'd once known who could judge a train's weight by the sound of it on the bridge, and Isla said, "You're making that up," and Felix said, "I am entirely not, his name was Osgood and he charged a penny for the privilege," and Isla laughed — third laugh of the day, Tam noted, and hated himself for noting it, because noting it meant he was already saying goodbye to these people in his head, keeping a ledger of what he'd lose.

Afterward, when the others slept, Tam sat up with the fire and took out his pencil stub and his little folded papers, and that was when Felix Blackwood cleared his throat from the darkness like a man announcing a bill at a hotel desk.

"Don't get up," Felix said, settling onto the log beside him. "Or do. Either way, I've been sitting over there listening to you sigh for twenty minutes, and a man can only take so much secondhand misery before supper goes bad in him."

"I'm fine."

"You're the opposite of fine. You're a walking advertisement against fine." Felix held out his hand, palm up. Not grabbing. Just open. "Show me the papers, Tam."

Tam's mouth went dry as chalk dust. "What papers?"

"The ones you've been folding into eighths and sleeping with under your collar like a love letter from somebody's disapproving mother." Felix's voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "I ran railroads for eleven years, son. You think I don't know what a man looks like when he's carrying a schedule?"

For a long moment the fire popped and the river muttered somewhere below the bank. Then Tam laughed — a thin, awful little laugh with nothing in it — and dug the papers out, and put them in Felix's open hand, because it turned out that when the moment finally came, the relief of putting it down was stronger than the terror of what came after.

Felix unfolded them. Read them. His face did something complicated and then settled into something very quiet.

"Mileposts. Times. Direction of travel." He looked up. "How long?"

"Since the crossing."

"And the marks on the posts?"

Tam nodded. His eyes were stinging and he willed them not to, and failed. "Chalk. Short stroke means we're heading south, long stroke means fast. Their riders sweep the line every—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Every six hours. I know the pattern. I helped write it."

Felix set the papers down on his knee, carefully, like they might go off. "Why?"

And there it was. The question Tam had been rehearsing answers to for four days, all of them lies, and now that it arrived he found he only had the truth, and the truth was so heavy he could barely lift it.

"Because I owe them," he said. "Not figuratively. There's a book. Meridian keeps a book of what people owe, and my name's in it, and the interest isn't money. Money you can earn. What I owed was — use. And if I didn't give use, then the book gets read aloud. To the harbor board, to the constables, to whoever matters. My sister's name is in my column too, because I signed for her lodging with company credit." He wiped his face with his sleeve, roughly, like punishing it. "Aldous Crane knew about the book. That's partly why he ran. And when he came through the office that last week, asking questions about concession permits, they gave me the job of finding out where his map went. Track the map, clear the page. That was the deal."

"And instead you stowed away on our handcar."

"Instead I ran," Tam said. "I told myself running was the same as refusing. It isn't. Running just means you keep carrying it." He looked at the fire. "So I started leaving the marks. Little ones. Chalk on a post, nobody hurt, right? Buy time. Tell myself I'd stop when we got close enough to Port Calloway that it wouldn't matter. But it always mattered, and I never stopped, and tonight I was going to write down this camp, and you were sitting there listening to me sigh."

Felix was silent a while. When he spoke, his voice had a rasp in it. "Dane's riders will find tomorrow's mark by midmorning. We'll be three hours ahead of them instead of eight."

"I'm sorry." It came out small. Tam made himself say it again, bigger. "I'm sorry. You've been decent to me. Both of you. She calls me 'the boy' but she saves me the crusty end of the bread because she thinks I like it, and I let her think it, and the whole time I was—"

"Telling Meridian where the crusty end of the bread was sleeping," Felix finished. "Yes."

Tam flinched. Felix held up a hand.

"I'm not going to pretend I'm not angry," Felix said. "I'm furious. I'm furious the way you're furious at weather — at something big and stupid that rains on everybody. Because here's what I know about Meridian's book, Tam. I'm in it too. Different column. Same ink." He fed a stick to the fire. "Three years ago I signed a route survey I hadn't verified, because the man above me told me to and because I was tired and behind schedule and flattered. Eleven men were on the first train over that trestle. Nobody died. Somebody could have. The board needed a name and mine was the cheapest, and ever since, every door in the territory has had the same word written on it in the same hand: *debt*." He glanced over. "So don't tell me what it costs to survive inside somebody else's ledger. I know the exchange rate."

"What are you going to do?" Tam asked. "Turn me out? Tie me up? Turn me over to Vane?"

"Marshal Vane is two days behind us and gaining, and I suspect she'd ask you better questions than I would." Felix rubbed his jaw. "No. Here's what happens. Tomorrow you leave your mark like always — same post, same chalk, same hour. Except the mark is going to say we camped somewhere we didn't, and moved faster than we did, and I'm going to help you draw it, because I've spent my life reading schedules and I'd like to spend one evening writing one."

Tam stared at him. "You want to feed them false marks."

"I want to buy us back the hours you sold. After that—" Felix's voice roughened. "After that, you're going to tell me the rest of it. All of it. Whatever you're still holding back, because I can hear it rattling around in you like a bolt in an empty car."

And there it was again — the other thing, the true thing, the thing that had been sitting under his ribs since the river crossing, heavier than all the chalk marks combined. Tam looked at the fire. Looked at the dark shape of Isla's bedroll, thirty feet away, breathing slow.

"She doesn't know," he whispered. "About her father."

"No," Felix said quietly. "She doesn't."

* * *

Tam told it the way you pull a splinter — fast, because slow is worse.

"They sent me to follow Crane in the last week. Watch where he went, who he talked to. I followed him to a warehouse on the tidal flats, past the freight yard. Dane was already there. I was supposed to stay outside. I heard most of it through the wall." His hands had gone cold, though the fire was close. "Crane wouldn't give up the map. Dane asked polite, twice. Then not polite. There was a — a noise. Like a crate dropping. Only wrong. And then nothing for a long time, and then Dane came out alone, wiping his hands, and he looked at me standing there like a fool with my notebook, and he smiled, and he said, 'Well, Wicks. Now you're really in the book.'"

Beside him, Felix had gone absolutely still.

"He didn't slip on the stairs," Tam said. "Whatever the constable wrote, whatever the harbor board believes — Crane didn't fall. Dane killed him for the map, and the map wasn't on him, and that's why they need me. Why they need *us*. Because the map walked out of that warehouse in a dead man's plan, and Dane has been chasing it ever since, and every mark I chalk on a milepost helps the man who killed her father find his daughter." His voice broke on the last words and he let it. "There. That's the whole ledger. Every line of it. Do what you want with me."

Felix didn't move for a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and steady and terrible, the voice of a man reading a schedule for a train that was going to arrive whether anyone liked it or not.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we draw Dane a beautiful lie. And the day after, Marshal Vane gets a full confession — yours, in front of witnesses, if I have anything to say about it. And Isla—" He stopped. Rubbed his eyes. "Isla deserves to hear it from you, not from me, and not on the run. But not tonight. Tonight she sleeps."

From across the dead fire, Isla's voice, thick with waking: "Hear *what* from him?"

The fire had burned to coals, and in their light Tam saw her sit up, saw her eyes go from him to Felix to the little folded papers in Felix's hand, and watched her understand — the way she understood everything, quickly and completely and without mercy — that the shape of her whole journey had just changed.

"Tell me," Isla Crane said, and there was nothing gentle in it at all.
