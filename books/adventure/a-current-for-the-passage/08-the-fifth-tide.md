## 8. The Fifth Tide

Isla Crane had imagined Port Calloway a hundred times on the long road down, and in every imagining it was smaller than this. In her mind it had been a fishing village with one crooked pier and a church bell gone green with salt. What rose out of the morning haze instead was a proper harbor town — three tiers of shingled roofs climbing the headland, a forest of masts, and beyond the breakwater, riding at anchor like crows on a fence line, the Meridian fleet.

Four ships. Steam cranes folded against their decks like sleeping herons, derricks swaying gently, and at their center, flying a house flag she now knew too well, the salvage barge *Iron Meridian* herself, squatting directly over the charted position of the wreck.

"They're here," Felix said, as if she might have missed them. He stood at the rail of the coastal steamer they'd paid their last decent coin to board, his knuckles white on the varnish. "They beat us."

"No," Isla said. She heard her own voice come out flat and strange, the way it did when grief put on its work clothes. "They didn't beat us. They got here first. Those are different things."

Tam Wicks, leaning against the rail a careful distance away — he'd been keeping a careful distance from everyone since the night of the confession — said nothing at all. He just looked at the fleet with an expression Isla couldn't read and didn't much want to.

The steamer slid into the harbor past a customs hulk, past oyster boats and a coal barge, and tied up at the commercial pier beneath a sign reading CALLOWAY PORT AUTHORITY in letters that had once been gold. The air smelled of tar, fish, and someone's breakfast frying, and under all of it, faintly, the iron smell of the sea itself.

"We have until dawn," Felix said. He'd said it four times since the trestle, like a man counting coins over and over to be sure they hadn't shrunk. "Permits expire at first light. The tide turns at—" he consulted his watch, that battered railroad watch he checked the way other men checked their consciences, "—eleven minutes past six. We have until dawn."

"Then we shouldn't spend any of it standing here admiring the view." Isla shouldered her father's satchel. It was lighter than it had been; the map lived inside her coat now, against her heart, where she'd taken to carrying it after the washout. After the water. After Felix's hand closed around hers in the flood and did not let go, not for anything, not even when the current tried its level best.

She still hadn't thanked him properly for that. There hadn't been time, and there wasn't language big enough anyway, so she carried the debt the way she carried the map: close, and quietly.

* * *

The harbormaster's office smelled of pipe smoke and old paper, and the harbormaster himself — a round, sunburnt man named Quill who looked like he'd been built by stacking barrels — listened to their story with the expression of a man who had heard every story the sea could produce and believed perhaps a third of them.

"Concession zone's out past the point," he said, tapping a chart gone soft at the folds. "Wreck sits in forty fathoms, if your paper's honest. Meridian anchored there yesterday evening. Their agent registered the survey claim same hour." He glanced up. "You'd be the Cranes, then. The ones the wire's been asking about."

Isla's stomach dropped. "Asking about how?"

"Missing persons inquiry." Quill shrugged, apologetic. "Filed by the salvage company. Says you're carrying stolen property and may be in distress." He smiled without warmth. "Meridian's thorough. I'll give them that."

Felix made a sound low in his throat. "They killed her father and filed him as a missing person."

"That," said a voice from the doorway, "is precisely the sort of thing I intend to prove."

Marshal Edith Vane filled the frame the way weather fills a window. Her coat was travel-stained, her horse was lathered behind her, and her eyes went around the room cataloguing everyone in it before settling on Tam Wicks with the particular attention a cat gives a mouse that has recently done something interesting.

"You," she said to Tam. "You're the one who ran messages for Dane."

"Yes, ma'am," Tam said, and to his credit he didn't flinch. Much.

"And you've got testimony for me."

"I've got testimony. Whether it holds is another matter."

"It'll hold if I say it holds." Vane came into the room and laid two documents on Quill's desk with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments. "Warrants. One for Sorrel Dane, questioning in the death of Aldous Crane. One for the seizure of any Meridian property connected to said death." She looked up, and for the first time something almost human moved behind the official mask — something tired. "Here's my difficulty. The warrants are paper. Dane is a company man with company lawyers, and my witness is a confessed spy who worked for the man he's accusing. A magistrate would laugh me off the bench."

"So get better proof," Isla said.

Vane turned to her slowly. "And where would you suggest I find that?"

"On the wreck." Isla set her father's satchel on the desk and opened it. "My father dove that site twice last spring. He kept a logbook. If Dane killed him to keep him off that wreck, then the wreck itself is the evidence — whatever my father found down there, whatever Dane was afraid of. You can't question a dead man, Marshal, but you can raise one."

Quill made a small noise into his pipe. Vane studied her for a long moment.

"You sound like your father," she said at last. "He testified before a commission in Calloway once. Same tone. Righteous and stubborn as a barnacle." She tapped the desk. "But here's my problem, Miss Crane. At dawn, every right to that wreck transfers to Meridian under their concession. If I move on Dane tonight, I need cause. If I wait for dawn, I need a miracle."

"Then we'd best go find one," Felix said quietly, "before the tide goes out and takes our options with it."

* * *

They took a room above the Salt & Anchor tavern because it was the only room, the town being full of fleet men ashore on final liberty, and because Tam argued — correctly, Isla admitted — that the last place anyone would look for them was directly underneath the enemy's drinking establishment.

It was a bad plan executed well, which Felix said was the story of his entire career.

Through the floorboards came the roar of the tavern below: Meridian crews singing, arguing, spending their hazard pay. Isla sat on the bed with the map spread across her knees while Felix paced the narrow space like a caged animal doing arithmetic.

"Dawn," he said. "Dawn, dawn, dawn. Even if Vane moves tonight, even if we could hire a boat — and we can't, we haven't two coins between us — we can't dive forty fathoms. We can't outrun four ships in anything we could afford. We can't—"

"Felix."

"—and the whole concession scheme is designed so that whoever holds the permits at sunrise owns everything under it, which means everything my promise is worth evaporates in about nine hours, so unless you've thought of something I haven't—"

"Felix." Isla held up the map. "Sit down. Look at this."

He sat. It was, she noted, the first time she'd ever seen him obey anyone promptly.

The map lay between them on the counterpane: her father's fine drafting hand, the coastline rendered in ink gone brown with age, the wreck marked with a small precise cross and the wax seal — broken now, weeks ago, in a rented room in a city that felt like someone else's life. And along the lower margin, half-hidden by a decorative border of waves her father had drawn to make the thing beautiful, the second annotation.

Felix had found it days ago, in the lamplight at the river crossing. A line of figures and symbols running along the border like a signature: numbers, tidal marks, a strange little glyph like a hook bent through a ring. He'd puzzled over it twice and given it up both times. *Surveyor's shorthand,* he'd called it. *Probably just depth soundings.*

"It's not depth soundings," Isla said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I grew up watching him draw." She traced the border with one finger. "When Father wanted to hide something, he didn't lock it away. He made it pretty. He used to say anything worth protecting should look like decoration, because nobody robs a picture frame." Her throat tightened. "He drew these waves himself. Every map he ever made had them. But these are wrong. Look — the spacing. They're not decorative intervals. They're measurements."

Felix leaned close. The lamplight caught the stubble on his jaw, the exhaustion in his eyes, and beneath both, the engineer's hunger waking up. "Measurements of what?"

"Tide heights. Times." She pointed. "This glyph isn't a flourish. It's a mooring ring. And this hook through it — that's a line passing under something. Under the anchorage." She looked up at him. "There's a second way in. He found it last spring. A submerged channel — maybe an old rail cut, maybe a natural passage — that runs beneath where the fleet is sitting right now. He marked the tide that clears it."

The room seemed to lean in. From below came a drunken chorus about a sailor and a mermaid, absurdly cheerful.

"Slack water," Felix breathed. "At the turn. Eleven minutes past six." He was already pulling out his watch, already calculating, his lips moving. "Low slack lasts maybe twenty minutes at this range. A shallow-draft launch could run it in ten if the pilot knew the cut. If the pilot *didn't* know the cut—" he grimaced, "—it's a rock shredder with a roof of water."

"My father knew it. That's what the annotation is. His notes for running it." Isla's hands were shaking, and she let them. "He drew me a door, Felix. He drew it months ago, before he ever met you, before any of this. He drew a way in for whoever brought his map home."

Silence. Below them, the mermaid song collapsed into laughter and breaking glass.

"That's why he gave it to a stranger," Felix said slowly. "Not just to deliver. To *use*. He wasn't leaving you an inheritance. He was leaving you an operation."

"He was leaving me a choice," Isla corrected. "Same as always. He never once told me what to do with my life. He just — left doors open and trusted me to walk through the right one." She folded the map along its old creases, carefully, the way you handle a living thing. "Well. I'm walking through."

A knock at the door. All three of them froze. Then Tam's voice, low: "It's me. And I've either got terrible news or a terrible idea, and honestly at this point I can't tell the difference."

Felix opened the door. Tam slipped in, wet to the knees, eyes bright with something between fear and fever.

"Harbor master's got a launch for hire," he said. "Old pilot boat, shallow draft, engine held together with prayer. Owner wants thirty dollars and won't take less." He swallowed. "Also, Dane's posted men on the pier. They've got my description. Yours too. And—" he hesitated, and Isla watched the guilt move across his face like weather, "—and I know where the fleet keeps its spare winch cable. The heavy stuff. The kind that could jam a salvage crane if somebody got close enough."

"Why," Felix said dangerously, "would that matter?"

"Because tomorrow at dawn," Tam said, "when those permits transfer, Meridian's going to start raising that wreck. And whatever your father found down there, Miss Crane — whatever Dane killed him for — it comes up on a Meridian cable unless somebody makes sure that cable has a very bad day."

Isla looked at the map in her hands. At the little hooked wave, the hidden door, her father's patient handwriting that had crossed half a country and a grave to reach her.

Outside, the first stars were coming out over the fleet at anchor, and the tide was beginning to think about turning.

"Thirty dollars," she said. "Someone find me thirty dollars by midnight. Tomorrow at slack water, we're going through my father's door — and Marshal Vane is going to be waiting on the other side with her warrants, her witness, and whatever my father left at the bottom of the sea to hang Sorrel Dane."

Felix was already reaching for his watch. Tam was already reaching for the window.

And Isla Crane, who had spent five tides learning to grieve, spent the sixth learning something harder: that despair was just defiance that hadn't found its door yet.

Hers had a hook through a ring, and it opened at 6:11.
