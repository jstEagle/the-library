## 9. Wreck of the Meridian Line

The second annotation had been staring at them all along, folded into the wax seal's underside like a moth pressed flat in a book. Felix had turned the map over a hundred times in six tides and never thought to look at the thing that held it shut.

"It's a survey mark," Isla said, her finger tracing the tiny stamped glyph. "Railroad grade. See the elevation figures? My father wrote them in his own hand — I'd know that cramped little four anywhere." She looked up at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen since the river crossing: not grief, not resolve, but the wild bright edge of an idea taking hold. "Felix. These depths. This isn't a wreck chart."

"No," Felix said slowly, feeling the shape of it settle into place like a rail joint clicking home. "It's a right-of-way."

A submerged rail cut. Aldous Crane hadn't just found a sunken salvage hulk inside Meridian's concession zone — he'd found the drowned bed of the old Calloway spur, a line Felix himself had helped survey twenty years ago before the sea took it back. The cut ran beneath the harbor shelf, under the very anchorage where the Meridian fleet now sat fat and confident on their permits, and it came up inside the breakwater where their winch barges moored.

"They'll never look for us from below," Tam said quietly. He'd been silent since the ridge, since the confession, since the word *Dane* had left his mouth like a splinter working free. "That's the whole trick of it. Nobody watches the water they think is empty."

* * *

They borrowed the launch from a fisherman named Osgood who asked no questions and accepted payment in the only coin Port Calloway respected at that hour: Felix's honest admission that he was about to do something foolish with another man's boat. Osgood had laughed until his whiskers quivered, handed over the key, and told him to bring back the propeller at least.

Slack water came at half past three. Felix knew it the way he knew his own pulse — the tide tables were just timetables written by the moon, and a man who could read one could read the other. The cut would be passable for perhaps forty minutes, no more, its roof of old stone close enough to scrape the launch's stack if he steered careless.

He did not intend to steer careless.

Isla sat beside him holding a lantern low, its light cupped so it wouldn't spill above the gunwale. Tam stood at the stern with the coil of chain they'd bought from Osgood's neighbor, and Marshal Vane crouched amidships looking profoundly unhappy about every single element of the plan, which Felix felt was the correct amount of unhappiness.

"For the record," Vane said, "this is piracy."

"It's trespassing at worst," Felix said, easing the throttle forward. "Piracy requires plunder. We're going to leave everything exactly where we found it."

"You're going to jam a winch cable on a licensed salvage operation."

"Licensed," Isla said, "by men who killed my father to keep him quiet about what's down there. If you'd rather wait for dawn and your warrants, Marshal, we can put you ashore."

Vane muttered something about the state of modern law enforcement and did not ask to be put ashore.

The mouth of the cut opened beneath them like a held breath. Felix killed the engine and let the launch drift on its own way, poling gently off the old stone walls where barnacles had colonized the ballast beds of a railroad that had died before some of its own engineers stopped believing in it. The lantern showed him things no map had ever shown: the cut's crown arching overhead, green-slicked and dripping; a signal post lying along the wall like a fallen soldier; and once, impossibly, a rusted handcar wedged in a maintenance alcove, as though its crew had simply stepped out one day and never come back.

"Twenty years," Felix murmured. "I signed the abandonment order for this stretch. My name's on the paperwork somewhere down here."

"Then you're technically inspecting your own property," Isla said. "Which makes this a site visit."

He laughed — actually laughed, forty feet under a harbor full of enemies — and understood, in the small cold part of himself that kept honest accounts, that he hadn't laughed like that since before the disgrace. Since before the debts. Since before a bleeding man had cornered him in an alley and made refusal impossible.

The cut began to rise. Light filtered down through cracks in the breakwater above, silver threads in black water, and then the roof opened and they were through — inside the anchorage, tucked against the inner face of the seawall, with the Meridian fleet looming over them like a congregation of black cathedrals.

* * *

The lead barge sat closest, its great salvage winch straining against the night's work: a cable running down into the dark toward the wreck of the *Cormorant*, the hulk Aldous Crane had given his life to find. Floodlamps lit the deck. Men moved across it in orderly silence. And at the rail, giving orders in a voice like a file drawn across iron, stood Sorrel Dane.

"There," Isla breathed. "That's him. That's the man who—"

"I know." Felix took her hand off the tiller gently. "Not yet. Not like this."

The plan was simple, which is to say it was terrible, which is to say it was the only plan they had. The winch cable ran through a fairlead at the barge's waterline. Jam it — foul it, wedge it, make it shriek and seize — and Dane would have to surface whatever he was hauling or lose the cable entirely. Either way, he'd have to explain himself, in front of witnesses, at an hour when honest salvagers should have been asleep. And Vane would be waiting with her questions and her warrant and, if Tam's testimony held, the beginning of a case.

"The fairlead's on the far side," Tam said. He was already shrugging out of his coat, folding it with the terrible neatness of a man putting his affairs in order. "Someone has to swim it. The chain goes through the sheave and locks back on itself. Thirty seconds' work in the water, less if the current's kind."

"I'll go," Felix said.

"No." Tam smiled, and it was the first unguarded smile Felix had ever seen on him — young and crooked and heartbreakingly ordinary. "You don't swim like a Meridian man. I do. I know their watch rotations, their lamp signals, all of it. They trained me for exactly this and then taught me nothing worth keeping except how to run." He paused. "Besides. I owe a debt. Crane's daughter shouldn't pay it. You've paid enough of other people's."

"Tam—" Isla started.

"Ash," Tam said. "My real name's Ash Wicks. Figured if I was going to die honest, somebody ought to know what to put on the marker." He grinned again, awful and bright. "Kidding. Mostly. Watch the lamp — two flashes means I'm clear, three means run."

He went over the side without a splash, because of course he did; Meridian had trained him well in the one art that mattered.

* * *

What followed, Felix would remember for the rest of his life in fragments, the way wrecks are remembered: in pieces, out of order, with gaps where the truth was too large to fit.

The lamp on the barge's rail guttering. A shout, thin across the water. The winch cable rising out of the sea like a harp string being plucked by something enormous, and then the sound — God, the sound — of steel seizing in a steel throat, a scream of metal that woke gulls from three harbors away.

Men pouring onto the deck. Dane's voice cracking across the fleet, no longer smooth, no longer filed-down calm, but raw: *"Cut it! Cut the cable, you idiots, CUT IT—"*

And then, above the chaos, a voice Felix knew, pitched to carry, pitched to carry forever:

"My name is Ash Wicks! I worked for Sorrel Dane! I watched him kill Aldous Crane on the Saltmarsh road, and I'm telling this marshal everything, and there's nothing you can cut that stops it!"

Tam — Ash — stood on the barge's deck with the seized chain wrapped around both fists, using the jammed fairlead itself as his anchor, his proof, his pulpit. The cable couldn't be freed without cutting it, and cutting it meant losing the salvage, and losing the salvage meant explaining to Meridian's owners why their lead agent had panicked over a wreck he claimed to own outright.

Dane lunged for him.

Felix was moving before he decided to move — launch surging out from the seawall, Isla at the tiller with her jaw set like her father's, Vane standing in the bow with her badge held high and her voice doing what badges are for. They came alongside the barge in a grinding crash of timber, and Felix went up the hull on hands and boots, and he reached the deck in time to see Dane wrench free of two deckhands, knife out, arm cocked—

—and stop. Because Ash had raised one fist, and in it, dripping, was the fairlead's cotter pin, the small stupid piece of iron whose absence had jammed the whole machine, and because behind him, lantern high, stood Marshal Edith Vane with three of her deputies swarming up from the harbor police launch that had been shadowing the fleet since midnight on her wire.

"Sorrel Dane," she said, in the voice of a woman who had waited five tides to say it, "you're under arrest for the murder of Aldous Crane. And son—" this to Ash, without looking at him, without softening, but not without warmth either, "—you're under arrest too. Accessory, obstruction, and about nine things I'll think of on the boat. Testify clean and maybe I spell your name right in the report."

Ash laughed. It turned into something else halfway through, and he sat down hard on the deck with the cotter pin still in his fist, and Isla reached him first, kneeling, saying nothing, which was the only right thing to say.

Dane fought. Of course he fought. It took four men and cost Vane a split lip, and in the last seconds before they pinned him his eyes found Felix's across the crowded deck, and what was in them wasn't rage. It was arithmetic. The look of a man recalculating a ledger that had just refused to balance, and finding, in the end, that the debt was his.

Felix looked back and gave him nothing. Not even anger. Anger was a currency, and Felix Blackwood was done trading in it.

* * *

They came back through the cut at the turn of the tide, the launch riding lighter than it had any right to, carrying one more soul than it had brought and somehow weighing less. The floodlamps of the Meridian fleet burned on behind them, confused and leaderless, permits expiring with the dawn.

Osgood met them at the slip, took one look at the propeller, and pronounced it a miracle. "Close enough," Felix said, and gave him back the key, and meant every word.

On the dock, Ash sat wrapped in Felix's spare coat, shivering, alive, handcuffed to a deputy who had thoughtfully also given him tea. Isla sat beside him. Neither of them spoke. Some ledgers, Felix thought, take longer to read than others.

Vane appeared at his elbow, notebook closed, lip split, looking — for the first time since the river crossing — almost satisfied. "Your name comes up in my report, Blackwood. Favorably. Try not to fall over from the shock."

"I'll brace myself, Marshal."

She studied him a moment longer. "There's a hearing in Calloway next month. The railroad board sends a man down for the concession disputes. A man with your maps and my testimony might find the trip worthwhile."

Felix looked south, down the dark spine of the old closed lines, where his past lay rusting in the weeds like a handcar in a flooded tunnel. Then he looked at Isla, and at Ash, and at the east, where the sky was going grey at the edges the way it does when a tide is ending and another one, impossibly, is about to begin.

"I'll think about it," he said.

And for the first time in six tides — maybe in six years — he found that he meant it.
