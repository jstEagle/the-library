## 10. A Current for the Passage

The sixth tide came in gray and gentle, the way tides do when they have no more quarrel with you.

Felix stood on the quay at Port Calloway with a pen in his hand and a paper on a crate in front of him, and he found that his signature — the same signature that had once moved locomotives and fortunes and, eventually, his whole life off the rails — felt lighter than it had in years. The ink caught the dawn light and shone wet for a moment before it dried into something permanent.

*All right, title, interest, and claim to the wreck of the* Meridian Line, *concession permit number forty-one, hereby assigned to Isla Crane.*

He signed it. He blew on it, out of habit, though there was no need.

"There," he said. "A promise paid."

Isla took the paper from the crate with both hands, the way you take something that might still be dreaming. She was salt-stiff and sleep-starved and had a bruise along her jaw from the trestle that had turned purple overnight, and she looked at the document for a long moment without reading it, because she already knew what it said.

"You could have kept half," she said. "Nobody would have blinked. Half of that wreck would buy back your name twice over."

"Half a promise is no promise at all," Felix said. "My father taught me that. Then the railroad taught me everything else."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound, but real — and folded the paper into her coat, over her heart, which Felix thought was exactly where Aldous Crane would have wanted it to travel.

Tam Wicks was not there to see it. That was the arithmetic of the thing, and Felix did not try to make it come out any other way than it did. A boy who jammed a winch cable with his own two hands so that a fleet couldn't haul up what wasn't theirs, and who lived just long enough to see Sorrel Dane hauled off in irons instead — that boy's name belonged on the salvage record too. Isla had seen to it. First line, above her father's. *Tam Wicks, who chose late, but chose.*

Marshal Edith Vane came down the quay as the sun cleared the headland, and she walked like a woman who had slept badly and won anyway. Her coat was dry for the first time since the river crossing. She stopped beside Felix and looked out at the water, where Meridian's fleet sat at anchor with its permits expired and its lead agent in custody, and she let the silence sit between them for a while, because she was the sort of person who understood that silence could be a courtesy.

"That's done, then," she said finally.

"That's done."

"I sent my report north last night. Full account. Crane's death, Dane's confession, Tam's testimony — I got it written down before the tide turned, word for word." She glanced at him sideways. "There's room in that report for a paragraph about you, Blackwood. About an engineer who reopened closed lines to keep a dying man's word. The railroad board reads my reports whether they want to or not. I can make them read this one twice."

Felix looked at the harbor. Gulls were working the pilings. Somewhere behind them, Isla was giving orders to a salvage crew in a voice that sounded more like her father every hour, and none of them seemed to mind.

"A paragraph," he said.

"Maybe two. I'm told I run long when I'm angry, and I've been angry at that board for eleven years."

He smiled. It surprised him how easily it came. "Vane. If your paragraph clears my name, they give me back my pension, my license, maybe a branch line in the interior if I grovel properly. And every man who signed the order that buried me gets to go on believing he was right, because the board doesn't overturn itself — it just quietly reassigns the blame to weather." He shook his head. "I spent three years wanting that. Wanting it the way you want a drink, or a debt forgiven. And then I spent six tides finding out what I actually wanted, which was to be able to stand on a quay at dawn and sign my name without flinching." He handed her the pen. "Keep the paragraph. Give it to Isla. She'll know what to do with it better than the board ever would."

Vane studied him for a long moment, the way she'd studied him at the river crossing — measuring, weighing, deciding whether he was lying. He'd passed the test then. He passed it now.

"You're a fool," she said.

"Frequently."

"The good kind." She pocketed the pen. "Then I'll write it anyway. Not for the board. For the record. Somebody ought to keep a true record; there's so damned few of them." She stuck out her hand, and Felix shook it, and her grip was exactly as honest as the rest of her. "If you change your mind, Blackwood, you know where the law keeps its office. It's usually near a train station. That's not an accident."

"No," Felix agreed. "It never is."

* * *

They found a breakfast place two streets up from the quay that smelled of coffee and fried onions and had benches worn smooth by fifty years of sailors, and the three of them — Felix, Isla, and Vane, who claimed she was off duty and then proceeded to ask questions like a woman very much on it — ate eggs and watched the town wake up.

"So what now?" Isla asked, around a piece of toast. "You ride south. To what?"

"To nothing in particular," Felix said. "Which is either a tragedy or a luxury, depending on the day. I've got a handcar with a repaired axle, a map book full of lines nobody else remembers exist, and a conscience that weighs almost nothing for the first time since the Kestrel Junction inquiry." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "I thought I'd follow the old coastal spur as far as it goes. There's a stretch below Harrow's Bend where the rails run right along the cliff and the sea comes up under you through the ties. Best view on the continent, according to a surveyor I used to drink with. I never saw it. Always meant to."

"On a handcar," Vane said. "Alone. Pumping yourself down a cliff line like some kind of mechanical pilgrim."

"When you put it that way it sounds less romantic."

"It sounds exactly as romantic as it is. That's the problem with it." But she said it kindly, and Felix understood that she understood: some men needed to arrive somewhere, and some men needed to be going somewhere, and he had finally figured out which kind he was.

Isla set down her toast. "The wreck'll take months to work. Proper salvage, careful, the way Dad would have wanted. I'll need people who know rail lines, since half the cargo went down in freight cars." She was looking at her coffee, not at him, in the manner of someone making an offer they'd rather not watch being refused. "And I'll need someone who can read a survey map faster than anyone I've ever met. The pay's terrible. The company's worse. But the tide tables are excellent."

Felix looked at her — at Aldous Crane's stubborn jaw set in a younger face, at the folded paper over her heart — and felt something in his chest that was not quite grief and not quite relief and was, he suspected, simply the feeling of being trusted, which he had forgotten the shape of.

"Isla Crane," he said gravely, "are you offering me employment?"

"I'm offering you a reason to come back up the line before winter. Whether you take it or not is your business."

"Then yes. On one condition."

"Name it."

"That we stop at Harrow's Bend on the way. I've been promised a view."

* * *

He left at mid-morning, alone on the handcar, because some stretches of track you have to pump by yourself before you can share them with anybody.

The coastal spur south of Port Calloway was everything the old map book promised and more — rust-brown rails threading the shoulder of the cliffs, the sea flashing far below through gaps in the ties, gulls riding the updrafts at eye level as if escorting him. His arms burned. His shoulders ached from six tides of fear and labor. Every push of the lever carried him further from a name he'd spent years trying to claw back and closer to nothing at all, and he found, pumping steadily into the bright morning wind, that nothing at all suited him fine.

At the top of the long grade above Harrow's Bend he stopped to breathe, and that was when he heard the whistle.

Far behind him, back up the line toward Port Calloway, a small engine — a yard shunter, borrowed or bought or bartered, trailing a single flatcar loaded with salvage gear — came around the headland with smoke standing straight up in the still air. At the throttle stood a figure with a bruised jaw and her father's jawline beneath it, and beside her, hanging off the flatcar's edge with his coat flapping, a lanky kid with a marshal's badge pinned crooked to his lapel, because Edith Vane had apparently decided that if Felix wouldn't take an escort home, she'd assign one to follow him instead, and Deputy Ollis had drawn the short straw and loved every minute of it.

The shunter whistled again — two short, one long, the old signal, *all clear ahead*.

Felix stood on his handcar on the empty cliff line with the sea roaring underneath him, and he raised his arm high and waved back.

Then he gripped the lever, set his boots, and pumped south into the open morning — unmoored, unpaid, and honest — while behind him, gaining slowly but gaining all the same, the ones he'd ride home to someday came on around the bend.
