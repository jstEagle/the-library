## 1. The Debt of Clear Skies

The optimists found me in a diner outside Marrow Flats, which tells you everything about how far I'd fallen. Men like Voss Corrigan don't do diners. They do boardrooms, private airfields, the forty-first floor of buildings with their own weather. But a debt is a leash, and a leash works from any distance.

He slid into my booth uninvited, set his hat on the table like it was paying rent, and smiled the smile of a man who has never once been surprised by bad news.

"Xanthe Amberdale," he said. "Chasing anything lately?"

"Sunsets. They're free."

His laugh was warm and entirely without humor. Two of his associates took the booths flanking mine — not menacingly, just present, the way weather is present before it turns. The optimists, we called them, back when my rig was new and my credit was theoretical: a consortium of investors who'd financed the *Provision* — my storm-chasing truck, radar array, the whole rolling cathedral of instruments — on the theory that catastrophic weather data would appreciate like real estate. It hadn't. Storms don't sign contracts. And when the market turned, they'd remembered that I was the only asset in their portfolio that could be repossessed personally.

"You've been hard to find," Voss said.

"I've been easy to find. You've been expensive to approach."

That got a real laugh out of him, brief as a squall. He ordered coffee he wouldn't drink and laid it out: three months of missed payments, compounding interest, and a final notice that had stopped being rhetorical. "I'm not here to break your legs, Xanthe. Legs are depreciating assets. I'm here to offer you work."

"I don't do collections."

"Nothing so crude." He produced a folder, thick as a brick, and squared its corners against the table with the tenderness of a man handling something dangerous. "A government survey team went into the Kestrel basin eight weeks ago. Six people. They haven't come out. Officially, they're delayed. Unofficially—" he tapped the folder once, "—they're a problem I've been asked to make go away quietly, and quiet costs more than loud."

"Asked by whom?"

"By people who prefer their questions unanswered."

I should have walked. Every instinct I'd honed over ten years of reading skies said *walk* — this was the green-black color on a horizon that means the storm has already decided about you. But the folder sat there, and inside it, I knew without looking, was money. Enough money. Enough to buy back the *Provision*, clear the ledger, start over someplace where nobody knew my name or my interest rate.

"How much?"

He told me. I made him repeat it, because the first time I'd assumed I misheard over the diner's griddle-hiss.

"And all you want is an answer? What happened to them?"

"All I want is certainty. Find the camp. Find the team. Find out what happened and come back and tell me, and the debt dissolves like fog at noon." He stood, resettled his hat. "One condition. You go alone. No radio check-ins. Whatever's up that river, it stays between us."

"That's not a condition, that's a eulogy."

"Then write a good one." At the edge of the booth he paused, and for one unguarded second the optimist looked tired. "For what it's worth, Xanthe — I read the roster. I know why you'll say yes."

He left the folder and went, and his men went, and I sat with my cooling coffee and opened it.

Six names. Six photographs. Survey leader, hydrologist, two technicians, a cartographer. And the fifth photograph, third from the top, printed slightly crooked the way government printers print things that matter to no one important:

**Dr. Imogen Vale. Field medic.**

I sat with that photograph a long time.

Three years ago, outside a town whose name I still can't say without tasting copper, a twister with the girth of a cathedral came down a valley road while I was pinned under the *Provision*'s axle, changing a tire I should have changed an hour earlier. The sky had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong, and the wind had that sound — the freight-train sound, the sound that means the world has stopped negotiating. Imogen Vale pulled off the road in a survey jeep she had no business driving that fast, dragged me out by the collar, and shoved us both into a culvert while the atmosphere above us tore itself apart like wet paper.

She'd held my head down in the mud and shouted over the roar, "Don't look up! Whatever you do, don't look up!" And I didn't, because she was a doctor and doctors know what kills people: curiosity, mostly.

Afterward she'd bought me coffee at a gas station and refused my thanks with a wave. "You'd have done it for me." I never saw her again. I owed her a life, and debts of that denomination don't appear on any ledger Voss Corrigan keeps — but they accrue all the same, and they compound too.

So that was the trap of it, neatly sprung. Not the money. The money was bait, but bait only works on fish that are already swimming toward something. What had me hooked was a woman in a mud-culvert saying *don't look up*, and me needing, after eight weeks of silence, to know whether she ever had.

* * *

I spent two days provisioning and one day lying to myself about it. Told myself it was a recovery mission, a walk in the woods with a paycheck. Told myself the Kestrel headwaters were just geography — granite, pine, meltwater, nothing a man with good boots and better instincts couldn't handle. Told myself I was going alone because alone was cheaper, not because whoever hired Voss wanted no witnesses, and not because witnesses, historically, have short bibliographies.

The *Provision* was impounded in a lot outside Marrow Flats, under a tarp that had begun to grow things. I visited her at dusk, ran a hand along her fender where the hail damage from the Calder County cell still showed, and promised her I'd be back. Trucks deserve lies too. It keeps their spirits up.

I drove as far as the road allowed — a decommissioned logging spur that ended at a gate with a chain and a sign: **CALDER COMPANY — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**, and beneath it, smaller, newer, meaner: **TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED TO THE FULLEST EXTENT OF PRIVATE LAW.**

Private law. I wrote that phrase down in my logbook because it had the ring of something I'd need later, the way you note a strange cloud formation that doesn't match any chart.

Beyond the gate, the basin opened up like a held breath. The Kestrel ran narrow and quick through a gorge it had spent ten thousand years arguing with, and the trail along its bank was faint enough to be a rumor. Eight weeks of silence ahead of me. Six names in a folder. One of them owed me nothing and I owed her everything, which is the worst kind of arithmetic.

I hiked until the light went bronze, then gray, then gone, and made cold camp above the waterline. That night I dreamed about the culvert — the roar, the mud, her hand fisted in my collar — and woke before dawn with the certain, sick knowledge that I wasn't going up that river for Voss Corrigan, or for the money, or even for the truth.

I was going because somewhere upstream, a woman who once told me not to look up had gone silent, and I had spent my whole life looking up. At storms. At skies. At the places where the world comes apart.

Time, maybe, to look down. Into the water. Where rivers keep what they take.

I broke camp in the dark and walked north, into the throat of the Kestrel, and if the river minded, it didn't say so. Rivers never do. They just carry you toward whatever's waiting — and by the smell of the wind coming down the gorge that morning, something upstream was waiting, and it had arranged itself very carefully indeed.
