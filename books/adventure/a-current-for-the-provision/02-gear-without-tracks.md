## 2. Gear Without Tracks

The Kestrel didn't announce itself. It just appeared one morning between two walls of granite, running green and cold and utterly indifferent to the fact that four government employees had apparently dissolved into it three weeks ago.

I'd been following it upstream for six days. Six days of eating oatmeal dusted with pine needles, of checking my backtrail out of habit even though nobody sane would follow me into this country, of talking to myself until I'd run out of things worth saying and started saying them anyway. The optimists who financed my rig used to joke that I was better company than a radio. They weren't joking anymore. Now they just wanted their money, and they'd stopped being particular about how.

But that's chapter one. This is where the river got strange.

* * *

*Day 11. Vasquez swears she heard machinery in the night. There is no machinery within a hundred miles of here. I told her it was the river rearranging its stones. She looked at me like I'd said something stupid, which is fair, because I had. Rivers don't rearrange stones at two in the morning with a rhythm like a diesel generator.*

*I am starting to understand why the Calder Company's maps show this stretch as a dry wash.*

—Journal of Survey Leader E. Marchetti, recovered from the Kestrel headwaters expedition

* * *

I found the camp on the seventh day, and my first thought — before dread, before anything — was that somebody had won a bet.

That's the only way I can describe it. Everything was too neat. The tents were struck and folded into squares so precise they could have been issued as geometry lessons. The cook stove sat on a flat stone, scrubbed. Four bedrolls, rolled tight, stacked under a tarp weighted down with river rocks placed at exact intervals, like a man who'd counted them twice.

And the instruments were still running.

That's what did it. Not the missing people — missing people are a thing I can file under bad luck or bad weather or bad decisions, all categories I have personal experience with. But the anemometer was still turning on its mast. The rain gauge still stood open to a sky that hadn't rained in weeks. A data logger blinked its little green heart in the dark of a tent, patient as a dog waiting for an owner who wasn't coming back.

You don't leave a data logger running unless you're coming back by Tuesday. You especially don't leave it running for twenty-two days.

I stood in the middle of that camp for a long time, doing the math every chaser learns to do: read the scene, don't impose on it. Storms tell you the truth if you let them. So do camps.

This camp was telling me a lie so elaborate it had to be true.

* * *

*Day 14. Found boot prints this morning. Okafor's, going downstream. Only his, and only going one direction, and stopping — not fading, stopping — at the waterline. The prints end where the water begins. I have walked that bank three times. On the far side there is nothing but rock face. You cannot climb it. You cannot wade it. The current there moves like something with an opinion.*

*I have not told the others about the prints. Reyes is frightened enough. Vasquez keeps hearing her machinery. And last night I dreamed about water again, which is ridiculous, because I do not dream about water. I dream about paperwork, like a normal civil servant.*

*Something upstream does not want us measuring it.*

—Marchetti

* * *

Here's what I know about deliberate arrangements: they're always addressed to someone.

A camp left tidy by people leaving in a hurry looks chaotic in specific ways — the half-packed bag, the pot left on the fire, the thing grabbed instead of the thing needed. A camp abandoned in disaster looks violent. But a camp arranged? Arranged is different. Arranged has an audience in mind.

I checked the food cache first, because hunger makes a poor detective. Sealed. Full. Three weeks' worth of rations for four people, wrapped against bears, untouched. Beside it, the survey team's spare boots, laces tucked in. Their satellite phone, battery dead — no, not dead. Battery removed, and the battery sitting next to it, also neat, like a pulled tooth laid on a saucer.

Nobody walks into the wilderness without boots. Nobody leaves a working phone behind unless the phone is the problem.

I sat down on a rock and ate half a ration bar from my own supplies and let the camp talk to me, and what it said was: *we didn't leave. We were taken, and whoever took us wanted the next visitor to know it.*

Because that was the part I kept circling back to. The arrangement wasn't concealment. Concealment would have been easy — scatter the gear, burn the tents, let the river take the rest. Instead someone had folded everything into a display case. Someone had spent time on this. Time is expensive in the backcountry; you don't spend it on tidiness unless tidiness is the message.

*We were here. We didn't choose to go. Look carefully.*

* * *

*Day 16. I am writing this quickly and hiding it badly on purpose. If we disappear, someone will come looking — the Department owes us that much, though the Department has never once paid a debt I've seen. Whoever you are: the answer is upstream. Everything about this valley is upstream. The flow gauges don't match the rainfall. The tributaries feed in but the main channel runs low. Water is going somewhere it shouldn't go, or coming from somewhere it isn't supposed to exist.*

*Vasquez found the wire this morning. Buried line, fresh splices, running upcanyon along the east wall. She says it's telemetry cable, industrial grade. She says it's newer than anything the government owns.*

*Reyes wants to turn back. I want to know what's drawing power through forty miles of wilderness. These are compatible positions and we are not handling them compatibly.*

—Marchetti

* * *

I found the tracks on the second day at the camp, and I use the word loosely, because "tracks" implies a trail, and a trail implies intent to arrive somewhere.

There were boot prints — four sets, matching four pairs of absent boots — leading out of camp toward the river. All four sets side by side. Not single file, the way people walk in bear country or bad terrain. Side by side, relaxed, spaced like people strolling. Like people who weren't afraid, which frightened me more than anything else in that canyon, because either they trusted whatever they were walking toward, or they'd been told there was nothing to fear, and both possibilities made my skin try to leave my body.

The prints went down the gravel bar. The gravel bar ended at the water.

The prints ended at the water.

I crouched there for an hour, maybe longer, long enough for the light to change and my knees to file formal complaints. Looking for exit prints on the far bank — nothing, sheer rock. Looking for drag marks — nothing. Looking for any sign of struggle, of hesitation, of a fifth set of prints belonging to whoever had arranged all this — nothing, nothing, nothing.

Four people had walked abreast into the Kestrel River and the Kestrel River had closed over them like a ledger.

Except rivers don't erase prints cleanly. Rivers smear. Rivers redistribute. And these prints stopped with their edges intact, crisp as stamps, as if the water had been careful. As if the water had been *instructed*.

I'm a storm chaser. People think that means adrenaline. It doesn't. It means you spend years learning to respect the difference between weather that happens and weather that's *built* — supercells organize themselves with a precision that looks designed, and the first time you stand under one you feel like a trespasser in someone's engine room. I knew that feeling. I was feeling it now, standing over four sets of obedient footprints.

Somebody owned this river. Not in the way the valley settlers owned their ditches and their water rights and their grandfather's handshake agreements. Owned it the way you own a machine.

* * *

*Last entry. The machinery Vasquez hears is real. I saw the cable junction box myself today, bolted to the canyon wall above the flood line, painted government grey but stamped with a logo I don't recognize — a circle split by a vertical line. Calder. The same mark is on the deed abstracts the Department sent us, redacted almost to confetti.*

*If you're reading this, whoever you are, understand: we didn't drown. Drowning leaves evidence. Whatever happened to us, it was arranged, and arrangements require an arranger, and the arranger is upstream.*

*Don't follow the river.*

*Follow the wire.*

—E.M., Day 17. No further entries.

* * *

I slept that night in Marchetti's tent, because it seemed rude not to, and because the alternative was sleeping outside a camp that was already watching me with all its terrible politeness.

In the morning I packed light. Left the team's gear exactly as I'd found it — evidence stays put; that's the first rule of reading a scene and the second rule of staying alive. Took only the journal, wrapped in oilcloth against the river I was about to walk beside.

Then I stood at the waterline where four sets of prints ended, and I looked upstream, where the canyon bent north into shadow, and I felt the thing I always feel under a building storm: that specific, electric clarity where fear stops being a weight and becomes information.

The wire ran along the east wall, grey insulation glinting at the splices, heading upcanyon like a thread someone had forgotten to snip.

Forty miles of wilderness, drawing power toward something that wasn't on any map.

"Okay," I told the river, because talking to myself had become a professional necessity. "Let's see what you're keeping."

The Kestrel said nothing, which is how I knew it had been paid to.
