## 3. What the Source Keeps

The river lied to me for two more days.

That's the thing about water nobody tells you when you're young and romantic about it: a river is a witness that will swear to anything. It'll show you a smooth green run and hide a strainer underneath. It'll sing you downstream and drop you over a ledge. The Kestrel had been lying since the camp — running shallow and apologetic, as if embarrassed by whatever it had swallowed upstream.

I followed it anyway. Not because I believed anymore. Because I'd stopped believing anything else.

The gorge narrowed on the second morning until the walls pressed close enough that I could have spat across the top of the current. The survey leader's journal rode against my ribs in a waxed pouch, and I'd read the last entries so many times I could recite them. *Marta says the flow numbers are impossible. There's more water leaving this basin than the sky puts into it.* And then, in a different hand, pressed hard enough to tear the page: *They know we know.*

I'd assumed that was where the journal ended because that was where she ended. Standing in that gorge with the cold coming off the water like a held breath, I understood my error. The journal didn't end there. It ended where the paper ran out. She'd kept writing right up until someone took the pen away from her.

Which meant she might have kept breathing right up until someone decided otherwise.

I climbed. That's most of what storm chasing ever taught me — how to climb things you shouldn't, in weather you shouldn't, and how to be honest with yourself about the odds while you do it. The gorge wall gave me handholds like it was daring me. Two hundred feet up, a seam opened in the rock, and through the seam I saw something that rearranged every map in my head.

Concrete.

A face of poured concrete, curved, older than any road I'd crossed to get here, furred with moss at the joints and streaked black with decades of seep. A dam. Not a government dam — I'd seen the survey maps, and there was nothing on them but wilderness above the falls. This structure wasn't supposed to exist. Which meant whoever built it had paid good money for it never to appear on paper.

And below it, pooled in the shadow of that illegal curve, sat the headwaters of the Kestrel River. The source. Not a spring, not a glacier melt — a reservoir, held back behind a lie, metered out to the valley below in whatever quantities suited whoever held the valve.

I thought about the valley settlers then. The ones who paid their water tithes every dry season, who drilled deeper wells every year and called it bad luck, who watched their orchards thin and blamed the sky. The sky hadn't robbed them. Arithmetic had.

Then I thought about Marta Voss, who had once pulled me out of a flooded culvert in Halloway County while the tornado sirens were still winding down, laughing at me the whole time, and I stopped thinking like a man gathering evidence and started thinking like a man holding a wrench.

* * *

Warden Corliss Hale had been tracking the intruder for six days, and he had come to respect him, which annoyed him.

Respect was a liability in his line of work. Calder didn't pay him to admire the quarry. They paid him to maintain an absence — the absence of the dam from every record, the absence of the survey team from every memory, and now the absence of one debt-ridden storm chaser from the world entire. Hale kept absences the way other men kept gardens.

But the man moved well. He moved like water, actually, which Hale found professionally offensive. He'd cut back along his own trail twice, walked the riverbed itself for a quarter mile where the gravel held no print, and once — this was the part Hale kept turning over — he'd built a small fire, brewed coffee, and left the fire burning as he walked away. An invitation. *Come look here while I go somewhere else.*

Hale had not taken the invitation. He'd gone where the man went instead, which was up.

Now he stood at the base of the service stair inside the dam's eastern abutment, listening. Above him, muffled by forty feet of concrete, came a sound that did not belong: voices. Several. Arguing in low, urgent tones, and beneath them a new sound, metallic and rhythmic — someone working a lock, or a bolt, or a chain.

Hale unslung his rifle and considered the arithmetic. Six prisoners, if all six lived. One intruder. One of him. The company's standing instruction was elegant in its simplicity: no witnesses above the falls. It had worked for eleven years. Eleven years of metered rain, of valley accounts receivable, of a board in Calder City toasting its own discretion.

He began to climb.

* * *

The door was river-steel and the lock was older than I am, but locks only keep out people in a hurry, and I had made myself very patient.

Inside, the maintenance gallery ran the length of the dam's spine, lit by caged bulbs that buzzed like trapped flies. And along the gallery, in bunks welded to the wall behind a mesh gate — bunks, not cells, which was somehow worse, the domesticity of it — were five people who turned toward me with the flat, uncomprehending stare of the long-imprisoned.

Five. I counted twice. The journal said six.

"Marta?" I said, and hated how my voice cracked on it, and then a woman at the back of the cage stood up slowly, one hand braced on the wall, and squinted at me like I was a trick of the light.

"You're not Calder," she said.

"No."

"Then you're either lost or stupid." She came closer, and eleven months of captivity fell off her face all at once, replaced by the grin I remembered from Halloway County. "Xanthe Amberdale. You look terrible."

"You look like you've been living in a dam."

"I have. It's damp." Then the grin went out like a snuffed lamp, and she gripped the mesh, and her voice dropped to something with iron in it. "Listen to me. There were six of us. Dr. Ansel Row is dead — they shot him at the falls when he tried to radio out, and they told us it was exposure, and we let ourselves believe it because believing it was easier than the alternative. Everything else you need is in the records office, second level. Flow logs going back eleven years. Purchase orders for the concrete. Names, Xanthe. Real names, signatures, the whole rotten ledger."

"Why haven't you taken it yourselves?"

She laughed, short and bitter. "Where would we take it? They fly the wardens in. We've got no boat, no radio they haven't stripped, and the passes close in—" she counted on her fingers, and I watched her realize, the way I'd realized days ago, "—in nine days."

"Eight," I said. "I checked the sky this morning. It's ahead of schedule."

Behind her, the others had come forward — a hydrologist with a beard gone gray before its time, two techs, a young surveyor who wouldn't stop looking at the gallery door. The hydrologist spoke first. "Son, whoever you are, if you open that gate, you're one of us. There's no walking away clean after that. Calder doesn't forget faces."

I looked at the lock. I looked at Marta. I thought about the optimists back in the city who owned my rig and very nearly my skeleton, and about how a bounty on a secret like this would settle every debt I had with interest to spare, and about how none of that was why my hands were already working the hasp.

"Justice around here doesn't sit in a courtroom," I said. "It sits upstream. I'm deciding which side of it I'm on."

The lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

Hale reached the gallery level in time to see the gate swinging open, and he did the professional thing: he assessed, he breathed out half his air, and he raised the rifle.

What he did not account for was the dam itself.

Eleven years of metered water had raised the reservoir higher than the original engineers ever intended — he knew this, it was in the reports nobody read — and the old spillway gates had been rusting in their housings since the second season, because maintaining them cost money and the water had never once needed releasing. The company's whole enterprise rested on the assumption that the river would stay put.

The river had never signed anything.

As Hale stepped from the stairwell, a groan rolled through the concrete — deep, structural, patient — and a hairline seam of water lanced from a joint in the wall and hit the floor between him and the prisoners with a sound like a whip. Everyone froze. Everyone except Xanthe Amberdale, who looked at the leak, then at Hale, and smiled the smile of a man who had spent his life reading weather and recognized an approaching front when he stood inside one.

"That wall's been asking to fail for years," Amberdale said. "You want to argue with it, or you want to talk?"

Hale raised the rifle another inch. Behind him, down the length of the gallery, the seam in the concrete widened by the width of a finger, and the Kestrel River — the true source, the stolen heart of an entire valley — began, very quietly, to make its own argument.
