## 4. Dry Season

The passes would close in nine days. I knew this the way a gambler knows the last hand is coming—by the color of the sky, the thinness of the wind, the way the larches had gone gold and started letting go of everything they couldn't carry into winter. Nine days, maybe seven if the weather turned mean, and between me and the low country stood forty miles of river, one imprisoned survey team, and a warden named Hask who shot first and filed paperwork never.

I'd spent two nights watching the dam.

That's what it was, though Calder's maps called it a "flow augmentation structure," which is the kind of language men use when they want a theft to sound like engineering. A concrete throat clamped across the gorge where the Kestrel was born, holding back a lake that shouldn't exist, feeding water down through turbines and pipes to wherever Calder decided the valley deserved a drink. Behind it, the survey team moved between two barracks like ghosts who hadn't been told they were dead yet.

Six of them. I counted heads at mess, counted again at dusk. Six, including Dr. Imogen Vale, who had once pulled me out of a flooded arroyo with a rope and a stream of insults so creative I still used some of them. She looked thinner. She walked like someone conserving not just calories but hope.

Hask ran the place with four men and a radio. The radio was the problem. You can outrun four men; you can't outrun a man sitting comfortably indoors calling for friends.

So I did what any sensible coward does: I stopped being sensible and got clever instead.

* * *

The records room sat below the turbine house, a concrete box full of filing cabinets and a wall of ledgers going back eleven years. Eleven years of diversion schedules, of payments to valley officials whose names I recognized from the market stalls in Amberford, of memos about "settler management" that read like a farmer talking about aphids. The survey team hadn't stumbled onto the dam by accident. They'd been hired by the government to find out why the Kestrel ran low every summer, and when their instruments answered the question, Calder answered back—with rifles and a locked gate and a story about bandits.

The plan, such as it was, came to me while I watched rain start to fall on the lake behind the dam—the first real rain in weeks, the sky finally paying its debts. The spillway. Every dam has one, and every spillway has a limit. And paper, as any drowning man knows, floats.

I couldn't carry eleven years of ledgers over forty miles of closing pass. But the river could carry them down. The same current Calder had stolen could be made to deliver the bill.

* * *

I went in during the change of watch, an hour before dawn, when even loyal men think about coffee. The fence was cattle wire and habit; the dogs were chained near the barracks and knew me by then as a smell that never quite materialized into anything worth barking at. I'd been feeding them jerky for three nights. Sentiment? Strategy? With dogs it's always both.

Vale saw me first. Of course she did. She was awake—she was always awake, she told me later, because sleeping meant dreaming about the day they'd taken her instruments away—and she caught my silhouette at the window and didn't scream, didn't move, just watched me pick the latch with the patience of a woman who'd spent five months learning exactly how long patience lasts.

"You're late," she whispered, when the door opened.

"I had errands."

"Xanthe Amberdale." She said my name like a diagnosis. "You absolute idiot."

"Probably. Can your people walk?"

"They can walk, run, or dance. Merrick broke his ankle in week two but it's set wrong and he'll complain the entire way, which frankly will keep morale up." She was already moving, waking the others with touches and names, and I watched five months of captivity organize itself into escape in under four minutes because these were people who mapped rivers for a living—they understood channels, gradients, the path of least resistance.

I gave them the route: up the goat trail on the north wall, over the shoulder, down into the tributary drainage where I'd cached food and a raft that was more optimism than boat. Forty miles. Seven days. It would hurt.

"And you?" Vale asked.

"I have a delivery to make."

She looked at me the way she had at the bottom of that arroyo, rope taut, both of us certain I was about to die and her deciding whether to let me. "Whatever you're planning—"

"It's a good plan."

"It's one of *your* plans. Those are mutually exclusive categories."

But she squeezed my arm, once, hard, and then she was gone into the dark with her people, and the camp was mine and Hask's.

* * *

He found me in the records room, because of course he did. The alarm wasn't the team missing—it was the turbine log showing flow spikes, because I'd opened the spillway gates a third of the way and the machinery was complaining about it.

Hask came through the door with a lantern and a rifle and the particular calm of a man who has killed often enough that it no longer requires emotion, only scheduling.

"The chaser," he said. He knew about me. That shouldn't have surprised me—Calder had known about the survey team too, and knowing things was apparently their whole business model. "The optimists' man. They put a price on you, you know. Quite a large one."

"I've heard. I'm told it doesn't cover the interest."

"You should have stayed in the storm business. Weather doesn't hold grudges."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't hold anything. That's rather its charm."

Behind him, through the records room's high window, the lake was rising against the spillway gates I'd cranked open—not enough to breach the dam, I'm not a monster, but enough to flood the lower galleries. Enough to reach the cabinets. Water was already sheeting across the floor, lifting ledger pages, sending eleven years of Calder's careful bookkeeping swirling past our boots like leaves.

Hask looked down at it, and for the first time his calm developed a crack. "You're destroying company property."

"I'm returning it to the shareholders. All of them. Downstream."

He raised the rifle. I'll say this for him: he didn't monologue, didn't gloat, didn't give me a speech about empires and necessity. He just raised it, the way weather raises wind, and I threw myself sideways behind the filing cabinets as the shot went off and the world went white and loud.

Then the river intervened.

Not dramatically—that's the thing nobody understands about water. It doesn't do drama. It does arithmetic. The spillway, running a third open, had backed water into the turbine gallery, and the turbine gallery had a drain, and the drain had been installed by a contractor who cut corners in a year when Calder was buying officials instead of concrete. The floor beneath Hask's boots simply stopped being a floor. It became a current. And currents, unlike companies, don't negotiate.

I heard him go—one shout, cut short, the rifle clanging off concrete—and then the sound of the river doing what it does to everything it takes, which is nothing at all. No anger. No ceremony. Just movement toward the sea.

I lay there among the floating pages, ears ringing, shoulder bleeding where a cabinet edge had found me, and listened to the most powerful man in the valley get audited by gravity.

* * *

I went out the way the water went, through the breach in the lower gallery, swimming the flooded channel with a satchel of the driest documents strapped to my chest—the ones I judged too important to trust entirely to the current. The rest, thousands of pages, rode the spillway down into the gorge, into the Kestrel proper, into the long brown ribbon that unspooled all the way to Amberford and the valley towns beyond.

Paper floats. I'd bet six lives and my own on it.

By the time I caught up with Vale's group at the tributary fork, the river below us was already carrying the evidence past settlements where farmers would pull soggy pages from their irrigation ditches, from their millwheels, from the nets of fishermen who'd wondered for eleven years why the Kestrel ran thin in August. Diversion schedules. Payment records. Memos. The whole secret, delivered free of charge, postage paid by the Calder Company.

Merrick, ankle and all, fished a page out of an eddy, read it, and started laughing—a raw, disbelieving sound. "This is a payroll record. This is a *payroll record* for the men who guarded us."

"Keep it," I said. "It'll be evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"That you were worth more than they paid."

* * *

We made the pass on the sixth day, ahead of the snow, and came down into the valley to find it already transformed. Not by armies—by talk. The pages had done their work faster than I'd imagined, passed hand to hand in taverns and meeting halls, read aloud to those who couldn't read, until the whole valley understood that the water they'd been rationing, the water they'd been *billed* for, had never belonged to Calder at all. It had been theirs, taken while they slept, sold back to them drop by drop.

There was a bounty on my head when I came down off the mountain. There was a larger one on Calder's secret, posted jointly by three valley towns, and I collected it in the square at Amberford in front of perhaps four hundred people, which rather ended my career as an anonymous man.

The optimists' debt—the rig, the interest, the threats—I settled the next morning, laying the money on the desk of a man named Corvel who smiled like a knife being sheathed and asked, too casually, where a debt-burdened storm chaser had come by that kind of cash.

"Found something valuable," I said. "Gave it away. Turns out truth pays better than storms, if you can survive the exposure."

"And now?"

"Now I'm expensive and famous and hunted by one company and beloved by several thousand farmers, so my odds have improved considerably."

* * *

On my last evening in the valley, before the warrants and the whispers caught up and I had to move on, I walked down to the Kestrel where it ran past the town—running high, running honest, running the way it had run before men in suits decided a river was a balance sheet.

Vale found me there. She stood beside me a while without speaking, which is the highest compliment a surveyor can pay.

"They'll rebuild," she said eventually. "Calder. Or something wearing Calder's clothes. Companies are like that."

"I know."

"You could disappear. Head south, change your name, go chase tornadoes until the debt collectors of the world forget your face."

"I tried invisible. Didn't suit me."

"No," she said, and almost smiled. "It never did."

Below us the water went on toward the lowlands, carrying silt and light and, somewhere in its memory, the pages of everything Calder had tried to keep. I'd spent years reading storms—pressure falling, wind turning, the sky stacking itself into violence—and I knew the signs as well as any man alive. Trouble was coming. It always comes for men who give away other people's secrets.

But that evening, watching the river run free, I found I didn't mind.

Some forecasts, after all, are worth standing out in.
