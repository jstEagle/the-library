## 1. Six Tides of Dust

The thief had a limp.

Wren Voss noticed it before she'd finished her tea — the way he favored his left leg when he thought nobody was watching, the careful half-step he took off the porch of the Saltglass Waystation like his knee had opinions about stairs. He'd paid for his water with coins counted out too slowly, and he'd kept his pack between himself and the room, and he'd looked at the door four times in ten minutes. She'd counted. Counting was most of tracking, if you were honest about it. The rest was patience, and patience was the one thing Wren had never managed to grow, no matter how many seasons she planted it.

He'd stolen a surveyor's transit case from the government depot in Harrow Flats. Inside: instruments worth more than the waystation, more than the thief's whole life probably, and — this was the part that actually mattered to somebody with a stamp and a seal — a set of calibration plates that the Meridian Survey needed for their work at the Rivergate. The clerk who'd hired her hadn't known what any of it did. He'd just known it was gone, and that gone things made clerks sweat through their collars.

Wren drained her tea, left three coppers on the table, and walked out into the white morning after him without hurrying.

That was the trick of it. Everybody thought tracking was about speed. Speed was for people who'd already lost the trail. Wren moved at the pace of a woman going nowhere in particular, which meant she reached everywhere exactly when she meant to.

* * *

The flats outside Saltglass were the color of old bone, cracked into a mosaic of pans and ridges that went on until the sky got tired of being blue and gave up to haze. Heat stood on the ground in visible columns. A dead sea lay somewhere out past the eastern horizon — you couldn't see it from here, but you could feel it the way you felt an empty room behind a wall: a flatness, a listening quality to the air.

Wren followed the thief's prints across the first pan. He was making for the old caravan cut, probably figuring the broken ground would shake any pursuit. It wouldn't. Broken ground remembered everything. Sand forgot; stone didn't. He'd stepped on a shelf of shale forty minutes back and left a scuff as legible as a signature: *Pell Amos, writes his e's lazy, favors the left knee, carries maybe sixty pounds and hates every ounce.*

She'd gotten his name from the waystation ledger. Pell Amos. Twenty-two, maybe twenty-three. The desk clerk said he'd come through twice before, always alone, always polite, always broke. Wren had been tracking people long enough to build whole lives out of details like that, and the life she built for Pell Amos was small and hard and not cruel so much as cornered. People didn't steal calibration plates because they wanted calibration plates. They stole them because somebody was buying.

Which meant somebody else might be following him too, and that was the only part of the job she didn't like. Not the heat, not the miles, not even the thief. Just the possibility of a second set of tracks appearing where there should only be one.

By midday she could see him, a smudge against the shimmer, moving with the stubborn rhythm of a man who had decided suffering was a plan. Wren stopped in the shade of a leaning salt-crust boulder, ate a strip of dried cactus fruit, and let him get further away.

This was the other thing people got wrong about tracking. You didn't chase. Chasing was a conversation, and the other person got a vote. You *arrived*. You knew where they were going before they did — the caravan cut led to the sink wells at Tallow Bend, and Tallow Bend had exactly one place selling passage east, and east was where the money for stolen instruments would be. So she drank, and ate, and watched the heat dance, and thought about nothing at all, which was its own kind of discipline.

Her brother Joss used to say she thought in straight lines because curves scared her.

Joss had been gone eleven years now, and she still argued with him in her head, which either proved he was right or that she'd never learned another way to keep someone.

* * *

She caught up to him at dusk, at the mouth of the caravan cut, where the walls rose on both sides like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down and become geography.

"Evening," Wren said.

Pell Amos spun, dropped the transit case — it clattered, and he flinched at the sound like it had bitten him — and put up his hands in one motion, which told her he'd done this before, the getting-caught part. "I don't have anything," he said. "You've got the wrong—"

"You're limping on your left knee. You write your e's lazy. You bought water at Saltglass with a coin that had a hole drilled through it, which means you keep your emergency money sewn somewhere and you're down to the emergency emergency." She walked closer, unhurried, boots quiet on the stone. "And you stole a case from the Meridian depot that has a government seal stamped on it in three places. So let's skip to the part where you tell me who's buying."

"I didn't—" He swallowed. Up close he was younger than she'd guessed, all elbows and sunburn, with the particular hollow look of someone who'd been hungry long enough that hunger had become a personality. "There's a broker in Tallow Bend. I don't know his name. Everybody calls him the Weighmaster. He pays by weight. I was going to—"

"Sell it by weight." Wren looked at the case. Calibration plates, machined to tolerances she couldn't imagine, sold by the pound. "That's almost impressive. In a direction."

"I need to get east." It came out of him fast, the truth finally outrunning the lies. "My sister's in the river villages. There's sickness — the wells are going bitter, everyone says so, and passage costs—"

"Everyone says so," Wren repeated. She'd heard it too, in three territories this year. Wells going bitter. Rivers running thin. The desert drinking itself dry from the inside out. It was the kind of story that started as rumor and ended as history. "Sit down. Drink something. You look like a dried apricot with opinions."

He sat down. He drank something. His hands shook around the flask, and she pretended not to notice, because noticing out loud would have cost him something he didn't have to spare.

"The case goes back," she said. "You walk with me to Tallow Bend tomorrow, we return it, and then — and I want to be clear that this is me being soft, because I'm famous for it — I'll pay your passage east myself. Out of my own purse. Which is not deep, so don't go diving in."

"Why?" He stared at her. "Why would you—"

"Because the alternative is turning you over to the magistrate in Harrow Flats, and the magistrate there has a quota, and you've got a knee that won't survive a work gang." She shrugged. "Stopping costs more than chasing. Always has. But sometimes the stopping is worth it."

He was quiet for a while. The light went from gold to copper to that brief violet the desert did each evening, like a bruise forming over the whole world, and then the cold came down the way it always did, sudden as a door closing.

"They say the Rivergate's cursed," Pell said, apropos of nothing, hugging his knees. "The survey team that went out there. My sister wrote me about it. Six of them, and then nothing. No word for two months. The government keeps saying they've relocated."

"Mm." Wren banked the little fire she'd allowed herself. "People say a lot of things about the Rivergate."

"Do you believe them?"

"I believe in tracks," Wren said. "Tracks don't lie. Everything else is gossip with ambition."

She didn't sleep much. She never did. She lay with her hat over her eyes and listened to the boy breathe and felt the familiar restlessness pacing circles in her chest, the feeling that had been her closest companion since Joss left — the sense that wherever she was, whatever she'd caught or recovered or fixed, the real thing was always one horizon further on. Keep moving and nothing can catch you. That had been the whole philosophy, once. Somewhere along the way it had stopped being a philosophy and become just a habit, the way a river stops being water and becomes a canyon.

* * *

They made Tallow Bend by the second noon, and that was where the courier found her.

Wren saw the sledge first — a proper government runner-sled, drawn by two mules that had cost somebody real money, flying the Meridian colors. Then the courier himself, standing in the street outside the Weighmaster's shed with a document tube under his arm and the expression of a man who had been riding for days specifically to ruin someone's afternoon.

"Wren Voss?" he called, before she'd even handed the case over to the depot man. Word traveled ahead of her; it usually did. Reputation was a kind of track too, and hers had gotten long enough that she sometimes tripped over it.

"Depends who's asking and what it costs me."

"Corporal Ede, Meridian Survey Command." He produced papers. Of course he produced papers. Government men and their papers — she'd seen men drown clutching less important documents. "I'm authorized to offer you a contract of recovery."

"Weighmaster first," she said, nodding at the shed. "Business before business."

Inside, the transaction went the way she'd predicted. The Weighmaster — a vast, mild man with spectacles and the hands of a jeweler — examined the transit case, sighed like a disappointed uncle, and paid Wren the recovery fee without argument. Pell Amos stood in the doorway looking like he expected to be struck by lightning on general principles. Wren pressed a fold of bills into his hand on the way out, enough for passage east and a little extra, and told him to buy his sister something sweet with it, and he looked at her the way people sometimes did — like she was a door they hadn't known was in the wall.

Then Corporal Ede spread his documents on a table in the shade, and Wren's afternoon got worse.

"Six members of the Meridian advance survey team," Ede said. "Departed Harrow Flats fourteen weeks ago, bound for the Rivergate ruins on the dead sea coast. Final transmission received nine weeks ago. Routine. Position report, water status, all normal." He laid out a map, and there it was — the drowned ruin at the edge of everything, where the old maps stopped pretending and started guessing. "Since then: nothing. A follow-up team sent six weeks ago found the camp intact."

"Intact how?"

Ede hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Wren had spent her life reading hesitations the way other people read weather.

"Tents pitched. Meals half-eaten. Instruments still running, still recording. Every piece of personal gear accounted for." He paused again. "No remains. And no tracks, Voss. Not leaving the camp, not arriving at it. The senior investigator walked the site for two days. He said—" Ede's jaw worked. "He said it was like the desert refused to remember them."

Wren looked at the map. The heat shimmered off the paper. Somewhere past the edge of it, a dead sea lay flat and silent under a sky that had forgotten how to rain, and in its bones a city that had once moved water the way hearts moved blood.

"No tracks," she said. "In any direction."

"In any direction. Including inside the tents. Including *under* the bedrolls." Ede met her eyes, and beneath the corporal's polish she saw the thing that had driven him across three hundred miles of flats: fear wearing a uniform. "Command wants it handled quietly. There are salvage interests circling — the water-technology under that ruin would be worth a fleet, and there's a commander named Brann who's been buying up charts all spring. Command would rather find their people first. Or find out what happened to them. Before it becomes a war."

"And the fee?"

"Triple your standard rate. Plus equipment, plus authority to hire local support." He slid the contract across. "There's a time element. The tides on the dead sea run in six-day cycles. At high tide, the lower ruins flood completely. If there's anyone alive down there in the flooded streets—"

"Six tides," Wren said.

"Six tides," Ede agreed.

She should have said no. Some part of her, the tired part, the part that had been running for eleven years and calling it a career, knew exactly what this was: the kind of job that ate trackers. No tracks meant no trail, and no trail meant she'd be walking blind into the one place in the world her skills were worthless, racing a fleet commander with more guns than sense, chasing people who had apparently been erased rather than killed.

But the other part of her — the restless part, the part that paced circles in her chest at night — was already leaning toward the horizon like a plant toward light.

And underneath both parts, in a room she kept locked, a third voice said quietly: *Joss went east, eleven years ago. Toward the water. Toward the Gate.*

"One condition," Wren said. "Everything your follow-up team recorded. All of it. Notes, sketches, the investigator's reports. I want every word."

"Done."

She signed. Ede packed his papers with the brisk relief of a man unloading cargo, and within the hour the runner-sled was a dust plume heading west, and Wren Voss stood alone in the street of Tallow Bend with a contract in her pocket and six days on the clock.

Pell Amos was still there, waiting by the east road with his passage ticket, and he watched her check her water, check her gear, check the angle of the sun.

"You're going out there," he said. Not a question. "To the Rivergate."

"Looks like it."

"My sister wrote that the water out there moves wrong." He shifted, embarrassed. "Against the wind, she said. Like it's walking."

Wren settled her hat and looked east, where the land flattened toward a sea that had died and a city that hadn't, quite.

"Then it'll be the first honest thing the water's ever done," she said, and started walking.
