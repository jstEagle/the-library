## 2. Gear Without Graves

Wren Voss had a rule about camps: you could tell everything about a camp from its edges. People were careless at the edges. They dropped things there, spat there, argued there, left the small honest debris of themselves where they thought nobody was looking. A tracker who read only the center of a camp read what people wanted her to see. The edges told the truth.

So she circled.

The Meridian survey camp sat on a low shelf of hardpan above the salt flats, half a day's ride from the Rivergate's distant gray smudge. Six tents. A cook fire burned down to white ash but swept in a neat ring — somebody had been tidy. A windbreak of stacked crates faced away from the prevailing easterly, which meant whoever built it knew desert weather. A solar still glinted on a pole like a lazy second sun.

Everything was correct. That was the first wrong thing.

Wren had walked into a hundred abandoned camps in her years as a ranger, and every one of them was wrong in some human way. A tent pole snapped because somebody got drunk and leaned on it. A latrine trench dug too shallow because morale was bad. Boots gone missing because somebody bet them on a card game and lost. Camps were like people: imperfect in ways that made sense.

This one was perfect the way a stage set is perfect.

She crouched at the eastern edge and looked for the truth anyway. Dropped thread. A button. A hair tie. The ghost of a boot heel pressed too deep by someone favoring a sore ankle. Nothing. The hardpan out here was old and baked and didn't take prints easily — she'd known that before she came, had planned around it, had brought her dusting kit and her patience. But even hardpan collected *something*. Grit blown against tent walls left tide-marks of fine silt. Rope ends frayed. Canvas scuffed where knees pushed through from inside.

The nearest tent wall was scuffed exactly nowhere.

"All right," she said quietly, to herself, because talking to yourself on a job was either madness or method and she preferred to think of it as method. "Let's see your edges."

* * *

She started with the cook fire, because food tells stories.

There was a pot on the iron grate, lid slightly askew, holding the desiccated remains of what had once been barley stew. Not scraped clean. Not dumped out. Half-eaten and left, which in the desert was close to sacrilege. You finished your stew or you carried it. Water went into that stew, and water out here was money, and blood, and time. Nobody left half a pot of water sitting under an open sky unless something interrupted them mid-bite.

But the ash was cold. Days cold. And the stew hadn't spoiled so much as mummified — the dry air had taken it the way it took everything, patiently, without malice.

Wren lifted the lid and sniffed. Barley, salt-pork, a whisper of dried pepperleaf. Somebody had cooked well. Somebody had been, right up until the last moment, having a decent evening.

Beside the fire sat six tin plates. Five of them held scraps. The sixth was clean, stacked neatly atop the others, as though its owner had finished first and done the polite thing.

Or as though five people had been eating and one person had never sat down.

She counted the plates twice. Then she counted the tents again — six — and stood there in the flat white light feeling the first true cold of the day, which was odd, because the day was hot enough to boil thought.

"Six tents," she said. "Five dinners."

Maybe one was on watch. Maybe one was sick. Maybe one ate alone because some people do. She wrote down none of this, because she kept her notes in her head where thieves and weather couldn't get at them, and she moved on to the instruments.

The survey team had come out here to chart the Rivergate's water-tables — government work, the kind of job that came with good gear and bad odds. Their equipment was still running. That was the part the courier's report had called "anomalous," in the way bureaucrats say *anomalous* when they mean *I don't want to think about it.*

A hygrometer ticked away inside its case, stylus tracing humidity readings onto a paper drum. Its ink reservoir was nearly empty, which meant it had been running unattended for days. A barograph beside it breathed its slow silver line up and down, up and down, recording tides of air pressure from a ruin that shouldn't have any. On a folding table, someone's field journal lay open, weighted with a stone, pen capped and laid parallel across the page like a soldier at attention.

Wren read the last entry upside down, which is how trackers read most journals, and felt her stomach do something slow and unpleasant.

It wasn't what was written. It was the handwriting. Neat, level, unhurried — the hand of a person planning to write more tomorrow. No final line trailing off. No blot where a pen dropped. No hurried scratch of *— heard something outside, going to look.* Just a completed sentence about salinity gradients, a period, and a capped pen.

People who are taken violently leave violent pages. People who flee leave frantic ones. This page left nothing at all. It simply stopped being written in, the way a clock stops being wound.

"Who caps their pen?" she asked the empty camp. The camp declined to answer.

* * *

By late afternoon she'd catalogued the whole site, and the catalogue read like a riddle composed by something that had studied humans carefully but never quite understood them.

Tents pitched properly, guy-lines tensioned, flaps tied back for airflow. Bedrolls laid out, some slept-in, some not. A deck of cards spread mid-game on a crate — a hand of seven cards face-up, waiting for a player who had apparently stepped away between turns. A boot by the door of the third tent, unlaced, its partner missing. A woman's hair ribbon tied to a tent pole, the kind of thing you tie when you mean to come back for it. A tin of pepperleaf tea, open, a spoon standing upright in it, fused to the rim by crystallized honey.

And sand. Sand everywhere, because the desert puts sand everywhere, that was the whole arrangement. Sand in the card game. Sand in the tea. Sand drifted in soft grey fans against the windward walls.

No tracks. Anywhere. Not around the camp, not between the tents, not leading in or out along any of the eight directions she checked, and she checked all eight plus the two verticals, because over the years she'd learned that people sometimes go up or down and amateurs forget.

Inside the tents, the floors were swept clean. Not tidy — *clean*, in a way that made no sense, because these were canvas floors laid on raw earth, and raw earth doesn't sweep. Yet the ground beneath each bedroll was smooth and bare as a tabletop, as if the sand itself had decided, politely, to be elsewhere.

Wren knelt in the doorway of the fourth tent and put her palm flat against that impossible floor.

Her whole life, the ground had talked to her. That was the family trade — the Amberdales had been reading sign since before the river villages had names, and Wren had inherited the knack the way other children inherited eye color. Every footprint was a sentence. Every drag-mark, a paragraph. She could follow a man across glass-smooth dunes three days after rain, could tell you his height, his limp, whether he was carrying a child or a rifle or both, whether he'd eaten recently and whether he'd regretted it. The ground never lied to her. The ground was the one honest correspondent in a world full of liars.

The ground under her palm said nothing. Worse than nothing. It said nothing *deliberately*, the way a witness says *I don't recall.*

"Fine," she told it. "Be that way."

She sat back on her heels and did the thing she did when a trail went cold: she stopped looking forward and started looking backward. What had happened here? Work backward from the stillness. Five people eating dinner. One plate unused. Instruments running, journals neat, cards mid-deal. Nobody packed. Nobody panicked. Nobody fought — no scuffs, no blood, no overturned anything. Whatever came for the Meridian team hadn't broken in.

It had been invited. Or it hadn't needed an invitation. Or —

Or there had never been a moment at all. That was the thought she kept circling and refusing to land on, the one that made her palms sweat despite the heat: that the five diners hadn't been interrupted mid-meal, but had simply *ceased*, the way a candle ceases when pinched, leaving the room exactly as it was except for the absence.

She shook it off. Rangers didn't get paid for philosophy.

* * *

She found the sixth tent's occupant at dusk, or rather, found the evidence of him.

The sixth tent was smaller than the others, pitched apart, upwind — the spot you give a stranger, or a superior, or someone nobody likes. Inside: a bedroll never slept in, a pack unpacked but untouched, and a government courier's satchel, official seal broken. Wren hesitated exactly one heartbeat over the ethics of reading another ranger's mail, then remembered that the other ranger was almost certainly dead, or worse than dead, and opened it.

The orders inside were hers. Or a copy of hers. Meridian Survey Command, requesting ranger escort and retrieval, priority flag, signed by the same gray functionary whose signature sat on the contract currently weighing down her breast pocket. Beneath the orders, a short note in different handwriting — quicker, less careful:

*Ranger V. — if you're reading this, we've been here nine days and the water's behaving strangely. Readings triple overnight. Team lead says instrument error. Instrument error doesn't hum. Going down to the Gate tomorrow to see for ourselves. If we're not back by your arrival, don't follow us in. Wait for the tide charts. — M.*

Wren read it three times.

*Instrument error doesn't hum.*

She looked up through the tent flap at the darkening east, where the Rivergate lay like a bruise on the horizon, and listened. Wind in the guy-lines. Her own pulse. The tick of the hygrometer behind her, faithful as a small heart.

And underneath it all — so faint she might have invented it, so faint she immediately decided she had invented it, because the alternative was unacceptable — a hum. Low. Patient. Like something enormous breathing through stone.

"Nope," she said firmly, to herself, to the tent, to the hum. "Not tonight."

Because here was the other thing, the thing she'd been refusing to look at all day, the thing sitting in the back of her skull like a splinter: light was failing, and she had intended to make camp here, in this camp, sleep in one of these beds, eat from these stores. Standard procedure. Why pitch your own shelter when a perfectly good one stands empty?

And now she could not imagine lying down in one of those bedrolls. Could not imagine pulling a canvas flap shut behind her in a place where the sand refused to hold a print. Her body had voted without consulting her, and her body had voted *anywhere else.*

That bothered her more than the hum. Wren Voss did not spook. Spooking was for greenhorns and townfolk. She'd slept in graveyards, in war zones, in a smuggler's den with a knife under her cheek and both eyes closed. Fear was a tool like any other; you used it or you put it away.

But she caught herself checking the tie-downs on her own gear, the ones she'd check before a long ride, and she hadn't decided to do that, and that was new.

* * *

She made her own camp four hundred meters west, downwind, small and mean — a one-woman fly sheet, a cold meal, a fire no bigger than a cupped hand. If whatever lived at the Rivergate read camps the way she did, let it read this one: *one rider, passing through, nothing here worth taking.* She'd spent her whole life making herself look like less than she was. Tonight the habit felt less like craft and more like prayer.

She sat up with the courier's note and the contract and her own head, and she did arithmetic.

Nine days the survey team had been here before vanishing. The stew said the vanish itself was maybe six days back. Government response time being what it was — glacial, with paperwork — the contract had reached her within days of the report, which meant somebody at Meridian Command had moved fast for once. Which meant somebody up the chain considered this important. Important enough to hire the best tracker on the flats, and Wren was under no illusions about which adjective had gotten her hired.

Important enough, maybe, that others would come too.

She fed a twig to her tiny fire and watched it catch, and let herself think the unwelcome thought all the way through for the first time: she was not the only vulture circling this carcass. The Rivergate was a drowned ruin sitting on the largest unmapped water reserve in the territory. Water, in the dying territories, was worth more than gold, more than guns, more than governments. There were men — and fleets — who would burn three villages for a working wellhead. Whatever the survey team had found, they hadn't found it privately for long, and they certainly weren't around to defend the claim now.

Somewhere out on that dead sea, she'd wager her best knife, ships were already moving.

The hum came again, faintly, from the east. Or she imagined it. She was beginning to suspect those were going to be the same thing.

"One problem at a time," she told herself, which was her mother's saying, and her grandmother's, and probably her great-grandmother's, the Amberdale women having always been firm believers in problems arriving in single file. "Find the team. Find out what took them. Get paid. Ride home."

Simple. Clean. A current with one channel.

She banked her fire, wrapped herself in her blanket, and lay looking up at the stars through the mesh of her fly sheet, listening to the silence where footprints should have been.

Sleep took its time coming, and when it came, it brought her nothing useful — just the image of that sixth plate, clean and stacked, and a voice that might have been memory or might have been the hum, asking, very politely, a question she couldn't quite hear.

She woke before dawn with the answer already dissolving, and the eastern horizon already changing.

At first she took it for cloud. Then for a heat shimmer. Then her eye — the tracker's eye, the one thing in the world that had never failed her until yesterday — resolved the gray shapes on the dead sea's far edge into hulls, masts, rigging, smoke.

Ships. A line of them, moving on the dry sea's ancient bed like fish swimming through salt, and at their head, cutting a wake through the flats, something big.

Wren stood up slowly, counting.

Then she started breaking camp, fast.
