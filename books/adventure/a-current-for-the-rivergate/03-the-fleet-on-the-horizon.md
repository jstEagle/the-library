## 3. The Fleet on the Horizon

Wren Voss had a rule about horizons: never trust one that looks the same two hours in a row.

The dead sea had been flat and silver and empty all morning, a sheet of salt-crusted water so still it looked poured rather than fallen. She'd been walking its northern rim since dawn, following the survey team's last known heading toward the Rivergate, and for two hours the horizon had given her nothing but glare. Then, between one blink and the next, it gave her ships.

Three of them. No—four. Low, dark hulls riding the shallow water like knives laid on a table, their sails furled, running on engines that coughed smoke into the clean desert air. Salvage rigs. Big ones.

Wren stopped, which was itself an event. She counted things when she stopped: water remaining, distance to the ruin, the angle of the sun, the number of ways this day could get worse. The counting was a habit from her ranger years, and like most of her habits it existed because the alternative was thinking about how tired she was.

Four salvage ships meant a fleet. A fleet meant a commander. And a commander meant paperwork with teeth.

She unslung her spyglass—a battered brass thing she'd won off a card sharp who'd cheated wrong—and put it to her eye. The lead ship resolved slowly through the heat shimmer: black hull, red waterline, and on the bow, painted large enough to read across half a mile of dead sea, a coiled serpent biting its own tail.

Korvane.

"Well," Wren said to the horizon, "that's not ideal."

* * *

The Korvane salvage fleet had a reputation that preceded it the way a rockslide precedes the rocks. They didn't loot wrecks; they digested them. Give them a sunken city and they'd return you a hole with a receipt stapled to it. Their commanders were famous for two qualities: an unfailing instinct for valuable ruins, and a creative relationship with other people's contracts.

Wren had the government contract in her coat pocket. It was written on proper courier stock, sealed with the Meridian Authority's stamp, and it named her specifically, which was flattering in a way she hadn't asked for. It also, she suspected, weighed less than whatever document Commander Ilsa Brann carried, if Brann's documents came backed by four armed ships.

The mathematics were simple and unpleasant. The Rivergate lay two days' hard walk south along the rim. The fleet, cutting straight across the shallows where the dead sea went glass-flat, would reach it in six tides. Maybe five, if their engines were better than they looked.

Six tides. Wren turned the number over the way she'd turn a stone to see what lived under it. Six tides to find a vanished survey team, figure out what erased them, and decide whether any of it was worth defending from a woman who owned four ships and, presumably, no conscience.

Her feet started moving before she finished the arithmetic. That was the other rule, the real one, the one that had kept her alive while better rangers became cautionary tales: *when the numbers go bad, stop counting and start walking.*

* * *

She made camp that night in the lee of a salt ridge, a low white spine of crusted earth that broke the wind off the sea. She ate dried beans and hardtack and told herself the beans were a stew. This was another of her habits, and it fooled no one, least of all the beans.

The fleet's lights were visible now, strung along the southern dark like a necklace someone had dropped. Campfires on deck. Engine glow below the waterline. They weren't hurrying. That bothered her more than speed would have. Hurrying meant worry; a leisurely pace meant certainty. Brann knew something Wren didn't, or Brann believed she did, and either way the woman was treating the Rivergate like property already deeded.

Wren fed a twist of scrub brush to her small fire and let herself think about the survey camp again, because not thinking about it had failed as a strategy all day.

Tents pitched. Meals half-eaten—one bowl still holding porridge gone to leather. Instruments running, logbooks open, a kettle sitting cold on a cold stove. Eleven people, eleven packs, eleven bedrolls. And sand. Sand everywhere, smooth and unmarked, including inside the tents, including *under* the sleeping rolls, as though the whole camp had been lifted, wiped, and set back down by a hand that took only the people.

In twenty years of tracking, Wren had read every kind of scene the desert wrote. Raiders left blood and boot-prints. Sickness left bodies and shame. Deserters left apologies in the form of stolen horses. But nothing—nothing—left *clean*. The world didn't work that way. Wind filled tracks, animals scattered bones, time blurred everything eventually. Time was the great smudger. Whatever had taken the Meridian team hadn't blurred anything. It had *edited*, and editing required intent, and intent required a mind, and minds were the one thing Wren had always been able to outlast.

You couldn't outrun a mind that lived in the ground, though. That thought kept arriving, patient as the tide, and she kept setting it down like a hot pan.

"Six tides," she told the fire. "One problem at a time."

The fire, being fire, declined to argue.

* * *

Dawn brought wind, and wind brought the sound of engines, and engines brought Wren out of a shallow sleep with her knife already in her hand.

The fleet had closed the gap overnight more than she'd liked. Through the spyglass she picked out details she'd been too far to see before: deck cranes, cargo nets, the glint of armed men along the rails. And on the lead ship's sterncastle, a figure at the rail watching the shore line with the fixed attention of someone reading a ledger.

Even at this distance there was something about the way the woman stood. Square. Unhurried. Hands folded behind her back like a schoolmistress surveying a classroom of children who hadn't yet learned what she knew about them.

Ilsa Brann. Wren had never met her, but the desert was small in the way that mattered—rumor traveled faster than water, and rumor said Commander Brann had once salvaged a hospital ship and billed the survivors for storage.

Around midmorning, the impossible happened: the fleet changed course toward her.

Wren stood very still on the salt rim and considered this. There were maybe forty people within fifty miles, and she was one of them, standing alone on a white ridge with a fire pit and a spyglass. Either Brann had excellent eyes, or excellent informants, or both, and none of those options improved Wren's mood.

A launch detached from the lead ship—a long black boat with an engine note like a wasp in a bottle—and ran for the shallows near her position. It grounded on the salt shelf thirty yards out, and three figures waded ashore: two sailors with the heavy shoulders of professional muscle, and between them a woman in a grey coat cut with the kind of precision that cost more than Wren earned in a season.

Commander Ilsa Brann walked across the salt flats the way she presumably did everything: as though the ground had been arranged in advance for her convenience.

"Ranger Voss." Her voice carried without effort, pitched like a voice used to being heard over engines. "You're a difficult woman to find. Fortunately, you're also a predictable one."

"I prefer 'consistent,'" Wren said. "It sounds like a virtue."

"It sounds like a liability." Brann stopped a polite six feet away, close enough for conversation, far enough for dignity. Up close she was older than her posture suggested, with grey coming in at her temples and eyes the flat color of deep water. "You have the Meridian contract."

Not a question. Wren didn't confirm it anyway. "And you're trespassing on a government survey site."

"The government," Brann said, "voided its claim to the Rivergate nine years ago, when it declared the region unrecoverable. Anything unrecoverable is abandoned. Anything abandoned is mine." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm even, the smile of someone who found the world genuinely amusing. "I have the documentation. You're welcome to read it. It's longer than yours, and better argued."

"Longer isn't better. I've read some very long ransom notes."

Something flickered behind Brann's eyes—not anger. Interest. "They said you'd be like this. The trackers they send out here stop asking questions after the first year. You've been asking them for twenty."

"I ask questions because the answers are usually shorter than the questions." Wren shifted her weight, casual, keeping the wind at her back out of old habit. "Here's one. Your fleet is headed for a ruin that swallowed eleven trained surveyors without leaving a footprint. What exactly do you think your hulls are worth down there?"

"They'll be worth whatever's under the water," Brann said simply. "The Rivergate was a water-engineering civilization, Ranger. Cisterns, condensers, purification works—the old texts say they made a dead sea drinkable once. Do you know what that technology is worth to the territories? What it's worth to *me*?" She glanced past Wren, south, toward the invisible ruin, and for just a moment the amusement drained out of her face and left something hungrier underneath. "Eleven surveyors is a rounding error. I've spent more people than that finding less."

"There it is," Wren said. "I was wondering when we'd get to the part where people are currency."

"People are *always* currency. The only question is the exchange rate." Brann said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse—she might have been discussing freight rates. Then the warmth returned, seamless as a stage trick. "Which brings me to my offer. You know the flats, the channels, the approaches. My charts of this coast are forty years old. Work with me—guide my advance teams, and when the Rivergate comes up, you take first pick of the finders' share. Enough to retire. Enough to *stop*, if that's what rangers dream about."

For one unguarded second, the word *stop* landed somewhere under Wren's ribs and stayed there, glowing like a coal.

Brann saw it. Of course she saw it. She'd aimed for it.

"No," Wren said.

"No?" Brann tilted her head, genuinely curious. "No negotiation? No counteroffer? Most people at least pretend."

"I've watched your fleet operate. Korvane takes the wreck and leaves the hole. I'm not interested in being part of the hole."

"A pity." Brann turned to go, then paused, and over her shoulder added, lightly, almost kindly: "Six tides, Ranger. When my ships make landfall, everything inside the Rivergate becomes fleet property—including anything you're carrying out of it. I'd think carefully about what's worth carrying." She waded back toward the launch, and the wasp-bottle engine swallowed her, and the black boat ran for the fleet like a splinter returning to a hand.

Wren stood on the salt rim and watched the ships settle back onto their southern heading, unhurried, certain, patient as arithmetic.

"Six tides," she said again. But saying it aloud made it worse, so she shouldered her pack and started walking instead.

* * *

She covered twelve miles before the light went, putting ridges between herself and the shore, and made a cold camp in the shadow of a leaning sea-stack crusted white with old salt. No fire tonight. Fires invited company, and she'd had enough company for one week.

She rationed her water carefully, drank, chewed hardtack, and checked her gear by feel in the dark the way she did every night: rope, flint, knife, spyglass, contract, the little tin of her brother's old ranger-badge polish she'd carried for eleven years for reasons she'd never once said out loud.

It was during this inventory that she noticed the beans.

She had two tins of dried beans. She was certain of it—certain the way you're certain of your own teeth. She'd counted them at the survey camp, and she'd eaten from one tin last night, and this morning she'd packed both.

There was one tin.

Wren sat very still in the dark and listened. The wind off the dead sea moved through the stack's hollows with a low organ-note. Somewhere below, water lapped salt rock. Nothing else. No breathing, no shifting, no small human sounds.

But her pack's side flap had been retied. Not badly—actually quite well, a neat ranger's knot, better than her own hurried lashings—but different. Someone had opened her pack, taken a tin of beans, and re-tied it *carefully*, which was either courtesy or contempt, and Wren honestly wasn't sure which insulted her more.

She looked out at the darkness beneath the sea-stack, at the jumble of salt-crusted boulders and driftwood at its base, and raised her voice just enough to carry.

"I don't know who you are," she said, "but you tied a better knot than I did, and you left me the hardtack, which means you're either polite or stupid, and the beans suggest hungry, and hungry and polite means desperate, and desperate means you're still here."

Silence. The wind played its organ-note.

"Also," she added, lying back against her pack and closing her eyes, "you stole from a ranger who is currently being hunted by four ships, so whatever trouble you were in before, congratulations. You're in mine now."

Above the lap of the water, very faintly—from somewhere among the rocks below—she heard a sound.

It might have been the wind.

It sounded almost exactly like a boy trying very hard not to laugh.
