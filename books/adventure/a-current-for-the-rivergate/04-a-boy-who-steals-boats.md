## 4. A Boy Who Steals Boats

The trick to stealing from a ranger, Tam had decided, was to do it while she was looking straight at you.

Not literally. Obviously. But in the way that mattered — while her eyes were on the horizon, on the waterline of the dead sea, on the six salvage ships crawling south like beetles with money. Wren Voss watched everything except the things she'd already catalogued, and Tam had spent two days being carefully, thoroughly catalogued. He was filed. He was finished. Which meant he was free.

He crouched at the stern of the *Kestrel's Debt* — a name so grim it could only belong to a woman who'd bought it off a man who owed her money — and went through her supply crates with the delicacy of a surgeon and the conscience of a gull.

Salt pork: three slabs. He took one.

Hardtack: a full tin. He took six rounds, because more than six would rattle.

Water: he didn't touch. Even river-orphans had lines, and hers was drawn around water like a fence with teeth. She counted her water twice a day, morning and dusk, lips moving over the numbers like a prayer. You did not steal from a woman's prayers.

What he wanted was smaller. The little canvas roll tucked under the chart box — the one with the needles and the gut-thread and the thin bone hooks she used for mending sails and, once, memorably, for stitching a cut on his forearm without asking permission first. He'd been thinking about those hooks all night. A boy who could fish the channels could eat without stealing, and a boy who ate without stealing could stop being a thief, technically, which was the closest thing to respectability the flats offered.

His fingers closed on the roll.

"You're holding your breath," Wren said behind him.

Tam did not jump. He was proud of that later, in the retelling, though in truth his whole skeleton tried to leave through the top of his head and only discipline kept it in place. He turned around slowly, canvas roll behind his back, wearing the expression he'd perfected at age seven: open, guileless, faintly stupid.

"Holding my breath?"

"Over the side." She nodded at the black-green water sliding past the hull. "You were leaning out. People hold their breath when they're deciding whether to jump."

"I was looking at fish."

"There are no fish."

"There are *some* fish."

"In the dead sea?" She came closer, boots quiet on the deck planks, and held out her hand. Not grabbing. Just open, palm up, patient as a stone. "Give me the sail-mending kit, Tam."

"How d'you know it's the—"

"Because you've been circling it since dawn like a moth round a lamp, and because you checked over your shoulder four times, and because you count your steps when you think you're being quiet. Left foot drags. It always drags."

He gave her the roll. There was no dignity in doing otherwise; there was barely dignity in anything anymore. But he kept his chin up while he did it, which was its own kind of currency.

"I wasn't going to sell them," he said.

"No."

"I was going to *fish*."

"I know."

That stopped him. "You know?"

"You've been staring at the water like it owes you dinner. And you asked me yesterday how deep the shallows run off the old ferry road, which is a strange question for a boy who claims he's never been past the salt line." She stowed the roll back under the chart box, then — and this was the part that made his stomach do something complicated — sat down on the crate beside it, close enough to talk, far enough not to loom. "Sit down, Tam."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit down before you fall down. You've been awake since before I was, and I sleep like a cat on a hot roof."

He sat. The deck was warm through his trousers, and the *Debt* rocked gently, and somewhere ahead the Rivergate waited under its drowned streets like a coin at the bottom of a well. Six tides. That's what the ranger had said. Six tides before Commander Brann's fleet arrived, and they'd already burned one getting clear of her picket boats.

"So," Wren said. "The channels."

"What about them?"

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"The thing where you answer a question with a question until I get tired. I've chased men across forty miles of flat pan who tried that. They got tired first."

Tam picked at a splinter on the crate edge. Below them, in the cabin, Sef the old ferryman snored like a landslide with opinions, and the government surveyor's journal — the one they'd taken from the abandoned camp, full of coordinates and increasingly unhinged marginalia — sat locked in the chart drawer where Wren kept it.

"The channels aren't secret," he said finally. "That's the thing nobody understands. Everybody knows *about* them. The fishing villages, the scavvers, Brann's people — they all know there are ways through the shallows to the Gate that don't show on any chart. What they don't know is which ones are real this season."

"This season."

"The flats move." He spread his hands. "Sand does that. A channel that was deep enough for a barge two years ago is a sandbar now, and a crack that was dry as a sermon last spring might be running eight feet. The charts are lies with good manners. But if you've grown up reading the water—" he tapped his own chest, "—you can tell. Color of it. Smell of it. The way the birds sit. There's a channel called the Needle that'll take us inside the Gate walls without passing a single one of Brann's pickets, and it's real right now, because I fished it eleven days ago."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Out on the water, the light was going long and coppery, and the salvage fleet was a smudge of smoke against the south.

"Why were you on my boat?" she asked. "Original answer this time. Not the one about wanting passage to the coast."

Tam looked at the water. At his feet. At the water again.

"Because everyone I knew who stayed on shore stopped," he said. "You heard what happened to the net-villages east of the Ferry Roads. One season the boats come in light, next season they don't come in at all, and the people just—" he made a gesture, vague and awful, fingers opening like something letting go. "Gone. No bodies. No tracks. My mother's cousin walked out to check his nets at dawn and the nets were fine and he wasn't there. So when I saw a ranger heading for the Rivergate, alone, armed, mean enough to survive it—" he shrugged, "—I figured wherever you ended up, you'd still be *there*. And I want to be somewhere that stays."

"That's a terrible plan. I'm planning to go toward the thing that eats people."

"Yeah," Tam said. "But you're planning to come back out. I can tell. You walk like someone who's already decided to survive it. Most people walk like they're asking permission."

Something crossed her face then — quick, gone before he could name it, like a fish turning under the surface. He was learning to read her the way he read the channels: color, weight, the small tells. She was easier than water in some ways and much harder in others.

"You stole from me," she said. "Twice. The bread on the first night, and now the kit."

"The bread was going stale."

"It was not."

"...The bread was going stale," he repeated, with conviction.

"And if I put you ashore at the next hard ground, you'd be stealing from someone less patient by sundown, and dead within the month, or walking into the shallows after your cousin's ghost." She said it flatly, no cruelty in it, just arithmetic — the same tone she used for distances and tide tables. "So here's what happens instead. You guide us through the Needle and whatever comes after. You eat from my stores, same ration as mine, no more sneaking. You keep Sef company, because he talks in his sleep and it unsettles me. And when this is done — if we live — I pay you honest wage and a letter of character, and you can be respectable or drown in respectability, your choice."

Tam blinked. "That's it? No — I don't know — oath? Beating? Throwing me in the hold?"

"Do I look like I have a hold?"

"You look like you *are* a hold."

For a second — half a second — he thought she might smile. It got as far as one corner of her mouth and then thought better of it, the way she seemed to think better of most soft things. But the corner stayed. He counted it as a win.

"One condition," Tam said.

"You're negotiating."

"Everyone negotiates with their captor. It's traditional." He stood up, brushing off his knees, and pointed north along the darkening coast, where the land flattened into the great white glare of the salt pans. "You teach me the tracking. The real kind — the footprints and broken-twigs kind, not just the water-reading. Because if we find your survey team, or whatever's left of them, I want to be useful for more than knowing which puddle isn't lying."

Wren studied him for a long moment. He resisted the urge to fidget, mostly.

"Left foot drags," she said at last. "We'll start with that. First lesson: stop dragging your left foot."

"That's not teaching, that's criticism."

"It's both. Everything worth learning starts as criticism." She rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and headed for the tiller. "Take the middle watch bell. Wake me at moonrise. And Tam—"

He paused, hand on the rigging.

"—the hooks. Ask next time. I'd have said yes."

He grinned at her back, wide and crooked. "Where's the fun in that?"

"There isn't any," she said. "That's rather the point of growing up."

* * *

The Needle, it turned out, was less a channel than an argument the sea was having with itself.

They entered it an hour before dawn, Sef at the bow sounding with a pole scarred by fifty years of exactly this work, calling depths in the flat sing-song of the old ferry guilds. *"Six and mud. Five and shell. Four — mind — four and shell."* The *Debt* slid through water the color of steeped tea, so narrow in places that the saltbush on either bank brushed the rails, and above them the sky went from ink to iron to a pale, bruised gold.

Tam lay along the bowsprit, reading the water aloud when Sef's arms tired, and it was the best morning of his life, though he'd have died before admitting it. Behind him he could hear Wren working the tiller, responding to each call without hesitation, trusting him — actually trusting him, with her boat and her neck and her mission — and the feeling of it was so large he had to make jokes to keep it from capsizing him.

"Careful on the turn," he called back. "There's a snag off the left bank shaped like a drowned mayor. Very disapproving."

"I've met mayors," Wren said. "I'll manage."

"Past the snag it opens up. Half a mile of good water, then the shallows braid — three ways, and the left one's the liar. Looks deepest. Runs into a sand trap you could bury a wagon train in."

"And the true way?"

"Right-hand channel, ugly as sin, weed-choked. But the weed grows in moving water." He tapped the rail. "Dead water grows nothing. That's the whole secret, really. Everything on these flats is trying to look alive. The channels that actually are, are the ones busy growing things."

Wren was silent a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had shifted — lower, careful, the voice she used for the survey journal's margins.

"Everything's trying to look alive," she repeated. "And the ones that are, are busy growing."

"That's what I said, yeah."

"Keep saying it. Write it down later." Something in her tone made the hair rise on his arms, though he couldn't have said why. "It might matter more than you think."

They cleared the braid by midmorning, and the Needle delivered on every one of Tam's promises: no pickets, no sails, no smoke but their own. The Rivergate rose out of the haze ahead by early afternoon — first the tips of the old sea-gates, black and enormous, then the rooftops of the drowned city breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, and the whole ruin wrapped in a silence so complete that Sef stopped humming mid-note and didn't start again.

Tam had seen the Gate from the fishing grounds, far off, all his life. Up close it was different. Up close it didn't look ruined. It looked *waiting*.

"We'll anchor in the lee of the third gate-tower," he said, and was pleased his voice only shook a little. "Water's deep enough to hide the hull, and there's a landing stair the scavvers never found. From there it's low-tide streets all the way into the middle of the ruin — that's where the journal's coordinates point, near as I can read Wren's handwriting, and no offense meant, but your handwriting looks like a spider fought a wasp on the page."

"Noted," Wren said dryly, coming forward to stand beside him at the rail. Together they looked at the drowned avenues, the water lying flat and gleaming between the walls, moving — very slightly, very wrongly — against the wind.

"Tam," she said quietly. "When you fished the Needle eleven days ago. Did you see anyone out here? Anything? Boats, lights, tracks on the landing stairs?"

He opened his mouth to say no.

Then he closed it, because memory has a way of arriving late, and what arrived was this: eleven days ago, at slack water in the Needle, he'd heard singing. Far off, thin, many voices braided together, coming from the direction of the Gate. He'd told himself it was wind in the sea-gates. He'd told himself that every night since, and every night the telling had gotten a little less convincing.

"There was singing," he said.

Wren went very still beside him.

"Whose?"

"I don't know." His mouth had gone dry. "It sounded like — like a lot of people. All in time. Like they'd been practicing a long, long while."

Ahead, in the flat water between the drowned streets, something moved — a ripple with no wind behind it, spreading outward in a slow, perfect ring, as if far below, something vast had turned over in its sleep and begun, patiently, to listen.

Wren's hand found his shoulder. Steady. Warm. Terrifyingly calm.

"Anchor here," she said. "Nobody goes near the water until we understand what's asking."

"Asking?" Tam said. "Asking *what*?"

But even as he spoke, he thought he already knew — and beneath the keel, faint and sweet and horribly patient, the singing started up again.
