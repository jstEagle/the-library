## 5. What the Water Remembers

The salt flats began where the desert gave up pretending to be land.

Wren had crossed them twice before, years ago, and both times she'd thought the same thing: *this is what the world looks like after it stops caring.* The crust stretched white and endless under a sky so pale it seemed embarrassed, cracked into hexagons the size of dinner tables, each one ringing faintly when her boots came down. Somewhere beneath them lay the bed of the dead sea, and somewhere past its far edge, the Rivergate.

Three days out from the survey camp, she was still counting tides.

Six, when they'd left. The fleet's masts had been smudges on the horizon at dawn, and Brann's ships moved with the fat confidence of something that had never once been in a hurry. Wren's crew — if you could call two people and a stolen skiff a crew — moved differently. They moved like people who knew that stopping was how you died.

"Left," Tam said from the bow of the skiff, which Wren was dragging on its runner-sled across the crust. "No — my left. Your right. The channel bends."

"I can see the channel bends."

"Then why are you going straight?"

"Because I'm testing whether you'll keep talking if I ignore you." She adjusted course anyway. The boy had been right about the flats so far, which annoyed her more than if he'd been wrong. He knew things no map showed — where the crust thinned over brine pockets, where the old ferry channels still ran beneath the salt like veins under skin. He pointed them out without being asked, then looked pleased with himself, then tried to steal dried apricots from her pack while she wasn't looking.

She was always looking.

"You know," he said, chewing, "for someone who hired me, you don't trust me much."

"I didn't hire you. You stowed away, ate half my water, and negotiated your way into employment before I could throw you off the sled."

"That's basically hiring."

"It's basically extortion."

Tam grinned. He was maybe fourteen, all elbows and salt-cracked lips, with the particular watchfulness of a child who'd grown up on the river villages' fringes — quick to laugh, quicker to count exits. He'd been running channels since he could walk, he claimed, back when the dead sea still lapped at the last fishing towns. He was the only reason they were making decent time. He was also, Wren reminded herself for the fourth time that morning, a means to an end. Nothing more. She did not keep strays. Strays slowed you down, and slowing down was —

Well. Slowing down was what had gotten the Meridian team erased, probably. She hadn't worked that part out yet.

The heat came up off the crust in shimmering sheets, and by midday even Tam had gone quiet, conserving words along with water. They walked. The skiff hissed behind them. The hexagons rang their dull bells, one after another, a sound like a thousand distant doors.

It was Tam who heard it first.

He stopped so abruptly that Wren nearly went into him. "Wait."

"What?"

"There's someone singing."

Wren listened. Wind, mostly. The creak of the sled runners. And underneath — faint, threadbare, carried flat across the salt — a voice. It wasn't singing, exactly. It was more like humming that kept trying to become words and failing, the way a dreamer talks.

She unslung her rifle without deciding to. Old habit. "Stay behind me."

"It's one person," Tam said. "Probably."

"*Probably* is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

They followed the sound for twenty minutes, Wren reading the crust as they went — and there, finally, she saw it: a dark shape ahead, low against the white, moving. Not walking. *Crawling*, in slow, patient circles, tracing a pattern into the salt with one finger. Around and around. Humming.

Wren circled wide, downwind, rifle up, every tracker instinct she owned telling her this was wrong. People lost on the flats ran for the horizon or lay down and waited. They did not draw spirals.

"Hello!" she called.

The figure stopped. Turned. It was a woman — gaunt, sun-blackened, wearing the grey field uniform of a Meridian government surveyor with the insignia burned nearly white by weeks of exposure. Her eyes found Wren with terrible precision, as though she'd been waiting for exactly her.

"Oh good," the woman said, in a voice like dry paper. "You're real. I was worried I'd started inventing people again."

* * *

Her name was Denna Okoro, and she drank four cups of water before she spoke another full sentence.

They got her into the shade of the overturned skiff while Tam rigged a canvas awning, and Wren watched her hands as she drank. Steady enough. Surveyor's hands, calloused, ink-stained at the nails. Whatever had happened to her mind, her body remembered its training. That was either reassuring or worse. Wren hadn't decided.

"The camp," Wren said, when the woman's breathing had evened out. "Meridian survey team, seven members, contracted to chart the Rivergate approaches. Tents pitched. Meals half-eaten. Instruments still running. No tracks. Any direction. Including inside the tents."

Denna set down her cup very carefully, as if it might go off.

"You've seen the camp," she said.

"I'm tracking the team. Or I was. Now I'm tracking whatever took them."

"Took." Denna laughed — a small, cracked sound with no humor in it. "That's a kind word. *Took* implies someone picked them up and carried them off. There were footprints, Ranger. I made them. Mine, going in. None coming out. Do you understand? I walked into that place and I am the only one who walked out, and I couldn't tell you which way I walked, because—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Because the current asked me my name, and I almost told it."

The silence after that was long enough for Tam to stop fidgeting.

"The current," Wren said.

"The water. Under the ruin. In the flooded streets." Denna lowered her hands. Her eyes were clear now, horribly clear, the clarity of someone who has seen the bottom of something deep. "It doesn't move like water moves. We measured it. Tide tables, flow rates, salinity gradients — proper science, three instruments cross-checking, and every reading contradicted the others. The water moves *against* the wind. Against the gradient. Sometimes it moves against gravity, gently, the way a cat leans against your leg. And when we stood close to it — when it touched our boots — it *noticed us*. I don't know how else to say it. Water isn't supposed to notice."

Tam had gone very still beside the awning post. "What do you mean, asked your name?"

"Not in words." Denna wrapped her arms around her knees. "In — offers. Feelings shaped like offers. When I put my hand in it, I felt *rest*. Real rest, the kind I haven't felt since I was a girl. I felt like I'd already finished everything I ever needed to do, and done it well, and someone was proud of me. And underneath all of it, a question. Not spoken. Just *there*, the way heat is there. *Who are you? Who are you? Who are you?*" Her voice frayed. "Marek answered it. Marek put both hands in and said his name out loud like he was introducing himself at a party, and the water took him so gently — he smiled, Ranger. He smiled the whole way down. And then the rest of them, one by one, because once you've seen what it gives, you can't unsee it. I ran. I'm not proud of how fast I ran."

Wren sat with that. Around them the salt flats ticked and rang in the heat, innocent as church bells.

"And these circles you were drawing," she said gently. "Out there."

Denna looked down at her own fingers, black with salt, as if noticing them for the first time. "Was I drawing circles?"

"Spirals."

"Oh." A pause. "I dream about the spiral. It's under the ruin. I drew it in my journal from the sonar returns before — before everything. Maybe my hands remember it better than I do." She said it lightly, but her shoulders had begun to shake, and Tam, without being asked, draped the spare blanket around her, and she clutched it like a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

* * *

The journal was waterproofed field-issue, scarred at the corners, and Denna surrendered it only after Wren promised — formally, with her ranger's oath — that she would read it and not burn it.

"Some part of me wanted to destroy it," Denna admitted, watching Wren turn the first pages. "Knowledge feels different now. Like carrying lit lanterns near a gas leak."

The early entries were ordinary and beautiful in the way field notes always are: sketches of salt formations, complaints about the coffee, a careful map of the approach channels annotated in a neat engineer's hand. Then, five days before the camp was abandoned, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. The margins filled.

Wren read aloud, slowly, so Tam could hear.

"*Day nine. Sonar mapping of the central structure complete. The Rivergate is not a ruin. Correction: it is a ruin the way a shell is a ruin. Something lived in it, and the something was built, not born. Beneath the flooded avenues there is a mechanism — a machine of a scale I refuse to write down until I've verified it, because Vey will laugh, and he will be right to laugh.*"

Tam shifted closer. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"*Day eleven. Verified. It is an engine. It sits under the city like a heart, and the channels are arteries, and the whole drowned street plan is organized around feeding it. The old civilization didn't build the machine to move water. They built it to make water — to pull it from the deep aquifer, from the sea itself, in a place where no water should be. A dead sea, and yet the Gate sits at its edge like a tap. That's why the cities fought over it. That's why everyone who ever mapped it died rich or died fast.*"

"*Day thirteen. Found the intake records. This is the part I want to burn. The engine runs on pressure differential, yes, fine, physics, but the differential requires — maintenance. Organic maintenance. The founders' inscriptions call it 'the giving.' Every generation, the city gave it people. Volunteers, at first. There are plaques. There are HONOR ROLLS. They thanked them by name, hundreds of names, carved in the flood walls, and the water in this dead sea stayed sweet for six hundred years.*"

Wren stopped reading.

Denna was looking at the horizon, not at her. "Keep going," she said quietly. "You need the last entry. Everyone needs the last entry. I've been reciting it to myself for three weeks to keep from forgetting which parts are true."

Wren turned the page. The final entry was written in a hand gone wild, letters leaning like grass in wind.

"*Day fifteen. The current is not a haunting. The current is the crew. The current IS the crew. Six hundred years of them, all the givens, still in there, still aware, still — asking. It wants names. It collects names the way the engine collects pressure. Marek is in it. I can feel him being happy and I would rather he were screaming. We are leaving tomorrow. If anyone reads this: the machine under the Rivergate is still hungry, and it has learned patience, and patience is the most terrible thing a hunger can learn.*"

The flats rang their small bells. Tam swallowed audibly.

"So the survey team," he said. "Your instruments. The tents."

"They're in the water," Denna said. "All of them. Being kept. It keeps what it loves, and it loves everything that answers." She turned to Wren at last, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Ranger, I have to ask you something, and I need you to answer honestly, because I've spent three weeks not knowing and it's worse than knowing. Is anyone coming to help them? To get them *out*?"

Wren thought about the salvage fleet on the horizon. About Commander Ilsa Brann, who looked at drowned cities and saw inventory. About six tides, now five and a half, and a government contract worth less than the paper it was printed on the moment a fleet with guns decided otherwise.

"Yes," Wren said.

It was, she reflected later, the first time in her career she'd lied to a survivor. It wouldn't be the last thing about this job that sat wrong in her chest.

* * *

They broke camp an hour before dusk, when the crust cooled enough to travel. Denna walked between them, refusing the sled seat out of some stubborn professional pride, and Tam orbited her like a nervous moon, refilling her cup before she asked, pointing out stable crust, chattering just enough to keep her anchored in the present tense.

Wren walked point and read the journal by the last light, committing the channel maps to memory. The approach routes were marked in red — *safe at low tide* — and annotated in that neat engineer's hand with warnings that grew terser as they neared the Gate: *do not touch the water. do not answer it. if it asks, walk away. IF IT ASKS, WALK AWAY.*

Underlined three times. The ink had smeared, as if the page had been wet.

"Ranger Voss." Denna's voice, from behind. Careful. Level. "When we get there. If the current asks me again—"

"I'll drag you out," Wren said. "I dragged a thief out of a flash flood in the Corvess washes two years ago. You'll be easier. He bit."

"I might bite."

"Get in line."

Behind her, she heard Tam snort, and Denna make a sound that was almost a laugh, and something in the ragged little procession loosened by a single degree. Wren kept her eyes forward. Found family, she thought sourly. What a disaster. Two strays in three days. At this rate she'd be running an orphanage by the Rivergate.

Then she crested the evening rise, and stopped thinking entirely.

Far ahead, past the last white miles of salt, the dead sea had come into view — a vast grey sheet under the fading sky, flat as hammered tin. And at its edge, tiny with distance, unmistakable even so: the broken arches of the Rivergate, rising from the shallows like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and changed its mind halfway.

Between the ruins and the fleet's last known position, nothing but open water and time.

Tam came up beside her and followed her gaze. "How many tides left?" he asked.

Wren checked the sun, the shadows, the angle of the light on the far arches. Did the arithmetic she'd been doing in her sleep.

"Five," she said. "And the fleet will see those towers tonight."

She shouldered the pack. Behind them, faint and patient, the sea waited — and somewhere inside it, she was suddenly certain, something that collected names had just felt five new footsteps on its shore, and begun, very gently, to ask.
