## 6. Brann's Offer

They came at dusk, the way trouble always does — politely first.

Wren saw the skiffs before she heard them: three dark shapes sliding across the dead sea's skin where the water lay flat as hammered tin. No running lights. No engine noise. That was worse than noise. Noise meant a captain who didn't care who knew she was coming. Silence meant one who wanted to see your face before you saw hers.

"Company," Wren said, and didn't stop packing.

Tam looked up from the cook fire he was absolutely not supposed to have lit, a half-burned flatbread in one hand. "The bad kind or the *we should pretend to be asleep* kind?"

"There's only the bad kind. The other kind is just weather." She kicked sand over his fire with two efficient strokes. "Douse that. Denna, under the tarp. If anyone asks, you're cargo."

"I'm not cargo," Denna said from her blanket by the rocks, in the flat voice she'd used since the flats. Then, after a moment: "I'm cargo," and let Tam pull her up and steer her into the shadow of the supply crates.

Wren counted their assets the way she counted everything: one skiff, patched twice; four days of water; a boy who could steal boats and had already stolen most of her patience; a madwoman who might be the only living witness to what waited at the Rivergate; and herself, which had been enough for twenty years of desert and was starting to feel like less than enough for this.

The skiffs fanned out. Not chasing. *Herding.* Wren watched the geometry of it and felt something cold settle behind her ribs, because it was good geometry. Whoever commanded those boats understood that a runner on open salt has nowhere clever to go unless they were cleverer than the net being drawn around them.

She wasn't feeling particularly clever. She was feeling the six tides ticking down in her chest like a second heartbeat.

"They're going to cut us off at the point," Tam said, crouched beside her, squinting. He'd gone still in the way he did when he was actually thinking instead of performing thinking, which was rarer than either of them admitted. "There's a spit of rock past the old ferry pilings. If they put ten men there—"

"Then we don't go to the point."

"Where do we go?"

"Nowhere. We wait."

Tam stared at her. "Wait? They've got guns. Probably. Definitely. I saw a harpoon mount on the middle boat, and I once saw a man lose an argument with a harpoon mount, and it did not go well for the man."

"You saw a lot of things on the river, apparently."

"I survived the river. Same thing." He hugged his knees. "You really think waiting is better than running?"

Wren thought about the survey camp. Tents pitched. Meals half-eaten. Instruments still humming. And no tracks anywhere, not even inside the tents, as if the people had been lifted out of their own footprints. Running was what she knew. Running was the whole architecture of her life. But she'd learned something in the last two days, standing in that impossible camp with her tracker instincts failing her for the first time since she was nine years old: sometimes the thing chasing you isn't faster than you. It's just patient, and you aren't.

"Running tells them where we want to go," she said. "Waiting makes them come to us. And if they come to us, they have to talk first. Nobody shoots a person they're about to make an offer to."

"What offer?"

"The kind I'm going to say no to."

* * *

They came ashore at full dark with lanterns hooded to slits, and the woman who stepped onto the salt first wore her authority the way other people wore armor — fitted, expensive, and clearly load-bearing.

Commander Ilsa Brann of the Korvane fleet was taller than Wren expected and moved like someone who had never once apologized for taking up space. Her coat was fleet-gray with brass at the cuffs gone green from real use, not decoration. Behind her came eight crew with level weapons and bored faces, which told Wren these were professionals, which told her Brann paid well, which told her Brann got results, which was the whole problem in a nutshell.

"Wren Voss." Brann said it like she was confirming a delivery. "Desert ranger. The government's hound. You found the survey camp."

"Found what was left of it."

"Which was everything except the people." Brann smiled without warmth, the smile of a ledger balancing. "I read the courier's report. Interesting reading. Gear intact. No tracks. My engineers think it's a hoax staged to inflate salvage claims. My gut says otherwise." She tilted her head. "Your gut too, I think. You wouldn't be running toward the Rivergate if your gut said hoax."

"We're not running toward anything. We're picnicking."

"With a stolen skiff and a traumatized surveyor?" Brann's gaze slid past Wren's shoulder, and Wren shifted to block the line of sight to the tarp where Denna hid. "Yes, we know about Okoro. Half my officers wanted to scoop her off the flats when our scouts spotted your camp. I said wait. I wanted to see what you'd do with her."

"And what did I do?"

"You kept her." Something flickered across Brann's face — not softness, but recognition, the way a gambler recognizes another player's tell. "That's the interesting part. A ranger who runs alone doesn't keep strays. Strays slow you down. So either you've gone soft, Voss, or you've decided these particular strays are worth more moving than abandoned." She spread her hands. "Either way, I can work with it."

"Can you."

"I can. Here's the shape of it." Brann began to walk, and Wren fell into step beside her because refusing to walk would have been a statement, and statements cost leverage. "The government contract is void. Meridian collapsed its survey charter when the team went silent — standard clause, thirty days, done. The Rivergate is unclaimed wreck. Under maritime law, and under the older law, the law of *whoever's standing on it*, it belongs to whoever takes it. I intend to take it. The water-technology under that ruin is worth more than every city on this coast combined. Fresh water, Voss. Made from nothing. Or made from *something*, we'll find out what. Either way, it's the difference between the Korvane surviving the next decade and becoming a ghost town with a flag."

"And me?"

"You're the best tracker working these wastes. You found a camp that eight of my scouts sailed past twice. I want your eyes. You find me safe routes into the ruin, you help me map what's down there, and you get ten percent of salvage value plus first claim on any government bounty reissued." Brann stopped and turned, and in the lantern light her eyes were steady and utterly without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Ten percent is generational money, ranger. It's a house. It's never tracking another petty thief across the flats again. It's *stopping*."

The word landed harder than Brann could have known. Stopping. Wren felt it hit the tired place inside her, the place that had been tired so long she'd stopped noticing, the place that whispered at three in the morning that a person could run forever and still die tired.

And that, precisely, was how she knew the answer.

"No," Wren said.

Brann didn't blink. "You haven't heard the terms fully. There's a hazard clause—"

"It's not the terms. It's the arithmetic." Wren nodded back toward her camp, toward the tarp and the boy and the broken woman under it. "You said you wanted to see what I'd do with Denna Okoro. Here's what I noticed about you, Commander. Your scouts found her and you left her. Not because you're cruel — I don't think you are. Because a half-mad survivor is dead weight until she becomes useful, and you don't carry dead weight. You'd have taken her if she could point at a map. Since she couldn't, you left her to the sun." Wren held Brann's gaze. "One day I'll stop being useful to you. Everyone does. Age, injury, a bad season, a better tracker. And when that day comes, you'll leave me on the flats exactly like you left her. Ten percent doesn't buy back the person you discard. I've buried enough people to know the difference between a partner and a tool, and tools get replaced."

For a long moment the only sound was the dead sea lapping at the salt, which it did without conviction, more habit than tide.

"You know what I admire about that speech?" Brann said finally. "It's completely correct. Every word. I *would* leave you. When you stopped being useful, I would leave you, and I'd sleep fine." She said it plainly, almost kindly, and Wren realized with a small cold shock that this honesty was the weapon. Most liars offered you a story. Brann offered you the truth and dared you to take the deal anyway, because the truth was that everyone else would do the same, and most people preferred a comfortable lie over an honest knife. "But here's what you haven't considered, ranger. Your boy steals. Your surveyor is mad. You're outnumbered eight to one on open ground with my guns covering your skiff. Refusing me isn't a moral stand tonight. It's a suicide note."

"Could be both," Wren said. "I'm versatile."

Behind her, she heard Tam make a small sound that was either terror or admiration. With Tam it was usually both.

Brann studied her for a long five-count. Then she did something Wren hadn't planned for: she laughed, short and genuine, and some of the iron went out of her stance. "Gods. You're exactly what the reports said. All right. One night's grace. Sleep on it. My boats hold position till dawn — call it professional courtesy, call it a trap, call it whatever helps you feel brave. But Voss—" and here the kindness drained away and left the arithmetic showing, bare and clean, "—the Rivergate doesn't care whose flag flies over it. Whatever took that survey team will take my crews too, and I'll feed them to it anyway, because that's what command *is*. The only question is whether you're standing beside me when I learn how it works, or whether you're part of what it teaches me." She turned back toward her skiffs. "Dawn, ranger."

* * *

They didn't sleep.

They sat around the re-lit cook fire — small, low, mostly for the light on Tam's face as he drew in the sand with a stick — and Wren laid out the truth: eight guns, three boats, a shoreline net, and dawn.

"So we fight," Tam said.

"We lose."

"So we run."

"They're faster and they know it. They'll run us down the coast and pick us apart, or herd us right back to Brann's flagship." Wren rubbed her eyes. "We need water she can't follow us into."

"There isn't any. I grew up on this coast, ranger. I know every channel from the Saltmark to the Gate, and they're all either dry or deep enough for her hulls." Tam poked at the sand drawing, then stopped. Poked again. His whole body changed, the way it did when a scheme arrived fully formed, which was roughly every third hour. "Except."

"Except."

"The Sighs." He looked up, grinning, and it was a terrible grin, the grin of a boy about to suggest something that would absolutely kill them all. "Old ferry channels under the pilings. They flood at high slack and drain at ebb, and the whole middle stretch is barely a hand's width under the keel at the best of times. Fleet boats can't take it — too much draft, too many barnacles on their bottoms, and believe me, I've heard fleet sailors complain about their bottoms extensively. But a patched-up skiff with a shallow belly and a pilot who learned to read water before he learned to read words?" He tapped his own chest. "That's me. I'm the pilot. And the bottom-haver."

"You want to sail through a channel named after drowning noises."

"The Sighs are named for the wind through the pilings, actually. The drowning noises are unnamed. Nobody who made them lived to name anything." He said it cheerfully, and then caught Wren's expression and added, "Mostly cheerful. Ninety percent cheerful. There's a bend near the third span where Ma Jorra lost a barge, but barges are clumsy. We're nimble. Nimble beats clumsy. That's just science."

From under the tarp, Denna's voice drifted out, thin and clear: "The water remembers the channels. The channels remember the boats. Ask nicely."

Everyone went quiet.

"She does that," Tam said. "It's very helpful and extremely creepy. I've decided to treat it as navigation advice."

Wren looked at the fire, at the boy, at the shape of the woman under the tarp, and felt the strange unfamiliar weight of it — not the weight of burden, which she knew intimately, but the weight of *company*. Brann had seen it in an hour. A ranger who keeps strays. Wren had spent twenty years making sure she weighed nothing, owed nothing, stopped for nothing, because stopping was dying and everyone who ever stopped got erased — by the desert, by the current, by whatever waited under the Rivergate wearing her brother's absence like a coat.

And now here she was, weighing plenty, owing two people their lives and maybe owing a third her sanity, about to bet all of it on a channel named for sighs.

"Dawn," Wren said. "We move at first gray, before their watch changes. Tam, you take us into the Sighs. I'll handle the tiller, you handle the shouting."

"That's my natural role."

"It is. Denna—" Wren paused. The woman had crawled halfway out from under the tarp and was looking at her with a focus she hadn't shown since the flats, sudden and terrible, like a lamp relit in an empty house. "Denna. Can you bail?"

"I can bail," Denna said. "I bailed for six years on the *Meridian Rose*. I can bail. I can tie knots. I know the survey journal by heart, every page, every coordinate." Her hands were shaking, but her voice, for one sentence, was steady as bedrock. "And I know what's under the Gate asked me my name. Don't answer it, ranger. Whatever it offers. Don't answer."

"Noted," Wren said, and meant it, and filed it next to the other dread.

* * *

First gray came up the color of old pewter, and the fleet boats sat on the horizon like patient sharks, exactly where Brann had promised. Wren gave them one long look — somewhere on that middle deck, a commander drinking coffee and believing she owned the morning — and turned her back on all of it.

The skiff slipped off the salt and onto the black water, riding so low Tam kept one hand trailing to feel the current. The old ferry pilings rose out of the mist ahead, rows of them, centuries of them, draped in weed and barnacle and the ghosts of ropes, and between them the water sighed — a long, hollow exhale through stone, over and over, like the coast itself breathing in its sleep.

"Third span," Tam murmured, all the performance gone out of him now, pure pilot, eyes reading the surface the way Wren read sand. "See the ripple line? That's the bar. We go left of it, hard left, and when I say lean, everybody leans."

"When do you say lean?"

"Usually right before it matters."

The pilings swallowed them. The world narrowed to black water, green stone, and the sighing, and behind them — faint, already receding — Wren heard the fleet's horns sound the dawn signal, and Brann's voice would be behind that signal, cool and unhurried, revising her arithmetic.

Let her revise. Wren had drawn her line in the salt last night and she'd die on the correct side of it, or live on it, which was newer and stranger and, she was beginning to suspect, the actual point.

The skiff threaded the third span. Ahead, through the mist, the drowned avenues of the Rivergate waited — and beyond them, faint as a pulse under the floor of the world, something in the water was keeping time.

"Left," Tam breathed. "Hard left. Everybody lean."
