## 7. The Cartographer's Daughter

Sef had been ferrying people across the Shallows for forty-one years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things worth teaching: the water always tells you where it's been, the wind lies before noon, and never — *never* — let a customer's dog on the boat first.

He was mending his third-best net on the deck of the *Patient Gull* when he saw them coming across the salt crust: a tall woman walking like the ground owed her money, a boy half her size carrying more than half her weight, and a third figure stumbling between them like a puppet whose strings had been cut and re-tied by someone in a hurry.

"Visitors," said his granddaughter Pell, who was eleven and considered herself the village's official lookout, mostly because nobody had told her she wasn't.

"I have eyes, girl."

"You weren't using them."

Sef grunted, which in the grammar of the drifting villages meant *fair point*, and shaded his eyes. The village of Lowmoor — if you could call forty lashed-together hulls a village — drifted where the old sea had died and left its bones behind, following the last thin channels of brine that still moved with the moon. They saw strangers perhaps twice a season, and the strangers were usually smugglers, which meant Sef could charge them double and feel righteous about it.

But this woman didn't walk like a smuggler. She walked like a ranger. He'd ferried rangers before, back when the government still bothered to send them out past the last well. They all walked like that — like stopping was a debt they'd have to pay interest on.

"Well," he said, standing up with the crackle of a man whose knees had opinions. "Pell. Fetch the good tea. Not the tea we drink. The tea we *show* people."

"We don't have any other tea."

"Then fetch the tea and don't tell them that."

* * *

The tall one introduced herself as Wren Voss, and Sef felt the name land somewhere under his ribs like a dropped anchor.

"Voss," he repeated.

"That's what I said." Her eyes were already moving over his deck, his rigging, the channel markers bobbing beyond — cataloguing, always cataloguing. Rangers never looked *at* anything; they looked *through* it to whatever might kill them next.

"And the boy?"

"Tam. He's with me."

"I'm with her," Tam confirmed, "in a strictly professional capacity."

"He stowed away on my supply wagon two hundred miles back."

"I improved the wagon," Tam said. "There was a loose axle. Also I ate some of the supplies, but only the ones that would've gone bad."

"The ones that would've gone bad," Wren said, "were going bad because *you* hid them under a tarp where the sun couldn't get at them."

Sef liked the boy immediately, in the way old men like stray dogs and weather that ruins other people's crops. He turned back to the ranger. "And your third? She looks like she's been swimming in her own head."

"Denna Okoro. Surveyor. We found her on the flats." Something flickered across Wren's face — quick, quickly buried. "She's not well."

Denna sat where they'd settled her, wrapped in a borrowed blanket, staring at the water with an expression Sef recognized from funerals. Every few minutes her lips moved. He caught the shape of the words once: *asked my name.* He decided not to ask. There were questions you asked strangers and questions you fed quietly until they went away.

"Well," Sef said briskly, because briskness was the tool he trusted most. "You'll want the crossing to the Rivergate side. Everyone who comes here wants the crossing. I'll tell you now what I tell everyone: I don't go past the Second Marker. Not for money, not for apologies, not for the government, which stopped paying anyway."

"We're not asking you to cross," Wren said. "We need information. Channels. What the water does out there."

"The water out there," Sef said, "does what it likes, and it has had a very long time to develop preferences."

Tam leaned forward. "Hidden channels. Deep ones. Ones the salvage fleets wouldn't chart."

Sef looked at him properly then. River-orphan. You could always tell — they had the look of children raised by current charts instead of parents. "Who taught you to read water, boy?"

"Nobody. That's how you know it's real learning."

* * *

It was later, much later — after the good tea that was the only tea, after Pell had extracted three stories and a promise of a story about sand-wyrms that Sef would have to invent by morning — that it happened.

Wren stood at the rail looking east, toward the drowned ruin on the horizon, and Sef came to stand beside her because old ferryman instinct told him that was where the conversation lived.

"You knew my brother," she said. It wasn't a question, which surprised him, because he hadn't said a word.

"What makes you say that?"

"When I gave you my name, you flinched. Small flinch. Left hand tightened on the net-mending needle. You've done it twice since when you thought I wasn't watching." She finally looked at him. "People flinch at my name for two reasons. Either they owe a Voss money, or they knew Joss."

Sef let out a long breath, the kind he usually saved for tying off heavy loads. "Your brother," he said, "was the only man I ever met who paid for a crossing with a map."

"A map?"

"Drew it himself. Right there on my deck. Ink and everything — proper ink, none of that berry-slap the orphans use. A chart of the channels around the Rivergate that was better than anything the government ever printed. Better than mine, and I've been reading these waters since before your mother was a rumor." He shook his head at the memory. "I told him it was worth ten times the fare. He said maps want to be used, not hoarded. Said his mother taught him that."

Something moved through Wren's face — Sef watched it happen and understood, suddenly, why rangers walked the way they walked. It wasn't impatience. It was the effort of holding still while something inside them wanted very badly to run.

"Our mother drew the survey maps for the eastern basins," Wren said. Her voice was level, which is its own kind of loudness. "Joss learned the pen. I learned the boots."

"There was a family resemblance," Sef offered gently. "You both had the look of people who'd rather be useful than comfortable."

"How long ago?"

"Nine years. Maybe ten. Time drifts out here same as we do." Sef squinted at the horizon, gathering the memory like netting. "He came asking about the Rivergate. Same as you. Wanted to know about the deep channels, the ones that flood at high tide. I told him what I tell everyone: nothing good lives in a ruin that drinks. He laughed. Said he wasn't going there for the water."

"What was he going for?"

"He didn't say. But he'd a way of talking about it—" Sef searched. "You know how some folk talk about a person they love who's gone missing? That tone. Like the place was a *who*. He kept calling it 'the Gate' like it had ears." He paused. "He went out on the morning tide. I watched him row until the haze took him. Never came back. Never sent word. And Voss — I've ferried a hundred fools out to that ruin, and every one of them either came back or had the decency to die where someone could bury them. Your brother did neither. The Gate just... closed behind him. Like water over a stone."

Wren said nothing for a long time. The village creaked around them, hull against hull, the sound Sef had slept to his whole life. Out on the flats, the light was doing that thing it did at dusk — turning the dead sea into something that looked almost alive, almost forgiving.

"He was twenty-two," Wren said finally. "I told him not to go. I told him the Rivergate was a hole in the government's maps for a reason." Her jaw worked. "Then I took a contract up north and told myself he'd be fine, because Joss was always fine, because Joss could talk his way into and out of anything, because—"

She stopped. Sef had the grace to pretend to be very interested in his rigging.

"Because stopping costs more than chasing," he said. "That's the ranger creed, isn't it? My father used to say the government breeds them that way. Dogs that can't lie down."

"It's not a creed. It's just true."

"Mm." Sef looked at her sidelong. "And how's that served you, girl? All these years of never stopping. Did you find him? Did you even once turn toward the Rivergate instead of away?"

The silence stretched long enough that Pell, spying from the cabin, began composing the story she'd tell tomorrow.

"No," Wren said at last. "I filed a report. The report came back stamped. I told myself the stamp meant something." She laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a person discovering a bill they'd been avoiding for nine years. "I've crossed half the desert since. I've tracked everything that leaves a track. And I never once came here."

"Why now?"

"Because the government's paying me now." She said it fast, too fast, and they both heard the seam in it. Then, quieter: "Because somebody finally made me look."

Sef nodded slowly. In his experience, that was how most pilgrimages started — not with revelation but with paperwork.

* * *

He found the boy at the stern that night, legs dangling over the water, teaching himself constellations off a stolen scrap of chart.

"She's going in there whether anyone helps her or not," Tam said, without turning around. "I've watched her for two hundred miles. She walks like the decision's already made and her body's just catching up."

"That's the Voss walk," Sef agreed, settling down beside him with a groan he didn't bother disguising. "Her brother had it too. Slower, though. Joss Voss walked like a man reading a room. This one walks like a man burning it down for warmth."

"Is that bad?"

"For her? Probably. For everyone else?" Sef shrugged. "Depends what needs burning."

Below them, Denna's voice rose once from the cabin — thin, repeating — and fell silent again. Tam glanced toward it. "The surveyor keeps saying the water asked her name. What does that mean, out here? Do the waters do that?"

Sef was quiet a moment. Forty-one years of ferrying buys a man a lot of stories, and most of them he tells for wine. But some he keeps, the way you keep a knife you hope never to use.

"There's an old ferryman's rule," he said. "Third thing I ever learned, after the wind and the dogs. The dead sea doesn't drown people, boy. It *keeps* them. Folk who go into the deep water past the Second Marker sometimes come back wrong — or come back not at all — and the ones who come back wrong all say versions of the same thing." He looked at the dark water below, black and patient as held breath. "That something in it wanted to know who they were. Like it was taking attendance."

Tam pulled his legs up onto the deck. It was, Sef noted with approval, the first sensible thing the boy had done all day.

"And her brother," Tam said quietly. "Joss. You think he's—"

"I think," Sef said, "that tomorrow I'm going to break my own rule and take you past the Second Marker, because that woman will swim it otherwise, and I'd rather be in a boat when the sea decides things than on shore wondering." He stood, knees complaining. "And I think you should sleep, because the tide turns early, and the Gate doesn't wait for anybody. It never did. Not even for a cartographer's son."

* * *

They left on the grey tide, before the sun had finished deciding to rise. Sef at the sweep, Wren at the bow with her eyes fixed east, Tam bailing enthusiastically and unnecessarily, Denna wrapped in blankets in the stern, whispering to the water like it was listening.

Which, Sef thought, easing the *Patient Gull* past the Second Marker for the first time in thirty years, was the whole trouble with this stretch of the world.

Everything out here listened.

Halfway across, Wren spoke without turning around. "Sef. When Joss drew you that map — did he mark anything on it? Anything strange?"

"As a matter of fact." Sef adjusted the sweep, remembering. "Little signs in the margin. Ranger-sign, I figured. Family marks. He said if anything happened to him, whoever came looking should follow the marks." He paused. "Why? You recognize them?"

For the first time since he'd known her — all twelve hours of it — Wren Voss smiled. It was not entirely a comforting sight.

"They're ours," she said. "He's leaving me a trail."

Ahead, through the morning haze, the drowned towers of the Rivergate rose out of the dead sea like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and changed its mind.
