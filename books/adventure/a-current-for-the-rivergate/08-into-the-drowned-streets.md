## 8. Into the Drowned Streets

Tam had stolen his first boat when he was seven, and he'd been stealing things ever since — mostly boats, occasionally bread, once a whole chicken that turned out to be somebody's pet. So he knew what it meant to move through a place quietly, reading the water the way other people read faces.

But he had never seen water read *back*.

They came down into the Rivergate at low tide, through a channel Tam knew from the smuggler days before the smugglers all stopped coming back. The channel was called the Needle's Eye, and it ran between two walls of salt-crusted stone so close together you could touch both sides of your boat at once. Above them, the sky was the color of old tin. Below them, the water was green-black and utterly still.

"Cheerful place," said Sef, from the stern of the lead boat. The old ferryman had one hand on the tiller and the other on his lucky charm, which was a dried fish head on a string, and which Tam personally thought was bad luck wearing a disguise.

"It gets better," Tam said.

"Does it?"

"No."

Wren Voss stood in the bow of their boat like she'd been carved there, her ranger's coat buttoned to the throat despite the heat, the survey journal open against her forearm. She hadn't slept much since they'd found Denna Okoro out on the flats. None of them had, really, but Wren wore it differently — she wore exhaustion the way other people wore a knife, close and quiet and ready.

"Tam," she said, without turning around. "How deep?"

He let the silence stretch just long enough to be annoying. It was one of his few pleasures in life these days.

"Twelve feet over the rooftops at high tide," he said. "Four feet over the streets right now. We've got maybe three hours before it turns."

"And after that?"

"After that we're swimming with whatever took the survey team." He grinned at her back. "So let's try not to get lost, Ranger."

She glanced over her shoulder at him. There was a ghost of something around her mouth that might, in better light, among kinder people, have been called a smile. "You're lost half the time yourself."

"I'm never lost. I'm *exploring*." He tapped his chest. "River-orphan rule one: if you don't know where you are, act like you meant to be there."

Behind him, Denna Okoro made a small sound. It wasn't quite a laugh. Nothing Denna did anymore was quite anything — she sat wrapped in a blanket in the second boat, holding the journal's twin copy against her chest like a child holds a doll, and every so often her lips moved around words nobody else could hear. Words about names. About being asked.

Sef caught Tam's eye and shook his head slightly. *Leave her be.* Tam hadn't been planning to do anything else. Mostly.

The Needle's Eye opened, and the Rivergate spread out below them.

* * *

Tam had grown up on stories of the drowned city. Every river-orphan had. The Rivergate was where the desert met the dead sea, where an empire older than any map had built its gates and canals and then, one day, stopped building and started drowning. The stories said the streets were paved with glass. The stories said there were fish with human teeth. The stories said the city sang at night.

The stories were wrong about the glass — it was tile, thousands upon thousands of tiles, blue and white and gold, still bright under four feet of clear saltwater — and wrong about the singing, or at least Tam hoped they were, because what he heard instead was worse.

Nothing. The city made no sound at all.

Their three boats slid down a flooded avenue wide enough for six carts abreast. On either side, buildings rose straight out of the water, their lower floors dark windows, their upper stories bleached bone-white by centuries of sun. Awnings hung in rotted ribbons. A stone balcony jutted overhead with a rusted lantern still hooked to its rail, as if someone had stepped inside for a moment and simply never come back out.

"Look at that," Sef murmured. He pointed with the fish head, which Tam felt undermined the gravity of the gesture. "That's ferry-work. That bracket. My grandfather's guild made brackets like that. We always said our work went back to the Gate days." His voice had gone strange and soft. "I thought it was a story we told ourselves."

"Everything's a story until you're standing in it," Wren said.

"That's supposed to be comforting?"

"No. It's supposed to be true."

Tam let them talk and kept his eyes on the water. This was the part the stories got right, or almost right. You could see straight down through the flood — the tiles, the doorways, the shapes of market stalls frozen mid-collapse, a cart with its wheels still on, everything preserved like flies in amber. And moving through it all, thin as smoke, were threads of current. Pale lines in the green, running against nothing, going somewhere with purpose.

Except the wind, such as it was, blew from the sea toward the land. And the threads were flowing the other way.

Inland. Uphill, more or less, given how flat everything was. Against the wind, against sense, against every law of water Tam had learned in twelve years of living on it.

"Hey," he said. "Ranger. Look at the current."

Wren looked. Tam watched her go very still, the way she did — not frightened-still, but calculating-still, like a hawk deciding whether the thing below it was worth the dive.

"Against the wind," she said.

"Water doesn't do that."

"This water does."

"That's my point!"

Denna lifted her head. For a moment her eyes were clear — clearer than they'd been since the flats — and she looked down through the hull at the pale threads sliding beneath them, and she said, in a voice like someone reciting a lesson she hated:

"It's not moving away. It's moving *home*. Everything here moves home." Then her eyes clouded again, and she pulled the blanket tighter, and she started whispering to the journal copy.

Nobody said anything for a while after that.

* * *

They moored the boats at what had once been a customs house — Tam knew the shape of it, the long low counter-stone just under the surface where harbor clerks had once taken their cut of everything that floated past — and waded up the Grand Market Street with the water at their knees.

This was the part Tam had promised himself would be fun.

It was not fun.

It should have been. The market was *incredible*. Stalls lined both sides of the street under arches of white stone, and though the goods had long since dissolved, the stalls themselves remained — counters, awning-hooks, little drawers for coins, even a scale hanging from a beam with its brass pans still balanced. Further along, a fountain basin held a foot of trapped seawater, and in it swam small silver fish that scattered like thrown coins when Tam's shadow crossed them. He couldn't help it; he laughed. An entire city, drowned and dead and silent, and the fish were still afraid of shadows. Some things were true everywhere.

"Keep moving," Wren said.

"You're the only person alive who could walk through a treasure city and say *keep moving*."

"I'm the only person alive walking through a treasure city looking for eight missing people."

"Nine," Sef corrected mildly. "There was a cook. Journals mention a cook."

"The cook counts," Wren agreed, and Tam saw her mark something in the margin of the journal. She counted everyone. He'd noticed that about her. She counted rations, tides, arrows, people — counted them twice, counted them like each number owed her money. Like if she stopped counting, things would start disappearing.

Or maybe, Tam thought, watching her check the waterline against a doorway's arch, like things already had.

The survey journal led them deeper. Denna's notes — the parts written in her own steady hand, before whatever happened out on the flats — described their route precisely: *Grand Market east to the Weighhouse, then south along the Processional to the first gate tower.* The Meridian team had been mapping the waterworks, tracing the channels that had once made this desert corner bloom. Somewhere ahead, according to the journal, was the heart of it. The machine beneath the gate.

Tam didn't love the phrase *the machine beneath the gate*. In his experience, machines were fine. It was the *beneath* that always caused trouble.

They passed a shrine set into a wall above the flood line, its niche empty except for a ring of candle stubs melted into a single lump. Someone had left flowers once — the stems had rotted to lace, but the shape of the offering was still there, pressed against the stone. Tam touched it as he passed and then wiped his hand on his trousers without thinking about why.

"Here." Wren stopped at a side street where the water ran darker. "Weighhouse is through there. Journal says the survey team logged a working pump mechanism in the sub-levels."

"Sub-levels," Sef repeated. "Of a drowned building."

"They were thorough people."

"They were *drowned* people," Tam said, "if we're being honest about the odds—"

The water moved.

Not the threads this time. All of it. The whole street, knee-deep and slack for hours, shifted in a single slow pulse, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep. Ripples ran upstream. The silver fish in the fountain basin went mad, battering the stone sides. And the temperature dropped — not gradually, not like evening coming on, but all at once, as if the water had remembered it used to be winter.

Tam's hand found Wren's sleeve before he decided to reach for it. She didn't shake him off. That was the thing about Wren Voss that Tam was still getting used to: she pretended she needed nobody, but she never actually pulled away.

"Sef," she said quietly. "The boats."

"Already thinking it." The old man was squinting back the way they'd come. "Tide's not due to turn for two hours. That wasn't the tide."

"No," Wren said. "It wasn't."

The pulse faded. The water settled. The wind, thin and dry, blew from the sea exactly as it had before — and the pale threads beneath the surface kept flowing inland, patient, unhurried, like a river that had all the time in the world because it *was* all the time in the world.

Denna had stopped whispering. She was standing perfectly upright now, blanket fallen from her shoulders, facing down the dark side street toward the Weighhouse. Toward wherever the threads were going.

"Denna." Wren's voice was gentle in a way Tam had rarely heard from her — careful, the way you're careful with a snare you're trying to spring without breaking it. "Denna, look at me."

"She's asking again," Denna said. Her voice was calm. That was the worst part, Tam thought. Not screaming, not weeping. Calm. "Do you hear it? She's asking everyone. She's been asking for years and years and no one answers, and now we're *here*, and she just wants—"

"*Denna.*"

Denna blinked. Looked at Wren. Looked at the water. Something like shame crossed her face, brief and painful, and she let Sef take her arm and draw her back from the edge of the street.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I'm sorry. I know how it sounds."

"How does it sound?" Tam asked, because nobody else would.

Denna looked at him — really looked, the clearest she'd been all day — and said, "It sounds like home. That's the problem. It sounds like home, and I haven't even lost anyone yet."

* * *

They made camp that night on the roof of the Weighhouse, because Wren refused to sleep below the waterline, and because Tam — who had argued cheerfully for the cozy submerged customs office with its intact fireplace — privately agreed with her and would have bitten anyone who suggested otherwise.

The fire was small and shielded. Sef cooked salt-fish and flatbread and complained about both, which was how he showed affection. Denna slept between them, finally, deeply, her breathing even for the first time in days. The city lay below them in the dark, black water and blacker streets, and far off — too far off to see, but not too far to feel — the salvage fleet was surely closing on the outer channels.

Tam sat on the roof's edge with his legs dangling over the drop, chewing flatbread, doing what he did best: listening.

Wren came and sat beside him. She didn't ask permission. Rangers didn't, apparently. But she handed him the last of the tea, which was basically the same thing.

"She's right, you know," Tam said.

"About what?"

"About it sounding like home." He turned the cup in his hands. "I grew up on the river channels. Orphans, we run in packs, and the packs have rules, and the biggest rule is the water provides. Water feeds you, hides you, carries you when your legs give out. You belong to the channels and the channels belong to you." He shrugged. "That thing down there? It's saying the same words. Just... bigger. Forever-er."

Wren was quiet for a while. Out on the black water, one of the pale threads glimmered faintly, running inland against the wind.

"My brother believed that," she said at last.

Tam went still. She'd mentioned Joss exactly once, back on the flats, in four words, and then closed like a door. Now here it was again, offered out over a rooftop in a dead city, and Tam understood — the way orphans understand these things — that this was trust, and it was fragile, and the correct response was to hold it very carefully and not make it a joke.

"What happened to him?" he asked softly.

"He went looking for the Rivergate. Years ago. Before the survey team, before any of it." Her jaw worked. "Everyone told him not to. I told him not to. He went anyway, because Joss never met a door he didn't want to open." She stared down at the water. "And I've spent every year since telling myself he's dead, because dead is an answer, and answers are better than not knowing."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing on top of the place he vanished." She drank her tea. "And the water down there sounds like home."

Below them, faint as breath, the current pulsed again — one slow roll through the drowned streets, ripples running the wrong way, patient as patience itself. And Tam, who had spent his whole life hearing things in the water that other people missed, heard something else underneath it this time.

Faint. Far down. Almost certainly imagination.

Almost.

It sounded, very distantly, like someone calling a name.

He didn't say whose name. But he saw Wren's shoulders stiffen, and he knew she'd heard it too — and that she recognized it, the way you recognize your own name called across a crowded market, even in a voice you haven't heard in years.

"Wren," he said carefully. "What did your brother sound like?"

She didn't answer. She was already on her feet, staring down into the black water, where the pale threads had begun, very slowly, to gather — all of them, every thread in sight, converging on the dark mouth of the Processional, flowing toward the first gate tower.

Flowing, Tam realized, in the shape of a path.

An invitation.

"Tide turns in ninety minutes," Wren said. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't. "Get Sef. Wake Denna gently. We're going down."

"Down where?"

"To find out," she said, stepping toward the stairwell leading into the drowned dark, "who's calling me home."
