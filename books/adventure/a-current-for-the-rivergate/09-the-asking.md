## 9. The Asking

They made camp on a shelf of old stone above the waterline, where the drowned streets of the Rivergate showed themselves at low tide like the bones of some enormous animal — arches here, a stairway there, the suggestion of a market square with its stalls long since rotted to lace. The dead sea lapped against it all with a patience that Wren found deeply offensive.

Water wasn't supposed to be patient. Water was supposed to be honest. It ran downhill, it filled the low places, it moved on. This water sat in its basin and *waited*, and everything about it set her teeth on edge.

"Camp's good," Tam announced, dropping an armful of driftwood he'd scavenged from the tideline. He was fourteen, maybe fifteen, and had the unshakable confidence of someone who had never once considered that the world might not cooperate with him. "Wind's off the flats, so we won't smell it. Mostly."

"I'll take mostly," Sef said. The old ferryman was easing himself down onto a flat rock with the care of a man whose knees had opinions and expressed them freely. "I've slept in worse. I've *sailed* in worse. There was a year out of the fishing villages when the whole catch was eels, and I tell you, boy, there is no smell on this earth like a hold full of dying eels."

"You've told us," Tam said.

"Twice," Denna added from the far edge of camp.

Sef beamed as though this were praise. "Good stories bear repeating. Bad ones you only need once."

Wren let them talk. She'd learned long ago that a camp that talked was a camp that wasn't watching the dark and imagining things in it, and right now she needed very badly for nobody to imagine anything. She checked the perimeter instead — habit, ritual, the small liturgy of a woman who had survived twenty years in the desert by never assuming the ground beneath her was finished trying to kill her.

The salt flats behind them were empty. Of course they were empty. They'd crossed two days of nothing but cracked white pan and the occasional fossil of something that had died embarrassed, and the only tracks were their own, stitched behind them like a seam.

Ahead of them, the water lay flat as poured glass.

That was the thing she kept coming back to. Flat. No wind for three days, and the open sea beyond the Rivergate should have been moving — swells, chop, anything. But inside the ring of drowned streets, the surface didn't ripple. It reflected the stars so perfectly that looking down felt like looking up, and twice now Wren had caught herself leaning over the edge of a submerged wall with the sudden irrational conviction that if she just kept going —

She'd stopped herself both times. She hadn't told anyone about either time.

"Wren." Tam's voice, careful. "Denna's up."

Denna Okoro had been up before, of course. She was up most nights, sitting with her knees pulled to her chest, saying fragments of names under her breath — her survey team, all six of them, cycling through like a rosary. *Oyelaran. Vashti. Merrick. Petit. The twins.* She said them the way other people prayed, and Wren had decided early on that the kindest thing she could do was pretend not to hear.

But tonight Denna wasn't sitting. Tonight she was standing, and she was facing the water, and she was walking toward it with the slow, deliberate gait of someone following instructions.

"Denna." Wren moved, fast and quiet, the way she did everything. "Denna, hey. Surveyor Okoro. Look at me."

Denna didn't look at her. Her eyes were open — that was the worst part, Wren thought later. Not sleepwalking. Wide awake, or something wearing wakefulness, fixed on the middle distance where the black water met the black sky and there was nothing at all to see.

"It's asking again," Denna said. Her voice was calm. Terribly calm. "It wants to know if I've remembered my name yet."

"Your name is Denna Okoro. Meridian survey team, third expedition. You're standing on a rock in your boots and it's cold, and in the morning Sef is going to make that terrible fish tea he makes, and you're going to complain about it, because complaining is the only thing keeping you upright. Come sit down."

"It knows my name now." Denna's bare feet touched the wet stone at the shelf's edge. When had she taken her boots off? Wren hadn't seen it happen. Nobody had seen it happen. "It's been holding it for me. All this time. It's been so heavy, Wren. Carrying yourself is so heavy."

Wren grabbed her by the collar and hauled her back.

Denna came without resistance, which somehow frightened Wren more than a fight would have. She weighed almost nothing. She turned in Wren's grip and looked at her with those clear, quiet eyes, and said, "You heard it too. Don't lie to me, ranger. You've been hearing it since the flats."

* * *

They sat her between Sef and the fire, wrapped in every blanket they owned, and Tam took first watch with a boat-hook across his knees like it was a spear and he was a warrior of legend rather than a boy who'd stolen his first skiff at age nine because, in his words, "it was there, and I was bored."

Wren walked the shoreline alone.

She told herself it was patrol. She was very good at telling herself things. It was practically a profession.

The water waited for her. That was how it felt — not that she came to it, but that it had arranged itself along the shore in advance, knowing exactly where she'd walk, the way a host sets out a chair for an expected guest.

And then it spoke.

Not words. She wanted to be precise about this, even alone, even with no one to insist she was mad. It was not words. It was more intimate than words, the way a smell can be more intimate than a letter. It arrived whole, already understood, already *agreed with*, and that was the trap of it — you never got to argue, because by the time you noticed it was talking, you'd already nodded.

What it offered her was rest.

Not sleep. Rest. The specific, aching rest she had not felt in nineteen years, since the night she'd left the ranger station at Cindermark with her brother's badge in her pocket and a promise in her mouth and the whole enormous desert ahead of her, and realized somewhere around the second dune that she would never stop moving again, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the empty bunk, and the empty bunk meant —

The water held that feeling out to her like a cupped hand holding water in the desert. *Here. You can put it down. All of it. The tracking, the contracts, the counting of days. You could stop.*

"I don't stop," Wren said aloud. Her voice sounded small and stupid against all that silence. "Stopping costs more than chasing. Everybody learns that or dies."

The current didn't argue. It simply changed what it was offering, smooth as tide changing direction.

It gave her Joss's voice.

Not a memory. She knew his memories — she had all of them, worn smooth from handling, his laugh, the way he whistled through his teeth when he read, the terrible joke about the surveyor and the sandworm that he'd told forty times and laughed hardest at himself every time. This wasn't that. This was *new*. This was Joss's voice saying something she'd never heard him say:

*"Wrennie. You found me. I knew you would. You always find things. It's the one thing you do better than running."*

Her legs nearly went out from under her.

She caught herself on a drowned wall, barnacles biting her palm, and stood there shaking while the water laid the voice out again, gentle, patient, *hers*: *"I'm tired, Wrennie. It's soft in here. It doesn't hurt. You could come find out. Or you could keep going the way you always do — forward, forward, forward — and I'll still be here. I'm not going anywhere. I'm everywhere. Isn't that funny? Little Joss Voss, everywhere at last."*

"Stop it." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Stop — he wouldn't say that. He'd say something dumb. He'd make a joke. He'd — "

*"He does make jokes,"* the current said, warm as bathwater, *"you should hear him. We all hear him. Everyone who comes in hears everyone else. That's the gift, Wren. Nobody is ever alone again. Nobody is ever lost. Your brother has been asking for you for six years. He asked so politely."*

Wren pressed her forehead against the cold stone and breathed.

This was the moment, she understood dimly, distantly, the way you understand a riptide from the beach — this was the moment people didn't come back from. Not because the water dragged them. Because they *leaned*. Because after enough years of carrying yourself, somebody finally offers to carry you, and it doesn't matter that the somebody is a drowned engine wearing your dead brother's voice. You lean anyway.

She thought about Tam back at the fire, probably narrating his watch to Sef whether Sef was awake or not. She thought about Denna's boots sitting empty by the flames. She thought about the fact that she had hired none of these people, paid none of them, promised none of them anything, and yet here they were, her ragged little constellation of strays, and the thought of the water taking any one of them made something in her chest go hard and hot and absolutely immovable.

"No," she said.

The current waited. It was very good at waiting.

"I said no." She pushed off the wall and stood straight, boots planted on the wet stone, and pointed at the flat black mirror of the sea like it was a thief she'd cornered in an alley. "You want to make offers? Here's mine. Leave my crew alone. Leave Denna alone — she's grieving, not asking. And if my brother is in there, you tell him his sister says he's a coward for hiding behind her voice, and that she's coming in after him on *her* terms, on *dry land*, and he'd better leave a light on."

The water rippled.

It was the first movement she'd seen in it, ever — a single ring spreading from nowhere, out toward the deep, like laughter, or like a message being passed along.

Then it went flat again, and the voice was gone, and Wren discovered she was crying, which annoyed her enormously.

* * *

She sat by the fire until her face stopped being damp, and Sef, bless him, handed her a cup of the terrible fish tea without a word and without looking at her, which was the single most tactful act she had ever witnessed from another human being.

"She tried again," Tam said quietly, nodding toward Denna, asleep at last between the blankets. "Hour ago. Got one foot in before I grabbed her. She's stronger than she looks, but she keeps saying the same thing."

"What thing?"

"That it's not tricking her." Tam poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "Everyone thinks the monster lies, right? That's the story. But she says it isn't lying. It really would give her rest. It really does have her team. It's just — " he searched for it — "like a bargain where everything on the sign is true and you still shouldn't sign."

Wren looked at him. Fourteen years old, orphaned by the river, self-educated in boats and theft, and he'd just summed up the entire horror of the Rivergate better than any government briefing ever had.

"That's exactly it," she said. "Everything on the sign is true."

"So how do we beat it?"

"We don't sign."

Tam grinned. "See, that's why I follow you around. Terrible plan. Great delivery."

She threw a pebble at him. He caught it, obviously, and looked insufferable about it, and for about ten seconds the camp felt like a place where people lived instead of a ledge above a drowning dark.

But sleep didn't come for Wren, and near dawn she found herself walking the shore again — because she couldn't help it, because the current had shown her something real inside all its velvet, and she needed to know if the rest was real too.

The tide was at its lowest. Whole avenues stood exposed, dripping, kelp-hung, ancient. And there, on a wall that must have been a customs house once, where the morning light hit it slantwise —

Marks.

Cut into the stone, low, half-hidden under a shelf of barnacle, easy to miss unless you were looking. A line of them, weathered but deliberate, spaced like a man writing carefully.

Wren's breath stopped.

She knew those marks. Every ranger raised in the Cindermark stations knew them — the old trail-sign, the ranger-sign, the code her father had taught her and Joss both on the porch steps, drawing in the dust with a stick. *Water ahead. Shelter. Danger, turn back. Keep going.*

And beneath the warning signs, in the same sure hand, a name-sign she would have known anywhere in the world, in any light, in any life.

Joss.

He was alive. He had been alive, all this time, cutting warnings into the walls of the drowned city for anyone stubborn enough to come looking — and the last sign, freshly cut, edges still sharp, read:

*Deeper. Careful. It listens.*

Wren put her fingers on the grooves and laughed, one broken, disbelieving sound, and the flat water out beyond the avenue rippled once, softly, like something answering.
