## 10. Joss's Mark

The tide gave them four hours. Tam swore it was four and a half; Sef, who had been reading water since before Tam's parents were born, said three and a half; Denna said nothing at all, because Denna hadn't said much of anything since the asking, except once in her sleep when she'd whispered *yes* to a room full of dry air.

Wren split the difference and set the pace for four.

They went down into the drowned streets with lanterns hooded to slits of amber, wading thigh-deep through avenues that had been market roads when the Rivergate was a city instead of a rumor. The water here was strange — not sea-salt, not river-fresh, but something between, warm as bathwater and moving in long slow pulses, like something breathing under a blanket.

"Left at the arch," Tam said, consulting the survey journal they'd copied out by firelight. He'd taken to carrying it inside his coat, against his chest, "so it stays warm," he claimed, though Wren suspected it was so no one else could grab it. "Denna drew it as a fish-market. There should be — yes." He pointed his lantern at a row of stone counters rising from the flood, their surfaces furred white with salt bloom. "Stalls."

"Fish stalls," Sef said, peering at one. "In a city that drowned itself on purpose. Somebody was an optimist."

"Somebody was hungry," Tam said. "Optimism's just hunger with better posture."

Wren let them talk. Talking kept the fear from pooling in the boat of the skull, where it did its worst work. She kept her eyes on the water ahead, on the way the current pulsed — toward the heart of the ruin, always toward, like blood returning to a heart.

And beneath that pulse, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, she could feel the other thing. The thing that had spoken to her on the causeway without words. That had offered her rest in her brother's voice.

She hadn't told the crew how close she'd come to answering.

*Four hours,* she reminded herself. *Move.*

* * *

The journal's coordinates took them past the fish market into what had been a civic quarter: a customs house, a granary, a temple whose dome had collapsed into the flood so that only its crown broke the surface, black and round as a beetle's back. The water shallowed here — waist, then knee, then ankle — which meant the streets rose toward some interior height, some core of the ruin the floods had never quite swallowed.

It also meant they could walk, and walking meant tracks, and tracks meant Wren's mind started doing what it always did: reading the ground. Except there was nothing to read. The stone floors were swept clean by centuries of tide. No dust, no silt, no sign that anyone had ever passed this way — not the survey team, not the ancient Gatekeepers, nobody.

Her instincts kept reaching for information and finding bare shelves. It was like being blindfolded in her own house.

"You're doing the face," Tam said quietly, falling into step beside her.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're chewing a map that lied to you."

"I don't have a face like that."

"Ranger Voss, I have watched you make that face eleven times since the salt flats. I count things. It's a whole thing I do."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. "The floor's wrong," she admitted. "There should be *something*. Even ruins leave leavings. This place is too clean. Like it tidies up after visitors."

Sef, behind them, made a low sound in his throat — the sound he made when the water told him something he didn't like. "Then let's hope it doesn't consider us messes to be cleaned."

Nobody answered that. Nobody needed to. They all walked a little faster.

* * *

The passage the journal described opened off the customs house — a stair descending below the waterline, its steps slick but climbable, the air inside smelling of wet stone and, strangely, of green things. Of growing things. In a dead city at the bottom of a dead sea, the air smelled like a garden after rain.

"That's new," Tam said.

"That's wrong," Wren said.

"Those can be the same thing," Sef observed, "out here."

The stair let them into a long gallery, half-flooded, its ceiling lost in darkness above. And it was there — ankle-deep in warm water, lantern raised — that Wren saw the marks.

Carvings covered the walls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not the formal glyphs of the Gatekeepers' civilization — those were geometric, patient, architectural. These were different. These were cut by hand, by many hands over many years, shallow and hurried in places, deep and careful in others. Lines spiraling into lines. Waves stacked on waves. And threaded through all of it, repeated again and again down the length of the gallery:

Ranger-sign.

Wren stopped walking so fast that Tam bumped into her back.

She knew those marks the way she knew her own hands. Every ranger family kept its own sign-language for the flats — hand-talk for wind and distance and danger, cut into trail-stones and water-barrels so any ranger passing through could read the news of the road without wasting breath. The Voss signs were older than most, older than the Meridian government, older than the salvage fleets. Her father had taught them to her on her mother's knees. *This is how we say water. This is how we say turn back. This is how we say I love you, which is the same sign as come home.*

The wall said *turn back*. Dozens of times, in her family's own hand-talk, carved into stone.

"Tam," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like sound underwater. "Read me the first line. Just — read it."

He squinted at the carving, tracing the grooves with one finger. "Turn... back? No — wait. Turn back is two marks. This has a modifier." He frowned. "'Turn back *now*.' Then this one says..." His finger moved along. "'The water asks your name. Don't answer.'" He looked up at her. "Who carved all this?"

Wren wasn't listening anymore. She'd found the signature.

Every ranger signed their work. Trail-marks, warnings, grave-cairns — you signed, because a warning without a name was just noise, and noise got people killed. At the end of the first line of carvings, small and sure and unmistakable, was the mark she'd last seen burned into a wooden post outside their family's station nine years ago, before the post rotted, before the station emptied, before everything.

Joss's mark. The crooked *J* with the wave struck through it, because he'd been twelve when he invented it and had thought himself very clever.

The gallery tilted. Or she did. Sef's hand landed on her shoulder, steadying.

"Voss," he said gently. "What is it?"

"It's my brother," she said. "My brother carved this."

* * *

They followed the carvings.

There was no question of doing anything else. The gallery ran deeper into the ruin, and the carvings ran with it, decades of them, layers of them — Joss's mark appearing year after year, the strokes changing as he'd grown, the boy's uncertain cuts becoming a man's confident lines. Wren read them the way she'd read any trail, and the trail told a story.

The early carvings were questions. *Where am I. What is this place. Is anyone out there.* One, dated by the depth of its weathering to maybe eight years ago, simply said, over and over, forty times down a stretch of wall: *Mira if you find this I'm sorry I took the good boots.*

"Mira," Tam said. "That a friend?"

"Our sister," Wren said. "She still has the boots. She wears them to spite him." Her voice cracked on *spite*, and she covered it by walking faster.

The middle years were different. The questions stopped. The warnings began — detailed, systematic, a ranger's mind turning survival craft onto a new wilderness. *The current speaks at slack tide. It uses voices you know. It cannot cross running water. It lies about time.* Some of the warnings were things they'd already learned the hard way; others were things they'd been spared learning, because Joss had learned them first and left them written in stone.

"He's been guarding this place," Sef said slowly. "All these years. For whoever came next."

"For us," Tam said. "For anyone. He didn't know who'd come. He just—" Tam stopped, jaw working. "He just kept doing it. Alone. For *years*."

Wren touched the newest carving, the freshest, near the gallery's far end. The stone dust still sat in the grooves, barely washed away. Less than a season old. *Turn back now. The gate wakes. It is hungry again. I cannot hold it much longer.*

*I cannot hold it much longer.*

Hope and horror arrived together, twins, inseparable. He was alive. Nine years of grief, of the closed casket of *never found*, of the guilt she carried like a stone in her boot — and he was alive, alive and *here*, alive and thinking, remembering Mira's boots, remembering his family's signs, remembering enough of who he'd been to spend nearly a decade carving warnings into the dark for strangers.

And he was part of the thing that ate people. Part of the current that had asked Denna's name, that had worn Wren's own memories like a mask, that had taken a government survey team of eleven people and folded them into its warm pulsing hunger. Alive the way a finger is alive while it's still attached to the hand.

"Wren." Tam's voice, very quiet. "If he's been down here nine years—"

"Then he knows the way to the machine," Wren said. "And he knows how it thinks. And he might be the only person alive who can tell us how to fight it." She turned to face them, and was distantly surprised to find her eyes were wet. "And he's my brother, and I'm getting him back, and if any of you have an argument, save it, because I'm not going to hear it anyway."

"No argument," Sef said.

"One argument," Tam said. "You're not going alone."

"I wasn't going to suggest—"

"You were absolutely going to suggest it. You have a face for it. It's the map-chewing face."

Despite everything — despite the dark and the drowning city and the breathing water — Wren laughed. It came out ragged, but it was real, and it felt like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying. "Fine. Together. But stay behind my lantern, both of you, and if the water starts talking in voices you know, you *don't answer*."

"Noted," said Sef.

"Cross my heart," said Tam. Then, softer: "He waited nine years for somebody to come. We're not leaving him waiting another tide."

* * *

The gallery ended at a door.

Not a ruin-door — those were all gone, rotted or dissolved. This door was whole, dark wood somehow unfouled after centuries underwater, banded in bronze gone green, and across its face someone had carved one final message, large enough to read from across the flooded chamber. Large enough to be read by lantern-light from a distance. Large enough, Wren thought, to be read by someone approaching in the dark who needed to stop before they got too close.

Her brother's hand. Her family's signs. Translated, this once, into letters, so that even a non-ranger would understand:

**STOP. THE NEXT ROOM IS WHERE IT LIVES. IT KNOWS YOU'RE HERE NOW. IT HAS KNOWN SINCE THE CAUSEWAY. IF YOU'RE READING THIS AND YOUR NAME IS VOSS — I'M SORRY ABOUT THE BOOTS. TURN BACK OR COME IN SWINGING. EITHER WAY, I'M GLAD YOU FOUND ME.**

Below the words, small, almost shy: the crooked *J* with the wave through it.

Wren stood in the warm ankle-deep water and read it twice, three times, her lantern trembling slightly in a hand that had held steady through sandstorms and gunfights and the loss of everyone she'd ever been ordered to protect.

*It has known since the causeway.*

Since the causeway. Since the night it had spoken to her in Joss's voice. Since the night it had offered her rest, and she had stood at the waterline with her mouth open around a *yes* she'd never said aloud.

It hadn't been luring her. It had been *welcoming* her. It had known exactly which bait to tie to which hook, because her brother was inside it, and it had been reading him for nine years the way she read trails.

Or — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome — because Joss himself had told it how. Because whatever was left of her brother in that warm dark water had wanted his sister found, and the current had obliged, the way a spider obliges a fly.

"Which is it?" she asked the door, quietly. "Is he calling me in, or is it?"

The water pulsed. Once. Warm against her ankles. Patient as tide.

Behind her, Tam unslung the coil of rope from his shoulder and began, with the brisk cheer of a boy who had grown up swimming channels no adult would enter, to rig a line between two bronze door-rings. "Come in swinging," he quoted. "Seems like clear instructions to me."

Sef checked the edge of his long knife, then nodded once, the nod he gave before bad crossings.

Wren put her palm flat against the door, over her brother's mark, and felt — or imagined she felt — a faint answering pressure, like a hand pressed to the wood from the other side.

"Hold on, Joss," she said. "I'm coming in swinging."

She pushed, and the door swung open on a room full of light that had no source, and the water rose to meet them like something standing up to say hello.
