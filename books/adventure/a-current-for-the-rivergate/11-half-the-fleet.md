## 11. Half the Fleet

Commander Ilsa Brann had a rule about names: she learned them only after a person proved they would still be useful tomorrow. It saved wear on the heart and kept the muster rolls honest. So it was that she knew exactly four of the forty-one souls aboard the *Korvane's Mercy* — her quartermaster Vetch, who cheated at cards and at nothing else; her engineer Sable-Ode, who spoke to engines more tenderly than to people; her helmsman, a silent Drowni woman called only Pilot; and her nephew Rell, who was seventeen and had begged to come on this run and whom she therefore refused to learn anything about at all.

"Landfall," Pilot said, which for Pilot was a speech.

Brann stood at the rail of the lead skiff and looked at the Rivergate.

She had seen drowned cities before — the sunken terraces of Callowmere, the salt-preserved bones of Old Ferrow — and they had all looked like corpses: things that had died and were done. The Rivergate did not look dead. It looked like it was holding its breath. Towers rose out of the grey-green shallows with their windows intact, their stone uncrumbled, as if the water had been asked politely to leave them alone and had agreed. Between the towers, the sea moved in long slow spirals, turning against the wind, against the tide tables, against every law Brann had spent thirty years profiting from.

"There," said Vetch, pointing with his chin because his hands were full of ledger. "Smoke. Small. Ranger camp, by the look."

"The ranger," Brann said. "Wren Voss." She turned the name over the way she turned over salvage claims — checking for hidden liens. "She found Okoro. She has the survey journal. She's been inside."

"You want her taken?"

"I want her watched. A tracker who can read that ruin is worth ten engineers who can't." She paused. "And she'll have a boy with her. River-born. Watch him too. Orphans know channels the charts forgot."

Rell, coiling rope nearby with the aggressive enthusiasm of youth pretending not to listen, said, "Aunt Ilsa, is it true the survey team just—"

"It's true they're missing," Brann said. "Missing men don't concern me. Water does." She nodded at the spiraling current. "That's enough flow to turn every pump in Korvane for a hundred years. Enough to fill the cisterns of every dry town on the coast. And it's been sitting here under nobody's flag, doing nothing, for a thousand years." She let that arithmetic hang in the salt air. "The government sent six people to count it. The government got distracted by its own paperwork. That makes it ours."

"And the people it took?" Rell asked. He had his mother's stubborn jaw. Brann had promised herself she wouldn't notice.

"Are a question for whoever owns the machine," she said. "Get the winches ready."

* * *

The advance team went in at low tide — twelve of them, led by a salvage master named Corrin Hale, who had once walked out of a burning freighter carrying two crewmen and a strongbox and had never once mentioned it without being asked. Brann chose him for exactly that reason. Men who carried other men out of fires were careful, and careful men came back.

She watched them wade into the flooded avenue through her spyglass, twelve helmets bobbing between the drowned market stalls, ropes strung hand to hand like beads on a string. The water reached their waists, then their chests. The current pulled them gently forward, helpful as a usher.

For an hour, everything went well. That should have warned her.

Hale's voice crackled up the wire-line: *"Structure's sound, Commander. Stone's like new. There are lanterns down here that are still lit — no oil, no flame, just glowing. Markets, housing, a temple district. It's a whole city, just... waiting."*

"Waiting for what?" Vetch muttered beside her.

*"There's writing,"* Hale reported. *"Same script as the survey journal sketches. Sable-Ode's apprentices are copying it. And Commander — there are figures in the walls. Carved. Hundreds of them. They look like—"*

The wire-line hissed. Then, faintly, distantly, it sang.

Not words. Not quite. A tone, low and sweet, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, rising until it filled the skiff, filled the ears, filled the space behind the teeth where a person keeps their own name. Vetch dropped his ledger. Pilot gripped the wheel and stared at nothing. On the shore, half a mile off, Brann could see the ranger's camp stir — figures spilling out of tents, running for the waterline.

Then the singing stopped, and the counting began.

"Hale," Brann said into the line. "Report. Headcount."

Static. Then Hale's voice, thin and strange, as though he were speaking around something held in his mouth: *"Nine of us at the rope, Commander. We were twelve. I— I can't remember walking away from anyone. I can't remember anyone being behind me. But there's room in the line for three more and the room is wrong, it's wrong, it's like a tooth gone from my—"*

The line went quiet in the way lines go quiet when the far end has stopped existing.

Brann lowered the spyglass. Out on the water, the great spiral current had brightened — a thread of pale light running through it now, three threads, braided, moving with a grace that no debris and no drowning man had ever managed. For a moment she could almost make out shapes in the light. Shoulders. A tilt of head. One of them seemed to be looking back at her.

She did not look away first. It seemed important not to.

* * *

They came ashore an hour later — nine of them, soaked, silent, roped together so tightly their hands were white. Hale stood at the front of them like a fence post someone had forgotten to finish painting. When Brann met him at the waterline he saluted, and his salute was perfect, and his eyes were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic he couldn't stop doing.

"Three," he said. "Ondo. The twins, Marrit and Cassel. We were roped together, Commander. I was holding Ondo's rope. My hand remembers holding it. My hand won't stop remembering it." He held up his right hand, which was indeed curled around the ghost of a rope, fingers clenched on nothing. "What do I write in the log?"

"The truth," Brann said. "Equipment loss, three. Cause unknown. Salvage continues."

Vetch made a small sound, quickly swallowed. "Commander. With respect. *Equipment?*"

"What would you call them?" Brann asked, mild. "Casualties? Casualties are dead, Vetch, and dead men stay put. These three are *assets*, currently misfiled. Which means they can be recovered, or traded, or written off. All three options require the machine. So we get the machine." She turned to Hale. "How long to rig the intake pumps?"

Hale blinked slowly, like a man surfacing. "Four tides. Three, if we cut corners."

"We don't cut corners. Corners are where the water takes people." She said it flatly, and something in Hale's face steadied, because here at last was an order shaped like the world he understood. "Three tides. Get your nine below the waterline before dark and keep them roped. If anyone hears singing, they clamp their mouths shut and hum. You can't answer a question with a closed mouth."

"Yes, Commander." He hesitated. "It asked us. Before it took them. It didn't use words but it — asked. Like it wanted permission." His ghost-rope hand trembled. "Did Ondo say yes?"

Brann considered lying to him and discarded it; lies were heavy cargo and he'd only drop them anyway. "I don't know," she said. "Find out what it asks, and bring me the question. Questions can be answered correctly."

He went. Vetch lingered, shuffling his recovered ledger like a man shuffling evidence.

"You felt it too," Vetch said. "On the skiff. The singing."

"I feel weather coming too. Doesn't mean I cancel the voyage."

"It took three trained salvagers in under an hour, Ilsa. In front of witnesses. The government team was twelve. Twelve, and now there's one madwoman wandering the flats talking about her own name." He lowered his voice. "At some point the arithmetic stops favoring us."

Brann smiled, and it was her real smile, the one she used for storms and creditors. "Vetch. Do you know why Korvane follows me?"

"Because you've never lost a ship."

"Because Korvane is dying of thirst and everyone knows it, and I am the only commander willing to say the ugly sum out loud." She gestured at the shining spiral in the bay, at the three bright threads braided inside it. "That thing ate three of mine today. Terrible. Unforgivable. Now hear the rest of the sum: the aqueducts of Korvane carry water to forty thousand people, and next summer they will carry dust unless that machine works. Forty thousand against three. Against thirty. Against three hundred, if it comes to it." She met his eyes. "I didn't make the exchange rate, Vetch. The desert did. I'm just the only one willing to pay it."

"That's a cold way to love a city."

"It's the only way that gets the water there." She picked up her spyglass again. "Now go count our pumps."

* * *

She found Rell on the foredeck of the *Mercy*, sitting where he shouldn't be, watching the light-threads in the current with his chin on his knees.

"You heard the singing," Brann said. It wasn't a question; his face had the same unfinished look as Hale's.

"Everyone heard it." He didn't look at her. "Marrit taught me dice on the crossing. She cheated. Badly. Everyone knew and nobody minded." His jaw worked. "You wrote her down as equipment."

"I wrote down what keeps the fleet alive."

"She had a laugh like a door hinge. Somebody should write that down somewhere." He finally looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and seventeen. "Is that what we are to you? Line items?"

Brann sat down beside him, which surprised them both. Below them the current turned, patient as a creditor.

"When your mother died," she said, "I carried her body two hundred miles across the flats because the ground was too hard to dig and I refused to leave her for the birds. I dug her grave with a salvage winch hook. It took eleven hours and it broke two of my fingers." She flexed those old-broken fingers now, absently. "I have buried every person this fleet has ever lost, Rell. I know all their hinges. But grief is a luxury good, and I can't afford it until the work is done. So I file people where they'll keep. Marrit is filed under *recoverable*. That is not disrespect. That is hope, in the only language I speak."

"And if she can't be recovered?"

"Then she goes in the ledger under losses, and I pay her family double shares, and I dig if there's ground soft enough." She stood, brushing salt from her coat. "Which is why the machine comes home whole. Because if it can give back what it takes, then nobody I've ever filed stays filed."

Rell wiped his face with his sleeve, roughly, the way boys do when they've decided crying is a chore they'll finish later. "Aunt Ilsa. The ranger's camp. They're moving toward the ruin."

Brann raised the spyglass. Sure enough: the ranger Wren Voss stood at the waterline with her ragged little company — the orphan boy, the mad surveyor wrapped in blankets, the old ferryman — looking out at the light-threads the way people look at a grave they've just found.

"They know something," Brann said. "The journal, the channels, the survivor. They've been reading this ruin while we've been measuring it." She lowered the glass, and the cold arithmetic clicked forward another column. "Move the timetable. Two tides, not three. Sable-Ode starts the intake survey tonight, with a full rope-line and a humming crew. And send a boat to the ranger's camp — a polite one. White cloth on the mast. Tell Voss I want to talk trade."

"Trade what?"

"Whatever she's found," Brann said, watching the small figure on the shore, "before the water finds it first."

Out in the bay, one of the three bright threads drifted closer to the fleet than the others, close enough that the lanterns along the *Mercy's* rail flickered in time with it, and for half a heartbeat Brann could have sworn the light had a face, and the face was listening.

Two tides, she thought. Whatever this place was, whatever it wanted, it had eaten a government team and three of hers, and it was still hungry, and it was still patient, and patience was the one currency she had never been able to counterfeit.

"Two tides," she said aloud, to the deck, to the fleet, to the listening water. "Then we take the gate."
