## 12. The Machine Under the Gate

Denna Okoro had spent eleven years learning to trust her instruments, and in the last three days she had learned to trust nothing at all — except, perhaps, the boy with salt-cracked lips who kept talking about biscuits.

"The deep channels," Tam was saying, crouched at the edge of the flooded stairwell, "run under the whole ruin. River-orphans used them before anyone had a name for this place. My mother's mother ran cargo through them." He glanced up at Wren. "You want the heart of it? The journal says northeast gallery, below the third tide line. That's not a walk. That's a swim."

"It's a dive," Wren corrected. "A swim is when you can see the bottom."

Denna hugged the survey journal to her chest and said nothing. She had written that coordinate herself, six weeks and one lifetime ago, in a hand steadier than the one she owned now. *Northeast gallery, below the third tide line. The resonance source.* She remembered thinking it would be a wellhead. A spring. Something you could put a gauge on and file a report about.

She had not imagined it would have a heartbeat.

* * *

They went down at slack water, when the drowned streets held their breath. Sef stayed above with the boat, because someone had to, and because — as he put it, tying off the line with the unhurried care of a man who had buried more friends than he could count — "old knees don't negotiate with stairs."

The water was warm. That was the first wrong thing. Dead seas are cold; Denna knew cold water the way she knew her own pulse. This was blood-warm, and it moved against them even when they stood still, a slow insistent pull toward the northeast, patient as a queue.

Wren led. Of course Wren led. Denna had watched the ranger for three days now, watched her move through the ruin like a woman crossing a room she'd memorized in a dream, and Denna understood something about her that she suspected Wren didn't understand herself: Wren Voss never stopped moving because stopping was where the grief lived. Denna recognized the architecture. She'd built the same thing out of survey schedules and instrument calibrations after her own losses, and look where it had gotten her — chest-deep in a dead city, following coordinates into the mouth of whatever had eaten her team.

Tam swam beside her, chattering to keep the fear at arm's length. "Did I ever tell you about the eel of Saltmere? Big as a rowboat. Old Marla swore it had opinions." He kicked along. "It did, actually. Opinions about bait."

"Tam," Wren said from ahead, without turning.

"Right. Quiet. Diving quietly."

But his hand found Denna's elbow in the dark and stayed there, and she was grateful enough not to pretend she didn't notice.

The gallery opened around them like a cathedral that had been drowned mid-prayer. Columns rose out of black water into black air. And beneath them — beneath everything — was the sound.

Denna had been hearing it for weeks, faintly, the way you hear your own name across a crowded market. Here it was not faint. It was a hum in the stone, a hum in the water, a hum in her teeth. Not mechanical. Mechanical things tick and whirr and grind. This *breathed*. In, and out. In, and out.

"There," Wren breathed.

Light came from below — not firelight, not lamplight, but a pale gold glow rising through the water like memory of sun. They surfaced into a vast chamber, an air pocket held under the vaulted roof of some ancient intake hall, and the light came up through a lattice of grates in the floor, and through the grates Denna saw the machine.

* * *

Her first thought, absurdly, was of a loom.

Her grandmother had woven, back in the river villages, and Denna had spent childhood afternoons watching thread become cloth, thousands of separate strands passing through the shuttle and emerging as one strong fabric. The machine under the gate was like that. Strands of light — no, not light, *current*, actual current, water made luminous with something older than electricity — ran through a mechanism the size of a village, gears the size of grain silos turning so slowly you had to watch a while to know they moved at all. Channels fed into it from every direction, the whole hydrology of the dead sea gathered and combed and drawn down into the heart.

And threaded through it, caught in the lattice like flies in amber —

People.

Denna's breath left her in a sound she didn't recognize as her own voice.

They hung in the glowing channels, dozens of them, scores, suspended in the flowing light. Not drowning. Not floating. *Held.* Their eyes were open. Their mouths moved, sometimes, in slow speech or slow song. Some had been there long enough that the current had begun to finish the work of dissolving them — outlines softening, edges going to gold — but their faces were still faces. Still looking out.

One of them raised a hand against the grate. Fingers spread. Pressed.

"Oh," Tam whispered. His humor had gone somewhere and left him. "Oh, that's — those are people. Those are *people*."

"Meridian colors," Denna said. Her voice came out flat, professional, the voice of a woman reading a gauge, because if she let it be anything else she would go down on her knees on the wet stone and stay there. "Second figure from the left. That's Adeyemi. That's — that's my team lead. Adeyemi Osei." Her training supplied facts the way a drowning man supplies bubbles. "Six members. Meridian Survey Team Four. I'm — I was the seventh."

Wren had gone very still, in the way Denna was beginning to understand meant the opposite of calm. "Can we reach them?"

"I don't know what reaching them means anymore."

"Then we find out." Wren was already wading toward the nearest access stair, because of course she was, because stopping was death and standing still was death and every instinct in that woman screamed against the sight of people held motionless in a current. "Tam, watch the water level. Denna—"

"Denna," said the chamber.

Every one of them stopped.

The voice hadn't come from any direction. It came from the water itself, from the hum in the stone, from the space between Denna's own heartbeat. It was many voices braided into one, the way her grandmother's threads became cloth, and underneath the braid she could hear — she would swear on her instruments, on her life, on her grandmother's grave — she could hear Adeyemi. Could hear little Pilar, who'd joined the team eight months ago and laughed at her own terrible jokes. Could hear them the way you hear a song you learned as a child: not words, just the shape of knowing them.

*Denna,* the current said again, gently, the way you'd call across a room to someone you loved. *You brought your name back. We've been keeping your place.*

"No," Denna said.

*You're tired.* It wasn't cruel. That was the horror of it. It was kind, the way a fever is kind at the end, the way exhaustion lies to you at three in the morning. *You've been tired for years. We remember your tiredness here. We hold it for you. Come and put it down.*

The water around her ankles had begun to pull. Not hard. Politely. An usher showing her to her seat.

A hand closed on her arm — Wren, grip like a shackle, and behind her Tam had both fists knotted in the back of Denna's jacket, a small anchor made entirely of stubbornness.

"She's not coming," Wren said to the chamber, to the water, to the hundred open eyes behind the grate. Her voice didn't shake. Denna wondered what it cost her. "None of us are. Whatever you are, whatever bargain you made with whoever built this place — the answer is no."

The current was quiet a moment. Then, almost amused, almost sad:

*Everyone answers eventually. Your brother did.*

Denna felt Wren flinch. Actually flinch — a full-body thing, there and gone, like a wire snapping under load. But the ranger only tightened her grip on Denna's arm and said, in a voice sanded perfectly flat, "Show me how to open these grates."

* * *

They couldn't. That was the finding of the next hour, conducted with Denna's instruments and Tam's channel-knowledge and Wren's refusal to accept conclusions, and the finding was this: the grates were not doors. They were not barriers at all. They were the machine.

"They're intake valves," Denna said finally, sitting back on the wet stone, journal open on her knees, her handwriting staring up at her from six weeks ago like a stranger's. "Look at the geometry. Everything feeds the center. Water comes in through a thousand channels — the surface channels, the deep channels, all of them — and it passes through the lattice, and the lattice is where—" She stopped. Started again. "The old civilization didn't build a pump. They built a *filter*. The water flows through the people. That's what makes it current. That's what powers it. Every drop that ever flowed out of this gate toward the living villages passed through someone who gave themselves to make it flow."

Silence, except for the breathing hum.

"That's why the villages downstream still have water," Tam said slowly. He was crouched by the grate, looking down at the golden figures with an expression far too old for his face. "That's why the dead sea has a live edge. It's not leftover magic. It's running. It's running *on*—"

"Yes," Denna said.

"And the survey team." Wren's voice. Careful. Careful the way ice is careful. "You said conscious."

Denna looked down through the grate at Adeyemi Osei, second figure from the left, whose mouth was moving in that slow silent speech. Six weeks ago Adeyemi had teased her about over-calibrating her instruments. Now Adeyemi hung in the engine of a dead civilization, dissolved to the shoulders in light, and her eyes tracked them. Tracked *Denna*. And her mouth kept moving, and Denna leaned close to the grate, close enough that the warm glow lit her face, and read the shape of it the way she'd once read gauges.

Not *help us*.

Not *save us*.

*Don't stop here.*

"Adeyemi," Denna whispered. "What happened? What did it ask you?"

The mouth moved. Slower now, effortful, a swimmer fighting a current to lift one arm.

*It asked our names,* Adeyemi's lips said. *We answered.*

Behind Denna, Tam made a small sound. When she turned, he was staring at the water lapping the chamber floor, and his face had gone the color of old rope.

"When I was stealing boats," he said, "down in the estuary slums — there was a story the orphans told. About the Rivergate. That if the water ever asked your name, you were supposed to lie." He swallowed. "Nobody ever said what happens if you tell the truth."

The hum rose, very slightly. In, and out. Patient as a queue. Somewhere in its braid of voices, Denna heard Pilar laugh — heard it clearly, warmly, unmistakably — and understood that the current was not threatening them.

It was advertising.

* * *

They climbed back toward the air pocket in silence, and the silence lasted exactly as far as the first dry ledge, which was where Wren Voss apparently decided that silence had done its duty.

"Options," she said, wringing out her sleeve with brisk violence, as though the water had personally offended her. "One: we leave, we report, the government sends someone better equipped. Two: we try to cut the team free. Three: we destroy the mechanism."

"You can't cut them free," Denna said. "I've been trying to say it and I'll say it plainly. There's nothing to cut. Adeyemi isn't trapped *in* the machine. Adeyemi is part of the machine. Pulling her out would be like pulling a thread out of cloth and expecting the shirt to survive."

"Then option two is out."

"Option three," Tam said, and then stopped, and looked at his hands.

"What?"

"The villages." He said it carefully, feeling his way. "The river villages. Sef's people. Half of them are a bad season from empty wells. If this thing dies, the current dies, and the water—" He looked up at Wren, and Denna watched a boy realize that the world does not come divided into good things and bad things, and that some choices are just different shapes of loss. "If we break the engine, we might be breaking the only tap half the coast has."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Down through the grate, the golden figures breathed their slow light. In, and out.

"We need to know more," she said at last. "How it chooses. Whether it can be starved instead of broken. Whether—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—whether the people in it can be brought back, or only released. Denna, your journal. The builders must have left records. Maintenance notes. Anything."

"There's a lower archive marked on my original survey grid," Denna said. "Below the tide lines. We'd need the deep channels to reach it."

Tam snorted. "You'd need me, you mean."

"We'd need you," Wren agreed, and something in her tone made it not a joke and not not a joke.

Denna closed the journal and held it against her chest, and for a moment — just a moment — she let herself feel the truth underneath all the professionalism: that she wanted, desperately, shamefully, to hear Pilar laugh again, and that the water downstairs was offering exactly that, forever, for the price of her name.

The hum breathed. In, and out.

And then, distantly, through fathoms of stone and water, came a sound that did not belong to the machine at all.

Engines. Many engines. The deep, greedy growl of salvage ships making way under full burn, close enough now that the vibration reached down into the drowned city like a knock at a door.

Tam's head came up. "That'll be Brann."

"How long?" Wren asked.

He listened the way river-orphans listen, with his whole body. "Tide and a half," he said. "Maybe less."

Above them, through the breathing light, the machine waited with its hundred open eyes — and Denna understood, with the clarity of a woman reading a final gauge, that whatever argument was coming, it would happen down here, in front of the engine, with the water listening to every word.

The current, she thought, already knew their names.
