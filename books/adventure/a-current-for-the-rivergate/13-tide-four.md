## 13. Tide Four

Tam had learned to count tides the way other boys counted coins, and by his reckoning they had two left. Two turns of the dead sea's slow breathing before the Rivergate drowned its lower streets again and Brann's fleet came up the channels with grappling hooks and guns.

Two tides. And here they were, sitting in a half-collapsed customs house on the ruin's second tier, arguing.

He should have known it would come to this. Every crew he'd ever watched from the reed-banks argued eventually — usually about water, usually about who deserved it. This argument was about a machine that ate people, which Tam supposed was at least more interesting than most.

"It works," Denna said. She stood at the broken window with her back to all of them, survey journal open in her hands like a hymnal. "That's what none of you understand. It *works*. The old civilization didn't build a monster. They built an answer."

"They built a meat grinder with a spout," Sef said from his corner, where he sat sharpening a boat-hook that hadn't needed sharpening since yesterday. The old ferryman did that when he was worried — worked an edge that was already true. "I've poached eels in these waters forty years, girl. I know what feeds a good current and what fouls one."

"The villages are dying." Denna turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and too bright, and Tam shifted uncomfortably on the windowsill because he'd seen that brightness before, down in the flooded market, right before the water started whispering. "Salt Hollow lost three wells last season. The Ashline settlements are drinking mud. There is a machine under our feet that can make a desert bloom, and you want to drown it because it's *hungry*?"

"I want to drown it because it *eats*," Wren said.

She was sitting on the floor, back against the wall, boots off for once — Tam had never seen her boots off, not even sleeping, until two days ago in this room. That small surrender scared him more than the machine did. Rangers didn't take their boots off. Stopping wasn't in their nature.

"Everything eats," Denna said. "You eat. The fleet eats. Korvane eats. At least this machine is honest about the price."

"And what's the price?" Wren asked quietly. "Say it plain."

Denna's jaw worked. Down below them, through the cracked flagstones, the water moved against the wind — Tam could feel it in the soles of his feet, that wrong slow pull, like the whole ruin was a lung drawing breath.

"You saw them," Denna said at last. "In the mechanism. My team. Merik and Osha and little Fenn. Still *conscious*, Wren. Not screaming. Not suffering. Aware." Her voice caught and steadied. "The current doesn't torture anyone. It asks. It has always asked. Everyone it holds chose—"

"Fenn was twenty-two," Wren said. "Did he choose?"

Silence. Somewhere below, water lapped stone in a rhythm almost like patience.

* * *

Tam slipped out onto the balcony while the grown-ups' silence got too heavy, the way silences did. He perched on the railing over the drowned avenue and dangled his heels, watching the green-black water slide between the old rooftops six stories down.

He'd lived on water his whole life and never trusted it until now, which was a strange thing to realize about yourself. Water had been his road, his roof, his pantry. Now it was full of voices offering people rest, and one of those voices had spent the last day trying very hard to sound like Wren Voss's brother.

Tam had heard it too, a little. Not Joss — the current didn't bother with him, or maybe couldn't find anything in him soft enough to hook. What it offered Tam was simpler: a channel home. A warm kitchen that didn't exist anymore. His mother's voice saying his name the way she used to before the fever year, before the river took everything sideways.

He'd put his fingers in his ears and recited boat parts until it went away. *Keel, thwart, gunwale, scupper.* Ugly words. Good ugly words. You couldn't be lulled by a word like scupper.

The door scraped behind him and Wren came out, boots back on, which relieved him more than he wanted to admit.

"You're sitting on a railing over haunted water," she said.

"I'm sitting on a railing over *deep* water. Haunted's a different problem." He scooted over. She didn't sit — rangers leaned, they didn't sit — but she leaned on the rail beside him, arms crossed, watching the current slide past below.

"You sided with me in there," she said.

"Wasn't a side. It was just obvious." He flicked a pebble into the dark. "Denna keeps talking like the machine's a well. It's not a well. It's a mouth. You don't hand a dying village a mouth and call it mercy."

Wren huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Where'd you learn to talk like that?"

"Reed-banks. You learn fast or you get talked out of your shoes." He glanced up at her. "But that's not why you came out here."

"No."

"You came out here because you're going to say something you don't want Sef and Denna to hear yet."

Wren was quiet long enough that the tide turned beneath them — he felt it, the faint shudder through the stone, the water below changing its mind about which way to go.

"There might be another way," she said finally. "Not destroy. Not feed. Something in between. But I need to check the intake channels first, and I need someone who knows hidden water better than any surveyor alive."

Tam looked at her. Then down at the black glide of the current. Then back at her.

"You're asking me to go swimming in the thing's throat."

"I'm asking if you know the channels."

"I know the channels." He swung his legs back over to the safe side of the railing and stood, brushing off his trousers with as much dignity as a boy in patched clothes could muster. "I want it noted that I'm doing this because you're the only person who ever chased me across the flats and *caught* me, and I've decided that means something. Not because I'm brave. I want that on the record."

"Noted," Wren said. "For the record."

* * *

They went down at slack water, when the current's pull went thin and confused, and Tam led them through the orphans' ways — the crawl-gap behind the old fishmonger's arch, the submerged stair that only looked like a dead end if you didn't know to feel for the third step's loose capstone, the low tunnel where you had to go flat and breathe from the air pocket shaped like a tilted bottle.

Sef came as far as the tunnel and no farther. "My heart's too old for bottle-breathing," he said, and posted himself at the entrance with the boat-hook and instructions to knock twice on stone if anything followed them in.

Denna came because she wouldn't be left, and because part of her — Tam saw it, everyone saw it — part of her was listening the whole time. Head tilted. Lips moving around names.

The intake channels were beautiful, in the way drowned things were beautiful. Old glazed tile gone pearl-grey under silt, arched ceilings furred with white mineral bloom, and running along the walls, carved in repeating lines, the same figure over and over: a circle with a spiral inside, feeding into a larger wheel.

"That's it," Denna whispered. "That's the intake sigil. The journal says the engine drinks through twelve gates." Her finger traced the spiral. "Twelve gates, and the main channel runs—" she stopped.

"What?" Wren asked.

"This isn't in the journal." Denna's voice had gone strange. "This route. These channels. The survey mapped the public cisterns, the grand avenues. Nobody mapped *this*." She turned slowly in the dark, lamp-light swinging. "Who built these maintenance ways? Who kept them secret?"

"Orphans," Tam said simply. "Somebody always lives in the cracks. Somebody always has." He put his palm flat against the wall, feeling for the draft that meant the deep gates lay ahead. "And somebody always knows which crack leads to the heart."

The draft came cold and salt-sharp from a black archway ahead, and beyond it, faint as a thought, they could all hear it now — not words, exactly. A hum. A chord. Many voices braided into one patient note.

Denna made a small sound and stepped toward it.

Wren caught her arm. "Denna."

"They're *right there*." Denna's composure cracked clean through, and underneath it she was just a woman who'd lost five friends and been told for days that they weren't really gone. "I can hear Merik. He's humming. He used to hum when he worked, he doesn't even know he does it, and I can *hear him*, Wren, he's right there—"

"Denna." Wren held both her shoulders now, firm, ungentle in the kindest possible way. "Listen to me. Whatever's humming, it learned that song from him. That's not him calling you. That's bait wearing his voice."

"You don't know that."

"No," Wren admitted. "I don't. But I know what it offered me, and it lied about my brother being free. So it lies."

Denna shook under her hands. Then, slowly, she nodded, and stepped back, and wrapped her own arms around herself like she was holding her ribs together.

Tam marked the spot anyway. He marked how far Denna had gotten in four steps before Wren stopped her. Four steps to the arch. He filed that away with the boat parts, in the place where he kept things that might matter later.

* * *

They were halfway back through the crawl-gap when the stone knocked twice.

Sef's signal. Tam froze, water to his chin in the low tunnel, and heard — felt — the deep note change. The patient chord below them bent upward, quickened, became almost eager.

Then light. Lamplight, many lamps, sweeping down the grand avenue above the tunnel's mouth, and voices — clipped, disciplined, fleet-voices.

"Advance team's inside," Wren breathed. "She moved early. Tide Four's barely turned."

"She's not waiting for tides anymore," Tam whispered. "She's making her own."

Boots on stone overhead. A fleet officer's voice, flat and carrying: "Survey point seven. Mark it. The engineer says the intake manifold's within two hundred meters — start the sounding lines."

Denna had gone rigid. Not with fear, Tam realized. With recognition. "Point seven," she murmured. "That's where we stopped. That's where we—" Her eyes went wide and fixed on the black archway behind them, where the chord was rising, rising, welcoming, and Tam understood with a sick drop of his stomach that the current knew too. The fleet hadn't found the machine on their own.

Something had opened the door for them.

"Denna," Tam started, but she was already turning, already walking, calm as Sunday, and Wren lunged through the water after her, and above them the fleet's lamps swept closer, and below them the current sang with twelve mouths at once—

And Tam, dripping in the dark, did the only useful thing left. He grabbed the loose capstone on the third step of the submerged stair and pulled, collapsing the orphans' way behind them, sealing the tunnel, buying them minutes — and cutting off their own retreat.

"Keel," he said to himself, teeth chattering. "Thwart. Gunwale. Scupper."

The chord below bent one last time — toward Denna, toward the fleet, toward all of them — and then, far off through the stone, came the first scream of the tide turning early, and Tam knew they had less than one tide left after all.
