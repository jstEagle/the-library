## 14. What Brann Buried

Commander Ilsa Brann had a rule about prisoners: feed them better than her own crew ate, and they talked twice as fast.

It wasn't kindness. Kindness was a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago, back when the cisterns of Korvane first dropped below half and the ration boards started printing schedules for thirst instead of grain. It was arithmetic. A frightened man who'd eaten well believed you didn't need him anymore. A comfortable man believed you might keep him alive. Both beliefs made him generous with his mouth.

The engineer's name was Pell Ovidu, and he had eaten two full bowls of saltfish stew before he said anything worth hearing.

"Third bowl?" Brann asked, sitting across from him in the hold of the *Korvane's Mercy*, which was not named for mercy at all but for the old belief that naming a thing invited it aboard. She'd argued against the name at the christening. She'd been overruled by a shipwright who believed in luck more than irony.

Pell wiped his beard. "You're going to hang me either way."

"I'm going to *use* you either way," Brann corrected. "Hanging is what happens after. Whether there's an after depends entirely on how interesting your third bowl makes you."

He studied her the way engineers studied things — looking for the load-bearing wall. She let him look. She was fifty-one years old, gray at the temples, scarred across one knuckle from a winch cable that had taken three fingers off a deckhand in her first command, and she had learned long ago that the fastest way to be underestimated was to be a woman with a reputation, and the second-fastest way was to be patient while people did it.

"You're not what the stories say," Pell offered.

"No one ever is." She ladled him another bowl herself, which surprised him visibly. "The stories say I'm a butcher. The truth is I've never killed anyone who wasn't trying to kill me first or standing between me and water. You're neither. So talk."

He talked.

* * *

What he told her, she already knew — but knowing a thing yourself and hearing it said aloud in a stranger's voice are different animals, the way a wound you can see is different from one you can only feel.

Korvane was dying.

Not dying the way poets meant it, all slow golden decline. Dying the way a lamp dies: still burning, still giving light, and every hour measurably closer to dark. The deep aquifer beneath the city had been dropping for thirty years. Ten years ago the drop became a fall. Five years ago the eastern districts went on day-rations, then half-day, then the water carts came only when the wind favored them, because the wind powered nothing anymore except hope. Two hundred thousand people. Wells gone brackish. Children whose entire understanding of rain came from stories.

Brann had grown up in those eastern districts. She remembered the taste of the last sweet water — a memory so precise it felt less like memory than like a splinter, something lodged under the skin of every day since. Her mother had given her the first cup and taken none herself, and had smiled while doing it, and seven-year-old Ilsa had drunk it all without thinking, because children do not think, and that was the whole of it, that was the seed of everything: someone had gone thirsty so she could drink, and no one had ever explained to her why that should feel like anything other than debt.

"So the machine," Pell said, setting down the third bowl. "The thing under the Rivergate. You believe the survey reports."

"I believe my own eyes," Brann said. "I've read the Meridian team's preliminary findings. An engine older than any nation on the coast, drawing water out of dead ground and dead sea and making it live. And doing it on fuel we don't have to mine, grow, or pay for." She paused. "Except we do pay for it. That's the part your government found so upsetting they made the survey team disappear rather than publish."

Pell's face did something complicated. "You know what happened to them."

"I know what happens to everyone who goes into that ruin without coming out again. They don't leave tracks." She'd stood at the survey camp herself, two tides ago, and walked the perimeter three times, and felt the same cold slide down her spine that Wren Voss must have felt — gear intact, meals half-eaten, instruments still running, and sand inside the tents smooth as a fresh-laid tablecloth. No tracks. Not even their own. As if they'd been lifted out of the world rather than out of the camp.

"And you're going in anyway," Pell said. It wasn't quite a question. It was the tone men used when they wanted you to hear the accusation without having to defend making it.

"I'm going in anyway," Brann agreed. "Two hundred thousand people are drinking dust in Korvane while the coastal villages fight over wells that give a bucket an hour. There is a machine under that ruin that ends that. Every engine humanity ever built runs on something burned. Coal, oil, wood, whale fat, men." She met his eyes. "I've simply decided to be honest about the fuel."

"That's a hell of a sentence, Commander."

"It's the truest one I own."

* * *

She dismissed him after that — locked, fed, alive — and climbed to the aft deck, where the Rivergate showed itself above the horizon line of the dead sea like the spine of a drowned animal. Evening light turned the salt flats pink. Somewhere out there, Voss and her ragged little family were moving through channels no chart recorded, and Brann's advance team had gone into the flooded streets yesterday and come back three souls lighter, and Brann had written the names in the ledger and moved the timetable up a tide.

Lieutenant Harka found her at the rail. He always knew where she went to be alone, which was one of the reasons she kept him, and one of the reasons she'd never promoted him past lieutenant — a man who could find you anywhere was too useful to make into an officer, and too dangerous to make into a friend. She'd never decided which category he sat in. Possibly both.

"The prisoner," Harka said. "You believe him?"

"He told me nothing I didn't already know. That's what made him useful." She watched the ruin. "Korvane has eleven months of water at current rationing. Eight if the summer turns cruel. I've buried that number so deep in fleet dispatches that even my quartermasters think we're at eighteen months. Do you know why?"

"Because crews desert over numbers."

"Because crews desert over *true* numbers," Brann said. "A lie keeps a ship sailing. I learned that before I learned navigation." She said it flatly, without shame, because shame was another currency she'd stopped carrying, and because Harka deserved honesty from someone, even if it couldn't be warmth.

"My mother's district went dry when I was nine," Harka said, after a silence. "We walked to the coast. Half our street didn't make the walk."

"I know. I read your file."

"You read everyone's file."

"It's cheaper than asking questions. People lie when asked questions. Files just get sad." She almost smiled. It was the closest she came these days; the muscle had atrophied. "Your street is the reason you follow me, Harka. Don't pretend it's loyalty to me personally. I'm not likable enough for personal loyalty."

"No, Commander."

"There. See? We understand each other perfectly. It's the only kind of marriage I've ever had time for."

* * *

Night came down over the dead sea, and with it the tide, and with the tide the sound — faint, at this distance, more felt than heard — of the Rivergate breathing. The survivors of the advance team wouldn't speak of what took the three. One of them, a rigger named Sallow, had started sleeping with his boots laced and a knife in his hand, and when Brann asked him why, he said, "So whatever comes for me finds me dressed for work," and she'd liked him marginally better after that, in the way she liked anyone who answered horror with logistics.

She stood her evening watch alone, as she did every night, and did the accounting she did every night, the one no ledger held.

Three of her people, gone into the water. Names in the book. Families, in two cases — she'd sent the standard letters, the ones composed by a clerk who had never lost anyone and it showed. She would write better ones herself later, or she wouldn't, and either way the letters changed nothing, and the machine under the gate would run whether its fuel was volunteered or taken, and Korvane would drink.

That was the shape of it. She had looked at the shape of it for years, turning it like a stone, looking for the seam. There wasn't one. There was only the choice, and the choice was: some people, or her people. Strangers she'd never see, or the city that raised her, that taught her letters off ration-board notices, that gave her a mother who went thirsty smiling.

Voss would say there was a third option. Voss, with her strays and her stowaway boy and her stubborn refusal to spend people like coin. Brann had watched her refuse partnership on the flats and thought, at the time, *amateur*. Now, watching the ruin breathe in the dark, she thought other words she didn't let reach her face.

*Amateur*, maybe. Or maybe the only person on this coast who hadn't buried something she couldn't afford to dig up.

Because here was the thing Brann had buried, deeper than the aquifer numbers, deeper than the letters she hadn't rewritten: she remembered the taste of the sweet water. She remembered her mother's smile. And on some nights — nights like this one, with the tide turning and the ruin exhaling — she understood, with terrible clarity, that if the machine demanded her mother's cup be refilled by emptying someone else's, some younger Ilsa, some girl still walking out of the eastern districts, would have said *no*.

And she had buried that girl so thoroughly that sometimes she could almost convince herself the grave was empty.

Almost.

"Commander." Harka at the cabin door, lamplight behind him. "Signal from the shore pickets. Voss's boat entered the eastern channels an hour ago. Heading for the gate."

"Of course she is." Brann turned from the rail. "Sound general quarters. Quietly — I want us moving before she knows we've seen her."

"And the prisoner?"

"Pell Ovidu stays fed and breathing. If we take the machine, I'll need engineers who understand it, and if we don't—" she paused at the door, looking back once at the ruin, black against the last of the light, "—then it won't matter what anyone eats."

Below decks, the engines woke. Above them, in the flooded heart of the Rivergate, something old and patient turned its attention toward the small warm lives moving through its streets — and among them, Brann knew without knowing how she knew, was the tracker Voss, walking toward the one bargain in the world that Brann herself, given the choice, might not have refused.

The tide began to rise.
