## 15. The Current's Bargain

Wren hadn't slept in two days, and the current knew it.

That was the thing she couldn't stop thinking about as she sat on the flooded steps of what had once been a merchant's house, boots dangling in water that should have been cold and wasn't. The current knew it. It knew everything about her — how long since she'd eaten anything that wasn't salted jerky, how her left knee ached when the tide turned, how many nights she'd spent sleeping upright against her pack because lying down meant stopping and stopping meant —

Well. Stopping had always meant dying, in her arithmetic. She was beginning to suspect the current had done its own sums and arrived at a different answer.

The water in the drowned street lay flat as poured glass. No wind reached down here between the walls of the old city; Tam said the Rivergate had been built to breathe with the tides, streets filling and emptying like lungs, and now that he'd said it Wren couldn't unsee it. The whole ruin inhaling. Exhaling. Waiting.

"You're talking to it again," Tam said from the doorway above her. He had a coil of rope over one shoulder and a stolen look of disapproval on his face that he'd absolutely learned from her.

"I'm sitting."

"You're sitting *at* it. There's a difference." He came down the steps and dropped beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. Fifteen years old and he'd already decided that proximity was a form of protection. She hadn't corrected him. "Sef says Denna's sleeping. Actually sleeping, not the walking-around-with-her-eyes-open kind."

"Good." Denna Okoro had slept badly since they'd pulled her back from the water's edge two nights ago, after the current had shown her — or claimed to show her — the faces of her survey team, smiling, whole, standing waist-deep in a street that no longer existed. "And you? You're supposed to be mapping the eastern channels with Sef."

"Done. Mostly. There's a junction where three tunnels meet and none of them go anywhere sensible, which Sef says means we haven't found the sensible one yet." He picked at the rope. "Wren. Can I ask you something without you doing the thing where your face goes all ranger?"

"My face doesn't do a thing."

"It does a *whole* thing. Like a door closing." He looked at her sideways. "When the current talked to you. In the market street. What did it say?"

*It offered me my brother's voice,* she didn't say. *It offered me rest like a warm bath I could sink into forever.* What she said was: "It said my name."

"That's it?"

"That's plenty." Because it had said her name the way nobody said it anymore — not *Ranger Voss*, not *hey, tracker*, not the flat transactional syllables of a contract. It had said *Wren* the way Joss used to, when they were kids and the word still meant something soft. "Names are levers, Tam. Whoever holds yours can move you."

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, which was one of the things about him she'd stopped pretending to find annoying. "Then don't let it hold mine."

"Too late," she said, and bumped his shoulder with hers, and felt him grin in the dark before he climbed back up to help Sef, and the water below her stayed perfectly, patiently still.

* * *

It came for her at slack tide, when even the breathing of the city paused.

She'd gone alone to the great basin at the heart of the ruin — against every instinct she owned, but the journal's coordinates pointed there, and so did the carvings, and so, if she was honest, did something in her own chest that she refused to name. The basin was a half-mile circle of black stone, and the water in it stood higher than anywhere else in the Rivergate, and it did not ripple. It reflected the stars with such perfect fidelity that looking down felt like looking up, and Wren had to keep her eyes on the stone rim to remember which way was sky.

The survey journal lay open on her knee. Denna's careful hand. *The machine is not beneath the gate. The machine IS the gate. We were wrong to think of it as infrastructure.*

"Wren."

Her brother's voice came out of the water the way warmth comes off a sun-baked rock — not heard so much as absorbed through the skin.

She didn't flinch. She'd promised herself she wouldn't flinch, and keeping promises to herself was the last discipline she had left. "Joss."

"Not yet." A pause, and then, gently, almost apologetically: "But soon. That's why I'm here. That's why it let me come."

The water brightened. Not light exactly — more like the memory of light, the way a desert night never goes fully dark because the sand gives back what the day gave it. Shapes moved under the surface. She made herself look. Faces, dozens of them, hundreds, drifting like lanterns in deep water, and among them — third row, slightly left, where he'd always been, the middle child who'd found his place by standing slightly apart — Joss Voss, twenty-six years old forever, looking up at her with an expression she recognized from the day he'd left: sorry, and certain, and hoping she'd understand.

"You've seen the marks," he said. "In the north arcade. My signs."

"Every warning cut wrong-handed," she said. "You always mirrored your letters. Ma beat it out of you twice and it came back anyway."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of him, and the water shivered with it, and around him the other faces stirred — the survey team, she realized. All six. Denna's people, awake in there, aware, watching her with eyes that held no terror at all, which was somehow worse than terror would have been.

"Wren." His voice again, closer now, coming from everywhere, from the stone under her boots. "I need you to listen. Really listen. Not ranger-listen. Sister-listen."

"I'm listening."

"The machine is failing." The gentleness in his voice cracked, just once, and showed the machinery underneath. "It's been failing for a hundred years. It runs on — " he hesitated, hunting for words a desert boy would have — "on people giving themselves. Willingly, mostly. That's the design. It can't take what isn't offered. But it's starving, and a starving engine starts pulling instead of asking, and that's what happened to Denna's team. That's what will happen to Brann's people, and then to Brann, and then — " he spread his hands, and the gesture was so familiar, so *Joss*, that Wren's throat closed — "then it pulls the river villages. Every well within two hundred miles feeds this thing. It's the reason the dead sea is dead. It drank it."

"And if someone fixes it?"

"If someone *feeds* it, it steadies. One willing soul, given freely, holds the hunger down for a generation. Maybe two." He looked at her, and she watched him arrive at the sentence the way you watch someone walk toward the edge of a cliff they've already decided to jump from. "One soul buys the villages time. Buys them water. Real water — the machine makes it, that's the whole horror of it, it makes *enough*. Enough for Korvane. Enough for the river towns. Enough that Brann wouldn't need to burn anything to get it."

The trap assembled itself in Wren's mind with the clean, terrible elegance of all the best traps. She could see every piece. "And the price of that water?"

"You know the price."

"Say it."

"Wren — "

"*Say it.*"

"One soul," Joss said softly. "Yours. And in exchange — " the water swirled, and the faces rearranged, and Joss rose toward the surface until he was only an arm's length below her, close enough to touch if touching were possible, which it was not, which was the entire point — "in exchange, I go free. Whatever's left of me. It lets me go, whole enough to walk out of here, live whatever life a man has left after ten years inside a song. And your crew walks out guaranteed. Tam. Sef. Denna. Safe passage, safe water, safe lives. The current keeps its bargains, Wren. It's the only honest thing down here."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Wren sat very still on the black stone and did the arithmetic she'd been running from since the moment she'd understood what the carvings meant. Her knee ached. Two days without sleep sat behind her eyes like sand. Somewhere above, Tam was mapping channels with a boy's stubborn hope, and Sef was telling stories to keep Denna anchored, and Brann's fleet was coming with the next tide, and every single one of those facts pointed at the same answer.

Take the bargain. Save everyone. Stop.

God, how it wanted her to stop.

That was the part nobody warned you about — that surrender could feel like competence. That laying down a weight you'd carried so long it had grown into your spine could feel like the smartest decision of your career. She thought about sleep that went deeper than sleep. About never tracking another thief across another flat, never counting tides, never waking with her hand already on her knife. She thought about Joss walking out of the water, real, breathing, blinking in the sun, saying her name with his own mouth.

She thought about her crew guaranteed. *Guaranteed.* No more racing fleets. No more choosing who to leave behind.

"Yes," she heard herself say, and her voice sounded strange, calm, like someone else's. "All right. Show me where."

The water bloomed with light. The faces turned toward her — not hungry, nothing so simple as hungry, but *relieved*, the way a drowning man is relieved, and she understood suddenly that the current had been lonely for a very long time, and that loneliness was the most dangerous thing in the world, because it made the drowning feel like company.

Joss's face broke open. "Wren — wait — I didn't think you'd — " and there it was, flickering across his features, horror under the joy, because some part of him had known she'd say yes and hated that part of himself for knowing it. "There's a stair, under the east arch. You walk down and the water takes you gently, I promise, it doesn't hurt, it's like — "

"Like falling asleep," Wren said. "I know. I've been tired for eleven years, Joss. I know exactly what it's like."

She stood. Her legs held. She walked along the basin rim toward the east arch, and the water followed alongside her like a dog that had been promised a walk, and every step she took felt lighter than the last, and that lightness frightened her more than anything the current had ever shown her, because she could feel herself starting to *want* it.

At the arch she stopped. Just for a second. Just to look back at the basin one last time — professional habit, cataloguing terrain, noting exits — and in the water Joss was rising, rising, his hand breaking the surface, reaching for her, and behind him the other faces, and behind them, far down in the deep where the light didn't reach, something vast turning over in its sleep like a whale adjusting its bulk, the engine itself, the starving thing, waiting for its meal.

Her boot was on the first step. The stone was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything down here was warm.

"Wren!"

Tam's voice, distant, from the upper avenues — just a shout, probably nothing, probably he'd found the sensible tunnel at last — and it hit her like a thrown rope, and she caught it without deciding to, the way you catch a child falling, the body moving before the mind votes.

She stood on the stair with the water lapping at her ankles and realized her hand was shaking.

Not from fear. From the effort of holding still. Because some enormous pressure was pushing her forward, gentle as a tide, patient as a tide, whispering *rest, rest, rest* — and the only thing opposing it was a fifteen-year-old's voice echoing off wet stone, and the memory of a boy bumping her shoulder in the dark and saying *don't let it hold mine.*

"Joss," she said aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle. "If I do this — if I take your place — you'll be free. Truly?"

"Truly." His face was right below her now, beautiful and terrible. "Wren, please. Either decide. The waiting hurts us both."

Either decide. Yes. That was the whole disease of her life, wasn't it — either/or, run or die, chase or be caught, never once in thirty-one years considering that a person might simply stand still on her own terms and let the world come to *her*.

She looked at her hand on the stair rail. Ranger-sign was carved there, old and faded, put there by some nameless predecessor: a small mark meaning *hold fast*, which rangers joked meant *die slow*.

"No," Wren said.

The water went utterly silent.

"No," she said again, louder, prying her fingers off the rail one at a time, each one a small war. "Not like this. Not traded. You want to save these villages, Joss? Then help me save them *without* feeding anyone to it. There has to be another way in. There's always another channel — that's the first law of water, you taught me that, you smug — "

"Wren, there *isn't* — "

"There is." And she believed it, suddenly and completely, the way she'd never believed any bargain the current offered — because it was a harder belief, an inconvenient one, the kind you had to build with your own hands instead of being handed. "Brann's fleet arrives tomorrow. Her engineers will tear this place open looking for the intake systems, and intake systems have gates, and gates can be opened from the wrong side. Everything that drinks has a mouth, Joss. And every mouth — "

"— can be made to swallow something it shouldn't," Joss finished slowly. Ten years in the current and the ranger was still alive in him somewhere, working the problem. His face changed. "The sea gates. The old ones. They sealed them when they built the engine — open seawater would — " He stopped. Looked at her. Something kindled in his drowned face that she hadn't seen since he was nine years old and had just figured out how the tide worked. "Wren. If someone opened the deep gates from inside — "

"Then we'd better find the person who knows the way in," Wren said, stepping backward off the stair, away from the warmth, into the cold honest dark, "before Brann does."

Above them both, faint and getting fainter, the current began — very quietly — to scream.

And in the upper avenues, a boy who stole boats came running toward the sound, rope over his shoulder, map in his head, shouting her name.
