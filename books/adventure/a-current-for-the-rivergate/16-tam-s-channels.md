## 16. Tam's Channels

Tam had always believed that the best hiding places were the ones nobody wanted. That was the whole trick of being a river-orphan: you couldn't outfight anyone, you couldn't outrun the harbor watch, but you could fit into spaces that made sensible people wrinkle their noses and move along.

Which was why he was lying flat on his stomach in a flooded sewer.

Well — not a sewer, exactly. Denna's journal called it a "conduit gallery," which sounded much fancier than it deserved. It was a stone tunnel about as wide as Tam was tall, half-drowned in water so still it looked like black glass, running beneath the drowned avenues of the Rivergate like a vein under skin. The air tasted of salt and old stone and something else underneath, something faintly sweet that made the back of his teeth hum.

He hated it down here. He also, for the first time in his short and thoroughly dishonest life, felt important.

"Tam." Wren's voice came down the tunnel from behind him, low and even, the way she talked when she was worried but didn't want the worry to be contagious. "You've been quiet too long. Quiet means either you found something or something found you."

"Give me a second," Tam called back. "I'm having a revelation. They don't rush well."

He was crouched on a ledge of cracked masonry, holding Denna's journal open with one hand and his little brass compass with the other, and looking at the thing that had stopped him mid-sentence ten minutes ago.

The wall of the tunnel was carved.

Not decorated — *carved*, deep and deliberate, channels cut into the stone in patterns that branched and forked and forked again, like the roots of a tree, or like — and this was the part that made Tam's heart do a strange stumbling thing — like the tide-charts the orphans scratched into the pilings under Saltrow pier. The same shapes. The same logic. Narrow channels feeding wide ones, wide ones feeding the sea.

He'd grown up reading those charts. Every river-orphan had. It wasn't school; it was survival. You learned where the water went and when, because the difference between knowing and not knowing was the difference between eating and drowning.

And here it was, the same knowledge, cut into stone by people who'd been dead for a thousand years.

"Wren," he said, and his voice came out smaller than he meant it to. "You'd better come look at this."

* * *

They gathered around the carving by lamplight — Wren, Sef, Denna, and Tam himself, four damp people in a dripping tunnel, which Tam privately thought would make a terrible painting and an excellent story if they all lived.

Denna touched the grooves with two fingers, careful as a woman touching a sleeping animal. Her hands had steadied since the Asking, since Wren had hauled her back from the water's edge, but she still flinched when the current spoke, and Tam had noticed she'd started humming to herself when the silence got too long. He didn't blame her. The silence down here had opinions.

"It's a map," Denna said. "No — more than a map. It's a schematic. Look at the main channel." She traced the widest groove, which ran straight as a spear through the center of the wall and off into the dark, in the direction of the heart of the ruin. Toward the machine. "This is the intake. This is how the engine drinks."

"The engine drinks," Sef repeated. The old ferryman sat on an upturned crate he'd insisted on carrying three miles through flooded streets for exactly this kind of occasion. "That's a cheerful way to put it."

"That's the accurate way to put it." Denna turned pages with wet fingers. "The survey team worked it out before — before. The Rivergate didn't have springs. It didn't have rain worth mentioning. Everything this city drank, everything its fields drank, came up through these conduits from the open sea, filtered through the machine at the center. Fresh water out of salt water, enough for a hundred thousand people." She looked up. "The price was the people."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Down the tunnel, the black water made a sound like slow breathing.

"But here's what I can't work out," Denna went on, and her voice changed — sharpened. She was a surveyor again, briefly, standing upright inside her own ruin of a self. "The intake doesn't only take people. Look — the channels run all the way to the outer sea-gates here, and here, and here." Her finger jumped between grooves near the edge of the carving. "The machine draws from the sea itself. The people are — additive. A catalyst. But the flow, the actual current—" She stopped. Her eyes went wide, and Tam watched a thought arrive in her like weather. "The flow comes in through the sea-gates."

"So?" said Tam. Then he heard himself, and winced. "Sorry. Habit. Go on."

"So the gates are mechanical," Denna said. "Old, yes. Crusted, certainly. But mechanical. And there are maintenance ways into them — service tunnels, gate-rooms. Cities like this never built anything you couldn't climb inside of and fix. Which means—"

"Which means somebody could open them," Wren said quietly. "All the way. Let the raw sea come through, unfiltered, unmanaged. Flood the intake."

"Flood the engine," Tam said.

The words hung in the tunnel. Somewhere far off, deeper in the ruin, the current seemed to shift — a long, low movement in the water, like a sleeper turning over. Tam got the distinct feeling they'd just said something rude within earshot.

Sef cleared his throat. "Forgive an old man's arithmetic, but if we drown the engine, we drown the water. The clean water. The water half the river villages are praying for." He looked at each of them in turn. "Brann wants the machine working. We'd be making sure nobody ever gets it working. Including the people who need it honest."

"I know," Wren said.

"And the survey team," Denna added, very softly. "What's left of them. What's *in* it." Her hand went to the journal, gripping it like a rail. "My team. If the engine drowns—"

"Denna." Wren's voice was gentle, but it didn't waver. "We don't know yet. We're not deciding anything tonight. Tonight we find out if it's even possible." She turned to Tam, and there it was — the look. Not pity, not exactly trust either, more like a woman checking whether a rope would hold her weight. "These channels. The real ones, the living ones, the ones the orphans use. Do they connect?"

And Tam, who had spent his whole life insisting he belonged to no one and owed nothing to anybody, heard himself say:

"Yeah. I think they do."

* * *

Here was the thing about the Rivergate that no government survey and no salvage fleet commander would ever understand, because none of them had ever been small and hungry and invisible in a place like Saltrow: the ruins were not empty.

The river-orphans knew. For generations, kids who fell through the cracks of the harbor towns had been paddling leaky skiffs out along the dead sea's margin, following the old ferry-routes their older brothers and sisters had mapped in chalk and memory. The drowned city's upper stories broke the surface at low tide in a dozen places — rooftops, bell towers, the crown of some ancient customs house — and the orphans used them the way seabirds use rocks. Camps. Waystations. Pantry caches sealed in oilcloth against the salt.

And beneath those rooftops, connecting them, ran the channels.

Not the grand conduits — the orphans couldn't have survived a minute in those, with the current breathing through them like a throat. No, the little ways. The drainage runs, the overflow spurs, the gaps where the old stonework had settled and cracked and opened passages barely wider than a child. Grown-ups couldn't fit. Grown-ups hadn't tried in a thousand years. The maps existed only in the one place adults never thought to look, which was the heads of children nobody wanted.

Tam had run those passages at eleven, twelve, thirteen, back when he'd still believed the Rivergate was just a playground with good hiding spots, before the year the current started *asking*. The older orphans had a rule about it, passed down like everything else: never answer the water. Never tell it your name. Swim fast, stay shallow, get out before the tide turns.

He'd thought they were being superstitious. He'd been eleven. He'd had a lot of confidence in his own cleverness in those days.

Now he lay in a crack in the world barely wider than his shoulders, lamp strapped to his forehead, worming through a passage that smelled of brine and centuries, and every instinct he owned was screaming the old rule at him. *Never answer the water.* Because the water was talking. Not in words — it never used words, words were too honest — but in tugs. Little pulls against his ankles where it lapped at his boots. A suggestion of warmth where the cold should be. Once, unmistakably, a rhythm against the stone beside his ear, exactly like fingers drumming.

It wanted him to stop. It wanted him to turn around. It wanted, more than anything, to be introduced.

"Nice try," Tam muttered, and kept crawling.

The passage spat him out into a gate-room.

He knew what it was before his mind caught up with his eyes — the shape of it was in the wall-map, in Denna's journal, and in the oldest orphan rhyme of all, the one they sang skipping ropes on the Saltrow seawall: *under the roof where the waters meet, the iron mouth has iron teeth.* Kids sang it without knowing what it meant. Tam stood in it now, lamp held high, and understood that he was the first person in a thousand years to sing that song in the right key.

The room was round, maybe thirty feet across, domed above, and dominated by the gate itself: a circular barrier of interlocking plates, green with age, filling an opening tall enough to swallow a fishing boat whole. Beyond it, visible through the gaps between plates, moved the open sea — the true sea, the dead gray expanse outside the ruin, pushing patiently against the mechanism like a horse against a closed stable door. Machinery crowded the walls: counterweights, chains thick as Tam's waist, wheels and ratchets crusted in mineral bloom. And everywhere, threading through it all, thin veins of that sweet-humming water, seeping in from the intake side, whispering.

Tam stood very still and let the ranger training he'd absorbed by osmosis do its work. *Look first. Touch nothing. Count your exits.*

Three exits: the crack he'd come through, a stair going up into darkness, and a low service crawl on the far side that matched the second spur on the wall-map. Good. Two gates in this arm of the ruin, the map said — this one and another half a mile on, both feeding the same main conduit, both needing to open together or the flood would just choke itself in the narrow ways. He checked the wheel mechanism at the gate's hub. Seized? He put his hands on it, tested —

—and the water at his feet went perfectly, deliberately still.

The whole room held its breath. Even the seep-veins stopped whispering. And in that enormous, engineered silence, Tam felt the current notice him. Really notice. Not the way it noticed Denna, hungry and coaxing. Something more curious. More *careful.*

It felt, absurdly, like being looked at by a very large dog that had just realized the small creature in front of it knew where the gate latch was.

"Yeah," Tam told it, his voice steady in a way that surprised him. "That's right. I know your doors. My family's known your doors longer than your owners did. So here's the thing." He patted the seized wheel, once, like greeting a bad-tempered mule. "In a day or two, someone's coming through here who isn't me, and isn't friendly, and thinks you're a pump she can own. And when that happens, me and mine are going to open every door you have and let the whole cold sea in to wash you out. So you can fight us, or you can think real hard about whose fault all this is. Your call."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the walls, a chain shifted — settling, or thinking.

Then, softly, the water began to move again, and Tam could have sworn — absolutely sworn, though he'd deny it under oath — that the current pulled *away* from his boots. Giving him room. Like something stepping back.

"Huh," said Tam.

Then he turned around, because behind him, from the dark of the upward stair, came a sound he knew better than any sound in the world: the scrape of a boot. A boot wearing a boot, attached to a person, descending.

He killed his lamp and pressed into the shadow of the counterweight, heart hammering, and counted the steps. Heavy. Unhurried. Armored, by the ring of it. Fleet-issue stride — he'd listened to Brann's advance teams enough over the past two days to know their walk, the walk of people who had never once in their lives needed to be quiet.

Two of them. Maybe three. Coming down to check the gate — Brann's engineers, mapping the machinery ahead of the main fleet. Ahead of Tide Five.

Tam slid toward the service crawl, moving the way he'd learned to move before he'd learned to read, and as he went, one thought rang in his head bright and clear as a struck bell:

The fleet was already here. And the gates had to open *tomorrow.*

* * *

He told them everything, breathless, in the conduit gallery, while Sef poured hot tea from a battered flask and pretended his hands weren't shaking. The gate-room. The wheel — rusted but sound, the mechanism intact beneath the crust. The second gate half a mile on. The fleet scouts in the stairwell. The current stepping back.

"It moved for you," Denna said, staring. "It's never moved for anyone. It took my whole team."

"It didn't move *for* me," Tam said. "It moved *aside.* There's a difference." He wrapped his hands around the tea and found them steadier than he expected. "Look — the plan works. It works, that's the horrible part. Open both gates at the same tide, the sea comes through, the engine drowns. But the wheels are down there, in the dark, in its front hall, and the fleet's crawling all over the ruin now. Somebody has to be at each gate and turn them together, and hold them against the pressure until the sea does the rest, and then get out before—" He stopped. Everyone heard the end of the sentence anyway. *Before the water takes them.*

Wren was quiet for a long moment, watching him across the lamplight with an expression Tam couldn't read — and then could, and wished he couldn't, because it was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic with people.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the rest."

"There's no rest that ends with you in that room, Tam."

"Somebody small has to go," he said. "Those service crawls are orphan-sized. You'd wedge in the first five feet and be a very sad, very stuck ranger. Sef's shoulders are wider than the doorways of most churches. And Denna—" He gentled, because Denna had gone white. "Denna hears them calling. All of them. She'd never make it to the wheel."

"I could try," Denna whispered.

"You'd stop at the water," Tam said, not cruelly, just truly, "and I love that you want to, but we need somebody who runs *toward* the exit." He turned back to Wren. "I know those passages better than the current does. That's not bragging, that's inventory. I'm the fastest thing in this ruin that isn't water, and I'm the only one who fits. You taught me the rule yourself — everybody carries, everybody's useful, nobody's spare. Well." He spread his hands. "Turns out I'm the linchpin. I'd like it noted that I hate the word."

Wren looked at him for a long time. Behind her, Sef had gone very still, and Denna had pressed the journal to her chest, and the black water breathed its slow breath down the tunnel, waiting, the way it always waited, patient as arithmetic.

"You'll have help," Wren said at last, and her voice had gone hard and bright, the voice she used when she'd stopped debating and started planning. "Joss is in there. Half of him is the current, but the other half has been leaving warnings in ranger-sign for years — he's been fighting it from inside this whole time. If anyone can hold a gate open from the other side, buy you seconds, keep the water off your back—" She stopped herself, jaw tight. "If anyone can, it's him."

"Then we go at tomorrow's low tide," Tam said. "Tide Four's almost spent. We've got one after that, and then Brann's whole fleet comes through the front door."

"One tide," Wren agreed. "One chance."

Tam nodded, and drank his tea, and discovered that his fear had settled into something new — not gone, but organized. Filed. Useful. It was, he realized, the same feeling he'd had the first time he'd stolen a boat: terror and rightness in the same hull, heading for open water.

Above them, far away, the ruin shuddered as the tide began to turn — and somewhere below, behind a green-crusted iron mouth, the sea leaned its weight against the door, and waited to be let in.
