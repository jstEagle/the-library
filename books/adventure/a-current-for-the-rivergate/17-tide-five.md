## 17. Tide Five

The tide came in wrong.

Wren felt it before she heard it — a pressure in her back teeth, the same old ranger's warning that had kept her alive through flash floods and sandstorms and one memorable encounter with a breeding colony of salt scorpions. She was standing on the third terrace of the Rivergate with Tam's spyglass pressed to her eye, watching the horizon where the dead sea met a sky the color of old tin, and the water was rising without wind to push it.

"They're coming," she said.

Tam took the glass from her hands without asking. He'd earned that much in nine days — the right to take things from her without asking, within reason. He braced his elbows on the crumbling balustrade, squinted, and went very still in the way he did when he was pretending not to be scared.

"That's not six ships," he said.

"No."

"That's — Wren, that's a lot of ships."

"Fourteen," Wren said. "Fifteen if you count the little one hanging back like it's ashamed of itself." She held out her hand for the spyglass. "Brann's main force. She waited for the fifth tide so she'd have deep water all the way to the western breach." She turned the glass on the fleet and picked out the Korvane flagship by its scarred prow, black paint gone gray, a ram bolted on like an afterthought that had killed a lot of someones. "She's done being careful."

Below them, in the flooded avenue they'd started calling the Long Street because naming things made them less terrible, Sef was hauling their skiff up onto a marble landing with the unhurried movements of a man who had spent sixty years letting tides tell him what to do. Denna Okoro sat on the landing's edge with her boots off and her feet in the water.

Wren's stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

"Denna," she called down. "Out of the water."

Denna looked up. Her face was calm in a way that frightened Wren more than any screaming ever had. Nine days ago they'd found her half-mad on the salt flats, repeating *it asked me my name* like a prayer worn smooth from handling. Nine days of food and sleep and Tam's terrible jokes had put flesh back on her and even brought back pieces of the surveyor — the precise way she catalogued things, the dry humor that surfaced like fish. But every day she spent a little longer looking at the water. Every day the looking lasted a little longer after dark.

"I'm just cooling my feet," Denna said. "It's a hundred degrees in the shade of a building that's been dead for four hundred years. Let me have my feet."

"Feet," Wren said. "Fine. Ankles stay above the surface. Ranger's orders."

"You're not my ranger."

"I'm the only ranger you've got."

Something flickered across Denna's face — almost a smile, almost grief — and she pulled her feet up and wrapped her arms around her knees. Wren let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the heat.

* * *

They made their stand at the western breach because there was nowhere else to make it. The plan — if you could call three people and an old ferryman a plan — was Tam's, which meant it involved channels nobody else could see and a schedule nobody else could keep, and it would not be ready until the sixth tide. Which meant they had to survive the fifth.

"The intake runs under the whole eastern quarter," Tam said, scratching a map into the dust of the terrace with a bit of broken tile. He'd been doing this for two days, talking faster and faster, drawing the orphan's geography of the Rivergate from memory — channels his mother's generation had used to smuggle, hide, and occasionally drown rivals. "The machine drinks through these. But the deep gates — here, and here — they connect to the open sea cuts. The ones past the shelf. Nobody uses them because the current's too strong going in." He looked up at Wren, and for once there was no joke waiting behind his eyes. "If we open them, the sea comes through. All of it. Unfiltered. The machine can't sort that. It'll choke."

"And to open them," Sef said mildly, from where he was mending a net that had nothing to do with anything except keeping his hands busy, "someone has to be inside when they open."

Nobody answered him. Nobody needed to. They'd been circling that fact for two days the way gulls circle a gut pile.

"We don't know that yet," Wren said, which was a lie, and everyone knew it was a lie, and everyone let her say it anyway, because that was what families did — even accidental ones, even ones assembled out of a stowaway, a half-mad surveyor, and an old man who talked to nets.

That was when the first shell hit the outer wall.

* * *

The sound was enormous and strangely polite, the way catastrophe always is at first — a cough of thunder, a shiver through the stone, a rain of ancient plaster. Then the second shell came and the politeness was over.

Wren got everyone off the terraces and into the colonnade as the fleet closed. Through the arches she watched Brann's ships come through the western breach in a line, guns firing in relays, methodical as a threshing crew. Marines poured ashore in armored boats, maybe eighty of them, moving through the drowned streets in squads with the easy confidence of people who had never once considered losing.

"They're heading for the machine," Tam said. He'd found a gap between two columns and wedged himself into it, small enough that the stone seemed to accept him as part of the architecture. "Straight down the Long Street. They're not even searching."

"She doesn't need to search," Wren said. "She has the survey journal now." Because Brann did — taken off the captured engineer, or bought, or tortured out of someone; it didn't matter how. The journal that had led Wren's crew through the submerged markets was leading Brann's marines to the heart of the ruin, and there was nothing between them and it but water and distance, and Brann had brought enough guns to solve both.

A squad cut up the ramp toward the colonnade. Wren counted eight, then stopped counting, because counting didn't help. What helped was that she'd spent nine days learning this ruin's throat and they'd spent nine hours. She pulled Tam deeper into the colonnade's maze of fallen pillars, along a route only visible at low tide, and the marines' boots splashed past the turning they never saw.

Twice more they nearly collided with the invasion. Twice more the ruin itself hid them — a submerged stair, a roof beam that made a bridge, one long swim through a flooded gallery where Wren kept a grip on Tam's collar and Tam, breath held, kept a grip on something that looked like terror but functioned like courage.

They surfaced in the Hall of Names, the great vaulted chamber where the machine's hum came up through the floor like a heartbeat heard through a wall. And that was where they found Denna.

She wasn't supposed to be there. She was supposed to be with Sef at the skiff, inland, away. But there she stood at the chamber's far edge, boots off again, barefoot on wet stone, facing the sunken stair that led down into the glowing water. The light from below moved across her face in slow green pulses, and her expression was the calmest Wren had ever seen on a living person.

"Denna." Wren waded toward her, water sucking at her knees. Behind them, faintly, the shells kept falling, and boots echoed in distant corridors, and none of it mattered even slightly. "Denna, look at me."

"They're singing," Denna said. Her voice was steady, professional, the voice she must have used for field reports. "I thought it was madness, the first time. I thought I'd lost my mind out on the flats and everything after was fever. But it's not madness. It's them. Meridian team, call sign Ashfall — that's Okafor, that's Lindqvist, that's Reyes, that's—" Her breath caught. "That's all of them. They're all still in there. Still thinking. Still *them*. They've been asking for me for nine days and I've been telling myself it was the heat."

"It's the machine," Wren said. "It takes your memories and wears them like clothes. You told me that yourself. You wrote it in the margin of your own journal — *it lies beautifully.*"

"It does lie beautifully." Denna smiled, and the smile broke Wren's heart clean in half, because it was a real smile, the first fully real one since the flats. "But here's what I figured out, ranger, sitting on that landing with my feet in the water while you were all planning your brave plans. It doesn't have to be lying about everything. My team is in there. Whatever the machine is, whatever it does — they are still somewhere in it, and they have been alone with it for months, and I am the last person on this earth who knows their middle names." She turned her head and looked at Wren directly, and her eyes were clear, clearer than they'd been since the salt flats. "You know what that's like. Don't tell me you don't. You've got a brother in there. Sef told Tam; Tam can't keep a secret, bless him. You've been hearing him for days and pretending it's the wind."

Wren's throat closed. "That's different."

"It's exactly the same. That's why you understand." Denna stepped backward, one step, toward the stair. Water lapped her ankles. "I ran from it once. Out on the flats, when it asked my name — I ran until my feet bled, and it saved my life, and I have hated it every single day since, because running isn't living, Wren. Running is just dying slowly with better scenery." Another step. Water at her calves. "My team stopped. They chose it, or they accepted it, or somewhere in that machine they're still choosing it every day. And I have spent nine days letting other people decide whether I go to them."

"Denna—"

"You let me choose," Denna said. "That's all. That's the whole thing. Everyone else would grab my arm. You're the only one who knows what it costs to stop, so you're the only one who knows it should be a choice." She was crying now, and smiling, and the green light moved over her like a blessing. "Tell the boy his channels were a good idea. Tell the old man his net-mending is a lie and I loved it. And ranger —" her voice softened, "— when you hear your brother in there? Listen closer than I did. Some of what it says is true. That's the trap. That's also the mercy."

She walked down the stair.

Wren did not grab her arm. Every muscle she owned screamed to grab her arm; her hands actually moved, actually rose, and she forced them back down to her sides and stood in the flooding green light and watched Denna Okoro descend into the current with her chin up like a surveyor walking into a new country. At the third step the water took her waist. At the fifth it took her shoulders. At the last, she turned back once — just once — and mouthed something Wren couldn't read, and then the light folded around her and she was gone, and the hum of the machine changed pitch, one note richer, one voice added to the choir.

Wren stood there for a long time.

Tam came and stood beside her. He didn't say anything, which for Tam was a eulogy. After a while his hand found hers and gripped it, small and grimy and steady.

"You let her go," he said finally.

"Yeah."

"Was that right?"

Wren thought about every person she'd ever left behind, every camp struck before dawn, every goodbye conducted at a dead run so it wouldn't have time to become a wound. She thought about Joss, who had stopped, and about herself, who had never once in thirty years allowed it.

"I don't know," she said. "But it was hers. That has to count for something."

Above them, through the shattered roof of the Hall of Names, the sky filled with smoke, and the sound of Brann's boots came closer, and the tide kept rising.

And beneath the floor, beneath the water, beneath everything — the current spoke. Not to Denna anymore.

To Wren. And this time, it used her brother's voice, and it said her name.
