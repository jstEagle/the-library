## 18. The Gates Below

Joss Voss had forgotten, over the years, what it felt like to have hands.

He remembered the idea of them — callused palms, the scar across his left knuckle from a snare wire, the way Wren used to slap her palm against his shoulder when she laughed. But feeling was a thing the current took early and gave back only in pieces, like a miser counting out coins. Inside the engine he was mostly memory wearing the shape of a man, drifting in the warm dark with ten thousand other memories, all of them humming the same low note.

So when he felt fingers — real ones, cold and chapped and unmistakably his sister's — close around what used to be his wrist, it was like being struck by lightning made of grief.

"Wren," he said, and his voice came out of him whole for the first time in six years.

She was there. In the deep chamber beneath the Gate, where the machine's heart turned its slow terrible turns, she stood knee-deep in water that glowed faintly gold, and she was *looking* at him. Not through him. Not past him, the way the others had, the way Denna Okoro had wept at shapes that weren't quite her people anymore. She was looking at him the way you look at someone standing on a platform as your train pulls away.

"You're shorter than I remember," she said.

"You're uglier," Joss said, and then his voice cracked clean down the middle, because she wasn't, she was exactly the same, sunburned and squinting and stubborn as a burr, and he had spent six years being water and he would have given every drop of it just to hear her say something mean to him again.

* * *

The current did not like this meeting. He could feel it the way you feel a change in weather — a pressure behind the eyes of the whole ruin, a note in the hum shifting from lullaby toward warning. The current had brought her here, in its way. It had shown her his voice, his face, dangled him like bait on a hook, because the current always led with its best offer. But it had not counted on them actually *talking*. Talking was friction. Friction was the one thing the current couldn't dissolve.

"Listen to me." Wren gripped his arms — or the memory of his arms; her hands sank in up to the wrist, and it should have been horrifying but instead it felt like coming home to a house with the door left open. "There's a bargain. I know there's a bargain. It offered me — it said if I take your place, it lets you go."

"Don't." The word came out of him fast and hard, harder than anything he'd said in six years. "Wren. Don't you dare."

"It's my choice—"

"It's *not*." He pulled back, or tried to; the current tugged at him like a parent correcting a child's posture in church. "I've been in here, Wren. I know what taking means. You don't stop. That's the lie of it, the beautiful lie — it tells you you'll rest, you'll be part of something, you'll never run again. And it's true. You'll never run again because there won't be enough of you left to want to." He thought of the survey team, their faces surfacing now and then in the golden dark, smiling placid smiles at nothing. He thought of how long it had taken him to remember his own name, and how much longer to remember hers. "You'd be here, and you wouldn't even know to miss yourself."

Her jaw set. He knew that jaw. He'd seen it set against sandstorms, against bad contracts, against their father, God rest him, and once — memorably — against a camel.

"So what's your plan?" she said. "Stay? Let me walk out of here and spend the rest of my life knowing my brother is soup?"

"That was the plan, yeah."

"Terrible plan."

"It's the only plan I've had since I was twenty-two!"

* * *

And then she told him about the boy.

He felt it happen before he understood it — felt her *decide* to show him, deliberately, the way you'd hand someone a lamp in a dark room. She opened her memory of Tam like a book: a river-orphan, all elbows and attitude, who'd stowed away in her supplies and stolen a boat and talked his way into staying by knowing channels no map had ever recorded. A boy who'd looked at the drowned ruin everyone feared and seen, not a monster, but a plumbing problem.

"The intake," Wren said. "The machine drinks from the dead sea, filters, feeds the current. But Tam says the old builders ran emergency channels from the open ocean — floodgates, deep below, for when they needed to flush the system. The channels still connect. They're just gated."

Something in Joss went very still. Stillness, inside the current, was dangerous — stillness let it pour into you, fill the cracks, smooth you over. But he held it anyway.

"I know those gates," he said quietly.

"You do?"

"I mapped half of them. Years ago." He laughed, and the laugh came out strange, rippling, more water than sound. "I couldn't tell anyone. Every time I learned something true about this place, I carved it. Ranger-sign. You found them?"

"We found them. Took us long enough to read them." Her voice wobbled on the last word. "You were leaving warnings. All this time."

"I was leaving *everything*." Six years of it rose in him at once — the desperate carving sessions in the moments the current slept shallow, the terror of forgetting which direction down was, the slow horrible arithmetic of realizing that nobody was ever coming, until somebody came, until *she* came. "I thought I was writing letters to no one. Turns out I was writing to you. That's — huh." He blinked, or did the water-equivalent of blinking. "That's actually the best thing that's happened to me since I got eaten by a building."

"Glad my tracking skills could brighten your afterlife."

"Half-afterlife. Don't flatter me."

But underneath the banter — and the banter was load-bearing, it was the rope bridge they'd always crossed between them — something enormous was turning over. Because here was the shape of it, and he could see the whole shape now, the way you finally see the picture in one of those puzzles made of scattered dots:

The gates could be opened. The open sea could come through. The machine would drown in water too wild and too salt and too *alive* to harvest, and the current's hunger would choke on it.

And the gates could only be opened from inside.

From where he stood.

* * *

"No," Wren said.

He hadn't said anything yet. He was fairly sure he hadn't said anything yet. But she was already shaking her head, arms crossed, ranger stance, immovable as a survey marker.

"You were about to volunteer," she said. "You get this specific look. You've had it since you were nine and you volunteered us both to help shear Mr. Halloway's goats."

"They needed shearing, Wren."

"You *volunteered*, Joss. There's a difference between helping and volunteering, and the difference is that one of them is stupid." She stepped closer, and the gold light moved across her face. "Find another way."

"There isn't one. You think I haven't spent six years looking for another way? The gates are counterweighted. They need a body — a will, a *self* — holding them open while the sea comes through. Anything less and the current slams them shut before the first wave lands." He spread his hands, these almost-hands, these memories of hands. "It has to be someone inside. And I'm the only someone inside who remembers why it matters."

"Then we find someone else inside. Denna's team—"

"Denna's team are spoons, Wren." It was crueler than he meant, and gentler than it deserved. "They're still in there, somewhere, I can feel them, little warm spots in the dark. But they couldn't hold a gate open any more than they could hold a conversation. The current ate everything except the smiling." He softened his voice. "Me, it hasn't finished eating. Six years and it hasn't finished, because I kept carving, kept fighting, kept being too damn stubborn to dissolve. I'm the strongest thing in here that isn't the current itself. If anyone can hold a door against a flood, it's a Voss."

"That's exactly why it shouldn't be you!" Her voice cracked and echoed off the drowned walls, and somewhere above them, faintly, the hum stuttered. "Because you're the strongest thing in here! Because you're my brother, and I didn't cross a desert and hire a crew and befriend a boat thief and stare down a fleet commander just to trade one Voss for another! I am *done* trading Vosses!"

For a moment neither of them spoke. The water around them turned slowly, patiently, like something waiting for an argument to finish so it could resume its meal.

Then Joss did the bravest thing he had done in six years, braver than the carvings, braver than holding his name together syllable by syllable through all that golden dark.

He smiled at her.

"Wren," he said. "Come here. Look at me. Really look."

She came. She always came. That was the whole tragedy and glory of her — she never stopped moving except for family, and then she stopped like a stone drops.

"What do you see?" he asked.

"My brother."

"Look harder."

She looked. And he let her see it — really see it, opened himself up the way she'd opened Tam's memory to him. He let her see the edges of him gone soft, the places where Joss ended and current began, the long slow erosion of six years. He wasn't dying the way a man dies. He was dissolving the way sugar does, sweetening the water that swallowed him, and there was no version of any future where the process reversed.

"I'm already halfway gone," he said gently. "The bargain doesn't save me, Wren. Nothing saves me. The only question left is what these last pieces of me *do*." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Option one: I drift here, getting thinner every tide, until one day I'm a warm spot in the dark smiling at nothing, and you carry that out of here on top of everything else you carry. Option two—" and here his voice did crack again, and he let it, because some cracks deserve an audience— "option two, I open a door, and the last thing Joss Voss ever does is drown a monster and save a boy's plan and give his sister her life back. Those are the options. I've done the math. I'm better at math than you, you once traded a rifle for a hat."

"It was a *very good hat*."

"It was a hat with a feather, Wren."

She laughed. It came out broken, but it came out, and he held onto the sound of it the way a drowning man holds a spar, because he knew — he understood with sudden perfect clarity — that this was the last new sound of her he would ever keep. He wanted to keep it well.

"You'll hold the gates," she said at last, "and the sea comes through, and then what happens to you?"

"Honestly? Best case, the flood tears me loose. Worst case—" he shrugged, a gesture he had to reconstruct from memory, and it felt wonderful, like finding an old coat still fits— "worst case I go out loud. Screaming, defiant, extremely rude to the current. Either way I'm not soup. I'm a door slammed in its face."

"And I'm supposed to just swim out and leave you."

"You're supposed to swim out and leave me," he agreed, "and then live so loudly it makes the whole desert jealous. Take care of the boy. He steals things — feed him regularly and he'll steal less. Be nice to the ferryman. And Wren—" he caught her hands, both of them, poured everything remaining into the grip— "*stop running.* That's all I ever wanted for you. You've been running since the day I left, and I have watched you from in here, do you understand? I've *watched*. Every year you went faster and carried more and stopped less, and I carved warnings into walls hoping one of them would say what I actually meant, which was never *beware the Rivergate*. It was *go home, little sister. Rest. You were never supposed to survive alone.*"

She was crying. He was fairly sure he was too, though crying worked differently when you were partly water; it was more of a general agreement between himself and the room.

"Sixth tide's coming," he said. "I can feel it turning. And there's a fleet coming with it — Brann's main force, I felt them hit the outer channels an hour ago. So whatever you're going to do, you'd better go do it, and you'd better do it fast, and you'd better let me do this one thing, because it's the first useful thing I've managed in six years and I will *not* have it taken from me by my baby sister's feelings."

"My feelings are load-bearing," she muttered.

"I know. Always have been." He pressed his forehead to hers, or the memory of it, and for one moment they were eight and eleven again under a single blanket during a dust storm, arguing about whose turn it was to stay awake and watch. "Go on, Wren. Open the sky for me. I'll hold the doors."

* * *

She went. Of course she went. She was Wren.

But at the edge of the chamber she turned back one last time, silhouetted in the gold-lit water, and called out: "Hey, Joss."

"Yeah?"

"When this is over — if there's any of you left floating around — you come find me. You hear me? Any of you. I'll be the one sitting still for once. Impossible to miss."

"I'll come haunt you," he promised. "Rudest ghost in the territory."

And she laughed, and was gone, climbing toward the light and the noise and the tide that was turning, and Joss Voss turned to face the deep gates of the machine that had eaten him, and set his feet, and reached for the counterweights with hands that were mostly memory and entirely willing.

Above him, far above, the first guns of Commander Brann's fleet began to fire.
