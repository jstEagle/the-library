## 19. Sixth Tide

The water went still.

Not calm — Wren had grown up on the flats and knew every kind of calm the desert could fake. This was different. Every channel in the drowned city stopped moving at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in. The black current that had been threading through the flooded avenues for six tides pulled itself taut, like a wire about to sing.

"Tam." Her voice came out steadier than her hands felt. "How long?"

The boy crouched at the edge of the intake basin, fingers spread on the wet stone the way he read water — not looking at it so much as listening through it. "Gates are turning," he said. "Deep ones. I can feel them in my teeth." He grinned, quick and terrified and fifteen years old all at once. "That's either very good or we're all about to be soup."

"Sef?"

The old ferryman had wedged himself against a broken archway above the basin, one hand on his rope, the other pressed flat over his heart like a man keeping his own door shut. "The river's holding its breath," Sef said. "I've seen it do that twice in sixty years. Both times, something drowned."

Below them, down the throat of the machine, Joss was opening the gates.

Wren couldn't see him. She hadn't been able to see him since the current took her down into the engine's heart and gave him back to her half-made of water and memory — his face recognizable, his edges wrong, his voice coming from everywhere the way the current's voice did. *Open the deep gates,* she'd told him. *Tam's channels. Flood it with open sea.* And he'd looked at her with their mother's eyes wearing the current's light, and he'd said, *You came back for me.*

*I never left,* she'd said, which was a lie, and he'd known it was a lie, and he'd smiled anyway, because that was what Vosses did for each other.

Now the whole ruin was waiting on him.

* * *

It started as a sound.

Wren had heard the current speak for days now — not words, exactly, but offers. It had worn her brother's voice first, then her own, then Denna Okoro's lost survey team, then nothing at all, just a warmth like a fire in a cold house, like rest, like permission to finally stop running. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever refused.

But this sound was not an offer.

It began low, under the stone, under the sea, a note so deep she felt it in the soles of her boots before she heard it in her ears. Then it climbed. The water in the basin shivered into rings. Loose salt on the steps around them skittered and danced. Somewhere far below, iron groaned — gates older than any nation grinding open on hinges that had waited centuries for a hand brave enough or foolish enough to turn them.

"That's him," Tam whispered. "That's Joss doing it."

"How can you tell?"

"Because it sounds angry," Tam said, "and everything else down there sounds *polite.*"

Wren almost laughed. It caught in her chest and came out as something between a laugh and a sob, and she let it, because there wasn't time to be embarrassed.

The scream built. That was the only word for it. The current — the patient, ancient, hungry thing that had swallowed a survey team and a civilization and, piece by piece across years, her brother — the current opened whatever passed for its mouth and screamed. The water in the basin stood up in spikes. Crustacean-things that had been drifting in the flooded market streets below burst apart. Overhead, the ruined dome of the great hall cracked along seams no one had known were there, raining white dust into the black water.

"It knows," Sef said. His voice was very quiet. "It knows what's coming."

"Good," Wren said.

She meant it. She had spent her whole life believing that survival meant never stopping, never turning your back, never giving anything a fair chance to take you. But standing on the rim of the machine while it screamed, she found she didn't feel like prey. She felt like a ranger who had finally cornered the thing that had been hunting her people for generations — except the trap wasn't hers. It belonged to a boy who stole boats and a brother who had traded himself away to buy warnings in carved stone, and all she had done was refuse to say yes.

Sometimes refusing was the whole job.

* * *

The open sea hit the engine like a hammer made of weather.

They saw it before they heard it — far out past the drowned avenues, where the dead sea met the last standing walls of the Rivergate, the horizon moved. Not a wave. A *wall*. Gray-green and white-maned, full of fish and kelp and cold honest salt, water that had never asked anyone's name, pouring down the channels Tam had mapped from memory and mischief and the orphan's education of a hundred hidden ways through a drowned city.

"The third gate's jammed!" Tam shouted over the roar. "No — no, wait — it's turning — *he's got it*—"

The wall arrived.

Water came through the intake basin like the world ending in the good way, the way floods were supposed to work — indiscriminate, mindless, alive. It hit the black current head-on, and where they met, the water turned white, then gray, then a churning brown that looked, for one strange moment, like an ordinary river in spate. Like the Rivergate doing what it had been built to do, ten centuries ago, before its builders decided a living current was cheaper than a working one.

The scream changed pitch. It went from anger to something worse: confusion. The current had spent a thousand years being the only voice in the dark. Now the dark was full of ocean, and ocean didn't listen, didn't bargain, didn't ask names. Ocean just poured.

Then the scream became many voices.

Wren heard them all at once and wished she hadn't. The survey team — Meridian's seven, taken whole, still conscious inside the mechanism the way Denna had sworn they were. The old builders, generations of them, the citizens who had been fed to their own water-technology until the civilization ran out of people to trade. And underneath all of it, threaded through it, familiar as a scar: *Joss.*

He wasn't screaming. He was singing. She couldn't make out the words over the flood, but she knew the tune — their mother's road-song, the one rangers sang on the flats to keep count of each other in a dust storm. He was singing it the way you'd sing a door open, or a door shut, or a goodbye.

"Hold on to something!" Sef bellowed, and then the floor of the great hall tilted, and the world became noise.

* * *

Afterward, Wren would never be able to say how long the drowning of the engine took. A minute. An hour. Time went soft and shapeless, the way it did in dust storms, when all you could do was keep your grip and keep counting the people beside you.

She counted. Tam, both hands locked in the rope Sef had rigged, yelling something joyful and profane. Sef, praying to a river god Wren was fairly sure he'd invented, in a dialect Wren was certain he had. Denna Okoro was gone — walked into the current at the fifth tide, chosen, and Wren had let her go, and she did not regret it, and she would carry the weight of it forever, and both of those things were true at once. That was another thing stopping had taught her, in the end: grief and rightness could share a bed without killing each other.

The screaming thinned. The voices frayed. One by one — or maybe all at once; the water made liars of ears — the taken went quiet. Not silenced. *Released.* She felt it happen the way you feel a knot come undone in your own shoulders: a loosening, a long exhale, the specific silence of something that has been holding on finally letting go.

And then the current's voice, the true one, the deep patient hunger under everything, spoke one last time.

It didn't use words. It used the feeling of her brother's hand on her shoulder when she was nine and had fallen behind on a march and been too proud to say so. It used the smell of their mother's tent. It used the wordless arithmetic of *stay, and none of this ever has to hurt again.*

"No," Wren said, out loud, to a flooding room, because some refusals deserved witnesses.

The voice faded. Not angrily. Almost gratefully, if a hunger could be grateful — the way a held breath is grateful to be released, even if releasing it means the end of the holder.

The water went clear.

* * *

She found him forty feet down, in what had been the engine's heart and was now just a flooded chamber with a dead machine in it, gears stilled, channels empty, the great intake throats yawning open and harmless as caves.

The open sea had scoured everything. The black threads of the current were gone. In their place lay things the flood had carried back: bones, mostly, old ones, and instruments — the survey team's gear, returned at last to within arm's reach of where it should have been abandoned — and, tangled in a drift of pale silt against the machine's flank, a man.

Wren went down after him before her brain finished voting on the idea. The water was cold and clean and ordinary, and it pushed at her like water does, no more, no less, and she thought, distantly, *this is the first time in weeks the water has only wanted to drown me,* and that felt almost like a kindness.

His body was whole. That was the thing she hadn't dared hope for. Half-dissolved, the current had shown him to her — edges wrong, skin like smoke. But the flood had given him back solid. Human. Thin, sunken-cheeked, years too old for the brother she'd lost, dressed in rags that might once have been a ranger's coat. There were carvings on his forearms — hundreds of them, the ranger-sign of their family, cut into his own skin over years, the same signs he'd been leaving on the ruin's walls. Messages home. *I'm here. I'm awake. Don't come.*

She'd come anyway. He'd have done the same for her. It was the family disease.

She hauled him up through the water, arm hooked under his chest, kicking for the gray light above, and she did not let herself think anything at all, because thinking was for later, and later was a country she might never be able to afford to visit.

Her hands broke the surface. Tam was there — how was Tam always *there*, the boy had the instincts of a dock rat and the loyalty of one too — hauling on her collar, on Joss's, screaming for Sef, and together the three of them dragged the body onto the wet stone of the basin rim.

He wasn't breathing.

Of course he wasn't breathing. He'd been part of a river for six years. Rivers don't breathe.

"Joss." She knelt over him. Her hands, which had never shaken tracking a thief across forty miles of open flat, shook now. "Joss Voss, you open your eyes, you absolute — you *idiot* — you stayed, you stayed for six years carving warnings into rock, and you don't get to stop now that it's *over*—"

"Ranger," Sef said gently, behind her. "Ranger, the tide's gone out of him. Some folk the river gives back and some—"

"I know how rivers work, Sef."

She started compressions anyway. Because she was a ranger, and rangers didn't stop, and for the first time in her life she understood that the rule had never really been about survival. It had been about this. About not quitting on someone while there was still a body to fight for.

One. Two. Three. Tam counted with her, voice cracking, getting the numbers wrong and starting over. Fourteen. Fifteen. The flood-water lapped at their knees, gentle now, ordinary, going about its business of being the sea.

On twenty-eight, Joss Voss coughed up half the dead sea and took a breath that sounded like a rusted gate opening.

Wren sat back on her heels and discovered she was laughing. Or crying. The distinction had stopped mattering somewhere around the fifteenth compression, and Tam was shouting, and Sef had both hands raised to his invented god, and Joss lay on the stones blinking up at the cracked dome with eyes that were only his own — tired, human, unlit by anything but daylight.

"You're heavy," Wren told him. Her voice was wrecked. "Six years in a river and you're still heavier than you look."

"Muscle," Joss rasped. "You should — see the other guy." He coughed. He looked past her, at the flooded hall, the dead machine, the clean gray-green water moving through it slow and easy, going nowhere, asking nothing. "Did it work?"

"The current's gone," Tam said. He was grinning and crying and dripping, and he had never looked more like he belonged anywhere. "Whole thing's just seawater now. Boring old seawater." He paused. "Sir."

"Don't call me sir," Joss said. "You're the one who saved us. I just turned a crank."

"He turned six cranks," Wren said. "Underwater. While screaming."

"That was the current screaming."

"You were harmonizing."

Joss laughed — a real laugh, rusty and disbelieving — and then winced and put a hand to his ribs, and Wren caught his arm, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because there are moments language is too small for, and brothers know to leave those alone.

* * *

They carried him up through the drowned streets as the sixth tide drained out of the city, and the city, for the first time in living memory, let them go.

Behind them, the Rivergate was coming apart. With the current dead, whatever had held the ruin's bones in patient suspension went with it. Arches folded. Towers leaned and sat down into the water with groans like tired animals. Whole avenues slumped beneath the surface, and the sea poured in to fill the gaps, indifferent and generous, until the Rivergate was simply a reef with ambitions.

Out on the dead sea, the Korvane fleet was running.

Wren stood on the highest surviving wall with Joss leaning on her shoulder and watched them go — the salvage ships turning hard, cranes swinging loose, barges cutting wakes toward the horizon. Brann's flagship held formation longest. Wren imagined the commander on her deck, watching a thousand-year-old prize collapse into the shallows, doing cold arithmetic on a loss no ledger had prepared her for. For one moment the flagship's rail glittered with figures — small, distant, motionless. Watching the ruin die. Watching, maybe, for survivors on the wall.

Then the flagship turned, and the fleet was a smudge, and the smudge was gone.

"They'll be back," Joss said quietly. "People like that come back."

"I know." Wren watched the water where the ships had been. "But they'll come back to a ruin with nothing in it. And we'll be the ones who know what's buried here, and what it cost, and who paid." She looked down at her brother — gaunt, ragged, breathing, *alive*, impossibly, unfairly, gloriously alive. "Besides. We've got a boy who knows every channel in this sea, an old man who lies beautifully, and a ranger who's very hard to lose."

"You lost me for six years."

"You came back," Wren said. "That counts double."

Below them, Tam was already rigging a litter for the descent, arguing cheerfully with Sef about knots, and the evening tide came in clean and slow over the drowning ruin, and somewhere out beyond the horizon, Commander Ilsa Brann was turning her fleet toward the river villages — and toward everything Wren loved that wasn't standing on this wall.

Wren tightened her grip on her brother's shoulder and started planning.
