## 20. Ash and Salt

Ilsa Brann had commanded the Korvane salvage fleet for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once sat on the ground.

There were reasons for this. Commanders sat on chairs, on thrones of salvaged teak, on the high decks of ships named for dead cities. Sitting on the ground was what happened to people who had lost things — and Brann had made a career out of never losing anything she couldn't take back.

Now she sat on the ground at the edge of the Rivergate ruin, her back against a salt-crusted piling, one boot gone entirely, her left arm bound in a sling made from a signal flag that had, until two days ago, flown from the prow of the *Korvane's Mercy*. The Mercy itself was aground three hundred meters out in the shallows, listing like a drunk leaning on a fence, its engine flooded with seawater that had come through channels no chart had ever recorded.

She was watching Wren Voss dig a grave.

That was the thing Brann could not stop watching. Not the ruin — though the ruin deserved watching, its drowned towers rising from the dead sea like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and changed its mind halfway. Not her fleet, scattered and humbled along the shoreline, crews hauling what was left of their salvage gear onto the flats. She watched Voss, who dug with the same relentless economy Brann had seen in her tracking, in her fighting, in everything the woman did — as if stopping would cost her more than the work did.

Except now, apparently, it didn't. Now Voss stopped every few minutes. Looked at the sky. Said something to the boy — Tam, the boat-thief — who laughed, actually laughed, standing ankle-deep in grave-dirt like it was nothing.

Brann looked away and took inventory instead. That was safer. Inventory always was.

*Ships:* nine of fourteen seaworthy. *Crew:* two hundred and six alive, four taken by the current inside the ruin, their names already chalked on the *Mercy's* hull because Korvane did not leave its dead unwritten even when there was nothing left to bury. *Water:* enough in the casks for six days, if they rationed hard. *Salvage claim:* worthless, because the water-technology at the heart of the Rivergate was now a drowned, screaming, silent wreck of an engine, and because the woman who'd broken it held the government contract, and because Brann's own engineers kept looking at her with that expression she hated most — pity.

"Commander."

Her first officer, Dree Halloway, stood over her with a cup of reed tea and the careful blankness of a woman who had watched her commander argue with a ranger, lose half a fleet, and get told off by a seventeen-year-old in the space of forty-eight hours.

"You're supposed to be resting," Dree said.

"I am resting."

"You're glaring at a gravesite."

"That *is* how I rest."

Dree handed her the tea anyway and sat down beside her without asking permission, which was either insubordination or loyalty, and after eleven years Brann had stopped trying to tell the difference. They drank in silence. Out on the flats, Voss lowered the survey team's fourth body into the earth — they'd found them over two days, drawn up from the flooded streets by divers working Tam's channels, carried out wrapped in sailcloth. Five of them. Okoro had been the sixth, and Okoro was still alive, sitting apart near the fire with a blanket around her shoulders and an expression Brann recognized from mirrors: the look of someone doing arithmetic about everything they'd lost.

"They're burying them with names," Dree said quietly. "Full names. Ranks. Where they were from. The boy's reading them out."

"I can hear him."

"The tall one — Vashti Meren, hydrologist, second grade. He's making jokes about it. About the names." Dree paused. "The families will want to know. When we get word back to Korvane."

Brann said nothing, because the word *Korvane* sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Her city. Her dying, thirsty, proud, stupid, magnificent city, three hundred leagues east across the salt, whose wells had been dropping a hand's width every year while its council argued about whose fault it was. She had come to the Rivergate to steal a miracle. She had told herself it was for Korvane. Some nights she had even believed it.

And now the miracle was dead at the bottom of a flooded engine, killed by open seawater and a ranger who refused every bargain ever offered to her, and Korvane was still thirsty, and Ilsa Brann was sitting on the ground with one boot.

"Commander," Dree said again, gentler. "The villages sent word. The river settlements — Sef's people. They're offering water."

Brann turned her head slowly. "Offering."

"Their old wells. The ones upriver past the silt-flats. Sef came across this morning with two elders. They say the wells run deeper than anyone mapped — fed by something under the ruins, clean since the current went quiet. Not enough for a city." Dree hesitated. "Enough for a fleet, if we share. Enough to get home and hold through the dry season, maybe longer, if the villages keep drawing and we keep hauling."

"And what do they want for it?"

"That's the thing, Commander. They didn't ask for anything. Sef said—" Dree's mouth twitched, the ghost of a smile fighting through professional gravity. "Sef said, *tell your commander the well doesn't keep accounts, and neither do we, but the ferry does, so bring coin for the crossing like everyone else.*"

Brann stared at her. Then, despite everything — despite the fleet, the dead, the stone in her chest — she laughed. It came out rusty, like an engine turning over after a long winter, and it hurt her ribs, and she laughed anyway.

"A toll," she said. "They're charging me a *ferry toll.*"

"Half a mark per head. Sef says orphans ride free, and he's decided you qualify."

*

The negotiation — if it could be called that — happened at midday by the fire, and it was the strangest negotiation of Brann's career, chiefly because the other side barely seemed to realize they were negotiating.

Wren Voss sat across the flames with her forearms on her knees, and next to her the old ferryman Sef, who looked like a piece of driftwood that had learned to talk, and next to him the boy Tam, who kept eating dates out of a pouch and offering them around as if they were all friends, which was either genuine warmth or the most sophisticated interrogation technique Brann had ever encountered.

"The claim's void," Voss said, without preamble. "Your government writ died when the survey team did. Mine covers recovery, not salvage rights, and there's nothing left down there worth the paper anyway. But the wells aren't mine to give or yours to take. They belong to the villages."

"I'm aware of how ownership works, Ranger."

"Are you? You flew a war fleet at a ruin full of ghosts to take water from people who drink out of ditches."

"We brought pumps," Brann said evenly. "And doctors. And a plan."

"You brought arithmetic." Voss's eyes were pale and steady and very tired. "I've seen your kind of arithmetic before, Commander. It always comes out with somebody divisible."

The fire popped. Tam offered Brann a date. She took it mostly out of sheer bewilderment.

"What I brought," Brann said, "was a city with ninety thousand people in it and wells going dry. You want me to apologize for that?"

"No. I want you to *count differently.*" Voss leaned forward. "Here's my offer, and it's the only one. Your engineers are good — better than good, I watched them work the pumps at the gate. You leave four pump-rigs and two engineers at the river villages for a year. Teach the well-keepers the maintenance, help them deepen the cisterns. In exchange, the villages grant Korvane drawing rights — shared, capped, written down where both sides can read it. Your ships haul water home. Nobody owns anybody."

Brann turned the date over in her fingers. "You're giving away your leverage. I could take the wells."

"You could try." Sef said it mildly, the way a man comments on weather. "My granddaughter's husband is a well-keeper and a worse shot than me, which is saying something, because I'm eighty-one and blind in one eye and I still put a bolt through a gull at forty paces last spring. Also there's forty rangers in those hills who owe Voss favors, and also—" he gestured with his pipe at the flats, at the grounded hulks of her fleet, "—you don't have a boot."

Brann looked down at her bare foot in the sand. "That's classified information."

"It's very visible information," said Tam.

And that was when Brann understood the shape of the thing being offered to her, and it nearly undid her more than the loss of the fleet had. Because Voss wasn't giving away leverage. Voss was handing her a way to save face — a deal structured so that Korvane got water, and engineers, and a treaty, and could go home telling the council that Commander Brann had secured the city's future through hard negotiation rather than crawling to fishing villages with her hat in her hands. It was mercy dressed up as commerce. It was the single most humiliating generous thing anyone had ever done to her.

"I'll want the cap raised in wet years," Brann said, because her pride required her to haggle over something.

"You'll get it. And in drought years the villages draw first, and your fleet waits its turn, and your engineers keep the rigs running anyway."

"Agreed." Brann stuck out her hand. Voss took it. Her grip was dry and strong and brief, a handshake like closing a door gently.

"One more thing," Voss said, not letting go yet. "The survey team. Their families get the truth. All of it — what the machine was, what it asked, how they died. No tidy story. They earned the ugly version."

For a moment Brann saw it again: the intake hall, the figures in the mechanism, faces smooth and dreaming, conscious in whatever way the current had let them stay conscious. Three of her own crew had walked into that light with their arms out. She had ordered the advance team in knowing the risk and calling it acceptable.

"They'll have it," she said. "In writing. Under my seal."

Voss released her hand. "Then we're done."

"That's it?" Tam said, around a mouthful of date. "No shouting? I was promised shouting. Sef bet me a knife there'd be shouting."

"There's still time," Brann said, and the boy grinned at her like she'd given him a present, and she found, to her considerable alarm, that she almost smiled back.

*

They buried the survey team at sunset, all five of them, in a row above the tide line where the salt grass grew in stubborn tufts. Brann went, and brought her officers, and made attendance fleet-wide, because Korvane honored its dead even when the dead weren't Korvane's — especially then, maybe, because it was cheaper than honesty.

Tam read the names. He'd written them out fair on a board in careful, crooked letters, and he read each one twice — once plain, and once the way, he explained solemnly, "river-orphans do it, so the water knows them and lets them rest": name, trade, home, and a wish for the road.

"Ondrey Vashti Meren. Hydrologist, second grade, from the Terraset terraces. A straight channel and sweet water behind you."

"Pell Amara Osei. Instrument-maker, from Lower Harrow. A straight channel and a warm fire waiting."

One by one. Brann stood at attention with her officers behind her and listened to a barefoot teenager bless five strangers in a language of ditches and ferries, and felt something in her chest shift — not break, nothing so dramatic, just settle, like cargo lashing down after a storm.

Okoro stood at the foot of the graves. She had insisted on walking there herself, waving off help, and when the reading was done she knelt and pressed her palm flat to the turned earth of each mound in turn, and Brann heard her say, very quietly, "You can stop now. All of you. You can stop."

Afterward, in the blue dark, Brann found Voss at the waterline, skipping stones badly into the shallows. The ranger threw like she tracked — all force, no finesse — and every stone sank with an indignant plunk.

"Terrible," Brann said, sitting down beside her. On the ground again. She was starting to get used to it, which worried her.

"I'm aware."

"Elbow's too high."

"Is that salvage-fleet expertise or are you just saying things?"

"My father fished the Korvane delta before the council taxed the boats out of existence. I skipped stones before I could write." Brann picked up a flat one, tested its weight. "He said the trick is you don't throw *at* the water. You throw *along* it. Let it carry."

She skipped the stone. Four hops, five, a clean bright line out across the darkening sea.

Voss watched it go with an expression Brann couldn't read. "Huh," she said finally, and picked up another stone, and adjusted her elbow, and got three hops, and looked so briefly, fiercely pleased with herself that Brann had to look away at the horizon to preserve both their dignities.

"The engineer you're lending us," Brann said. "Halloway volunteers. She's the best I have."

"I know. She asked Voss—asked *me* yesterday. Word travels."

"Nothing travels faster than a woman escaping my command." Brann stretched her bad arm carefully. "Ranger. One question, and then I'll spare you my company. The bargain the current offered you. Your brother's freedom, water for your people, your place in the machine. Why refuse? Don't give me the speech about counting differently. I wrote that speech myself, years ago, and I never once believed it."

Voss was quiet long enough that a wave came in and went out.

"Because it wasn't a price," she said at last. "It was a trade. And everything the current ever traded for, it kept. My brother's been part of it for eight years, Commander, and he was *still in there*, still himself enough to warn people, still himself enough to choose — and the whole time it called that survival." She turned the stone over in her hand. "I've spent my whole life moving because I thought stopping meant dying. The current just said it out loud. And I finally understood that everything I'd been running toward was the same trap, just with better scenery."

She threw the stone. Six hops. She didn't gloat, which somehow made it worse.

"So no," Voss said. "I didn't refuse a price. I refused to be *paid*."

Brann looked out at the black water, at the ruin's towers standing patient under the stars, at the lights of her battered fleet strung along the shore like a necklace with half its stones missing. Ninety thousand people in Korvane, and wells going down a hand's width a year, and here was the answer, and the answer was: share, and stay, and count differently, and be paid in nothing but the company of people you never meant to keep.

It was the worst business model she had ever heard.

"First convoy leaves in three days," Brann said, standing, brushing sand from her one boot. "Halloway's rigs, your treaty, my seal. And Ranger — when the wells are sunk and the cisterns are deep and Korvane is drinking water that didn't cost anyone their name — I intend to come back here and skip stones until I beat six hops."

"Won't happen."

"Everything's negotiable, Voss."

"Not that."

But she was almost smiling, and so, horribly, was Brann, and they parted at the firelight's edge like two people who might, in some unimaginable future, be something adjacent to friends.

*

Brann slept that night in a hammock slung between two pilings, because her cabin aboard the *Mercy* was knee-deep in seawater and her dignity was knee-deep in something else entirely.

Near dawn she woke to voices — low, easy, unguarded. She lay still and listened. At the fire, Voss and the boy and the old ferryman and the mad surveyor who was becoming steadily less mad, passing a pot of tea around, arguing cheerfully about which village to visit first, whether Sef cheated at cards (he did; everyone agreed), whether Tam was allowed to steer the ferry or only pretend to. Okoro was laughing at something. Actually laughing.

Nobody was talking about leaving. That was the strange part. Brann knew travelers — she employed two hundred of them — and travelers always talked about the next port, the next job, the getting-gone. These people talked like the staying was the plan.

*Enough, if shared,* the villages had said. Brann turned the phrase over as the sky went grey, then gold. Enough, if shared. It shouldn't have worked. Arithmetic said it couldn't. But the wells ran deeper than anyone had mapped, and the current was quiet, and somewhere out on the flats a ranger who never stopped had finally stopped, and the world had failed to end.

Brann closed her eyes. For the first time in eleven years, Commander Ilsa Brann of the Korvane fleet fell asleep on the ground, unguarded, in enemy territory, and was not afraid.

Three days later, when the first water convoy stood ready and Halloway embraced her commander and went over the side to the villages without looking back, Sef ambled up the gangplank, pressed half a mark into Brann's palm — "crossing fee, refundable if you behave" — and told her there was news from upriver.

"News," Brann said.

"Mm. The well-keepers say the springs under the ruins are rising. Clean water, coming up stronger every week since the gates opened." The old man smiled with all his remaining teeth. "Seems the Gate isn't finished giving. Seems it wants to be *known* now, not feared. Mapped. Guarded. There's folk asking who'll teach the new guides, and who'll train rangers to walk the shore and watch the tides." He tipped his hat. "Thought your ranger might want to hear it. Thought she might be needing a reason to stay."

Brann looked down the beach to where Wren Voss stood at the tide line with the boy beside her, both of them skipping stones, neither of them in any hurry at all.

"Well," Brann said, pocketing the half-mark. "Isn't that interesting."
