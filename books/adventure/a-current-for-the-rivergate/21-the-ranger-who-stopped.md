## 21. The Ranger Who Stopped

The trouble with stopping, Wren discovered on the third morning at the river villages, was that her body didn't believe in it.

She woke before dawn the way she always did — one breath from sleep to standing, hand already finding the knife under her bedroll — and then stood there in the dark of Sef's guest room like a fool, listening to chickens she had no reason to chase.

*No contract,* she told herself. *No tide clock. No fleet.*

Her feet didn't care. Her feet had opinions that predated contracts.

She gave up, dressed, and went out into the grey light, where she found Tam already awake on the ferry dock, skipping stones across the river with a technique that could only be described as enthusiastic rather than effective.

"You're up early," he said.

"I'm always up early."

"Yeah, but you're usually *going* somewhere when you do it." He lobbed another stone. It skipped twice and sank with dignity. "You've been pacing the dock since yesterday. Sef says you're wearing a groove in his planks."

"Sef should be grateful. His planks were getting soft anyway."

"He says that too. He says it louder every time you walk by."

Wren sat down beside him on the edge of the dock, boots dangling over water that actually moved — clean, slow, brown-green river water carrying leaves and a very indignant duck. After weeks of the dead sea's glass-flat silence and the Rivergate's hungry current, ordinary moving water still made her chest ache in a way she couldn't name.

Below them, the village was waking. Smoke from cook fires. The smell of bread. Somewhere, someone was arguing cheerfully about a fence.

It had been eleven days since they'd pulled Joss's body from the flooded engine room. Eleven days since Brann's fleet had limped away from a collapsing ruin, and Denna Okoro had walked into the current with her team's names in her mouth, and the water had gone quiet for the first time in a thousand years.

Eleven days, and Wren still woke reaching for a knife.

"Does it ever stop?" Tam asked quietly. He'd gotten good, in the last week, at asking things quietly. "The waking up ready to run."

"I'll tell you when it happens."

He nodded like that was an acceptable answer, which was one of about four hundred reasons she hadn't sent him home yet. Not that he had a home. That was rather the point of him.

* * *

Sef found them an hour later, as promised, complaining loudly about his planks.

The old ferryman moved slower now than he had on the salt flats — the crossing had cost him something he pretended it hadn't — but his voice remained fully operational. He came down the dock carrying three bowls of porridge and the expression of a man who had decided other people's grief was best managed through aggressive feeding.

"Eat," he said. "Both of you. You look like survey reports."

"That's oddly specific," Tam said.

"I've read survey reports. They look exactly like you two." He handed out the bowls and lowered himself onto the dock with a grunt that took several seconds. For a while, the three of them ate in the kind of silence that wasn't empty.

Then Sef said, without looking at her: "The marker stone came up from the quarry this morning. Good granite. The mason wants to know what words you want on it."

Wren's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

They'd buried Joss two days after arriving, up on the bluff above the villages where the rangers' old waypoint cairn stood — the one her father had helped build, back when the river settlements still trusted the ranger corps enough to feed them. The badge had gone into the ground with him. Ranger-sign, second class, Voss, J. The metal had been water-marked and pitted, half its face dissolved, but Wren had cleaned it as best she could with sand and stubbornness until the crest showed again.

She'd told herself the burial was closure. She'd used the word *closure* and everything, in the firm tone she used on people who wouldn't listen.

The stone was different. A stone meant she intended to come back and visit it.

"Words," she said.

"Words," Sef confirmed. "Or no words. Some folk prefer no words. Though in my experience those are the ones who need them most."

Tam was watching her sideways, porridge forgotten, which for Tam constituted a major life event.

What did you put on a stone for a man who had spent seven years as part of a living current, leaving warnings carved in their family's sign language for anyone who might come looking? *Beloved brother* was true and useless. *Ranger* was true and incomplete. *He stayed so others could leave* —

That one. Maybe that one.

"He warned people," Wren said finally. "For years. Nobody knew he was doing it. He couldn't leave, so he made sure anyone who came after him had a chance to get out." She looked at the river. "Put: *Joss Voss. He stayed so others could go free.*"

Sef was quiet a moment. Then he nodded once, the way he did when he agreed with something completely, and said, "Good granite deserves good words. That'll hold."

"And put the ranger-sign under it," Wren added. "The full set. He'd want it readable."

"The mason will charge extra for signs."

"The mason can bill the government survey office. I'm told I have a claim number now."

Sef snorted into his porridge. Tam grinned. And just like that, the moment passed the way moments did in villages — folded into the day, carried off by chores and breakfast, held gently instead of stared at.

Wren noticed she was breathing easier than she had been. She decided not to examine why. Examining things was how you talked yourself back onto the road.

* * *

The freed survivors had become, improbably, a fixture.

There were nine of them — six from the Meridian survey team, pulled half-conscious from the engine's mechanism when the gates opened, plus three of Brann's advance crew whom the current had taken and released and who wanted nothing further to do with Commander Ilsa Brann or any fleet anywhere. They'd convalesced in the village's longhouse for a week, and instead of dispersing, they had begun, one by one, to acquire jobs.

The surveyor named Pell had fixed the mill wheel and now refused to leave it. Two of Brann's ex-engineers had gotten into a loud professional disagreement with the well-diggers and were winning. The youngest surveyor, a nervous young man called Ovid, had been adopted by the village children within forty-eight hours and appeared to have surrendered unconditionally.

"They're staying," Sef observed that afternoon, watching Ovid lose a game of sticks to a seven-year-old with visible delight. "All of them, far as I can tell. Nobody's asked them to."

"Somebody should tell them they're allowed to go," Wren said.

"Why? You planning to?"

She didn't answer. Sef didn't push. That was the arrangement between them: he fed her, she fixed his dock, and neither of them named the thing growing in the space where her next contract should have been.

But the question followed her through the afternoon, down to the boat shed where Tam was supposed to be repairing the skiff and was instead teaching two village boys to tie knots that would absolutely fail at critical moments.

"—and the trick is, you make it look complicated so nobody checks it," Tam was saying.

"That is not the trick of any knot," Wren said.

Tam jumped a foot. "Rangers shouldn't sneak up on people. It's rude."

"Rangers sneak up on people professionally. It's most of the job." She looked at the skiff. "Is that hull patched or decorated?"

"Patched. Mostly. Decoratively patched." He wiped his hands, suddenly businesslike, which was his tell for wanting to ask something. "Hey. So. The government courier came through this morning."

Wren went still. "I know. I talked to her."

"She talked to me too." Tam picked up a rope and began coiling it with tremendous concentration. "Said there's a mapping commission forming. For the Rivergate. Now that the current's quiet, they want guides who know the channels. Properly paid. Long-term." He glanced up. "River-orphan channels, specifically. Apparently somebody told them there's a boy who knows ways even the old charts don't show."

"Did somebody."

"Somebody with a government claim number and a big mouth."

Wren leaned against the shed post. "You don't have to take it."

"I know I don't have to take it." He set the coil down. "That's the weird part. I keep waiting for somebody to tell me what I *do* have to do, and nobody does, and it turns out that's — " He gestured vaguely at the river, the village, the entire concept of Tuesday. "A lot. Freedom's a lot, is my point."

"It is," Wren said. "Nobody warns you."

"So are you taking the commission?"

And there it was, turned around on her, the way Tam always managed to do — casually, with a rope in his hands, so she couldn't pretend it was a hard question being asked by anyone but herself.

The courier had laid it out plainly that morning: the Rivergate needed mapping, guarding, understanding. The villages downstream needed to know what the water would do now that the engine was dead. Someone with ranger training, ruin experience, and a documented history of telling salvage fleets to go to hell would be ideal. The posting was permanent. Permanent meaning *stay*.

Every instinct she owned recoiled from the word. Seven years of never stopping. Seven years of keeping moving because the moment you stopped, you had time to count what you'd lost — and she'd lost a brother, a father, a corps that had rotted from the inside, and a version of herself who'd believed survival was the same thing as living.

Except.

Except Joss had stayed, and it had nearly destroyed him, and the difference — the whole difference, the lesson carved into a drowned city — was that he hadn't *chosen* it. The current had taken his choice. Staying freely, staying because there were people worth staying for, was the one thing the current had never understood and never offered.

"Tell the courier yes," Wren said.

Tam's head came up. "Yeah?"

"For the commission. Both of us, if you want it. Guides need trainees, and you're the only person alive who knows the deep channels." She held up a finger before he could speak. "Conditions. One: you learn actual knots. Two: if you ever patch a hull decoratively again, I'm assigning you to well duty with the engineers."

"They'd love that. They argue beautifully."

"Three." She paused, and found the third condition harder to say than the first two. "We stay through winter, minimum. No running south when the work gets dull. We finish what we start here."

Tam looked at her for a long moment, and then did the thing he did instead of hugging people, which was punch her lightly on the shoulder and immediately find something else to look at.

"Winter," he said. "Deal. But I'm building a sled. If we're staying through winter, there will be sledding on the bluff. Non-negotiable. Write it into the contract."

"I'll inform the government."

"Do. Officially. Use the seal."

* * *

She went up the bluff alone at dusk.

The path was one her feet knew from childhood, though it had been fifteen years since she'd climbed it — switchbacking through sage brush to the flat crown of rock where the old waypoint cairn stood watch over the river bend. Her father's hands had placed half those stones. She'd forgotten that until this week, and remembering had felt less like grief than like finding money in an old coat.

The new marker stood beside the cairn, raw granite pale in the fading light, the mason's chiseling fresh and sharp:

**JOSS VOSS**
*He stayed so others could go free*
— and beneath it, cut deep and clean, the full ranger-sign, every glyph readable.

Wren sat down in the dirt beside it, the way you sit beside a person, not a stone.

"Hey," she said.

The river murmured below. A night bird asked its one question and answered itself. Somewhere downhill, Tam was losing an argument with the skiff, and Sef was winning an argument with dinner, and nine broken survivors were learning to be neighbors.

"I kept moving for seven years because I thought if I stopped, I'd have to feel all of it at once," she told the stone. "Turns out you feel it anyway. Just — slower. In installments." She picked up a pebble and turned it in her fingers. "You'd laugh at me. You always said I ran like the desert was chasing me. You said it nicer than that. You said everything nicer than that."

The wind moved through the sage. It smelled like home, which was a smell she'd stopped trusting a long time ago and was, apparently, learning again.

"I'm staying," she said. "Not because I'm done running. I don't think I'm ever going to be entirely done — my feet are going to wake up tomorrow trying to leave, same as today. But I'm staying anyway. That's the whole trick, I think. You stay *anyway*." She set the pebble on the marker's base, the way travelers left stones on cairns. "Anyway. The commission starts next month. Tam's insufferable about it already. You'd like him. He steals boats and lies about knots, and he's the bravest person I've met since you."

She sat until the stars came out, and then a while longer, and when she finally rose, her legs were stiff from sitting still — properly still, for hours, the longest she'd gone motionless in seven years — and it felt like an achievement rather than a failure.

At the top of the path she paused and looked back once.

The marker caught the last light. Below it, the villages glowed along the riverbank, and beyond them the dark line of the flats stretched toward the distant, drowned ruin where the water now ran slow and clean and quiet.

Quiet — but not silent. She'd learned that much. Standing at the Rivergate's edge some evenings, she could feel it, faint as a pulse under bandages: the tide turning, patient, remembering. Whatever lived in that water now was smaller and sadder and free, and it turned with the moon like any honest sea.

Tomorrow she'd draft the commission's first survey plan. Tomorrow Tam would demand his sled clause in writing. Tomorrow the freed survivors would keep fixing things nobody had asked them to fix, and Sef would complain about his planks, and Wren Voss — ranger, runner, sister — would wake before dawn and, for the first time in seven years, choose to lie there a while longer, watching the ceiling, listening to the river, going nowhere at all.

She smiled at the thought, took one last look at her brother's stone, and started down toward the lights.

Behind her, the tide turned.
