## 22. A Current for the Rivergate

The tide came in on schedule, which was the first miracle, and it came in clean, which was the second.

Wren Voss stood on the old ferry landing at the edge of the Rivergate with a cup of tea going cold in her hand and watched the water move through the drowned streets below. It ran slow now. Deliberate. The way water runs when nothing is pulling at it from underneath, when no engine older than memory is drinking at its source. A year ago that current had moved against the wind. It had whispered. It had *asked*.

Now it just went where tides go.

"You're doing it again," Tam said, appearing at her elbow the way he always did — soundless as a river-orphan, though he was seventeen now and nearly as tall as she was, which he mentioned often and without humility.

"Doing what?"

"Standing still." He said it like an accusation, but there was a grin under it. "A whole minute, maybe two. Sef will want it written down. *Day three hundred and forty-one: the ranger stood still of her own free will.*"

"Your job is training guides, not keeping a ledger on me."

"It's one job. I multitask."

She let him have it. A year ago she'd have been moving already — boots on, contract half-read, gone before the tea finished steeping. Stopping had always felt to her like dying in installments. Now she stood on a dead city's doorstep every morning and drank her tea while the sun came up over the salt flats, and the strange part, the part she still hadn't gotten used to, was that nothing bad happened. The world did not end because Wren Voss paused.

Below them, three new guides were picking their way along the marked causeway toward the low gate — two river kids out of the eastern villages and one ex-salvager who'd jumped Brann's fleet after Sixth Tide and never quite explained why. Tam had named them all after channels. The salvager was Deepcut, because he'd once fallen into one and come out the other side, allegedly. Tam taught them the way he'd learned everything: by stealing their assumptions first and then handing back something better.

"Watch the silt line," his voice carried up faintly from the causeway. "If the silt's wet above the waterline, the channel shifted overnight and you don't trust your map, you trust the mud. Mud doesn't lie. Maps lie. Governments lie—" he caught himself, glanced up at Wren, and amended, "—*some* governments lie."

"The Meridian government," Wren called down, "paid for this school."

"And buried a survey team under false names until we dug them out and gave them their real ones back." Tam shrugged, unrepentant. "Both things are true. That's what I teach them. Both things are true."

Wren looked back at the water.

They'd buried the survey team properly in the spring, up on the ridge where the ground stayed dry, eleven markers with eleven real names carved by Sef's own hand, because the old ferryman had the steadiest chisel work for fifty miles and refused to let anyone else do it. Denna Okoro's marker faced the sea. She'd walked into the current at Tide Five, choosing her team over the world, and Wren had let her go — had stood in the flooded avenue and *let her go*, which even now felt less like grief than like finally learning a language she'd been failing at her whole life. People got to stop. That was the lesson. People got to choose it.

Denna's marker read: *SHE ASKED THE WATER ITS NAME, AND IT ASKED HERS, AND SHE ANSWERED.*

Tam had wanted something shorter. Sef had overruled him.

* * *

The school — nobody called it anything grander than the school, though the Meridian charter pinned up inside officially titled it the Rivergate Institute of Channel Navigation and Ruin Safety, a name so bureaucratic that Tam had framed it upside down as a joke and then claimed it worked better that way — occupied three connected buildings on the high shelf above the ruin. Classrooms in the morning, drills in the afternoon, and in the evenings, if you were lucky, Sef cooking on the old ferry galley he'd hauled up piece by piece and reassembled with the stubbornness of a man who believed food tasted better near water.

Wren taught there twice a week. Not navigation — Tam had that, and better than she ever would; the boy thought in channels the way she thought in distance. She taught the other thing. The thing no one had taught her.

Today's class was twelve rangers, most of them fresh from the interior stations, all of them carrying the same look she used to carry: the coiled readiness, the refusal to sit with their backs to doors, the way their eyes kept measuring exits even during roll call. She knew that look like her own handwriting.

"Tell me about stopping," she said, and watched twelve faces close like fists.

A woman in the front row — Kess, out of the Dry Fork station, good tracker, worse sleeper — said flatly, "Stopping gets people killed."

"Sometimes," Wren agreed. "So does not stopping. I chased a thief across forty miles of flats once because I couldn't stand to break stride. Caught him. Nearly died of heat. For a man who'd stolen a sack of meal." She let that sit. "I told myself it was competence. It was fear wearing competence's coat. There's a difference between moving because you're going somewhere and moving because you're afraid of what happens if you're still long enough to feel it."

Kess's jaw worked. "And what does happen?"

"That's the assignment," Wren said. "Go sit somewhere. One hour. Nothing in your hands. Come back tomorrow and tell me what you found out there in the quiet." She paused, and gave them the only joke she owned: "Fair warning. Most of you will find out you're tired. Some of you will find out why."

They filed out muttering, and Wren gathered her notes, and Tam leaned in the doorway with the expression of a student grading his teacher.

"One hour of nothing," he said. "You're cruel."

"They'll thank me in a year."

"I thanked you in a year. Well. Eleven months." He pushed off the doorframe. "Sef wants us at the galley tonight. He says he found clams."

"Sef always says he found clams. Half the time it's snails with ambition."

"He's an artist, Wren. Let him dream."

* * *

The evening came down gold over the flats, and the galley smelled of smoke and garlic and whatever the clams actually were, and for a while Wren just sat in it — the noise, the argument about whether Deepcut had or had not gotten lost in the Weeping Stair channel (he had), Sef telling the clam story with escalating detail, Tam correcting him on points of geography and being pelted with a bread roll.

Her brother's badge sat in her pocket. She touched it without thinking, the way you touch a scar.

Joss's body had come out of the water with the gates' last surge, and they'd buried him beside the survey team, under a marker Sef carved with hands that shook only once. But the badge — his ranger badge, the one he'd worn walking into the Rivergate all those years ago — she'd kept. She'd meant to bury it with him. She'd stood at the grave with it in her fist and found she couldn't open her fingers.

So instead she wore it into class some days, and showed it to rangers who thought stopping was dying, and said, *my brother stopped, and here's what it bought.* Not the machine's bargain — never that, never the trade where you dissolve into something hungry and call it peace. Joss had chosen the gates in the end, chosen to drown the current's hunger with his own hands, and that choice had cost everything except meaning. That was the difference she tried to teach, and mostly failed, and sometimes, with someone like Kess, mostly didn't.

"You're touching the badge again," Sef observed, setting a bowl in front of her. They were clams. Actual clams. "You know, my daughter used to do that. Touch the thing she couldn't put down and pretend it was nothing."

"What did she do about it?"

"She put it down eventually. Took her six years and a very patient ferryman." He sat across from her, joints cracking like kindling. "The marker's holding up. I checked the lettering Tuesday. Salt gets into everything, but stone outlasts salt, if you cut it deep enough."

"You cut it deep enough."

"I cut everything deep enough. It's my only virtue." He slurped a clam with tremendous dignity. "Brann's ships passed the outer mark yesterday. Two of them. Survey vessels, proper ones, flying the Korvane colors and the village flags both."

Wren set down her spoon. "Mapping?"

"Mapping. And taking readings at the wells. The old wells." Sef's eyes were shrewd and kind at once, which was a trick Wren had never managed. "Word from the east is the sharing compact holds. Six villages, one ledger, water measured by need and not by fleet tonnage. Brann signed it herself. In ink, they say. Not blood, more's the pity — the story would be better with blood."

"She'd say stories are how villages waste water."

"She'd be right, too. That's what frightens everyone about her." Sef refilled her bowl without asking. "She sent something for you. Refused the courier. Said it had to wait for a face."

That night, alone at the landing with the tide turning below her, Wren opened the package. Inside was a single page in a commander's blunt hand:

*Voss. The engineers tell me the engine chamber is stable and dry and will stay that way, and that the current reads as ordinary seawater now, which they insist on calling a scientific anomaly rather than a miracle because it keeps them employed. My city drinks from the wells. It is enough. It is not comfortable, and I hate enough, but I am learning it, slowly, the way you learn a language late in life. I am told you teach rangers to stand still. This is me standing still. Do not write back. Brann.*

At the bottom, smaller, almost grudging: *The water runs clean. You did that. I would have done it worse. Someone should say so and it will not be me again.*

Wren read it twice, folded it, and put it in her pocket next to the badge, where the hard things lived.

* * *

The last morning of the season, she walked the causeway down into the ruin itself, alone, as she did every few weeks. Not because the maps needed checking — Tam's students had mapped every street, every channel, every door, and hung the great chart in the school hall where visitors gawked at it. Not because anything needed guarding; the guard rotation ran smooth, and the ruin's dangers these days were loose masonry and tourists with more courage than sense.

She walked it because the silence deserved witnesses.

The flooded market avenue was bright with reflected light, fish moving through the old stalls like tenants who'd finally been given honest rent. The machine chamber below was sealed behind a gate Tam's engineers had welded, and beyond the gate, nothing stirred. Whatever had lived in the current — asked names, made offers, traded survival for souls — it was gone. Drowned in open sea, screaming, silent. The surveyors' instruments agreed. The rangers' instruments agreed. Even Sef, who trusted his knees more than any instrument, agreed.

But Wren stood at the low gate at slack water, and she listened anyway.

Because here was the thing she taught and could not prove and would not stop believing: the current had been built by people who wanted to survive past all reason. And people did not change that much. Somewhere out in the world there would always be another hungry engine, another bargain too good, another commander with a dying city and cold arithmetic. The Rivergate was quiet. Quiet was not the same as forever.

The water turned beneath her — out now, the ebb beginning, slow and clean and obedient to nothing but the moon — and Wren Voss, who had spent her whole life running from stillness, stood perfectly still and watched it go.

Then she turned, climbed the causeway, and went home to teach.

The tide, faithful as ever, began to turn again behind her.
