## 1. The Debt Collector Smiles

The storm had a smell before it had a shape—ozone and wet iron and the green rot of kelp torn up from deep water—and Ada Okonkwo had learned to read that smell the way other people read letters from home. She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Debt*, one hand on the spoke, the other shielding her eyes against a sky the color of a bruise going bad, and she thought: *ninety minutes, maybe less, before it makes landfall at Cape Verren.*

She was right about the storm. She was almost always right about storms.

It was everything else she got wrong.

"Coming about," she said to no one, because there was no one to say it to. The crew question had resolved itself two seasons ago, when the last of them—old Maren, who'd taught her to read a squall line like sheet music—had taken her severance in the form of Ada's apology and a bus ticket inland. Now it was just Ada and the boat and the debt, and the debt rode with her everywhere, quiet as ballast, heavy as ballast too.

She ran the *Kestrel* hard toward the cape, because that was the whole architecture of her living: get ahead of the weather, plant the sensor array where the data brokers wouldn't think to look, sell the readings to the insurance consortiums and the coastal engineers and anyone else who paid for the privilege of knowing what the sky intended. Storm chasing wasn't glamorous work anymore. The romance of it had been strip-mined decades ago by drone fleets and satellite arrays. What was left was the margins—the near-shore microbursts, the tidal squalls, the ugly little systems too small and too fast for the big machines to bother with. Ada lived in those margins the way a barnacle lives on a hull: stubbornly, low to the water, taking what the current brought.

The first gust hit at 4:17 by her cheap wristwatch, and it hit like a hand shoved flat against the boat's shoulder. The *Kestrel* heeled, recovered, heeled again. Ada laughed—the short, involuntary laugh that only came out of her when the wind arrived, the one sound she couldn't fake or suppress—and hauled the array housing up onto the foredeck, lashing it down with hands that knew the knots better than they knew their own names.

That was when she saw the boat behind her.

It came out of the rain wall without hurry, which was the first wrong thing. Nobody came out of a squall line without hurry. It was a white-hulled motor yacht, forty feet if it was an inch, gleaming even under the gray light, riding the chop the way a knife rides a whetstone. And standing at its rail, one hand raised in cheerful salute, was a man in a pale linen suit that should have been soaked through and somehow wasn't.

Ada's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

Mr. Pell.

She knew him the way you know a toothache: intimately, dreadfully, and always a little before he announced himself. He was the Vane Syndicate's collector for the whole eastern reach, and he collected the way some men fished—with patience, with pleasure, with a genuine and unfeigned delight in the sport of it. He had never once raised his voice to her. He had never once needed to.

She considered running. The thought lasted exactly as long as it took her to look at the *Kestrel*'s fuel gauge, which read a quarter tank, and then at the storm building behind the yacht like a second, larger ship made of cloud. There was nowhere to run that Pell hadn't already priced into his afternoon.

So she did what she could do: she ran the numbers instead. Three months past due. Principal plus the Syndicate's interest, which compounded the way mold compounds in a wet hull—quietly, constantly, until one day the wood is soft all the way through. Eleven thousand, four hundred marks, as of the last letter. Probably twelve now. Twelve thousand marks, and Ada had four hundred and sixty in the coffee tin bolted beneath her bunk, and the tin was not negotiable.

The yacht slid alongside, close enough that she could see the man himself clearly now: broad, silver-templed, with a face like a friendly butcher and eyes that did all their arithmetic behind the smile. Mr. Pell lifted a silver flask in greeting.

"Miss Okonkwo!" he called over the wind, and his voice carried the way good voices carry in open air, warm and rounded, a voice built for toasts. "Magnificent conditions! You've picked yourself a beauty today."

"It's a working day, Mr. Pell." She kept both hands on the wheel. Her knuckles had gone the color of the foam. "If you're here to talk money, you picked a poor time."

"I'm here because I heard the weather report," he said, beaming, "and I thought to myself, *now there's a woman who appreciates the sea.* And I said, well, we oughtn't let her appreciate it alone." He gestured grandly at the boiling sky. "May I come aboard? My captain will hold station."

Every instinct she owned said no. Every practical bone in her body said that *no* was a currency she didn't have enough of. She tied off at the nearest mooring buoy inside the cape's lee—a skillful bit of work, done fast, done one-handed while the gusts tried to take the line away from her—and watched Pell step across the gap between decks as lightly as a man stepping off a veranda.

Up close he smelled of cedar and something faintly medicinal, like a doctor's office that had been decorated by someone with excellent taste. He looked around the *Kestrel*'s cramped cabin with frank, unhurried appreciation, the way a man looks through a house he is considering buying.

"You live well," he said.

"I live."

"Yes." He settled himself onto her bench as though it were upholstered. From his breast pocket he produced, of all things, a small paper bag of candied ginger, opened it, and offered it to her. She declined. He ate a piece himself, thoughtfully. "I'll be honest with you, Miss Okonkwo, because I believe honesty between professionals is worth more than any contract. The office has grown—restless. About your account."

"The office can grow restless in line at the ferry dock for all I care."

He laughed, delighted, a real laugh, and that was the worst part of him, that the laughter was real. "There she is. That's why I like coming out here personally. The office sends letters. Letters are dead things. But you—" he pointed the ginger bag at her like a toast— "you have *texture*."

"Pell." She sat across from him, deliberately, forcing herself into the posture of a negotiation rather than a supplication. Her heart was going like a bilge pump in a flood, but she'd learned long ago that fear was mostly a matter of posture. Straighten the spine, level the chin, and the fear had to queue up behind the performance. "Say what you came to say. I've got a storm to beat."

"A storm to *beat*." He savored it. "You know, most people run from them. You chase. There's a lesson in that, isn't there? About running and chasing." He set the bag down, folded his hands, and for one moment—one clean, cold moment—the friendliness drained out of his face entirely, the way water drains from a lock, and what remained underneath was smooth and patient and old. "Twelve thousand, two hundred marks. Due in full."

Ada kept her breathing even. "And if it isn't?"

"Then the Syndicate takes the boat." He said it the way a man recommends a restaurant. "Which would be a tragedy, because I've watched you handle this vessel, and it would break my heart to see her skippered by one of our—well. Our people are competent, but they lack your poetry." He paused. "Or, alternatively."

"Alternatively."

"Alternatively, there is work." He reached into his jacket and laid a folded paper on the table between them, smoothing it flat with two fingers. A manifest. Salvage contract, Syndicate letterhead, terms dense as ballast stone. "A recovery operation, six weeks, coastal waters. Good pay. Enough to clear you and leave margin. The fleet sails from Harrow Inlet on the morning tide after next."

Ada looked at the paper and did not touch it. Signing aboard a Syndicate vessel was how people disappeared—not dramatically, not with gunboats and sirens, but quietly, in the way of debts that never quite finish being paid. She'd known two chasers who'd taken Syndicate contracts. One had come back rich and strange, flinching at doorways. The other hadn't come back at all, and the Syndicate had sent flowers to a memorial service nobody held.

"Why me?" she asked. "You've got crews."

"We have crews," Pell agreed pleasantly. "What we don't have, currently, is a pilot who knows the Verren shallows the way you do. And we may need such a pilot." Something moved behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "The season's closing. Tides are dropping toward the equinoctial lows. Windows are getting narrow. Narrow windows favor the skilled." He smiled again, wide and warm, and stood, brushing nothing whatsoever from his immaculate trousers. "Six days, Miss Okonkwo. Sign the contract and sail with us, or bring the twelve thousand to my office at Harrow Inlet by the same tide. Either way, I'll see you on the water. It's where you belong."

At the rail, pausing with one foot already over the gap, he turned back as if struck by a charming afterthought.

"Oh—and do give my regards to the storm. I do envy you that. All I ever get to chase is paperwork." His laugh rolled back across the water, genuine as sunrise. Then he was aboard the white yacht, and the yacht was turning, unhurried, back toward the open sea, and the rain took it the way the sea takes everything, gradually and then completely.

* * *

Ada did not chase the storm after that. Not properly.

She ran the array anyway—habit is a kind of spine—but her heart wasn't in the telemetry, and the numbers came back mediocre, and she sold them cheap to a broker who complained about the resolution and paid her ninety marks, which bought diesel and canned peaches and nothing else. She sat in the cabin that night with the lamp turned low and the contract unfolded on the table, reading it for the fourth time, hunting for the trap she knew was in there the way you hunt for a splinter you can feel but not see.

It was in the seventh clause. *Vessel and operator subject to fleet command discretion for duration of engagement.* Fleet command discretion. Which meant Pell. Which meant that whatever the fleet was actually doing out there—and salvage fleets did not need shallow-water pilots unless they were salvaging something in shallow water they weren't supposed to be in—she'd be sailing into it with her name signed to it, complicit in advance, prepaid in advance, owned in advance.

She thought about the coffee tin. Four hundred and sixty marks. Six days.

She thought about Maren, inland somewhere, probably sleeping in a room that didn't rock.

Outside, the storm had passed on north, and the water lay flat and black and indifferent, and the stars came out over the Verren flats like scattered coins on a dark table—wealth beyond counting, spendable by no one. Ada pressed her palms flat against the table and felt the boat breathe under her, the old girl's bones flexing with the swell, and understood with total clarity that she would rather burn the *Kestrel's Debt* with her own hands than sign clause seven.

Which left five days to find twelve thousand marks, or a miracle, or a third option that hadn't introduced itself yet.

She slept badly, dreamed of white hulls, woke before dawn with salt crusted in her eyelashes and a hunger that had nothing to do with food. Supply run, then. Fuel, water, the peaches. And maybe—if the tide tables were kind and the flats were exposed—a walk along the low-water line below Cape Verren, where the retreating sea sometimes gave things back. Driftwood. Wreckage. Cargo lost off the freight lanes, still sealed, still saleable. It was thin pickings, beachcombing. It was also the only legal idea she had left.

The morning was gray and enormous. The flats stretched out at extreme ebb, miles of wet sand and tidal pools holding the sky upside down, and Ada walked out onto them with her boots sucking and her collar up and her mind running figures that refused to resolve.

She found him a half mile out, past the third sandbar, where the pools went from ankle-deep to knee-deep and the light came slantwise through the mist.

He was lying on his side in the shallows like something the sea had decided, at the last moment, not to keep. An old man, weathered down to leather and wire, one hand pressed against his belly where a dark bloom was spreading through his shirt with terrible patience. His other hand was clamped around a canvas case, and he was not letting go of it, and when he heard her boots in the water he turned his head with an effort that seemed to cost him a year, and his eyes found hers—clear, ferocious, dying—and he smiled.

"Well," the prospector rasped. "Took you long enough. I've been watching you walk for ten minutes, girl. Deciding." A bubble of red at the corner of his mouth. "Deciding if you were the one."

Ada stood in the cold water with the tide running out around her ankles and the six-day clock ticking in her chest, and every self-preserving instinct she possessed told her to turn around, walk away, call no one, know nothing.

She knelt down beside him instead.

"Who shot you?" she asked.

His laugh was a wet whisper. "Wrong first question," he said, and pulled the canvas case tighter against his chest. "Right first question is whether you can keep a promise. Because I've got something worth more than your twelve thousand marks, and I haven't got twelve minutes left to argue about it."
