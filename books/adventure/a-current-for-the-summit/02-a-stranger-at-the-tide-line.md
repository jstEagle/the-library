## 2. A Stranger at the Tide Line

The tide was going out and taking Ada's last good mood with it.

She had come to Halfway Landing for diesel, coffee, and a new bilge pump—three things, in that order of likelihood—and she was leaving with one and a half. The diesel had been easy; the coffee had cost her the last of the cash she kept in her boot, folded small enough to pass through a handshake without a whisper. The bilge pump had been the problem. Old Sefu at the chandlery had turned it over in his hands twice, looked at the price tag, looked at her face, and said nothing at all, which from Sefu was a whole sermon.

"You'll take it on account," he'd said finally, not asking.

"I don't do accounts anymore."

"Everybody does accounts." He'd wrapped it anyway, in brown paper gone soft as cloth from years of wrapping things, and pushed it across the counter like contraband. "Pell's boys were in Tuesday. Asking after you."

Ada had felt the name settle into her chest like cold water finding a seam. *Pell.* Mr. Pell, who smiled the way a hook smiles. "What did you tell them?"

"That I hadn't seen you since the spring." Sefu had shrugged, an old man's shrug that carried no weight and all of it. "Which is true, near enough. But Ada—they weren't asking the way people ask. They were asking the way people count."

Now she was counting too: the hours until dark, the fathoms under her keel, the days until Pell came to collect what she owed with that terrible gentleness of his. The *Kestrel* sat low on her mooring, riding the ebb, her hull carrying three seasons of deferred maintenance the way a body carries old grief—invisible until it wasn't.

She cast off without ceremony. The channel out of Halfway Landing wound between mudflats that the falling tide exposed by the acre, brown-gold flats ribbed with the day's last light, stinking gently of salt and sulfur and everything the sea decided each morning it could live without. Gulls stood ankle-deep in the shallows with the patience of creditors.

It was on those flats that she saw him.

At first she took him for driftwood—a pale shape half-buried where the water had left it, maybe fifty meters off the channel marker, in the kind of place boats didn't go and walkers didn't reach. Then the shape moved. Not much. A hand lifting, falling. The gulls nearest it took two steps back, unhurried, and resumed their waiting.

Ada swore, once, comprehensively, and put the helm over.

The *Kestrel* drew too much for the flat this far up the tide line, so she anchored short and waded the last stretch, boots filling with cold slurry, the pump wrapped in its soft paper held high against her chest like something rescued from a fire. The man lay on his side in a shallow of standing water, and up close there was no mistaking him for anything but what he was: a prospector, sixty if he was a day, in gear older than her boat, with a beard gone white at the chin and eyes that were still working even though the rest of him had clearly given notice.

There was blood in the water around him. Not a lot—not anymore. That was the worst part. Gut wounds bled hard first and then stopped bleeding because there was nothing left to bleed.

"Don't," he said, when she knelt. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Don't waste your time on me. Waste it listening."

"I can get you to Halfway Landing. There's a medic—"

"There's a man in Halfway Landing who sells information to whoever pays, and I am worth more dead than alive right now, girl, so sit down and listen before you decide how charitable you're feeling." He coughed, and the cough cost him something he didn't have to spare. "Ede. My name is Ede."

"Ada."

Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, or amusement, or just the random firing of a dying brain. "Well," he said. "That's either luck or a joke, and I'm too far gone to care which."

She did kneel then, in the cold water, because standing suddenly seemed like a thing other people did. She checked him with hands that knew triage: the wound low on his left side, entry only, no exit she could find, the skin around it gray as the mud. He'd been shot hours ago. Maybe a day. He'd crawled, or dragged himself, a long way—the track of him was written in the flat behind him, a furrow like something a wounded animal leaves.

"Who did this?"

"The same people who are going to do worse to you, if you keep owing them money." He watched her flinch and almost smiled. "Sefu talks. Everyone talks. It's the one honest industry on this coast." His hand moved, trembling, toward the pack strapped to his chest—he'd been wearing it like armor, she realized, like a man carrying something against his heart. "Help me with this."

She should have said no. She understood that even then, with a clarity that would come back to haunt her in installments. Every instinct she'd sharpened over five years of debt and running said: *a dying stranger is a liability, a dying stranger with enemies is a catastrophe, walk away, wade back, weigh anchor, forget the shape on the tide line.* Self-preservation was the one skill she'd mastered completely. It had kept her alive when cleverness hadn't and courage hadn't. It was the whole of her religion.

She unstrapped the pack.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth inside waxed canvas inside more oilcloth, was a chart. Hand-drawn, on paper that had been folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone to lace. She could feel the weight of it, the way you feel the weight of a thing that matters, which is mostly imagination but never feels like it.

"The *Meridian Star*," Ede said. "Went down forty-one years ago in the Vane basin, north of the Serrated Grounds. Official record says cargo of machine parts and tinned goods. Official record is a lie. She was carrying salvage already claimed—recovered from three wrecks the Syndicate sank themselves for the insurance, and papers besides. Ledgers. Proof of who they paid and who they owned." His breath whistled. "They've spent four decades making sure nobody finds her. And now the season's closing, and the basin will silt shut for another year, and there are six tides left in which anyone could reach her. Six. I counted. Counting is what I do."

"Why are you giving this to me?"

"Because you're the only boat in thirty miles that isn't theirs, and because you're desperate enough to try and stupid enough to succeed, and because—" he stopped, and the stopping seemed to hurt more than the wound. "Because I asked Sefu about you. Months ago. Before they caught up with me. You chase storms. You go where sensible people don't. I needed someone like that."

"You needed someone expendable."

"I needed someone *free*," he said, with sudden force, gripping her wrist with a strength that surprised them both. "There's a difference, and you're too young and too broke to know it yet. Listen. One promise. One. You take this map to my daughter. Nara Okonkwo—" and here Ada's breath caught, because Okonkwo was her own name, borrowed and kept, a name she'd taken at nineteen from nowhere and no one, and hearing it in a dying man's mouth felt like being called by a ghost. "Nara Okonkwo, Port Merrow, the house with the blue door on Canner Street. You give it to her. Not to any fleet. Not to any magistrate, not to any buyer, not to the Syndicate even if they put a gun in your mouth. To *her*. She decides what it's worth. Do you understand? Forty years I ran from her mother's memory and from what I used to be, and I am not going to let the last true thing I own end up in some fleet's strongroom. Promise me."

The tide was still going out. Somewhere behind her the *Kestrel* swung gently on her anchor, patient as a dog. Ada thought about Pell's smile, about the arithmetic of debt, about how the fastest way to die on this coast was to owe two people at once.

"If I promise," she said slowly, "I keep it. That's the only currency I've got left. So understand what you're asking. If I carry this map, I'm carrying whatever comes with it. Whoever shot you might shoot me."

"They will," Ede agreed cheerfully. "Almost certainly. That's why I picked you."

A laugh escaped her—short, involuntary, appalled. The gulls scattered and resettled.

He made her open the oilcloth right there, kneeling in the shallows, so she could see what she was swearing to. The chart beneath was beautiful and mad: soundings in a cramped antique hand, tidal tables copied out for dates decades past, annotations in three colors of ink, and at the center, drawn with the obsessive precision of a man who had spent a lifetime looking, the outline of a basin and, within it, a hull. *Meridian Star*, written beneath in letters that had faded from black to the color of weak tea.

"Six tides," he said again. "After that, the sand closes over the channel and she sleeps another year, and by next year I'll be bones and the Syndicate will have bought whatever government hasn't already sold itself. Six tides, Ada. And a daughter who hasn't seen my face in twenty-two years and has every reason to spit on it." His grip on her wrist loosened, finger by finger, like a tide going out. "I can't ask her forgiveness. I stopped deserving the right to ask for things a long time ago. But I can send her the truth. It's not much. It's the only inheritance I've got that isn't poisoned."

"What happened to you?" she asked, because she couldn't help it. "Who shot you?"

"A man I trained," Ede said. "Twenty years ago I taught half their navigators everything they know. That's the joke of it. That's the whole joke." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "Promise me."

And here was the moment, and she knew it even as she lived it—the hinge of it, the exact point where the current changed. She could close the oilcloth, put the pack back on his chest, wade away, and let the flats have him. In a week he'd be a report, in a month a rumor, in a year nothing. Her debts would remain exactly as survivable as they were this morning. Pell would smile. The world would continue.

But the man was dying alone on a mudflat with the gulls keeping vigil, and the last thing he wanted on earth was not wealth, not revenge, not even life—it was for a promise to travel further than his body could. And Ada, who had spent five years learning that everything on this coast had a price, found herself holding the one thing she'd sworn never to touch again: someone else's need, heavier than her own.

"I promise," she said. "To Nara. To no one else. On my boat and my name."

"Your name," Ede murmured. "Keep that too. Names matter more than money. Money you can lose honestly." He fumbled at his collar and pressed something into her palm—a brass compass case, worn smooth, warm from his skin. "For Nara. She'll know it. Tell her—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell her the lighthouse at Cape Verren. Tell her I finally saw it lit."

Then his eyes went past her shoulder, to the channel, and whatever he saw in her face made him smile—or grimace; at the end they were the same expression.

"Boat coming," he said. "Go. Now. Take the channel north, not south—south is where they'll look. And Ada." He caught her hand one last time. "Count your tides."

She waded backward through the shallows, watching him, until the water reached her thighs and he became a shape again, and then a smudge, and then the flats took him the way they take everything—without malice, without hurry. Behind her, far down the channel, a boat was indeed coming: white hull, twin engines, running lights already on against the dusk, moving with the particular confidence of a vessel that has never once been chased.

Ada reached the *Kestrel*, hauled the anchor, and stood a long moment at the wheel with the pack against her ribs and her heart going like a auxiliary engine.

North, then. Against every habit of her life, north—toward the Serrated Grounds, toward six tides, toward a blue door on Canner Street and a woman who would spit on her father's memory and a wreck full of proof that could hang half the coast or drown everyone who touched it.

She opened the throttle. Behind her the white hull slowed at the tide line, and a figure in the bow raised something—glasses, probably, or a light—and swept it across the emptying flats where an old man lay very still, keeping his secrets.

Ada did not look back again. But she counted, under her breath, the way Ede had told her to, and by the time the Serrated Grounds rose black against the last of the light, she had reached the number that mattered:

Five tides left. And somewhere behind her, already, the Syndicate was counting too.
