## 3. Six Tides

The map was drawn on the back of a chart so old the paper had gone the color of weak tea, and it smelled of kerosene and blood. Ada spread it across the chart table in her cabin with hands that still shook, though not from fear—she'd stopped being afraid of dying men years ago. It was the living who frightened her now. The living sent men like Mr. Pell.

She had washed Ede's blood off her deck at dawn, hosing the flats' gray silt and the darker stain of him over the side while gulls screamed their opinions from the rigging. Now she sat alone in the lamplight of her cabin—the *Kestrel* rode at anchor in a roadstead she'd have preferred not to name aloud—and studied what a dying man had trusted to a stranger.

The chart beneath the ink showed a stretch of coast she knew by reputation and nothing else: the Serrano Reach, forty miles of drowned marshland where the sea came in twice a day and took back half of what it gave. Old wrecks littered those flats like bones in a drawer. Salvage men called the Reach the Widow's Pantry, because it fed you once and starved you after.

Ede's hand had been steady even as his life drained out of him. He'd drawn in red grease pencil—a prospector's tool, cheap and waterproof—and he'd drawn with the economy of a man who had spent decades memorizing coastlines he could never afford to work.

A tidal basin, closed on three sides by sandbars that shifted with every season's storms. A channel through the northern bar, marked with soundings in his cramped notation: *four fathoms at low slack, less than one at flood*. And in the heart of the basin, a single X with a name beside it in letters pressed so hard they'd nearly torn the paper:

*MERIDIAN STAR.*

Ada sat back. The lamp flame leaned in some draft she couldn't find, and the shadows in the cabin leaned with it.

She knew the name. Everyone on this coast knew the name, though most people had filed it away with other old disasters—the ferry that burned, the freighter that broke up on the Teeth, the salvage ship *Meridian Star*, lost with all hands eleven years ago in a storm the papers had called an act of God. Lost with all hands, and lost with her cargo manifest sealed by court order, which was the kind of detail that had always made Ada's teeth itch. Ships sank every season. Ships didn't get gag orders.

Ede had believed otherwise. She remembered his voice, wet and ragged against her shoulder as she'd tried to stop the bleeding: *They didn't sink her, girl. They scuttled her. I watched them do it.* And then, when she'd asked who: *The same ones who own your debt.*

He'd known about Pell. She never told him. He'd seen the Syndicate's mark stamped on her stern registration papers when he'd come aboard—or maybe he'd simply looked at her face, at the particular flinch a debtor develops around cheerful men, and read it there.

Below the X, Ede had written a list. Not cargo—though the word *cargo* appeared, underlined twice—but categories. *Ledgers. Correspondence. Sealed holds, aft of bulkhead nine.* And at the bottom, in handwriting that had begun to fail, the letters wobbling: *Proof enough to hang them all. Worth more than the gold they say she carried. Tell my Nara first. No fleet. NO FLEET.*

Ada touched the paper lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was friendly.

The gold, if there was gold, would be fortune enough. Eleven years of unclaimed salvage sat in legal limbo until someone proved discovery; the first finder's claim on a wreck like the *Meridian Star* would clear her debt ten times over, buy the *Kestrel* outright, buy her a life with no Mr. Pell in it anywhere. That was the arithmetic she wanted to do. That was the arithmetic she made herself do, twice, three times, because the other number on the page—the one written in wobbling letters about proof and hanging—was a different kind of arithmetic entirely, and it ended with her at the bottom of the sea or at the end of a rope, depending on how fast the Syndicate worked.

She got up and made coffee she didn't drink, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

By midmorning she'd swallowed the coffee cold and taken the dinghy ashore to the supply post at Halfway Landing, because a plan built on empty fuel tanks wasn't a plan, it was a wish. The Landing was a straggle of sheds and a fuel dock on a spit of higher ground, the kind of place where the news arrived late and left early, and Ada kept her head down and her questions small while she loaded diesel drums and canned food and a fresh drum of two-stroke for the tender.

It was the harbormaster's radio, playing too loud behind his office window, that told her what she'd already begun to suspect.

"—salvage fleet out of Corvane anchoring through the weekend, four vessels including the flagship *Vane Fortune*, provisioning at the Reach approaches. Harbor authority says the Syndicate's licensed a survey of the Serrano flats. Spokesman calls it routine recovery operations."

Routine. Ada stood in the doorway of the chandlery with a case of oilskin matches under her arm and felt the word go through her like a fishhook.

Four vessels. The *Vane Fortune* herself, which meant somebody senior was aboard, which meant this was not a survey. The Syndicate did not send its flagship to watch divers poke at sandbars. They were here for the same map she held, or for whatever remained of the man who'd drawn it—which meant they might already know Ede was dead, and might already be asking who found him.

She paid in cash, smiled at nobody, and walked back down the dock with her collar up. At the fuel pump, a deckhand from one of the local boats was telling another about lights he'd seen offshore the night before—"three of them, riding high, big as churches"—and Ada listened with the particular attention of a woman counting exits.

Here was the shape of it, laid out plain as the chart itself: the Syndicate had come to the Reach with a fleet, licenses, and patience. She had come with a borrowed map, a debt, and a boat that could outrun anything afloat if she kept her distance but couldn't outrun four sets of eyes working a grid. If they swept the basin before she reached it, the wreck was theirs, the proof was theirs, and Ede died in the mud for nothing. If they caught her inside their perimeter, she wouldn't even rate a trial. Debts, she'd learned, were the gentlest instrument the Vane family owned.

And yet.

She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* that afternoon, the map open on the chart table behind her, and made herself look at the tide tables instead of the horizon. This was the part she was good at. This was the only part, maybe, but she was very good at it.

The Serrano basin flooded and drained on a twelve-hour-and-twenty-five-minute rhythm, and the channel through the northern bar—Ede's four fathoms at low slack—was passable perhaps ninety minutes per cycle, and only on the bigger tides. She ran the numbers the way other people prayed. Spring tides were building toward the equinox; the next six cycles would carry enough water to float her in and out over the bar. After that, the neaps came on, and the channel would close like a throat. Six tides. Twelve days, give or take, and then the Reach would keep its secret until the next spring series—weeks the Syndicate could spend dredging the flats at leisure.

Six tides, and somewhere out there, four ships.

She thought about walking away. She made herself think about it properly, the way you probe a sore tooth: sail north, sell the map to the highest bidder—there were always bidders, rival houses, federal men, journalists with more courage than sense—and let someone else run the gauntlet. But every buyer she could name either feared the Syndicate or fed it, and the moment the map changed hands, the clock started for whoever bought it, and the odds stayed the same, and her debt stayed on the books, compounding at Pell's smiling rate. Selling the map just meant renting her problem to someone else and staying poor.

She thought about Ede's face. Not the wound—she'd seen enough wounds that they blurred together—but the moment before, when she'd promised. *No fleet,* he'd said. *Tell my Nara first.* She hadn't promised to dive the wreck. She'd promised to carry the map true. There was a version of her that could honor that promise from a safe harbor, and that version of her had a name, and the name was coward, and she'd been called worse by better men than her own reflection.

The wind came around with the evening, backing into the northeast the way it did before weather, and Ada stood at the rail and let it push at her like a hand on her chest, testing.

Fear was in her, no mistake about it. It sat in her stomach like ballast, cold and specific. But she'd chased storms for nine years, and she had learned the one lesson the sky taught better than any teacher: fear was fuel, if you pointed it right. Panic ran in circles. Fear, aimed, ran straight.

She went below, took up a pencil, and began to mark the tide tables.

First tide: reconnaissance. Run the coast dark, lie up in the marsh channels south of the basin, see the fleet's pattern with her own eyes. Second tide: the run in over the bar at low slack, if the recon allowed it. Third and fourth: the work, whatever the hold gave up. Fifth: out, heavy, slow, praying. Sixth: margin. Nothing worth having ever survived without margin.

She wrote the schedule on the back of a grocery list and looked at it and understood that she was looking at the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be. Every line of it assumed she could move faster than men who owned the harbors, the courts, and half the constabulary between here and Corvane. Every line of it assumed a boat, a body, and a luck that owed her nothing.

Outside, the last light went off the water, and the first stars came out hard and cold over the Reach. Somewhere beyond that darkness, lanterns were burning on the decks of the *Vane Fortune* and her sisters, and men were drawing grids on charts of their own, closing on the same coordinates from the seaward side.

Ada rolled Ede's map into its case, lashed the case inside her storm coat where it rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and went forward to raise the anchor.

The *Kestrel* came about toward the northeast with the last of the daylight, her bow lifting to the long swell, and Ada felt the fear in her gut change weight—not lighter, never lighter, but keener, honed to an edge she could steer by. Six tides. Whatever waited in the Serrano basin, she would reach it before the sea closed the door, or she would learn what the door was like from the inside.

She was thirty minutes past the harbor light when she saw the running lights ahead, close in under the land where no honest traffic ran—two vessels, dark-hulled, moving without navigation lamps, holding station across the channel like a hand across a doorway.

And standing at the rail of the nearer one, outlined against the last gray of the sky, a figure raised one arm—not in threat, not quite in greeting—and Ada's hand went cold on the wheel, because she knew the build, the set of the shoulders, the easy tilt of the head.

Not Pell. Someone else. Someone who, by tomorrow's gossip along the whole damned coast, had jumped ship from the *Vane Fortune* itself.
