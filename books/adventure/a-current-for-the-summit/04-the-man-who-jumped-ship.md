## 4. The Man Who Jumped Ship

Idris Vane had been counting the fleet's sins the way other men counted money—compulsively, in the dark, with a growing horror at the total.

He stood at the stern rail of the *Vane Fortune* as she wallowed through the grey swells off Cutter's Reach, and he did what he had done every night for eleven days: he watched the water and lied to himself about how long he'd been planning this. The truth was uglier. He hadn't been planning it at all until three nights ago, when he'd seen the manifest.

Navigators saw manifests. That was the arrangement. Charts, cargo weights, draft calculations—the arithmetic of moving things across water. Idris was good at arithmetic. He had been good at it since he was nine years old, since his father had put a slate in his hands on the deck of a coastal trader and said, *the sea forgives nothing, boy, but it respects a man who can count.* He could count. He could read a tide table like scripture. What he could not do, apparently, was stop reading once he'd started.

The *Meridian Star*. Salvage priority: absolute. Cargo classification: not bullion, not machinery, not the rum and rifles the Syndicate's front companies usually chased. Paper. Sealed document cases, forty-one of them, logged under a routing code Idris recognized because he'd seen it once before—in the file that had passed across Commodore Vane's desk the week after his brother Tam's body washed up under the pilings at Greywater Quay.

Tam had drowned. That was the finding. Tam, who had grown up swimming the cold brown rivers of the interior like an otter, who had once swum a flooded channel to fetch help while their father bled from a winch accident. Tam had drowned in four feet of sheltered water with his boots dry.

Idris had believed the finding for two years because believing it was easier than the alternative. Then he'd read the manifest, and the alternative had climbed out of the file and sat down beside him at the chart table, patient as death, and refused to leave.

"Navigator."

The voice came from behind him—Mr. Pell, of course, because it was always Pell, materializing out of the ship's shadows with that smile that never reached anything above his cheekbones. Idris turned and gave him the deferential nod the hierarchy required. Third-rank navigator to second-rank enforcer: nod, wait, speak when spoken to.

"You're up late," Pell said pleasantly. He leaned on the rail beside Idris as though they were friends, as though they were two men enjoying the night air together rather than a wolf standing next to something it hadn't yet decided whether to eat. "Trouble sleeping?"

"The storm line to the south," Idris said. It was even true. There was a storm building down there; he could feel it in the barometer he kept in his cabin like a priest keeps a relic. "I want the fleet's course adjusted before morning."

"Mm." Pell studied the horizon with the expression of a man admiring a painting he intended to burn. "The Commodore values thoroughness. That's why you're aboard." A pause, silk over wire. "You're not having doubts, are you? About the mission?"

"No, Mr. Pell."

"Good. Because a man who has doubts is a man who makes mistakes, and a man who makes mistakes—" Pell smiled fully now, warm as lamplight, terrible as a locked door, "—well. We look after our mistakes, Idris. That's what family means."

Family. The word landed where it always landed, in the bruise. The Syndicate called itself a family the way a spider calls its web a home. Everyone inside it was either eating or eaten, and the eating was always described as care.

"I'll have the revised course by dawn," Idris said.

Pell clapped him once on the shoulder—a hand like a shackle—and moved off down the deck, whistling something tuneless and cheerful. Idris watched him go and felt the last door inside himself swing shut.

Three nights ago he had only been afraid. Tonight he was decided.

* * *

The problem with defecting from the Vane Syndicate was that the Vane Syndicate owned the water.

Not legally—legally they owned a shipping concern, six salvage licenses, and a constellation of shell companies whose names changed like weather. But practically, functionally, in every way that mattered at sea: the water was theirs. Their fleet ran the coast from Greywater to the Summit Reaches. Their paid eyes sat in every harbor office. Their listening posts—Idris had plotted them himself, another sin added to the ledger—covered the channels like nets cover fish.

So he didn't run. Running was what they expected, and running men left wakes. Instead he did the thing no one defected by doing: he stayed at his post, kept his charts immaculate, adjusted the fleet's course south around the building storm exactly as promised, and waited for the one moment the routine would offer him.

It came on the fourth night, at the resupply anchorage off Cutter's Reach, when the *Fortune* sent her tender ashore for water and provisions and Idris volunteered to oversee the loading. Volunteering was in character. Navigators were fussy about their stores; everyone knew it. He went ashore with the shore party, helped roll barrels, complained amiably about the quality of the bread, and then, in the ten minutes between the tender's departure and its return, walked out of the lamplight along the tideline and into the dark, carrying nothing but a waterproof satchel, a knife, and the copy of the fleet's sailing orders he'd spent eleven nights memorizing and three hours transcribing by matchlight.

He didn't take a boat. Boats were tracked. He took the flats.

The tidal basin north of Cutter's Reach drained at low water into miles of glistening mudflat and sand, cut with channels deep enough to float a skiff and shallow enough to break an ankle. Local salvagers ran it in flat-bottomed craft during the season, which meant the Syndicate ignored it, which meant it was the one stretch of coast where Idris might move without appearing on someone's ledger. He knew the basin's rhythms from the survey charts—he had drawn half of them—but knowing a place on paper and knowing it under your boots in the dark were different countries, and he crossed the border badly. Twice he went into the mud to his knee. Once he lost a boot entirely and dug for it with his bare hands, heart hammering, certain that every light on the horizon was a search party.

By the time the grey came up out of the east he was three miles into the flats, soaked to the waist, one boot, and free. Free, he discovered, felt almost identical to terrified. The difference was mostly in what you told yourself.

He found shelter in the hull of a derelict—a grounded coastal freighter, beached so long she'd become geography, her ribs green with weed, her name plate long since pried away. He wedged himself into the shadow of her engine housing, ate half a tin of the bread he'd stolen from his own ship's stores, and began the work that would keep him alive: watching.

Because here was the truth he'd carried off the *Fortune* along with the satchel. Defection was not escape. Defection was a trade. You traded the devil you served for a chance—only a chance—at surviving him, and the currency of that trade was information, and the only buyers for Syndicate information were people the Syndicate wanted caught.

Which meant he needed to find one particular person. A storm chaser named Ada Okonkwo, debt-holder to the Syndicate, skipper of a patched grey ketch that the harbor clerks called *the Wren*, and—as of the intercepted supply requisitions Idris had read two days before jumping—recently provisioned for a long solo run into exactly the waters where the fleet was headed.

The fleet hunted documents. Okonkwo, if Ede's map was real—and the Syndicate clearly believed it was, or they wouldn't have burned fuel chasing a dead prospector's ghost—was hunting the same wreck. Two currents, one destination. Idris had spent his whole life reading water. This confluence he could read blind.

All he had to do was survive long enough to reach it, and then convince the most suspicious woman on the coast not to put a knife in him.

* * *

He found her on the third day, and it nearly went the way the knife predicted.

The *Wren* came ghosting up the outer channel at first light, riding the ebb with the kind of economy that made Idris's professional heart ache—no wasted sail, no wasted motion, the helm answering her hands the way a good instrument answers a musician. He'd seen her twice before, from a distance, at Greywater: a compact woman with close-cropped hair and a way of looking at harbors as though cataloguing exits. Up close, through his spyglass, she looked worse than that. She looked like a woman who had already decided everyone alive was a problem to be solved.

She anchored in the lee of the derelict hulk—his hulk, his shelter—and Idris understood within minutes that she'd done it deliberately. The wreck broke the wind and hid her mastline from the open sea. Good seamanship. Also, inconveniently, it put her thirty yards from the man hiding in her shadow.

He weighed his options for an hour while she slept at anchor, and then, because every hour increased the odds of a Syndicate patrol sweeping the flats, he stopped weighing and acted.

He waded out along the channel at slack water, hands visible, empty, and called up to the deck in a voice pitched to carry without carrying too far:

"Skipper Okonkwo. I need to talk, and you need to hear it. If you're going to shoot me, shoot me now, because the alternative is worse for both of us."

She came up from below fast—faster than he'd guessed she'd move—and there was a shotgun in her hands before her head cleared the companionway, leveled with the unhurried steadiness of someone who had done this before and would do it again without losing sleep.

"You've got five seconds," she said, "to explain why I shouldn't feed you to the crabs."

"My name is Idris Vane. Until four days ago I was third-rank navigator aboard the *Vane Fortune*." He let that land. Her eyes narrowed—not surprise, calculation; she was pricing the information already. "I jumped ship because I read the fleet's true orders, and what I read is going to get you killed unless you know it. The Syndicate isn't sending six vessels after salvage cargo, Okonkwo. They're sending them after paper. And they've been ordered to leave no witnesses at the site. None. That includes a storm chaser in a grey ketch whose name is already on their board."

Silence. The tide sucked softly at the mudflats. Somewhere inland a curlew cried its thin, rising note.

"You expect me to believe a Vane defected," she said at last. Her voice was flat, unreadable, but her knuckles had whitened on the stock of the gun. "Vanes don't jump. Vanes get thrown, and what's left floats."

"I'm not blood. I'm hired. There's a difference, and the difference is that I can leave." He kept his hands wide, his voice level. Every word was rehearsed and every word was true, and he knew—with the cold clarity of a man doing arithmetic on his own life—that the truth was the only currency she'd accept, and even it might not clear. "I know the fleet's route. I know their timetable, their patrol stations, their listening posts. I know which channels they think are impassable, because I'm the one who told them so. And I know what's in the hold of the *Meridian Star* that's worth six ships and a body count."

Something flickered across her face at the name. Confirmation, swift and quickly buried. She had the map. Ede's map. The whole rotten machine clicked forward one gear.

"Why tell me?" she said. "Why not just vanish? Run for the interior, disappear. Men do."

"Men try." He let a breath out slowly. Here it was—the part he'd rehearsed least, because it was the part that was actually true, and true things were harder to say smoothly. "Because vanishing doesn't fix anything. Because the people who killed my brother are going to keep killing people until somebody stops them, and I can't stop them alone, and you—" he gestured at the ketch, at her, at the whole improbable shape of her, "—are already sailing toward the same wreck with half the information and none of the protection. I'm offering a trade. My knowledge for passage. Nothing else."

"That's never all anyone offers."

"No," he agreed. "But it's all I'm asking for."

She studied him for a long moment, and Idris stood in the cold shallows and let himself be studied, and understood that whatever she decided in the next thirty seconds, she was deciding it with the same instrument he used—weighing risk against necessity, cost against gain—and that this, perversely, was the most hopeful thing about her. A woman who trusted easily would get them both killed. A woman who trusted nothing would too.

"Deck," she said finally, jerking the shotgun toward the ketch. "Hands where I can see them. You come aboard unarmed, you sleep aft where I can hear you breathe, and you give me one reason—one—to doubt you, I'll maroon you on the first sandbar I find. Are we clear?"

"Perfectly."

"And Idris?" Something moved behind her eyes, some old weather. "If you're lying to me, the crabs won't be quick enough to save you from me."

He climbed aboard the *Wren* with salt water streaming from his clothes, and as her deck came solid under his boots he felt the strange double weight of what he'd done settle onto him: grief and relief, terror and purpose, all of it fused into a single hard knot in his chest. He had traded the devil's house for a stranger's boat. He had bet his life on a woman with a shotgun and a dead man's map, and on his own ability to keep wearing sincerity like a coat that fit.

It fit well. That was the trouble. He'd worn it so long he sometimes couldn't feel the lining.

Below decks, while she made tea with a pistol within reach and asked her first question—"Start with the route. Exact"—Idris unrolled his copied charts on her table and began, calmly, precisely, to sell the Syndicate's secrets back to them at the price of his survival.

And it was only later—much later, watching her trace the fleet's projected course with one blunt finger, following the line straight toward the Meridian Star's grave—that he let himself notice the flaw in his arithmetic, the variable he hadn't priced in.

The fleet wasn't just sailing for the wreck.

Pell was sailing for *her*.
