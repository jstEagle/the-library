## 5. Terms of Passage

The cave Ada had chosen for the night was less a cave than a wound in the cliffside, a hollow where the sea had worried at the rock for ten thousand years and given up halfway. It was dry above the tideline, deep enough to hide a fire's glow from anything seaward, and narrow enough at the mouth that one person with a knife could make entry expensive. She had checked all of this twice before she'd let the man she'd pulled out of the surf sit down against the far wall.

Idris Vane—no, not Vane, he'd said, Idris, just Idris, and the way he'd said it told her the surname was something he'd cut off like a gangrenous finger—sat with his wrists bound in front of him by her own spare line, watching her build the fire with the patient attention of a man cataloguing everything she owned.

She let him look. Let him see the salt-crusted charts, the patched sail folded in its bag, the storm barometer wrapped in oilcloth that she handled the way other people handled infants. Let him do his arithmetic. Men like him were always doing arithmetic.

"You tied my knots wrong," he said.

"I tied them tight."

"Tight isn't right. A bowline backed up with a slip hitch—that's what you want on a prisoner. If he struggles, it cinches. What you've got will loosen." He flexed his bound hands, demonstrating. "You could learn things from me. That's rather the point."

Ada struck the match and touched it to the tinder without looking at him. "The last man who told me I could learn things from him was selling me an engine with a cracked block."

"And did you buy it?"

"I bought it. Then I made him swim home behind the boat until he confessed." The kindling caught. Orange light climbed the walls of the hollow, and Idris's face came out of the dark piece by piece—the high cheekbones, the bruise blooming along his jaw where the rocks had gotten their share of him before she had, the eyes that were lighter than she'd expected, gray-green, the color of shallow water over sand. Handsome in the specific way that men who worked indoors and lied professionally were handsome: well-fed, well-rested, hands too clean for the work they claimed to have left.

She sat down across the fire from him, laid her knife on the stone between them where he could see it, and began cleaning her boots with a rag as though he were no more interesting than weather.

"Ask your questions," he said. "I know you're going to. You've been circling them since you hauled me aboard."

"Fine." She set down the boot. "You say you're a navigator for the Syndicate fleet."

"I was. Past tense matters."

"You say you jumped ship because you saw what they were hunting. Not cargo. Documents."

"Ledgers," he said. "Yes."

"From the Meridian Star."

"From the Meridian Star." He said the name evenly, but she caught it—the half-breath before it, the small hitch of a man stepping onto ice he wasn't sure would hold. "You have the map. Ede's map. Don't bother denying it; I watched you burn the wrapping on the beach this morning. Wax paper, red seal, prospector's cipher. There are maybe four such maps in existence and the Syndicate holds two of them."

"So you followed me."

"I followed the map. You happened to be carrying it." Something moved through his face then, quick as a fish under a boat—there and gone, unreadable. "Though I'll admit the distinction stopped mattering somewhere around the second reef."

"The fleet knows you're gone."

"The fleet knows I'm gone."

"They'll assume you went after the same prize."

"They'll assume exactly that. Which is why every hour we sit here burning driftwood is an hour Pell closes the gap." He leaned forward into the firelight, and the shadows moved on his face like water. "Ada. Ask the real question. You've been saving it all evening and it's making you tedious."

She looked at him across the flames for a long moment. Then she picked up the knife—not threateningly, just to hold it, the way a person holds a thing they trust—and asked it.

"Why should I believe a single word that comes out of your mouth?"

* * *

He didn't flinch. She gave him credit for that. Most men, when a woman held a knife and asked them why they shouldn't be liars, found religion or humor or outrage. Idris did none of these. He simply sat back against the stone and considered the question as if it deserved consideration, which, she thought sourly, was probably its own kind of performance.

"You shouldn't," he said finally. "Believe me, I mean. On principle. You've known me for eleven hours, nine of which I spent unconscious or vomiting seawater. Everything I've told you about myself is unverifiable. My name, my rank aboard the Fortune, my reasons for leaving—all of it rests entirely on my say-so." He turned his bound hands over, studying the rope. "A rational woman would put me back in the surf and be done with it."

"A rational woman would."

"But you haven't. Which tells me either you're not rational, or you need something from me badly enough to gamble." His eyes came up and met hers across the fire, and there it was again, that quality she couldn't name yet—directness without warmth, honesty-shaped, like a door painted on a wall. "So let me tell you what I can offer that doesn't require belief. Facts. Checkable ones."

"Go on."

"The fleet sailed from Corrinne Basin nine days ago. Six vessels, flagship the Vane Fortune, Captain Ossler commanding, Mr. Pell riding along as the Syndicate's courtesy. Their first anchorage was the Sable Keys—they'll have watered there two days, no more, because Pell travels light and hates idle crews. From the Sable Keys they'll run the outer passage to the Meridian Shoals, staying wide of the Gannet Rocks because the Fortune draws nineteen feet and Ossler is careful to the point of cowardice." He recited it flatly, a list, a litany. "They expect the basin around the wreck to close for the season in six tides. They plan to be inside it on the third."

Ada kept her face still, but her mind was moving fast. The Sable Keys matched what the harbor gossip at Tern Landing had said—she'd heard it herself, three days ago, fishermen complaining about Syndicate tenders buying up fresh water at premium prices. And the six tides matched the map. Ede's dying arithmetic, scratched in the margin in a hand that shook: *basin shuts with the spring tides done. count six.*

"That's verifiable," she said slowly. "Or it's a story built out of true bones."

"Everything I give you will be checkable after the fact. That's the nature of what I'm offering. Navigation is a promise about the future—you can only audit it by surviving it." He smiled then, briefly, and it transformed his face into something younger and more dangerous. "I'm asking you to extend credit, Ada. I understand if your rates are punitive."

"My rates are punitive."

"I assumed."

She stood, mostly because sitting still felt like losing, and walked to the mouth of the hollow. Outside, the tide was coming back in over the flats, filling the channels with silver sound, and far to the south a smudge of cloud sat on the horizon like a bruise forming under skin. Weather coming. Maybe nothing. Maybe the edge of the system the barometer had been muttering about since noon.

Behind her, Idris said quietly: "There's a thing I haven't told you. About why the ledgers matter."

"Don't tell me."

"Why not?"

"Because if you tell me a reason, I'll have to decide whether it's true. And I can't decide that yet. Right now you're a useful stranger with a good story. In five minutes you might be a liar with a better one." She turned. The firelight reached her only faintly; she knew how she must look to him, half in shadow, knife still loose in her hand. "I need your charts, Idris. I don't need your soul. Keep the soul."

For a moment he said nothing at all, and in that silence she saw something flicker across his face that looked almost like respect—or hunger, or recognition, the expression of a man meeting someone who played the game the way he played it. Then he laughed, low and genuine, and the sound startled her with how much she liked it.

"Keep the soul," he repeated. "God. What did the sea do to you, Ada Okonkwo?"

"The sea didn't do anything to me. People did. The sea's the only honest creditor I've ever had—it takes exactly what it says it'll take." She crouched and fed another branch to the fire. Sparks swarmed upward toward the crack of sky above the hollow. "Now. Terms."

* * *

They negotiated the way sailors negotiate everything: quickly, precisely, and with the understanding that the real contract would be written later, in weather, when words ran out.

"Here's what I want," Ada said. "Your knowledge of the fleet's route, their timing, their thinking. Every day. You'll draw me the passages they'll use, the anchorages they favor, the shortcuts they think nobody knows. When we reach the basin, you'll get me into the wreck ahead of them or die trying, and I genuinely mean that second part."

"Understood. And in exchange?"

"In exchange, I keep you alive until the wreck. Food, water, a berth, my back in a fight. After the wreck—" she shrugged—"we settle accounts. If you've been straight with me, you walk away with whatever share a defector deserves. If you've been playing me—" she let the sentence hang, and let the knife finish it.

"That's not terms. That's a hostage arrangement with amenities."

"Yes," Ada agreed pleasantly. "That's exactly what it is."

He studied her across the fire, and she watched him study her, and there was something almost comfortable in it, two professionals taking each other's measure with the honesty of adversaries. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a night bird called across the flats, one long descending note.

"One amendment," Idris said. "If it comes down to it—if the fleet has us cornered and the only way out is through something ugly—I want to know you won't sell me back to them. Whatever they've offered you, whatever they'll offer you. Pell will offer. It's what he does. He offered me a promotion three days before I jumped, with that smile of his, and I want you to understand that smile is the most dangerous thing on that coast."

"Pell's already offered me plenty," Ada said. "He collects my debt with a smile too. I know the merchandise."

Something shifted in his expression at that—a quickening, a recalibration. "Your debt. To the Syndicate."

"That's my business."

"How much?"

"None of yours."

"Is it why you're running for the wreck? Money?" He said it without judgment, which somehow made it worse. "Because if it's money, fine, money's honest. But you should know what else is in that hold. And you should know that once you've seen it, money stops being the question."

"Then I'll keep my eyes shut," she said, "until I decide to open them."

He shook his head slowly, and there was something in it—not quite pity, not quite warning. "You can't. That's the trap of it. That's the whole design. The ledgers aren't cargo, Ada, they're a current. Once you're in the water with them, they carry you whether you swim or not." He leaned forward, firelight catching the gray-green of his eyes. "I've read fragments. Enough to know that the names in those pages aren't syndicate names. They're magistrates. Harbor commissioners. Half the coastal governments bankroll themselves out of that wreck's history, and the Vane family has been collecting the interest for thirty years. My brother read those pages. That's all I'll say tonight. That's the amendment to your terms: you keep me alive, and I keep telling you the truth one piece at a time, as you can bear it. Agreed?"

The word *brother* landed in the space between them and stayed there, glowing like a coal kicked out of the fire. Ada looked at it for a long time. She thought about debts—hers, Ede's, the dead prospector's blood drying into the wax paper in her cabin—and about promises, and about how every promise she'd ever kept had cost more than the ones she'd broken.

"Agreed," she said. "One piece at a time."

She cut his wrists free. He rubbed the circulation back into his hands, flexed his fingers, and did not reach for any weapon, did not make any sudden move, simply sat there being unbound in her territory and letting her watch him do it. That, too, was a kind of payment. She noted it the way she noted a shift in wind: data, possibly meaningful, possibly nothing.

"There's a chart in my kit," he said, nodding toward the bundle she'd confiscated with his boots. "Oilskin roll, brass clasp. The eastern passages. Drawn from the Fortune's own surveys—soundings the Admiralty doesn't have. Including a channel through the Squeeze that Ossler marked impassable."

"The Squeeze is impassable. I've run that coast eight years."

"The Fortune's draftsman never ran it at slack water with a following sea. I have." He held her gaze. "It saves us a full tide, Ada. Maybe two. It's also the reason I'm still breathing, because I'm the only navigator alive who believes it can be done."

Outside the hollow, the smudge of cloud on the southern horizon had thickened while they talked, stacking itself into something with shoulders. Ada felt the old pull in her chest, the one that had never once cared about debt or danger or sense—the storm-chaser's pull, the wanting-to-be-where-the-air-is-moving. Five tides now. Maybe four and a half, if the weather meant to spend them faster than the moon did.

She looked at Idris across the dying fire—at the defector with unverifiable truths and a dead brother and charts drawn by cowards—and made the bargain official the only way she knew how.

"We sleep in shifts," she said. "You first watch. If you move wrong, I'll know."

"How?"

"Because I sleep like the sea does. With one eye open and no mercy in it."

His mouth curved. "Terms accepted," he said, and took the first watch, and she lay down with her back to the stone and her knife under her hand and did not sleep at all—but somewhere in the grey hour before dawn, listening to him breathe and to the tide turning below, she caught herself cataloguing the sound of his voice the way she catalogued weather, and understood with cold clarity that she was in more danger than she'd planned for.

Far to the south, the storm stacked its shoulders higher, and the first fat drops of rain began ticking against the rock above the hollow like fingers counting down.
