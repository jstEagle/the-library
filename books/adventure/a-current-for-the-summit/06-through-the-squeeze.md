## 6. Through the Squeeze

Idris had drawn the Vane Fortune's charts for three years, and in all that time he had never once looked at the Kestrel Narrows without feeling the particular cold that came from knowing a thing was forbidden. The Syndicate's masters of the fleet had a word for the channel: *impassable*. They had it inked on their master plates in red, with the notation that any navigator who proposed it be relieved of duty and, if he insisted, of other things. The Narrows ate boats. That was the official history, and like most official histories Idris had helped maintain, it was true only in the way that served the men who paid for the ink.

The truth was narrower and more interesting: the Narrows ate boats that did not respect the tide.

He stood at Ada's shoulder in the wheelhouse of her chase boat—the *Sable Tern*, she called her, with the possessive tenderness other people reserved for children—and watched her read the water ahead. Rain had begun an hour ago, fat drops that struck the windshield like thrown gravel, and beyond the glass the sky over the headland had gone the color of a bruise going bad. The barometer on her bulkhead had been falling all morning in a slow, steady slide, the needle's honest confession of what was coming off the open sea.

"You're sure about this," Ada said. It wasn't a question. She said it the way people say things they have already decided, just to hear how the decision sounds in the air.

"No," Idris said.

Her mouth twitched. "Good. If you'd said yes, I'd have thrown you overboard."

"The Syndicate believes the Narrows can't be run except at slack neap water, and even then only by pilots who've done it fifty times." He unrolled his chart across her instrument console, weighting the corners with her coffee mug, a spool of electrical tape, a brass compass older than both of them put together. "That belief is worth more to us than a day's lead. Pell's fleet will go around the headland. That's nine hours of open water in a building sea. We go through here—" his finger traced the channel, a serpent's throat barely two hundred meters wide at its pinch, "—in forty minutes."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we're reef wreckage, and Pell won't even have to waste fuel looking for us."

Ada studied the chart the way he had seen her study weather: not reading it so much as interrogating it, waiting for it to lie. Her hands were steady on the wheel. Her eyes were not quite steady on him, and he understood why. He had given her three days of half-truths and one full truth—his brother's name, spoken once into a fire's smoke—and she had accepted neither as payment nor as proof. She was running with him because his knowledge was real and her alternatives were drowning. Trust would come later, if it came at all, and it would come earned in installments.

"Forty minutes," she said. "What's the tide doing?"

"Flood starts in twenty-six minutes. Current through the pinch runs four knots against us before it turns. After it turns, we ride it." He tapped the tide table penciled in his own hand along the chart's margin—figures he had copied from memory, because the originals lived in a locked drawer aboard the Vane Fortune and he would rather have cut off the hand that opened it than be caught doing so. "We enter on the last of the ebb, fight the worst of it for ten minutes, and then the flood carries us through like a hand closing around the hull."

"And the storm?"

"The storm is our friend until it isn't. Wind out of the southeast piles water into the eastern entrance—that raises the depth over the shoal by half a meter, maybe more. It also means that if we're late, or slow, or unlucky, the same wind puts breaking seas across the pinch that no boat survives." He met her eyes. "There is a window. It is not a wide one."

Ada looked at the sky for a long moment. Rain crawled down the windshield in ropes. Somewhere behind them, thirty miles back along the coast, the Syndicate fleet was provisioning, and somewhere ahead of them, under sand and green water, the Meridian Star waited with its dead men's ledgers and its fortune no one had claimed in twenty years.

"Then we don't be late," she said, and pushed the throttle forward.

* * *

The eastern entrance to the Kestrel Narrows announced itself first as a change in the water's voice. Out in the open roadstead the sea spoke in long, rolling syllables, swell after swell, patient as accounting. At the entrance the voice changed—shortened, sharpened, broke into argument. Current meeting current. Reef lying in wait under a fathom of green. Idris felt the sound in his sternum before he could pick out its sources with his eyes, and some animal part of him, the part that had signed aboard a Syndicate ship at nineteen because the alternative had been a fishing town with nothing in it, whispered that he was about to die in a place his own charts had condemned.

He silenced it with arithmetic. That was the trick of navigation nobody told you when you were young: it was not a science so much as a discipline of fear. You did the numbers until the numbers were louder than the whisper.

"Stand off the starboard side," he said. "Watch for the white water over Gannet Rock. When you see it, bring us down its east edge—close enough to spit on it. There's deep water in its shadow, but only for thirty meters. Then it shelves fast."

"Gannet Rock." Ada's voice was flat and concentrated. "You named your rocks."

"The Syndicate named them. I just memorized the names of everything they wanted forgotten." He didn't say: including me. He didn't say: my brother's name is on a list like that too. Some truths were cargo you carried below decks.

The rock showed itself forty seconds later—a ragged white scar on the gray water, foam standing up over stone like smoke from a drowned fire. Ada took the *Sable Tern* toward it with a calm that frightened him more than any panic would have. She didn't flinch at the breakers. She read them. He watched her eyes move across the white water, sorting it, finding the rhythm underneath the chaos, and then she swung the wheel and the boat slid into the rock's lee so close that Idris could have touched the wet black stone if he'd leaned far enough.

"Depth?" she snapped.

"Four fathoms. Three and a half. Hold that line—" He checked the lead line's marks, the soundings he'd taken from memory and prayed were memory and not invention. "Three fathoms. Shelf coming. Come left five degrees. Now."

The boat heeled. The shelf passed beneath them—he felt it in the way the water's texture changed under the hull, a smoothness where there should have been turbulence—and then they were through the entrance and inside the throat of the Narrows, and the world contracted to walls of wet granite and a corridor of churning green.

It was beautiful. That was the thing that undid him, in the first minute, before fear finished reclaiming him. The channel ran between cliffs streaked white with bird lime and hung with kelp that lifted and fell in the surge like hair underwater. Rain slanted through the gorge in silver veils. And the water itself moved with a muscular, deliberate power—not chaos but intent, the whole ocean funneling through a gap it resented, carrying them along whether they willed it or not.

"Current's turning," he called. "Feel it? We're starting to ride it now."

"I feel it." Ada's hands moved on the wheel in small corrections, constant, almost invisible. "Talk to me. What's next?"

"The Pinch, eight hundred meters. Two hundred meters wide, and there's a mid-channel sill—reef ridge, six feet under at low water, but the flood's already putting two feet over it. We go over the sill on the standing wave, dead center, no less. Miss center and the eddies on either side will spin us onto the walls."

"A standing wave. On a reef. In a rising storm."

"Yes."

She glanced at him, and in that glance, rain-streaked and furious with concentration, there was something that was almost laughter. "You are the worst navigator I have ever hired."

"You haven't hired me. You've kidnapped me with a bargain I can't refuse."

"Same thing on this coast." She faced forward again. "Center of the wave. Dead center."

* * *

The Pinch came at them the way all the worst moments of Idris's life had come: faster than preparation, louder than thought.

He saw it first as a line across the channel—a seam where the smooth flood current broke into standing crests, the water piling over the hidden sill and refusing to fall, holding its shape against the very physics that should have destroyed it. A river flowing uphill over a mountain. Beyond the seam the channel bent hard to port around a cliff face that shed its own counter-currents, and past the bend he could see nothing at all, only spray and the next unknown.

"Throttle back to half," he said. "Let the current do the work. If you power over the wave you'll bury the bow."

"If I throttle back too much the eddy on the right takes my stern."

"That's why you'll steer."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment in which the rain seemed to hang motionless in the air—Iridis understood that he had handed this woman his life entire, folded into a set of instructions, and that there was nothing left for him to do but watch. He had spent three years aboard the Vane Fortune learning to control: courses, cargoes, the small economies of fear that kept a crew obedient. Control had been his whole profession. And now he stood in a wheelhouse he did not command, in a channel his employers had written off as a grave, trusting his existence to a stranger's hands on a stranger's wheel, and the strangest thing—the thing he would remember years later, in other storms, in other lives—was that it felt like relief.

Ada hit the standing wave dead center.

The bow rose. The whole world tilted into gray sky and hammering rain, and for one heartbeat the *Sable Tern* climbed the stationary mountain of water like a creature ascending something alive—and then the crest passed beneath the keel and they were sliding down its far side, the hull humming, spray sheeting over the wheelhouse roof, and the channel bent away to port and Ada was already turning into the bend, riding the flood current's outer seam exactly as he had described it, as though his words had been drawn on the water and she was following the lines.

"Eddy!" he shouted, as the cliff's shadow threw its counter-current across their path.

"I see it." She saw it before he said it. Of course she did. She had been reading water since before he'd learned to read charts; the sea was her first language and navigation only his second. She feathered the throttle, kissed the wheel, and the eddy's grip slid off the stern like a hand missing its catch.

And then they were through.

Through the Pinch, through the bend, into the widening lower reach where the cliffs stepped back and the channel opened its throat and the flood current set them down the last mile like a current setting a leaf. The rain still fell. The storm still built behind them, a black wall walking up the horizon with the patience of something that owned the century. But inside the wheelhouse there was a silence, and in the silence Idris heard a sound he had not heard from himself in longer than he could calculate.

He was laughing.

It started low, in his chest, and came up out of him helpless and rusty, a mechanism unlubricated by years of disuse, and Ada turned from the wheel and looked at him as if he'd begun speaking in tongues.

"What," she said.

"We're through." He heard his own voice crack on it. "Do you understand—we're *through*—Pell is nine hours around the headland in seas that will make him miserable, and we are through the channel he thinks is a coffin, and it worked, the soundings were right, I remembered them *right*—"

"You weren't sure." Her eyes narrowed, but the corner of her mouth was fighting her. "You gave me figures you weren't sure of."

"I gave you figures I was ninety percent sure of."

"Ninety percent." She said it the way Pell might have said it, and for a second the resemblance made Idris's stomach drop—until he saw that she was laughing too, quietly, helplessly, her shoulders shaking against the wheel. "I staked my boat and both our lives on ninety percent."

"What was the alternative? The fleet?"

The laugh went out of her then, but not the warmth. Something else remained in its place, quieter, steadier. She looked at him across the wheelhouse, rain-wet, salt-crusted, and whatever she saw in his face must have matched something in hers, because neither of them looked away.

"You did well," she said. Simply. As if it cost her something to say it, and she paid anyway.

"So did you." He meant it more than he had meant anything in three years. "I gave you a line. You walked it."

* * *

They anchored in the lee pool below the Narrows' southern mouth while the storm arrived properly—as if the channel had been merely the storm's herald, and this was the court itself. The wind came in screaming gusts that flattened the pool's surface to white scud, and the *Sable Tern* rode her anchor in long, plunging arcs, and inside the cabin Idris discovered that his hands, which had been stone-steady through the whole passage, were shaking now that it was over.

Ada noticed. She always noticed; he was beginning to suspect it was her defining talent, this attention to what people tried to hide. She didn't comment. She put a tin cup of something hot into his hands—tea, terrible tea, boiled twice—and sat down across the little table from him with her own cup, and for a while they simply listened to the storm work.

"My brother used to sing in weather like this," Idris heard himself say.

He hadn't planned to say it. The words came up the way water comes up through a deck seam—finding the weakness without being asked. Ada said nothing. She had learned, in three days, when silence was the better question.

"He was the navigator, really. I just had the certificates. Tam had the gift—he could feel a current the way you feel a floor under your feet. The Syndicate found out he'd been reading the cargo manifests. Not stealing. Reading." Idris turned the cup in his hands. "They asked him questions for two days. Then they asked the sea one."

"And you ran."

"I survived. Running implies a direction." He looked up at her. "I have a direction now. It's the first time I've had one since him."

Ada held his gaze across the table, and the storm hammered the cabin top, and outside the anchorage the whole coast was darkening into night. When she finally spoke, her voice was rougher than the wind.

"When we reach the Meridian Star," she said, "and if the ledgers are there, and if they say what you think they say—what happens to you afterward? Revenge doesn't leave survivors, Idris. I've seen what it does to the people carrying it."

He considered lying. It would have been easy; it was nearly reflexive. Instead he said, "I don't know. I've been asking myself since the fire. All I know is that today, in that channel, for about forty minutes, I wasn't carrying it. I was just navigating. It was—" He searched for the word and found it and was embarrassed by it and said it anyway. "Joyful. It was joyful. I'd forgotten that existed."

Something moved through Ada's expression then, brief and unguarded, like light through a torn cloud. Recognition. She knew the feeling. He could see that she knew it—the storm chaser's joy, the joy that lived only inside catastrophe, the happiness that required danger to exist. Two people who had both learned to feel safe only when everything was trying to kill them.

"Get some sleep," she said at last, standing, collecting the cups. "Tide turns at 0400. We run the coast road north while the storm covers us, and tomorrow—" she paused at the cabin door, silhouetted against the rain-gray light, "—tomorrow we find out what Ede's map is really worth."

She went up to keep the anchor watch alone. Idris lay in the narrow bunk with the boat rolling under him and the storm shouting overhead, and sleep, when it finally took him, was dreamless and deep—the first such sleep in three years.

He woke at 0350 to Ada's hand on his shoulder and a different quality of darkness outside, and climbed into the wheelhouse to find her pointing north along the coast, where a cluster of lights sat on the water that had no business sitting there.

Running lights. Anchored. Arranged in a picket line across the approaches to the northern basin.

"Pell's advance ships," Idris said quietly. His blood went cold and clear. "They didn't go around the headland. Someone sent them through the Narrows."

Ada stared at the lights, then at him. "Nobody runs the Narrows in a storm."

"No," Idris agreed. "Nobody does. Which means whoever's commanding that squadron knows the channel better than the Syndicate's own charts admit." He reached for his chart case, and his fingers stopped halfway, and a thought arrived that he would spend the rest of the voyage trying to unthink:

Someone had taught them.
