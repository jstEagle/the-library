## 7. What Pell Knows

The Vane Fortune rode at anchor in the gray morning like a cathedral that had learned to float—three hundred feet of black-hulled certainty, her superstructure white as bone, her name picked out in gold leaf along the bow. Mr. Pell stood on the aft deck with his hands folded behind his back and watched the provisioning barges come alongside, and he did the thing he always did when he was about to deliver bad news: he smiled.

It was not a cruel smile. That was what people got wrong about him, always, and it was why he was so very good at his work. Cruelty was a blunt instrument, and blunt instruments left marks, and marks invited questions. Pell's smile was a courtesy extended across a gap. It said: *I understand your position. I am not your enemy. The arithmetic is simply against you.* Men had signed away their boats, their houses, their daughters' futures to that smile, and most of them had thanked him at the end.

He checked his watch. Seven minutes past eight. The captain of the Fortune kept a punctual ship, and Pell respected punctuality the way other men respected scripture.

Below, in the chart room, they would be waiting for him. He took the stairs rather than the lift—he liked the small ritual of it, the pause at each landing, the way it let him compose each sentence before the next one needed saying—and let himself into the room where three men sat around a table scarred by forty years of dividers and coffee rings.

They were not, strictly speaking, his superiors. That was the fiction. In truth, Mr. Aldous Vane's organization ran on a principle older than any org chart: the man who collects the debt owns the debtor, and the man who owns enough debtors owns everything else. But these three—Corrigan from Operations, the thin one called Sable whose title nobody used aloud, and old Marchetti, who had signed the original salvage charters back when the Syndicate had still bothered with paperwork—these three signed the orders Pell executed, so superiors they were, in every way that mattered at sea.

"Mr. Pell." Corrigan gestured to the chair opposite. "You look rested."

"I slept beautifully," Pell said, taking the seat. "There's nothing like open water for the conscience."

Sable made a sound that might have been a laugh. Marchetti said nothing. He was studying Pell with the flat patience of a man who had watched forty years of collectors come through this same door, and Pell felt the familiar prickle at the back of his neck—the one that told him old Marchetti could see all the way down to the mechanism.

"The navigator," Corrigan began. "Idris Voss. Walk us through it."

Pell walked them through it. He was good at this part; he had rehearsed it twice in his cabin, once aloud, which he found embarrassing but effective. The defection had happened at the coaling stop at Halfway Light. Voss had gone ashore with the shore party, ostensibly to verify the tide tables, and simply not returned. His bunk was stripped. His personal charts were gone—the working copies, not the masters, though Pell suspected Voss had memorized the masters years ago, because Voss was the sort of man who memorized things. And the launch boat was missing, which meant he'd had help or planning or both.

"He was our best route-finder," Corrigan said. It wasn't an accusation, exactly. It was worse. It was grief, corporate and bloodless.

"He was," Pell agreed. "Which is why I don't think it was impulse. A man doesn't jump ship on impulse after six years of faithful service. Something showed him the water was rising."

"And what would show a man that?"

Pell spread his hands. "I've been asking myself that since dawn. Voss saw the manifest addendum. Anyone who worked the navigation desk saw it eventually—it's not classified, it's just dull. Coordinates, tidal windows, cargo priority codes. Dull until you know what the codes mean." He paused, and here came the smile again, deployed now as armor rather than instrument. "Until you know we're not chasing silver."

Marchetti spoke for the first time. "Tell them."

So Pell told them, in the tone of a man reciting weather, because the tone mattered—if he delivered it like a confession it became one, and if he delivered it like a briefing it stayed a briefing, and Pell intended to remain a man who attended briefings.

"The Meridian Star went down eleven years ago carrying more than bullion and bonded cargo. She carried paper. Ledgers. Eleven years of the Syndicate's true accounts—not the ones we file, the ones we keep. Payments to harbormasters in four ports. Payments to two governors' private secretaries. Payment schedules for the coastal patrol cutters that somehow never inspected our salvage runs. Names, dates, amounts, countersignatures." He let that sit in the room like smoke. "Half the governments on this coast are in those books, gentlemen, and the other half are for sale. If those ledgers surface—in a courtroom, in a newspaper, anywhere—they don't just embarrass us. They take down everyone who ever shook our hand, and every one of those men will spend the rest of their lives explaining how it wasn't their fault. And the way a man explains that is by giving us up. Completely. Cheerfully. With documentation."

Silence. Somewhere above them, a crane groaned as it swung a crate of chain aboard.

"So the fleet isn't a salvage operation," Sable said softly.

"The fleet has never been a salvage operation," Pell said. "The cargo is bait. The ledgers are the mission. We find the wreck first, we burn the paper, and we salt the site so thoroughly that no honest diver will ever trust what's left of it. That has been the mission since the day the Meridian Star's last surviving officer sold us her final position. Everything else—the tides, the season window, the expense reports—is theater."

"And Voss knew this?"

"Voss read the addendum the way he reads everything. Completely." Pell turned his teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a small fussy gesture he permitted himself only in private company. "I believe he also read something else. There was a name in the ledgers' index—a crew list, an incident report from nine years ago. The Voss boy. The brother. The one who worked our records office in Port Anselm and drowned under circumstances the coroner called accidental."

"You think that's why he jumped."

"I think," Pell said carefully, "that a man who jumps ship over money can be bought back, and a man who jumps over pride can be flattered back. But a man who jumps over his brother's body—" he lifted the cup, drank, set it down with a click, "—that man isn't running *from* something anymore. He's running *toward* it. Those are different animals entirely, and you hunt them differently."

Corrigan leaned forward. "Then say the word, Mr. Pell. What do we do?"

What Pell wanted to say was: *nothing*. What he wanted to say was: let the man go, write off the charts, run the operation clean, because every hour they spent hunting Idris Voss was an hour the wreck sat unguarded and an hour in which the arithmetic of witnesses grew worse. What he wanted, privately, in the locked drawer of himself where he kept the things he never said, was to go home. To his apartment above the chandlery in Port Anselm, to his records, his tidy columns, the quiet life of a man who collected debts and asked no questions about what the debts were *for*.

Because here was the thing Pell had begun to suspect, in the small hours, in the creaking dark of his cabin: he had spent twenty years being the Syndicate's smile, and smiles wear out. He had collected from drowning men until he could no longer quite remember whether he was the kind of man who threw ropes or the kind who weighed anchors. And lately, when he looked at the young ones coming up through the ranks—all elbows and appetite, hungry for exactly the promotion being dangled in front of him—he felt something he refused to call fear and therefore called indigestion.

But none of that was a briefing. So instead he said:

"We assume Voss makes contact with local talent. There's a storm-chaser works these waters—Okonkwo. Small boat, big reputation, debts with us that are—" he consulted a card he didn't need to consult, "—substantial. If Voss needs a pilot through the basin country, she's the likeliest hire, and she's already motivated to say yes. I want her account flagged. I want every harbor clerk from here to the delta reporting her movements. And I want the fleet underway by noon tide."

"And if you find them?" Sable asked. "Voss. The woman. Whoever's carrying that map."

Pell looked at the table. At the coffee rings, at the decades of decisions soaked into the wood like stains into a hull.

"No witnesses," Marchetti said, before Pell could answer. The old man's voice was dry as ballast. "That's the order, Mr. Pell. From upstairs. No witnesses. Not the defector, not the prospector if he's alive—and he won't be—not the woman, not anyone who's seen the addendum or heard its name spoken. You'll make it clean. You're good at clean."

And there it was. The thing beneath the thing. Pell had known it was coming—that was why he'd rehearsed the briefing twice, why he'd taken the stairs slowly, why he'd worn his best coat. Because *no witnesses* was a phrase with a specific grammar, and in that grammar, the man who executed the order was always, eventually, listed among the nouns.

He thought of the coroner's report on the Voss boy. *Accidental.* He thought about how many accident reports he had personally delivered to grieving families with a smile and a condolence envelope, and how he had never once, until this grey morning, wondered what the man who ordered the accidents had looked like while he gave the order.

"Yes sir," Pell said. His smile arrived on schedule, warm and correct, and held. "No witnesses."

Corrigan nodded, satisfied. Sable was already writing. Only Marchetti kept looking at him, that long flat look, and Pell understood suddenly that the old man had heard the hesitation—that Marchetti had spent forty years listening to collectors hesitate, and filed each one somewhere, and that Pell's name had just gone into the file.

Dismissed, he climbed back up into the daylight. The barges were pulling away, loaded and light. On the foredeck, sailors were unfurling canvas with the easy rhythm of men who believed the ship they served was a ship like any other. The wind had freshened from the southeast. It smelled of rain and far-off thunder, of a season turning, of storms gathering out beyond the headlands where the charts went vague.

Pell stood at the rail and let the wind move his coat around him, and he did the arithmetic he was so famous for. Six tides, give or take, before the basin closed. Two ships lost to weather last season. One defector with a perfect memory. One debtor with nothing left to lose. And himself—a smiling man, mid-deck, mid-career, carrying an order in his breast pocket like a stone.

*No witnesses.*

He took out his watch, closed it, and gave the order to make way.

The Vane Fortune came about with ponderous grace, her wake unspooling white behind her, and steamed south toward the tidelands—toward the wreck, the ledgers, the woman named Okonkwo, and whatever version of himself Pell would have to be by the time the sixth tide came in. Above the horizon to the southeast, low and bruise-colored, the first wall of the storm was building, patient as a creditor.

It was going to be, Pell thought, adjusting his cuffs, a very interesting week.

* * *

By nightfall the fleet had formed up in column astern of the flagship—four hulls, running lights doused, threading the coastal dark toward the basin country. Pell dined alone in his cabin on roast duck and half a bottle of white wine, and afterward he sat at his desk with the fleet's master chart spread before him and did what he always did before a collection: he studied the debtor.

Okonkwo. Ada Okonkwo. He had a folder on her thicker than most, because he'd built it himself over three years of cheerful, courteous ruin. The boat, the *Kestrel*, mortgaged twice. The equipment liens. The medical debt from a partner who'd died in a squall four seasons back—Pell remembered sending flowers to that one, genuinely meant, and remembered also filing the lien the same afternoon. She was good. By every report she was better than good; she read water the way Marchetti read men. And she was cornered, and cornered people were predictable, and predictable people were collectible.

Except.

He turned his pen over in his fingers and looked at the chart without seeing it. Except that cornered people were predictable right up until they weren't. Except that a woman with nothing left to lose had a wider range of motion than any man in the Syndicate's employ, Pell included. Except that somewhere out in that darkness, a defector with a dead brother and a perfect memory was sitting in some smoky harbor bar or borrowed bunk, telling a woman with nothing left to lose exactly where the bodies were filed.

Pell poured the last of the wine and drank it standing at his porthole, watching the phosphorescence boil green in the flagship's wake. Twenty years, he thought. Twenty years of smiles and condolence envelopes and clean work, and it had bought him a cabin on a fine ship, a folder full of ruined lives, and an order in his pocket that had his own name written between its lines, in ink no one could see yet.

He was not afraid. He made a point of not being afraid; fear was bad arithmetic. But unease sat in him like ballast, low and permanent, and as the Vane Fortune drove south through the dark toward the tidelands, Mr. Pell stood at his porthole practicing his smile in the black glass, and noticed—for the first time in twenty years—that it took effort.

Out ahead, unseen, the sixth tide was already on its way.
