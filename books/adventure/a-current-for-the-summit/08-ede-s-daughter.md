## 8. Ede's Daughter

The detour cost her a tide and a half.

Ada told herself it was arithmetic—two hours up the coast road to Saltmere, two hours back, and the *Kestrel* could still make the third low water with room to spare. But she knew the shape of a lie when she was living inside one. The truth was that Ede had died holding her wrist with a grip like rusted cable, and the last coherent thing he'd said wasn't the coordinates or the warning about the fleet. It was *Nara. Tell Nara I kept it.* And a promise made to a dying man in a pool of his own blood was the only kind of debt Ada had ever paid without being chased for it.

Saltmere sat where the river gave up and became estuary—a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets, a chapel with a lightning-split steeple, a single street that ended at the mudflats like a sentence cut off mid-word. Ada tied off at the public dock between a crab boat and something that had once been a pleasure yacht and was now a planter full of dead geraniums, and walked up into town wearing her storm-chaser's coat and trying not to look like what she was: a woman carrying another woman's inheritance in a waterproof tube against her spine.

She asked at the chandlery first. The man behind the counter had forearms like hams and the flat stare of someone who priced everything, including answers.

"Nara Ede." He chewed on the name. "You mean Nara Voss. She married. Doesn't use his name anymore." He said *his* the way you'd spit out a fishbone. "Net shed past the chapel. The blue one. But I wouldn't if I were you."

"Why not?"

"Because she throws things." He rang up nothing and looked at her. "And because whatever you're selling, she stopped buying years ago."

"I'm not selling."

"They all say that." He turned back to his ledger, which seemed to be the local religion.

The net shed was exactly as advertised—blue paint gone chalky, roof sagging like an old man's shoulders, but the door hinges were new and oiled, and through the gaps in the boards Ada could see order inside: nets hung by weight, floats sorted by size, tools on pegboard outlines painted in their own silhouettes. A woman who had built discipline out of wreckage. Ada recognized the architecture. She lived in it herself.

She knocked.

"Closed."

"It's about your father."

Silence long enough that Ada counted her own heartbeats. Then the door opened six inches, on a chain, and one eye regarded her—dark, level, unimpressed. The eye traveled down Ada's coat, took in the salt stains, the rope burns on her hands, the tube strapped across her back, and came back up with its verdict already rendered.

"You're from a fleet."

"No."

"A salvage company."

"No."

"Then you're worse. You're freelance." The eye didn't blink. "He sent you."

"He didn't send me. He—" Ada stopped. Rehearsed speeches curdled in her throat. "He died three days ago. On the flats below Graystone Point. I found him. He was dying when I found him. There wasn't anything—I couldn't—" She heard her own voice fray and hated it, and made herself finish anyway. "He gave me something. He asked me to bring it to you. That's all. That's the whole of it."

The chain rattled. The door opened.

Nara Ede—Voss—was perhaps thirty, built like the coastline itself, all hard edges over deep water. Her hands were wrapped at the knuckles, and Ada noticed the wraps were fresh, the kind you put on after hitting something. Or someone. She wore grief the way some people wore wet wool: close, miserable, refusing to take it off in front of company.

Inside the shed smelled of tar and hemp and old coffee. Nara poured two cups without asking, set one on a barrel within Ada's reach, and did not sit.

"Show me."

Ada unslung the tube. Her fingers hesitated on the cap—not over the map, not anymore; she'd memorized every line of it by candlelight—but over the other thing, the small oilcloth packet Ede had pressed into her hand along with it. *For her. Not before. She'll know why.* She set both on the barrel between them.

Nara opened the map case first. Her face did something complicated and then went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes. "This is his drafting hand," she said quietly. "Older. Before he got sloppy." She rolled it closed with more care than she'd handled anything else in the room, including, Ada suspected, her own children, if she had any. Then her eyes came up. "Where exactly."

"The tidal flats. Graystone Point. Gut wound. He'd been shot hours before, maybe more—he'd packed it with moss and kept walking. Kept walking." Ada shook her head. "I've seen men give up on less. He was navigating by pain and he still had his bearings."

"That sounds like him." Nara said it like an accusation. She picked up the oilcloth packet, turned it over twice, and set it down unopened. "What did he tell you? Exactly. Word for word. Don't clean it up."

Ada gave it to her straight: the promise, the phrase about keeping it, the delirium near the end when he'd talked to people who weren't there. "He apologized," Ada said. "To somebody named Odile. And somebody called the Fourth. I don't know who they are."

Something moved behind Nara's eyes—recognition, quickly caged. "Odile was my mother. She's been dead eleven years." She finally sat, perching on the edge of a net chest as though sitting were a concession she might revoke. "Now you'll answer my questions, because I have them, and I've had them for twenty years, and everyone who could answer is either dead or lying."

"Ask."

"What was he doing out there? Graystone flats are forty miles from anywhere he had business. Nobody walks forty miles on a gut shot unless they're going somewhere specific or running from something specific. Which was it?"

"I think both. I think he was going to the basin—the wreck—and something caught him before he got there."

"The wreck." Nara's voice flattened further, if flattening were possible. "The Meridian Star."

"You know it."

"I know he spent the last six years of his life obsessed with it. Drawings on every surface of our house until I was twelve. Coordinates muttered in his sleep. Then one day the drawings stopped and so did he, mostly, and he started taking work with—" she paused, choosing, "—with people I wasn't allowed to ask about. And then he left. Not dramatically. No shouting. Just gone one morning, and a letter every few months after that, each one shorter than the last, none of them ever saying where he was or what he was doing, and every single one of them smelling faintly of engine oil and fear." She stood again, restless, and Ada understood she was a woman who could not be still inside this conversation. "So. What was he doing out there?"

Ada weighed it. Every instinct honed by six tides and a Syndicate deadline said *tell her nothing, protect the map, protect yourself.* But there was a debt-collector's smile waiting for her back home too, and she knew precisely what it cost to carry knowledge alone, and looking at Nara's face she saw the same isolation worn thinner, longer, all the way down to the bone.

"He was going to salvage it," Ada said. "Or reach it, anyway. It holds cargo. Valuable cargo. And he wanted it delivered to you—not sold, not turned over to any fleet. To you."

"To me." Nara laughed, and it was an awful sound, joyless as a gull's cry. "He leaves when I'm twelve. Sends me scraps for twenty years. And now he dies trying to hand me a fortune, like money was ever the thing that was owed." She picked up the oilcloth packet again and held it without opening it. "Did he tell you what he was? Before?"

"He didn't tell me anything except the coordinates and your name."

"Then let me educate you, since you rowed all this way." Nara's voice dropped into something older than anger. "My father was Syndicate. Not a debtor, not a runner, not some poor fool they squeezed. He was *in*. Surveyor, then navigator, then salvage master. He drew charts for the Vane Fortune's predecessors when the Fortune was still called something else. He knew where every wreck on this coast was buried before the storms that sank them finished cooling. Everything he had, everything we had—the house, my schooling, the boat he taught me to sail on—we had because the Syndicate paid him, and the Syndicate pays for exactly one thing." She opened her wrapped knuckles, flexed them. "Loyalty. And when he finally grew a conscience—or got scared, I never learned which—he ran, and they let him run, because a man like Ede is worth more loose than dead. Loose, he draws maps for freelancers. Loose, he leads treasure-hunters onto reefs. Loose, he's bait."

The word landed in Ada's stomach like ballast.

"Bait," she repeated.

"Bait," Nara said. "Think about it. A dying prospector, conveniently found by a stranger, pressing a map into her hands. You think that story ends anywhere good?" But even as she said it, Ada watched doubt cross her face—because she had read the drafting hand, and whatever else Ede had been, the map had been drawn in earnest, in the old careful style, and Nara knew it too.

"He was shot," Ada said. "Nobody shoots their own bait in the gut and leaves him to crawl forty miles."

"Unless the shot wasn't theirs. Unless he was running from them *toward* the wreck, and they let him bleed." Nara closed her fist around the packet. "That's how they do it. They don't chase. They wait. They're very patient people, the Syndicate. They smile while they wait."

Ada thought of Mr. Pell's smile, the warmth of it, the way it never reached past his teeth. She thought of the salvage fleet provisioning at Corren's Landing, and the six tides counting down, and how every mile of open water since Ede died had felt too easy, too unwatched.

"How do you know so much?" she asked. "If he left when you were twelve."

"Because he came back. Twice. When I was nineteen and again four years ago. Both times at night, both times smelling of salt and terror, both times talking in circles until I lost my temper and—" she gestured at her wrapped knuckles, "—well. The second time he told me enough. That he'd seen documents aboard the Star when it went down, or knew what was in the hold besides cargo. That half the coastal governments would burn if it ever surfaced. That's why nobody had salvaged it in thirty years, he said. Not because it was hard to find. Because it was *dangerous* to find. Because the people who sank it—or failed to save it, he was never clear—were still alive, still powerful, and still watching the basin." She looked at Ada with sudden, piercing directness. "Who else knows you have that map?"

"One person. Maybe two. A defector from the fleet."

"A defector." Nara said the word like she was tasting spoiled milk. "And you trust him."

"No."

"But you're sailing with him."

"Yes."

Nara studied her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her laugh again, softer this time, almost human. "God. You're just like him. You know that? Same look. Same stupid nobility with a boat attached." She turned away, and her shoulders, which had been armor all morning, finally dropped a fraction. "He kept it," she murmured, mostly to herself. "Twenty years, and he kept it. All those letters saying nothing, and the whole time he was—" She stopped, jaw working. When she spoke again her voice was scraped raw. "I'm not going to forgive him. I want that understood. Dead men don't get forgiveness handed to them by strangers. Whatever he was to you—a saint, a martyr—that's your business. To me he was a man who chose the Syndicate over his family and then chose a wreck over coming home, and I will bury him angry, and that's my right."

"I'm not asking you to forgive him."

"No. You're asking me to take the packet." Nara looked down at the oilcloth in her fist. "And I will. But understand what you've done, storm-chaser. You didn't deliver a map today. You delivered the last twenty years of my father's life, and now I have to figure out what to do with them, and I am *not* a woman who does nothing."

She unwrapped the packet. Inside was a photograph, creased white with folding—a young Ede in Syndicate grays, standing on a deck Ada recognized from her nightmares, the Vane Fortune's predecessor, and beside him a woman with Nara's exact dark eyes, and between them a girl of maybe eight. On the back, in Ede's old careful hand: *Before. When I still believed the current only ran downhill.*

And beneath the photograph, folded smaller, a second sheet. Nara smoothed it on the barrel and went absolutely rigid.

It was a chart fragment. Hand-drawn, same drafting hand, but newer—the ink barely faded. It showed the approach to the tidal basin from the seaward side, the channel markers, the sandbars that shifted with the season. And in the corner, a notation in Ede's cramped script:

*Second half. If the first map travels, this completes it. The hold opens true only from the channel no fleet will risk. —E.*

Ada leaned closer, her pulse climbing. "This is the eastern approach. Through the Squeeze."

"You know it?"

"I know of it. Everyone says it can't be run."

"Everyone says that about everything my father ever did." Nara folded the fragment with the photograph and tucked both inside her coat, and when she turned back to Ada her grief had been transmuted into something harder and far more useful. Purpose has that chemistry. "Here's what happens now. You're going to tell me when you sail, and I'm going to be on your deck, because that map is half mine by blood and half mine by promise, and because whoever shot my father is going to be at that basin, and I have questions that only a gun or a witness can answer." She held up a hand before Ada could object. "Don't argue. You need crew you trust, and you clearly don't trust the defector, and I've run these flats since I could walk. My boat's faster than yours in shallow water and I don't scare."

"Everyone scares."

"Then I'll scare productively." For the first time, something that was almost a smile crossed her face, and it was Ede's smile, and it hurt to look at. "One condition. Whatever's in that hold—whatever proof, whatever ledgers, whatever names—it doesn't go to any fleet, and it doesn't go to any buyer. It goes where it can burn the Syndicate down. That was his unfinished business. Now it's ours."

Outside, the light had begun to fail, and Ada thought of the tide tables pinned above the Kestrel's wheel, of the hours bleeding away, of Idris waiting at the dock pretending not to watch the road. Six tides. Five and a half now.

"Nara," she said carefully. "There's something else you should know. The fleet isn't just hunting the cargo. They think we have the map. And there's a chance—my defector thinks there's a chance—they let your father go on purpose. That they wanted the map moving, wanted someone to lead them to it."

Nara was quiet for a moment. Then she reached under the net chest and came up with a canvas tool roll, which she opened on the barrel to reveal not tools but a short-barreled shotgun, cleaned and oiled and hungry.

"Good," she said. "Then I won't feel guilty about what I'm planning to do to them."

* * *

They walked down to the dock together as the last light went copper on the water. Idris rose from the Kestrel's rail where he'd been leaning, and Ada watched the two calculations meet across ten feet of planking—his quick assessment of Nara, hers of him, two survivors pricing each other to the penny.

"This is Nara," Ada said. "Ede's daughter. She's coming with us."

Idris's eyebrows lifted. "Does she know what she's sailing into?"

"She knows better than either of us," Ada said. "Her father drew half the charts."

Nara stepped aboard without asking permission, looked around the Kestrel's deck with a professional eye, and then fixed Idris with a stare that could have stripped barnacles. "You're the defector. My father was Syndicate too, once. So before you decide whether to trust me, understand—I know exactly how you people say sorry." She smiled Ede's terrible smile. "And I know how you say goodbye."

Out past the harbor mouth, far down the darkening coast, thunder was building over the water where the Syndicate fleet lay at anchor—and the fourth tide was coming whether they were ready or not.
