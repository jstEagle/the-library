## 9. The Ledger's Shadow

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Idris finally spoke, and he chose the moment deliberately, because a man confesses best when the light is too low to see his face.

They were beached in a cove on the seaward side of Gannet Shoal, hull of the *Kestrel* grinding softly against sand with each swell, and Ada sat across the coals mending a chafe in the jib sheet with the patient, furious concentration of someone who would rather do anything than look at him. She'd barely spoken since the third tide. Since the listening post. Since they'd run blacked-out through a channel no wider than her mast is long while a Syndicate hydrophone array listened to their engine note and catalogued it.

He watched her hands work. Needle in, needle out. She stitched the way she steered—economical, exact, as if waste were a moral failing.

"You should sleep," she said, without looking up.

"I will."

"You won't."

"No," he admitted. "I won't."

She bit the thread and tied it off, and only then did she raise her eyes to him across the fire, and Idris understood that she had been waiting for this all evening—that Ada Okonkwo read silences the way other people read charts, and his had been screaming at her since sundown.

"Say it, then," she said. "Whatever it is you've been carrying since the *Vane Fortune*. Say it now, before we're inside the basin, where I can't afford to doubt you."

*Where I can't afford to doubt you.* Not *before I stop doubting you.* She was honest even in her dishonesty, and something about that undid him more than any interrogation could have.

He fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch.

"My brother's name was Tomas," he said.

Ada went very still. He felt the stillness from across the flames, the way you feel a barometer falling.

"He was four years older than me. He joined the Syndicate first—navigation, same as me eventually, because that's what we were, a family of men who knew water. Our father ran pilot boats out of Corren Bay before the Syndicate bought the harbor rights and made pilots into employees. Tomas went to work for them because there was nowhere else. That's the thing nobody tells you about the Vane Syndicate. It doesn't conquer. It just arrives, and then everywhere else has closed."

The stick burned through in the middle and folded into the coals. He didn't reach for another.

"Tomas was good. Better than good—he could read a tide table like scripture, and old Mr. Vane himself used to say so at the fleet dinners, put his hand on Tomas's shoulder in front of everyone. My brother believed that hand meant something. For eleven years he believed it." Idris heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when he was holding something down under pressure, like a hatch against flooding water. "Then three years ago he was assigned to inventory a recovered wreck. A salvage job. Routine. Except the hold wasn't cargo. It was paper. Sealed tubes, oilcloth, wax-stamped. And Tomas—who was curious the way our father was curious, which is to say fatally—read them."

"The ledgers," Ada said quietly.

"The ledgers." He nodded. "Names. Dates. Payments. Half the coastal magistrates on the Syndicate's payroll, customs officers, harbor masters, two federal auditors. Everything the Meridian Star carried when she went down, or enough of it that the Syndicate has spent thirty years making sure no one else ever finds the rest. Tomas copied pages. He thought copies would protect him. He thought being valuable would protect him. He thought—" Idris stopped. Started again. "He thought the hand on the shoulder meant something."

The tide was coming back in below the tideline, filling the cove inch by inch, and the sound of it seemed very loud in the silence.

"They found out how?"

"A clerk. There's always a clerk. They strung it up as an accident—tomas went down inspecting a hull patch, air line fouled, terrible tragedy, full honors. I stood at the funeral next to Mr. Pell himself, who put his hand on my shoulder and said the Syndicate takes care of its families." Idris looked up, and let her see his face fully in the firelight, because that was part of the confession too. "And I smiled at him. I shook his hand. I took Tomas's berth and I navigated for them for three years and I waited, because I'm a coward, Ada, and cowards are patient. Then came the order that changed everything. The fleet wasn't provisioning for salvage. We were hunting coordinates—the same coordinates you carry—and command said the words *no witnesses*, casual as ordering dinner, and I understood that whatever Tomas had found, it was still alive down there, still dangerous enough to kill over, and that I had been sailing toward my brother's grave for three years calling it employment."

Ada hadn't moved. The needle hung forgotten in her fingers, the mended sail draped across her knees like a shed skin.

"So no," he said. "I didn't jump ship for conscience. Conscience is what I told myself. The truth is smaller and uglier. I jumped ship because the ledgers killed my brother and I want the Syndicate to burn, and I have spent three years learning exactly where to strike the match."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the flats a night bird called, once, and got no answer.

Ada set down the sail. Carefully, the way she did everything, but he saw her hands weren't quite steady, and took a strange comfort in that—that she wasn't iron all the way through after all.

"Do you know what I was doing three years ago?" she said.

He shook his head.

"Drowning. Literally. My boat went down in the Squall Line incident—you'll have seen the reports, they blamed pilot error, my error—and I lost everything except the debt. The boat I chase storms in now? Leased. Every run I make, half goes to Pell's people, and the interest grows faster than I can outrun weather, which is the one thing I used to be able to outrun anything." She laughed, short and without humor. "So here's what I understand about your brother, Idris. He trusted an institution because trusting was cheaper than fighting. And here's what I understand about you. You've been fighting so long you don't remember how to do anything else. And both of those are ways of dying slowly."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's not." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving her face into planes and shadow. "Here's my answer. If we find those ledgers, and you get your hands on them, what happens? Walk it through with me. You take copies to whoever will print them. The Syndicate burns a few names, buries a few clerks, promotes a few Pells, and keeps sailing. Or you don't take copies anywhere—you use them as leverage, trade them back for Tomas's name cleared, and the Syndicate sails anyway. Or—" her voice dropped, and gentled, which was worse, "—or you burn the whole thing down knowing it takes us with it, because vengeance doesn't check who's standing next to the powder."

"And if I promise—"

"Don't promise." Sharp now, cutting him off. "Promises are what Tomas made. Promises are what I made to Pell's smiling face every quarter. I don't want promises. I want to watch your eyes when you tell me what you are."

The tide lapped closer. The fire sank lower. Idris found that his hands had closed into fists without his permission, and opened them deliberately, finger by finger, the way you'd open a cramped helm grip after a long blow.

"What I am," he said slowly, "is a man who spent three years memorizing the shape of a wound. And what I want—what I actually want, underneath the burning—is for someone to say my brother's name out loud in a room where it means something again. Not vengeance. *Restoration.* There's a difference. Vengeance wants the Syndicate to hurt. Restoration wants the truth to exist whether they hurt or not." He met her eyes. "I can't prove to you tonight which one wins, on the day, in the hold, with the papers in my hands. Nobody could. All I can tell you is that I know the difference exists, and I'm asking you to help me choose right instead of choosing fast."

Ada was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the fire burned down to embers and the first gray suggestion of dawn touched the eastern rim of the cove.

"My mother used to say," she said at last, "that damage recognizes damage. That two broken things can either cut each other worse or fit together at the break lines. She said you can't know which until you try, and trying is the most expensive thing there is." She picked up the sail again, folded it, stowed it. "So here are my terms, Idris. Not promises—terms. You navigate us to that wreck. When we open the hold, I touch the ledgers first. Whatever happens after—with the Syndicate, with the courts, with your brother's name—I decide it with you, not around you. And if the day comes when your vengeance and my survival point different directions—" she held his gaze, unflinching, "—you tell me before you act. That's the whole price. Tell me first. Can you pay it?"

"Yes," he said. And found, to his surprise, that it cost him nothing, because it was the first true thing he'd offered anyone in three years.

She nodded once, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch—not trust, not yet, but the decision to spend trust like money she couldn't afford, which he was beginning to understand was simply how Ada loved things: recklessly, on credit, against all sense.

"Sleep," she said, softer now. "Fourth tide waits for no confessions."

He lay back against the hull and did not sleep, not really, but drifted in that gray country between, and once—just once—he felt her sit down beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his, neither of them saying anything, two damaged things fitted together at the break lines, holding.

It was Nara's voice from the ridge above the cove, sharp with alarm, that broke the spell an hour later:

"Ada! Sail on the horizon—two of them—and they're not running from the storm. They're running *with* it."

* * *

Idris was up the dune before the echo died, glass to his eye, heart going like a drum in a gale.

Two sails, southeast, riding the leading edge of a building squall with the confidence of ships that knew exactly where they were going. And he knew that heading. Knew it the way you know a scar.

They weren't searching the coast.

They were converging on the Meridian Star's basin—and the sixth tide was still two days away.

"They've got the schedule," he said, lowering the glass, voice grim. "Someone gave them the tidal window. Which means someone knows we're coming—and knows exactly when the sea will open the grave."

Ada took the glass. Her jaw tightened as she studied the horizon, and when she spoke, her voice was flat and cold as deep water.

"Then somebody's been ahead of us this entire time."
