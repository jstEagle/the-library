## 10. Third Tide, False Harbor

The anchorage appeared on no chart Ada owned, which was precisely why she trusted it.

She'd found it the way she found most things worth having—by reading water the way other people read faces. A notch in the cliffs south of Gray Harrow, half-hidden behind a shelf of black rock, with a basin deep enough to float the *Kestrel* and a lip of shallows that would keep anything with a deeper draft standing off like a beggar at a locked door. The tide tables said she had eleven hours. Eleven hours of still water, hot food, and sleep that didn't come in twenty-minute installments with one hand on the tiller.

"You're sure about this," Idris said. He stood at the bow with the lead line coiled in his fist, watching the cliff shadows the way he watched everything now—like a man who had learned that scenery could lie.

"I'm sure I'm tired," Ada said. "And sure the fleet isn't. Their draft won't let them follow us in here even if they wanted to."

"That's not what I asked."

She brought the *Kestrel* around the rock shelf on a breath of wind, feathering the mainsail so the boat slid the last hundred yards in near silence. "No," she admitted. "I'm not sure of anything except that we can't keep running on no sleep and cold biscuit. Tired sailors make mistakes. Mistakes get us killed faster than Pell."

Idris dropped the lead line twice, calling depths, and then they were inside—a pocket of green-black water cupped between stone walls, quiet as a held breath. Gulls nested somewhere above them, complaining in the dark. Ada got the anchor down in one clean drop, set a snubbing line to the rocks, and for the first time in three days felt her shoulders come down from around her ears.

They ate properly for once. Idris heated a pan of salt pork and tinned beans over the little spirit stove while Ada rigged the storm canvas as a shelter over the cockpit, and for an hour it was almost civilized. He told her about a harbor town up north where his brother used to buy oranges off the boats, how the whole quay smelled of them in autumn, and Ada listened because listening to him was easier than thinking about what waited three tides out—the wreck, the ledgers, the fleet combing the coast for them like men dragging a river for a body.

"Your watch first or mine?" she asked when the pan was scraped clean.

"Mine." He was already climbing into the cockpit, settling himself against the wheel with the ease of a man who had spent his life sleeping upright on steel decks. "Sleep. You steer like a drunk when you're tired, and I say that with respect."

Ada crawled into the berth under the storm canvas and lay there listening to the water lap the hull, and the gulls, and Idris humming something tuneless under his breath—and sleep took her like a trapdoor opening.

* * *

She woke to silence.

Not the good kind. Not the flat calm of a settled night, but a silence with a shape to it, a hole in the world where sound should have been. The gulls had stopped.

Ada came up out of the berth with her hand already finding the knife under the thwart, every nerve lit. She lay still in the dark and listened. Water on the hull. Wind in the rigging, faint. And beneath it—so faint she might have invented it—a rhythmic clicking from somewhere beyond the eastern wall of the anchorage. Mechanical. Patient. Like an insect made of brass.

She moved to the cockpit without standing up, keeping her head below the gunwale. Idris was awake too; she saw it in the way he held himself, absolutely still, eyes fixed on the same stretch of shadowed cliff.

"Tell me you hear it," she breathed.

"Two minutes ago," he whispered. "I was going to wake you."

"What is it?"

He didn't answer right away, and when he did, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reciting something he'd hoped never to recite again. "Relay keyer. Low power. It's how the Syndicate's remote stations report—no radio operator needed, no lights, no smoke. Just a machine tapping code into a wire or a beam, on schedule, all night long." His jaw worked. "Every harbor I ever charted for them, we logged whether one would fit. Hidden stations. Listening posts. They call them *ears*."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. "In here?"

"There's a cove past that shelf of rock. I can't see it from here, but if I were placing a station, that's where I'd put it—east-facing, out of the weather, sightline down the whole approach channel." He turned to look at her, and in the starlight his face was stripped of everything charming, everything calculated, leaving only something raw. "Ada. If there's an ear in this cove, it heard us come in. It may have counted our heartbeats."

The clicking stopped. In its absence the silence became enormous, pressing against her eardrums.

Then, distantly, from far out beyond the cliffs, a sound rolled across the water that made the hair rise on her arms: a ship's horn. One long blast. Then another. Not a navigation signal. Navigation signals meant *I am here*. This was a question being asked, and answered, and passed along—she could hear the faint echo of a third horn farther south, relaying the message down the coast like a shout passed hand to hand through the dark.

"They're not searching anymore," Ada said quietly. "They know."

"The station will have transmitted the moment we entered the basin. Position, draft, heading, probably a description of the boat." Idris was already moving, folding the storm canvas with quick, economical motions. "Pell doesn't need to guess anymore. He just needs to arrive."

"How long?"

"If the nearest picket ship is where I'd have stationed one—" He stopped. Closed his eyes briefly, as though consulting a map printed on the inside of his eyelids. "Ninety minutes. Maybe two hours. They'll want daylight for a close-water operation, but Command has been burning patience for days. They might come in the dark and be sloppy about it."

Ada looked at the eastern wall of the anchorage, black and featureless against the lesser black of the sky, and felt the wilderness turn its face toward her—felt the whole coast rearrange itself in her mind from refuge into instrument. Every cove a mouth. Every headland an eye. She had spent her life believing the wild places belonged to no one, that the sea kept her secrets because it kept everyone's. But the sea wasn't keeping secrets. The sea was being *read*, page by page, by men who paid for the reading, and she had sailed straight into the margin of their book and sat down to cook dinner.

"We go now," she said. "On the dark water, before their picket closes the mouth."

"Through the approach channel? We came in on the last of the flood with light enough to see the rocks. Going out now, on the ebb, in the dark—"

"I know the water, Idris. It's the only thing I know better than they do." She was already at the windlass, breaking the anchor free of the bottom. "But I need your ears and your memory of their patrol patterns. Tell me where they aren't."

"That list gets shorter every day."

"Then start talking fast."

* * *

They came out of the anchorage with sails double-reefed and the engine at a low mutter, sliding along the base of the cliffs where the ebb current ran strongest and the Syndicate's searchlights—when they came—would be looking for movement in open water, not shadow hugging stone.

Ada steered by feel and by the sound of the water against the rock. There was a language in it. A smooth hiss meant depth; a stuttering slap meant a shoal reaching up; a hollow boom, echoing off stone, meant a cave or a cut and a chance. She had learned this language as a girl, running supplies through island channels with her father before the debts and the Syndicate and the slow grinding-down years, and tonight it all came back to her like blood returning to a numb limb.

"Contact," Idris said softly. He was standing at the masthead rail with a pair of battered binoculars, though there was nothing to see yet but stars. "Two points off the port bow. Running dark, but I can hear her engines—big, slow, idling. Sitting on the channel mouth like a spider."

Ada eased the *Kestrel* closer to the cliff until the rock wall seemed close enough to touch. "Range?"

"Half a mile, maybe less. If we hold this line we pass within four hundred yards of her."

"Can she see us?"

"Not unless someone looks hard at exactly the right patch of black water." He lowered the binoculars. "But Ada—that relay station. It'll have given them more than our position. It'll have given them our *draft*. They'll know we have to use this channel, and channels have mouths, and mouths are where you post guns."

As if the words had summoned it, a white light bloomed on the water ahead—not a searchlight sweeping, but a single fixed flare rising slowly on a parachute, drifting down on its little silk canopy, turning the whole channel mouth into a stage washed in dead white light. Under it, Ada could see the picket ship clearly: a gray trawler conversion, deck gun forward, figures moving along her rail.

And under it, she could see herself—every inch of the *Kestrel*, every sail, every silhouette, lit up like a specimen pinned to a board.

"Hard to starboard," Idris said, very calmly. "There's a back-eddy behind the northern point. Ebb runs against itself there, slack water in the middle of the stream. If we can reach it before that flare burns out—"

"It's a trap-shaped piece of water."

"It's the only piece of water they aren't watching, because it's the one piece no sane sailor enters at night."

Ada grinned, and was surprised to hear herself do it. "Good thing we left sane back at the anchorage."

She put the helm over. The *Kestrel* came round with a shudder, sails luffing for one terrible, screaming moment—canvas noise carries at night, carries over water like a gunshot—and then filled again on the slanted back-current and drove for the northern point with the flare dying above them, its light thinning from white to gray to nothing.

Darkness fell back like a curtain. Ada steered into the eddy blind, trusting the feel of the helm, the song of the water, Idris's low running commentary—"easy, easy, rock off the port quarter, ten yards, five"—and then suddenly the chaos of the ebb smoothed beneath them and they were sitting in a pool of stillness no wider than a church, hidden behind a shoulder of stone, while forty yards away the Syndicate trawler's engines idled and voices carried across the water with obscene clarity.

"—flare's burned, skipper. Nothing showed."

"Station says a sloop, shallow draft. She's in the channel somewhere. Wake the gun crew and stand by the searchlight. She has to come out eventually."

Ada and Idris crouched below the level of the gunwale, shoulder to shoulder in the dark, barely breathing. His hand had found hers without either of them deciding it, gripping hard enough to hurt, and neither of them let go.

"She has to come out eventually," Idris repeated in a whisper against her ear, and there was something bleakly funny in it. "Except we don't. That's the trick, Ada. Everyone thinks the sea has only two directions—in and out. But there's always a third direction, if you're desperate enough to take it."

"Which is?"

He pointed, and she followed his finger upward, to where the cliffs fell away northward into a long, low rampart of broken rock—the kind of ground charts dismissed with a frown and a warning symbol. Beyond it, dim in the starlight, open water. Open, ugly, reef-strewn water, the kind that ate keels for breakfast.

"The tide's making," he said. "In ninety minutes there'll be enough water over the outer bar to float us—if we can find the gap in the reefs in the dark, with no light, while that ship watches the channel mouth."

"And if we hit?"

"Then we hit, and the Syndicate saves themselves the trouble."

Ada looked at the broken water beyond the rampart, and at the man beside her, and at her own hands steady on the wheel, and understood that some part of her had already decided. It had decided the moment she'd woken to that wrong silence. She would not be read. She would not be a line in someone's ledger, an entry in someone's log, a position plotted on someone's chart. Whatever else the sea took from her, it would not take that.

"Get forward," she said. "Watch for white water. Call it early and call it true, because I'll be steering by your voice alone."

He squeezed her hand once, hard, and went forward into the dark.

* * *

It took them forty minutes to cross a mile of water, and Ada would remember those forty minutes for the rest of her life.

Idris's voice never rose and never faltered. *"White water, port bow, wide—come right a hair. Good. Dead ahead, narrow breaker, thread it between the two dark patches—trust me—now. Now hard to port!"* And Ada obeyed each word as if it came from the sea itself, hauling the wheel over, feeling the keel whisper across sand that should have opened the hull, feeling the boat heel and stagger and recover, until the breakers fell astern and the water beneath them went deep and black and rolling, and the open sea took them like a mother taking back a runaway child.

Behind them, the flare went up again over the channel mouth, lighting a patch of empty ocean. She heard the trawler's horn sound again—one long blast, another, another—but this time the message it carried had a different flavor. She couldn't have said how she knew. She just knew. Somewhere out in the dark, Mr. Pell was being told that his quarry had slipped through a hole in his net, and that the net had been woven from information, and that the information had been perfect, and that somehow none of it had mattered.

"He'll stop being polite after this," Idris said. He had come aft and stood beside her at the wheel, salt spray freezing in his hair. "Pell. Courtesy was his armor. Men like that get dangerous when the armor cracks."

"Let him crack." Ada found she meant it. "We've got two tides left to reach the Meridian Star, and he's got a fleet that knows exactly where we are. So we don't hide anymore, Idris. Hiding is done. From here on, we race."

Ahead of them, far to the northeast, the horizon had begun to organize itself into a long, bruised line of cloud, stacked and swelling, with lightning flickering inside it like thought. A new system, big and mean, building over the open water between them and the tidal basin.

Idris followed her gaze and went very still. "That's going to be a storm."

"Yes."

"You're looking at it like—"

"Like a door," Ada said, and put the *Kestrel*'s head toward it.
