## 11. Nara's Choice

The letters had been arriving for eleven years, and Nara had read every one of them the same way: standing at her kitchen window, coffee going cold in her hand, as if she might catch her father lying through the glass.

Ede wrote beautifully. That was the first betrayal. A man who could not hold a job, a marriage, or a promise could still turn a sentence like a carpenter turns a joint—clean, flush, nothing showing. His letters from the coast were full of weather and light. *The flats went gold this morning, all the way to the shipping lanes. You'd have loved it, little bird.* They were full of small economies dressed up as adventures: *Found a whole crate of signal flares washed up past the point. Traded two for a new bilge pump.* They were full of next time. *When the season closes, I'll come inland. When I've settled the last of my accounts, I'll come inland.*

He never came inland.

Nara had stopped believing him somewhere around the fourth year, though she had never stopped reading, because a person can hate a letter and still need it the way a drowning woman needs driftwood that is visibly on fire. She had married, divorced, kept her mother's house, taken work at the harbor master's office in Corren Bay where she stamped manifests for ships she would never board and learned, without meaning to, how cargo moved along this coast—and who moved it. The Vane Syndicate's name appeared on enough paperwork that it stopped looking like a company and started looking like weather. It was simply there, in every season, bending everything toward itself.

So when the letters changed, she noticed.

It was in the spring, three months before Ede died on the flats with his blood soaking into sand that Ada Okonkwo would later kneel beside. The change was small. His handwriting, always cramped, became cramped differently—the letters leaning hard into the right margin, as if he were writing against something. And the content shifted. Fewer sunsets. More questions disguised as observations. *Saw a survey crew at the old tidal basin today. Funny place to survey. Nothing out there but sand and one very stubborn wreck.* Then, a week later: *If anything happens to me, look where we buried your grandmother's ring. Not the grave. The other place. You know the one.*

She did not know the one. That was the cruelty of it—he had written as if she did, as if some shared childhood code existed between them that eleven years of silence had not erased. She turned the sentence over for days. *Your grandmother's ring.* Her grandmother had been buried with no ring; the family had been too poor, and the ring had been sold decades ago to pay for—Nara had always assumed—drink. But her father had been the one to sell it. He had been fourteen. She knew this because her mother had told her once, in the flat voice mothers use for family wounds, and then never spoken of it again.

*Not the grave. The other place.*

It took her nine days to find it, and when she found it she laughed until she cried, alone in her kitchen at midnight, because it was so exactly like him. Under the loose hearthstone. In the house he had abandoned. In the house *she* now lived in. He had hidden his secret inside her life without asking, the way he had hidden everything inside her life without asking—his debts, his silences, his occasional desperate appearances on her doorstep smelling of salt and lies.

Inside the cavity beneath the stone, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was half a map.

Nara spread it on her table under lamplight and understood immediately that it was half—that the paper ended mid-coastline, mid-sentence almost, the inked soundings stopping at a channel marked only *THE SQUEEZE — DO NOT ATTEMPT ON THE FLOOD*. Beyond that edge, nothing. But what was there was enough to stop her breath. Tidal curves annotated in her father's hand. Depths. A basin drawn with obsessive care around a shape labeled, in block capitals pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper: *MERIDIAN STAR — HOLD INTACT — LEDGERS — GOD FORGIVE ME.*

Ledgers.

Her father had worked the Syndicate's coastal runs once, long ago—she had pieced that much together over years, from a tattoo half-glimpsed at a funeral, from a man at a bar who called him *Vane's boy* and got a broken bottle for it. She had assumed he'd run cargo. Rum, untaxed diesel, people, she had told herself, but cargo. The word *ledgers* rearranged everything. Ledgers meant records. Records meant names. And *God forgive me* meant her father had spent eleven years writing her postcards about golden flats while sitting on proof of something that needed forgiving.

She should have gone to the magistrate. She knew that even then, standing over the map with her heart slamming. There were honest channels. There were federal offices in Corren Bay with brass plaques and waiting rooms.

But she had spent her adult life watching honest channels bend around the Syndicate like water around a piling. She stamped their manifests. She knew which inspectors took their vacations in Vane-owned properties. And more than that—more than strategy, more than sense—there was the simpler fact that her father had died bleeding on a tidal flat with this knowledge in his possession, and someone had shot him for it, and the rage that rose in her when she thought of that was not the kind of rage you hand to a bureaucracy.

She was going to find whoever had found him first. And she was going to finish what he started.

* * *

Finding Ada Okonkwo was easier than it should have been, which told Nara something about how carefully the storm chaser was—or wasn't—hiding.

Harbor offices talk. Nara had spent eight years learning their dialect. Within two days she knew that Okonkwo had taken on fuel at a back-channel dock, paid in cash, bought twice the provisions a solo runner needed, and asked odd questions about the northern basin tides. Within four days she knew about the second man—the defector, the Syndicate navigator whose disappearance had made even the corrupt constables nervous, the one traveling under Okonkwo's protection. And within six days she had a boat.

Not a good boat. A nineteen-foot open skiff with a temperamental engine she'd bought off a fisherman who owed her a favor from an incident involving falsified tonnage reports that she would deny to her grave. She provisioned it herself, checked the charts herself, and told no one—not because she trusted no one, though she didn't, but because the moment anyone official knew where she was going, the Syndicate would know too, and whatever had killed her father would come looking for the rest of the set.

She caught them on the fifth day, at dusk, in the lee of a shingle spit south of the listening post neither of them yet knew was a listening post.

She saw the masthead light first—a single lantern swinging at anchor, gold against a sky the color of wet slate. Then the boat itself, low and dark and scarred, riding easy on the slack water. There was a fire on the shingle, small and shielded, and two figures near it: a woman crouched over a chart case, and a man standing watch with the particular restless posture of someone who expected the horizon to bite.

Nara cut her engine fifty meters out and let the skiff drift, and in that drifting silence she made herself look at Ada Okonkwo properly, because she had heard the name now often enough to build a picture, and pictures lie.

What she saw was a woman younger than her reputation—lean, weather-darkened, hands moving over the chart case with a tenderness Nara recognized and hated recognizing. That was how her father touched things that mattered. That was how he had touched the letters, probably, sealing each one like it was a small ship he was sending across a sea that wanted it sunk.

They hadn't seen her. Slack water, dusk, a hull with no running lights. Nara could have drifted past and away, could have followed at a distance for days, could have done any of the patient careful things the sensible version of her plan required.

Instead she reached down, opened her own chart case, and took out the half-map in its oilcloth.

Then she struck her lantern and held it high, and called across the water in a voice she barely recognized as her own—her father's coast-voice, inherited without her consent:

"Ada Okonkwo! You're carrying my father's map, and I'm carrying the other half!"

* * *

The man came up fast, hand inside his coat, and the woman came up faster, and Nara watched Ada Okonkwo take in the skiff, the stranger, the raised lantern, and the words, and saw the exact moment calculation lost to recognition. It happened in her eyes—a flicker, a narrowing, a grief arriving out of order.

"You're Ede's," Ada said. Not a question.

"Nara." She held the lantern higher. "And if you put me off this water, I'll follow you anyway. I've got six days of fuel and no reason left to go home."

Idris said something low to Ada—Nara caught only *trap* and *we don't know*—and Ada lifted a hand to quiet him without taking her eyes off the skiff. "Come alongside, then," she called. "Slow. Hands visible."

Nara brought the skiff in with the exaggerated care of a woman who had handled boats for eight years from behind a desk and knew precisely how much she didn't know. The hull kissed the larger boat's gunwale. Idris grabbed the painter. And then she was stepping across the gap—stepping into the space where her father had stood, on the deck where her father's map lay, handing the oilcloth bundle to the woman her father had chosen with his dying breath.

Ada unwrapped it right there, by lantern light, and Nara watched her face as the second half of the coastline bloomed out of the creased paper. Watched her go still. Watched her look up with an expression that was equal parts wonder and alarm.

"This completes the approach channel," Ada said quietly. "The inner route. Everything I've been guessing at for five days—" She stopped. Started again. "Where was this?"

"Under a hearthstone. In my house. Which used to be his house." Nara kept her voice level with effort. "He hid it there instead of sending it. Instead of telling me. Eleven years of letters, Mrs. Okonkwo, and not one word of this. So don't stand there looking sorry for me. I didn't come for sympathy."

"I wasn't offering it." Something moved through Ada's face—respect, maybe, or its close cousin. "I promised him I'd deliver the map to you. Not to a fleet. To you."

"And you will have delivered it. Here I am. Delivered." Nara looked past her at the chart spread on the deck, at the basin drawn in her father's hand, at the words *LEDGERS — GOD FORGIVE ME*, and felt the ground of the last three months shift under her. "Now I'm staying."

Idris laughed—one short, disbelieving note—and Ada shot him a look that silenced it. "That's not how this works," Ada said. "This isn't a salvage party. This is a debt run with a body count attached. The fleet hunting us doesn't take prisoners, and I can't—"

"My father bled out on a tidal flat with a hole in him the size of my fist," Nara said, and her voice cracked on *fist* and she hated it and kept going anyway. "Somebody shot him for what's in that hold. Somebody with Syndicate money behind them. You think I can go back to stamping manifests? You think I can sit in Corren Bay reading the shipping news while you sail into that basin?" She pulled the folded papers from inside her jacket—Ede's last letters, the ones with the survey crews and the strange margins—and dropped them onto the chart. "He wrote to me the whole time. He couldn't tell me, so he *coded* me, and I'm the only person alive who can read it. Look at the margins. Look at the dates against your tide tables. He mapped the basin's moods for three years, Mrs. Okonkwo. Your half shows you the door. Mine tells you which nights it opens."

Silence, except for water working against the hulls and the fire popping on the shingle. Idris leaned over the letters, and she watched him read the way navigators read—with his finger, tracing, checking—and when he looked up at Ada there was something in his face that Nara had learned to distrust in men and trust in data: reluctant confirmation.

"She's right," he said. "These annotations match the fleet's internal tide tables. Better than ours, actually. Ede had years on us." He paused. "The Syndicate thought he was harmless. A drunk prospector with a grudge. They never knew he was surveying them."

"He was surveying them for *me*," Nara said, and heard the break in it again, and this time let it stand. "Every letter was practice. Every coded line was him building something he could leave me that wasn't apologies. And then they shot him before he could—" She stopped. Breathed. The slate-colored sky was going fully dark now, and the lantern threw their three shadows long across the deck, tangled together like something growing.

Ada gathered the letters slowly, squared their edges, and laid them beside the joined halves of the map—two pieces of one coastline, meeting at last at a channel called the Squeeze.

"I promised your father one thing," she said. "I keep my promises. But I make terms, and I make them plain." She held Nara's gaze, and Nara saw the exhaustion in her, deep as ballast, and the stubbornness laid over it like plating. "You come aboard, you work. You stand watches. You learn the lines and the anchors and where the flares are stowed, because if things go wrong out here, wrong happens fast. And when we reach the hold—if we reach the hold—you get answers about your father from the only other man alive who knew him at the end." A glance at Idris. "He knew Ede. Fleet-side."

Nara turned to Idris sharply. "You knew him?"

"I signed onto his run once, years ago. Before." Idris's face did something complicated. "He taught me a knot I still use. He also stole our spare compass and left a note apologizing for it." A pause. "Everyone loved him and nobody trusted him. It was quite a gift."

Despite everything—the fear, the fury, the grief that had been her constant passenger since the hearthstone—it startled a sound out of Nara that was almost a laugh. Almost. It broke halfway and became something else, and she turned her face to the water until it passed.

"Terms accepted," she said finally.

Ada put out her hand, and Nara took it, and the grip was hard and brief and real—the first handshake she'd had in years that wasn't a formality. Then Ada turned to practicalities: berthing, water rationing, the watch rotation, her voice settling into the clipped rhythm of command while Idris began striking the anchor and Nara stood in the bow of her own poor skiff deciding whether to tow it or scuttle it.

She scuttled it. Opened the seacocks herself and watched the little boat that had carried her out of her old life settle and slide under the black water without ceremony, taking with it the return ticket she'd been pretending was in her pocket since Corren Bay.

* * *

Later—deep in the night, with Ada below and Idris at the helm and the boat running dark along a coast her father had drawn for her line by line—Nara sat in the cockpit with the joined map across her knees and read it the way you read a letter from the dead.

The two halves fit almost perfectly. Almost. There was a seam where the paper had aged differently, a slight misalignment in the soundings, and she found herself pressing the pieces together with her thumbs as if she could press eleven years closed the same way. *Here,* she thought. *Here is where he stopped writing to me and started writing for me. Right along this edge. Right at the Squeeze.*

Idris spoke without turning from the wheel. "Get some sleep. She'll want you on dawn watch, and she doesn't forgive missed sleep."

"In a minute."

He was quiet a while. Then: "For what it's worth—I think he'd have liked this. You showing up armed with his own homework."

"For what it's worth," Nara said, "if you ever give me a reason to think you sold us, I will put you off this boat myself, and I won't wait for land."

"Fair," Idris said, and sounded, horribly, like he meant it as a compliment.

Nara folded the map along its seam and went below, past the berth where Ada slept like a woman expecting interruption, and lay down in the narrow space that was hers now, and listened to the hull work against the swells. Somewhere ahead, past the Squeeze, past the basin, a wreck full of her father's sins and salvations waited under the sand. Somewhere behind, a fleet was hunting them. And somewhere in the waterproof wrap beneath her jacket, thirty pages copied in her own hand were already riding toward a future her father had bought with the worst years of his life.

She slept, finally, without dreaming of the kitchen window.

She woke to Ada shaking her shoulder and gray light in the companionway, and to words that emptied the night's small peace out of her like a pulled plug:

"We've got company astern. Two sails, running dark, matching our speed." Ada's face was grim and calm at once. "And your father's map has something sewn into it that I think you'd better see."
