## 15. Betrayal at Fourth Tide

Nara felt the tide before she saw it—a pressure in her ears, a hollowness in the wind, the way the gulls went quiet all at once, as if someone had drawn a curtain over the sky. Her father had taught her to read water when she was six years old, standing knee-deep in the shallows off Corren Point with his big hands wrapped around her small ones. *The sea talks first,* he'd said. *People lie. Water doesn't.*

The water was talking now. And what it said was: *you are not alone.*

She was on watch, curled in the bow of Ada's boat—the *Kestrel's Debt*, a name that had made her snort the first time she'd heard it, though she understood it better now—when the sound came across the flats. Engines. Not one. Three, maybe four, running low and easy, the way ships run when they aren't hurrying because they already know where you are.

"Nara?" Idris's voice from below, sleep-rough. He'd taken the middle watch and surrendered it early, claiming his shoulder was bad. It had been bad for three days. Everything about him had been slightly wrong for three days.

"Patrol," she said. "Northeast cut. Coming slow."

He was up the ladder fast for a man with a bad shoulder. Too fast, some traitorous part of her noted, and she hated herself for noting it. That was the thing about suspicion—it didn't announce itself like weather. It seeped. It got into the bilge of everything and sat there, sloshing, until every look became evidence and every silence became testimony.

Ada came up last, pulling her jacket on, hair still wet from washing in a rain catch. She looked at the horizon for perhaps four seconds, then at Idris for one.

"Fourth tide's tomorrow at dawn," Ada said. "They're not patrolling. They're herding."

"Then we move now, through the—" Idris began.

"The shingle channel's dry until midnight," Nara said. "You know that. You drew us the chart."

A pause. Small. The kind of pause a man makes when he realizes he has made an error so small that only someone watching for errors would catch it.

"I misremembered," Idris said evenly.

*You misremembered.* Nara turned away so he wouldn't see her face, because her face right then would have told him everything—that she had spent eleven days on this boat learning the exact grain of his voice, the way it roughened when he lied and smoothed when he told the truth, and that she could no longer tell which was which, and that this frightened her more than Pell's guns did.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: they had been ahead of the fleet for two tides. Then suddenly they weren't. The listening post at False Harbor had caught them by luck—or so Ada had argued, pacing the deck at midnight, jaw tight. But luck didn't explain the patrol boat that had found them in open water two days later, close enough to count figures on the rail before fog saved them. Luck didn't explain why the Syndicate's cordon kept tightening along exactly the routes Idris recommended, always a half-day behind, always just missing them, as if someone were walking a dog on a very long leash.

And now this. A patrol converging on their anchorage within an hour of their arrival, at a spot chosen from Idris's own charts.

The engines grew louder. Through the gray morning light Nara could make out shapes now—two fast cutters and something heavier behind them, riding low, gunmetal-colored against gunmetal water.

"We can't outrun them loaded like this," Ada said. "Not with the draft we're carrying."

"Then we run the mangrove shelf," Idris said. "Shallow water. They can't follow with those hulls. I know a passage—"

"You know a passage." Nara heard her own voice come out flat and cold, a stranger's voice. "Of course you do."

Idris looked at her. Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt, she thought, or not only guilt. Hurt. And that hurt made her angrier, because how dare he be hurt, how dare he stand there looking wounded when the evidence stacked up around him like sandbags against a flood.

"Nara," Ada said quietly. "Not here."

"Yes here." Nara's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the rail. "Ada. Two cutters and a tender, converging on a hiding place only one person aboard knew about. Ask him where else he's led them. Ask him about the signal flare we never saw but they clearly did. Ask him—"

"I'm asking you to trust me," Idris said.

"That's the problem." Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the tremor, hated that grief lived so close under her anger that she couldn't tell them apart anymore. "Trust is what my father gave the Syndicate. Thirty years of it. And it bought him a gut wound on a mudflat and a daughter who learned he was a liar from a stranger. So forgive me if I've developed a professional interest in men who say *trust me* while the guns get closer."

The first cutter fired a warning shot. The crack of it rolled across the water, and the gulls exploded off the mudflats in a white shrieking cloud.

Ada moved fast after that. She took the *Kestrel's Debt* out through the shallows on the western side, threading oyster bars with the casual brutality of a woman who had grown up breaking boats and her heart on this coast, while the cutters swung wide, unable to follow into the skinny water. For twenty minutes there was only engine noise and spray and Nara braced in the bow calling out bars and channels from her father's old knowledge—*starboard, starboard, hard over, mind the stake*—and for those twenty minutes Nara almost forgot. Almost.

Then they were through, into a drowned forest lagoon where the mangroves closed overhead like a green cathedral, and Ada killed the engine, and the silence came down around them thick as wool, and there was nowhere left to hide from each other.

* * *

They anchored in the shade of the trees, hull ticking as it cooled, water the color of steeped tea beneath them. Nara sat on the foredeck with her back against the mast and watched Ada and Idris stand facing each other near the stern, and she understood that whatever happened next would happen between those two, and that she had forced it, and that she would force it again.

"Say your piece," Ada said to Nara. "Both of you."

"He signaled them," Nara said. "Or left a mark, or a light, or—I don't know the method. But the method doesn't matter. The pattern does. Every position they've found us in, he chose. Every escape route he offered, they anticipated. He knew the patrol schedule at False Harbor too well. He knew the tender's draft restrictions. He—"

"I was Syndicate for nine years," Idris said. His voice was low and terribly controlled. "Yes. I know their procedures. That's what you bought when you took me aboard. My knowledge cuts both ways and I warned you of that on the first night."

"You warned her," Nara said. "Did you warn her about the second chart? The one folded inside your kit roll, the one with our route marked in fresh pencil?"

Silence. Even the water seemed to stop moving.

Nara hadn't meant to reveal that. She had gone through his things on the third day—she wasn't proud of it, but grief had made her a searcher, a reader of other people's letters—and found the chart and told herself there was an explanation and had carried the unexplained thing inside her ever since, a stone swallowed whole.

"There is a second chart," Idris said carefully, "because I keep a duplicate record of our course. Standard practice. If anything happens to me—"

"If anything happens to you." Nara laughed, and the laugh came out ugly. "That's exactly what a man says when he expects to be extracted. When his employers need to recover his notes."

"My employers," Idris said, and now his control slipped, just a fraction, heat coming through, "burned my brother's body in an incinerator and called it a boiler malfunction. I have spent two years positioning myself to destroy them. You think I'd hand them the ledgers back to save my own skin?"

"I think people do worse for less," Nara said. "I think my father did worse for less. He told himself stories too. Everyone tells themselves stories. *I'm only the navigator. I'm only following orders. I'll get out clean.*"

"That's enough." Ada's voice didn't rise. It never needed to. It came out level and hard as a keel. "Nara. Make your demand. Say what you actually came up here to say."

Nara looked at her. Ada stood with her arms crossed, weight on her back foot, the way she stood in weather, and her eyes were steady and exhausted and full of something Nara recognized with a sick lurch: the look of a woman who has already decided and is waiting to see how much it will cost her.

"Maroon him," Nara said. "There's a fishing station on the point, eight miles north. Leave him there with supplies and a radio. If he's innocent, he can prove it later. If he's guilty, we've cut the leash. Either way, we go into the fifth tide without a knife at our backs."

"And if he's innocent," Ada said, "and we leave him, and the Syndicate picks him up from that station—which they will, because they pick up everyone eventually—then they'll have two years of his memory of our route, our plans, our faces. And I'll have made a man who might be the only honest person on this coast pay for crimes he didn't commit, because fear is easier than faith." She uncrossed her arms. "No."

"No?" Nara stared at her. "Ada. The evidence—"

"The evidence is a pencil line and a coincidence and your grief wearing a detective's coat." Ada said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I know grief. I know what it does to the eyes. Everything looks like a conspiracy when you've lost someone to liars. But I also know this man bled for us in the Squeeze. I know he navigated us through a named storm on instruments half of which were lying. And I know that if I throw him over at the first hard question, then the Syndicate has already won—they've made me so afraid of betrayal that I commit it myself."

"So that's it." Nara's voice shook. "You love him."

The words fell into the space between them and lay there like something dropped from a great height.

Ada didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing; she didn't flinch at all. "Maybe I do. Maybe that's exactly why you should listen to me. Love makes me check his math twice, Nara. It doesn't make me blind. It makes me careful. There's a difference, and you're too angry right now to see it."

"And you're too—"

"Don't." For the first time, something moved in Ada's face, quick as a fish under the surface. "Don't finish that sentence. Whatever it was."

Idris stood apart through all of it, hands loose at his sides, watching Ada with an expression Nara couldn't read—something between gratitude and devastation. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I'd rather you marooned me than doubted her judgment. She's earned better than being second-guessed by both of us."

"Oh, that's very noble," Nara snapped. "Very rehearsed."

"Nara." Ada stepped forward, and took Nara's face in her two hands, the way no one had done since her mother died, and Nara hated how much she leaned into it, how her whole body wanted to surrender to being held. "Your father sent you to me carrying half a map and thirty years of shame. You think I don't understand inherited debts? Mine was money. Yours is worse—it's a name. But hear me: we are three days from the wreck. We are three days from the truth, all of it, his and Ede's and the Syndicate's. I am not splitting us on the doorstep of the answer. Whatever's wrong gets fixed at the source. Do you hear me?"

Nara heard her. She heard her and believed her and resented believing her, and when Ada let go, Nara stepped back and hugged her arms around herself and looked out at the tea-dark water and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that wouldn't break something they'd all need intact.

But she caught Idris's eye once, across the deck. And she let him see what was in hers: *I'm watching you. I will always be watching you. And if you break her, there is no tide far enough, no water deep enough.*

He held her gaze and didn't look away, and that, more than anything, frightened her—because guilty men run, and innocent men argue, and Idris only stood there and received her hatred like a man receiving a debt he already knew he owed.

* * *

They ran north through the afternoon, silent except for necessity, a crew of three pretending to be a crew of one purpose. Nara took the helm for the middle stretch and drove the boat harder than Ada would have, punishing the water, punishing herself. Behind her she heard Ada and Idris speaking in low voices, and she made herself not listen, and failed, and caught only fragments—*the tracker theory, the case lining, Ede's letters*—fragments that sounded like defense and sounded like conspiracy, depending on the angle of her heart.

Near dusk, the radio crackled. Ada tuned it, and a voice came through, smooth and courteous as oiled wood, a voice Nara had never heard but knew instantly, the way you know a shape in the dark.

"*Miss Okonkwo. Miss Ede.*" A pause, warm as a handshake. "*Mr. Voss sends his regards. He wants you to know the fourth tide comes early tomorrow—and so will we. Bring the ledgers up gently. They're worth more than all three of your lives combined, and he'd hate to see value wasted.*"

Static. Then nothing.

Nara looked at Idris. Idris looked at Ada. And Ada, standing at the wheel with the last red light of the sun on her face, said the words that split the world cleanly in two:

"How did he know Nara's name?"

No one answered. The engine droned. Somewhere behind them, far out in the darkening water, the Syndicate's fleet was turning toward the basin, patient as the tide itself.

And Nara, gripping the rail, felt the cold certainty settle over her like spray: someone on this boat had told them. Someone always had.
