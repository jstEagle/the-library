## 16. The Truth in the Bilge

They had given him the bilge because it was the farthest place on the boat from Ada.

Idris understood the arithmetic of it. Nara had made her accusation at dawn, standing in the cockpit with salt-crusted hair and her father's fury transposed into her own voice, and Ada had not defended him—not out loud, not with words. She had only looked at him for a long moment, that gray-green look she wore like weather, and then said, "Bilge pump's been temperamental. Fix it or bail." And that was the sentence: guilty until proven useful.

So he bailed. And he thought.

The bilge of the *Kestrel's Debt*—a name that had seemed like a joke when he first came aboard and no longer did—was a cathedral of rust and old grease, lit by a single work lamp Ada had lowered on a cord. Water sloshed around his ankles with every swell, cold enough to make his teeth ache. Above him, through three decks of planking, he could hear the two women moving, speaking rarely, and every silence between their footsteps landed on him like a verdict.

He had gone over the night of the patrol a hundred times. The Syndicate cutter had come out of the fog channel at fourth tide with the confidence of a ship that had never once doubted its heading. Not searching. *Arriving.* They had run dark, cold engine, no lights, anchored in a cove Idris himself had chosen precisely because it sat in the shadow of a headland that ate radio signals. He had checked. He had been so careful about checking.

Which meant the signal hadn't come from the boat.

He worked the pump valve while his mind ran the problem the way he used to run fleet routes aboard the *Vane Fortune*—coldly, completely, without mercy for anyone including himself. A signal needed a source. The boat was clean; he'd swept her twice, once for his own reasons before Nara ever boarded. The cove was dead ground. That left the things they carried. Their gear. Their clothes. Their—

His hands stopped on the valve wheel.

The map case.

Ede's map case, oilskin-wrapped, brass-buckled, the thing that had started all of this—the dying man's last gift, passed hand to hand like a relay baton. Idris had handled it a dozen times. He had watched Ada sleep with it lashed inside her jacket in the bad anchorage after third tide. It had been within arm's reach of both of them, always, since the flats where Ede died.

And Ede had carried it for years. Through Syndicate territory. While the Syndicate hunted him—or while they *herded* him—and had somehow, impossibly, always known where he would surface next.

Idris climbed out of the bilge with water streaming off his forearms and his heart going hard against his ribs. He didn't go up. Not yet. He stood on the ladder's bottom rung in the lamplight and made himself finish the thought, because finishing thoughts was what had gotten his brother killed, and he refused to die of an unfinished one.

If the tracker was in the case, then Ede had either known or not known. If he'd known, he'd have ditched it—which meant he hadn't known, which meant the Syndicate had planted it without his knowledge, which meant everything Ede had believed about his years of evasion, his cleverness, his survival, was a fiction someone else had written for him. They hadn't been chasing the old man. They'd been *farming* him. Letting him run just far enough to see who came to meet him. Who bought his maps. Who pitied him.

Who took him in.

Ada had taken him in. Ada had held his hand on the flats while the tide came in around them both.

And if they were watching the case, they had watched Ada from the first hour. Every harbor. Every rendezvous. The storm run. The squeeze. The listening post at false harbor—that hadn't been bad luck, that had been a *check*, a confirmation ping, the handlers verifying their bait was still swimming where they wanted it.

They were never ahead. They had never once been ahead. They had been permitted.

Idris's hands were shaking, and he looked at them with something close to fascination, because he had not shaken during the defection, nor during Pell's searchlight sweep, nor lying flat on deck while tracer rounds stitched the water six feet off the beam. But this—this small brass buckle and a dead man's trust—this undid him.

He went up into the gray morning light to prove it.

* * *

Nara met him at the companionway with a boathook held not quite casually across her body, and he had to give her credit: she'd spent four days among strangers on open water and she still moved like someone who expected the world to owe her nothing.

"Still bailing?" she said.

"I need the map case."

"No."

"Nara." He kept his voice level, kept his hands visible, the way you spoke to a frightened animal or an armed one. "I know where the signal came from. I need to show you, and I need you to let me do it in front of Ada, because if I'm wrong you can maroon me at the next light and I'll swim ashore myself."

Something flickered behind her eyes—hunger, he thought, the hunger of a woman who had spent days hating a man and wanted, more than anything, to be told she'd been right. Or wrong. Either would end the waiting.

"Ada's on the foredeck," Nara said finally, stepping aside just enough. "You get five minutes. Then I decide which of us is sorry."

Ada stood at the bow rail with her back to them, watching the long gray line of the sea where it worried against the shoals. She didn't turn when he came up behind her, but her shoulders changed—he knew her well enough now to read her in silhouette, the way sailors read cloud banks—and the change was not welcoming.

"You found something," she said. "Or you're about to tell me you did."

"The map case. Give it to me."

She turned then. Her face was hollowed out, sleepless, and there was a bruise along her jaw from the escape that she hadn't mentioned and wouldn't. "You understand how this looks. You asking me to hand you the one thing we can't lose."

"I understand exactly how it looks." He held out both hands, palms up, empty. "That's why I'm asking instead of taking. Look at my hands, Ada. Look at my face. I have lied to you about some things—I told you that on the second night, by the fire, and I'll tell you again now—but I have never lied about wanting Pell's fleet at the bottom of the sea. And whatever is in that case has been sitting beside your heart for eleven days."

The wind pushed at them. Somewhere off the shoals a gull cried once and gave up.

Ada reached inside her jacket. She did it slowly, watching him the whole time, and when she drew the case out—oilskin darkened with salt spray, brass buckle green with age—she held it against her chest a moment longer, the way you hold something you're about to be told is cursed.

"If you're wrong," she said quietly, "about this, about any of it—"

"I'm not wrong." And the terrible thing, the thing he would carry afterward, was how certain he sounded. How certain he *felt*. Certainty had been his profession once. He'd navigated three hundred tons of other people's greed through waters that killed the careless, and he had never once been as sure of a heading as he was of this.

She put the case in his hands.

* * *

He did it on the foredeck, kneeling, with both women standing over him—one hoping to condemn him, one hoping, God help her, to be proved a fool. He unbuckled the strap. He unwound the oilskin. Inside lay the map itself, folded into its old creases, Ede's cramped annotations marching across the vellum like ants, and beneath the map, sewn into the lining of the case itself, a ridge that should not have been there.

"There," Nara said. "What's that?"

"A seam." Idris worked his thumbnail under it. His fingers were steady now; the shaking had burned off somewhere between the bilge and the deck, replaced by the flat clarity of a man walking into a room where he already knows the body is. "A seam that doesn't match the others. Feel it. It's stiff. Oilskin goes soft with years. This hasn't."

He cut it with his knife. One stroke, careful, the way you open a patient.

Inside the lining, wrapped in waxed cloth no bigger than a cigarette tin, sat a transmitter. No larger than a man's palm. No antenna visible—nothing so crude. Just a dull metal shell, a seam of its own, and a faint, almost subliminal hum he felt through his fingertips rather than heard. Fleet-issue. He knew the make. He had signed for crates of them aboard the *Vane Fortune*, back when signing for crates was his whole moral universe and he'd never once asked what the crates were for.

Nobody spoke. The sea filled the silence, slapping the hull, indifferent.

"That's—" Nara started, and stopped.

"Fleet beacon," Idris said. "Short burst, coded, near-undetectable unless you're sweeping for it specifically. Which they were. Because they installed it." He set it down on the deck between them, gently, the way you set down a scorpion. "It's been in that lining for years. Look at the stitching—they re-sewed the seam over it with matching thread. That's craftsman work. That's patience. Somebody sat down with this case and a needle and closed it up so well that a dying man carried it across two hundred miles of coast without ever feeling the weight."

Ada had gone very still. It was the stillness he'd learned to fear and love in equal measure, the stillness of her thinking at storm speed. "Ede didn't know," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Ede didn't know." Idris heard his own voice crack on the second word and let it. "Ada—they weren't chasing him. Think about it. A gut-shot prospector evading the Vane Syndicate for *years*? A man with no boat worth the name, no money, no protection? He survived because they let him survive. He was a lure. They seeded him with that beacon and turned him loose to see who'd bite. Who'd take pity on an old man bleeding out on the flats. Who'd pick up his map and start running."

"And I bit," Ada said.

"You bit." He couldn't soften it. Softening it would have been another kind of lie. "Every port we've made since the flats, they've had our position. False harbor wasn't bad luck—it was a checkpoint. They were confirming the bait was still traveling. The patrol at fourth tide didn't find us. We were *delivered*."

Nara made a sound low in her throat, and when Idris looked at her, her face had changed entirely. The suspicion was gone. What replaced it was worse—grief with nowhere left to land, grief that had been aimed at the wrong man for four days and now swung around looking for a target big enough to deserve it.

"My father," she said. "They used my father. He died on those flats believing he'd finally outrun them, and the whole time—" Her voice failed and restarted. "The whole time he was theirs. Like a dog they'd tagged."

"He wasn't theirs," Idris said. "Not at the end. Whatever they planted in his case, they couldn't plant anything in him. He chose you, Nara. Out of everyone he could have sent that map to—a fleet, a broker, a buyer—he chose the daughter who wouldn't speak to him. That choice was his. That was the one thing in this whole business they didn't sew shut."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she knelt, picked up the beacon, and held it in her fist like something she meant to crush, and did not crush, because crushing it would tell them nothing and keeping it might.

"It's still live," she said. "Isn't it?"

"Probably."

"Then they're listening right now." She turned it over in her fingers. "They've been listening to us talk about them for eleven days."

"Not necessarily talking." Idris's mind was already running ahead, the navigator in him seizing the wheel. "These units burst on schedule or on command. If it's scheduled, they're getting position fixes, maybe audio snippets if the model's new enough. If it's command-triggered, then someone's been sitting on a console pressing a button whenever they want to check on us." He met Ada's eyes. "Either way, we now know something they don't know we know."

Ada crouched beside them, and for the first time since the accusation she reached out and touched him—just two fingers against the back of his wrist, brief, but it went through him like current. "Say it," she said.

"We keep it." He said it slowly, testing each word. "We don't destroy it. We feed it. We let them think their bait is still swimming. We let them think Idris the traitor is still steering, that the girl is still cargo, that you're still running scared toward fifth tide. And then we choose where the beacon leads them—not them choosing where it finds us."

"Feed it where?"

He looked north, toward the basin, toward the sandbar where the *Meridian Star* slept under six fathoms of tide and twenty years of silt, where Pell's landing party would be waiting because Pell had been waiting all along, patient as a needle in a seam.

"Toward the wreck," he said. "Exactly where we were going anyway. Because here's the part that reframes everything, Ada—the fact that they let us come this far means they *want* us to open that hull. They want us to breach the hold, confirm the ledgers are intact, do the dangerous work. Then they take the cargo, take the evidence, and take the three witnesses who know it exists. We were never racing them to the prize. We're digging it up *for* them."

The wind shifted. Ada rose to her feet, and he watched the change move through her the way he'd watched weather fronts move across the flats—something gathering, something deciding.

"Then fifth tide," she said, "we stop being permitted."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day becoming liars together, and it was the closest thing to communion the three of them had yet shared.

Nara stripped the beacon's waxed wrapping and rebuilt it exactly as they'd found it, working with her father's tools, her jaw set, saying only once, quietly, "He taught me to sew sailcloth when I was nine. I guess this is what that was for." Idris showed her how the unit nested back into the lining, how the seam had to sit or a searcher's thumb would catch it. Ada altered their course by degrees—small corrections, the kind a frightened crew would make, nothing that said *we know*—and began, aloud, within range of the thing, to rehearse a version of themselves: tired, divided, short on water, arguing about whether to make for the basin at all or run for foreign water and sell what they found.

Idris played his part worst and best. Worst because acting guiltless when you'd been accused was its own torture, every glance from Ada landing like a hook. Best because he discovered, doing it, that he had nothing left to hide from her. The fire confession, the brother, the vengeance he'd nursed like a coal in his chest for six years—all of it was already hers. What remained was only the truth, and the truth, for once, was the most useful thing aboard.

At dusk he went forward alone. Ada joined him without being asked, and they stood at the rail watching the light die over the shoals, the beacon humming its small treachery below their feet.

"They'll have a ship between us and the basin," she said. "Maybe two. Pell will want to take us at the wreck, where there's no jury."

"Pell will want to take us anywhere Command tells him. And Command wants it quiet. Ledgers implicating half the coastal governments don't survive a firefight with witnesses." He paused. "Which means they'll want us dead somewhere the ledgers can be explained away. A wreck. A storm. An accident with three bodies and no story."

"Then we give them an accident," Ada said. "On our terms."

He turned to look at her. In the last light her face was all planes and resolve, and beneath the exhaustion there was something he recognized from the eye of the storm—from that impossible calm center where she'd told him who she intended to be if they lived. She was not afraid anymore. That was the horror and the gift of the beacon both: they had never been free, but knowing it, truly knowing it, was its own kind of freedom. A prisoner who learns the walls' exact dimensions can begin to draw on them.

"One condition," Idris said. "When this is done—when Pell's plan is ash on the water—you let me testify. Everything. My name attached. My brother's name attached. I didn't jump ship just to survive, Ada. I jumped to be *counted*."

"You'll be counted." She said it like a course plotted, final, unarguable. "But first we have a tide to catch."

Below their feet, faithful and damned, the beacon pulsed its little heart out into the dark—telling the Vane Syndicate, at this very hour, that its bait was swimming slow and scared and exactly on schedule.

Let them believe it, Idris thought. Fifth tide was coming. And for the first time since the flats, the trap had two ends.
