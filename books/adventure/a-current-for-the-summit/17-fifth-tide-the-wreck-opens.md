## 17. Fifth Tide: The Wreck Opens

The sea began to leave at three in the morning, and it left like a debtor clearing out in the dark—quietly, thoroughly, taking everything of value with it.

Ada stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Grace* and watched the basin drain. It was not like any low tide she had ever chased. Ordinary tides breathed; this one exhaled. The water slid backward across the flats in long silver sheets, uncovering sandbars that had no business existing, ribbed and gleaming like the spines of buried animals. The moon was a thin paring of bone overhead, and by its light she could see the channel markers Ede had drawn on his map—black rocks shaped like broken teeth, a drowned pine leaning at an angle no living tree would choose—emerging one by one from the retreating water, exactly where a dead man had said they would be.

"Extreme spring," Idris said behind her. He had the almanac open across his forearm even though he'd memorized it two days ago. "Moon's perigee. We won't get water like this again this season. Maybe not this year."

"Then we don't waste it." Ada spun the wheel and took the *Grace* down into the emptying basin.

The boat complained the whole way. She was a storm chaser, built with a shallow draft and a stubborn heart, but she was not built for this—threading between rocks that had spent decades sharpening themselves on hulls, over sand that came up to meet her keel like a hand reaching for an ankle. Ada ran her aground on purpose in the end, easing her up onto the last shelf of wet sand with the tenderness of a woman setting down something breakable, and killed the engine.

Silence. That was the first thing. The basin, emptied, held a silence so complete she could hear the tick of the hull cooling, the drip of water off the anchor chain, her own pulse in her ears. Out beyond the basin mouth, the ocean still breathed against the bar, a long low percussion, but in here the world had been switched off.

"Look," Nara said.

She was at the bow, and her voice had gone strange—flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a door closed against something enormous. Ada came forward and followed her pointing hand.

The *Meridian Star* was rising out of the sand.

Not rising—being uncovered, which amounted to the same thing. The extreme tide had stripped away forty years of sediment, and the wreck stood revealed down to her waterline: a coastal freighter, three hundred feet of rusted steel lying on her port side in the shallows, her starboard rail breaking the surface like a fin. Her superstructure had collapsed into a tangle, but her hull was whole—whole, and sealed, and furred with barnacle and weed so thick she looked less like a ship than a reef that had dreamed it was a ship. The sand had kept her. The sand had *hidden* her. Ada understood suddenly why no salvage fleet had ever found her: she was only a wreck twice a year, for a handful of tides, and the rest of the time she was seafloor.

Ede had spent thirty years learning that rhythm. He had died for it.

"She's bigger than the map made her look," Idris said.

"Maps lie by omission." Ada was already moving, hauling the dive bags and the cutting gear up onto the foredeck, her hands working while her mind raced ahead of them. "We have maybe four hours of true low water, then the flood starts, and once it starts it doesn't negotiate. We go in through the cargo hold—here." She tapped the map, where Ede had inked a small X against the starboard side, midships, with a note in a hand gone shaky near the end: *plate gave way in the grounding. I went in this way, 1979. It will not have healed.*

"It's been forty-six years," Nara said.

"Rust doesn't heal. It only gets worse." Ada looked at her. Ede's daughter had her father's jaw and none of his softness; the last week had carved her into something harder than grief. "You can wait on the boat."

"I didn't row across a bay in the dark to wait on a boat." Nara shouldered her pack. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, were the pages she'd been copying since the third tide—Ede's letters, her own notes, the coordinates cross-checked against harbor records Idris had recited from memory. Whatever was in that hold, she intended to have a second version of it before the sea closed over it again. Ada had stopped arguing with her about this on the second day. There was no arguing with that kind of need.

* * *

They crossed the flats on foot, because the *Grace* drew too much even for this. It was like walking on the skin of the world. The sand was firm and ribbed, printed with the tracks of things that had come out to feed in the drained basin—gulls, a fox, something heavier that had left a drag-marked trench—and every few steps the ground gave up some small evidence of the drowned: a bottle green with age, a shoe sole, a fish skeleton picked clean and arranged by the tide like a diagram. The air smelled of iodine and old iron.

The wreck grew as they approached until it stopped being a shape and became a country. Ada had seen wrecks before—storm chasers collect them the way crows collect shiny things—but she had seen them drowning, half-swallowed, apologetic. This one was not apologetic. Lying on her side with her guts of rusted plate exposed to the moon, she looked like something that had been murdered and left, and the sand had been an accomplice for four decades.

Ede's X was where Ede had said. A hull plate near the starboard bilge keel had buckled inward in the grounding, and forty years of rust had eaten the buckle into a gap wide enough for a thin man with a pack—if he exhaled first.

"Me," Ada said, before either of the others could claim it. She was the thinnest. She was also, though she didn't say it, the one who had promised a dying man.

She went through sideways, one arm forward, pack trailing, the rusted edge of the plate scoring a line through her jacket that she'd feel tomorrow. Inside, the dark was absolute and wet and breathing—air moving through the wreck in slow tides of its own, stirred by the sea beyond the bar. She got her headlamp on. The beam found a corridor of collapsed decking, a rain of orange-brown scale hanging from overhead plates like stalactites in a cave made of a ship, and, knee-deep in the silt of the deck, the unmistakable geometry of cargo cradles—rows of them, receding into the dark, most still lashed with webbing gone to lace.

"Hold's open," she called back, her voice going flat and strange in the steel throat of the ship. "Come on through. Watch the plate edge—it's sharp as a promise."

Idris came through second, then Nara, and then the three of them stood in the belly of the *Meridian Star* with their lights swinging, and for a long moment nobody said anything at all.

The hold was a cathedral. That was the only word Ada's mind would give her. The cargo cradles ran away into the dark on both sides like pews, and above them the deckhead soared, furred and dripping, and their headlamps made three small suns that turned the drifting rust-motes into falling snow. Forty years of silence lay over everything like a second cargo.

"Ledgers would be sealed," Idris said quietly, and his voice had changed—the navigator's clipped efficiency gone, replaced by something rawer. He'd told her once, in the eye of the storm, with her mouth against his wet skin, what the Syndicate had done to his brother for reading these pages. "Waterproof cases. Steel document boxes, probably, in a dedicated compartment. The Syndicate shipped paper like it was plutonium." He raised his light. "Aft. The secure compartment would be aft of the cargo hold, behind a bulkhead."

They moved aft, wading through silt that sucked at their boots, past cradles holding shapes under rotting tarpaulin—machinery, crates, a pallet of something that clinked when Ada's light touched it. The fortune Ede had promised her was here; she could feel it, the way you feel weather coming. She found she didn't care. Not the way she'd expected to care. Somewhere between the debt and the storm and the man who had bled out on the flats with her hand pressed to his wound, the fortune had stopped being the point and become the vehicle.

The bulkhead was aft, and it was intact, and its door was a steel door with a wheel handle, and the wheel turned.

Ada's heart stopped, then started hard. Unlocked. Sealed, gasketed, *unlocked*—because the last man through it had expected to come back, and hadn't.

Inside, the compartment was dry.

Not damp. Not seeping. *Dry*—the gasket had held for forty-six years, and in the cone of her headlamp Ada saw steel document cases racked in rows, stenciled with a shipping line's crown-and-anchor mark and, beneath it, in smaller letters that made Idris make a sound like a man being struck: *VANE MARITIME — DO NOT MANIFEST.*

"Ada." Idris's voice had gone very quiet. He had a case in his hands and was working the latches with fingers that were not steady. "Ada, look at the dates."

The first ledger opened to a manifest page. Cargoes that had never been cargoes. Tonnage that had never existed. And in the margins, in a clerk's neat hand, the true entries—names, payments, percentages. A port commissioner's name. A customs chief's. A senator's. Page after page after page, forty years of the coast's respectable men selling their signatures, and the Syndicate keeping the receipts.

"They kept proof against their own partners," Nara whispered. "In case the partners got ideas."

"They kept proof against *everyone*," Idris said. "This is the whole machine. This is—" He stopped. He was holding the ledger the way you hold something wounded.

Ada stood in the dry dark of a dead ship with forty years of crimes in her hands and felt the moment balance on its point. This was the fortune. This was the thing Pell's fleet had crossed a sea to burn. She could take one case—just one—find a buyer in some port where names were cheap, and buy back her life twice over. The thought was there, honest and ugly, and she let it stand in the light with the rest of them before she set it down.

"Copy everything," she said. "Nara, the camera. Idris, start with the earliest volumes—we take the oldest first, they're the foundation. We have three hours and I want every page that matters photographed twice."

They worked. The compartment filled with the small sounds of urgency: the click of the shutter, the whisper of acid-free paper, Nara's breath counted deliberately slow, Idris murmuring dates like a prayer. Ada photographed and carried and stacked, and somewhere in the middle of it—kneeling in a dead man's dry vault, holding a ledger open to a page where a governor's name sat beside a number with too many zeros—she started to laugh. Quietly. She couldn't help it.

"What?" Nara said.

"He was right. The crazy old man on the flats. It was all here. He wasn't mad, he was *patient*." Ada pressed her palm flat against the page. "Thirty years he carried this alone because nobody would believe a prospector over a shipping crown."

"Then we make them believe," Nara said, and for one moment—her light on her face, the ledger between them—they were not a debtor, a defector, and a daughter. They were witnesses.

That was when Idris, at the compartment's narrow porthole, went silent.

"Ada." His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "The ridge."

* * *

The ridge was a spine of black rock on the basin's northern shore, the highest ground for miles, the kind of feature a careful man would note on a map and a military man would note in a different kind of book. Ada had crossed it off as cover two days ago—too far away to matter, too exposed to hold anyone.

She was wrong.

She came up to the porthole and looked out through the salt-hazed glass, and the triumph that had been filling her chest like floodwater turned, in the space of one heartbeat, to ice.

There were men on the ridge. A dozen of them, maybe more, dark against the last of the moon, and they were not moving like salvagers. They had come down to the ridge's seaward shoulder and arranged themselves along it with the unhurried economy of professionals, and as her eyes adjusted she picked out the details that made her stomach drop through the sand: the tripod-mounted light that wasn't a camera, the shapes of rifles slung, and, at the ridge's crest, a figure standing apart from the rest with his hands clasped behind his back, looking down at the wreck with the posture of a man admiring a property he had finally come to collect.

Even at this distance, even across a quarter mile of drained seabed, Ada knew the set of those shoulders. She had watched them across a debt collector's cheerful desk. She had heard that voice wish her a good morning while explaining how her boat would burn.

Mr. Pell had brought a landing party. Mr. Pell had not raced them across six tides at all.

"He let us get here," she heard herself say. The words came out level, which surprised her. "The listening post. The patrol at fourth tide. The tracker in Ede's case." She turned and looked at Idris, and there was no accusation left in it—there hadn't been since the bilge—but there was something worse: understanding. "We were never ahead of them. We were the dredge. We cracked the hull open for them."

Idris's jaw was working. Nara had gone white, her arms wrapped around the ledger cases as if she could shield them with her body.

Below them, out on the flats, a sound carried across the drained basin with terrible clarity: the grind and splash of an inflatable launch coming through the basin mouth on the last of the low water, running dark, riding for the wreck's exposed starboard rail. A second followed it. A third.

The tide had four hours left in it. The flood after that would seal the basin for the season. And between this dry room and the *Kestrel's Grace* lay a quarter mile of open sand with no cover on it at all, and on the ridge above, men with rifles were beginning, unhurried, to come down.

Ada closed the ledger in her hands, and looked at the fuel lines she'd noted on the way in—rusted, ruptured, still holding forty years of diesel in the wing tanks above their heads—and started, very calmly, to count.
