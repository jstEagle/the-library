## 18. Standoff in the Hold

The water came for them the way debts come—slowly, then all at once.

Ada stood ankle-deep in the flooded hold of the Meridian Star, the ledgers heavy against her chest where she'd strapped them inside her oilskin, and watched the tide walls rise at either end of the basin like two gray hands closing. Above, on the ridge, lanterns were being lit one by one. Seven of them. Maybe eight. Pell's landing party, arranged along the crest with the patient geometry of men who had done this before.

"Ada." Idris's voice came low from the dark of the corridor behind her, where the hull's broken spine rose like the ribs of something long dead. "They've got a line rigged down the scarp. Ropes and cleats. They mean to come down."

"Then they mean to get wet," Ada said. "The basin floor won't hold a dozen men and their gear. The sand is soup. Look at the channel mouth—it's already running."

It was. The narrow throat through which they'd entered on the fifth tide was now a brown torrent, white-capped where it tore over the submerged bar. In an hour it would be impassable even to a swimmer. In two, the whole basin would be sea again, and the Meridian Star would be a rumor under twelve feet of cold water.

Which meant everyone down here—the three of them, and any Syndicate man fool enough to descend—was standing inside an hourglass.

A voice carried down from the ridge, amplified by cupped hands and the wind's odd acoustics, arriving clear and almost gentle.

"Miz Okonkwo! Might we talk?"

Mr. Pell. Even at eighty yards and across rising wind, she could hear the smile in it.

* * *

Pell crouched at the lip of the ridge, rain beading on his oiled hat, and felt the old familiar unease sitting in his stomach like swallowed ballast. Below him the wreck lay open, its hold exposed to the dying light, and he could see the woman moving through it—small, quick, deliberate. She had stripped the ship of its treasure and now she was simply waiting, which told him more than shouting ever could.

Waiting meant leverage. Or waiting meant a trap. With this one, he suspected both.

Behind him, Voss—the new man, Command's eyes—shifted his rifle against his shoulder. "Orders are orders, Mr. Pell. No witnesses."

"I heard the orders, Voss." Pell kept his voice pleasant. He always kept his voice pleasant; it was the one discipline that had never failed him. "I'm considering the *manner* of them."

He raised the speaking trumpet they'd brought ashore and let his charm do its work, pitched to carry.

"Miz Okonkwo! You've had a fine run—I'll grant you that, and I grant few things freely. But look at your position! The tide's coming in, my boats are offshore, and I have eight rifles on this ridge. Now—" he paused, letting courtesy do the threatening's work, "—I am authorized to offer terms. Hand over what you found, walk up this slope, and you'll be treated as guests. There's a promotion in it for me, and passage home for you. Nobody needs to drown today."

Silence from below. Then her voice, thinner with distance but steady as a keel:

"And if we refuse?"

Pell smiled at the darkening water. "Then the tide does our work, and we collect what floats."

* * *

In the black of the aft compartment, Nara knelt over the opened strongbox with a diver's slate and a grease pencil, copying by feel as much as sight. Her fingers moved fast—she'd spent three years transcribing harbor manifests in Port Calloway, and speed lived in her hands now like a second pulse. Page by page: dates, vessel names, payments, initials. *Kestrel Point customs, 40,000. Harbormaster Oduya. The governor's seal on a bill of lading that never existed.* Her father had died carrying proof that half the coast was bought, and every page she copied was a nail in the coffin he'd never been allowed to have.

Her light was a shuttered candle stub, hooded so no glow escaped the hatch above. Twice already boots had crossed the deck overhead—Syndicate men testing the hull, finding it rotten, retreating. Each time she'd gone still, pencil lifted, heart hammering against the ledger pages strapped beneath her jacket.

She should hate Ada for refusing to maroon Idris. Some part of her still did. But watching Ada stand in the flooded hold and bargain with a man who'd come to kill them, Nara understood something her father's letters had tried to tell her for years: there were people who ran toward the thing that frightened them, because someone had to carry the truth out of the water, and the water did not care who drowned.

She turned another page. *Vane Fortune, fuel transfer, 900 barrels, reserve tank, starboard hold.* She stopped. Read it again. And looked, slowly, toward the forward section of the wreck, where Idris had gone twenty minutes ago and not come back.

* * *

Idris worked by touch alone.

The reserve tank was exactly where the old manifest said it would be—a rusted iron belly squatting in the forward hold, still holding, by his estimate, better than half its nine hundred barrels of naphtha-thick fuel oil. Forty years in the sand had made the seams weep; the air around the tank was sweet and chemical, and every scrape of his knife against corroded metal sounded, to his ears, like a struck match.

He'd done this arithmetic before, in another life, aboard ships where the sums ended in men. Fuel, water, pressure, time. He knew what a valve would bear and what it wouldn't. He knew how much force it took to shear a forty-year-old coupling—and he knew that when it sheared, everything above the bilge line would become a bomb looking for a spark.

His brother had read these ledgers and died for it. His brother had believed paper could beat money. Idris had spent two years believing the opposite, and then one night on the Vane Fortune he'd watched Pell smile at a man he was about to ruin, and understood that the smile was the whole empire—that cruelty without charm was just violence, and violence could be fought, but charm had to be *undone*.

So he undid things. A fitting bracket here, loosened until it held by habit rather than strength. A vent pipe there, cracked so seawater would creep in on the rising tide and find the fuel. Not sabotage yet—sabotage could be discovered. Just preparation. An argument the ship itself would make, if and when Ada gave the word.

From somewhere aft, muffled through two bulkheads, he heard Pell's voice, warm as brandy, making promises. And beneath it, fainter, Ada's answer—level, unhurried, buying minutes the way she bought everything: with nerve.

He put his shoulder against the last bracket and began, gently, to work it loose.

* * *

"The ledgers stay with me," Ada called up the slope, "and we walk out of here alive. That's the trade."

"That's not a trade, Miz Okonkwo, that's a wish." Pell's voice drifted down, unbothered, almost fond. "But I admire a woman who opens negotiations high. Let me improve my offer. Your navigator—the defector. Deliver him, and you and the girl keep the boat and your lives. Command wants *him*, not you. You're a debtor, not a criminal. There's a difference, and I respect it."

Ada stood in the rising wash, water at mid-shin now, and let the silence stretch. Behind her, in the dark, she could hear the small industrious scratch of Nara's pencil—still going, still copying—and further forward, the faint metallic whisper of Idris doing whatever it was he'd been doing for the past hour. She didn't ask anymore. That was the strange grace they'd arrived at: she pointed the bow, and he found the channel, and neither demanded the other's whole account.

"They killed his brother," she said. "You know that. Everyone below a certain desk knows that. So tell me, Mr. Pell—when you deliver him, what happens? A trial? Or a quiet pier at night?"

A pause. For the first time, the warmth in Pell's voice developed a hairline crack. "Miz Okonkwo, I deliver people. What happens after delivery is above my station."

"Above your station." She laughed, short and hard. "That's what Ede Okonkwo's map was, too. Something above someone's station. You've been following us since the flats, Pell. Did you ever wonder why a dying prospector had a tracker in his map case? Who sewed it there? Because I have. And I think the answer is: somebody whose station is above yours. I think when this is done, you're not promoted. I think you're *tidied*."

On the ridge, Pell said nothing for a long moment. Rain ticked against his hat. Beside him, Voss shifted impatiently, and Pell raised one finger—not at Voss, but at the darkness, as though asking the whole world to wait.

"You're lying," he said finally, but the brandy had gone out of his voice.

"Am I?" Ada waded a step deeper into the hold. "Sixth tide, Mr. Pell. Last one of the season. Whatever you're going to do, the sea's going to do it first."

* * *

Nara heard the exchange through the hatch above her head, each word falling into the hold like stones into a well. *Tidied.* She wrote the word on her slate without meaning to, then wiped it away. Her father had used a different word for the same thing, in his last letter: *retired.*

Four pages left. She copied faster.

And then the candle guttered—no, not guttered. *Moved.* Air was coming through the forward bulkhead, a slow push of foul-smelling breath from the belly of the ship, and Nara's stomach clenched around a memory of her father's voice, teaching her cargo types on his knee: *fuel oil, little fish, heavier than water, sweeter than death.* She looked at the black seam of the forward hatch and understood, all at once, what Idris was doing back there, and what it would cost, and why Ada hadn't stopped him.

She looked down at the four uncopied pages. Then at the hatch. Then at the pages.

She picked up the pencil.

* * *

Pell gave the order at full dark.

"Two teams down the scarp. Ropes only, no lamps below the third tier. Voss takes point. We go quiet, we go fast, and we take the woman's cargo off her before the water does." He paused, and heard himself add, softly, "No shooting unless they shoot first. I want this clean."

Voss looked at him with flat young eyes. "Command said no witnesses, Mr. Pell."

"Command isn't standing in a tidal basin with a rising sea and a wreck full of fuel vapor, son. Command is dry." Pell settled his hat. "We take the ledgers, we take the defector, and the sea can have the rest. That IS no witnesses, if you're imaginative about it."

But as the first rope went over the edge and the first boots began their careful descent into the dark, Pell stayed at the top a moment longer, looking down at the black water swallowing the wreck, and Ada's words sat in him like a hook: *you're not promoted, you're tidied.* He thought of the sealed orders in his cabin, the ones he wasn't cleared to open until the cargo was secured. He thought of Voss, who reported to someone whose name Pell had never been given.

He thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he had never once seen what happened to a man after delivery.

Then he took the rope, and went down into the dark with his men.

* * *

In the hold, Ada felt the change before she saw it—the particular hush of the basin as the channel mouth closed fully, the water climbing her thighs now, warm-blooded cold, the wreck groaning around them like a living thing being asked to die twice.

"Nara," she said quietly. "Time."

"Twenty minutes to the deck line," Nara said, emerging from the aft dark, slate hugged to her chest, grease-stained to the elbow. "Maybe less. Ada—the forward hold. It's full of vapor. I can smell it from here. If anyone fires a lamp down here—"

"Nobody's firing anything." Ada's hand found the rail of the collapsed companionway, steadying herself against the ship's shudder. "Idris. How long?"

From the forward dark, his voice, calm as a depth reading: "Whenever you say. One word. But Ada—when it goes, it goes everywhere. There's no standing in the doorway on this one. We're either out through the stern breach before it lights, or we're part of it."

Above them, faintly, came the sound of boots on wet rock. Descending.

Ada looked at Nara—at the slate pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, at the four uncopied pages she'd chosen to leave behind because some prices are paid in advance. She looked toward the forward dark where a man she loved was kneeling beside forty years of buried fire, waiting for her word the way a fuse waits.

And out on the black water, the sixth tide came on—patient, enormous, indifferent to ledgers and debts and the small bright stubbornness of people—closing over the bar, closing over the channel, closing over the last way out.

The first lantern appeared at the top of the scarp.

"Ada," Idris whispered from the dark. "Say the word."
