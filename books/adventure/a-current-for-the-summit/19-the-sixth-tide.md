## 19. The Sixth Tide

The sea began to leave before dawn.

Ada stood at the lip of the basin and watched it go—watched the water slide back off the flats in long silver sheets, draining through channels it had cut for itself over centuries, retreating the way a crowd retreats from something terrible about to happen. The tide was going out farther than it had gone all season. Farther than the charts promised. The moon had conspired with the wind, and the ocean was pulling its fist back for one last punch.

"Extreme low," Idris said beside her. He said it quietly, the way men say things they don't want the universe to overhear.

"How long?"

"Ninety minutes before it turns. Maybe less if that swell line means what I think it means." He nodded toward the horizon, where the storm Pell's fleet had scattered from two days ago was circling back like a dog that hadn't finished its argument. It wore a new face now—reorganized, sullen, enormous—and it was dragging a surge behind it that would fill this basin faster than any tide on record.

Behind them, down on the exposed seabed, the *Meridian Star* lay naked in the sand.

Ada had imagined the wreck a hundred times since Ede put his map in her hands with his blood still wet on it. She had imagined masts, a hull, some dignity of ruin. What she saw instead was a wound. The salvage ship had broken her back on the bar forty years ago and the sand had swallowed her whole, and now, with the sea withdrawn, she emerged like something exhumed—a black spine of keel running sixty yards across the flats, ribs of ironwood jutting up through the wet gravel, her hold split open along one side like a rib cage pried apart by impatient hands. Kelp hung from her bones in ropes. Barnacles crusted every surface in gray constellations. She smelled of tar and dead things and old iron, and she was, without question, the most beautiful thing Ada had ever seen.

"There," Nara said. Her voice cracked on the word. She had not slept; none of them had. She pointed to the split in the hull, where the morning light fell into the darkness of the hold and stopped, blocked by a collapse of cargo and sediment four decades deep. "The ledgers will be in the strongroom. Aft, below the waterline. My father's letters were specific."

"Your father," Ada said, "was Syndicate for eleven years and never told you."

"No." Nara shouldered her pack—the one with the waterproof wraps, the copying paper, the pens she'd been filling all night by lamplight. "He didn't tell anyone anything. That was the problem with him alive, and it's going to be the problem with him dead, because we're standing on everything he knew and half the coast wants it buried deeper than he is."

Idris touched Ada's elbow. Just that. Two fingers, light as gull-down. "Pell's boats launched twenty minutes ago. I counted engines."

"I know. I heard them too."

"They'll be on the ridge before we're out of the hold."

"I know that too." Ada looked down the long slope of the basin, at the wreck lying in her shallow grave, at the ridge beyond where the land climbed away toward the tree line—and she thought about the fuel.

She'd found it yesterday, on their first reconnaissance, when the tide had given them only a quarter hour. Aft of the strongroom, in what had been the engine compartment, the *Meridian Star* still carried her bunkers: tanks of salvager's diesel, sealed since the sinking, corroded but intact. Forty years of fuel, pooled and preserved in the dark like a heart that had never stopped being full. Idris had put his hand flat against the tank plating and said, *One spark down here and the whole stern goes.* He'd said it as a warning.

Ada had heard it as an option. She'd been carrying the option around ever since, in the place behind her sternum where she used to carry the debt.

* * *

They crossed the flats fast, three figures on a seabed that had no business holding human weight, boots punching through the crust of wet sand into the sucking gray beneath. The air smelled of iodine and exposed mud, the smell of a world turned inside out. Gulls screamed somewhere above them, outraged at the feast the tide had uncovered—crabs, stranded fish, an old net heavy with rot.

The hold swallowed them one at a time. Ada went first, down through the split plating, into a dark that smelled of rust and brine and something older, something like a church. Her lamp threw shadows that moved wrong. The collapsed cargo rose around them in dunes—bales swollen to twice their size, crates fused into conglomerate, the whole mass cemented by four decades of tide into a single geological formation. Somewhere inside it, sealed in lead-lined cases, sat the ledgers. Every debt the Vane Syndicate had ever written down. Every official bought, every portmaster paid, every coastal government that had signed its name in the margins of other people's ruin.

Forty years of crimes, waiting in the dark to be read aloud.

"Strongroom hatch is under there." Nara's lamp swept the dune of cargo. "We dig from the top. Idris, the brace points are—"

"I see them." He was already moving, driving pitons into the old frames, rigging lines so the collapse above wouldn't become a collapse below. His hands were steady. Ada watched them and thought about the night in the eye of the storm, those same hands, and made herself stop thinking about it, because thinking about it would make her hesitate, and hesitation today would cost more than money.

They dug.

It was brutal, close work—pry bars and hands and the short-handled spade from Ada's kit, hauling cargo piece by piece out of the way while the wreck groaned around them like a ship still trying to die properly. Sediment rained. Water seeped up between the deck plates below them, cold and patient, the returning sea already announcing itself through the hull. Ada lost track of time. There was only the next bale, the next crate, the next inch of buried steel.

Then Nara's pry bar rang against something that wasn't cargo.

"Here." Her voice came out strangled. "Ada. Here."

They cleared it together. A hatch, round, riveted, painted once in the Syndicate's corporate green—a green Ada knew from Mr. Pell's cufflinks, from the trim on the *Vane Fortune*, from the ledger covers themselves. The lock had rusted to lace. Nara hit it twice with the pry bar's flat and it gave like an old man's promise, and the hatch swung open on a compartment dry as a tomb.

Inside, racked in rows, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed against four decades of salt water: the ledgers.

Dozens of them. Ada lifted the nearest and felt its weight—paper and ink and consequence. Through a tear in the oilcloth she could see columns of figures, initials, dates. Names she recognized. Ports she'd run from. Interest rates that were just theft wearing arithmetic.

"That's it," Nara whispered. She had both hands pressed over her mouth. When she took them away her eyes were streaming, though her face didn't otherwise move at all. "That's everything he died carrying."

"Copy fast," Ada said. "Wrap faster. We have maybe forty minutes before—"

"Before we're invited to leave." Idris stood at the mouth of the hold, looking up the slope of the basin. His face had gone very still, the stillness he wore when the navigator in him was reading bad news off the horizon. "Ada. Come look."

She climbed up beside him.

On the ridge, silhouetted against the bruised sky, men were moving. Six of them, maybe eight, spreading out along the crest with the unhurried competence of professionals who had done this many times and never once been interrupted. One of them knelt and set up something on a tripod. Glass flashed.

And walking down the slope ahead of the others, alone, hands in his pockets, coat immaculate even after two storms and a shipwreck's worth of chasing—came Mr. Pell.

* * *

He stopped thirty yards from the wreck, at the edge of the wet sand where the footing turned treacherous, and called out in the same warm voice he'd used in Ada's galley a lifetime ago, the day he'd smiled at her over her own table and told her how much she owed.

"Miz Okonkwo! You've made a remarkable nuisance of yourself." He spread his arms, taking in the basin, the wreck, the drained and gleaming flats. "Look at this. Look at what you've found. All I ever wanted was to share in your success."

"You wanted the ledgers," Ada called back. Her voice carried flat and clear across the seabed, the way sound does over water-smoothed ground. "You've wanted them for forty years. Ede was bait. You let him carry that map around like a lure on a line, and when he finally hooked someone desperate enough to bite, you followed the fish home."

Something flickered across Pell's face—not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt. Recalculation. "Ede was an employee with delusions of retirement. And you, Miz Okonkwo, are a debtor in possession of stolen property, standing on ground my employers own outright. Now. You're going to bring those ledgers up this slope, and I'm going to write you a receipt, and then—" he smiled, and it was the smile from her galley, warm as a heating stove, "—we're going to discuss your balance."

"The originals don't leave this hold," Ada said.

"Miz Okonkwo—"

"Nara!" she shouted, without turning her head. "Now."

Below her, in the dark, she heard Nara's pen already moving. Fast strokes. Pages turning. And Idris sliding down past the strongroom, past the engine room, into the belly of the wreck where the tanks waited full and forty years patient.

Pell's men started down the slope.

They came carefully at first, testing the sand, and Ada understood with a cold clarity that had nothing frantic in it: the tide was turning. She could feel it in her boots before she could see it—water rising through the sediment, the flats beginning to drink. In an hour this whole basin would be a churned cauldron between collapsing tide walls, and Pell knew it too, which was why his men had stopped being careful. They fanned out wide, cutting angles, closing the distance to the wreck with drawn weapons held low.

"Pell!" Ada's voice again. "How many ledgers do you need? How many names? Because I've seen the first page, and your name isn't in the margins. Do you know whose is?"

He kept coming. But slower. She'd found the seam in him—the one she'd suspected since chapter one of their acquaintance, since the day he'd smiled at her across her own table with something tired behind the charm. Men like Pell believed in the ledger. Believed the book was the power. Not the men above him, not the machine—*the book*. He'd spent his whole career one promotion away from mattering, and the thing that would make him matter was sitting in a hole at his feet.

"Last chance, Miz Okonkwo."

"Come take them."

He came.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way catastrophes do—each fragment complete and lit and separate, and no time at all between them.

Nara bursting up through the hold hatch with the copied pages wrapped tight against her chest, oilcloth and wax and waterproof canvas, screaming at Ada to move. The first shot cracking off the wreck's ironwood ribs and going somewhere over the water. Idris's face appearing in the dark below, one nod. That was all she needed. One nod.

Ada went down into the hold.

Not away from it—into it. Down past the strongroom, down the tilted companionway into the engine room where the air was thick enough to chew, where the tanks rose out of the black like sleeping animals. Idris had already done the work: the valve on the main bunker cracked open, diesel whispering out across the deck plates in sheets, finding every seam, every bilge, every low place forty years had carved. The smell hit her like a wall. She dropped to her knees at the tank bank and did the last part herself, because it was hers to do—stripped the insulation off the battery bank, wired the terminals together the way her father had shown her when she was nine and the world was still a place where fathers taught daughters useful things, and ran the leads down into the pooling fuel.

"Ada." Idris at the companionway, silhouetted against the gray light above. Shots hammering the hull now, ringing like a church bell rung by madmen. "Ada, it's time."

She looked at the wires. She looked at him.

"If I stay down here when it goes—"

"You won't." He came down the last steps and took her wrist, and his hand was shaking, and that undid her more than the guns. "You hear me? You don't get to die paying someone else's debt. That's the whole point. That was always the whole point."

Above them, boots on the hull. Voices. Pell's voice, closer now, inside the wreck, calling down into the dark with the warmth gone out of it at last, stripped to the cold wire underneath: *Miz Okonkwo. Miz Okonkwo, we can still be reasonable.*

Ada closed the circuit.

Nothing. A heartbeat of nothing, the worst heartbeat of her life—

Then the flash ran down the bilge like lightning finding a river, and the *Meridian Star*, dead for forty years, exhaled fire.

* * *

They came up the companionway into a world made of noise. The stern of the wreck lifted on its own explosion, cargo and plating and burning diesel thrown across the flats in a rain of black confetti, and the shockwave picked Ada up and set her down hard ten feet from where she'd been, ears roaring, vision white. She crawled. She remembered crawling—over plates that shivered under her hands, toward daylight, toward Nara's scream, toward the hole in the world where the hold's mouth had been.

Behind her, the fire found the rest of the fuel and the wreck began, in earnest, to die.

She got out. She would never know how. She got out onto the flats with Idris dragging her by one arm and Nara on her other side, and the three of them ran—or stumbled, or were thrown—across sand that was no longer sand but slurry, the returning tide meeting the blast wave and going mad, walls of brown water building at the basin's mouths and folding inward like hands closing.

Pell was still on the flats.

Ada saw him through the smoke, and the image would stay with her longer than the burns, longer than the deafness that took a week to fade. He had reached the wreck. He had actually reached it—he'd come down the slope into the killing ground for the book, because the book was the only god he'd ever kept faith with—and now he stood ankle-deep in burning diesel runoff with a ledger case in his hands, one of the originals, torn from the strongroom in the seconds before the fire. Holding it. Holding it while the water came.

"Drop it!" Ada screamed, and her voice came back to her as a stranger's. "Pell—it's paper! Drop it and swim!"

He looked at her across fifty feet of boiling shallows. And she watched him understand—watched the arithmetic finish behind his eyes, the final entry posted in the great ledger of himself—and she watched him choose.

He chose the case.

The tide wall took the ridge road first, cutting off his men, and then it took the flats, and Mr. Pell went down reaching for the fortune that had defined him, and the sea—which forgets less than anything alive—closed over the both of them without comment.

* * *

The basin ate everything.

Ada and Idris made the high shelf on the north wall as the two tide walls met over the wreck's grave with a concussion she felt in her teeth. Nara was already there, soaked, bleeding from a cut over one eye, clutching the wrapped pages to her chest with both arms like a child. Behind them, where the *Meridian Star* had lain, the water boiled over burning fuel, flames riding the chop in yellow ropes, and black smoke stood up against the storm sky in a column visible, Ada knew, from ten miles off—from the ridge where Pell's surviving men were even now deciding whether grief or self-preservation owned them.

Her hands were burned. Her left ear rang with a high pure tone, a note held forever. She was missing, she realized distantly, one boot.

Idris pulled her back from the edge, away from the heat, and held her face in his shaking hands and looked at her the way navigators look at fixed stars.

"You're alive," he said. "Say it."

"I'm alive."

"Say the rest."

Ada looked out at the burning water, at the grave she'd made, at the fortune and the evidence and the enforcer all dissolved together into the same indifferent sea. Somewhere in that churn were forty years of written crimes, and somewhere in her pack—no, in Nara's arms—were the copies, every page, wrapped in wax and oilcloth and stubbornness. The Syndicate's originals were gone. But testimony wasn't paper. Testimony was people. Three of them, standing on a shelf of rock above a drowning basin, holding the truth in a waterproof wrap and each other up by the elbows.

"We're alive," she said. "All three of us. And we're not done."

The storm hit the coast twenty minutes later, and the three of them went down into the trees to meet it.
