## 20. What the Sea Kept

The sea gave her back the way it gave back everything it did not mean to keep—grudgingly, in pieces, on its own schedule.

Idris saw her first as a smear of color against the gray churn, a scrap of oilskin snagged where the retreating tide had dumped her against a shelf of black rock. The basin was emptying behind him, the sixth tide running out with all the violence of a thing insulted, and the water that had swallowed the Meridian Star was now dragging everything loose back toward open ocean—splinters, rope, a ship's boat torn from its davits, and Ada Okonkwo.

He went in without deciding to. Later he would remember it that way: no thought at all, just his boots filling, then his chest, then the cold closing over his ears like a door. The current took him sideways immediately, slammed him against something soft and yielding—a body—and he caught a fistful of her jacket before the sea could reconsider.

She was heavy with water and limp in the wrong way. He got an arm across her chest, hooked his chin over her shoulder, and kicked for the rock shelf, and the whole time the basin roared around them like a crowd that had paid to see blood and was being denied its show. His shoulder found stone first. He hauled. The water fought him for every foot of it, sucking at their legs, furious at being robbed, and when at last he dragged her onto the wet black rock above the reach of the surf, he collapsed beside her with his lungs full of salt and his hands shaking so badly he could not work the zipper of her jacket.

"Nara!" His voice came out shredded. "NARA!"

* * *

She came down the ridge at a dead run, slipping twice, the waterproof wrap clutched to her chest like an infant. She had been higher up, he learned afterward—had seen the fuel go, had seen the whole eastern wall of the wreck fold inward and the fireball stand up out of the sea like a second sun, had seen Pell's landing party scattered into the surf and the flagship's boats turning back. She had been screaming Ada's name into wind that ate sound. Then she had seen nothing at all, and had kept screaming anyway.

Now she dropped to her knees on the rock and put her ear against Ada's chest, and Idris watched her face while she listened, and the three seconds she took were the longest of his life, longer than the channel, longer than the storm's eye, longer than every lie he had ever told to stay alive.

"She's breathing." Nara's voice cracked clean through. "Idris—she's breathing."

Ada was not beautiful in that moment. She was gray-lipped and bleeding from a gash along her hairline, one arm bent at an angle that made Nara swear softly and competently, ribs visibly wrong beneath the soaked oilskin. Her hands were torn to ribbons from the rock. When Idris peeled back an eyelid, her pupil answered the light slowly, sluggishly, but it answered.

"Ada." He leaned close. "Ada Okonkwo. You're out. You're out of the water. Do you hear me?"

Her lips moved. No sound came. Then, faintly, a word shaped more by breath than voice:

"...told you."

He laughed—or made a sound that wanted to be laughter and came out closer to sobbing. "You told me what?"

"Fuel... stores." Her eyes opened halfway, unfocused, fixed somewhere past his shoulder at the smoking ruin of the basin. "Told you... they'd go."

"You told me they'd go," he agreed, and pressed his forehead gently to hers, salt and blood between them. "You were right. You were right about everything. Now stop talking and let us carry you."

They splinted the arm with a broken oar and strips of Nara's shirt. They wrapped the ribs with tape from the emergency kit in the skiff—the Syndicate's own skiff, abandoned on the ridge when Pell's men fled for the Fortune, which was a joke Idris intended to savor someday when he had the strength. They got her into the boat's stern, cushioned with everything soft they owned, which was almost nothing.

Before they pushed off, Idris stood once and looked back at the basin.

There was nothing left to look at. The Meridian Star was gone—not sunk but unmade, her hull folded into the sand, her superstructure shattered, the ledgers' iron strongbox somewhere under forty feet of churning water and half a million tons of shifting seabed. Smoke still rose from the fuel slick, thin and black and already thinning. On the far ridge, small figures moved among the rocks—Pell's survivors, or what remained of them—too far away to matter and too broken to pursue. Somewhere out beyond the headland, the Vane Fortune rode at anchor, and Idris found that he could not make himself care. Let them search. Let them drag the basin for a year. The originals were gone, and gone was the only place the Syndicate could never follow.

"They'll say we destroyed evidence," Nara said quietly, beside him. She had followed his gaze. "That's what they'll say. That we burned the proof."

"They can say whatever they like." He turned back to the boat, to Ada's gray face, to the wrap in Nara's arms. "We have the truth. That's worth more than paper."

It was only partly true and they both knew it. Paper was exactly what mattered now—specific paper, specific pages, in specific hands. But the words felt true in his mouth anyway, and after the last six tides he had decided he was entitled to a few untrue-feeling truths.

They pushed off into open water.

* * *

The skiff was never meant for three people and a wounded woman across open sea, and they made a poor procession of it. Nara took the tiller because her hands were steadiest; Idris sat in the stern with Ada's head in his lap and did the only work left to him, which was watching her breathe.

In and out. Slow, shallow, hitching sometimes when the swell rolled them, but in and out. He counted the breaths the way he had once counted bearings, the way he had once counted the days until his brother's trial, the way he had counted everything in his life because counting was how you held on to time when time kept trying to take things from you. In and out. Nineteen years old, Kofi had been, when they came for him. In and out. Fourteen days since Idris had jumped ship off the Vane Fortune with a stolen chart and a plan so thin you could read a ledger through it. In and out.

"Stop staring at her like that," Nara said, without turning. "You'll wear a hole in her."

"I'm counting."

"I know what you're doing." A pause; the tiller creaked. "I did it all night after the fourth tide. After the patrol. Counting her breaths through the hull." Another pause, longer. "I owe you an apology, Idris Vane."

He went very still. "You know my name."

"Ede knew your family. Before." She kept her eyes on the horizon. "He wrote about it. The navigator's son who read too much. He wrote that the Syndicate would kill that boy someday, and that when it happened, someone should remember it wasn't justice—it was housekeeping." Her jaw tightened. "When I saw the tracker in the map case, and understood what Ede had been used for—I thought maybe you'd signaled them. It fit. It fit too well. That's why it convinced me. Because I needed it to. Because if it was you, then my father hadn't sold us all. He'd just been robbed."

"And now?"

"Now I know my father sold us all anyway." Her voice did not break, but it bent, the way a mast bends before it goes. "And I know you didn't signal anyone. And I am sorry, is what I'm saying, for standing on that deck and telling Ada to maroon you. And I'm saying it out loud because my father spent thirty years not saying things out loud, and look where it got everyone."

Idris looked down at Ada's face, slack and salt-streaked in his lap, and thought about all the things none of them had said out loud, and how much of the last six tides had been built from silence stacked on silence.

"Apology accepted," he said. "For what it's worth, I thought about signaling them. At the third tide. The listening post. One transmission and I'd have been back aboard the Fortune with a clean record and a promotion." He watched the swells. "I couldn't do it. Not because I'm good. Because she looked at me like she already knew I wouldn't, and I couldn't stand to prove her wrong."

"That's not nothing," Nara said. "Being unable to disappoint someone. Half the world's evil gets done by people who've got nobody left to disappoint."

* * *

They ran through the afternoon with the coast a low blue rumor to starboard, staying wide of the shipping lanes, wide of the anchorages, wide of everything. Twice Idris spotted sails and made Nara alter course without either of them discussing it; the habit of being hunted had grown into their bones, and neither of them trusted it to be finished yet. The Fortune would send boats along the coast. The Fortune would want witnesses. But the storm had cost the fleet two ships and its patience, and the smoke still rising off the basin would occupy them for days, and so for now—for this one gray, salt-bleeding afternoon—they were simply three people in a small boat, and the sea, having taken everything else, could not be bothered to take that.

Ada woke near sunset.

It was not a gentle waking. She surfaced the way she did everything, fighting, and the first breath she drew came with a sound like tearing canvas, and then she was coughing seawater and trying to sit up and Idris had both hands on her shoulders holding her down while she cursed him with a fluency that, under other circumstances, he would have admired.

"Lie still. Your ribs—"

"I know my ribs." She lay back, gray-faced, blinking at the sky. Her eyes tracked slowly: the sail, the tiller, Nara, him. "The ledgers."

"Gone," Idris said. "All of them. The basin has them now."

"The copies?"

Nara lifted the wrap. "Waterproof. Oilskin over waxed canvas over oilcloth. Papa taught me to pack documents for monsoon country when I was nine. I used to think it was a strange thing to teach a child." She set it in Ada's lap, and Ada's ruined hands closed around it with a grip that had nothing weak left in it anywhere.

"How many pages?"

"Forty-one. The ones I copied during the standoff. Names, dates, disbursements. Governors, magistrates, harbor masters. Six coastal governments, at minimum." Nara's voice was steady, but her knuckles were white on the tiller. "It's enough. It's enough to start."

Ada looked down at the wrap for a long moment. Then up at the sky, where the first stars were coming out over the water, indifferent and clean.

"He died on the flats with this in his coat," she said. "Ede. He carried it twenty years and it killed him, and he gave it to a stranger because his own daughter wouldn't speak to him." She turned her head, carefully, toward Nara. "I'm sorry. I promised him I'd bring you the map, and instead I brought you into a gunfight."

"You brought me the truth about my father," Nara said. "Both truths. The bad one and the worse one. Nobody else alive could have done that." She looked away, out at the darkening water. "He chose you because you were nobody to him. That's not an insult. That's the only kind of person you can trust with the worst thing about yourself."

Ada's eyes found Idris's then, and held, and there was something in them he had not seen before—not the wariness, not the calculation, not even the tenderness of the eye of the storm. Something quieter. Something that looked, unbelievably, like peace.

"We lost it," she said. "The fortune. All of it. Do you understand that? We are three people in a stolen skiff with no money, no ship, no cargo, and a Syndicate that wants us dead, and I have never—" Her voice caught. She laughed, wincing around her ribs. "I have never felt this rich in my life."

* * *

They anchored after dark in a cove Idris knew from his fleet days—a deep-water notch behind a fisherman's island, unmarked on any chart the Syndicate bothered with, because there was nothing there to steal. Nara rigged the sail as a shelter. Idris heated water over a spirit stove and got broth into Ada, spoonful by spoonful, while she complained with diminishing conviction. Her fever was low but present; her breathing rattled on the left side; she needed a doctor, real bandages, real rest, and none of those things were available tonight, and all of them might never be.

But she was alive. He kept returning to that fact the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. He had watched the fuel go up. He had watched the sea close over the place where she had been standing. And here she was, propped against the gunwale, arguing with Nara about whether the broth needed salt, alive, alive, alive.

"You should sleep," he told her.

"So should you."

"One of us should."

"Then we're at an impasse." She leaned her head back and looked at him sideways, and even bruised and half-drowned she managed to make the look land somewhere below his ribs. "Sit here anyway."

So he sat. The cove breathed around them—water on stone, the halyard ticking against the mast, Nara asleep under the sail within minutes, sleeping the way only the truly exhausted sleep, instantly and totally, her hand still curled near the waterproof wrap.

"Idris." Ada's voice had gone quiet, almost gone. "When we get to the magistrate. When this is done. What happens to you?"

He considered lying. It was his oldest skill, and she was half-asleep, and it would be easy.

"I don't know," he said. "I defected. I helped sink a Syndicate operation. There will be people who want me for that, on both sides. I may need to be someone new." He paused. "I've been practicing. Idris Vane is a name I inherited from a man who worked for monsters. Maybe the next name fits better."

"What name?"

"I haven't chosen yet." He looked at her. "I was thinking of asking someone whose judgment I trust."

Ada smiled—small, tired, real. "Ask me tomorrow," she said. "Ask me when I can feel my face. Tonight I'm just going to listen to the water and be grateful, and tomorrow I'm going to be ambitious again, and you can have whichever version you prefer."

"Tomorrow, then."

Outside the cove, the night went on doing what nights did—turning, breathing, keeping its own counsel. Somewhere north, the Vane Fortune rode at anchor over an empty basin, and men were drafting reports that would end careers, perhaps including their own. Somewhere south, a federal magistrate slept unaware that forty-one pages were coming for him. And here, in a cove too small to matter, three people who had nothing left but the truth lay in a stolen boat and listened to the sea keep what it kept, and let go of everything else.

Ada's hand found his in the dark. Her fingers were torn and cold and closed around his with surprising strength.

"Tomorrow," she murmured, and was asleep before the word finished leaving her.

Idris stayed awake a while longer, counting her breaths, and for the first time in fourteen days—not since the deck of the Fortune, not since Kofi, not since he was a boy reading his father's charts by lamplight—he was not counting because he was afraid of losing something.

He was counting because he wanted to know exactly how long this lasted.
