## 21. Testimony

The magistrate's office sat above a chandlery in Port Merrow, up a staircase that smelled of tar and old paper, behind a door with frosted glass so clouded by salt air that the letters of his name had worn to ghosts. Nara climbed those stairs twice before she found the courage to knock.

The first time, she had turned around on the third step and gone back down into the street, where the morning fish carts were rattling past and the gulls were screaming their usual grievances, and she had stood there with the oilskin packet under her coat against her ribs like a second heartbeat, telling herself that this was foolishness, that men like Magistrate Corren were fossils, that her father's faith in them had been the faith of a younger man who hadn't yet learned what the world did to people who believed in institutions.

The second time, she knocked.

"Come," said a voice like gravel poured slowly.

Corren was older than she had imagined—Ede's letters had made him sound like a mountain, and mountains did not usually come in the form of a stooped man with ink-stained fingers and reading glasses pushed up into thin white hair. He looked at her the way a surveyor looks at unfamiliar terrain: patiently, completely, missing nothing.

"You're Ede Okonkwo's girl," he said. Not a question.

"I'm Nara Okonkwo." She set the packet on his desk. Her hands were steady. She had rehearsed this moment for eleven days, through three ferries and one night in a harbor flophouse listening to rats in the walls, and in all that rehearsal her hands had never once shaken. "My father asked me to bring you something. Before he died."

Corren did not reach for the packet at once. He took off his glasses, folded them, set them down beside a brass lamp whose shade was dented as though it had survived some long-ago violence. Then he looked at her again, and something in his face shifted—not softening exactly, but a settling, like a man accepting the weight of something he had been braced for across many years.

"He finally sent it," Corren said quietly. "Forty years I waited for that letter to come. Forty years, and I'd begun to think I'd written my last report about the Vane family for nothing." He drew the packet toward himself but did not open it. "How did he die?"

"Gut-shot, on the flats below Graywater Point." Nara heard her own voice go flat and procedural, the way it went when feeling pressed too close to the surface. "He gave a map to a woman named Ada Okonkwo—no relation—and made her promise it would come to me, or to no fleet at all. The ledgers are copies. The originals are at the bottom of the tidal basin where the *Meridian Star* went down. A man named Pell drowned trying to keep them from the water."

"Pell." Corren said the name the way a doctor says the name of a disease he has been tracking for decades. "Aldous Pell. Yes." He untied the oilskin wrapping with fingers that trembled slightly—not from age, Nara thought, but from something closer to reverence. Inside were the pages Nara had copied by lamplight in the wreck's hold while guns waited on the ridge above, each sheet sealed in waxed wrap against the damp, each one transcribed in her own careful hand because the originals could not be risked and photographs would have required light they did not have.

She watched Corren read. She watched the color leave his face by degrees, the way tide leaves a flat—not all at once but in slow, inevitable withdrawal, exposing things that had always been there beneath the surface. Shipments logged and vanished. Payments routed through harbormasters' offices in six towns. Names she recognized from newspaper obituaries and wedding announcements, names attached to judges and councilmen and two federal customs officers, all of them fed from the same ledger, all of them marked in her father's cramped, patient hand with dates and amounts and, in one column that made her stomach turn even now, initials that stood for people who had asked too many questions and stopped asking anything forever.

Idris's brother's initials were among them. She had found them on the fourth night of copying, and had sat with the page for a long time before sealing it, thinking of the way Idris said his brother's name—carefully, like carrying something breakable across ice.

"These are authenticated?" Corren asked at last. His voice had changed. It was quieter and harder at once, the voice of a man who had just been handed a weapon forty years too late and forty years exactly on time.

"My hand copied them. My word stands behind them. And there are three witnesses who saw the originals before they were destroyed—one of them a Syndicate navigator who defected, one of them the woman who sank the wreck, and one of them me." She paused. "I understand that makes us compromised sources. I understand what their counsel will say about us."

"They'll say we're liars, thieves, and blackmailers," Corren said. "They'll say it loudly and hire very good people to say it." He began, methodically, to rewrap the pages. "And then they will lose, because ledgers do not lie, Miss Okonkwo. People lie. Ledgers only count. And your father counted everything." He looked up at her, and for the first time his eyes were wet. "He was the best clerk I ever knew. Did he ever tell you that? Before—" he gestured vaguely, taking in the whole distance between then and now, the whole ruined country of Ede's middle years, "—before he went over to them?"

"No," Nara said. "He never told me much of anything. That was rather the problem."

* * *

It did not happen the way she had imagined it, in the fevered rehearsals of the passage south. There were no sirens, no headlines shouting from the corners, no dramatic perp-walks in the rain. Justice, it turned out, arrived the way weather arrived in the shoulder season: as a change in pressure that most people felt without being able to name.

First came the quiet things. Corren swore out warrants in chambers, behind closed doors, with a federal marshal named Okafor who had spent nineteen years being passed over for promotion because he would not stop filing reports about cargo discrepancies in Vane-chartered vessels. Okafor read the ledger copies for two days without sleeping more than four hours, Nara later learned, and on the third day he began making arrests in an order calculated not to alert the top of the structure until its legs had already been cut away.

The harbormaster of Saltreach was taken at breakfast, gently, without handcuffs visible, escorted to a car by two men who might have been accountants. The customs officer in Bellhaven resigned suddenly, citing family reasons, and was arrested at the ferry terminal trying to board with two suitcases and a forged passport. A judge in the interior—Nara had to sit down when she read that name in Corren's office, because she had stood before that judge once, at nineteen, in a dispute over her mother's estate, and had thought him stern and fair—was suspended pending investigation, and the investigation lasted nine hours before it became an indictment.

The newspapers caught up eventually, the way newspapers do. THE VANE PAPERS, the coastal editions called it, and for two weeks the front pages were a cascade of names and numbers, and the editorial writers discovered reserves of outrage they had somehow never located during the decades when the outrage might have done some good. Nara read every word of it. She told herself it was research. She knew it was really a kind of hunger—to see, printed in cold type, confirmation that it had all been real, that the gun barrels on the ridge and the tracker sewn into her father's map case and the long gray years of his silence had all pointed toward something true enough to topple.

The Syndicate fractured precisely the way Idris had predicted it would, sitting across the fire from her on the night after the sixth tide: not with a crash but with a rot, outward-in. The fleet's remaining ships were impounded or sold off by creditors. Mid-level men began testifying in exchange for leniency, each one eager to describe how he had always been uneasy, always been a small cog, always been about to get out. The Vane family itself—the patriarch, the sons, the fortress office in the capital—held out longest, hiring the very good lawyers Corren had promised, and for a month it seemed possible they might simply outlast the storm, the way they had outlasted every storm for fifty years.

Then Okafor found the second set of books. Nara never learned all the details of how—a defector within the family's own accounting house, someone whispered; a safe-deposit box in a bank that had failed, others said—but the effect was simple. The second set of books matched her father's first set, entry for entry, figure for figure, and no army of lawyers could argue with arithmetic confirmed by the enemy's own hand.

On a gray Tuesday in late autumn, Nara stood in a courtroom gallery and watched the eldest Vane son enter a plea. He did not look like a monster. He looked like a tired man in an expensive coat who had run out of coast to run along. She felt nothing that she had expected to feel—no triumph, no catharsis, none of the burning satisfaction she had carried through the tidal basin like a lantern. What she felt instead was quieter and stranger: the sensation of a door closing gently somewhere deep inside her, and of the room beyond it finally being still.

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, Marshal Okafor shook her hand with a grip like a mooring line. "Your father built this," he said. "Forty years ago he built it, and it held. You should know that. Whatever else happened to him, whatever he did in the dark years—the foundation he laid held the whole weight."

"He'd have wanted to hear that," Nara said. "He'd also have argued with you about the load calculations for an hour."

Okafor smiled for the first time in her presence. "Corren says the same thing. Says Ede Okonkwo could find an error in a saint's arithmetic." His smile faded into something more careful. "There will be reprisal attempts. Small ones, probably—the organization is bleeding out, but a dying animal bites. The magistrate has arranged protection for you, for Okonkwo, for the navigator. Take it or don't, but know it's offered."

"I'll take it," she said, surprising herself. Once, she would have refused on principle, the way her father had refused everything, alone, armored in self-sufficiency until the armor became a coffin. But she had watched Ada sail into a named storm and understood something new about strength—that it was not the refusal of help but the wisdom to know which currents to ride.

* * *

They buried Ede Okonkwo in the churchyard at Graywater Point on a day of high thin clouds, with the wind coming off the flats in long clean gusts that smelled of salt and drying kelp.

Nara had chosen the plot herself: the rise at the eastern edge, where the ground was firm and the view ran all the way down to the tideline where he had died. The stone was local granite, unpolished, and the inscription had taken her three weeks to settle. In the end it was short, because everything essential about him had always been short:

**EDE OKONKWO**
*He kept the count.*

Ada had driven up from the south in the boat-turned-truck arrangement that Nara had given up trying to understand, arriving the evening before with Idris in the passenger seat arguing amiably about navigation charts spread across the dashboard. They stayed in the village inn, the three of them, and ate supper at the common table with fishermen who had known Ede distantly, warily, the way coastal people knew everyone—by reputation and rumor and the occasional shared drink—and who, hearing now what the quiet prospector had actually been, raised their glasses to him with the uncomplicated respect of working people for a job finally finished.

At the graveside, the minister said the standard words, and Nara listened to them with half her mind while the other half watched the clouds move. When it was done and the small congregation had dispersed, she remained standing at the head of the grave, and Ada came to stand beside her, hands in pockets, saying nothing, which was one of the many things Nara had come to value about her.

"He trusted someone," Nara said at last. "That's what I keep coming back to. After everything they did to him—after he spent twenty years as their instrument—he still trusted that the truth would find a place to land if he just got it far enough downstream." She shook her head. "I don't know if that's faith or foolishness."

"Both," Ada said. "Usually both." She crouched, touched two fingers to the cold granite, briefly, the way you'd check a hull for stress. "For what it's worth—I promised him the map would come to you or no fleet. Best promise I ever kept. Worst-paid, too." The ghost of her crooked smile. "Idris keeps saying the Syndicate owes us back wages. I keep telling him the sea settled our accounts."

"The sea settles nothing," Nara said. "It only holds things. We're the ones who settle." She looked down at the stone, at the words she had chosen, and felt the last knot inside her—the one wound around the question she had carried since the flats, the question she had shouted at a dying man and never gotten answered—finally loosen. *Why didn't you come home? Why did you choose them over me?*

She had found the answer, in the end, not in any confession but in the ledgers themselves. In the margins of the earliest pages, in her father's young handwriting, were notes—not accounting notes, but human ones. Dates of arrests prevented. Names crossed out and rewritten in code, lives rerouted around the Syndicate's attention by a clerk who was quietly, dangerously, sabotaging the machine from inside his chair. He hadn't chosen them over her. He had stayed inside the beast because it was the only position from which he could blunt its teeth, and he had paid for that choice with the one thing the beast demanded: her.

"I forgive him," she said aloud, to the granite, to the wind, to herself. "I want that written somewhere the stone can't hold. I forgive him."

Ada put a hand on her shoulder, brief and hard, and said nothing, and it was enough.

They walked down together toward the tideline, where Idris waited with his collar up and the wind pulling at him like it wanted to recruit him. Beyond him the flats stretched out gray-gold to the horizon, and far out, where the water deepened, Nara could just make out the faint disturbance on the surface that marked the basin where the *Meridian Star* lay under its shroud of sand—the place where her father's last secret and the Syndicate's greatest treasure had gone into the dark together, and where, she suspected, they would both remain until the sea decided otherwise.

"Corren sent a message this morning," Idris said as they reached him. He held out a folded paper, and there was something in his face Nara had learned to read carefully—a tension that wasn't fear, exactly, but readiness. "The trials start in spring. They want testimony. Yours, mine, Ada's. Under oath, in open court, names attached."

Nara took the paper but didn't unfold it. She looked instead at Ada, who was watching the horizon with the particular stillness she wore when a storm was building somewhere beyond sight of land—an expression Nara had come to think of as listening.

"Well," Ada said. "Spring's a long way off. And I hear the current runs south this time of year."

Idris grinned. Nara, despite everything—despite the grave behind her and the trials ahead and the whole unfinished business of a life rebuilt from wreckage—found herself grinning too.

The wind came up the flats, clean and strong, carrying the smell of the next storm, and the three of them turned toward it.
