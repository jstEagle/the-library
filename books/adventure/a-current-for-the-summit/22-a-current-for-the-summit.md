## 22. A Current for the Summit

The headland had not changed.

Ada had half expected it to—half wanted it to, the way a person wants a wound to scar over visibly, so the world can see what happened here and adjust itself accordingly. But the grass grew in the same wind-flattened combers it always had, silver-green and stubborn, and the gulls still wheeled over the tide flats below with their small greedy cries, and the sea went on doing what the sea does: erasing, erasing, erasing.

Three weeks since the sixth tide. Three weeks since she had stood in the collapsing dark of the Meridian Star's hold and pulled the lever that gave the wreck back to the water. Three weeks since Pell drowned reaching for ledgers that were already pulp, his hand closing on nothing but foam and rot as the basin swallowed him whole. She could still see it sometimes when she closed her eyes—not the drowning itself but the look on his face in the last second before the wall of water took him. Not fear. Recognition. As if he finally understood that he had spent his whole life chasing something the sea had never intended to give anyone.

She had almost drowned too. Her body still carried the proof of it—a rib that ached when the weather turned, a long rope-burn across her palm that had scabbed, softened, and begun at last to fade into something that might one day be only a line instead of a wound.

She stood now where Ede had died, at the lip of the headland where the grass gave way to bare rock, and looked down at the flats that had held him. The tide was out. Far out, the sand gleamed pale and wet under a bruised afternoon sky, stitched with channels of standing water that caught the light like spilled mercury. Somewhere down there, once, a gut-shot prospector had pressed a map into a stranger's hands and made her promise a thing she hadn't understood yet.

*Deliver it to my daughter. Not to any fleet.*

Ada crouched and laid her palm flat against the rock. It was warm from the thin sun that had fought through all morning, and rough, and real.

"You'd laugh," she said quietly, to no one, to Ede, to whatever part of a man the wilderness keeps after it takes the rest. "You'd laugh yourself sick."

Because it had worked. Not the way either of them imagined—the fortune gone, burned and drowned with the wreck, every salvageable credit of it traded for a handful of copied pages wrapped in oilcloth—but it had *worked*. The magistrate in Corren Port had taken Nara's testimony behind closed doors for six days. Then the doors opened, and the arrests came down the coast like weather: two harbormasters, a shipping commissioner, the entire Vane Syndicate board of liquidation seized by federal marshals before they could scatter their assets into the usual offshore dark. The papers called it the Meridian Reckoning. They got most of the details wrong, which suited Ada fine. Names were currency, and she had learned exactly how expensive hers could be.

Her debts were gone—not paid, *dissolved*, along with everything else the Syndicate had owned when the marshals froze its books. Mr. Pell's cheerful ledger, with Ada Okonkwo's name inked in the margin beside a number that had once seemed large enough to own her, was now evidence in a federal archive. She had asked, once, half-joking, whether she could have a copy. The clerk had looked at her as though she were mad.

Maybe she was. It had felt, for eleven years, like the truest thing about her—that number, that debt, that weight. She had built her whole life around carrying it the way a mule builds its gait around a load. And now the load was gone and her spine still curved for it, still braced against a weight that wasn't there.

She straightened up. The wind off the water pushed through her hair, salt-sharp, and somewhere beneath it she smelled the thing she had been smelling since dawn, faint but unmistakable, the smell that lived in her blood older than any debt:

Rain coming. A long way off, but coming.

* * *

Idris found her there an hour later. She heard him before she saw him—she always did now, had learned the particular rhythm of his footfall on rock, unhurried, deliberate, a navigator's walk—and she didn't turn, just listened to him come up the slope and stop beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched.

"Nara says the boat's ready," he said. "She says if we're not aboard by slack water she's leaving without us, and she says it in a tone that makes me believe her."

"She would." Ada smiled at the horizon. "She's her father's daughter."

"She's angrier than her father ever was."

"She's honest about it. That's different."

They stood together in the wind. Below them the flats lay bare and shining, and beyond the flats the sea ran gray-green to the horizon, and beyond *that*, far out where the water met a sky going the color of old pewter, a storm was building. She could read it the way other people read letters: the towering cumulus stacked up like a fortress wall, the anvil top shearing east on the upper winds, the green-black light gathering underneath where the rain curtains hung. A named storm, or soon would be. The forecasters would give it some bland syllable tonight and the shipping lanes would bend around it and the coastal towns would nail down their shutters, and Ada Okonkwo would do none of those things.

She felt Idris watching her watch it.

"You're doing the thing again," he said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at a storm the way other people look at—" He stopped. Started over. "The way you used to look at me. Before you decided whether to trust me."

"Careful," she said. "That's dangerously close to flattery, and I've seen what happens to men who flatter me near open water."

"What happens?"

"They end up navigating for me."

He laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she had coaxed out of him maybe five times in all the weeks since the eye of the storm, since the night in the calm center when they had finally spoken plainly and then stopped speaking altogether. She turned and looked at him properly. He was thinner than when she'd met him, harder, sunburned across the cheekbones; the Syndicate navigator who had jumped ship with a stolen chart and a dead brother's name in his mouth had been replaced by someone leaner and more himself, the way driftwood is more itself after the sea is done with it. There was a new scar on his forearm from the fuel fire. He wore it without hiding it.

"So," he said. "Where next?"

It was a simple question. It was also the question—the one that had been circling them both for three weeks like a bird deciding whether to land. Because the answer determined everything: whether this partnership, this fragile unnamed thing between them, was a season or a life; whether the boat pointed north or south or out; whether Ada Okonkwo, debt-free and name-clear for the first time since she was nineteen years old, would finally do the thing free people do and choose.

She let the silence stretch. Let him stand in it. That was fair—he had made her stand in plenty of silences, back when she couldn't verify a word he said and needed his charts anyway.

"I had a letter from the magistrate's office," she said at last. "Yesterday. They want me to testify formally. In session. On the record." She watched his jaw tighten. "I told them yes."

"Ada—"

"It's done, Idris. The copies are public record now. My name's already in the filings—Nara made sure of that, God help her, she cross-referenced everything. There's no version of this where I stay a ghost." She shrugged. "And I find I don't mind. That's the strange part. Eleven years I hid my name because of what it cost. Turns out it was worth more than I thought. Worth spending."

He was quiet a moment. "Pell's people are scattered, not gone. You know that. A syndicate like Vane doesn't die with its board. It goes to ground, grows back."

"I know."

"And you're going to stand in a courtroom and say your name out loud anyway."

"I'm going to stand in a courtroom and say *his* name out loud," she said. "Ede's. He carried this longer than any of us. He died on that headland holding it alone. Somebody's going to say his name in daylight, in front of everyone, and mean it clean." She looked down at the flats. "That's the last piece. That's the cargo I never delivered until now."

The wind shifted. Out past the horizon the storm wall had begun to lean, its whole vast mass tilting eastward with the turning of the upper air, and the light under it had gone that peculiar bruised gold that meant the rain was organizing itself into something serious. Ada felt the old pull in her chest, the current she had been born to, and this time—for the first time she could remember—it didn't feel like being dragged. It felt like being asked.

"My mother used to say the wilderness forgives nothing," she said. "She said it like a warning. Like it was a reason to stay small, stay ashore, keep your head down and your debts paid." She shook her head slowly. "But I've been thinking about it, and I think she had it backwards. It forgives nothing *and forgets less*—that's true. Every mistake you make out there is written down somewhere permanent. But that's not cruelty. That's honesty. It's the only place left that tells you the truth about yourself every single time, no matter how much money you have or who you owe." She turned to face him fully. "I spent eleven years owing people lies. I'm done with it."

"And the storm?" He nodded toward the horizon. "That's a bad one building. Another three days and it'll be on the coast."

"I know."

"We could run ahead of it. Or around it. Or we could sit in port and drink cheap rum while it passes, like sensible people."

"Have you ever once known me to be sensible?"

"Once," he said. "In the hold of the Meridian Star, when you hesitated before pulling the lever. For about four seconds, you were the most sensible woman on that coast."

"And then?"

"Then you pulled it."

"Then I pulled it." She smiled, and felt the smile reach all the way down to whatever it is in a person that decides things. "Testimony first. Two days in Corren Port, saying names into a record. And after that—"

"After that?"

"After that, we chase it."

She turned back to the horizon, to the storm assembling itself out of heat and water and wind, vast and indifferent and utterly honest, and she felt the current take hold of her—not the Syndicate's current, not the debt's current, not any current anyone else had set running, but her own, the one that had been there all along underneath everything, waiting.

"Anywhere it runs," she said. "That's where we go. Anywhere the current runs."

Idris looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached over and laced his fingers through hers, rope-burned palm against fuel-scarred hand, and together they stood at the top of the headland where a dying man had once handed a stranger a map, and watched the storm come on.

Below them, far out on the flats, the first white line of surf marked the tide beginning to return.

It was, Ada thought, going to be a magnificent season.

* * *

They came down off the headland as the light failed, taking the switchback path two abreast, and found Nara waiting at the skiff with her arms crossed and the last of the daylight burning orange on the water behind her. She had Ede's compass strung around her neck now—Ada had given it to her after the testimony, and Nara wore it the way other people wore wedding bands.

"Well?" Nara said. "Corren Port first, or are we already famous?"

"Testimony in two days," Ada said, stepping into the boat and taking the tiller. "Then open water."

Nara looked at the storm on the horizon, then at Idris, then at Ada, and something in her face loosened—the grief transmuted at last into something that could carry weight without crushing the bearer. "He would have hated this plan," she said. "Too reckless. Too many storms."

"He would have loved it," Idris said, casting off the line.

"He would have pretended to hate it," Nara corrected, settling into the bow, "which is the same thing."

Ada opened the throttle and swung the bow seaward, into the deepening gold of the channel, and the headland fell away behind them—the place where a promise had been made, and kept, and outlived its maker. Ahead, the first outriders of the storm were already reaching down to touch the water, long gray fingers of rain walking across the sea toward them, patient as anything the ocean had ever sent.

Ada steered straight for them.

The current was running, and for once in her life, she was running with it—free, named, and exactly where she belonged.
