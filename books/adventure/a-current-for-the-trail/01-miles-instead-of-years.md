## 1. Miles Instead of Years

The thief had a head start of nine hours and no water sense at all.

Theo Ashgrove read the story in the sand the way other men read letters: the horse had favored its near hind since dawn, the rider had dismounted twice to walk it, and somewhere past the third mile marker he'd stopped bothering to cover his tracks. Amateurs always did. The desert forgave nothing and remembered everything, which was why Theo had chosen it — a country that kept better records than he did, so that his own could stay blank.

He rode with his collar up against the wind that came off the flats like a held breath let out slow. The sun stood two fists above the horizon, laying his shadow long behind him, as if even that wanted to stay where he'd been rather than go where he was going.

Seven years. He did the arithmetic without meaning to, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Seven years since the expedition, seven years of contracts taken short and abandoned early, of towns left before anyone could learn his middle name, of miles counted instead of days because miles didn't ask questions. He had walked — he had actually sat down once with a pencil and figured it — something over eleven thousand miles on horseback and on foot. Enough to circle the world halfway. Not enough, apparently, to outrun one dry spring in the Redstone Range.

He put the thought down the way you set down something hot. There was a thief ahead of him, and thieves were simple. Thieves were the kind of problem a man could solve by sundown.

* * *

He found the stolen horse first, standing hipshot in the shade of a leaning sandstone slab, its saddle still cinched. The thief had gone on foot into a maze of washes that braided through the low hills — bad country for a man carrying a canteen, worse for a man who didn't know that the washes all drained toward a sink with no exit.

Theo followed at a walk. No hurry. The heat would do half his work, and thirst the rest.

He caught sight of him just past noon: a lean figure in a dust-colored coat, moving with the stumbling urgency of a man who had realized too late what kind of mistake he'd made. Theo swung wide, cut the angle, and came down into the wash ahead of him, sitting his horse in the only shade for a quarter mile, hat brim low, rifle across his knees.

The man stopped dead when he saw him. Then, because they always did, he started talking.

"I bought her fair," he said. "Off a man in Candelario. I got a bill of—"

"You stole her from the livery at Salt Fork," Theo said. "The bill of sale is written in your own hand. I've seen your handwriting. It's better than your tracking."

The man's shoulders dropped. He was young — twenty-five maybe, with the kind of face that hadn't decided yet what it was going to be guilty of. "You're the ranger," he said. "They said there was a ranger wouldn't quit."

"They say a lot of things." Theo dismounted, took the rope off his saddle, and tossed it over. "Tie that around your wrists. You'll ride behind me. Your horse walks tied to mine, and if either animal suffers because of you, I'll make the rest of your life very educational."

The young man tied the knots. They were good knots, Theo noted. That was the trouble with this country — it produced men who could do everything except stay out of it.

They rode back east through the afternoon, the thief silent behind him, the flats going gold and then bruise-colored as the light failed. Theo counted the miles without wanting to. Fourteen to the water cache. Six more to the way-station. Numbers were safe. Numbers stacked up between him and everything else, a wall built one unit at a time, and if he laid enough of them he could almost forget there was anything on the other side worth remembering.

Almost. The desert had a way of leaving gaps in walls.

* * *

The way-station at Salt Fork was a stone hut with a stove, a cot, and a window that framed forty miles of nothing. Theo turned his prisoner over to the deputy there, ate a supper of beans and salt pork standing up, and was cleaning his rifle when the deputy came back in holding a telegram flimsy like it might bite.

"For you. Came up from the district office marked urgent."

Urgent, in Theo's experience, meant someone else's emergency wearing his name. He took the paper anyway.

**RANGER ASHGROVE STOP SURVEY PARTY FOUR MEN ONE WOMAN DEPARTED REDSTONE STATION TWELVE DAYS AGO STOP FAILED TO REPORT AT EAST SUPPLY STOP RELIEF RIDER FOUND CAMP ABANDONED GEAR INTACT NO SIGNS STRUGGLE NO TRACKS LEAVING CAMP STOP PROCEED REDSTONE IMMEDIATELY STOP YOU KNOW THAT COUNTRY STOP**

He read it twice. *You know that country.* Whoever had written the dispatch had never met him, or they'd have phrased it differently. Or maybe they had met him. Maybe somebody in the district office had a long memory and a short sense of mercy.

"No tracks leaving camp," the deputy said, reading over his shoulder. He whistled. "Five people don't just evaporate."

"Out here they do." Theo folded the flimsy along its creases. "Sand takes tracks inside an hour if the wind's right. But gear intact means they weren't rushed. And no graves means nobody died there. They walked out under their own power, or they were taken walking."

"Where would they walk to? East Supply is dead flat country."

Theo looked at the window, at the dark coming down over the flats, and felt something old shift in his chest — not dread exactly, and not eagerness either, but the familiar gravitational pull of a direction. Any direction. That was the whole secret of his life, the engine under everything: give him a heading and he could keep the past at his back. Standing still was the only thing he couldn't survive.

Redstone Range. The name sat in him like a swallowed stone. He had sworn, seven years ago, with the spring dying under his hands and a woman's brother buried in the slide they'd caused, that he would never go back. He had kept that oath the way he kept all his others — technically, briefly, and until it became inconvenient.

"The survey party," he said. "Who's on it?"

Deputy shrugged. "List's on the back."

Theo turned the flimsy over. Four names he didn't know. And beneath them, typed in the same flat capitals:

**VANE, I. — TOPOGRAPHER**

He read it three times. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind changed and began to carry the smell of high cold air, the first breath of a season turning somewhere far off in the mountains.

Vane. He knew that name. He had spent seven years making sure he'd forget it, and here it was, typed by a stranger's hand, waiting for him at the end of a wire.

A sensible man would write back declining. A sensible man would cite the conflict, the history, the hundred reasons the district could find another ranger. Theo folded the flimsy into his breast pocket, next to nothing, and began checking his gear by lamplight — water bags, rope, the spare horseshoes, the tin of matches he always carried double.

"You're going," the deputy said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Any direction," Theo said, "is better than standing still."

He said it lightly, the way he said most true things. The deputy laughed, and Theo let him, and went on packing in silence while the wind outside rose and rose, carrying down from the Redstone Range the first thin promise of snow.
