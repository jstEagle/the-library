## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The camp looked like a photograph of itself.

Theo Ashgrove stood at the edge of it for a long moment before stepping in, the way a man pauses at the door of a house where something has just gone wrong. Four tents, pegged and guyed with textbook neatness. A cook fire burned down to white ash, raked flat. Instruments in their cases, cases latched. A brass transit on its tripod, still leveled, still aimed at nothing. Six bedrolls — he counted twice — six, though the survey team had been seven men and women strong when it rode out of Fort Meridian three weeks ago.

No blood. No scuffle. No tracks leaving, which was the part that sat wrong in his chest like a swallowed stone. The ground around the camp was hardpan and gravel, and the wind had been working it for days, but even so, even allowing for that, there should have been *something*. Hoofprints going out. Drag marks. A dropped canteen. A camp abandoned cleanly was rarer than a camp abandoned bloody, and far more troubling. Blood told you what happened. Nothing told you only that someone had wanted nothing known.

He walked the perimeter slowly, hat brim low against the glare. The Redstone Range shouldered up ahead of him, red and black strata folded like cloth left too long in the sun, and above the highest saddles the sky had begun to assemble its argument — thin cirrus drawn out into long gray filaments, the first handwriting of a storm that would, within four or five days, close every pass in the range until spring. He'd read the clouds his whole life. These were reading early this year, and reading mean.

The seventh bedroll was missing from the count, but not missing from the camp. He found its owner's traces near the water barrels: a tin cup set upright on a rock, deliberate as a signature. A boot heel dug into the dirt, one print, pointing south toward the slot canyons that cut into the range's flank like knife wounds. And scratched into the barrel stave with a knife point, small enough that you'd miss it if you weren't looking for small things:

*P. went to find water. Follow the dry wash.*

Theo straightened and looked south. The wash ran down out of the range, bone-dry, its banks cut deep by some ancient flood. Somewhere up that throat of stone, a man named Pell had gone looking for water in country that hadn't given any freely since — 

He stopped himself. Seven years of practice at stopping himself.

He swung into the saddle instead and rode into the wash.

* * *

The walls narrowed. That was the way of slot canyons; they started wide enough to drive a wagon through and ended narrow enough that a man turned sideways had to exhale to pass. The light came down in ribbons, amber and dusty, and the sound of his horse's hooves changed from a clatter to a hush as the sand deepened. Theo dismounted where the canyon pinched and went on foot, leading the animal, because a man who rushed in a slot canyon was a man who drowned in a flash flood or walked off a drop with no bottom to it.

He found the first sign of Pell a half mile in: a strip of shirt cloth snagged on a tamarisk root, pale blue, torn clean. Then a smear in the sand where something heavy had been dragged sideways toward an overhang. Then, wedged in a crack above his head, a survey stake with a ribbon of red cloth tied to it — not a marker left by the team. Tied recently. Tied by someone who wanted to be found, or wanted whoever followed to know they were expected.

His hand went to his rifle without his deciding anything about it.

Around the next bend the canyon opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, light falling through a crack in the rimrock a hundred feet up, and against the far wall, wrapped in a survey tarp, sat Ilsa Vane.

She was thinner than the last time he'd seen her, which had been across a hearing room in Fort Meridian seven years ago, and her hair was shorter, and there was a new scar through one eyebrow. But her eyes were the same, and they were fixed on him with an expression he recognized the way you recognize weather coming: cold, patient, certain.

"Ashgrove." She said it the way you'd name a disease. "They sent *you*."

"I volunteered," he said, which was true, and which sounded worse than a lie.

"Of course you did." She didn't move from the wall. Her rifle lay across her knees, and her hand rested on it in a way that was not quite casual. "Seven men ride into the Redstone and vanish, and the Army's answer is Edmund Dunmore's favorite ghost. Did they tell you what happened here? Or did they figure you'd already know?"

"I don't know what happened here."

"No." She almost smiled. It wasn't a kind shape. "You wouldn't. You never knew what happened anywhere. Things just dried up around you, didn't they? Springs, rivers, people."

The words landed where she'd aimed them. He let them. "Where are the others?"

"Camp. Lower down, past the fork — two of them twisted ankles in the scree, one's fevered. I've been running supplies between here and there while we waited for someone to come find us." She tilted her head. "Waiting took eleven days. Interesting arithmetic, the Army's."

"The passes close soon. I'm here ahead of them, barely."

"And Pell?"

"Gone after water, by the note on the barrel. How long?"

Her jaw worked. "Nine days. He said the maps were wrong. Said there had to be water in the limestone, that the old-timers' charts showed a river through the range before the surveys stopped marking it." She watched him say nothing, and whatever she saw in his face made her go very still. "You know why the old charts stopped marking it."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't insult her with an answer.

"My brother mapped that country," Ilsa Vane said quietly. "Before your expedition. Before the spring at Coyote Wells went dry overnight and forty head of cattle died and my brother rode out to trace where the water had gone and never came back. They found him at the edge of the sink, Ashgrove. Just the edge. The rest of him is somewhere under the range, in whatever hole your crew covered up with blasting powder and lies." She rose, slow, the tarp sliding off her shoulders. "So understand me. I signed onto this survey because the Redstone was the last uncharted basin, and I have spent seven years learning everything about it, waiting for the day someone sent me here. And then they sent *you* to fetch me out, and God help me, I don't know whether to shoot you or follow you."

Above the crack in the rimrock, the ribbon of blue sky had gone the color of pewter. The wind changed, and came down the canyon carrying the smell of snow from peaks neither of them could see yet.

"You'll follow me," Theo said. "Because I'm the only one who knows where the water went. Which means I'm the only one who knows where Pell will look for it — and where your brother is."

She stared at him a long moment. Snow-smell moved between them like a third person in the chamber.

"If you're lying," she said finally, "I'll leave you in the limestone with the rest of your secrets."

"That's fair," Theo said, and picked up her pack.
