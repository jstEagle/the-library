## 3. The Current Beneath

The sink opened in the earth like a wound that had never learned to close.

Theo found it at dusk, following the thread of signs Pell had left — a boot heel dragged through alkali, a strip of shirt cloth snagged on greasewood, and then, impossibly, a trail of wet prints. Wet, out here, where rain hadn't fallen since June. The prints led down a slope of broken limestone into a bowl two hundred yards across, ringed with rock the color of old blood, and from somewhere below came a sound Theo had not heard in seven years.

Water.

Not the thin gossip of a seep. A river's voice — deep, patient, moving under stone.

He sat his horse at the rim and did not move for a long time. Ilsa Vane drew up beside him, her mule blowing hard behind her, and he saw her go still in the same way, head tilting, listening, her face doing something complicated that she shut down before it finished.

"You know this place," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I knew a place like it." He dismounted because his legs wanted to run and a man couldn't run on horseback. "Different range. Same kind of country. Limestone underneath everything, holding water like a sponge holds blood."

"Say what you mean, Ashgrove."

He meant: I helped kill one of these. He meant: we drilled it, diverted it, capped it, and filed papers saying it had failed naturally, because the company needed the water rights dead and the government survey needed to find nothing. He meant: a woman named Adah Reyes drowned in the collapse when the cap gave way, and we buried her report with her. But what he said was, "Pell went down there. We'd best follow."

The storm had been building all day over the high passes — a bruise-colored wall pressing south, smelling of iron and snow. They had maybe a night, maybe less, before it closed the crossing. Every hour they spent descending into that hole was an hour stolen from the climb out. Theo knew it. Ilsa knew it. Neither of them said it.

They left the animals hobbled at the rim and went down on foot, using the last gray light. The sink's floor was slick travertine, terraced like the inside of a shell, and the river sound grew until it filled the skull. Then Ilsa stopped so abruptly Theo nearly walked into her.

"Look."

A seam in the rock, waist-high, breathing cold air. And past the seam — light. Not firelight exactly. A pale, wavering glow, as if the dark itself had been persuaded to shine.

Theo struck a match and shielded it, and the flame bent toward the seam like a thing called home.

* * *

The passage widened into a cathedral.

Their lantern showed walls of flowstone hung in frozen curtains, white and amber, dripping still after ten thousand years of the same work. And beneath them, visible through gaps in the living rock, the river ran — fast, green-black, glittering where their light touched it, a current forty feet down and utterly indifferent to them. It had never stopped. It had only gone deeper, found another channel when men sealed the first one, and kept its own counsel in the dark ever since.

"There," Ilsa whispered.

Pell lay on a shelf of stone above the water, wrapped in his own bedroll, one leg splinted with a survey stake and bound with wire. He was gaunt, feverish, his lips cracked black — but alive. His eyes found the lantern and he began to laugh, a dry terrible sound.

"The keeper of the water," he croaked. "Come to count your miles over my grave."

"Pell, it's Ashgrove. We're getting you out."

"No." He tried to rise and failed, clutching the stone. "No, you don't get to say that name down here. Down here we use true names." His gaze slid past Theo to Ilsa, and something almost gentle moved through the madness in it. "Tell him, Vane. Tell him who taught you to read contour lines. Tell him why you took this survey."

Ilsa's hand had gone to her belt, not for a weapon — for a folded paper she carried against her ribs, Theo realized, the way other people carried letters from home.

"My brother Tomas mapped these ranges," she said. Her voice was level the way ice is level. "Four years ago. When the springs along the eastern route failed — all of them, in one season, all at once — his report said it wasn't natural. Said somebody had cut the aquifer. The company called him a liar. The territory called him a troublemaker. In the winter after, he rode out to prove it and didn't come back." She unfolded the paper with hands that did not shake, though her breath did. "His last letter said he'd found the diversion records. Signed by a field engineer named T. Ashgrove."

The lantern hissed. The river ran on below, patient as judgment.

"Say it isn't so," Pell whispered, grinning with cracked lips. "Go on. Say it."

Theo looked at the water. Seven years he had ridden away from it, and here it was, running under his feet the whole time, under every mile he'd counted, the same current wearing the same stone. There was no direction left. There was only down.

"It's so," he said.

The words came out small and then larger, the way water opens a crack.

"We were contracted to assess the eastern aquifer for the Cobre Irrigation Company. What we found would have killed their dam project — the water table was too high, the limestone too unstable. So the company asked us to make the problem disappear, and we did. We drilled relief channels and diverted the feeder veins into a deep fault, capped the springs, and reported natural failure. My signature is on the survey. My hands did half the work." He turned to face Ilsa fully, because running from her eyes was the one distance he could no longer measure. "Your brother was right about every line of it. And when he came asking, the company sent men to discourage him, and I signed the affidavit that said he'd falsified his data. That affidavit is the reason no one looked for him properly. If he died out here, some part of it is mine."

The silence afterward was not empty. It was full of the river.

Isla stood very still. Then she took one step forward, and another, until she was close enough that he could see the storm-light reflected small in her eyes, and her hand rose — and closed on his collar, not to strike but to hold, the way you hold a thing to keep it from going over an edge.

"Do you know what I planned?" she said quietly. "For seven years I planned it. I'd find you in some nowhere town and I'd put the truth in front of you and I'd watch. That was all. Watch you be small." Her grip tightened. "And now I'm standing in the truth and it's a hole in the ground, and there's a blizzard coming over the pass, and Pell has a fever climbing toward his heart, and I have to choose between watching you be small and getting us out alive."

"Then choose the pass," Theo said. "Take Pell. Leave me the lantern and the rope. I'll file the confession myself, every page, under my own hand. You'll have your brother's name cleared without dirtying yours."

"That's not justice. That's just another man riding away."

"Maybe." He held her stare. "But it's the first direction I've ever pointed on purpose."

Above them, faint through the limestone, came the first sound of the storm arriving — wind rising over the rim, and with it, fine as flour, the first snow sifting down through the sink's open throat, drifting past the lantern light like ash falling upward.

Pell laughed again, softer now. "Storm's early," he murmured. "Pass closes tonight. Nobody crosses till spring." His eyes rolled toward the green-black current below. "Unless somebody knows where the water goes."

Theo followed his gaze — and felt the old surveyor's instinct wake in him like a second pulse. Rivers under limestone always found daylight eventually. Somewhere downstream, this one broke surface; he could read it in the draft pulling warm through the southern passage, in the smell of sagebrush riding underground air.

There was a way out that wasn't the pass.

He looked at Ilsa. She looked back, snow melting on her cheeks like tears she refused to claim, and nodded once.

"Get Pell up," Theo said, turning toward the southern dark. "We're going to follow the river home."
