## 1. The Last Train Out

The last train out of Copperline Junction left at 6:14 in the morning, and Theo Reyes was the only passenger aboard who hadn't been paid to be there.

He sat in the second car — the first had been sealed off with a padlock and a company notice that read *NOT IN SERVICE* in letters so official they seemed to apologize for nothing at all — and watched the escarpment country slide past the fogged glass. The engine was an old Baldwin the railroad had been meaning to retire for eleven years, and it complained about the grade the way an old man complains about stairs: loudly, rhythmically, and without any real hope of being listened to.

There were four other souls on the train. A conductor named Pell, who had worked this line for thirty-one years and now spent his final run checking tickets nobody held. Two track inspectors riding in the caboose, playing cards with a deck missing the jack of hearts. And up front, a fireman whose name Theo had never learned, though he'd seen him a hundred times at the water stop at Mile Forty, waving with a gloved hand like they were old friends.

Theo checked his watch. 6:31. They were seventeen minutes behind schedule, which didn't matter, because there was no schedule anymore. There was no timetable pinned to the station wall at Copperline. There was just a date circled in red grease pencil on the dispatcher's board — *LAST RUN — OCT 12* — and beneath it, in smaller type, the phrase the company always used, the phrase Theo himself had typed into more reports than he could count:

*Line rendered redundant by revised traffic projections.*

Redundant. He turned the word over in his mind like a stone found in a boot. Somewhere south of here, in a warm office with a window that opened onto a parking lot, a man he'd never met had decided that two hundred miles of rail, forty-three bridges, six tunnels, and every town between Copperline and the coast were redundant. And somewhere north of that office, Theo Reyes, licensed surveyor, third generation, had drawn the maps that made the decision easy.

The train rocked through a cut and came out above the river valley, and there it was — the escarpment, rising out of the morning mist like the back of something enormous and patient. Limestone and pine, shadow and green, running north as far as the eye could follow. Theo had surveyed its southern shoulder twenty-two years ago, fresh out of his apprenticeship, his father's transit case still smelling of his father's pipe tobacco. He remembered thinking then that no line of steel would ever belong in country like this. He had been right, in the way that mattered least.

"Ticket," said Pell, materializing beside him with the weary ceremony of a man performing his own funeral rites.

Theo handed over his stub. "You could just wave me through, Pell. You know I don't have anywhere else to be."

"That's exactly why I check." Pell punched the ticket with a click that sounded final. "Rules are what's left when everything else gets taken away. A man ought to hold onto something." He lingered, looking out the window at the passing pines. "You drew this one, didn't you? The Copperline extension."

"I did."

"Good line. Smooth grades. My back's grateful after thirty years." Pell said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "Shame about it."

"Yes," Theo said. "It is."

Pell nodded once and moved down the aisle, punching the empty air where other tickets might have been.

* * *

Theo spent the morning doing what he always did on trains, which was cataloguing. It was a habit from his surveying days, a compulsion really: he could not pass a landmark without fixing it, measuring it, filing it away. But lately the catalogue had changed character. These days he mostly inventoried closures.

The Marrow Creek branch — closed '09. He had walked that alignment himself, staking grades through alder bottomland while a crew boss named Duchamp sang French songs off-key and swore the creek would flood the ballast every spring. It flooded every spring. The company closed the line rather than raise the trestle, and Duchamp took a job hauling timber and died when a load shifted, and Theo had gone to the funeral and said nothing, because what was there to say? *I'm sorry your livelihood was redundant.*

The Kestrel Pass route — closed '11. That one had hurt more, because Kestrel had been beautiful engineering, switchbacks climbing through the notch with a gradient so gentle the old-timers called it "the stairway." Theo had surveyed the alternative mainline through the flats, straighter and cheaper and flatter in every sense, and the company had chosen his line over the stairway, and within four years the flats line was washing out every winter while Kestrel Pass grew weeds through its ties. He had stood at the top of the abandoned notch once, alone, wind coming through the gap with a sound like a choir holding one long note, and felt something he couldn't name at the time. He could name it now. It was shame.

The Silver Fork tramway. Closed. The Harbor loop. Closed. The little spur into Alder Basin that served nothing but a cheese factory and a church camp, closed with such efficiency that the rails were pulled before anyone thought to ask the church camp if they minded.

By his own hand or under it, Theo Reyes had helped erase four hundred miles of country from the map of living things. Not maliciously. That was the terrible part. He had been good at his work — careful, honest about elevations, meticulous about curves — and the work had been used the way a good knife is used, for whatever the hand holding it wanted. He had told himself for years that a surveyor draws what is, not what should be. But the older he got, the less comfort there was in the distinction. A man who measures a thing for the people who mean to remove it is not neutral. He is simply the first step of the removal, wearing boots.

The train slowed at Mile Eighty-Seven, where a water tower leaned over the tracks like a drunk uncle at a wedding, and stopped.

It wasn't scheduled to stop. Nothing was scheduled anymore. Theo leaned toward the window and saw the reason: a cow, enormous and unbothered, standing square in the middle of the right-of-way, chewing with the serene entitlement of an animal that had never heard of revised traffic projections.

The fireman climbed down and waved his hat. The cow considered him. The inspectors came forward from the caboose and stood in a row, hands in pockets, watching the standoff with the appreciation of men being paid by the hour for the last time. Pell appeared at Theo's seat.

"We'll be a few minutes," he said. "You want coffee? There's some in the caboose. It's terrible."

"Then yes."

They walked back together along the swaying vestibule, and Pell talked the way lonely men talk on last days — about his wife in the city who had already rented their apartment to someone else, about the conductor's watch his grandfather left him, about how he meant to learn the cello, God help everyone. Theo listened and answered when answering was wanted, and all the while part of him stood apart, taking inventory again: *one conductor, one cow, one career, closed.*

In the caboose, the card game paused to make room. The coffee was indeed terrible, scorched and bitter, and Theo drank it gratefully. One of the inspectors, a heavy man with forearms like dock rope, squinted at him.

"You're Reyes, ain't you? The surveyor?"

"I am."

"My brother worked the Kestrel Pass local. Danny Ostrander. Says he met you once, up at the notch. Said you knew that line better than the men who ran it."

"I knew it well enough to help close it," Theo said.

Ostrander shrugged, unbothered, dealing cards. "Lines close. Rivers dry. My brother says the funny thing is, the country doesn't care. Deer on the ties within a year. Alders through the platforms. Country takes it back like we borrowed it." He flipped a card onto the floor of the caboose. "You going home, then? To the city?"

"I suppose."

"Huh." Ostrander studied his hand. "You don't look like a man going home. You look like a man whose house burned down and nobody told him yet."

Theo laughed despite himself. "That's a hell of a diagnosis, Mr. Ostrander."

"Card player's instinct. I can tell what a man's holding even when he keeps his face shut." He tapped the deck. "Sometimes a man stays in a losing game too long because folding feels like dying. Ain't always about the cards."

The train lurched. From up front came a triumphant bellow — the cow had been persuaded, or had simply finished chewing — and the whistle blew long and low across the valley, echoing off the escarpment wall, and Theo set down his cup and looked out the little rear window of the caboose at the country falling away behind them, and felt the truth of it settle in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He wasn't going home. Home was a boarding room and a pension file and a shelf of survey reports, each one a small tidy obituary for a piece of the world. Going home meant sitting in that room with the catalogue of closures, growing old the way a line grows old after the last run: officially present, functionally gone.

* * *

The waystation appeared at half past noon, out of a bend in the river, and it was barely worth the name. A platform of gray boards. A depot with one window boarded and the paint gone the color of old bone. A sign reading *HARROW'S BEND*, the H dangling from one screw like a man from a ledge. Behind the depot, a dirt road wandered off toward the escarpment and lost itself in the trees, and beyond the trees, the wall itself rose sheer and green-shadowed, close enough now that Theo could see the strata in the limestone, layer upon layer, a hundred million years of patience stacked against the sky.

The train stopped anyway. Pell later claimed it was a signal check; the inspectors claimed it was the cow's cousin; Theo suspected, then and ever after, that the train simply stopped because it was tired, and because Harrow's Bend was the kind of place where stopping felt permitted.

"I'll stretch my legs," Theo said.

"Two minutes," said Pell. "We're behind schedule we don't have."

The platform boards creaked under his boots. The air smelled of river water and pine pitch and woodsmoke from somewhere unseen, and it was warmer here than the city, softer, and the river ran loud around the bend below the depot, brown-green and muscular, heading north into the escarpment country with the confidence of something that had never once needed a traffic projection.

There was a bench on the platform. On the bench sat an old man asleep, or nearly — a weathered figure in a canvas coat, a canvas pack at his feet, a hat over his eyes. As Theo passed, the man stirred and spoke without lifting the hat brim.

"Last one, is it?"

"The last one," Theo said.

"Huh." A pause. The river filled it. "Rode the first one through here, summer of '61. Whole town turned out. Brass band. Fella gave speeches about progress." The hat tilted slightly, and one bleary eye regarded Theo. "You with the railroad?"

"I was. I'm done as of today."

"Done." The old man tried the word, rolling it like tobacco. "My daughter says that word like it's a door opening. Her mother said it like a door closing. Same word." He settled back. "Which is it for you?"

Theo looked down the track, where the rails ran gleaming and empty toward the city, toward the boarding room, toward the shelf of obituaries. Then he looked at the road behind the depot, wandering off into the trees toward the wall of the world.

"I don't know yet," he said honestly.

"Well." The old man resettled his hat. "River'll tell you. River tells everybody eventually. Most folks just don't speak the language till it's too late to do anything about it."

From the engine came Pell's voice, thin with distance: *"All aboard! Last call, Mr. Reyes!"*

And here is the thing Theo would try to explain for the rest of his life, to Wren when she asked, to Hark over cards, to the county board itself once, awkwardly, under oath: he did not decide. There was no moment of resolve, no grand turning. His feet simply made the choice his grief had been circling for twenty years, the way water makes a channel — slowly, invisibly, and then all at once, with the whole weight of the man behind it.

"Mr. Reyes!" Pell called again. "Train's leaving!"

Theo stood on the platform of Harrow's Bend, one foot toward the rails and one toward the road, holding a ticket stub in his pocket for a journey he was not going to finish, and heard the river say the only word it knew.

"Tell them I missed it," he called back.

The whistle blew — long, mournful, forgiving — and the Baldwin gathered itself and pulled away, and Theo Reyes stood alone on the last platform of the last line he would ever abandon, listening to the sound fade up the valley until the escarpment swallowed it whole.

The old man under the hat chuckled softly. "Well, I'll be. First one in forty years to get off here instead of on."

"You live around here?" Theo asked.

"Nobody lives around here," the old man said, standing stiffly, shouldering his pack. "Some of us just haven't left yet." He started down the dirt road, then paused. "There's a saloon in the next settlement upriver, if you're walking. Muddy floor, honest whiskey. Tell them Silas sent you — they'll water it either way, but they'll feel bad about it."

And he trudged off into the trees, whistling something tuneless, leaving Theo standing in the middle of the road with a surveyor's eye, a dead career, and the sudden, ridiculous, unmistakable feeling of a door opening — walking, one boot at a time, into the escarpment country.
