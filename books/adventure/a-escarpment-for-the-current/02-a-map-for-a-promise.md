## 2. A Map for a Promise

The saloon in Kettle Falls was called the Tin Lantern, though no one could say why — it had neither a tin roof nor, as far as Theo could determine on his first squinting survey of the place, a working lantern. It had a floor that had given up any pretense of being level, a bar polished by forty years of forearms and elbows, and a smell that was equal parts woodsmoke, spilled beer, and wet dog. There were two dogs in evidence at the moment, both asleep under the stove, which meant the smell was running about even.

Theo Reyes stood in the doorway with river mud to his knees and let the warmth roll over him like a blanket. Outside, the last train he would ever ride on that line was already a memory — he'd watched its red marker light dwindle up the valley until it was indistinguishable from a star, and something in his chest had gone quiet and strange, the way a house goes quiet after company leaves.

He'd told himself he was only stopping for the night. A bed, a meal, a look at the timetable. In the morning he'd decide whether to go home to the city and the drafting office and the slow accumulation of small shames, or —

Or what, exactly? He didn't know yet. That was the trouble with walking away from a train in the middle of nowhere: you were trusting the middle of nowhere to have an opinion.

"Beer," he said to the man behind the bar, who was built like a rain barrel and had the expression of someone who'd heard every possible request and been impressed by none of them.

"Beer's flat."

"I've had a long day."

"Flat it is." The barrel poured with the gravity of a man administering medicine. "You off the last train?"

"The last train."

"Huh." He said it the way other men said *condolences*. "Name's Odum. You'll want the room at the end; the middle one has a roof problem when it rains."

"It's not raining."

"It will be."

Theo took his beer to a corner table near the stove, because a man who has spent twenty years reading landscapes learns to trust the warm spot. From there he could see the whole room: two trappers playing cards with the joyless concentration of men doing arithmetic, a freight handler asleep sitting up, and —

And, in the far corner, an old man who did not belong to the room at all.

He was folded into a chair like a coat someone had dropped, wearing a canvas coat gone the color of the river and boots that had been resoled past all decency. His beard was white and uncombed and held, Theo noted with some professional interest, at least three small twigs, as though the man had recently been dragged through brush and hadn't bothered to file a report about it. His skin had the gray-yellow shine of fever. In front of him on the table sat a tin cup, untouched, and a leather packet that he kept one cracked hand resting on, the way a farmer rests a hand on a fence post — not holding it so much as confirming it was there.

The old man caught him looking. Instead of looking away, as most people would have, he looked back, steadily, with eyes that were still bright under all that ruin, and beckoned.

Theo hesitated. Then, because the alternative was drinking flat beer alone with his thoughts, and his thoughts had been poor company since the waystation, he crossed the room.

"You're the railroad man," the old man said. His voice was gravel rolled in honey, worn smooth but deep. "Off the last train. I can always tell. You people walk like the ground owes you money."

"That's fair," Theo said. "I'm not with the railroad anymore. As of about four hours ago, I'm not sure what I'm with."

"Nobody is. Some of us just admit it sooner." He pushed out the opposite chair with his boot. "Sit down, then. You've got an honest face, which in this country is either a virtue or a liability, and I haven't got time left to find out which."

*Time left.* Most men said it carelessly. This man said it the way a surveyor says *last benchmark* — precisely, and meaning it.

"My name's Crane," the old man said, when Theo had sat. "Silas Crane. And before you ask, no, I haven't got anything worth stealing except this—" he patted the leather packet "—and I intend to give it away tonight, so if you're a thief, you're too late and out of luck."

"I'm not a thief."

"Shame. Thieves are easier to negotiate with." He coughed then, a deep wet sound that went on long enough that the card players glanced over and the dogs stirred under the stove. When it passed, Silas Crane sat back looking smaller, and grinned at Theo with surprising mischief. "Don't make that face. I've been dying for six weeks. I've gotten quite good at it. The trick is to keep your sense of humor; everything else goes anyway."

"What does the doctor say?"

"The doctor says I should go to the city." Silas snorted. "The doctor also charges a dollar a visit and once prescribed me whiskey for a toothache, so between the two of us, I trust my own diagnosis. My lungs are full of river silt and sixty years of campfire smoke, and they've decided to close for business. I've surveyed enough in my day to know a condemned structure when I'm living in one."

Despite himself, Theo felt the corner of his mouth move. "What did you survey?"

"Everything." Silas waved a hand that took in the walls, the escarpment beyond them, the whole dark shoulder of the country. "Trails, timber, mineral ground. Mostly the upper river. Forty years of the upper river." His hand came down and settled again on the leather packet, and the mischief drained out of his face, leaving something older and more careful behind. "Which brings me to you, railroad man. What's your name?"

"Reyes. Theo Reyes."

"And what do you do, Theo Reyes, when you're not abandoning trains?"

The question landed harder than it should have. Theo turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "I'm an engineer. Surveyor, mostly. Route work."

"Route work." Silas repeated it slowly, tasting it. "Meaning you find where the lines go."

"Meaning I find where the lines go."

"And where they stop going." The old man's bright eyes didn't blink. "I've watched your kind work these thirty years. First the surveys come through, all brass instruments and good manners. Then the graders. Then the trains. And then, ten years later, more surveys — quiet ones — and the trains stop, and the towns dry up, and everybody acts surprised, like the weather done it." He leaned forward. "Was it you? Did you draw the line that killed Kettle Falls?"

Theo thought about lying. He was surprised how briefly. "The Ashford cutoff," he said quietly. "Six years ago. I ran the preliminary survey myself. They told me it was a realignment. By the time I understood it was a replacement — that the new line made this branch worthless — the drawings were signed and filed." He looked into his cup. "So yes. In a manner of speaking, I drew the line that's killing this town. I rode the funeral train today."

Silas studied him for a long moment, and whatever he was looking for, he seemed to find it, because he nodded slowly and sat back.

"Well," he said. "Honesty. That's rarer than gold in this country, and worth about the same, most days." He picked up the leather packet, weighed it in his palm, and set it on the table between them. "But today it might be worth considerably more."

* * *

The packet was tied with a length of waxed cord, knotted and reknotted many times, and it was stained — grease, old and dark, soaked through the leather in a spreading bloom. Silas worked the knots with fingers that shook, and Theo found himself wanting to help and knowing better than to offer.

"Thirty years ago," Silas said, working, "I found something in the upper river country. Under the escarpment, where nobody looks because the official maps say there's nothing there to look at." The last knot gave. "Official maps are drawn by men, railroad man. Men get paid. Remember that."

He opened the packet and drew out a sheet of paper — heavy stock, folded into eighths, soft as cloth from handling. Even across the table Theo could see it was covered edge to edge in fine pencil work: contour lines, watercourses, bearings, elevations, and marginal notes in a cramped, confident hand. Grease stains overlaid parts of it like continents on a globe.

"This is—"

"A map," Silas said. "Of the upper river. Of the source. Of what's under the source." He smoothed it flat with both palms, tenderly, the way a man smooths a child's hair. "There's a vein up there, Reyes. Copper, and copper's cousin silver, running in limestone like a ribbon through a cake. Rich enough that when the concession men finally understood what their own ground was worth, they couldn't believe their luck — and then couldn't believe their maps, because their maps said the vein wasn't there. So they fixed the maps instead of believing them."

Theo went very still. Falsified surveys were the kind of thing you heard about over drinks and never quite believed, the way you heard about ghosts. But he had spent his career watching institutions behave badly in small ways, and small ways, he knew, were just large ways seen from farther off.

"They falsified the survey," he said.

"They falsified *my* survey." Silas's voice stayed level, but his hand flattened harder on the paper. "I was hired to run the boundary work twenty years back. I did it honest. When I saw what they'd done with my numbers — moved the boundaries, buried the vein under land the permits don't cover, and blamed the discrepancy on me — I raised hell. Nobody hires a hell-raiser twice. I lost the work, the license, the name. My girl—" he stopped, and something moved behind his face like a fish under ice. "My girl stopped writing around then, too. Can't blame her. Hard to explain to a daughter why her father keeps fighting a war nobody else can see."

The fire ticked in the stove. One of the dogs dreamed audibly.

"So the map," Theo said carefully. "It shows the true boundaries. The real survey."

"It shows everything. Where the vein runs, where the source rises, where the markers *should* be versus where the company's liars put them. It's the only copy. The original field book went missing years ago — I have my suspicions where, and they wear good suits." Silas looked up, and the fever-brightness in his eyes was suddenly fierce. "The concession permits expire in thirty days. Renewal hearing at the county seat. After that, if nothing's filed, they log the source country clear to the waterline and plow the evidence under with the stumps. Thirty days, Reyes. That's the whole window God's giving us."

"*Us,*" Theo repeated.

"I'm getting to that." Silas drew a breath that whistled. "I meant to carry it myself. Then I meant to hire a man. Then I met three men in this very room who'd sell their mothers for a drink, and I revised my ambitions." He slid the map across the table until it rested under Theo's hand. "Carry it to my daughter. Wren Crane. She lives upriver — headwaters country, past the gorge, near where the spring comes out of the limestone. She'll be hard to find and harder to convince, but show her this, in my hand, and she'll know it's true, because she'll hate how well she knows the handwriting."

"Why me?" Theo asked. "You've known me ten minutes. I could be one of the men you'd sell your mother to avoid."

"You could." Silas grinned, terrible and warm. "But you rode the last train out of a town you helped kill, and you walked into the rain instead of riding home, and you sat down when a dying stranger crooked his finger, and you admitted the worst thing you ever did inside of five minutes of meeting him. That's either an honest man or a very good actor, and actors don't walk into the rain." He tapped the map once, under Theo's fingers. "Besides. You're a surveyor. You'll read it right. Half the promise is carrying it; the other half is understanding what you carried."

Theo looked down at the map. Up close it was beautiful — decades of patient observation pressed into pencil, corrections layered over corrections, the handwriting of a man thinking on paper. And there, in the lower margin, a note in different ink, added later: *Wren — if I'm gone, follow the water up. All the way. Don't stop where it's easy.*

His chest did something complicated.

"Silas," he said slowly, "I'm not the man for this. I'm a washed-up route engineer with a bad reputation among people whose bad opinion ought to be a compliment, and I came into this saloon tonight without a plan further than breakfast. I can't promise you—"

"You can," Silas said. "That's the thing about promises, Reyes. Nobody ever feels qualified. If you waited till you felt qualified, nothing would ever get promised, and nothing would ever get done." He put his hand flat on the table beside the map, palm up, an old-fashioned gesture from an older time. "Swear it. On whatever you've got left that you believe in. Mine's nearly spent, so I'll take yours even if it's small."

The card game had ended; the trappers were watching now, along with Odum behind the bar, and the sleeping freight handler had become a waking one. Theo was aware of all of it, and aware, more sharply, of the map under his hand — of the weight of a dying man's thirty years pressing up through grease-stained paper into his palm.

He thought about the waystation. About standing on the platform watching the red light shrink to a star, feeling the specific grief of a man whose life's work is a list of things that no longer exist. He had come into the Tin Lantern hoping the middle of nowhere would have an opinion.

Apparently it did.

"On my name," Theo said. "On what's left of it. I'll carry it to Wren Crane, at the source, before the permits expire. Thirty days."

Silas closed his eyes. For a moment Theo thought the effort had cost him consciousness. Then the old man exhaled, long and shaking, and when he opened his eyes they were wet and bright and laughing all at once.

"Well," he said. "Well. Thirty years, and it turns out all I needed was one honest fool on the last train." He began folding the map, guiding Theo's hands through the creases — "along the old folds, boy, it remembers" — and pushing it back into the leather packet, and pushing the packet across the table. "Now listen. Voss—"

"Voss?"

"There's a man named Voss. Works for the concession. Good suit, better manners, and eyes like a fish. If he offers you money for this packet — and he will, he's thorough — the answer's no, and then you leave town before he stops offering and starts arranging." Silas gripped Theo's wrist with a strength that startled him. "Promise me that part too. No heroics. Deliver it. Dead heroes deliver nothing."

"I promise."

"Good." The grip loosened. The strength went out of Silas Crane all at once, like water finding a crack, and he sagged back into his chair, grinning at the ceiling. "Ha. Look at that. Lighter already. Sixty pounds of map lighter." He waved vaguely at Odum. "Barkeep! Whiskey for my friend here. He's just taken on a job that'll age him forty years, and he should start the aging process comfortable."

* * *

Silas Crane died a little after four in the morning.

Theo had taken the end room, the one without the roof problem, and slept the sleep of a man who has made a decision and doesn't yet have to live in it. He woke once, near three, to the sound of coughing through the wall — deep, rhythmic, stubborn, like surf. He lay listening to it and thought absurdly: *he sounds like the river will.* Then he slept again.

Odum knocked at gray dawn. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to; his face had already held the meeting and voted.

Silas lay in the back room on a cot, hands folded, face finally at peace and finally, Theo thought, looking his age — which was impossible to guess, somewhere between sixty and the beginning of time. The twigs were still in his beard. Someone had lit a candle at the head of the cot, though the window was already giving light, and the flame burned the way flames do in daylight: barely there, but insisting.

"He paid for the week in advance," Odum said. "Month ago. Said he'd either need it or he wouldn't, and either way the whiskey was covered." The barkeep rubbed his jaw. "First time anybody's pre-paid their own wake. We'll give him a decent send-off. Trappers'll want to. He mapped half their traplines for free over the years, the old fool."

Theo stood at the foot of the cot for a while. He was not a praying man — instruments and arithmetic had sanded most of that off him — but he found himself doing sums instead, which was its own kind of prayer. Thirty days. Upriver through country the map marked with warnings. A daughter who might shoot first, given the family history of disappointment. A man named Voss with fish eyes and a company checkbook.

"Mr. Odum," he said. "Is there an outfitter in town? Somebody who sells canoes and doesn't ask questions?"

"Questions cost extra," Odum said. "Hark's your man, down by the landing. Tell him I sent you, and tell him—" he glanced at the cot, and his voice roughened slightly, "—tell him Silas finally found somebody to take it upriver. He'll know what it means. Half this town's been listening to that old man talk about the source for thirty years. We figured it was like his teeth. Something he used to have."

Outside, the promised rain had arrived, fine and steady, beading on the tin sign above the door. Theo stood under the eave with the leather packet inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it with every breath — a small greasy heartbeat of obligation.

Thirty days. One dead prospector. One estranged daughter. One river he'd never seen, running toward a truth powerful men had already killed paper to bury.

He turned up his collar and stepped into the rain, heading for the landing, and the map rode against his ribs like a second pulse.

Behind him, in the Tin Lantern, the candle at Silas Crane's head went on burning in broad daylight — barely there, but insisting.
