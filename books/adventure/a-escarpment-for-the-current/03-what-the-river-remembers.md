## 3. What the River Remembers

Theo Reyes had a rule about maps, learned the hard way over nineteen years of making them: never read one in bed. Maps were like old letters that way. You'd tell yourself you were only glancing, and then it was three in the morning and you knew every contour of a place you'd sworn to forget.

He broke the rule anyway.

The room above the waystation saloon had a bed that sagged in the middle like a tired mule and a window that looked out on the rail yard, where the last rolling stock sat cooling under a sky going the color of dishwater. He'd propped the lamp on the washstand, spread Silas Crane's map flat beside it, weighted the corners with his watch, a tin of tobacco, a boot, and a coffee cup he would later drink out of without thinking about what else had touched it.

The map itself was grease-stained, folded so many times along different axes that it opened like a flower — or like something trying to escape. The river ran down its length in blue pencil, confident and clean, from the escarpment country at the top to the lowlands at the bottom. Around it clustered annotations in a cramped, slanted hand: *shallow gravel bar, portage left, 300 yds.* *Bees. Leave them be.* *Water good below falls, bad above.* And once, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn through: *DO NOT TELL THE COMPANY.*

Theo turned the lamp wick up and bent closer.

It wasn't the river that held him. It was the margins. Every surveyor develops tics — little private habits of hand that show up whether they mean them to or not. Theo himself dotted his *i*'s slightly left of center and always drew north arrows with a flourish on the tail, a habit he'd caught from someone years ago and never shaken. He knew other men's tics the way a wife knows a snore.

Silas Crane's notes were one kind of hand. But the base map — the river itself, the escarpment walls drawn in fine parallel hatching, the little triangles marking rapids, the depth soundings penciled along the channel in a neat descending column —

Theo sat back on the edge of the sagging bed.

He knew that hand.

He pulled his satchel onto the bed and dug past spare shirts until he found the thing he carried the way some men carry a saint's medal: a field notebook, water-stained, rubber-banded shut, filled in the last year of Elias Marsh's life. Marsh had taught him surveying when Theo was twenty-two and all elbows and certainty. Had taught him how to read water before he trusted it, how to shoot a transit through morning fog, how to admit an error out loud before it grew teeth. Then Marsh had died — heart, alone, in a boarding house in a town whose name Theo had to look up even now — and the railroad had closed the man's last three lines inside eighteen months, efficient as a broom.

Marsh drew his rapids as triangles. Not chevrons, not squiggles, not any of the four conventional symbols. Triangles, apex upstream, because — he used to say — *the river points at what's coming for you, boy. Might as well point back.*

The map on the bed was full of triangles. Apex upstream. And there, at the bottom margin, half worn away by grease and thumbs: a north arrow with a flourish on the tail.

Theo's own flourish. Learned from this very hand.

He sat there a long while, listening to the wind worry the telegraph wires outside, feeling something turn over in his chest that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite hope but lived in the same neighborhood.

Elias Marsh had drawn this map. Or at least the bones of it. Which meant somebody had surveyed the upper river — properly surveyed it, soundings and all — years ago. And Theo had spent a career in the archives of the Northwestern & Cascade. He knew their holdings the way he knew his own scars. There was no upper-river survey in them. No file, no trace, no index card. He'd have remembered; the escarpment country was the great blank space on every map he'd ever worked from, the region the company marked *unsurveyed — no traffic warranted* in that bland bureaucratic handwriting that could erase a mountain range without raising its voice.

*No traffic warranted.*

Somebody had warranted it. Somebody had measured it to the yard. And then somebody had made it disappear.

Theo got up, poured the dregs of yesterday's coffee into the cup — the one touching the map, he realized too late, and decided the map had survived worse — and stood looking out the window at the dead rail yard. Two engines, cold. A line of empty ore cars rusting at the couplings. The escarpment itself rose beyond, blue-gray in the early light, holding its clouds like a man holding his cards.

"Second survey," he said aloud, testing the words Silas had scrawled in the margin. They didn't get any less strange for saying them.

Downstairs, the saloon smelled of sawdust, spilled beer, and the particular despair of a town that had heard the word *abandonment* applied to its railway. The barman was stacking chairs with the enthusiasm of a man digging his own grave. One customer remained: an old woman asleep sitting up, hat over her eyes, a teacup balanced on her knee with the gravity-defying serenity of long practice.

"I need supplies," Theo told the barman. "River supplies. Canoe, paddles, food for two weeks. Where does a man get those?"

The barman looked him over — the city boots, the satchel, the general air of a man who owned more fountain pens than knives — and his face performed a small tragedy of pity. "You want Hark," he said. "End of Front Street, past the church that isn't a church anymore. Tell him Pete sent you, though I can't imagine why I'd do him that favor."

* * *

Hark's outfitting post turned out to be a barn-sized building of gray weatherboards with a porch stacked high enough with crates to constitute a fortification. A sign above the door read HARK'S GOODS — OUTFITTING & SUPPLY, and beneath it, in smaller letters someone had added by hand: *NO CREDIT. NO EXCEPTIONS. DON'T ASK.*

The man behind the counter was built like the crates outside: square, scarred, and clearly capable of taking considerable weight. He had a beard that seemed to be considering becoming a second career and eyes that did their arithmetic fast.

"Pete sent me," Theo said.

"Hm." Hark said it the way a judge says *overruled*. "Pete sends me a lot of things. What are you wanting?"

"A canoe. Good one. Paddles, a pack, provisions for fourteen days, rope, a tarp, a camp stove if you've one that works."

"Fourteen days." Hark leaned both forearms on the counter, which creaked but held, much like Hark himself appeared designed to do. "Whereabouts?"

"The upper river."

The change was small but total. It was like watching a lake freeze — nothing moved, and yet everything was suddenly different. Hark's arithmetic eyes finished their sum and arrived at a number he didn't care for.

"No," he said.

"I haven't told you what I'm—"

"You're wanting the upper river. That's the whole of it, isn't it? Fourteen days' provisions only fits one direction on this earth, friend, and it's up." He straightened. "I don't sell passage to the upper river."

"It's a free country."

"It is," Hark agreed pleasantly. "And the upper river is free too. Free of law, mostly, and free of people who come back down it. That concession company owns everything above the falls on paper, and owns most of what's below it off paper. They don't care for visitors. Last fella went up asking questions came back floating, and the coroner wrote *drowning* on him even though he was found in six inches of water with his boots dry." Hark shrugged, a geological event. "I sell goods. I don't sell funerals."

Theo set Silas's map on the counter between them.

He hadn't planned to. Later he'd chew on that — on the fact that some part of him had already decided to trust this grumpy wall of a man, or perhaps had simply wanted, badly, to show the map to someone who might know what it meant. The grease stains caught the lamplight from the window. Hark looked down at it and did not touch it, which Theo noted. Men who handled contraband daily developed careful hands.

"That's Marsh's work," Hark said quietly.

"You know Elias Marsh?"

"Everybody on this river knew Eli Marsh. Best surveyor these hills ever swallowed." Something moved behind the beard that might have been grief wearing work clothes. "They say he went soft at the end. Started talking about a vein upriver big enough to buy the territory twice over. Company called him a drunk. Called his survey fraudulent." Hark's eyes came up. "Eli Marsh never drew a fraudulent line in his life. He'd re-shoot a bearing ten times rather than let one crooked number live."

"They erased his survey."

"They erased *him*, first chance they got, and the survey after." Hark pushed the map gently back across the counter, not quite touching it, the way a man returns a hot iron. "Now. Here's my problem. You walk out of here, you go find another outfitter — and there's Kessler down in Millbrook who'll sell a canoe to a corpse if the corpse has cash — and you go up that river and get yourself killed, and that's your business. But if I sell to you, I'm in the ledger. Voss's men read ledgers."

"Voss?"

Hark's face did something complicated. "Forget I said that name."

"Too late."

"Forget it faster, then." Hark rubbed his jaw, beard rasping under his palm like sandpaper on oak. He looked at the map again. At Theo. At the window, where the street lay empty in the flat gray light. When he spoke again it was lower, and slower, and Theo understood he was hearing the truth arrive one reluctant piece at a time.

"Eli Marsh had a partner, near the end. Old prospector, half mad, half genius. Crane, his name was. Silas Crane." He tapped one thick finger an inch above the map, not touching. "When the company burned Eli's name, Crane kept the only copy of the true survey. Hid it somewhere upriver, they say. Twenty years the company's been looking. Twenty years, and men have gone up that river looking *for* them, and come back changed or come back floated." He fixed Theo with the arithmetic eyes. "So. Which are you? A buyer for the company, or a fool?"

Theo thought about lying. He was surprised to find he'd already decided against it before the thought finished forming.

"A fool," he said. "A dying man gave me this map last night and made me promise to carry it to his daughter at the river's source. He died this morning. I never met the daughter. I don't know what's at the source. All I know is the handwriting on this map belongs to the best man who ever taught me anything, and the company says he never existed." He paused. "Also I paid for a train ticket home that I'm now going to waste, which my accountant side considers the most damning evidence of foolishness yet."

Hark stared at him for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut — and it transformed his whole face into something younger and more dangerous.

"Well," he said. "That's the stupidest reason I ever heard for going upriver." He stuck out a hand like a boat cleat. "Which means it's probably the honest one. Hark Nilsen. Come pick your canoe."

* * *

An hour later Theo stood on the muddy bank behind the post, watching Hark load a sixteen-foot cedar canvas canoe with the tenderness of a man dressing a child for winter.

"She's called the *Marianne*," Hark said. "Built her myself, and she's forgiven worse paddlers than you'll ever be. Knees bent, weight low, and when the water talks to you, *listen* — don't argue. Arguing with water is how rivers make widows."

"You're a poet."

"I'm a widower," Hark said, without ceremony, and lashed down the last pack. "Provisions are in the green bag — flour, beans, salt pork, tea, and a sack of dried apples my late wife used to make, God rest her, sweeter than anything deserves to be. Eat them slow. They're the last of her." He straightened and pointed one blunt finger upriver, toward the escarpment, where the clouds had begun to tear open and show hard blue sky above the rock. "First day is easy water. Second day you'll hit the gravel shallows — your map will know them. Third day, the timber jam below the first gorge. After that—" He hesitated, and for the first time the arithmetic eyes looked uncertain, like a sum that wouldn't resolve. "After that you're in country I sold goods into once, twenty years ago, and never saw a customer return from. There are men up there who answer to no permit and no preacher. If you see smoke that isn't yours, you put water between you and it. If you see fresh-cut stakes — company stakes, orange-painted — you look at them long enough to remember, and no longer."

"Why?"

"Because the men who cut them count how long you look." Hark handed him a paddle, then, after a visible internal struggle, a second smaller parcel wrapped in oilcloth. "Biscuits and a letter. If you happen to pass a cabin on the east bank below the second falls — there's a woman lives rough up there, traps the creek mouths. Crane's girl, if the blood holds true. Her father sent money through me for years and she sent every cent of it back unopened, and I carried both directions like a damn fool post office because I liked them both and hated neither, which in this country passes for diplomacy." His jaw worked. "If you see her — and I'm saying *if*, mind, she's shot at better-intentioned visitors than you — you tell her nothing except that her father died owing nobody. She'll want to know that. She's wanted to know it for twenty years without knowing she wants it."

Theo took the parcel. "Why give me this and not send it yourself?"

"Because I'm a coward with good intentions, and the river eats cowards slower than it eats heroes, so I've lasted." Hark shrugged his geological shrug. "Besides. You're the one carrying a dead man's promise. One more errand won't sink you. Probably."

Theo pushed the *Marianne* into the current and climbed aboard, knees bent, weight low. The canoe took the water with a grace that surprised him, swinging its nose upstream as if it had opinions about destination.

"One more thing," Hark called from the bank, hands cupped around his mouth. "The man who asked me about you this morning — well-dressed fellow, gloves, smile like a bill of sale — he'll ask again. When he does, I'll have forgotten your face entirely. That's the only help I can afford you."

"What was his name?"

"He gave it as Voss." Hark's face darkened. "But names are cheap on this river. It's what a man measures that tells you who he is."

Theo raised a paddle in salute and let the current take him. Behind him, Hark's post shrank to a gray smudge among the alders, and ahead the river ran silver and quick beneath walls of green-shadowed stone, climbing toward the escarpment and whatever waited there — a hidden survey, a stranger daughter, a vein of truth twenty years buried.

At the first bend Theo glanced back once, an instinct he'd never trained out of himself, the habit of a man who had spent his life leaving places.

On the far bank, half-hidden in the alders, a horse stood saddled and riderless.

Or rather — he corrected himself, squinting — riderless was the wrong word. The rider was there. He was simply standing very still beside his horse, in a gray coat and pale gloves, watching the canoe go by with the patient attention of a man checking figures in a ledger.

Watching him. Making a note.

Theo bent to his paddle and did not look back again.
