## 4. Men Who Buy Silence

The morning after Silas Crane died, Theo did what he always did when the world handed him something too heavy to carry standing up: he sat down and ate breakfast.

He took a table at the Kettle & Crumb, the only establishment in Millbrook that could charitably be called a restaurant and only if you were feeling generous about the word *establishment*. The coffee tasted like it had been brewed from old railroad ties, which Theo suspected was more or less true. The eggs came gray. The toast came burnt on one side and raw on the other, an achievement of engineering so improbable he almost admired it.

Silas's map lay in his coat pocket, folded into its oilskin, pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

He'd spent half the night telling himself he'd done his duty. He'd stayed with the old man until the end. He'd closed Silas's eyes and seen to it that Doc Prewitt signed the certificate — *exposure, fever, exhaustion of the heart* — and paid for a plot in the churchyard with money he didn't have. As far as Theo could see, the promise had been fulfilled in every way that mattered to a reasonable man.

Except the map was still in his pocket, and he hadn't given it away, burned it, or mailed it anywhere, and some part of him — the part that had once loved drawing lines across empty country before he'd learned what those lines were used for — wanted very much to know where it went.

*Upriver,* Silas had rasped, gripping Theo's wrist with a strength that seemed borrowed from somewhere beyond the fever. *To my girl. Wren. At the source. You swear it, engineer. Swear it on your iron.*

Nobody swore on iron anymore. But Theo had sworn, because dying men deserve their ceremonies, and now the words sat in his chest like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

"You want more coffee?" asked the girl behind the counter, whose name tag said DOTTY and whose expression suggested she had already decided against liking him.

"No," Theo said. "Yes. Both."

Dotty poured without comment. That was the thing about mill towns and rail towns — they didn't waste words, and they respected a man who knew what he wanted even when what he wanted was contradictory.

Theo was halfway through his second cup of tie-water when the stranger walked in.

You could tell he wasn't from Millbrook the way you can tell a heron isn't a crow. Everything about him was longer and finer and better fed. His suit was gray wool cut close at the waist, his boots were polished to a wet shine, and his hat had never once been rained on. He paused in the doorway just long enough to let the room notice him, then crossed to Theo's table as though he'd been walking toward it all his life.

"Mr. Reyes." It wasn't a question. "May I?"

"Depends who's asking."

"Voss." The man sat anyway, uninvited, arranging himself on the chair opposite with the ease of someone accustomed to chairs being arranged for him. "Aldous Voss. I represent the Meridian Concession Company."

Theo set down his cup. "Congratulations to them."

Voss smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like everything else about him. "I'll come directly to the point, since I understand you're a man of schedules. You were acquainted with Silas Crane."

"I buried him this morning."

"Yes. My condolences." Voss's condolences sounded like they'd been ordered from a catalog. "Mr. Crane was, prior to his death, in possession of certain documents belonging to my company. Survey materials. Stolen property, Mr. Reyes. We've been looking for them for some years."

"Have you."

"We have." Voss leaned forward slightly, and his voice dropped into a register meant for confidential transactions — the voice men use in back rooms and bank offices, warm as a stove and about as honest. "I'm told he passed something to you last night. A map, perhaps."

Theo looked at him for a long moment. Outside the window, a dray horse clopped past hauling lumber, and somewhere down the street a hammer rang on iron, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a town going about its business while two men discussed things that made the business of towns look simple.

"Who told you that?" Theo asked.

"A good question." Voss acknowledged it with a tilt of his head, conceding the point the way a chess player concedes a pawn — gladly, because he'd already seen ten moves past it. "Let's say the information found me. What matters is whether it's accurate. And here's what matters beyond that." He produced a leather folio, opened it, and slid a paper across the table. "Three thousand dollars. For the document, no questions, no records, no hard feelings. You walk away richer than you arrived, and Mr. Crane's unfortunate episode ends where it should have ended years ago."

Three thousand dollars. Theo's annual salary, twice over. Enough to pay off the debts he'd run up burying Silas, enough to buy a ticket home, enough to stop hearing the sound of rails being pulled up behind him every time he closed his eyes.

He looked at the paper for a long time. Then he looked at Voss.

"Mr. Voss," he said, "if the map is stolen company property, why not simply report the theft? Get a warrant. Send the law after it. Why pay three thousand dollars to a stranger over eggs?"

Something flickered behind Voss's eyes — quick, there and gone, like a fish turning under water. "Because litigation is slow, and my employers are not patient men."

"And because stolen survey materials aren't worth three thousand dollars unless they show something the company doesn't want shown."

The flicker again. Longer this time. When Voss smiled again, it took a fraction of a second too long to arrive. "Mr. Reyes. You're an engineer. A professional man. You understand how these things work. Documents get misplaced. Boundaries get redrawn. It's the nature of the industry. There's no villainy in it — just tidiness. My employers are offering you the opportunity to help everyone tidy up. Including yourself."

"Tidiness." Theo turned the word over. He thought of lines erased from ledgers. Of routes surveyed and struck from record. Of a career built, brick by careful brick, on making things disappear. He knew exactly what tidiness looked like. He'd been paid to produce it for eleven years.

He pushed the paper back across the table.

"I made a promise," he said.

"To a dead prospector." Voss said it gently, almost kindly, the way one might point out that a bridge was out. "A feverish old man you met last night in a saloon. Mr. Reyes, I say this with genuine respect: promises to dead men are the cheapest kind. They cost nothing to keep and nothing to break, and nobody is left to know the difference. Whereas three thousand dollars—" he tapped the paper, "—is very much alive."

"The map goes to his daughter."

"Ah." Voss sat back. Something in his posture changed — not softer, but more attentive, the way a cat becomes attentive. "And where does one find the daughter?"

"That," Theo said, "sounds like a question you'd pay for too."

For a moment neither man moved. Then Voss laughed — a real laugh, brief and genuine, and somehow that was worse than the smile. "You're not what I expected, Mr. Reyes. I was told you were a broken man. Discarded by your own railroad. I assumed you'd take the money." He stood, buttoned his coat, and retrieved the unsigned paper, folding it away with neat, economical movements. "Keep the map, if you like. Keep the promise. But do consider: men who carry things are responsible for what happens around the carrying. That's not a threat. It's arithmetic."

He put a coin on the table — more than enough for both breakfasts — touched his hat brim, and walked out into the gray morning, unhurried, a man with nowhere in particular to be and all day to be nowhere.

Theo discovered his hands weren't entirely steady, and took another sip of coffee to cover it.

"Well," said Dotty, appearing with the pot. "That fella's been sitting in his motorcar outside since six this morning. First time he's come in, though." She refilled Theo's cup. "You in trouble?"

"No," Theo said. Then, honestly: "Not yet."

* * *

He spent the rest of the day learning what arithmetic felt like.

It started small. He went to the general store for rope and matches, and when he came out, a man in a brown coat was reading a newspaper across the street — the same page, Theo would have sworn, that he'd been reading an hour earlier outside the hotel. Theo tested it the way any cautious man tests a suspicion: he doubled back through the alley behind the feed store, waited, watched. Brown Coat ambled past the alley mouth ninety seconds later, newspaper still in hand, not reading it at all now, just holding it, the way a man holds a prop.

Then there was the second one. This fellow was better at it — a lean, sunburnt type in range clothes, leaning against the livery fence whittling something, who managed to be within sight of Theo everywhere Theo went without ever quite looking at him. Whittling Man was a professional. Brown Coat was not. Together they made a team that was thorough but not invisible, and Theo understood that they weren't trying to be invisible. They were trying to be *seen*. Just enough. Just enough to remind him of the arithmetic.

By late afternoon he'd confirmed it a third way: his hotel room had been entered. Nothing taken, nothing left, but the oilskin packet in his bag had been re-wrapped with the fold facing the wrong direction, and Theo was an engineer — he noticed folds. They'd searched while he ate lunch. Which meant they hadn't found what they were looking for, because what they were looking for wasn't in the bag.

It was in his coat. It had been in his coat all day, riding against his ribs, while he drank coffee and bought rope and let two hired men follow him around a town of four hundred people like a parade of one.

He sat on the hotel bed in the failing light and allowed himself a short, bitter laugh. Eleven years of mapping country for men in offices, and he'd never once thought about what it was to be *mapped* — tracked, noted, plotted on somebody else's chart. It was instructive. He resolved to remember the feeling the next time he drew a line through a valley where people lived.

So. Options.

Option one: give Voss the map. Take the money. Go home. The promise would sit in his chest forever next to all the other swallowed things, another line erased, and he'd die someday with a chest full of erased lines and nothing written on him at all.

Option two: stay in Millbrook and wait for Voss to escalate. Arithmetic, Voss had called it. Men who carry things are responsible for what happens around the carrying. Sooner or later the carrying would become inconvenient, and inconvenient carriers had ways of having accidents on stairs and under horses.

Option three.

Theo laid the map out on the bed and studied it by lamplight. Upriver. Thirty days before the concession permits expired — Silas had said so, gasping it like a countdown. Somewhere at the head of that blue inked line, a daughter named Wren. Somewhere along it, whatever it was that made a company willing to pay three thousand dollars and hire watchers and burn — no, he didn't know about burning yet, not then; that knowledge was still waiting for him upstream like a door waiting to be opened.

The map's annotations ran along the margins in a cramped, confident hand — depths, hazards, portage lengths, notes in a shorthand Theo half recognized. *Gravel bar shifts after rain — keep right.* *Fallen spruce, portage 200 yds, left side.* Whoever drew this had run the river recently and often. And beneath the practical notes, smaller, fainter, almost hidden: *2nd survey — see cabin.*

Second survey. See cabin.

Theo sat with that a long while. Then he packed his bag, deliberately, loudly, leaving the lamp burning, and went down to the lobby and paid for another night in cash, visibly, letting the desk clerk see him do it. Let them watch the hotel tonight. Let Brown Coat and Whittling Man drink their coffee and hold their newspapers.

At four in the morning, in the dark, he went out through the washroom window.

* * *

The livery stable smelled of hay and horse and honest work, which after the last twenty-four hours felt like a bath. Theo had arranged everything the evening before with the stable boy — a sharp-eyed, gap-toothed kid named Ferb who took Theo's extra five dollars with the gravity of a bank president and asked no questions, mostly, Theo suspected, because questions interrupted counting.

"They're still on your hotel," Ferb reported, delighted, as he saddled the mule Theo had hired. "One of 'em fell asleep in the chair by the desk around midnight. Old man Petersen's charging him for the room anyway. Says sleeping customers count."

"Sound policy." Theo checked the girth, checked the pack, and checked the oilskin packet one more time — coat pocket, right side, against the ribs. "Ferb. If a well-dressed gentleman comes asking which way I went—"

"I never saw you." Ferb grinned. "Everybody knows I never see anything. It's why everybody tells me everything."

Theo swung up onto the mule, who expressed an opinion about early mornings by pinning her ears, and rode out through the dark streets of Millbrook with the stars still out and the east not yet thinking about getting up. Behind him the town slept on top of its secrets, and somewhere in it two hired men guarded an empty hotel room, and somewhere above it all Aldous Voss was presumably asleep in a fine bed, dreaming tidy dreams.

The trail left town heading north along the creek bottom, and where it forked — one branch climbing toward the wagon road, the other dropping toward the river flats — Theo took the river.

The map said the water was the fast way and the quiet way, and the map, so far, had been right about everything it promised to be right about. He could hear the current in the dark, a low continuous talking, like the country murmuring to itself. In a few hours the outfitter Hark would open his shop downriver and find a note under his door requesting a canoe, provisions, and discretion, and would either sell them to Theo or laugh him into the street, and either way Theo would deal with it when the sun came up.

For now there was just the trail, the mule's steady plodding, the cold coming off the water, and the map riding warm against his ribs.

Behind him — far behind, back at the edge of town, where the road bent past the last lamppost — a horse nickered, and another answered it. Two horses. Riders saddling in the dark, moving with purpose.

They'd found the window. Or the desk clerk had sold him cheap. It didn't matter which. What mattered was the sound of hoofbeats starting, faint but unmistakable, falling into rhythm behind him like a second pulse.

Theo clicked his tongue and the mule broke into a trot, and the river flats opened ahead of him, silver-gray under the first light, wide and cold and full of running water that remembered everything.

"Thirty days," he said aloud, to nobody, to the mule, to a dead man's promise. "Hold on, Wren Crane. Your father's map is coming."

Behind him, the riders came on.
