## 5. Upstream Against Everything

The river did not want him there.

Theo understood this within the first hour, when the canoe he'd bought from Hark — a cedar-strip beauty the outfitter had called "sound as a church" and priced like a cathedral — grounded itself on a gravel bar with a grinding shudder that traveled up the paddle and into his teeth.

"Right," Theo said to the river, to the canoe, to nobody. "We'll do it your way."

He climbed out into water that came up over his boot tops and cold enough to make him gasp, hauled the canoe a hundred yards over stones worn round as loaves of bread, and relaunched her into deeper water. The current took hold then, gentle for the moment, and carried him north beneath walls of spruce that leaned out over the banks like old men conferring over a fence.

Behind him, the waystation was already gone from view. Ahead of him, according to the grease-stained map folded in his breast pocket, lay thirty days of everything Silas Crane had ever learned about this country, written in a hand that shook but never lied.

Or so Theo hoped. He'd known maps that lied before. He'd drawn some of them himself.

* * *

The first day taught him the grammar of the river: riffle means shallow, V of smooth water means channel, standing wave means rock underneath pretending otherwise. He read the water the way he'd once read track charts, and by late afternoon he'd stopped grounding the canoe every ten minutes and only every forty.

The map helped more than it should have.

That was the thing that kept nagging at him. At the mouth of the first tributary — an unmarked creek coming in from the west — the map showed a small cross and a note in Silas's cramped hand: *Bad landing. Mud to your knees. Go 200 yds further, gravel bar on left.* Theo had laughed at that, dryly, alone in a canoe, and then ignored it, because he was tired and the mud bank looked fine.

It was not fine. It was mud to his knees, exactly as advertised, and when he finally wrenched his boots free with a sound like a kiss being taken back, he stood calf-deep in muck and felt something turn over in his chest that wasn't quite laughter and wasn't quite grief.

"All right, Silas," he said. "You win."

Back on the water, he dug out the map and studied it properly for the first time since leaving Hark's landing. It wasn't just a route. It was a conversation. Every hazard had its annotation, every annotation its opinion. *Sweepers both sides after the bend — hug the RIGHT even if it looks wrong. It looks wrong. Do it anyway.* And further down: *Gravel shifts here every spring. If the bar's moved, read the current, not my marks. The river doesn't keep promises. I do.*

Theo found himself grinning like a fool, alone on the water, at a dead man's jokes.

He'd spent twenty years making maps that were meant to be obeyed without question — clean lines, official stamps, no room for argument. This map was the opposite of all that. It assumed you would doubt it. It argued with you. It was a map drawn by someone who expected the reader to be smarter than the paper and loved them for trying anyway.

By the time he made camp that night on a shelf of flat stone above the water, he'd stopped thinking of Silas Crane as a feverish stranger who'd died holding his hand. He was starting to think of him as a colleague. Maybe the best one he'd ever had.

* * *

Day two brought the timber.

A flood years back — the map dated it, *the June freshet, '09, worst in living memory* — had dropped whole trees across the river in stretches, and where the water ran shallow around them, portage was the only answer. Theo learned to judge a carry by eye now: two hundred yards through alder tangle, four hundred over a mossy blowdown where every step sank six inches into rot. He learned to carry the canoe on his shoulders the way Hark had shown him, yoke balanced on the back of his neck, arms up like a man surrendering to the sky, which was roughly how it felt.

At the third portage of the day, halfway along a carry that the map had cheerfully labeled *the Long Drag — sing if it helps*, Theo set the canoe down against a birch, sat on it, and ate hardtack and smoked fish while a pair of gray jays scolded him from the branches and then, with the shameless confidence of their kind, came down and stole half his lunch anyway.

"You're welcome," he told them.

One of them perched on the gunwale and looked at him with an expression of frank assessment, and Theo thought, absurdly, of the boardroom in the city where men in good suits had signed off on abandoning the Kettle Valley spur — his survey, his recommendation, his line — in eleven minutes flat, including time for coffee. These birds had more honest business sense. They took what was offered, said what they meant with their whole bodies, and didn't pretend the taking was a favor to anyone.

He got up, shouldered the canoe, and sang, badly, because the map had told him to.

* * *

It was on the third day that the annotations began to frighten him a little.

Not the hazards themselves — he'd come to trust those the way you trust a steady hand on a rope. It was the precision. A fallen pine bridging the narrows, noted with the year it fell. A spring bubbling up on the east bank, *good water, cold enough to hurt, drink your fill*. A stretch of rapids described so exactly — *three drops, the second lies about its depth; ferry left to right between the waves or swim* — that running them felt less like adventure than like following instructions from a man standing at his shoulder.

Which, Theo realized, pulling out below the last drop with his heart going like a stamp mill, was exactly what it was.

Nobody wrote like that from memory. Nobody remembered the lie in a wave's face twenty years on. These notes had been checked and rechecked, season after season, a man coming back up this river again and again to make sure his words would keep someone alive. Silas Crane hadn't drawn a map. He'd built a handrail, plank by plank, for whoever came after him.

And then Theo turned a bend, looked ahead, and understood why.

The escarpment stood up out of the forest like the spine of the world.

He'd seen it on surveys, of course — a contour line, a shaded relief, numbers on paper. Paper had not prepared him. The wall of rock rose sheer for eight hundred feet, gray limestone streaked with the black runnels of a hundred springs, crowned with wind-bent pines that looked from below like moss on a tombstone. The river slid out from under it, calm and green and suddenly deep, as if the stone had swallowed all the noise of the rapids behind and given back silence.

Theo let the canoe drift. There was no current to fight here, no work to do, nothing required of him except to look, and looking took everything he had.

His whole career, he had drawn lines across country like this. Closure lines. *Abandon section. Remove rail. Salvage ties.* He had sat in offices a thousand miles away and erased places he'd never bothered to see, and the erasure had been easy, because on paper a place is just a place, and one line looks much like another.

There were no lines here. There was only the wall, and the water, and the smallness of one man in a cedar canoe, and the enormous indifference of it all to whether the railroad ever came back.

"Silas," he said quietly, "you old son of a gun. You lived under *this*."

The map, in his pocket, seemed to weigh more than it had that morning.

* * *

He camped that night on a gravel terrace at the base of the wall, where a fire ring of stones older than any trapper's marked a spot someone had used many times. The map confirmed it: *My camp, most nights, better than any bed I ever paid for.*

Theo built a small fire, boiled tea, and ate beans from a tin with a pocketknife, and when the light went amber and then rose-colored on the face of the escarpment above him, he sat with the map open on his knees and read it the way he'd once read letters from home — slowly, twice, hoarding it.

That was when he noticed the marginalia he'd missed.

Down in the lower corner, cramped into the space beside the border rule where a lesser man would have put nothing, Silas had written a block of text in pencil gone silver with age:

*If you're reading this, you've come further than most and you deserve the truth, which is short. The company's survey of the upper concession is a fiction. I helped draw the true one, years back, and they buried it and buried me with it. The vein runs under ground their permits were never cut to cover. There is a second survey. It still exists. It is upstream, past the wall, where the water begins, and it will prove everything — if it hasn't been burned. Trust the river. Trust the girl. Trust no man in a good coat.*

Theo read it three times. The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere upstream, in the blue dusk under the wall, a loon called once and got no answer.

*A second survey.* His mentor's name surfaced in his mind unbidden — Aldous Wren, no relation to the girl, dead these six years — and with it a memory he hadn't visited in a long time: Aldous coming back from a contract upcountry, tight-mouthed and grim, saying only, *Some jobs you take and some jobs take you,* and never speaking of it again. Theo had been young. He'd assumed money trouble. He'd assumed, the way young men assume, that it wasn't his business.

He looked at the note again. *Trust the girl.* So Silas had a daughter who knew something, or was meant to. Wren. The name on the promise Theo had sworn over a dying man's hand in a muddy saloon: *carry it to the source before the permits expire.*

Thirty days. He'd used three.

He fed the fire another stick and watched the sparks climb the dark face of the escarpment, and he thought about how a man spends twenty years drawing lines and telling himself the lines are the truth, and how it takes one dead prospector's penciled margin to show him that the truth was the thing the lines were drawn over, all along, like paint over grain.

"I'm coming," he said to the map, to the wall, to the source somewhere beyond it where the water began. "Just so we're clear."

The loon called again, closer this time, and this time something answered — not another loon. Something farther off, upriver, thin on the wind.

A whistle. Three notes, low, deliberate. The kind of signal a man makes when he wants to be heard by friends and identified by strangers.

Theo put out the fire with careful handfuls of river water until the coals hissed black, and sat in the dark under the great wall, listening, with the map sealed in oilskin against his chest and his hand resting on the hatchet Hark had thrown into the deal for free, which Theo now suspected had not been generosity.

The whistle did not come again. But the silence after it had a different texture than the silence before, and Theo Reyes, who had spent his life reading what country meant rather than what it said, pulled his blanket up under his chin and kept watch until the stars wheeled toward morning.
