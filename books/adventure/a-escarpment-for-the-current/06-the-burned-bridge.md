## 6. The Burned Bridge

The river had opinions about Theo Reyes, and none of them were flattering.

It had spent two days telling him so — shoving his canoe against rocks that the map swore weren't there, then revealing, smugly, that they were exactly where Silas Crane's cramped marginal notes said they'd be. *Rocks at the bend like a dog's teeth,* one note read. *Hug the left or lose your teeth.* Theo had hugged the left. He still checked his molars with his tongue out of habit.

By the third morning he'd developed a grudging relationship with the dead prospector's handwriting. It was like being lectured by a man who was frequently drunk but never wrong. *Portage here, unless you enjoy swimming.* He did not enjoy swimming. He portaged.

The escarpment rose on his right all morning, a wall of grey limestone wearing a green cap of pine, so tall and so continuous that it stopped feeling like scenery and started feeling like company. Theo found himself narrating to it in his head, the way he used to talk to locomotives during long night runs. *Nice wall you've got there. Very solid. I respect a thing that stays where it's put.*

He was thinking about bridges when he smelled the smoke.

Not woodsmoke from a campfire — this was older, sourer, the smell of creosote and tar and treated timber gone to char. It drifted downriver on a breeze that had no business carrying anything but pine and cold water, and Theo felt the hair on his arms lift before his brain caught up and supplied the reason.

The map said there was a bridge.

Silas had drawn it in careful profile, a trestle crossing the river at a narrow gravel flat, with a note beside it: *Old logging trestle. Rotten but standing. Road on far side runs back to Hark's — three days if you need to run home, boy.* The word *boy* had been underlined twice, which Theo chose to interpret as affectionate rather than condescending.

Three days if you need to run home.

Theo brought the canoe around the last bend, and there it was.

Or rather, there it wasn't.

* * *

What remained of the trestle stood in the river like the ribs of something enormous that had died mid-stride. Six pilings on the near side, blackened to stubs. Two more leaning drunkenly mid-channel, their tops still holding fragments of crossbeam that creaked in the current like a door in a draft. On the far bank, the road Silas had promised ran off into the trees — visible, intact, and utterly unreachable, unless Theo had recently grown wings or developed strong feelings about drowning.

The fire hadn't been old. That was the first thing he understood, standing in the shallows with water lapping at his bootlaces. Old burns weathered; the charcoal softened, moss moved in, the river ate the debris. This burn was sharp-edged. Char still flaked off the pilings in sheets. Ash lay along the gravel bar in drifts, and every step he took raised little grey ghosts that settled on his sleeves like guilt.

Somebody had burned this bridge within the last week. Maybe within the last few days.

Theo beached the canoe and stood among the wreckage for a long time, doing the arithmetic he didn't want to do.

A burned bridge cut two ways. It could mean somebody upstream didn't want visitors. Or it could mean somebody downstream didn't want him going back.

He walked the length of the ruin, hands in his pockets, cataloguing the way he'd once catalogued rail grades. The fire had started on the far side — the wind direction told him that, the way the char deepened toward the north ends of the beams. Somebody had crossed first, then torched it behind them. Deliberate. Methodical. Not a squatter's carelessness, not lightning, not a campfire gone wrong. A job.

Near the base of the third piling, half-buried in ash where it had fallen when the span collapsed, he found an iron plate the size of a dinner plate — literally, he thought, the size of his grandmother's good dinner plates, the ones with the blue willow pattern she'd never let anyone eat off. It was a survey marker plate, the kind companies drove into bedrock to anchor a boundary claim. The face of it had been scarred by fire, but the stamped brand survived in the center, crisp as the day it was struck:

**MERIDIAN CONCESSION CO. — SURVEY DIVISION**

Theo crouched over it for a long moment.

Meridian. The name Voss had said so smoothly across the saloon table, the name attached to the offer of triple his fee, the name that owned the concession slated for closure, the name on the permits expiring in thirty days. Meridian Concession Company, whose property line, according to every official map Theo had ever pulled, ended twenty miles downstream of where he now stood.

And here was their survey plate, anchored in bedrock, forty miles past their own boundary.

He sat back on his heels in the ash.

"Well," he said aloud, to the escarpment, because talking to geological formations had become a habit somewhere around day two and he saw no reason to stop. "That's interesting."

It was worse than interesting. He turned the plate over in his hands, feeling its weight, its permanence. Survey plates weren't dropped. They were driven, witnessed, recorded. Somebody from Meridian had stood on this exact gravel bar with a sledgehammer and legally, officially, marked ground the company claimed it didn't own. And then somebody — probably the same somebody — had burned the only bridge that led back here, erasing the road, erasing the access, erasing the easy way for any curious inspector to come see what Meridian had been quietly marking in the dark.

Voss wasn't chasing him for theft.

The thought arrived whole, the way bad news does, fully dressed and already holding the paperwork. Voss didn't care about Silas Crane's grease-stained map. A map was paper; paper could be argued with. What Voss cared about was what the map led to — the source, the vein, the second survey, whatever waited upstream that required a forty-mile fence made of fire and distance. Voss wasn't recovering stolen property. He was burying evidence, and Theo was carrying the shovel handle.

Theo looked upriver. The escarpment marched away north, patient and enormous, hiding whatever it hid.

Then he looked back downriver, the way he'd come: three days of water behind him, and beyond that the burned trestle's twin logic — if Meridian had cut the road back to Hark's, they'd have men watching the lower river too. Maybe watching Hark himself. The outfitter's face floated up in Theo's memory, skeptical eyebrows and all, and he felt a small cold pinch of worry for the man. Hark had sold him a canoe and a lecture. In certain company, that was a crime.

So. Retreat meant running a gauntlet with his pockets full of evidence. Retreat meant leading Voss's riders straight back to the only man in the territory who'd shown him a scrap of decency. Retreat meant arriving at the permit hearing in thirty days with nothing but a story and ash-stained cuffs.

Or.

Theo stood up, brushed off his knees, and discovered that his body had apparently held a vote without consulting him, because his feet were already pointed upriver.

"Fine," he told them. "Fine."

* * *

He camped that night on the gravel bar beneath the ruined trestle, because the alternative was paddling in the dark through water that had already tried to kill him twice, and Theo Reyes had limits. He built a small fire — small enough to cup in his palms if a rider came — and heated beans in the dented tin pot Hark had thrown into the deal for free, "because you'll starve otherwise and I don't want the ghost."

The ghost, Theo reflected, had turned out to be generous with advice from beyond the grave. He unrolled the oilskin packet and studied the map by firelight, and there was the trestle, drawn in Silas's sure hand, annotated in the margin: *Standing as of '09. Rotten. Don't trust it with a load.*

Silas had trusted it with himself, though. There was a smaller note beneath, almost an afterthought, in ink that had faded differently than the rest — added later, Theo guessed, maybe years later: *If the bridge is gone when you come, don't turn around. Gone means somebody's afraid of what's up here. Afraid men go to trouble. Trouble means you're close.*

Theo read it three times.

"You absolute old goat," he said softly. "You knew."

Of course Silas had known. A man doesn't spend thirty years prospecting a river without learning how rivers get fenced off. Silas had written instructions for a world in which the bridge was already ash, because some part of him had always known it would be. He'd handed Theo a map and a promise and a prophecy, wrapped in one grease stain, and then he'd had the poor manners to die before explaining any of it.

Theo fed a stick to the fire and watched the sparks climb.

Here was the thing he kept circling, the thing that sat in his chest like a stone he couldn't cough up: he should have turned around. Any sensible man would turn around. The sensible ledger was clear — one canoe, half-provisioned, one dead client, one living enforcer with hired riders and a company behind him, versus one promise made to a feverish stranger in a muddy saloon. The sensible column lost. It lost badly.

But Theo had spent fifteen years being sensible for institutions, and look what it had bought him: a career measured in closures. Lines surveyed, approved, abandoned. Towns shrunk to sidings and sidings shrunk to memory, each one initialed in his neat engineer's hand. He had once believed the work was neutral — that maps were maps, that steel went where the numbers said, that erasure was just another word for efficiency. He'd told himself that so many times the story had worn smooth as river stone.

Silas Crane had died clutching a piece of paper that proved somebody had worn smooth stories too — and had worn them deliberately, with a sledgehammer and a brand and a box of matches.

Theo looked at the iron plate lying by his boot, firelight moving on the stamped letters.

"Trouble means you're close," he quoted, and laughed — a short, surprised sound, alone in the dark, the kind of laugh that arrives when fear finally changes clothes and walks out the door wearing commitment's coat.

Because that was what had happened, he realized. Somewhere between the smell of the smoke and the weight of the plate, the fear had transmuted. It hadn't left — he could still feel it humming in his teeth, a fine wire of it — but it had changed jobs. An hour ago it had been the fear of a man being chased. Now it was the fear of a man committed, which was a different animal entirely: quieter, harder to spook, and much less interested in retreat.

He banked the fire, rolled into his blanket, and lay looking up at a sky crowded with stars, the escarpment a blacker black against them to the east.

"Thirty days," he murmured. "One daughter. One hearing." He paused. "One very large wall of rock that has been excellent company, thank you for asking."

Sleep came easier than it had any right to.

* * *

Dawn came up grey-gold, mist standing on the water in columns, and Theo broke camp with the economy of a man who had learned on trains that everything you own is everything you must carry. He loaded the canoe, pushed off into the current, and let the river take him north, past the leaning pilings, past the ash drifts, past the far-bank road that ran home to a place he was no longer going.

At the last moment he dug the iron plate out of his pack and set it on his knees, considering. Evidence, certainly. Weight, definitely — it must have gone four pounds, and four pounds of iron in a canoe was four pounds of argument with every rapid ahead.

But it was also proof. Physical, stampable, hold-it-in-courtroom-light proof that Meridian's surveys reached where Meridian's maps said they didn't. Voss could burn bridges. He could burn cabins, maybe — the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, and Theo filed it under *worry about later*. But he couldn't burn a branded plate that was sitting in a canoe, and a canoe, Theo resolved, was going to stay within arm's reach of him from now until the hearing, sleeping arrangements notwithstanding.

He wedged the plate under his seat and paddled on.

The river narrowed as the morning aged, quickening between walls of alder and spruce, and the escarpment leaned closer overhead, close enough now that he could see individual pines clinging to its crown like stubborn hairs. Around noon he passed the mouth of a side creek and slowed, because Silas's map showed a symbol there — a small circle with a dot, repeated twice more at intervals upriver, unexplained.

*Second survey hidden somewhere upstream,* the margin note had said, back on the first night beneath the great wall. Theo had taken it for a prospector's rambling. Now, with a branded plate riding under his seat and a burned bridge at his back, it sounded less like rambling and more like coordinates waiting for a key.

He was still puzzling over it — pencil hovering above his notebook, canoe drifting lazily sideways in the current the way canoes do exactly when you're not paying attention — when the current itself changed its mind.

It happened fast, the way rivers do. One moment the water slid green and slow around a bluff; the next it dropped, visibly dropped, the whole surface tilting toward a throat of white ahead, and the drifting canoe swung its nose into it with the enthusiasm of a dog spotting an open gate.

"Oh — no, no, hang on—"

Theo dug the paddle in. The river dug back. The map, he would think later, had probably warned him about this stretch too, and he had probably been too busy admiring an iron plate to read it, and Silas was probably saying *I told you so, boy* from wherever dead prospectors kept their armchairs.

The canoe shot toward the white water, and the last thing Theo heard clearly before the roar swallowed everything was the river, finally, saying something he agreed with:

Faster.
