## 7. Hark's Bargain

Hark Odum had run his outfitter post at the mouth of the Kettle for nineteen years, and in all that time he had developed a personal philosophy he liked to call the Three Rules of Staying Out of Trouble. Rule One: sell to everybody. Trappers, prospectors, surveyors, fishermen, the occasional fugitive — money spent the same no matter whose pocket it came out of. Rule Two: know nothing about anybody. A man who knew nothing could tell nothing, and a man who could tell nothing was worth nothing to trouble. Rule Three: when trouble found you anyway, pour it a drink and wait for it to leave.

For nineteen years the system had worked beautifully.

On the morning the two men in gray canvas walked up his dock, Hark looked at them over the rim of his coffee cup and thought, with genuine sorrow: so much for Rule Three.

They were not local men. That was plain from thirty feet off. Local men walked like the ground owed them something — loose-kneed, patient, hands already busy with some small task. These two walked like the ground had been invoiced and they'd come to collect. The tall one had a face like a hatchet that had been left out in the rain too long, all soft edges gone to rust. The short one was rounder and more cheerful-looking, which somehow made it worse. Cheerful men doing ugly work never sat right with Hark. It was like a bear wearing an apron. You kept waiting for the joke, and the joke was always on you.

"Morning," said the tall one, stopping at the counter without touching anything, which told Hark he'd been briefed. Men who didn't know a place touched everything. Men who did know a place touched nothing. "You're Odum."

"Depends who's asking and what they're asking for." Hark set down his cup. "If it's rope, I've got rope. If it's trouble, I'm fresh out till Thursday."

The cheerful one laughed. It was a good laugh, warm even, and Hark hated how much he wanted to laugh along. "Mr. Voss speaks well of you," the man said.

"Never heard of him."

"He figured you'd say that." The cheerful one produced a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it flat on the counter, smoothing it once with two fingers, the way a dealer shows a card. On it was a pencil sketch of a face Hark had sold a canoe to four days ago. Reyes, the name read underneath. Railroad man, by the look of the gear he'd bought. Bought too much of it, if Hark was honest, and paid too fast, and asked the wrong questions in the wrong order — which way does the current set above the Narrows, is there any road past the burned trestle, has anyone been upriver this season. Questions with a shape to them. Questions a man asks when he's following something instead of running from it.

Hark looked at the sketch for exactly as long as it took to decide not to look surprised, then longer, because looking unsurprised was its own kind of surprise.

"Canoe fella," he said. "Bought a sixteen-foot cedar strip off me. Paid cash. Seemed pleasant enough."

"Where'd he go?"

"Upstream, I expect. That's generally where canoes go."

The tall one leaned in. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and something medicinal, like a liniment applied to a hurt that hadn't healed yet. "Mr. Odum," he said, and the name came out of his mouth like something he'd stepped in, "let's not do the country dance. We've got a long day and you've got a business. Mr. Voss would prefer this stay friendly. Friendly is cheap. Unfriendly gets expensive, and the first thing that gets expensive is licenses."

There it was. Hark felt it settle into his chest like a stone dropped down a well — not a big stone, but deep, and taking its time hitting bottom. His trading license. Nineteen years of it. He'd renewed it every spring at the county seat, paid his fee, shaken the clerk's damp hand, and driven home feeling like a man who owned something. Now these two stood on his dock holding it up by one corner like a fish they'd caught in his own pond.

"Company license?" Hark asked mildly. "I recall it being a county license."

"Everything's a company license," the cheerful one said pleasantly, "if the company pays enough of the county's bills. And the Concession Consolidated pays a great many bills around here. Road grades. Schoolhouse shingles. The judge's stove wood." He smiled. "You'd be amazed what a cord of good oak buys."

Hark poured three cups of coffee. It was not hospitality so much as stalling, but his hands wanted something to do and coffee was what they knew. He pushed two cups across the counter. The tall one ignored his. The cheerful one drank his all at once, hot, without flinching, which told Hark the man had been raised somewhere hard and had decided to be cheerful about it as a form of revenge.

"Here's my situation," Hark said. "I sold a man a canoe and some goods. That's my trade. What he did after he walked off my dock is between him and the river."

"Sure," said the cheerful one. "And here's our situation. Either you come along and help us find him — you know this water better than any map we've got — or you don't, and next month there's a fellow from the county office out here counting your inventory against your license, and fellows like us have learned that counting goes faster when the counter's motivated." He set a second paper on the counter. This one wasn't a sketch. It was a list, written in a tidy hand, of every item in Hark's storeroom, or near enough. Somebody had been through his place while he slept, or while he sat in the saloon losing two dollars at cards, and had written it all down. The flour. The powder. The oilskins. Even the crate of dried apples nobody ever bought.

Hark looked at that list for a long moment. Then he did the thing he'd done nineteen times before at the county office: he signed himself over for another year.

"Fine," he said. "I'll pole you up as far as the Narrows. After that the water gets mean and my boat stays home."

"Fair enough," said the cheerful one, and offered his hand, and Hark shook it because refusing a handshake was a message and messages got people counted.

* * *

They left at midmorning, the three of them poling up the slack water below the rapids, and Hark spent the whole first mile learning his passengers the way he'd learned to read water — by what the surface hid. The tall one watched the banks like a hawk watches a henhouse, cataloguing every landing, every deadfall, every place a man might pull out. The cheerful one watched Hark. Not suspiciously. Almost fondly. That was the worst part. The cheerful one asked questions the way a friend asks questions — where's the best camp above the falls, do the moose come down to drink in September, is it true about the whirlpool under the escarpment — and every answer Hark gave went into some ledger behind those smiling eyes.

By noon Hark had made his decision, though he'd made it around dawn without admitting it to himself, the way most of his decisions actually happened.

It came down to a simple piece of arithmetic, the kind he trusted more than any license. Theo Reyes had paid cash, thanked him twice, and carried his own crates down to the water. Voss's men had broken into his storeroom and written down his dried apples. One of those men was a customer. The other kind was a customer only until the counting started. Nineteen years of Rule One had taught Hark that everybody's money was green; nineteen years of living had taught him that not everybody's was clean.

Besides — and here was the part Hark would never say aloud to a living soul — there was the girl. Wren Crane. Silas Crane's girl, up at the headwaters somewhere, fierce as a marten and half as sociable. Hark had sold her traps and cartridges for six years and she'd never once let him finish a sentence, but she'd also never once shorted him, and in Hark's book that put a person ahead of most preachers. If the railroad man was headed up toward her — and why else follow a dead prospector's river? — then the railroad man was carrying something that belonged to her. And Hark Odum did not hold mail hostage. Not for judges' stove wood, not for licenses, not for anybody.

So at the Narrows, where the river pinched between walls of gray granite and the current went from suggestion to argument, Hark called the halt.

"End of my water," he said, backing the boat into an eddy beneath a leaning birch. "From here up it's portage lines and bad news."

The tall one scanned the gorge with those hungry eyes. "There's a trail?"

"There's a trail the way there's a rumor. Mostly true, mostly dangerous." Hark climbed out onto the bank, stretching his back with a groan he didn't entirely need, buying seconds. "Now, I'll tell you what I told the canoe man, since you're bound to ask anyway. There's a cache up this stretch. Old trapper custom — you stash goods along the route so you're never caught empty. Mine's in the alders, maybe six miles up, left bank, under a split boulder with a blaze on it. Dried goods, oilskin, a spare paddle. Anybody traveling this river honest is welcome to it. That's the custom."

"Is it now," said the tall one.

"It is now," said Hark. "That's what makes it a custom."

What he did not tell them — what he would carry to his grave with a smile on his face — was that the cache in the alders was real, and stocked, and that yesterday evening, while the two gray-canvas gentlemen were still walking up his dock in his imagination, Hark had knelt in the wet moss beside that split boulder with a pencil stub and a scrap of wrapping paper, and had written the shortest, truest letter of his life.

He'd thought about the wording for an hour. Too vague and the man would ignore it. Too specific and it might fall into the wrong hands — though Hark reckoned the odds of Voss's men finding an unblazed cache in six miles of alder thicket were roughly the odds of the river flowing backward out of politeness.

In the end he'd kept it simple:

*REYES — IF THIS IS YOU, TWO MEN ON THE WATER BEHIND YOU. ONE TALL AND MEAN, ONE SHORT AND SMILING. THE SMILING ONE IS THE DANGEROUS ONE. THEY KNOW THE RIVER AS FAR AS THE NARROWS AND NO FARTHER. THEY HAVE MONEY AND NO PATIENCE, WHICH IS WORSE THAN GUNS. TAKE THE SIDE CHANNEL ABOVE THE FALLS IF YOU'RE DESPERATE ENOUGH. EVERYBODY ELSE DIES THERE, BUT SO WOULD THEY, AND THEY KNOW IT. — A FRIEND*

He'd wrapped it in oilcloth, weighted it with a stone, and set it square in the middle of the cache where no honest traveler could miss it, because honest travelers opened caches like children opened presents.

Now, standing on the bank at the Narrows while the two hired men argued about who would carry the heavier pack, Hark allowed himself the smallest private pleasure of his long career: the knowledge that somewhere upstream, a stranger was going to open a box of dried apples and find out that decency still had a pulse in this country.

"Odum," the tall one called. "You coming?"

"I'm going," Hark said, stepping back into his boat. "Down. My water ends here, remember? You boys want the trail, it starts at that blaze and it lies to you the whole way up." He poled out into the current, then paused, struck by a final inspiration — the kind of small, mean, beautiful idea that comes to a man late in life and makes him feel twenty years younger. "One thing," he called back. "Fellow named Voss. When you see him again — tell him the flour on his list is rancid. Been meaning to swap it out for weeks."

The cheerful one laughed again, that warm terrible laugh, and waved.

Hark poled downstream with the current helping him for once, and by the time the Narrows fell out of sight behind the first bend he was already composing, in his head, the note he'd leave in his own window in case the girl came downriver looking for her father's friend: *Wren — trouble on the water, headed up. If you see a railroad man, feed him. He's carrying your inheritance.*

Rule One: sell to everybody. Rule Two: know nothing about anybody.

Rule Three, Hark decided, watching the gray granite walls slide past, needed amending. When trouble found you anyway, pour it a drink — and then, quiet as moss growing, slip a note into the alders and let the river do the rest.

Six miles upstream, under a split boulder, a scrap of oilcloth waited in the dark with a dead man's route and a live man's warning, and the whole future of the escarpment country sat balanced on whether one exhausted stranger would think to stop and open a box of dried apples.
