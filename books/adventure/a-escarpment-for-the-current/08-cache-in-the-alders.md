## 8. Cache in the Alders

The morning of the fourth day upstream, Theo woke to find a spider had built a web across his face sometime in the night.

He lay very still under the canoe he'd propped on two paddles as a lean-to and considered this. The web was anchored to his hat brim at one end and to a spruce root at the other, and its architect sat in the center of it, patient as a landlord, waiting for him to do something foolish. Theo respected that. He'd spent twenty years working for men exactly like it.

"Morning," he said.

The spider did not answer. Neither did the river, which was unusual — the river had opinions about everything, and it shared them freely, all day, all night, in a voice like gravel being argued over by gravel. But this stretch ran quiet through a long alder flat, water the color of weak tea sliding over sandbars, and for half an hour after waking there was nothing in the world but mist off the current and the smell of wet bark and the small fact of a spider who had, apparently, decided Theo Reyes was furniture.

He eased out from under the canoe without breaking the web. It felt important, somehow, not to break it. He couldn't have said why. Maybe because everything else on this river seemed to exist specifically to be broken — portage trails drowned under blowdown, blazes chopped out of trees, a whole country surveyed once and then un-surveyed by men with better handwriting than conscience. A web left standing felt like a courtesy the wilderness extended to almost no one, and he wasn't going to be the one to end it.

He ate cold oats mixed with river water and a fistful of dried blueberries, and he got back in the canoe, and he pushed on toward the cache he didn't yet know existed.

* * *

Hark had told him about it without telling him about it, three days before Theo ever launched. They'd been loading provisions at the outfitter's dock, and Hark — a man built like a rain barrel with a beard that suggested he'd lost several arguments with bees — had paused over the last sack of flour and said, in the tone of a man discussing weather:

"There's a place upriver where the alders come down to the water on the north bank. Mile marker's a lightning-split birch, if it hasn't gone over. Old trapper custom, leaving goods in the crook of things. You never know what you'll find in the crook of things."

"I thought you said nobody goes upriver," Theo had said.

"Nobody honest," Hark had said, and shoved the flour at him, and that had been the end of the conversation, or so it had seemed at the time.

Now, poling up a slow reach with the sun finally burning off the mist, Theo turned the memory over again. He'd been turning it over for two days. There had been something in Hark's voice — not warmth exactly; Hark's face didn't seem built for warmth, more for enduring — but a kind of gravity, the way a man sets down a heavy thing carefully because he can't say outright what it is.

Silas Crane's map lay open on the thwart beside him, weighted with a stone, protected under its sheet of oilskin. Theo had taken to reading it the way other men read scripture: looking for comfort, finding instructions, understanding maybe one line in ten. The dead man's hand was cramped and certain, every bend of the river inked in brown that had gone the color of old blood, and the annotations ran along the margins in pencil that faded and sharpened as though Silas had made these notes over years, returning again and again, adding to them the way you'd add to a letter you kept meaning to send.

*Gravel bar shifts after high water — trust the willow, not the bar.*

*Portage LEFT here. Right side looks easier. Right side is a lie.*

And, near the top of the page, in letters pressed so hard they'd torn the paper slightly:

*Second survey lies above the falls. Ask no one. Trust the crook of things.*

Trust the crook of things.

Theo looked up. The alders were thickening along the north bank, a gray-green wall of them, their catkins hanging like small tassels. And there, fifty yards on, leaning out over the water at a drunkard's angle, stood a birch split clean down its middle by some old lightning strike, both halves still alive, still leafing, growing in two directions at once like a man trying to serve two masters.

"Well," Theo said aloud, to the spider he'd left behind, to the river, to nobody. "Crook of things."

* * *

The cache was wedged in the fork of the split birch, four feet above the high-water mark: a tin biscuit box, rusted at the corners but sound, lashed down with a length of tarred cord that had been tied recently enough to still smell of pitch.

Theo beached the canoe and stood on the bank for a long moment, hands in his pockets, looking at it.

Here was the thing about being forty-one years old and having spent your entire adult life making maps for an institution: you learned that nothing free appears in the wilderness. Every blaze, every cache, every friendly dock had an owner, and owners wanted things. He had learned this lesson in the ordinary way, through paychecks, which is to say slowly and at great expense. A stranger's kindness on a lonely river was either a trap or a debt, and either way it would cost him.

But he also remembered Hark's face when Voss's name had come up at the outfitter's — how the big man had gone carefully blank, the way men go blank around powerful things, the way Theo himself went blank in boardrooms. And he remembered that Hark had sold him the canoe at half its worth and thrown in the oilskin without being asked, and had watched him paddle away with an expression Theo had taken, at the time, for simple regret at a lost sale.

He opened the tin.

Inside: a paper-wrapped brick of tea. A coil of snare wire. Three candles. A block of salt pork in cloth. And folded on top of all of it, in paper that had been handled often, a note.

The handwriting was Hark's — he recognized the blunt, downward-slanting letters from the supply ledger — and it read:

*REYES. Men came asking after you the day after you left. Two of them, plus a third who does the talking. Name of Voss behind it, though he keeps his own hands clean. They know the river route. They bought my ledger, which means they know where you bought your flour and how much of it, and they are laughing about the flour. Trackers hired out of Fort Meridian, brothers, name of Pell. Good in the bush, bad tempers, worse teeth. I told them you were green and would wreck at the first rapids. They believed me because men believe what flatters them.*

*I could not refuse them and keep my license, and my license feeds six people. Understand that or don't. But understand also this: I am sorry, and the tea is good, and the side channel at the falls is marked unrunnable on your map because a man named Aldous marked it so, and Aldous never ran anything harder than a desk. Look at the water yourself and decide.*

*Burn this.*

*H.*

Theo read it twice. Then he sat down on the bank with his back against the split birch, in the exact spot where the lightning had done its dividing work, and he read it a third time, slower, the way you reread a letter from someone you'd underestimated.

They knew the route. Of course they knew the route. He'd been telling himself since the burned bridge that Voss was guessing, casting a wide net up a long river, and he had known even as he told himself that it was a comfortable story, the kind of story a man buys the way he buys cheap whiskey — knowing it's thin, drinking it anyway.

The Pell brothers. He tried to summon them into his imagination and got two vague shapes with rifles and, per Hark, disappointing dentition. That was the trouble with being hunted by strangers: your mind insisted on drawing them, and your mind drew them ten feet tall. He made himself stop. Better to think of them as weather. Weather you could read, prepare for, sometimes outrun. Weather you could not reason with.

And the third thing — the thing he kept circling back to, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone —

Hark had compromised. Hark had handed over his ledger and pointed armed men upriver after him, and then Hark had walked out to a split birch and left tea and candles and a warning, and asked forgiveness in the same breath as he explained why he needed it.

Theo found, examining himself honestly, that he wasn't even angry. What he felt was closer to recognition. Twenty years of signing things he hadn't fully read, of letting the institution speak through his pen — he knew exactly what it cost a decent man to stay employed inside something rotten. He knew the arithmetic of it. Keep the license, feed the six, tell yourself the compromise is temporary. He'd run that arithmetic for two decades and called the answer a career.

"Thanks, Hark," he said to the river generally. It seemed as good an address as any.

Then he took out his matches and burned the note in the tin, as instructed, watching the curl of smoke rise straight up through the alder leaves into a windless sky. Some instructions you followed precisely. It was the only loyalty Hark had asked of him, and it was cheap, and cheap loyalties were worth honoring loudly.

* * *

He reached the falls by midafternoon.

The map had promised them with a single ominous pencil stroke and the word *PORTAGE*, and for once the map undersold. The river narrowed between walls of granite, gathered itself with an audible change in pitch — a deepening hum Theo felt through the hull before he heard it — and then dropped thirty feet in a quarter mile of white ruin. Spray hung over the whole gorge like breath on a winter window. Even from a hundred yards upstream, sitting in eddy water, he could feel the falls in his back teeth.

The portage trail ran along the south bank, well-trodden, blazed, easy. Any reasonable party — any party with two trackers and rifles and a schedule — would take the south bank, walk their boats around, and pick up the current below.

The side channel branched north a hundred yards above the drop, slipping away between alders into what the map called, in Silas's hardest-pressed pencil, *UNRUNNABLE — DO NOT — ALDOUS WAS RIGHT HERE*.

Theo pulled the canoe into the shallows at the fork and got out and walked it, wading, reading the water.

The channel was narrow, maybe twelve feet across, choked at its head with sweepers — alders leaning right across the current, their branches combing the flow. Beyond them the water dropped fast through a slot of gray rock, and he could see white water, real white water, the kind that ate canoes and occasionally the people in them. It was, objectively, a terrible idea. It was the kind of water a sensible man portaged around, and Theo Reyes had been a sensible man for forty-one years and it had gotten him a pension plan and a head full of closed lines.

But.

He crouched at the channel mouth and did the thing he actually knew how to do, the one skill twenty years of railroad surveying had ground into him deeper than any shame: he read terrain. Gradient, sight lines, hazards. The first fifty feet were ugly — sweepers, a mid-channel rock you'd have to ferry hard to miss. Then the slot widened briefly into a pool, a recovery pool, a place a battered boat could gather itself. Below the pool the channel stepped down in three short drops, each one runnable if you entered it straight, each one fatal if you entered it sideways. And below those, visible from where he crouched, the channel rejoined the main river a full mile below the falls — a mile of quiet water, empty banks, no trail, no blazes, no reason for anyone to be there.

Aldous had marked it unrunnable because Aldous had never looked at it. Silas had written *DO NOT* and then, apparently, had gone and looked anyway, and had added *ALDOUS WAS RIGHT HERE* in a hand that shook slightly, which Theo chose to interpret as respect rather than terror.

It was a gamble. He knew that. If the Pells came down the main channel while he was committed in the slot, he'd be a man in a wooden egg rolling down a staircase with no way to stop or turn. But the Pells were trackers, and trackers moved on land, along trails, at walking pace, and the south-bank portage would eat an hour of their day. And Voss's men, Hark had written, believed he was green. Green men did not run unrunnable water. Men believed what flattered them.

Besides — and this was the thought that tipped it, the thought he'd have been embarrassed to say aloud — Silas Crane had trusted this route to no one. Had trusted it, in the end, to a stranger in a muddy saloon, because the stranger was the only option left. Theo had inherited a dead man's faith and a dead man's enemies, and the least he owed the old man was to travel his river the way the old man must have traveled it: alone, watchful, and slightly against his own better judgment.

"All right, Silas," he said. "Show me."

* * *

He ran it.

He would spend years afterward failing to describe it properly, and would settle, eventually, on telling people it had been "a bit bumpy," which was the kind of lie rivers teach you. The truth involved the sweepers taking his hat, a moment of pure geometric horror in the slot where the rock he'd mapped from shore appeared to have relocated itself, the pool where he knelt in eighteen inches of river and laughed until his ribs hurt, and three drops taken straight and true with the bow punching through foam and the escarpment wall flashing past close enough to touch. The canoe — Hark's canoe, bless it, built by someone who understood that a boat is a promise — took it all with a flex and a groan and one new scar along the starboard gunwale, and delivered him, drenched and howling, into the mile of quiet water below.

He drifted. He wrung out his shirt. His hands wouldn't stop shaking for a quarter mile, and he let them shake, because it seemed polite to acknowledge what they'd just done.

The banks here were different — steeper, darker, the escarpment rising on the north side in long shoulders of limestone shadowed with cedar. No trail. No blazes. No sign that human beings had ever bothered with this stretch except to avoid it. The loneliness that had been building in him since the saloon — since the train, if he was honest, since twenty years of trains — settled over him now like the spray off the falls, fine and cold and everywhere.

He was alone on a river that wanted him dead, carrying a dead man's map toward a living woman who might reasonably shoot him on sight, pursued by men whose teeth Hark had felt compelled to warn him about, trusting an outfitter who had sold him out and saved him in the same afternoon.

Trust, he thought, dipping his paddle. That was the whole economy now. Not money, not credentials, not the railroad's good name — none of it meant anything past the first rapids. Out here the only currency was who you believed, and how much, and whether they'd earned it. Hark had paid in tea and burned paper. Silas had paid everything and left only a map. Wren Crane, whoever she was, held the entire account outstanding.

The light was going gold and long. Ahead, the river bent toward the escarpment's first great wall, and somewhere beyond it, according to a dead man's pencil, lay a second survey and a daughter and whatever truth was worth killing over.

Theo found a gravel bar — trusting the willow, not the bar — and hauled out to camp.

That was when he saw the smoke.

One thread of it, thin and gray, rising off the ridge to the north. Too far to be a campfire's neighbor. Too steady to be accidental. Somebody was up on the escarpment top tonight, keeping warm, keeping watch, keeping pace.

He banked his own fire small, down in a hollow, behind the canoe. He ate salt pork from Hark's tin and drank Hark's tea and did not sleep much, and in the gray hour before dawn he pushed back out onto the black water, paddling soft, heading for the falls — the second falls, the ones the map promised were worse.

Behind him, on the ridge, the smoke thinned and vanished.

Ahead, faint on the wind, carried downriver against all sense, he thought he heard a whistle — two notes, low then high, like a man calling a dog.

Or signaling a brother.
