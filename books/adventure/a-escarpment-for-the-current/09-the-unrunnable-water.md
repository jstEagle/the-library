## 9. The Unrunnable Water

The side channel began the way most bad ideas begin: quietly, with a certain seductive logic.

Theo stood at the split in the river where the main current bent west around a gravel bar and a narrower channel slipped north into a gap in the escarpment wall — a slot of green water no wider than a wagon road, walled in by limestone the color of old bone. The morning was cold enough that his breath hung around him like a thought he couldn't shake off.

*Unrunnable,* said the map, in Silas Crane's cramped hand. Below it, smaller, almost apologetic: *or near enough. I ran it twice. Second time on purpose.*

"That's not reassuring," Theo told the map. "That's a man bragging about surviving his own mistake."

The map didn't answer. Maps never did; that was the trouble with them. They told you where things were and let you draw your own conclusions about whether you were fool enough to go there.

He crouched at the gravel bar and considered the alternatives one more time, the way a man re-reads a letter he's already decided to send. Behind him, downriver, were two trackers with a company payroll behind them and a burned bridge at their backs to prove they meant business. Ahead, through the gorge, was whatever Silas had survived twice. Hark's note — *they know the river route, boy, all of it* — sat folded in his breast pocket like a stone.

"Near enough," Theo said, and pushed the canoe into the green water.

* * *

For the first mile, the channel almost apologized for its reputation. It ran quick and clear over polished rock, threading between boulders the size of sleeping cattle, and Theo found himself almost enjoying it — the cold spray, the echo of his own paddle knocking off the canyon walls, the absurd private pleasure of being somewhere no timetable had ever reached. He'd spent fifteen years drawing lines across country from the window of a speeder, reducing rivers to blue squiggles and mountains to contour hatching. This was what the squiggles actually felt like. Cold. Alive. Indifferent to him in a way he found, perversely, restful.

Then the walls narrowed, and the river stopped apologizing.

It happened between one stroke and the next. The banks drew in until they were scarcely a canoe-length apart, the water darkened from green to a bruised slate, and the sound changed — not the chatter of rapids anymore but a low, continuous roar, the voice of a great deal of water being forced through a very small opinion of itself. Theo back-paddled hard, looking for an eddy, a gravel spit, anything to pull out and portage.

There wasn't anything. That was the design of the place. The map had tried to tell him this too — a small cross drawn at the head of the gorge with the notation *no landing. commit or climb out and walk, if you can.* He'd read it. He'd even understood it. Understanding a thing and believing it, it turned out, lived in different counties.

The current took the bow and swung it, casual as a hand turning a page.

What followed did not feel like minutes. It felt like a series of separate disasters, each one arriving with its own full attention. There was a ledge where the whole river dropped three feet into a churning trough and the canoe went airborne long enough for Theo to notice, with strange clarity, that his hat was gone. There was a hole below the ledge that swallowed the bow and spat it sideways, and there was a rock shaped like a church pew that caught the stern and spun him so fast the canyon became a blur of gray and green. There was a moment — he would remember it always, the way you remember the exact wording of a telegram — when he was upside down in cold so total it felt less like temperature than like substance, and the world was a muffled roar, and his hands were still on the paddle because some animal part of him hadn't gotten the news yet.

Then the river picked up the canoe like a toy it was done with and broke it against the canyon wall.

The crack came through the hull and up through his knees, and then he was in the water, and the water was not a thing you swam in so much as a thing you were briefly permitted inside of. It rolled him. It held him under long enough that he saw, quite distinctly, the pale underside of the surface glowing above him like a ceiling in a house where nobody lived. His left shoulder hit something hard. His right boot filled with river. Somewhere in the churn he had the presence of mind — or the cowardice, he couldn't decide which — to stop fighting and let the current have him, arms crossed over the oilskin packet strapped to his chest, and the current, having made its point, carried him along like a grudge it intended to keep but not act on today.

He washed out onto a gravel shelf perhaps half a mile downstream, on hands and knees, coughing the river back out of himself.

* * *

He lay there a long time. Long enough for the sun to move visibly along the canyon rim, which at that latitude and season took some doing. The roar of the gorge continued behind him, patient, unimpressed.

Inventory, when he finally worked up the will to take it, went like this:

One oilskin packet containing Silas Crane's map, dry. One man, mostly functional, bleeding modestly from the left shoulder and both shins. One boot full of river. No canoe. No paddle. No hat — and he mourned the hat, genuinely, because Hark had sold it to him with the words *"a man who dresses like he expects to arrive somewhere gets treated better,"* and Theo had liked being a man who expected to arrive somewhere. No tent. No food beyond three strips of dried venison and a tin of beans that had ridden in his coat pocket and survived, miraculously, unopened and unbent. Half a box of matches in a waxed paper twist, which he checked four times, counting them each time as though the number might improve with attention. Eleven matches. A hatchet, because it had been lashed to his belt and the river apparently had standards about what it would take. And the clothes he stood in, which stood in question themselves, being soaked through and already steaming faintly in the thin sun.

Half his gear gone. More than half, really, if gear was measured by what kept a man alive past sundown.

"Well," Theo said to the canyon. His voice came out scraped and small and echoed anyway, bouncing off the limestone like the canyon was laughing and wanted him to know it. "You could've just robbed me."

The canyon declined to negotiate.

He got up. Getting up was its own argument — every joint filed a complaint, and his left shoulder answered roll call with a hot wire of pain that made him see white at the edges. But nothing was broken that he could determine by the crude method of moving everything and noting what screamed. He wrung out his socks, which was a gesture more than a solution, and turned once, slowly, to look back upstream at the gorge he'd come through.

From here it didn't look like water at all. It looked like a seam in the world, a black stitch running through the escarpment, with mist rising off it in slow ropes. Somewhere in there was the bottom half of his canoe, lodged in the rocks, becoming geology.

Silas had run it twice. Theo turned that fact over and found it heavier than it looked. A man didn't run water like that twice for gold alone. Gold you could dig for anywhere; men dug for it in flat, sensible places all the time. No — a man ran a gorge like that twice because the thing at the end of it mattered more than the reasonable odds against reaching it. Because somebody had to see it. Because a survey had been erased, and erased things don't stay buried unless someone is willing to be unreasonable on their behalf.

*I trusted no one with this route,* Silas had written, in the margin beside the second cross. Not *I showed no one.* Trusted. As though the route itself were a confessor, and the trust ran uphill.

Standing there dripping on the gravel, Theo finally understood it. You didn't keep a secret like this by hiding it from people. People could be bought — he'd watched Mr. Voss buy a town's worth of silence before breakfast. You kept it by making the price of reaching it high enough that only conviction could pay. The river wasn't a barrier. It was a filter. It asked every traveler the same question — *how much do you mean it?* — and it collected its toll in gear, in blood, in pride, and sometimes in everything.

Today it had taken a canoe and a hat. Theo resolved, shivering, to remain a bargain.

* * *

He walked. There wasn't anything else to do, and doing nothing in wet wool at altitude is how men die of decisions they already made. The canyon widened by degrees, the way a fist opens, and by late afternoon he'd gained the bench of land above the flood line where the pines started — sparse, wind-bent trees clinging to the limestone like opinions nobody had asked for.

He gathered wood with hands too numb to feel the splinters. The hatchet helped. Fire, when it finally caught — match seven, after two failures he chose not to count aloud — was less a comfort than a religion. He fed it like an altar attendant, stripped his outer clothes and hung them on sticks around the flames, and sat wrapped in the oilskin packet's spare wrapping cloth, eating the venison in pieces so small the meal lasted twenty minutes and satisfied him for nearly ten.

The beans he saved. A man who has eleven matches learns to think in terms of later.

Night came down into the canyon like a lid. The stars arrived in force — no town within fifty miles to dilute them — and Theo lay close to the fire with his boots off and his feet toward the flames and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the gravel bar.

He was alive. That was column one, and it was a short column, and lately he'd learned to be grateful for short columns. Column two: the map was dry, the promise was intact, and the source lay somewhere ahead, higher up, past whatever else the river had been saving for him. Column three: Voss's trackers were behind him, presumably staring at the same gorge he'd just survived, and either finding a way around it — a day lost, maybe two — or concluding he was dead, which would be convenient and also, given how he currently felt, only technically inaccurate.

And column four, the one he kept turning face-down and it kept turning itself back up: his own signature, on a witness line, years ago, on paperwork he'd signed without reading because the railroad was paying him and the railroad was the railroad and that was that. He'd told himself on the raft of his own good intentions, all downriver, that he was delivering a dead man's map. Clean errand. Honest grief. But the truth was he was walking — swimming, drowning, crawling — toward a document that might have his own name on it, in his own hand, next to the word *approved.*

Silas had trusted no one. Maybe because everyone was compromised. Maybe especially the ones who didn't know they were.

"You should've sent me a letter," Theo told the fire. "Given a man time to prepare."

But of course Silas had written letters. Wren had said so — unsent, a drawer full of them. A man who wrote letters every year and mailed none of them was a man who understood exactly what he deserved and had decided to accept it rather than argue. Theo knew the type. He was, he suspected with a fresh chill that had nothing to do with the river, becoming the type.

He banked the fire, pulled the damp wool back over his skin, and lay watching the stars wheel over the canyon rim. His shoulder throbbed in time with his pulse. Somewhere upstream, water fell over stone with a patience that made human urgency look like a child's tantrum. He thought about his mentor, Aldous Wren — no relation, or maybe relation, the map's handwriting suggested things he wasn't ready to say out loud — and about all the closed lines, all the blue squiggles, all the tidy erasures he'd called a career.

The river had reduced him to eleven matches, three strips of meat, a tin of beans, and a promise. Stripped down like that, he discovered, there wasn't much left to hide behind, and what little remained was surprisingly load-bearing.

"I mean it," he said aloud, answering the gorge's question at last. "However much that's worth."

The river roared on, neither accepting nor refusing the terms.

He slept in fits, waking once to the sound of something large moving through the pines — elk, probably, or the canyon collecting interest — and once to check the matches again. Eleven. Still eleven. In the gray hour before dawn, stiff as a fence post and smelling of woodsmoke and river, Theo Reyes got up, put on his half-dried clothes, ate half the beans (saving the rest for a tomorrow he was now formally committed to believing in), and stood looking upriver at the escarpment wall, which rose out of the morning mist like the spine of the world.

The map, dry and creased and grease-stained, showed the channel bending east above the gorge, climbing toward a fork marked with a single word in Silas's hand: *near.*

Two days' travel, if the terrain allowed it. Three if it didn't. And somewhere ahead, at the end of all that water's patience, a daughter who didn't know her father was dead, and a spring that powerful men had already begun staking out for destruction.

Theo folded the map away, tightened the oilskin, and started walking.

He did not hear the rifle shot until the third echo came back off the canyon wall — and it had come from ahead of him, not behind.
