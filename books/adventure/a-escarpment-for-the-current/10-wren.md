## 10. Wren

The marten had been dead for three hours by the time Wren Crane reached it, and she was glad of that, because it meant she didn't have to look it in the eye while she reset the trap.

She crouched in the alder shadow at the edge of the beaver meadow, knife out, and did the arithmetic she did every morning this time of year. One marten, prime winter coat, maybe eleven dollars at Hark's post if he was feeling honest and fourteen if he was feeling guilty. Two more days of flour. Half a day of coffee, which was not a luxury but a structural necessity, like roof shingles or a conscience. She could stretch the sugar another week if she drank the tea bitter, which she would, because she'd been drinking things bitter her whole life and had gotten quite good at it.

"Sorry, friend," she told the marten, and made it quick.

Her father had taught her to apologize to trapped animals. That was the kind of man Silas Crane was — tenderhearted in all the directions that cost him nothing. He'd wept over a snared rabbit when she was nine, then walked out the door two weeks later with his pack basket and his promise to be back before the ice went rotten, and hadn't come back before the ice went rotten, or before the next ice, or the one after that. Eleven years now. Letters came sometimes, when they came at all, carried downriver by trappers who shrugged apologetically: *Silas says hello. Silas says he's close to something big. Silas says tell Wren—*

Tell Wren what. Tell Wren he loved her, presumably, in the way men love from a great safe distance, where love costs postage and nothing else.

She wrapped the marten in burlap and slung it over her shoulder and stood a moment in the cold morning light, breathing steam, listening to the river. The headwaters ran shallow and quick over limestone here, loud even in freeze-up's first weeks, a sound she had hated for years and had lately, to her considerable irritation, stopped hating. You couldn't hate a river. It wasn't the river's fault who'd left along it.

She climbed back toward her cabin — a lean-to, really, chinked with moss, that she'd built herself at seventeen out of spite and windfall cedar — and hung the pelt on the stretching frame beside the others. Seven pelts now. A good month. Enough to eat and enough to be angry with something left over.

It was on her way back out to check the far line, cutting up through the spruce along the concession boundary, that she found the marker again.

* * *

She'd found the first one in September, and it had ruined her whole autumn.

A brass-capped stake driven into a granite shoulder above the river, stamped with the Meridian Concession Company's mark, and set — this was the part that had kept her up nights — in a place where no survey line had any business being. Wren knew that country the way other women knew their kitchens. Her father had trapped these headwaters before the company ever filed its papers, and he'd taught her every bend and bluff, and the boundary on the official map — the one Hark kept behind his counter, the one everybody treated like scripture — ran a full mile east of that stake. Maybe more.

Somebody had moved the line. Or somebody had drawn it wrong on purpose.

She'd gone looking for more stakes after that, and found them: six, then nine, then a dozen, marching uphill through country the permits were never supposed to touch, each one neat as a hymn, each one wrong. And the thing about it — the thing that had turned her anger from the low warm banked coals of ordinary grievance into something with a draft under it — was that nobody else seemed to see them. The company men who came through twice a season never looked at the ground. They looked at their papers. Papers said the line was there, so the line was there, and the actual iron-and-brass truth standing in the moss might as well have been a ghost.

So Wren had started doing what her father should have done instead of writing letters: she'd started keeping records. Rubbings of every stamp on tracing cloth she'd traded two marten for. Distances paced and noted in a school composition book. Sketches of each stake against its landmarks, dated, signed. She didn't know yet what she'd do with it. She only knew that when the world lies to you in ink, you answer back in ink, and you keep your handwriting better than theirs.

Today she knelt at stake number thirteen, high on the shoulder below the escarpment wall, and copied the stamp again though she had it by heart. The brass was bright. Newer than the others. Somebody was still working this line, still extending it, still quietly walking a lie uphill toward the source of the river.

Toward her river.

"No," she said aloud, to nobody, to the company, to her father, to the whole arrangement of the world. "You don't get this too."

She was rolling the tracing cloth when the light changed.

Not the sky — the sky had been flat gray since dawn and intended to stay that way. It was movement, downriver, at the edge of the gravel bar below the second rapids: something pale struggling at the waterline, then upright, then staggering. A man. A tall man, moving like a chair with a broken leg, lurching from stone to stone, and then sitting down hard in the shallows and staying there as if the idea of standing had been struck from the law.

Wren went very still, the way she did when anything crossed into her country uninvited. Marten. Moose. Man. Same rule: watch first.

He didn't move for a long moment. Then he got one knee under himself, and she saw him take inventory — patting his chest, his hip, a canvas roll clutched against his ribs like a mother with a child — and whatever he found there seemed to satisfy him, because he let out a breath that carried even across the water, and laughed. Actually laughed. A short, cracked, disbelieving sound, the laugh of a man who has just been informed by his own body how lucky he is.

Then he tried to stand again and failed, and sat there in the freezing shallows with the water pulling at him, and Wren's anger — which had been having such a productive morning — found itself abruptly without work to do.

"Oh, hell," she muttered.

* * *

Here is what Wren Crane knew about strangers, and she knew it the way she knew traplines: thoroughly, and from bad experience.

Strangers came upstream for two reasons. The first was profit, and those ones wore good boots and asked questions with smiles in them, questions like *you live out here alone?* and *your father around?* and they always, always looked past her when they asked, as though the answer must be standing behind her wearing trousers. The second kind came upstream running from something, and they were worse, because they brought the thing they were running from, the way a dog brings burrs.

This man was neither, or he was both, or he was something new. He wore city clothes ruined past saving — good wool gone gray with silt, boots that had never met a portage and had clearly lost the argument anyway. He had no pack worth the name. He had a canoe, or the remains of one; she could see the shattered bow wedged in the rocks downstream, gnawed white, like something the river had chewed and spat. And he was hurt, or half-drowned, or both, and he was holding that canvas roll against his chest the way she'd hold a pelt worth fifty dollars, or a letter worth more.

She weighed it. She was honest enough with herself to admit she weighed it longer than she needed to. There was a version of Wren Crane who walked away, who let the river finish its paperwork, who had earned the right to mind her own fire after eleven years of minding it alone. That version had a strong case. That version had been arguing since she was fourteen.

But the other thing — the thing underneath the anger, the thing she fed and watered and pretended wasn't there — was that she had spent eleven years watching a empty river trail, waiting for somebody to come up it. Anybody. And now somebody finally had, half-dead and ridiculous, and turning him away felt like answering her own prayer with a slammed door.

"Fine," she said to herself. "Fine."

She took the spear off her shoulder — a real one, ash-shafted, barbed, built for sturgeon and equally persuasive with people — and went down to the gravel bar at an angle that gave her the sun and him the glare.

"Hoy," she called.

His head came up. Even across thirty yards she could read the sequence on his face: relief, gratitude, and then — arriving late, the way sense always does with drowning men — wariness, as he took in the spear, the stance, the general situation.

"That's far enough," she said, when he'd managed ten staggering steps toward her. "Sit down on that rock. Both hands where I can see them."

"I—" His voice was wrecked, scraped raw. He coughed, tried again. "I'm not going to trouble you. I just need to know where I am."

"You're on my beach," Wren said, "in my country, bleeding on my rocks, and you're asking where you are." She let that sit. Up close he was younger than she'd guessed — thirty, maybe, dark-haired, hollow-eyed, with the particular pallor of a man who'd been cold to the bone and hadn't noticed yet, which was worse than shivering. Shivering meant the furnace still worked. "What's your name?"

"Theo," he said. "Theo Reyes." And then, because he was either braver or stupider than he looked: "I'm looking for someone. Wren Crane."

The river went on talking. The wind moved the alders. Somewhere overhead a raven made its dry remark.

Wren found that her heart had begun doing something complicated and unwelcome, hammering and sinking at once, and she was furious at it, furious at him, furious at the whole treacherous machinery of hope. Because there was only one reason a stranger dragged himself out of the upper river asking for Wren Crane by name, and the reason was named Silas, and men who came asking after Silas Crane came either with money or with news, and this man had no money.

"What about him?" she said, and was proud of how flat her voice came out.

"He sent me." Theo Reyes fumbled at the canvas roll with fingers too stiff to manage the knots. "I was supposed to — I promised him — it's yours, I think. He said to bring it to you. To the source. Before the permits expire." He got the last knot loose and held the roll out to her, arms shaking, eyes fever-bright and absolutely steady. "I'm sorry. About your father. He died four days ago. In a saloon in Kettle Landing. Fever. He wasn't alone at the end. I want you to know he wasn't alone."

The spear point drifted down until it rested in the gravel, and Wren stood there in the cold and let the words land, one after another, like stones dropped into deep water.

Dead. Four days. Alone except for a stranger, in a saloon, in Kettle Landing, forty miles and eleven years from anywhere she'd ever been able to reach him.

She thought: *I never answered the last letter.* She thought: *I burned the one before that without reading it.* She thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *he wrote me every year, and I let him believe—*

And underneath all of it, arriving last and quietest, the thought she'd carry longest: *he sent me something. At the end, he was thinking of me.*

"Give it here," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she decided to let it.

Theo handed over the roll. Inside the oilskin was a map — hand-drawn, grease-stained, soft as cloth from handling — and she knew the hand before she'd traced the first contour line. She'd know that hand anywhere. She'd learned her letters copying it, sitting on a woodpile at seven years old while her father drew her rivers that didn't exist yet.

Every year, she thought. Every year he wrote, and I threw it back at him, and all along he was carrying this. Carrying me. Right here, in ink, the whole time.

She looked up. Theo Reyes had sagged onto the rock she'd assigned him, shivering now in earnest, lips going gray, watching her with the careful blankness of a man trying not to intrude on grief.

"You're half-dead," she said.

"I've been worse," he said, which was obviously a lie, delivered with the small stubborn pride of a man who intended to go on lying about it.

"Get up, then. Slowly. If you fall on my beach I'm leaving you there on principle." She turned upriver, then stopped, and looked back at him — this battered, foolish, faithful stranger who had run a river to pieces to keep a promise to a man he'd known for one night. Something moved in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name.

"There's a fire at my place," she said. "And soup. And you can tell me everything he said. Every word. Start with why a railroad man comes up the unrunnable water carrying my father's map — and don't lie to me, Mr. Reyes, because I'll know."

Behind them, far downriver, faint as a rumor, came a sound she'd learn to dread before the week was out: a single rifle shot, flat and deliberate, rolling up the valley like a knock at a door.

Wren's head came up. Theo's did too.

"Company men don't shoot this early in the season," she said slowly. "Unless they're looking for something."

Or someone, she didn't say. But she was already counting the days since Kettle Landing, and doing the arithmetic she did every morning, and finding that this time the numbers had teeth.
