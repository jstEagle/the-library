## 11. Two Maps, One River

*Theo*

He woke to the smell of woodsmoke and the flat of a spear tapping his cheek.

Not a knife. Not a boot. A spear — a straight ash shaft with a barbed point that had been whittled by someone who understood both fish and people. Theo lay on his side in the shallows where he'd crawled out of the gorge the night before, wrapped in his oilskin like a burrito nobody had ordered, and he looked up into the business end of it.

"Morning," he croaked.

The woman holding the spear did not say good morning back. She was maybe thirty, lean as a river birch, with mud-colored hair tied back and eyes the exact gray-green of water over limestone. She'd built a fire within reach of him sometime in the night, he realized — there were fresh coals, and a tin kettle sitting near them — which meant she had watched him shiver and half-drown for hours and had decided, apparently, to let him marinate.

"You've been lying in my shallows since moonset," she said.

"Your shallows," Theo managed. "I didn't realize the river had been claimed."

"Everything up here is claimed. That's rather the problem." The spear tip drifted an inch closer. "Who are you and why are you floating down my river in pieces?"

"Theo Reyes." He tried to sit up and discovered that his body had filed a formal complaint overnight. Every muscle had signed it. "I was running the gorge."

Her eyebrows performed a small, eloquent act of disbelief. "Nobody runs the gorge."

"I'm beginning to see the flaw in that consensus."

"Where's your canoe?"

"In the gorge. In several locations. I could draw you a map of where each part went, but honestly the map would be more useful than the canoe at this point."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but a scout sent ahead of one. Then it was gone, replaced by the spear-point stare again. "You came downriver alone? Through the unrunnable water?"

"I had a map that said I shouldn't."

"And you ran it anyway?"

"I had a map that said *they* wouldn't follow me through it." He worked himself upright, wincing, and saw her glance sharpen at the word *they*. "There are men behind me. Three, maybe four, hired trackers. They want something I'm carrying."

"What?"

Theo hesitated. He had been robbed once already by trusting too fast, and nearly killed twice by trusting too slow. But the fire was warm, the kettle was steaming, and this woman had stood watch over a stranger all night when the easier thing would have been to let the river finish its work.

"A map," he said. "Drawn by Silas Crane."

The change in her face was not gradual. It was like watching ice go out of a spring pool — one moment solid, the next gone, and underneath it something moving fast and deep. The spear lowered perhaps two inches.

"Say that name again," she said quietly.

"Silas Crane. Old man, white beard, hands like tree roots. Feverish. He traded me this map for a promise, three weeks ago now, in a saloon in Harker's Mill. He died the next morning." Theo fumbled at the oilskin roll, fingers clumsy with cold. "I promised him I'd carry it upriver. To the source. To his daughter."

The spear dropped all the way to rest against her shoulder, forgotten. She stared at the bundle in his hands the way people stare at a grave.

"He's dead," she said. It wasn't a question. Her voice had gone very level, the way voices do when they're holding something heavy and can't afford to shake.

"Fever took him. He knew it was coming. That's why he wanted the map delivered fast." Theo peeled back the oilskin and unfolded the grease-stained paper between them, careful of the damp. "Wren," he said. "He said your name was Wren."

For a long moment Wren Crane did not touch the map. She crouched above it with her fists on her knees, reading it from a foot away, the way you read a letter from someone you're angry at — every line examined for evidence.

"That's his hand," she said finally. "That stupid little hook he puts on the sevens. He used to mark my height on the doorframe with the same hook. Every birthday. Like I was a survey point." Her jaw tightened. "He drew this and never once drew me a letter."

"He wrote letters," Theo said gently. "He told me so. Said he wrote one every year and couldn't ever figure out how to send them."

"Couldn't figure out how to *mail* them, more likely." But her voice cracked on the word *letters*, and she turned away quickly, busying herself with the kettle, pouring hot water over pine needles in a battered tin cup. When she handed it to him, her hand wasn't quite steady. "Drink. You look like something the river rejected."

"It did reject me. Twice."

"Pine tea. It tastes terrible and it fixes everything." She sat back on her heels and looked at him properly for the first time — the scrapes, the missing gear, the ruined boots. "You really walked into the escarpment country carrying a dead prospector's map, alone, with hired men after you."

"When you put it in that order it sounds less like a plan than it felt at the time."

* * *

*Wren*

She should have sent him on his way. That was the sensible thing. Strangers up here meant trouble, and trouble up here meant company men, and company men meant the kind of week where you slept in the alder thickets and moved your traps twice.

But he had Silas's map. And he had run the gorge — the actual gorge, the water she'd been told since childhood no living soul could run — because a dead man's note said the trackers wouldn't follow. Which meant either he was a fool or he was keeping a promise, and she'd spent enough nights alone to know that fools rarely made it through the unrunnable water, and promise-keepers were rarer than gold and worth more.

So she fed him. Dried trout and the last of her oatcakes, and he ate like a man who'd been polite about hunger for days, and she pretended not to notice.

It was when he spread the map fully open on a flat stone that she forgot to be guarded.

"Look here," Theo said, tapping a cluster of annotations upstream of the falls. "These aren't route notes. See the symbols? Bench marks. Elevations. This is surveyor's shorthand — someone ran instrument lines along this stretch. And these numbers—" he traced a column of figures in the margin, "—these are bearing corrections. Someone found errors in another survey and recorded what the true lines should be."

Wren went still.

"Show me the symbols again," she said.

She got her own papers from the hollow tree where she kept them — oilcloth-wrapped rubbings, dozens of them, taken from brass markers she'd found over the past season on concession land where markers had no business being. She'd documented them because they confused her, and she documented confusing things the way other people bit their fingernails. She laid the rubbings beside the map.

Theo leaned over them. She watched his face do something complicated.

"These match," he said slowly. "The bench-mark symbols on your rubbings — same style, same hand. Same hook on the sevens." He looked up at her. "Your father surveyed this country. Recently. Within the last few years."

"Silas hasn't—" She stopped. Corrected herself, because correcting herself was going to become a habit today and better to start early. "Silas hadn't been up here in eleven years. Not since—" Since he'd left. Since the argument. Since the door slam that had lasted a decade. "He couldn't have set these markers."

"No. But he copied the hand perfectly, or..." Theo's brow furrowed, and then she watched the thought arrive in him like weather. "Or these markers weren't set recently. They were set years ago and *hidden*. Covered over. Pulled and re-sunk where nobody would look." He smoothed the map flat. "Wren, listen. Silas told me the concession's official survey is wrong. He said the vein everyone's fighting over doesn't run where the permits say it runs. He said powerful men paid to have the truth erased from the record."

"I know the official survey," Wren said. "Everyone upriver knows it. It puts the ore body inside the concession boundary — that's why the permit exists, why the company has men staking ground, why Voss's riders treat the whole watershed like a private road." She pulled one rubbing free and slapped it down next to the map's margin figures. "But I found this marker forty yards *outside* the staked line. And this one—" another rubbing, "—a mile outside. If the vein follows these bench elevations, if it runs along the strike these bearings describe..."

"Then it runs under land the permits were never drawn to cover," Theo finished. His voice had gone quiet and strange. "The whole concession is a fence built around the wrong field. The real vein lies outside it entirely — and the official survey was falsified to hide that, because if the truth came out, the permits would be worthless and the claim would belong to whoever proved it first."

They sat with that. The river talked to itself over the riffle below the camp, patient, unhurried, as if it had known all along and was waiting for them to catch up.

"My father knew," Wren said. The anger she'd carried for eleven years shifted under her like a log roll in a current. "He found it. He found the fraud, or helped find it, and instead of telling anyone he drew a map and hid it and ran off prospecting like a—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles against her mouth.

"Or he tried to tell someone," Theo said carefully, "and learned what happens to people who tell."

"You don't know that."

"No," Theo admitted. "I know something else, though. Something worse, probably." He rolled up his sleeve, almost absently, the way a man does when he's about to show a scar. "I'm a railroad engineer. Was. I surveyed closed lines for fifteen years — routes the companies abandoned, grades they erased. And before that, I apprenticed under a man named Aldous Merrick. Best surveyor in the territory. He vanished from the records twelve years ago. Disgraced. Accused of falsifying a mineral survey and disappearing with the originals." He tapped the map's margin, the neat columns of corrected bearings. "This is his hand too, Wren. Underneath your father's notes. Some of these lines are Merrick's original work — the survey he supposedly faked. Your father didn't forge anything. He *rescued* it."

Wren looked at the map, at the two hands braided together across the grease-stained paper — the mentor's precise lines and the old man's stubborn hooks — and felt the shape of the story rearranging itself around her, the way a river rearranges itself around a fallen tree.

"So the company destroyed Merrick to bury the survey," she said slowly, working it through, "and blamed him for the fraud he actually uncovered. And Silas—"

"Took the originals and ran. Kept them hidden for twelve years. Until he was dying, and there was no one left to trust except a stranger off the last train." Theo rubbed his face with both hands. "There's more. There's a second survey hidden somewhere upstream — Silas wrote about it in the margins. Notes, instruments, the physical evidence. If it still exists, it proves everything."

"Then we find it."

Theo looked at her. "We."

"You ran the gorge to keep a promise to a man you met once in a saloon." Wren heard her own voice come out harder than she intended, and softer underneath the hardness than she'd allowed anything to sound in eleven years. "I've spent a season documenting markers I didn't understand, alone, telling myself it didn't matter. You're telling me it matters. So yes. *We.* Unless you'd rather float back downriver and explain to Voss's trackers how you lost the map."

"I'd genuinely rather fight the gorge again."

"There's a brave answer."

"It's not bravery, it's arithmetic. The gorge only nearly killed me. Voss would definitely kill me."

Despite herself — despite everything, the grief and the fury and the decade of silence — Wren laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate hinge in spring, and it surprised them both. Theo smiled, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the river filled the silence the way it fills everything: completely, without trying.

"We leave at first light," she said, standing, brisk again, because feeling things was dangerous and packing was safe. "The second survey — Silas's notes point toward the old burn country above the amphitheater. I know the ground. It'll take two days hard walking."

"Wren." Theo had gone still, looking at something on the map's lower corner. "There's a witness line here. On the original certification block. Two signatures — Merrick's, and a junior surveyor who witnessed the boundary readings." He turned the paper toward her, and in the fading light she read the second name, small and careful in a young man's hand:

*T. Reyes.*

The fire popped. Somewhere upriver, a loon called once and got no answer.

"I signed it," Theo said quietly. "Twelve years ago. I stood on that boundary line and I watched the readings and I signed my name without asking why the numbers didn't match the terrain. I was twenty-four and I wanted to keep my job." He looked up at her, and there was no excuse anywhere in his face. "I helped make your father a fugitive, Wren. I just didn't know it until now."

She should have been angry. Part of her reached for the anger the way you reach for a familiar tool. But she looked at this soaked, scraped, half-drowned man who had carried her father's map through the unrunnable water on nothing but a promise, and what she felt instead was something more complicated and far more useful.

"Then you'll testify," she said. "At the permit hearing. Thirty days. You'll stand up in front of the board and unsign your name."

"Yes," Theo said. No hesitation. It was the first thing anyone had asked of him, she suspected, that he answered that fast.

"Good." She threw him a blanket. "Sleep. First light means first light — I've seen your dawn punctuality and I wasn't impressed."

And it was while they were breaking camp in the gray morning, Wren rolling the two maps together into one oilskin bundle — his father's-hand-in-all-but-blood and her own rubbings, folded against each other like hands clasped — that she looked up and saw, far downstream on the far ridge, a thread of smoke rising where no camp should be.

Trackers didn't build cook fires. But they did signal.

"They crossed the gorge," she said.

Theo followed her gaze, and she watched the color leave his face and the resolve arrive right behind it, close as brothers.

"Then we'd better find that second survey," he said, "before they find us."
