## 12. The Second Survey

The morning after we compared the maps, Wren woke me by dropping a pinecone on my chest.

"Up," she said. "You snore like a landslide. If anyone was within a mile of us, they'd have found us by now and shot us out of simple mercy."

"I don't snore."

"You do. It's the only honest thing about you." She was already rolling her bedroll with the brisk violence of someone who had been awake for an hour and resented everyone who hadn't. "Come look at this."

I came and looked at this.

She had spread both documents on a flat stone beside the cold fire pit — Silas's grease-stained map on the left, her rubbings of the survey markers on the right — and weighted the corners with four stones of suspiciously equal size, which told me she'd spent real time choosing them.

"Here," she said, tapping the map where the river bent north beneath the escarpment's second wall. "My father drew a symbol I couldn't make sense of. A square with a slash through it. I thought it meant bad water, or deadfall, or one of his moods."

"And now?"

"Now look at your mentor's notation style." She slid one of her marker rubbings across. The paper bore the ghost of a stamped aluminum cap: *E.M., 1908*, and beneath it a string of coordinates in a cramped, slanting hand. "Every marker I've found has that same square-and-slash scratched nearby. Small. Easy to miss unless you're looking for it. And every one of them is inside ground the concession permits say is worthless."

I crouched over the papers until my knees complained. The symbols matched. Not approximately — exactly, down to the little hook at the tail of the slash, the way a man crosses a letter he's written ten thousand times.

"E.M.," I said. "Elias Marsh."

"Your mentor."

"My mentor." Saying it aloud made my chest feel strange, as though a splinter I'd carried for years had shifted position. "He taught me everything. Chain measurement, level runs, how to read water before you trust it. He used to say a surveyor's first duty is to the ground itself, not to whoever's paying him to describe it." I laughed, but there was no humor in it. "And then he disappears off the face of the earth in 1909, and Silas Crane takes the blame for losing the original survey of this whole concession, and everyone shakes their heads about what a careless old fool Silas must have been."

Wren's jaw tightened. "Careless old fool," she repeated flatly. "That phrase got around. I heard it from trappers when I was fourteen. My father never once defended himself. He just said, *the river knows where things are, whatever the paper says.* I thought it was senility talking."

"It wasn't senility." I stood, working blood back into my knees. "It was a man carrying something too heavy to put down and too dangerous to explain. Wren — if these markers are where I think they are, then the vein doesn't run under the concession land at all. It runs under land that was never permitted. Which means the official survey didn't just have errors in it. It was drawn to hide the vein entirely."

"Drawn by whom?"

"By somebody who could draw." I looked down at the slanting hand on her rubbing, and my stomach did something slow and unpleasant. "By Elias Marsh. And then Elias vanished, and the company needed somewhere for the missing survey to go, and a half-mad prospector who kept poking around the headwaters was standing right there."

Wren was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone very even, which I was learning was worse than shouting. "Then we find the cabin."

"What cabin?"

"The one the map points to." She tapped the square-and-slash again. "My father didn't just mark hazards, Reyes. He marked *destinations*. Every symbol on this map is a place he went. This one's three miles up the side channel, under the wall, where the alders thin out. He wrote *stone that remembers* next to it. I always thought that was poetry." Her mouth twisted. "It's limestone. Limestone holds tool marks for a hundred years. Stone that remembers."

I stared at her. "How long have you known how to read this?"

"Since I was twelve and decided that if my father wouldn't come home, I'd learn to read his leaving." She began stuffing the documents into the oilskin without waiting for my answer. "Pack up. We're burning daylight, and you're slow."

* * *

The side channel was everything the map promised and less than I hoped. The water ran shallow and argumentative over shale, so we walked the canoe up its spine, Wren hauling the bow line and me pushing from behind, both of us soaked to mid-thigh and pretending not to notice. The escarpment rose on our left all morning, a gray-green rampart dripping with seeps, and the farther upstream we went the more the country seemed to fold itself around us like a hand closing.

"Tell me about him," Wren said, after an hour of nothing but water-noise. "Marsh."

I considered lying, or at least decorating. I'd gotten good at both in the railroad towns. But there was something about a woman who'd learned to read her father's absences at age twelve that made decoration feel obscene.

"He was the best surveyor I ever knew, and the worst-tempered," I said. "He drank coffee that could strip paint and he had a rule: never sign anything you haven't walked. Every line, every boundary, every witness mark — you walk it yourself or you don't put your name to it. He said ink is a promise the ground can hold you to." I stepped over a slick shelf of rock, nearly went down, recovered with what dignity remained. "I signed a lot of paperwork in those years, Wren. Boundaries, closures, abandonment filings. I didn't walk most of them. Nobody does, really. There isn't time, and the company says the field men already verified, and the field men say the office already verified, and round it goes."

"So you broke his rule."

"Everyone broke his rule. That's rather the point of rules like that — they exist so that when the fraud comes along, it has plenty of respectable company to hide in." I heard my own bitterness and was surprised by it. "He'd have flayed me. He probably tried, once or twice, and I thought he was just being old-fashioned."

"Where did he go? After?"

"No one knows. One winter he didn't come back from a solo run in the northern district. Company filed him as resigned. No body, no letter, no forwarding address." I paused. "Silas Crane knew. Silas must have known. Why else would he keep a map in Marsh's notation style?"

"Maybe they worked together," Wren said. "Maybe Marsh drew the true survey, hid it, and gave my father the key to finding it. And when the company realized the true survey was loose—" she shrugged, "—they needed a thief. A drunk old prospector nobody would believe. It's tidy. Tidy like a trap."

We came around a bend where the channel narrowed between two shelves of limestone, and the alders thinned exactly as the map had promised, and there it was.

The wall of the escarpment split. Not dramatically — no thundering canyon mouth, nothing a passing eye would mark. Just a seam, maybe eight feet wide, running back into the rock like a door left ajar a century ago. Alders grew thick across its threshold, but the stems were younger than the ones around them, and beneath them the ground was bare limestone, swept clean by wind funneling through the gap.

"Stone that remembers," I said quietly.

Wren was already pulling alder stems aside. "Somebody's kept this clear," she said. "For years. Look — cut stumps, regrown. My father came here. Regularly." Her voice caught on *regularly*, and I pretended to be very interested in securing the canoe.

The seam opened after forty feet into a hollow — a collapsed sink in the escarpment's shoulder, walled with gray stone, floored with moss and needle-fall, roofed by a lace of branches. Sunlight came down in pale columns. And against the far wall stood a cabin.

It was small, low-roofed, built of split cedar so weathered it had gone silver, with a stone chimney that had been repaired more than once with mortar mixed from the local lime. A root cellar door lay flush in the hillside beside it. Everything about it said *built to last and built to be missed.* From the ridge above, I realized, you would see nothing but forest. From the river, nothing but wall.

Wren stopped at the edge of the clearing and didn't move.

"That's his chimney work," she said. Her voice had gone small. "He taught me to stack stone when I was nine. He had a way of — the way the corners are keyed. Nobody else stacks like that." She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth for a second. Then she straightened, because she was her father's daughter, and walked in.

* * *

The door wasn't locked. It wasn't even latched. Inside, the cabin was dry and dark and smelled of cedar and cold ash and, faintly, of pipe tobacco long smoked — the same smell, I realized with a jolt, that had clung to Silas Crane's coat in that muddy saloon.

There was a bunk with a blanket folded at its foot with military precision. A table. Two chairs. On the table, a tin lamp, a box of matches gone soft with damp, and a ledger bound in oilcloth.

Wren untied the oilcloth with hands that were not quite steady. The ledger fell open, and the light through the doorway fell across the first page, and I forgot how to breathe.

The cramped, slanting hand. The hooked crossings. The heading, ruled off neat as scripture:

*TRUE SURVEY OF THE UPPER RIVER CONCESSION — E. MARSH, SURVEYOR. WITNESSED AND COUNTERSIGNED.*

Below it, page after page, the whole lie undone in careful figures. Boundary descriptions that bore no resemblance to the concession's filed plat. Bearing notes, chain measurements, level runs — thousands of hours of walking, done properly, done the way Marsh had taught me, each line walked and each line sworn. And threaded through it, in a different, looser script I recognized from thirty years of reading railroad correspondence: annotations by a second hand. *Verified. Agrees. See sketch, p. 41.* The handwriting was Silas Crane's.

"They did it together," Wren whispered. She was turning pages with two fingers, gentle as handling a bird. "Marsh surveyed it. My father verified it. They were partners."

"And then the company saw what they'd found," I said, "and realized the vein was worth more than the permit, and more valuable still if nobody knew it existed. So the official survey gets redrawn. The vein gets buried under worthless-paper ground. And the two men holding the true version become a problem." I turned a page and stopped. "Except Marsh didn't run. He *hid*. He built this. He stayed within sight of the thing he'd measured, all these years, keeping it."

"Alone." Wren's voice cracked on it. "All that time. Ten miles from my mother's house, and he never—" She shut the ledger carefully, as if it might bruise.

"Wren—"

"Don't." She said it without heat. "Not yet. Let me be angry at the company today. I'll get to him tomorrow. There's an order to these things."

There was, I supposed. I'd just never followed it myself.

While she sat with the ledger, I explored the rest of the cabin, because grief needs a task and mine has always been inventory. A shelf of tinned goods, ancient. A rack of tools, oiled and orderly. A second oilskin packet on the bunk, which held maps — Marsh's own field sheets, the originals, drawn in the same slanting hand, each corner bearing the square-and-slash. And beneath the bunk, a flat wooden case with a brass latch.

I should have let Wren open it. I didn't think of that until I'd already lifted the lid.

Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, were the instruments. A transit, its brass gone green but its glass intact. A chain coiled in its canvas bag. A notebook of level books. And on top, a leather document wallet, fat with papers, tied with a bootlace.

I untied the bootlace because my hands were doing it before my mind voted.

The top sheet was a witness certificate. Standard form, the kind attached to any filed survey, the kind I'd signed hundreds of times without walking a step. *I hereby certify that I personally observed the following measurements taken upon the ground described herein…*

Elias Marsh, surveyor. Silas Crane, witness.

And below Silas's name, in a younger version of a hand I knew better than my own — because it *was* my own —

*T. Reyes, assistant, witness.*

I sat down on the bunk without meaning to. The bunk creaked. Wren looked over.

"What?" she said. Then, seeing my face: "*What?*"

I held the certificate out. She read it. I watched her read it, watched her eyes reach the third signature and stop there, and I watched her look up at me with an expression I will remember as long as I live — not anger, worse than anger. Recognition.

"You were *there*," she said.

"I don't—" I started, and stopped, because the memory was arriving whether I wanted it or not. A spring morning, nineteen years ago. A limestone hollow I'd half forgotten, a wall with a seam in it, an old man with paint-stripping coffee telling me to hold the chain taut and *look at the ground, boy, not the paper.* A day of measurements I'd assumed were routine practice — a teaching exercise, I'd thought, the old man drilling the new hire. I'd signed the certificate at the end because signing certificates was what you did, the way you breathed, the way you didn't ask questions. I'd walked every chain length of it. I'd done the one honest day of witnessing of my career and never known what it was for, because Marsh never told me, and I never asked, because asking questions in that profession was how you stopped getting assignments.

"I was twenty-three," I said. "He brought me out for what I thought was a training run. We measured boundaries all day. He made me sign the witness form. I signed dozens of forms that year. I never—" My voice failed and restarted. "I never came back. He asked me once, near the end, if I'd ever wondered why some surveys get filed and some get lost. I said I figured paper got lost the way paper does. He looked at me for a long time. Then he said, *That's what worries me about you, Theo. You're good enough to know better.* And three months later he was gone, and I told myself he'd retired, and I believed it because believing it was easier."

Wren set the certificate down on the table with great care, the way you set down something that might go off.

"So the company's story," she said slowly, "is that my father stole the survey. And the truth is that my father and Elias Marsh *made* the survey, and hid it, and the only other living soul who ever touched the original document — who *witnessed* it, whose signature makes it legally real — is sitting on my father's bunk looking sorry for himself."

"That's a fair summary."

"You could have made this true years ago. Your signature. You could have stood up at any hearing, any filing, any tavern argument, and said *I witnessed the original.*"

"I didn't know what I'd witnessed." It sounded like the excuse it was. "And even if I had — Wren, who listens to a railroad man against a company? I'd built my whole living on not asking. You don't get to spend fifteen years not asking and then suddenly have answers."

"No," she agreed. "You don't. You get to spend fifteen years not asking, and then a dying man puts a map in your hands and you finally have to." She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever moved behind her eyes, she kept it there. "You're going to testify. At the hearing. With that signature."

"If I'm alive to."

"You'll be alive." She said it like a weather forecast. "Because I've decided you will, and I'm better at deciding things than you are."

I almost smiled. Then I heard it.

A sound from up on the ridge above the sink — faint, wrong, out of place in that folded country. Not a bird. Not wind. The particular dry clack of a boot sole on loose scree, moving with purpose, and then another, offset from the first. Two sets. Coming down toward the seam in the wall, toward the only entrance either of us knew.

Wren heard it a heartbeat after I did. She blew out the lamp that wasn't lit, which told me everything about how rattled she was, and then she was moving — ledger into oilskin, oilskin into the wooden case, case thrust into my arms.

"Back wall," she breathed. "Root cellar. My father would have had a way out. He *always* had a way out."

From the seam in the wall, drifting down through the alders, came a voice I recognized — smooth, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once hurried anywhere except toward money.

"Mr. Reyes," Mr. Voss called, pleasant as Sunday. "We know you're in there. And I'd very much like to talk about your signature."
