## 13. Riders on the Ridge

### Theo

The cabin smelled of pine pitch and old paper, and for one whole hour Theo Reyes allowed himself to believe they were safe.

He sat at a plank table with the second survey spread before him, weighted at the corners by four river stones Wren had chosen with the seriousness of a surgeon selecting instruments. Morning light came through the single window in a long dusty blade and lay across the document like something holy. And it was, he supposed. Here was his mentor Aldous Merrick's hand — that upright, patient drafting script Theo would have known anywhere, would have known blind, would have known if someone read it to him through a wall. The boundary line ran true here. The vein ran where Silas Crane's grease-stained map said it ran. And there, along the lower margin, small and certain as a confession:

*Witnessed: T. Reyes.*

His own signature. His own younger, lazier hand.

"You keep looking at it like it might apologize," Wren said from the doorway. She had a snare wire in her fingers and was working it into a loop without watching her hands, the way other people whittled.

"It might," Theo said. "Documents have surprised me before."

"Documents don't apologize. Men do." She hung the snare on a peg, then paused, head tilting the way a heron's does before it strikes. "Horses."

He heard nothing. Then he did — a soft iron clink, far off, up on the ridge above the cabin. The sound of a picket pin being driven, or a stirrup shifting. The kind of sound the forest made when it was pretending not to.

"How many?"

"Two. Maybe three." She was already moving, pulling a canvas satchel from under the bunk, sweeping their supplies into it with an economy that told him she'd packed this bag in her head many times before, though never for this reason. "The scree field's north. If we go now, quiet, we're over the ridge before they're off their horses."

Theo looked once more at the survey. He couldn't take all of it — it was too large, too fragile — but he could take the corner sections, the ones bearing the boundary lines and the witness signatures. He folded them with hands that wanted to shake and put them inside his shirt, against his chest, where the oilskin map already lived. Two documents now, riding over his heart like a second set of ribs.

"The rest of it?" Wren asked.

"We burn it."

She looked at him. "You just spent half the morning kissing it."

"I kissed the truth of it. Voss can't be allowed to find the whole sheet intact and claim *he* recovered the original." He was already dragging the little iron stove forward onto the table, opening its door. "If it burns, it burns as evidence destroyed by the men who needed it destroyed. That's a story too."

"That's a cold story."

"It's the only one that keeps us alive."

Outside, up on the ridge, a horse blew hard through its nostrils. Close. Too close. They'd been seen coming down, or they'd been watched the whole time, and every second Theo stood here weighing ethics was a second some hired man was shortening his stirrups.

Wren struck a match off her thumbnail and touched it to the survey's edge. Old paper caught fast, hungry, the flame running up the margins like water finding a channel. She fed it into the stove and shut the door, and smoke began almost immediately to seep out through the chimney crack — which, Theo realized with a lurch, was itself a signal, a gray flag saying *we are here, we are burning things, come quickly.*

"They'll see the smoke," he said.

"I know." Wren took down her father's rifle from the pegs and checked it with two brisk motions. "That's why we're leaving through the alder thicket instead of the scree. Scree's what they'll watch."

"And the alder?"

"The alder is where I go when I don't want my father to find me." Something crossed her face — grief arriving late, the way it always seemed to with her, like mail delivered to the wrong address. "Worked for twenty years. Should work for three more minutes."

* * *

### Wren

The alder took them in like a crowd takes in a fugitive — close, indifferent, full of elbows.

Wren went first, bent double, pushing the whippy branches aside so they wouldn't whip back into Theo's face. Behind her she could hear him breathing, quick and controlled, and the faint papery rustle of the folded survey against his chest. A city man, a railroad man, moving through brush like a man wading through soup — but he kept up, she'd give him that. He kept up and he didn't complain, and when a branch raked across his cheek and drew blood he didn't even say a word about it, just wiped it and came on.

They were fifty yards from the cabin when the first shot cracked.

It wasn't aimed at them. It was aimed at the cabin door, and it hit the door, and the sound of it went through the alders like a stone through a window. Wren dropped flat without deciding to; her body had opinions older than her thoughts. Theo landed beside her in the wet duff, breathing hard.

"They didn't see us," he whispered.

"They saw the smoke. They're announcing themselves." She wormed forward on her elbows. "Company men. They want us to run so they can shoot us running. It looks better in a report."

"You've thought about this a lot."

"I've lived next to these people my whole life. You learn how they talk." She peered back through the stems. Through a gap she could see the cabin's clearing, and two riders coming down into it — big men on heavy horses, rifles across their saddles, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been the prey. One dismounted and kicked open the cabin door. The other sat his horse and looked around the clearing slowly, the way a man reads a page.

Then the mounted one looked directly at the alder thicket.

Not at it, exactly. Near it. But near enough that Wren's heart stopped being a heart and became a bird throwing itself against the inside of her ribs.

"Move," she breathed.

They moved. Not running — running made noise, and noise made bullets — but going fast in the low crouching way she'd learned trailing wounded animals, using every deadfall and every shadow. The ground rose toward the ridge shoulder, and the alders gave way to pine, and the pines gave way at last to the thing she'd been dreading and counting on all at once: the scree.

A whole hillside of it, loose gray stone from fist-size to coffin-lid, tilted forty degrees, waiting to betray anyone who touched it. Above them the ridge line cut the sky. Below and behind, voices — the trackers had found their trail, or guessed it, and were coming up through the pines at a jog.

"There's a gully," Wren said. "Crosses the scree about halfway up. If we make the gully we're below their sightline the rest of the way."

"If we miss the gully?"

"Then we're gray shapes on a gray slope and they're riflemen."

Theo looked at the slope, then at her, and did the thing she was beginning to hate and rely on in equal measure: he nodded and followed her.

The first ten steps were fine. The eleventh was not. A slab the size of a barn door shifted under her boot with a groan like a ship changing its mind, and suddenly the whole hillside was talking — stones clicking, sliding, cascading, a long rattling applause that would carry a mile. Wren froze with her arms out. Below, a voice shouted, sharp and glad.

"Run," Theo said.

They ran. It was like running on a landslide, because it was one; every stride cost twice and bought half. Stones leapt past her knees. Somewhere behind, a rifle spoke and the bullet went *whap* into scree somewhere to her left, spraying grit. Another shot. Then Theo made a sound — not a cry, more a grunt of furious surprise — and stumbled, and Wren turned to see him go down hard, sliding, grabbing for purchase, the folded papers flying free from his shirt and skittering away across the stones like a pale bird.

"No no no—" She threw herself after the papers, caught one corner as it slid past, and the motion pulled her off balance, and the world tilted, and she was sliding too, both of them now, down and sideways in a hissing gray river of stone.

The gully saved them. It came up out of nowhere the way gullies do, a four-foot lip of rooted earth, and they crashed over it into the shadowed cut and lay there gasping among ferns while stones continued to trickle past overhead like the tail end of a sentence.

Theo rolled onto his back. There was a red scrape down one side of his face and his hands were torn and shaking, and he started, absurdly, to laugh.

"What," Wren said, "is funny."

"I used to draw maps of country like this." He held up one bleeding palm. "Neat little lines. Contours. I marked slopes like this one 'unstable, avoid' and I never once — not once — imagined what it felt like to be the pencil point." He laughed again, breathlessly. "I'm the pencil point, Wren. We're all just somebody's pencil point."

"Save the philosophy for flat ground." But she was almost smiling, and she hated that, and she let it happen anyway. She got her knees under her, checked the papers — intact, both folds, damp with sweat — and stuffed them back into his shirt, patting his chest harder than strictly necessary. "Guard these with your ribs. All of them."

Above the gully's rim, boots crunched. Close. Twenty yards, maybe less.

* * *

### Theo

They came up the gully at a crouch, and the gully narrowed, and the narrowing was the problem.

Theo heard them before he saw them — two sets of boots, unhurried now, conversational even. Men who believed the day's work was nearly done.

"—smoke was still coming off it when we topped out. Boss wants ash samples, can you believe that—"

"Boss wants everything. That's why he's boss."

Then around the bend of the cut, ten feet away: the first tracker, a wide man with a beard like a shoe brush, raising his rifle with the mild expression of a man about to shoo a chicken.

What happened next happened in the order that geology intended. Wren kicked the one rock in the entire gully that mattered. It went down the cut, gathered friends, and arrived at the tracker's ankles as an avalanche in miniature. The man went over backward with a yell, his rifle discharging skyward, and his companion — young, freckled, startled — swung his own weapon toward the noise and thereby toward Theo, who did the only thing his body offered: he charged.

It was not brave. It was arithmetic. Ten feet closing at a dead sprint gives a rifleman perhaps one shot, and frightened men shoot high. The shot went high. Then eighty pounds of former railroad engineer hit a nineteen-year-old in the sternum and they went down together into the ferns, and Theo discovered that terror is an excellent teacher of wrestling.

"GUN!" the boy wheezed, thrashing. "GUN, HOLLIS—"

Theo got a knee on the rifle barrel and drove it into the mud. Hands found his collar. Somewhere above, the bearded man — Hollis, presumably — was swearing his way back up the gully with murder in his throat, and Theo understood with perfect clarity that in approximately six seconds this arithmetic was going to stop working.

Then the rifle came free of the mud, because Wren had it, and Wren had never in her life pointed a weapon she wasn't prepared to use.

"Up," she said. "Both of you. Slow."

Hollis froze at the bend of the gully, half-crouched, hands rising by degrees. For a moment nobody breathed. Theo scrambled off the boy and got his feet under him, and the four of them arranged themselves into the oldest picture in the world.

"Now walk backward," Wren said pleasantly, "and think very hard about whether your boss pays enough."

"He pays plenty," Hollis said. His eyes flicked — left, right, measuring. Theo had watched surveyors do that same triangulating glance for fifteen years, and he knew exactly what it meant. *Range. Cover. Angle.* "Plenty enough for two more bodies."

"Hollis," the boy said nervously, "there's two of them and they got the gun—"

"Son, I've shot worse odds than—"

Wren fired past his ear.

She didn't aim at him. Theo would swear afterward, to boards and lawyers and anyone who asked, that she did not aim at him — that the round snapped through the space beside Hollis's head and buried itself in the gully bank with a sound like a wasp hitting a window. But Hollis dropped flat anyway, and the boy bolted, and in the scrambling half-second that followed, Wren grabbed Theo's sleeve and hauled him up-gully at a dead run.

They went over the ridge top in a hail of two wild shots and dropped down the far side into pine shadow, running now with everything they had, the papers hammering against Theo's chest like a second heartbeat.

* * *

### Wren

The raft was Theo's idea, and it was a terrible idea, and it was the only idea.

Below the ridge's far shoulder the river made a long slow bend, deep and green, and on the bank stood the ruins of a trapper's drying rack and a lean-to built — Wren realized as they tore into it — from the same cabin timbers her father must have helped cut thirty years ago. The wood was silvered and sound. Theo fell on it with a borrowed hatchet like a man possessed, prying planks, splitting crosspieces, working with a frantic competence that surprised her until she remembered: he'd built bridges. Bridges were just rafts that had gotten above themselves.

"Rope," he said. She threw him the coil. "Lashings every foot. Water will find any gap we give it."

"Water finds every gap regardless. My father taught me that."

"Your father," Theo said, driving a lashing tight, "taught me more than he ever knew."

They worked without stopping, because behind and above them the woods still carried sounds — horses being led along the ridge, voices calling to each other, the patient industry of men cutting off retreat. Twice Wren glanced upstream and saw, far off, movement at the water's edge, and said nothing, and lashed faster.

The raft was ugly. Eight planks, two crossbeams, a steering sweep lashed at the stern, their remaining gear bundled in the middle and covered with the oilskin. It looked like something the river itself had coughed up. Theo stood back and surveyed it with the critical eye of his old profession.

"She'll float," he decided.

"She'll float *low*."

"Low is floating." He put his hands on his knees, breathing hard, and looked at her, and in the slanting afternoon light she saw how worn he was — the scraped face, the ruined hands, the gray coming in early at his temples. "Wren. If they catch us on open water, we can't run and we can't hide. Everything we have — the map, your rubbings, those pages — it all goes in the river or goes to Voss. You understand that."

"I understand that you keep explaining things to me like I'm the one who needs convincing." She shouldered her pack. "Get on the raft, surveyor."

They pushed off into the current, and the river took them the way it took everything — without malice, without mercy, entirely according to its own opinion. The raft rode better than it deserved. The bend carried them out of sight of the ridge, and the ridge's silence closed behind them like a door.

For a while neither spoke. The water talked instead, that endless gravel-voiced mutter. Wren sat with her father's rifle across her knees and watched the banks slide past, and thought about the sound the survey pages had made burning, and about her father's handwriting on a map she'd hated for twenty years, and about how the two men who'd shaped her whole life — the one who left and the one who'd signed the lie — were both sitting in her history now, one dead and one dripping wet beside her, trying to make it right with their hands.

"The hearing," Theo said quietly. "Nine days. Whatever happens now, whatever they take from us — the hearing is where this ends. One way or the other."

"Then we'd better arrive with something left to say."

"We will." He looked at her, and there was no doubt in his face, and she found — to her considerable irritation — that his certainty was load-bearing. She leaned on it without meaning to.

They rounded the next bend together, raft groaning, river hurrying, and Wren saw what the river had been keeping from them: downstream, where the valley narrowed, a boat sat anchored midstream. Company gray. Two men aboard. And on the far bank, a third figure on horseback, riding parallel, keeping easy pace.

Waiting for them, patient as the water, all the way down.

"Well," Wren said, and checked the rifle's action with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a period at the end of a sentence. "So much for floating low."
