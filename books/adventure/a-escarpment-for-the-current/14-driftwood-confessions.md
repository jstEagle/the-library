## 14. Driftwood Confessions

The raft moved the way grief moves — slowly, and always in the same direction.

Theo had built it from the cabin's floorboards and roof planks, lashed with the same tarred cord Silas Crane had apparently used to lash everything he'd ever owned, and it rode the river like a drunk uncle at a wedding: functional, embarrassing, and impossible to abandon. There was no rudder worth the name, only a steering oar that fought him at every bend, and no shelter except a lean-to of canvas that had once been the cabin's door. Wren lay beneath it, shivering through her fever, her shoulder bound in strips of Theo's last clean shirt boiled in river water because there had been nothing else and no time to find anything else.

The bullet had gone through the meat below her collarbone, high and clean, which was the only mercy the morning on the ridge had offered. Clean, the old trappers said, meant you might live. Dirty meant you certainly wouldn't. Theo had cleaned it as best he could with whiskey from Hark's cache flask and a needle he'd held over a match flame while his hands shook worse than hers did. She hadn't cried out. She had watched him work with an expression of furious concentration, as though the wound were a survey error she intended to have corrected on paper later.

Now, two days downstream, the fury had burned down to fever, and the fever talked.

"Third bend," she murmured, eyes closed against the canvas shade. "He always said third bend. You have to read the water before the water reads you."

Theo eased the oar through the current, keeping them wide of a strainer of drowned alders. "Your father?"

"He wrote it down." Her hand moved on the blanket, tracing letters only she could see. "Everything down. Like the river was keeping minutes."

The current took them around the bend, and the escarpment rose on their left, gray and enormous, wearing its crown of pine like a man too proud to take off his hat in church. Theo looked at it and felt the familiar ache of a man looking at country he'd helped erase. Somewhere up in that stone, if the second survey told true, ran a vein of ore rich enough that men in county seats had decided a river could be reclassified out of existence. He had signed papers that made it so. He had signed them without reading past the signature line, because his mentor Aldous Merrick had laid them in front of him and said, *Theo, boy, the board wants this by Friday,* and Friday had come, and Theo had wanted to be the kind of engineer the board trusted.

He thought about that a great deal now, poling a raft of stolen floorboards through country he'd helped condemn, while the daughter of the man his company had ruined burned with fever behind him.

* * *

Toward evening the river widened and slowed, and Theo found a gravel bar where the current braided shallow enough to ground the raft without wrecking it. He beached them, gathered wood, got a fire going small and smokeless the way Silas's marginal notes had taught him — *green boughs over coals, keep it low, the upper river has ears* — and made broth from the last of the dried fish and a handful of wild onions he'd dug at noon. The smell of it woke Wren partway.

"You're burning it," she said.

"It's broth."

"That's what my father said when he burned things." She tried to sit up, failed, and let him prop her against the pack. Her face was flushed and hollowed at once, sweat standing on her forehead, but her eyes found him with terrible clarity. "Theo Reyes. Railroad man." She said it like a diagnosis. "You look like a man rehearsing a speech."

"I don't have a speech."

"Everyone who says that has a speech." She accepted the tin of broth and drank, wincing when the heat reached somewhere raw inside her. "Go on, then. Say whatever it is before the fever takes me again, because I'll tell you now, I say things in fevers I mean more than anything I say awake, and I'd rather hear yours first for once."

Theo fed another green bough to the fire and watched the smoke crawl low along the gravel. The river went on talking to itself, patient, unhurried, the way it had been talking since before any of them were born and would go on after. It seemed as good a congregation as any.

"When I was twenty-six," he said, "I signed a boundary document."

Wren said nothing. She had the stillness of her father about her, he realized — the stillness of people who listen with their whole bodies.

"It was the Kestrel concession extension. My first big filing. Aldous Merrick brought the papers himself, which should have been the first warning, because Aldous didn't carry paper, Aldous sent boys to carry paper. But he set it on my desk and he said the board needed it countersigned by Friday, and he stood there while I read the first page, and I—" Theo stopped. Started again. "I read the first page. The first page was fine. It was all coordinates and legal description and it was fine. And then he said, *You've read it, then,* and I said yes, and I signed pages two through eleven without reading a word, because he was Aldous Merrick, because he'd taught me everything, because refusing him felt like telling your own father you'd checked his arithmetic."

The fire popped. Downriver, a loon called, one long descending note, like a question nobody would answer.

"What was on those pages?" Wren asked quietly.

"The boundary." Theo turned his hands over and looked at them, these hands that had drawn thousands of miles of line, true lines, good lines, and one line that wasn't. "The real vein runs under the north bank. Under land the permits never covered. The document I signed moved the boundary south — drew it along the wrong side of the river, neat as you please, with a datum shift buried in paragraph four that I would have caught if I'd read it, because datum shifts are *my subject*, Wren, it's the one thing I'm actually good at, and I missed it because I trusted the man handing me the pen." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Eleven pages. Thirty seconds of ink. And a river erased, and a prospector named a thief, and thirty years of a man's life written off as a lie."

Wren had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice had a strange doubled quality, half fever, half something colder underneath. "Silas used to write me. Every year. A letter, on my birthday."

Theo looked up.

"They never came," she said. "Not one. Twenty-two birthdays, and not one letter. My mother said he was dead, then she said worse than dead, then she stopped saying anything. And every year I'd think — maybe this year. Maybe the mail finally found me." Her breath caught, ragged. "And now you're telling me the reason there were no letters is that the world agreed my father was a liar, and liars don't get to write to their daughters, because who'd believe—"

"Wren—"

"No." She pressed her good hand flat against her sternum, as if holding something in place. "No, wait. That's not how I know. I don't know how I know. The fever knows things." She laughed, thin and unsteady. "The fever told me last night that the river keeps minutes. Maybe the fever also kept copies."

She sagged back against the pack, exhausted by the effort of being angry, and Theo sat with the oar across his knees and felt the confession sit between them like a third person at the fire.

"I can't undo it," he said. "I want to be clear about that, because I've spent three days wanting to say something that would undo it, and there isn't one. I signed. The fraud went through. Men I never met got rich off a line I drew blind, and your father spent thirty years carrying the blame for a forgery he didn't commit, and I carried—" He stopped. "I carried nothing. That's the worst part. I carried nothing at all. I forgot. Do you understand? I forgot I'd ever signed it. It was one Friday among ten thousand Fridays until I saw his handwriting on that grease-stained map and remembered the whole thing in about four seconds, all of it arriving at once, like a train you hear coming for years and years and then it's just suddenly *there*, on top of you."

"The train metaphor," Wren murmured. "From a railroad man. How original."

Despite everything, Theo huffed something that was almost a laugh. "I deserve that."

"You deserve worse," she said, but her voice had lost its edge, gone soft and fever-loose. "But you're the first one who's confessed anything. Voss offered me money once, did you know that? Two years ago. Came up the river dressed like Sunday, offered me cash to stop poking around the concession markers. Said my father had been a troubled man who'd embarrassed himself. I threw a fish at him." She smiled faintly at the memory, then it faded. "He wasn't embarrassed, though. He was frightened. I could see it. Frightened men offer money. Honest men offer answers."

The fire burned down to coals. Theo added wood, kept it low, green boughs over the top. Above the escarpment the first stars were coming out, hard and white, and the river turned silver-dark and kept moving, hauling their little raft of confessions toward wherever rivers decide such things end up.

"Tell me about the letters," he said. "The ones you never got."

"There's nothing to tell. They never existed." She shifted, winced, settled. "Except I dreamed about them. Isn't that stupid? All those years, I dreamed there was a sack of them somewhere. Brown paper. Tied with twine. Sitting in some depot nobody worked at anymore, on a shelf, waiting." She opened her eyes and looked at him across the fire, and the clarity in them was awful and total. "You worked depots."

"I surveyed them," Theo said carefully. "Lines to them, mostly. Closures, mostly."

"Closures." She tasted the word. "That's what you do. You close things."

"It's what I did."

"And now?"

Theo looked at the river. "Now I'm drifting downstream on a murdered man's floorboards, carrying his map to his daughter, who already found the source without me, and who took a bullet meant for evidence I helped bury. So I suppose now I'm trying to open one."

The silence that followed was long, but it wasn't hostile. It was the silence of two people discovering they're in the same boat in every sense, which is either a tragedy or a beginning depending entirely on the weather.

"My father," Wren said at last, drowsily, "used to say that a map is just a promise you make to strangers. That every line on it says *I walked here, and I'm telling you true.*" Her eyes were closing. "He'd have liked you, I think. In twenty years. After he finished being furious."

"That seems fair."

"Don't let me sleep too deep. Fever's worse when I sleep too deep." But even as she said it she was going under, pulled down by blood loss and exhaustion and the strange permission of having been told the truth at last. Her breathing lengthened. The fire ticked. The loon called again, farther off, and got its answer this time from somewhere upstream.

Theo sat watch. He took out Silas's map, unwrapped it from its oilskin, and studied it by firelight — the sure strokes, the marginal notes in that cramped, patient hand, the little annotations that had predicted every hazard from the first rapids to the gorge: *shallow gravel, drag left; deadfall portage, 300 yards, mosquitoes biblical; do not trust the east channel after rain.* A promise made to strangers. He had been a stranger, and the map had kept every promise to him, and the man who made it had died on a saloon floor believing his daughter hated him.

"Not if I can help it," Theo said aloud, to the fire, to the river, to nobody.

Because he knew something Wren didn't yet, something he'd found folded into the oilskin with the map, a letter Silas had made him swear to deliver and that Theo hadn't yet had the heart — or the courage — to hand over. It was addressed in the same cramped hand: *For Wren, when she stops being angry at me.* Theo had read exactly one line of it, by accident, checking that it was what Silas said it was, and that one line had undone him completely, and he had rewrapped it and not spoken of it since.

Tomorrow, he thought. If the fever breaks tomorrow, I'll give it to her. Tonight she needs sleep, and I need to be worth trusting first.

He banked the fire, checked her bandage, and settled against the pack with the oar across his knees.

* * *

He must have slept, because he woke to moonlight and to Wren sitting up, pale as the gravel, eyes bright with fever and something else.

"Theo." Her voice was hoarse but level. "The fever broke about an hour ago, and I've been lying here listening to you talk in your sleep, and I need you to know two things." She held up fingers. "One: you apologized to somebody named Aldous approximately forty times, and honestly, he doesn't deserve it. Two—" The second finger trembled slightly. "Two, you said there's a letter. From my father. That you're carrying."

The river slid past, silver and indifferent. Somewhere far downstream — or perhaps upstream, it was impossible to tell in the dark — Theo heard a sound that froze him mid-breath: the low, steady knock of an engine-driven boat, working its way upriver against the current.

Wren heard it too. Her eyes went wide and clear.

"Tell me that's Hark," she whispered.

Theo reached for the map, and the oilskin, and the letter he'd sworn to deliver, and said, "We're about to find out whose side the river's on tonight."
