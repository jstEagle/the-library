## 15. Hark Breaks Ranks

Hark had been in the outfitter business for twenty-six years, and in that time he had learned three things worth passing on: never sell a greenhorn a canoe he can't carry, never trust a man who polishes his boots before breakfast, and never — under any circumstances — let the company men know exactly how much you know.

He had broken all three rules within the span of a single week.

The third one was the one that was going to get him killed.

He stood behind his counter that morning, ostensibly inventorying coils of rope, actually watching Mr. Voss's two hired riders through the front window. They'd taken up residence on his porch like a pair of crows on a fence rail, and they had the same patient, ugly confidence about them. The tall one — Fenner, he'd learned — was whittling something. He'd been whittling the same stick for two days and hadn't produced anything but a growing pile of shavings, which told Hark the man wasn't whittling to make a thing. He was whittling to be seen whittling. It was a message, the way everything about these men was a message.

*We're not leaving. We're comfortable. We have nowhere else to be.*

The shorter one, whose name Hark had never bothered learning because he answered only to "hey," was asleep against the porch post with his hat over his face.

Hark counted rope coils he didn't need to count and thought about Theo Reyes.

It had been eleven days since the engineer had walked out of this very store with a secondhand canoe, forty pounds of flour and salt pork, a coil of line, and a look on his face that Hark had seen maybe four times in twenty-six years. It was the look of a man who had already decided how he was going to die and found it acceptable. Hark had sold him the good tarp anyway. No sense sending a man upriver into weather with a leaky roof over his head just because he'd made peace with the river.

And now here were Voss's men, camped on his porch, waiting for word that would let them go collect whatever was left of him.

"You want coffee?" Hark called through the screen door. "I'm making coffee."

Fenner didn't look up from his stick. "We're fine."

"It's good coffee. I roast it myself."

"We're *fine*, old man."

Old man. Hark was fifty-one. He made the coffee anyway, loud, banging the pot around more than strictly necessary, and while it percolated he looked at the back of his own store — at the storeroom door, and beyond it the loading dock, and beyond that the alder thicket that ran down to the water where he kept a cache that no living soul knew about except one dead prospector who used to trade him beaver pelts for tobacco.

Silas Crane. Rest his ornamental soul.

Hark poured two cups, carried one out to the porch, and set it on the rail next to Fenner without a word. Then he went back inside and sat down at his ledger and did what he'd been doing for three days, which was losing an argument with himself.

The argument went like this:

*Side one:* You have a license. You have a store. You have a bad knee and a good reputation and a daughter in the county seat who thinks you're a respectable man. These men work for people who own half the valley's paper. Stay out of it. Sell them nothing, tell them nothing, wait them out. Companies are like weather; they blow through eventually.

*Side two:* You sold that man a canoe. You watched him walk down to the water carrying a dead man's promise, and you said nothing when you could have said plenty, and now there are two armed men on your porch who mean to follow him upriver and do something to him that will require a shovel afterward.

*Side one again:* Not your fight.

*Side two:* You took Silas Crane's pelts every winter for fifteen years and listened to him talk about his girl the whole time, and you never once told him to go home to her, which makes you complicit in at least one man's loneliness, and you'll be damned if you'll add a second name to the list.

Hark had been losing arguments to himself his whole life. It was why he was fifty-one years old and still running a store instead of retired on a porch somewhere. But this particular argument had stopped being an argument sometime around dawn, and what remained was just arithmetic.

Two men on the porch. One river. One raft, if Theo had gotten as far as Hark figured he had — and Hark figured he had, because the map Silas drew led through water that got too shallow for anything bigger, and a man alone with a shattered canoe builds a raft or he drowns, and Theo Reyes struck Hark as the kind of stubborn that floats.

Two men. One rifle apiece, probably. Maybe a shotgun between them.

One Hark, one boat, and a lifetime of knowing every bend of that river better than any man alive, including the ones who owned its paper.

He closed the ledger.

* * *

The trouble with breaking ranks, Hark reflected that evening, was that it required a certain amount of theater first, and Hark had never been much of an actor. His whole performance consisted of being gruff, which fortunately came naturally.

"They're getting restless," Fenner said, appearing in the doorway as Hark banked the stove. "Voss wants movement tomorrow. You're going to take us upriver."

"Am I," Hark said.

"You know the water. We don't." Fenner leaned against the doorframe and smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has been told he's charming by people afraid of him. "Company's prepared to make it worth your while. And to make things unpleasant otherwise. License, lease, the building itself — all of it sits on paper somebody else holds, old man. You understand how paper works."

"I understand paper," Hark said. "I also understand the upper river's in spring flood and I've got one boat."

"So we take your boat."

"And I stay here."

Fenner considered that. "Sure. You stay here."

Which was, of course, the whole plan, laid out plain as a survey line: use Hark's knowledge, take Hark's boat, leave Hark stranded on the bank to watch his own livelihood float away with the men who'd come back down it. Hark nodded along like a man agreeing to terms, and inside his chest something settled — that cold, clean feeling he remembered from the one winter he'd spent trapping, the moment before you spring the trap. Not fear, exactly. Just the understanding that the next thing you did could not be undone.

"All right," he said. "First light. There's a stretch above the burned bridge where the channel splits and you'll want my eyes on the helm. Sleep well."

Fenner grinned and went out onto the porch to tell his sleeping partner the news.

Hark banked the stove, then went out the back, across the dock, and down into the alders in the dark, moving slow on his bad knee, carrying a lantern with the wick turned so low it was barely a coal. The cache was an oilcloth-wrapped crate sunk beneath a root mass at the waterline, and he hauled it up gasping, and by lantern light he wrote the note he'd been composing in his head for three days.

*REYES — IF YOURE ALIVE TO READ THIS, TWO MEN COMING UP AFTER YOU BY BOAT. FENNER IS THE TALL ONE, SHOOTS FIRST AND APOLOGIZES NEVER. THEYLL REACH THE BURNED BRIDGE BY TOMORROW MIDDAY. TAKE THE EAST CHANNEL AT THE SPLIT — ITS LOW THIS TIME OF YEAR AND THEIR DRAFT WONT CLEAR IT. I AM COMING UP BEHIND THEM WITH MY SUPPLY BOAT AND I INTEND TO BE AN INCONVENIENCE. FORGIVE ME FOR NOT SAYING ANY OF THIS WHEN YOU WERE STANDING IN MY STORE BUYING FLOUR LIKE AN HONEST MAN. SOME LESSONS COME LATE. — H.*

He sealed it in a tin, weighted it, and wedged it into the fork of the biggest alder at eye height, exactly where Silas Crane had taught him, years ago, to leave messages for men coming upriver — because Silas had always assumed, correctly as it turned out, that someday someone would need to come upriver after him.

Then Hark went back to his store, loaded his supply boat by lantern light with the unhurried thoroughness of a man packing for a long trip — flour, salt pork, coffee, bandages, the good whiskey he saved for funerals, and, after a moment's consideration, a second rifle — and lay down in the loft without undressing.

He slept fine. That surprised him most of all.

* * *

They left at first light, as promised. Fenner took the bow with a rifle across his knees; the short one — Hark had finally learned his name was Dill, which somehow made him worse — worked the stern line. Hark steered.

For six miles Hark gave them honest piloting. He ran the riffles clean, lined the boat past the sweeper, and talked the whole time, because talking was his gift and his camouflage. He told them about the flood of '09. He told them about the moose that had once swum the narrows alongside his boat for a quarter mile, regarding him with what Hark maintained was professional courtesy. Dill laughed twice despite himself. Fenner didn't laugh at anything.

At the split below the burned bridge, Hark pointed the bow east.

"Channel's faster this side," he lied, "but it saves an hour."

It did not save an hour. What it saved was Hark's conscience, because the east channel ran shallow over gravel bar for nearly two miles, and forty minutes in, with a grinding crunch that Hark felt in his teeth, the boat came to rest on a bar with six inches of water on either side of it and a mile of it ahead.

"What is this?" Fenner said, standing up in the bow, and the boat rocked, and Hark held the tiller and wore the expression of profound regret he'd practiced in the mirror that morning.

"Low water," he said. "Must've dropped since Thursday."

"You said—"

"I said it saved an hour. In June it does. Rivers change, son. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anybody learns."

Dill was already over the side, hauling at the bow line, boots sucking in gravel. Fenner stood in the shallows glaring upstream as though the river had done it deliberately, which, in Hark's private opinion, it may well have.

That was when Hark stepped back into the boat, shoved off with one oar, and poled away downstream into deeper water.

It took Fenner a full five seconds to understand what he was seeing. Hark counted them. He wanted to remember them.

"*Hey!*" Fenner's voice cracked across the water. "*OLD MAN!*"

"Sorry about your boots!" Hark called back, and meant it — Dill had lost both to the mud, and no man deserved that. "There's a trail out on the west bank, two miles down! Follow it north and you'll hit the road by dark!"

Then he put the tiller hard over and let the current take him, and the last thing he heard was Fenner firing one furious shot at the sky, which told Hark everything he needed to know about the man's discipline and confirmed everything he needed to know about his marksmanship.

His hands shook for the next mile. He let them. Nobody was watching, and a man ought to be allowed to shake after a thing like that.

* * *

He found the smoke first — a gray thread standing up off the south bank where the river bent beneath a wall of scree — and then the raft, pulled up among the willows, built of cabin timbers and lashed with a skill that spoke well of whoever had built it and ill of their available materials.

And then the people, which was the part Hark had not fully planned for, because he had imagined finding Theo Reyes alone, and instead there were three figures rising out of the willow brush: the engineer, gaunt and river-worn; a big quiet man Hark recognized with a start as the packer Marta's boy from two valleys over, holding a rifle with the uncomfortable grip of a man holding it for someone else; and a young woman with her arm bound in a bloody sling, standing with her weight on her good side and her chin up, looking at Hark's boat with an expression he could only describe as *auditing*.

"That's far enough," she said.

"Wren Crane," Hark said. "You've got your father's squint."

Her chin stayed up but something moved behind her eyes. "Who are you?"

"Hark. Outfitter, lower landing. Sold your daddy tobacco for fifteen years and never once charged him full price, which he never noticed, which tells you what kind of businessman he was." He eased the boat toward the bank. "I've got news, coffee, flour, bandages, and a bottle of the good stuff. Also two men behind me on foot who'll reach this bend by tomorrow noon if they find the trail, which they might, because the tall one is mean but not stupid."

Theo Reyes came down to the waterline. Up close he looked like a man who had been wrung out and hung to dry. "Hark," he said slowly. "You wrote the note."

"Cache in the alders. Did you get it?"

"Took the east channel because of it. Nearly drowned anyway." Theo almost smiled. "The map called it unrunnable."

"The map was right. You're lucky."

"I'm aware."

Wren hadn't moved. She was still looking at him the way she'd been looking at him, and Hark realized, with the small cold shock of a man reading his own bill of sale, that she was deciding whether to trust him, and that her decision mattered more than Fenner's rifle, more than Voss's paper, more than anything else on this river. Silas Crane's daughter had grown up into a woman who weighed men in the hand like ore samples, and Hark understood in his bones that her father had died of exactly this — of being weighed and found light.

So he did the only thing he could think of. He reached under the thwart, brought up the bottle of funeral whiskey, and held it out.

"He asked me once," Hark said, "about nine years back, whether I thought you'd take it as a bribe if he sent you a winter coat through the post. I told him coats aren't bribes. He sent it anyway. Did it fit?"

The silence went on long enough that the river filled it. Then Wren's shoulders came down half an inch, which from her was a landslide.

"It fit," she said. "I wore it four winters. I told myself it was from somebody else."

"He'd have wanted you to have it from him."

"I know that *now*." Her voice caught on the last word and she turned it into a cough, badly, and Hark had the grace to look at the water while she recovered. When she looked back up her eyes were wet and furious and decided. "Get the boat tied off, Mr. Hark. Tell me about these two men. And then tell me why an outfitter with a license to lose just threw it in the river."

"Because I've been losing arguments with myself all week," Hark said, "and I finally won one."

* * *

They made camp in the willows, and over coffee and fried salt pork — which disappeared with a speed that told Hark how long it had been since any of them had eaten properly — he gave them the news, and the news was heavy enough that he made them sit down for it.

"Permit hearing's in nine days," he said. "County seat. Full board, public record, the works. Voss means to have those permits renewed before anyone can file a challenge, and here's the part that matters—" he tapped the table board of the raft, "—he's not waiting for the hearing to settle the question. There's a logging crew staged at the lower camp. Forty men, saws, blasting powder. Word I got before I left is they move on the source country day after tomorrow. Fell the timber, strip the banks, and by the time the board meets, whatever's up there will be stumps and mud, and a challenge filed against a wasteland isn't worth the stamp."

"Nine days," Theo said. He was staring at the fire. "The source is four days' run from here even with a good boat and luck."

"Three and a half with my boat," Hark said, "if we run the narrows by moonlight and nobody objects."

"Nobody's going to object," Wren said, "because nobody's going to be awake enough to."

"There's a problem," Hark admitted. "Fenner and Dill. On foot, behind us. If they reach the lower camp and report where we are, Voss can send the crew upriver to meet us head-on, and my boat doesn't outrun forty men with rifles."

"Then they don't reach the lower camp," Wren said.

Everyone looked at her. She was pale from the wound and steady as a survey stake, and she pointed upstream with her good arm.

"The narrows pinch down to thirty yards with walls on both sides. One boat, running dark, draws no attention. Two men walking a trail in daylight—" she shrugged. "My father trapped that country twenty years. There's a place above the pinch where the scree comes down to the water. Loose rock, steep grade. A man with a lever action could keep two men pinned in that draw until doomsday, and I am a *very* good shot with my left hand, and I've got nothing to do for nine days but heal."

"You'll do no such thing," Theo started.

"She'll do exactly that," Hark said, "or some version of it, because telling that woman what she'll do is like telling the river which way to run," and he was pleased to see the ghost of a grin cross Theo Reyes's exhausted face, because it was the first proof Hark had seen that the man had a sense of humor buried in there somewhere, under all that guilt.

They worked out the plan by firelight, passing the whiskey, arguing happily about channel lines and sightlines and who would sleep when. The big packer — Alden, his name was — turned out to know the west bank trails and volunteered, quietly, to shadow Fenner and Dill rather than let Wren do it alone, and Wren accepted this with the air of a queen granting a favor, and Hark liked her enormously.

Late, when the others had rolled into their blankets, Theo stayed up with Hark at the fire. For a while neither of them said anything. The river talked instead, the way it always did, patient and repetitive, like an old man retelling his favorite story.

"Why'd you do it?" Theo asked finally. "Come up after us. You had a store. A life."

Hark fed a stick to the fire and thought about it honestly, because the man had earned honesty.

"When I was young I ran traplines with my uncle," he said. "Good country, up the Little Fork. Then the company bought the paper and posted it, and my uncle spent two years writing letters and filing objections, and every letter came back stamped and polite, and in the end they logged it all and the creek silted up and the marten left and my uncle took to drink and died of it. I was nineteen. I learned that paper beats people unless people show up with better paper." He looked across the fire at Theo. "You're the first man I've met in thirty years who might have better paper. Seemed worth a boat ride."

Theo nodded slowly. "Nine days," he said.

"Nine days," Hark agreed. "So drink your coffee and get some sleep, engineer. Tomorrow we run the narrows by moonlight, and day after that—" he pointed his cup upstream, into the dark where the escarpment stood unseen, holding up the sky, "—we introduce Mr. Voss's logging crew to the fastest water in the territory, and we pray to God they can't swim."

Upstream, faint on the night wind, came a sound none of them could have heard yet but all of them would remember: distant, rhythmic, patient as the river itself. The first ring of an axe, far up the valley, falling on green timber.

Hark heard it a heartbeat before the others did, and watched their faces change, and knew that whatever rest any of them had been counting on was finished.
