## 16. The Forbidden Source

*Theo*

The river had been getting younger all morning.

That was the only way Theo could describe it — the water running clearer, quicker, more certain of itself, as though it hadn't yet learned the weight it would carry downstream. They had left the raft at the last bend where the channel split into threads too thin to float a hatbox, and now they walked the gravel spine of the riverbed itself, boots in the shallows, packs high on their shoulders. Wren led. She had been leading for two days without saying so, the way she did everything: without announcement, without permission asked.

Hark brought up the rear with the supply boat's remaining gear lashed into a pack frame that creaked like an old floor. He had not stopped grumbling since dawn, but Theo had learned that Hark's grumbling was simply the sound of a man content. When Hark went quiet, you worried.

"Spring country," Wren said, stopping so suddenly that Theo nearly walked into her pack. "Feel it?"

He did. The air had changed — cooler by degrees, damp, carrying a mineral smell like struck flint and wet stone. The escarpment rose ahead of them in a long gray wall, and somewhere along its base the river was being born.

They climbed through alder and then through limestone rubble, the footing treacherous, every stone wearing a slick coat of moss. Twice Theo reached back to steady Wren and twice she was already steady, already three steps ahead, her wounded shoulder held close but her balance perfect. The fever had broken two nights ago. She didn't talk about the shoulder. She talked about the ground instead, reading it aloud as they went — a bear trail here, old burn there, a seam of pale rock that meant water underneath.

"There," she said.

Theo came up beside her and forgot how to breathe.

The escarpment curved inward here, a great half-moon of gray limestone fifty feet tall, weathered into columns and hollows, hung with ferns that caught the midday light and turned it green-gold. And from beneath the wall — from a dark mouth in the rock no wider than a wagon — the river rose. Not trickled. *Rose*, full-bodied and cold, spilling over a shelf of stone into a pool so clear that the bottom seemed painted on the air above it. From the pool, the water gathered itself and went away south, becoming everything they had paddled, portaged, bled over.

The source. Silas Crane's promised ground.

"It's real," Wren said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like someone reading words off a page she didn't believe. "All this way and it's real."

"It was always real," Theo said. "That was the whole crime."

She looked at him then, and he wished he'd kept his mouth shut, because what was in her face wasn't wonder anymore. It was arithmetic. A father who had walked out of her life chasing this. Thirty years of letters unsent, birthdays missed, a girl grown hard in trapline cabins learning to hate a man for a thing that turned out to be true and beautiful and stolen from him before he could ever bring it home.

Wren stepped down onto the flat apron of stone beside the pool, and that was when Theo saw them.

Stakes.

Fresh-cut stakes, white wood still bright against the stone, driven into the earth in a ring around the spring — around the pool, the amphitheater mouth, the fern-hung walls. He counted as he walked the circle. Fourteen stakes, each one flagged with strips of orange surveyor's ribbon, each one stamped near the head with the same brand he'd found in the ashes of the burned bridge: a diamond enclosing the letters *N.C.C.* New Caledonia Concession Company.

At the head of the ring, facing the spring like a preacher facing his congregation, stood a wooden placard nailed to a post:

**CLEARANCE ZONE 7 — FELL AND GRUB BY PERMIT EXPIRY. NO ENTRY WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION.**

"They've been here recently," Theo said quietly. He knelt at the nearest stake. The earth around it was still dark, still disturbed; chips of fresh-cut wood lay scattered like snow. He pressed his thumb into the soil and it gave easily, cool and damp. "Days. Maybe a week."

"A week." Wren laughed, one short ugly note. "A week. My father spent thirty years walking toward this place and they've had a crew out here with mauls and ribbon, marking it up for the saws like it's a fence line."

"Wren—"

"No." She held up her good hand. "Don't. Don't tell me to be calm. I've been calm since the gorge. I've been calm since the cabin, since the bullet, since the raft, since my shoulder went septic and I hallucinated my own funeral. I have been *extremely* calm." She pulled the hatchet from her belt. "I am done being calm."

She crossed to the placard and put the hatchet through it in one stroke, splitting the post clean. The board clattered against the stones. Somewhere in the amphitheater wall, water dripped in answer, patient as a clock.

Theo let her have the moment. Then he said, "We need those stakes standing."

She turned on him, eyes bright with fury, and he watched her work it through — fury, then reason, then the grim discipline he was beginning to recognize as her native tongue.

"Evidence," she said finally.

"Evidence. Every stake, every ribbon, every stamp, photographed and copied before anyone comes to pull them. If we cut them down, Voss's lawyers will say we fabricated the whole thing. If they stand, and we can prove they were here, dated—" He patted the oilskin wallet inside his jacket, where Silas's map lived alongside the pages of the original second survey they'd rescued from the burning cabin. "Then the board has to look at it."

Wren stared at the ring of white stakes a long moment. Then she drove the hatchet into the post stump — pinning the wrecked placard upright, a trophy — and slung off her pack.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm keeping the sign."

* * *

*Wren*

Her father had described it to her exactly once, when she was nine years old and still believed everything he said.

*"There's a bowl of stone under the big wall, chickadee, and the river starts right in the middle of it like the country cracked open and let the water out. Cold enough to hurt your teeth. You'll see it someday."*

She had waited eleven years for someday. Then she had stopped waiting and started hating, which was easier and required less packing.

Now she stood in the bowl of stone and the water was exactly as cold as he'd promised — she'd waded in to her knees just to know, and come out gasping, laughing, furious at herself for laughing — and her father was dead in a muddy saloon four hundred miles downstream, and the only inheritance he'd left her was a grease-stained map and a stranger who kept his promises better than any blood relation ever had.

She should have felt peace. That was what the books said. Instead she felt something crack open in her chest that had nothing to do with limestone, and Wren Crane — who had not cried since she was twelve and had sworn it on a Bible she didn't otherwise consult — sat down on a warm flat rock in the middle of her father's promised ground and wept like the spring itself had come up through her.

She hated it. She did it anyway.

Reyes had the decency to be busy. He moved around the ring of stakes with Hark's little box camera, working methodically, photographing each stake from two angles with a ruled notebook laid beside it for scale, calling out numbers in a low voice — *stake seven, bearing off the pool, forty feet, brand legible* — while Hark copied the stamps into a ledger with the careful hand of a man writing down something he intended to testify to. Neither of them looked at her. She understood it was a kindness, and the understanding made her cry harder, which was ridiculous, and she cried about that too, until finally there was nothing left and she wiped her face on her sleeve and got up and went to work.

Because there was work. There was always work, and work was the thing that held her together when nothing else would.

While Reyes photographed, she took rubbings. Charcoal stub and tracing paper, the same technique she'd used on the mystery markers all summer — the ones that shouldn't exist, the ones that had started this whole terrible journey before Silas's map ever reached it. She worked stake by stake, pressing paper to the branded wood and rubbing until the diamond-and-letters came up dark and undeniable. Fourteen rubbings. Fourteen photographs. Between them, Hark measured the ring and sketched it, noting distances to the pool, to the cave mouth, to the escarpment wall, fixing the stakes to the landscape so they couldn't be quietly relocated or explained away.

It was Hark who found the crew camp.

"Up here," he called from the talus slope at the amphitheater's edge. "You'll want to see this."

It was tucked under an overhang a hundred yards east: a canvas fly stretched between two boulders, beneath it a cold fire ring, a crate of stakes — two dozen more, cut and stamped and ready — a coil of orange ribbon, and a sledgehammer with the company name stenciled on the haft. Beside the crate, a clipboard with a work order.

Wren read it twice to make sure her eyes weren't lying.

**Zone 7 clearance prep — complete staking NLT 20th. Fell order issues upon permit confirmation. Crew to withdraw after staking. No further presence required.**

"The twentieth," she said. "The hearing is the eighteenth."

"So they stake it now," Reyes said slowly, coming up the slope with the camera hanging from his neck, "and if the permits are confirmed, the crews move in within days. And if anyone challenges—" He tapped the line *no further presence required*. "They withdraw. Nothing here but a few stakes and an empty camp. Nothing incriminating. It's tidy."

"It's *Voss*," Wren said. "Tidy is his whole personality."

Reyes almost smiled. Then he looked at the work order again, and the smile died, and Wren saw him do the same arithmetic she'd done: nine days' margin, minus travel, minus whatever Voss was doing right now to make sure the hearing never heard their testimony at all.

"We copy everything," Reyes said. "The order, the crate, the hammer. Hark, your ledger — date and time, please, and sign it."

"I don't sign things," Hark said. "Signing things is how a man ends up in front of boards." But he signed it, pressing hard enough to tear the paper slightly, and Wren decided she would forgive him every grumble between here and the grave.

* * *

*Theo*

Late afternoon light came down the amphitheater wall in long amber bars, and Theo used it. He photographed the spring itself — the dark mouth in the rock, the pool, the first hundred yards of young river — and then, because some instinct older than surveying told him to, he photographed the walls too, column by column, fern by fern. If the company cleared this zone, the felling would reach the rim. Everything in these frames might be the last record of what stood here.

He thought about Silas constantly now. Walking the ring of stakes, he kept imagining the old man's stride — that rolling prospector's gait Theo had watched cross the saloon floor on the last night of his life, fever-bright, map in fist, betting everything on a stranger with a railroad paycheck and a guilty conscience.

*"Before the permits expire,"* Silas had rasped. *"Thirty days. You get it to my girl, you hear? She'll know what it means even if nobody else does."*

Silas had known. Had probably known for years — that the vein ran here, under land the concession papers never covered, that the official survey was a fiction drawn by a man named Voss and witnessed by a fool named Reyes. Had known that the only way to prove it was to walk the river to its beginning and stand on the ground itself.

And had died two miles from a train station, trusting a promise to carry him the rest of the way.

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

Theo blinked. She was standing at the pool's edge, arms crossed, watching him with the flat assessing look she used on everything — weather, strangers, her own grief.

"What face?"

"The guilty one. You get it when you're thinking about my father. Like a dog that's been caught near the bread." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Sit down, Reyes. You're no use to anybody marinating."

He sat. The rock was warm from the day's sun, absurdly comfortable, and the sound of the spring filled the amphitheater — a low constant voice, older than any survey, indifferent to permits and boards and men with brands on their hammers.

"He trusted me," Theo said. "A complete stranger. Because I was there, and because I was the only person in that room who looked at his map instead of through it."

"Maybe." Wren crouched, dipped her hand in the pool, flicked water at the stones. "Or maybe he was dying and desperate and you were the last door available. People aren't chess players, Reyes. Mostly they're just tired." She paused. "Either way, you came. Four hundred miles of river, one gorge, one bullet, one burned bridge. Most men would've sold the map to Voss and bought a new life with the money."

"Most men would've slept better."

"Would they?" She looked at him sidelong. "You sleep like a rock. I've seen it. You snore like a landslide. Whatever you're carrying, it isn't keeping you up nights anymore. So maybe stop performing remorse and start finishing the job."

Theo laughed — actually laughed, for the first time since the saloon — and the sound went up the limestone walls and came back changed, bigger, like the amphitheater approved of it. Wren tried to keep her face stern and failed, the corner of her mouth betraying her, and for a moment they were just two exhausted people sitting at the beginning of a river, sharing the joke of being alive.

Then Hark whistled from the rim.

Not his ordinary whistle — his *trouble* whistle, two sharp notes, the same signal he'd used on the river when the trackers' smoke showed downstream. Theo was on his feet before the second note ended, camera clutched to his chest like a child.

On the southern rim of the amphitheater, small against the sky, three figures stood looking down into the bowl.

Even at that distance Theo knew the center one. The way he stood — perfectly still, hands clasped behind his back, head tilted with the patience of a man admiring architecture — belonged to no one else in the world but Mr. Voss.

"He followed us up the side channel," Wren breathed. "The unrunnable water. Nobody runs that."

"Somebody portaged it," Theo said. "Somebody who knew we would run it."

Below them, the spring went on speaking in its low stone voice. Above them, Voss raised one hand — not a threat, not yet. A greeting. A courtesy. The gesture of a host welcoming guests to a property he already considered his own.

And Theo understood, with a cold certainty that settled into his bones like the spring's chill, that the man had not come to take the evidence.

He had come to take the boats.
