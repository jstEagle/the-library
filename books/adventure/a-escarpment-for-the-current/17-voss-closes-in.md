## 17. Voss Closes In

Edmund Voss had always believed that a man's reputation was simply the last map anyone bothered to check.

He stood at the lip of the limestone amphitheater in the gray hour before dawn, boots planted on wet stone, and looked down at the spring that had cost him eleven years of careful work. The water rose out of the rock floor like something exhaled — a great clear breath of a river, beginning without ceremony. It did not know it was valuable. It did not know it was supposed to be anonymous. It simply rose, and fell away down its channel, and went about the business of becoming the river he had spent a decade teaching everyone to ignore.

Around it, his stakes stood in their fresh white ring. Twenty-two of them, driven by his own crew three weeks ago, each one painted with the company brand and tagged for immediate clearance. From up here they looked almost ceremonial. A crown of little wooden teeth around the mouth of the country's best-kept secret.

Voss allowed himself one long moment of professional appreciation, because he was, whatever else he was, a surveyor first. He had been a surveyor before he'd been anything else — before the money, before the title, before men in city offices started saying *Mr. Voss* in that particular hushed tone that meant *we would prefer not to know what you do*. He had once loved this work the way Theo Reyes clearly still did: the long arithmetic of angles, the satisfaction of a line drawn true.

That was precisely what made Reyes so dangerous. Not the map. Not the girl. Not even Hark's treachery, which Voss had predicted within an hour of the outfitter's shifty handshake. No — what made Reyes dangerous was that he drew lines the way Voss used to draw them, before Voss learned that a line could be bent if you bent yourself first.

Below him, at the spring, three figures moved in the early light. The girl was crouched at the water's edge, filling a tin cup, her bandaged shoulder making her movements lopsided as a gull in wind. The outfitter stood apart, arms crossed, radiating the guilt of a man who had recently fired warning shots at his own employers. And Reyes —

Reyes was photographing the stakes.

Of course he was. Methodical as a church clock. Voss watched him kneel at marker after marker, notebook open on his knee, copying numbers in that small careful hand Voss had last seen on a witness line fourteen years ago. There had been a time when the two of them had shared a tent in country much like this, arguing over whether a bearing was off by half a degree while the rain tried to drown them both. Voss remembered liking him. That was the inconvenient part. He had genuinely liked him.

He checked his revolver out of habit, found it where it always was, and began the descent into the amphitheater.

* * *

The stone took him down in long switchbacks, slick with spray, and by the time his boots touched the spring meadow the light had gone gold and honest. He hated how beautiful it was. It would have been easier if the place were ugly. Swamps were easy to bury. But this — the walls rising sheer and pale, the water so clear you could count the pebbles on the bottom, the whole basin holding the morning like a cupped hand — this was the kind of country that made juries sentimental.

The girl saw him first. She came up off her knees with a spear she had absolutely no intention of using well, but Voss respected the gesture anyway. Sentiment mattered. Sentiment was leverage.

"Mr. Reyes," he called, pleasantly, from thirty feet out. "You've lost weight. The river diet, I presume."

Reyes turned slowly. Up close, the man looked like a coat that had been left out in weather — hollowed, sunburnt, one eyebrow still carrying the scab of some recent catastrophe. But his eyes were steady, and Voss noted with professional disappointment that there was no fear in them. Only a kind of tired recognition, as though Reyes had expected him the way you expect rain.

"Voss." Reyes said the name like a diagnosis. "I wondered when you'd stop sending other people to do your walking."

"I'm told the gorge run nearly killed you." Voss spread his hands. "For the record, I never authorized that channel. I said *follow him*. My trackers have always had more enthusiasm than judgment. It's why I pay them so little."

"You're standing on land your permits don't cover," the girl said. Her voice was rougher than her years. Wren Crane. Silas Crane's daughter, and every bit the old man's stubbornness given legs. "We've got the rubbings. We've got photographs. We've got the original survey."

"Ah." Voss smiled at her, gently, because cruelty wasted on people who couldn't be bought back was just poor bookkeeping. "Miss Crane. I'm sorry about your father. Genuinely. He was a better prospector than he ever got credit for, which was, unfortunately, the entire problem."

"He died thinking I hated him."

"Yes," Voss said, and let the word sit there, because sometimes the truth was the sharpest tool in the kit. "Men who carry secrets tend to die carrying the wrong ones. It's practically a trade requirement."

Hark shifted his weight and muttered something that sounded like *this is a bad spot and we're all standing in it*, which Voss felt was uncharitable, since Voss had personally arranged for it to be a bad spot.

* * *

But business first. Voss had learned long ago that threats were the second move, never the first. Threats were for men who had failed to make the offer attractive, and Voss did not intend to fail.

"Mr. Reyes," he said, and took two steps closer, hands visible, voice dropping into the register he used for boardrooms. "May I show you something?"

Reyes didn't answer, which Voss chose to interpret as permission. He reached into his coat — the girl's spear tracked him the whole way, bless her — and produced a folded document, crisp despite the journey. He held it out.

Reyes took it. Voss watched him read, and watched the exact moment the words landed, because he had built his career on reading faces the way other men read contours.

It was a letter of reinstatement. Railroad letterhead. Full restoration of license, seniority retroactive, a named position — Chief Surveyor, Western Division — with a salary figure that made Hark inhale audibly through his nose. And beneath it, already signed, the signature of the same director who had accepted Reyes's resignation with a handshake and no eye contact.

"They'll take me back," Reyes said quietly. Not a question. Worse than a question — a hope, which was exactly what Voss had come to kill or keep.

"They're begging to take you back. They need a chief surveyor who isn't compromised, and you are, at present, the only man in this territory whose name carries any clean weight at all." Voss let that settle. "One condition. You hand over the original survey, you testify that Silas Crane forged the boundary documents himself — a dying man's confusion, very sad, the board will eat it with a spoon — and you go back to drawing lines for people who will finally appreciate them."

"And the source?"

"The source stays a rumor." Voss gestured at the amphitheater, at the water rising pure and indifferent between them. "Look at it, Theo. Look at it properly. In five years this is a mill town. In ten it's a scar with a company store. You know the arithmetic as well as I do. Every wild place becomes a ledger line eventually. All I'm offering you is the chance to be paid for the arithmetic instead of ruined by it."

He paused, and then — because he meant it, and because meaning it was the only currency Reyes would accept — he added:

"We were the same kind of men once. You know we were. I've read your early field notes. Beautiful work. Precise, honest, a little too fond of the country for your own good. So was I. Then I learned the lesson you're about to learn the hard way: the map doesn't serve the land, Theo. The land serves the map, and the map serves whoever holds it. That's not corruption. That's just the profession, stated plainly. I'm offering you the chance to state it plainly too, and get rich doing it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them a hawk rode the thermals off the escarpment, screaming once, twice, indifferent to all of it.

Reyes looked down at the letter for a long moment. When he raised his head, he was smiling — a small, tired, genuinely warm smile that Voss found deeply irritating.

"You kept my field notes?"

"Don't flatter yourself. I kept everyone's files."

"You kept mine for fourteen years." Reyes folded the letter along its crease, carefully, the way he did everything. "Edmund, I spent most of my career erasing lines. Closed routes. Abandoned spurs. Whole towns taken off the maps because the arithmetic said so. I told myself it was just the job." He held the letter back out. "Then I met a dying man who showed me a river somebody erased to hide a vein, and I recognized the handwriting. Mine. And yours. And I realized the only difference between us is that you decided the erasing was the job, and I haven't decided anything yet — except this."

He dropped the letter onto the stones between them.

"No," he said.

Just that. No speech, no fury. Voss had prepared rebuttals for pride, for fear, for greed, for the girl's sake, for honor in every one of its tiresome costumes. He had not adequately prepared for a tired man simply declining.

"That," Voss said, "is a remarkably expensive word."

"It usually is."

* * *

What followed took less than four minutes, and Voss conducted it with the efficiency he brought to all regrettable necessities.

The girl moved first — of course she did; Crane blood ran toward trouble like water toward low ground — and Voss caught the spear shaft and turned her gently but firmly against the rocks, pinning her good arm, saying, "Miss Crane, please, I am trying very hard not to make this worse for everyone," which was true, and which she answered by driving her heel into his instep hard enough to rearrange his priorities for several seconds.

Hark threw a punch that missed by a generous margin and then sat down on a boulder, declaring loudly that he was neutral, that he had always been neutral, and that neutrality was a proud tradition among outfitters. Nobody believed him, including Hark.

And Reyes — Reyes went for the satchel. The original survey, wrapped in oilskin, tucked under the supply pack. Voss saw the calculation land in the man's face a half-second too late: protect the document, and lose everything else.

Voss got there first. He always got there first; it was the one talent the years had sharpened rather than dulled. He wrenched the oilskin free, backed away with the revolver out now and level, breathing hard, a stone bruise blooming on his foot, dignity approximately intact.

"Thank you," he said sincerely. "Truly. This is the only copy?"

"The only copy," Reyes said, and Voss heard the truth of it, because Reyes was constitutionally incapable of lying well.

"Good." Voss retreated toward the switchbacks, keeping the trio covered, the oilskin bundle under his arm like a stolen hymnal. At the base of the climb he stopped, and allowed himself one last look at the spring — the water rising, the light on the limestone, the girl bleeding a little through her bandage and glaring at him with her father's exact expression — and felt, briefly and unwelcome, the ghost of the surveyor he had been, the one who would have knelt at that water and recorded it honestly.

He put the ghost away. He was good at that.

"One piece of professional advice before I go," Voss called down. "The hearing is in eight days. The county seat is nine miles of nothing, and you have no boats, because I'm taking both of them. Your friend's supply skiff is moored below the lower bend — I've already sent for it. Walk fast, Miss Crane. Bleed slower. And Mr. Reyes—" he tapped the oilskin against his chest, "—when you testify, testify well. I want it to at least be interesting."

He climbed out of the amphitheater with the original survey against his heart and the sound of the river beginning behind him, and by the time he reached the ridge top he had already begun composing the fire he would set tonight — because a document this dangerous should never survive to embarrass anyone, and because Edmund Voss had learned long ago that the surest way to erase a line was to burn the paper it was drawn on.

Behind him, faint on the wind, he thought he could hear Reyes already talking — measuring, pacing, counting miles.

Nine miles of nothing, Voss thought, and permitted himself a thin smile.

Let's see what the railroad man remembers.
