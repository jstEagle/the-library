## 18. Nine Miles of Nothing

The boats were gone. That was the arithmetic we woke to on the gravel bar, Voss's wake still flattening out downriver, and the county seat a hundred and forty miles away by water and nine miles by air — if you could walk on air.

"You're smiling," Wren said from where she lay propped against a drift log. Her shoulder was bound in Hark's spare shirt, soaked through at the knot again. "We've lost everything and you're smiling."

"I'm not smiling." I was smiling. "I'm remembering. There's an old logging grade that runs along the top of this escarpment. Cut in '09, abandoned by '21. It goes within four miles of Harlow's Crossing, and Harlow's Crossing has a rail line that still runs freight twice a week."

Hark squinted up at the escarpment wall rising gray and sheer above us, five hundred feet of limestone with a seam of scree and cedar where a determined goat might have made it. "And how do we get to the top of this grade?"

"The same way the men who built it did. There was an incline. A switchback trail for the mules. The map shows it."

"Whose map?" Wren asked.

I patted the oilskin inside my coat. "Mine. Twenty years ago. Before I understood what I was drawing."

That shut us all up for a moment. Then Hark stood, cracked his back like a rifle shot, and said, "Well. Nine miles of nothing beats a hundred and forty of Voss," and began breaking camp.

* * *

Getting Wren up the switchback took the whole morning. She refused to be carried at first — refused it with a fury that would have been impressive under other circumstances — but by the third turn of the trail her face had gone the color of the limestone and her legs had stopped taking instructions. Hark simply picked her up then, the way you'd pick up a sack of feed, and she was too far gone to argue.

"Put me down," she managed once.

"Nope."

"I can walk."

"You can't. And I've carried heavier things than you up worse hills than this. Carried a piano once. Don't ask."

She didn't ask. But somewhere around the fourth switchback, half-conscious against his shoulder, she said something I wasn't meant to hear: "You don't even know me."

"Don't need to," Hark said. "Your father bought his boots off me for eleven years and never once let me shortchange him. Blood tells."

The trail topped out into a country I knew better than I wanted to. The escarpment top was all wind-bent jack pine and blueberry barrens, and running through it, straight as a ruled line, was the old grade — a raised bed of ballast gone soft with moss, the ties rotted to black lace, the steel long since pulled. Twenty years ago I had walked this line with a transit and a notebook and written *abandon* beside every milepost. I had been proud of the neatness of it.

Now I walked it again with a wounded woman on an outfitter's back and a dead man's map against my ribs, and every milepost felt like a small accusation.

"How far to the crossing?" Wren asked when they let her down onto a tie to rest.

"Six miles as the grade runs. The grade doesn't run straight, though. It curves around two muskegs and a rock cut." I hesitated. "There's something else. The trestle over Miller Creek burned last year. We'll have to ford."

"Of course it burned," Wren said. "Everything burns on this river."

"Not everything," I said, and thought of the spring in its amphitheater, clear and cold and older than any of us, and kept walking.

* * *

By mid-afternoon Wren couldn't walk at all. The fever had come on fast, the way fever does when a wound has been dirty too long, and she was talking to her father — arguing with him, mostly, in a low furious mutter about letters and years and who had left whom.

"She needs a doctor," Hark said. "Not a train. A doctor."

"A train gets us to one by tomorrow morning. Walking gets us there Thursday, if she lasts."

"That's not a comforting sentence, Theo."

"It's not a comforting situation."

We made a litter of the oilskin groundsheet and two paddles salvaged from the wreck of my canoe, and Hark and I traded carrying ends while the grade curved north through the muskeg. Wren weighed nothing. That frightened me more than the fever itself — the way illness hollows a person, like water wearing through limestone.

At the rock cut she surfaced long enough to say, "Theo. If I die up here, put me at the spring. Not a churchyard. The spring."

"You're not going to die."

"Promise anyway. It costs nothing."

It cost a great deal. "I promise," I said, and walked faster.

* * *

We came down off the grade at dusk with the light going copper in the west, and below us, maybe a mile out across the barrens, I heard it: the long complaint of a locomotive taking the curve at Harlow's Crossing, three notes descending, like something calling and being answered.

"They'll be at the water tank now," I said. "Taking on water. They stop eight minutes. Maybe ten if the engineer's lazy."

"Then we'd best not be lazy," Hark said, and picked Wren up, and we ran.

I say we ran. Hark ran. I ran the way a man runs when he is fifty-one years old and has spent twenty of them behind a desk: badly, with much internal negotiation. The barrens tore at our shins. The ground was all hummock and hidden water. Twice I went down and got up without deciding to. Ahead of me Hark ran with Wren slung across his shoulders like a yoke, steady as a metronome, an outfitter's flat terrible endurance, and I fixed on his back and followed it.

The water tank rose out of the twilight, black and gaunt, and beneath it sat the freight: a Consolidation locomotive breathing steam, a caboose, eleven cars of pulpwood. A brakeman stood on the tender shoveling nothing, waiting for the tank to fill.

"Hoy!" Hark bellowed. "HOY THERE!"

The brakeman turned. What he saw, I expect, was a madman carrying a bleeding woman out of the wilderness at full gallop, pursued by a second madman waving his hat. To his credit he didn't reach for anything. He just shouted down into the cab, and a head appeared, and then another head, and by the time we reached the engine we were surrounded by the entire crew, six men in overalls looking at us the way men look at weather that shouldn't exist.

"We need the train," I gasped. "County seat. Tonight. There's a woman hurt."

The engineer came down off the cab — a big man, sixty-odd, gray mustache, the kind of face that had been making decisions since before I was born. He looked at Wren. He looked at Hark. He looked at me, and frowned, and said:

"Do I know you?"

"You might," I said. "Theo Reyes. Railroad surveyor. I closed this line."

Something moved through his face. Recognition, and under it something harder. "Reyes," he said. "The abandonment report. Mile 14 to 61. Your signature's on it. My brother ran mixed traffic out of Harlow's Crossing for nineteen years. When your report went through, he lost his run."

The whole crew had gone quiet. Eight minutes were burning down. Somewhere behind us the dark was filling up with men who wanted Wren silent and the map ash.

"I know what I signed," I said. "I know what it cost. I can't undo it. But this woman has evidence that will undo something worse, and she has until morning, and you have a train pointed the right direction." I pulled the oilskin packet partway from my coat so he could see the map, Silas's grease-stained hand, the route to the source. "There are men behind us who burned a bridge to keep this quiet. They will burn more."

The engineer looked at the map a long time. Then at Wren, whose eyes had opened, fever-bright, and who said with perfect clarity: "Mister, I have been shot, and I am extremely annoyed about it, and I would take it as a personal favor."

That broke them. The brakeman laughed first, then the fireman, then the engineer himself, a great helpless bark of a laugh. "Load her in the caboose," he said. "Doc Ellery keeps office hours at the county seat till ten, and we make good time downhill." He turned to climb back up, then paused. "Reyes. This run was scheduled for elimination next quarter. Another paper with your kind of signature on it, I expect." He jerked his chin at the caboose. "Make this one count."

* * *

Wren rode in the cupola with the conductor's coat over her and the caboose swaying under her like a cradle. Hark sat with her; I stood on the rear platform watching the escarpment slide backward, black against the last light, and thought about signatures.

Twenty years ago I had drawn a line through this country and called it progress. Tonight the same rails I condemned were carrying the only thing that mattered, clicking south at forty miles an hour, and the rhythm of them — that old familiar *da-DUM, da-DUM* I had loved since boyhood — sounded different now. Not like erasure. Like a pulse.

Hark stuck his head out the door. "She's sleeping. Real sleep, not fever sleep." He handed me a tin cup of coffee the conductor had produced from nowhere, strong enough to trot a horse across. "You did all right back there."

"I closed their line."

"And tonight it saved her life. Funny thing about lines, Theo. Doesn't matter what you drew them for. Matters what runs on them after."

I drank the coffee. It was terrible. It was the best thing I had ever tasted.

Ahead, past the locomotive's smoke, the county seat showed as a smudge of electric light — the courthouse clock, the hotel, the hearing room where in nine days' time the permits would live or die. Nine days. Voss had the original survey, but Voss did not have Wren's rubbings, or my photographs, or my memory, or a crew of railroad men who owed me nothing and had helped us anyway.

The brakes sang. The town grew. And in the cupola, waking just enough to see the lights, Wren Crane smiled and said, "Right on schedule," which worried me, because I hadn't told her the schedule, and because she looked exactly like her father when she said it.
