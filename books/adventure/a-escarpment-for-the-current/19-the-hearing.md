## 19. The Hearing

The county courthouse in Harlow Bend smelled of floor wax and old paper and, faintly, of the river — because half the town had come in off the water that morning and nobody had thought to wipe their boots. Theo Reyes stood at the back of the hearing room with his hat in his hands, watching three men in suits arrange themselves at the front like chess pieces deciding which of them was the king.

Wren sat beside him, her left arm bound in a sling Hark had improvised from a flour sack and a length of rope. The wound had gone bad on the train — fever, then chills, then a gray look around the eyes that had frightened Theo more than any rapid — but the county doctor had lanced and dressed it at dawn, dosed her with something bitter, and pronounced her "too stubborn to die this week." She'd taken it as a compliment.

"You're sweating," she said.

"It's a warm room."

"It's October."

Theo wiped his palms on his trousers anyway. Nine days ago he'd been a man adrift on a raft made of a burned cabin, holding a dead prospector's map and a living woman's fury, racing an army of lawyers he couldn't see. Now the lawyers were here, all of them, filling two rows of benches with good wool and better watches, and Theo understood suddenly why Silas Crane had trusted no one with anything.

The board occupied the raised bench: three commissioners, a stenographer, and a water clock that ticked with the patience of geology. Chairman Aldous Prewitt was a small round man with spectacles thick as jar bottoms and the air of someone who had spent forty years wishing people would simply fill out the correct forms.

"Order," Prewitt said, to a room that hadn't been disorderly. "This is a continuation of hearings regarding Concession Eleven, the Meridian Timber & Mineral Company, application for renewal of extraction permits covering the upper Kettle River watershed." He peered over his spectacles. "Counsel will identify themselves for the record."

"Counselor Bram Fitch, for Meridian," said the tallest of the suited men, rising with the smoothness of a man who'd rehearsed standing up. "We're prepared to proceed immediately. Our survey documentation is complete and certified."

"Then let's have it," said Prewitt, "and God willing we'll be done by supper."

* * *

Fitch was good. Theo had to give him that. For two hours the lawyer walked the board through the concession's case with the unhurried confidence of a man reading from scripture: the original boundary survey, conducted eleven years prior; the certified plat maps; the testimony — sworn, notarized — of the company's chief surveyor, one A. Voss, who had personally verified every monument line and corner stake along the upper river.

Voss himself took the stand mid-morning. He looked exactly as he had at the spring, except cleaner: gray suit, gray eyes, the composed face of a man who had never once been surprised by anything, including being caught. He testified in a level, reasonable voice about datum points and witness trees, about the rigor of his methods, about the regrettable tendency of prospectors and squatters to misread professional documents.

"The boundaries were surveyed correctly," Voss said, "and marked correctly, and filed correctly. Any suggestion otherwise comes from parties with a financial interest in confusion."

"And the spring itself?" asked Commissioner Doyle, the youngest of the three, a lean woman with ink-stained fingers who had asked more questions than the other two combined. "The headwaters zone. Your survey places it outside the protected corridor?"

"Outside it entirely," Voss said. "By nearly two miles. The source lies within the extraction block. I staked it myself three weeks ago."

Three weeks ago. While Wren was bleeding on a raft and Theo was carrying her through nine miles of scree, Voss's crews had been hammering stakes into the limestone amphitheater where the river began. Theo felt Wren go rigid beside him and put a hand lightly on her good shoulder. *Not yet,* the hand said. *Wait.*

"Do you swear these are true and accurate copies of the original field surveys?" Doyle asked, nodding at the plat maps fanned across the exhibit table.

"I do," Voss said. "I drew them."

And there it was. Theo felt the sentence land in his chest like a spike driven home. *I drew them.* Not *I verified them.* Not *they were drawn under my supervision.* *I drew them* — the words of a man so certain no one would ever check that he'd stopped bothering to lie carefully.

Commissioner Doyle looked at the maps a long moment. Then she looked up — past Voss, past Fitch, out into the room — and her gaze snagged on the back wall, on the tall man in the dusty coat holding his hat like a man in church.

"Mr. Prewitt," she said. "Before we recess, I'd like to hear from the public. It's my understanding there are parties present who wish to speak against the permit."

Fitch was on his feet instantly. "Madam Commissioner, the public comment period closed—"

"The comment period closed," Doyle said pleasantly, "for written comments. This is a hearing. People are heard at hearings. That's rather the point of them." She turned a page. "Is there a Mr. Theodore Reyes in the room? The record shows a statement was filed yesterday evening."

Every head in the room turned. Theo's stomach performed a maneuver he associated with the worst water he'd ever run.

"That would be me," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect.

* * *

Walking to the front of that room was, Theo decided later, harder than the gorge. Harder than the burned bridge, harder than the night he'd shivered alone with half his gear gone and the map pressed to his ribs like a second heartbeat. Because the gorge hadn't been watching him. The gorge hadn't included two rows of lawyers taking notes, or Voss sitting ten feet away with his gray eyes doing arithmetic, calculating exactly how much trouble one railroad engineer could possibly be.

He was sworn in. He gave his name, his former occupation — *former*, and the word tasted strange and clean — and his business before the board.

"I'm here about the survey," Theo said. "Mr. Voss has told you he drew the plat maps now in evidence. That's true. What he hasn't told you is that he didn't draw them from the field work. He drew them from nothing. The original survey of the upper Kettle — the real one — was done eleven years ago by a man named Edmund Hale, and it says something very different from what's on that table."

Murmurs. Fitch half-rose; Prewitt waved him down with the weary authority of a man who had raised teenagers.

"On what basis do you make this claim?" Doyle asked.

"Because I was there." Theo reached into his coat and brought out the oilskin packet, and his hands, which had shaken all morning, went still. "In 1911 I was an assistant on Ed Hale's crew. Junior rodman. I carried the stadia rod up every mile of that river, and when the survey was finished, I signed the witness lines. All of them." He laid the original survey on the exhibit table, unwrapped the oilskin, and turned it toward the bench. "Including this one."

Doyle leaned forward. So did Prewitt, squinting through his jar-bottom spectacles. The stenographer's keys stopped clicking.

"Witness signature, southeast corner monument, township line four," Theo read aloud, because his voice needed the work. "*T. J. Reyes.* That's my hand. I was twenty-three years old and I didn't know what I was signing, and I've spent eleven years finding out."

"Objection," Fitch said smoothly. "Relevance. This document is uncertified, unfiled, of unknown provenance—"

"Its provenance is a cabin above the third gorge," Theo said, "where Ed Hale hid it when your client decided his survey was inconvenient. You'll find the cabin if you look. Or you can ask Mr. Voss, since he's the one who sent men to burn it."

That landed. Theo watched it land — watched Fitch's composure flicker, watched two of the Meridian lawyers exchange a glance that lasted a half-second too long, watched Voss sit perfectly still with the stillness of a man conserving everything for one moment.

"Mr. Voss's survey places the spring inside the extraction block," Theo went on, before anyone could stop him. "Ed Hale's survey places it a mile and three-quarters inside the protected corridor, on land the permits were never meant to touch. And the reason the company prefers Mr. Voss's version is simple. There's a vein of high-grade ore running under that corridor, and if the truth of where it sits ever got out, the permits wouldn't just be denied — they'd be evidence."

"Allegations," Fitch said, "are not measurements."

"No," Theo agreed. "Measurements are measurements." He looked at Doyle. "I have those too."

* * *

They came out of the oilskin packet one at a time, and Theo laid each on the table like a man dealing cards he already knew would win.

Wren's rubbings first — charcoal impressions on butcher paper, taken from company stakes along the upper river, each stamped with the Meridian brand and a section number. She'd made them over two years, she told the board later, in a voice like flint, because she kept finding markers where markers shouldn't be and nobody downstream would tell her why. Laid beside Voss's plat maps, they told a story without needing a single word: the stakes did not match the maps. Not by feet. By miles.

Then the photographs. Hark had a cousin in Harlow Bend with a camera shop and a grudge against Meridian dating back to a timber dispute in ought-nine, and the cousin had developed Theo's plates overnight, muttering the whole time about what the world was coming to. Now the prints went from hand to hand along the bench: the limestone amphitheater, green water rising out of the rock like breath; the ring of fresh-cut stakes, white wood bright against the stone; the painted numbers matching, section for section, the extraction block on Voss's maps.

"That's the source of the Kettle," Theo said. "Photographed eight days ago. Staked for clearance six days after that. Under a permit that, according to the document Mr. Voss certified, doesn't cover it — and according to the document he buried, never could."

Silence. The water clock ticked. Somewhere behind Theo, Wren shifted in her seat, and he heard Hark murmur something low and steady to her, the way you talk to a horse in thunder weather.

Commissioner Doyle held the photograph of the spring at arm's length, then close, then looked up at Voss.

"Mr. Voss. These stakes bear your section markings."

"They do."

"They do not match your certified plat."

"There are any number of explanations for field discrepancies—"

"Two miles," Doyle said, "is not a discrepancy. Two miles is a different river." She set the photograph down with great care, the way you set down something you don't trust yourself to handle quickly. "Chairman, I move we suspend all Meridian permits pending a full investigation of the concession's survey records, including forensic comparison of the Hale document against the filed plats, and including — " she glanced at her notes — "the circumstances of a fire at a structure above the third gorge, which the county sheriff may wish to hear about."

"Seconded," said Commissioner Marsh, who had spoken perhaps nine words all day and chose them well.

Prewitt removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on, and regarded the room over the top of them like a schoolmaster deciding whether to cancel recess forever.

"In thirty-one years on this board," he said, "I have suspended permits twice. Both times I lost sleep. I suspect tonight I'll sleep fine." He raised a hand. "All in favor of suspension pending investigation."

Three hands went up. The gavel came down once, sharp as ice cracking, and the room erupted — lawyers surging up, the stenographer protesting, somebody's hat knocked askew, Hark letting out a whoop from the back that he immediately tried to disguise as a cough.

Through all of it, Theo stood at the witness table with his empty hands flat on the wood, shaking now, finally, now that it was over — shaking with terror and triumph in equal measure, indistinguishable, the way floodwater and clear water are indistinguishable once they're in the same channel.

Voss rose from his chair. For one moment their eyes met across the crowded room, surveyor to surveyor, and Theo saw something in the gray face that wasn't anger. It was worse. It was recognition — the look of a man seeing his own reflection in a window and hating the glass.

Then the sheriff's deputies were moving through the crowd, and Voss straightened his cuffs, and allowed himself to be escorted out with the courtesy of a guest leaving early, and Theo understood that this was not the end of anything. Lawyers circled. Companies appealed. Men like Voss had friends in places Harlow Bend had never heard of.

But that was tomorrow's river.

* * *

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, the afternoon light came slanting gold through the cottonwoods, and Wren stood beside him with her sling and her stubborn chin, watching the crowd disperse.

"You did it," she said. "You actually stood up in front of all of them and told the truth."

"Took me long enough."

"Eleven years."

"Eleven years," Theo agreed. Down the street, Hark was arguing joyfully with the camera-shop cousin about the cost of developing plates, a negotiation that appeared to involve both men buying each other dinner. "You know what the strangest part was? When I signed that witness line at twenty-three, I thought I was just — signing. Paperwork. But it turns out a signature is a promise. It just takes some people a decade to read what they promised."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then she reached into her coat — awkwardly, one-handed — and produced a letter. Creased soft as cloth, addressed in a careful spidery hand: *Miss Wren Crane.*

"One of his," she said. "The doctor found them in Silas's kit. There are fourteen." Her voice caught and steadied. "I'm going to read them tonight. All of them. I thought — " She stopped, started again. "I thought you should know that whatever's in them, I'm glad you carried the map. Even if it nearly killed us both."

"Especially because," Theo said.

She almost smiled. Then her eyes went past him, down the steps, and sharpened. "Theo. Who's that?"

A boy was climbing the courthouse steps two at a time — maybe sixteen, telegraph bag slung across his chest, breathing hard from a long ride. He scanned the dispersing crowd, found Theo by some instinct, and thrust out a yellow flimsy like a man delivering a verdict.

"Telegram for Reyes. Sign here."

Theo signed. He unfolded the slip with fingers that had gone cold despite the sun, and read it twice, and felt the ground shift beneath him the way it shifts when you learn the bank you're standing on is gravel.

It was from the railroad. His old office. Eight words:

*RETURN IMMEDIATELY. HALE SURVEY MATTERS REQUIRE YOUR EXPLANATION.*

Beside him, Wren read it upside down, quick as a wren, and laughed — a short, hard, unafraid laugh.

"Well," she said. "Looks like the river runs both directions."
