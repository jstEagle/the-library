## 20. What Loyalty Costs

The county courthouse smelled of coal dust and old paper, and Wren Crane had never loved a building more in her life.

She stood at the back of the hearing room while the board clerk read the suspension aloud — *all permits pertaining to the upper watershed suspended pending formal investigation* — and she watched the words land on the men in good suits like stones dropped into still water. Ripples everywhere. Mr. Aldous Voss did not ripple. He sat very straight with his hands flat on the table before him, and when two deputies came down the aisle toward him he rose of his own accord, buttoned his coat, and went with them as though he were merely stepping out to check on a survey crew.

Only once did he look back. Not at Theo, who had just finished testifying. Not at Hark, who sat scowling by the stove with his arms crossed like a man who'd been dragged somewhere against his will and intended everyone to know it.

Voss looked at Wren.

There was no malice in it. That was the strange part, the part that would keep her up that night and several nights after. He looked at her the way a man looks at a sum that has come out wrong — not angry at the numbers, merely obliged to find where he'd erred. Then the door swung shut behind him, and the room exhaled all at once, a hundred people breathing out who hadn't known they were holding it.

"Well," said Hark. "That's that."

"That's not that," said Theo quietly. "That's only the beginning."

Wren would learn, over the following days, exactly how right he was.

* * *

They lodged at the Commercial Hotel because it was the only one in town with rooms facing away from the rail yard, and Wren had discovered she could no longer sleep through the sound of coupling cars. It reminded her too much of the escarpment — iron striking iron, everything hauled off to somewhere else.

On the third morning after the hearing, a lawyer came to see her.

He introduced himself as Pemberton, of Alder, Pemberton & Grange, counsel for the Meridian Concession Company, and he was the smoothest thing Wren had ever seen walk upright. Grey suit, grey voice, a smile you could have used to shave with. He set a leather folder on the little table in the hotel parlor and did not open it.

"Miss Crane. I'll be brief, as I know your time is valuable."

"My time is free," said Wren. "It's the truth that costs."

Something flickered behind the smile — recalibration. "You've made certain allegations regarding survey work conducted under company authority some years ago. The board has seen fit to suspend our permits while these matters are reviewed. The company intends to cooperate fully with that review." He opened the folder now, and inside were documents, neat as pressed flowers. "However. I'm instructed to inform you that any claim you might file upon lands within the former concession boundary will be contested. Vigorously. On grounds of prior corporate interest, on grounds of your father's—"

"Say it," said Wren. "Whatever you're going to say about my father, say it to my face."

Pemberton folded his hands. "Silas Crane was convicted of theft of company property in absentia eleven years ago. There is a judgment outstanding. The company is prepared to be generous — generous beyond what the law requires — if the outstanding parties withdraw their testimony and return certain materials currently in their possession."

"The materials being the original survey."

"The materials being stolen property."

"It wasn't stolen." She kept her voice level, though her hands wanted otherwise. "It was hidden. There's a difference, Mr. Pemberton, and the difference is that hiding something takes courage and stealing it takes a key."

For the first time, the lawyer looked at her properly — not as an obstacle but as a person, which she suspected was rarer for him than honesty. "You should understand what you're up against," he said, almost gently. "The company has been contesting claims since before you were born. We have never lost one that mattered. Think carefully about whether this hillside is worth the rest of your life."

"I already have," said Wren. "I spent eleven years deciding what was worth my life, and I chose wrong once. I won't do it twice."

Pemberton closed the folder. At the door he paused. "Your father," he said, "was offered a settlement once. A small one. He turned it down. I always wondered why." And then he was gone, leaving the parlor smelling faintly of bay rum and money.

Wren stood a long time looking at the door. Then she went to find Theo, because there was a question scratching at her now that she couldn't reach alone: *what settlement? Why?*

And underneath it, quieter, worse: *if the company offered him money, why did he live in a tent and eat beans?*

* * *

She found him in the hotel dining room doing what he did every evening now — spreading papers across a corner table and redrawing things. Since the hearing he'd taken to sketching the watershed from memory, line by line, correcting as he went, muttering figures under his breath like a man saying his prayers. Hark called it "Theo's penance homework." Theo pretended not to hear and Wren suspected he secretly liked the name.

She sat down across from him and told him about Pemberton. All of it, including the last part.

Theo put down his pencil. For a while he just looked at the tablecloth, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she'd been doing.

"A settlement," he said finally. "Eleven years ago. That would have been right after the second survey disappeared." He rubbed his jaw. "Wren, when I worked under Marsh — my mentor, Elias Marsh — there was a standing practice. When a surveyor found something the company didn't want found, they didn't fire him. Firing makes noise. They bought him. Quiet annuity, relocation, a new name on a new map somewhere far away. Most men took it."

"And my father didn't."

"Your father didn't." Theo said it simply, without drama, and somehow that made it heavier. "Which means every year he stayed out there, every winter in that cabin, every trapline — he wasn't stubborn, or not only stubborn. He was holding ground. He was the only witness left standing on it."

Wren looked at her hands. They were chapped from the river and the raft and the fever weeks, and she thought about how Silas's hands must have looked at the end, when a stranger named Reyes had knelt beside him in a muddy saloon and taken a map off him like taking a weight off a dying man's chest.

"How did he die?" she asked. "Exactly. I've asked you pieces of it and you keep giving me pieces."

Theo didn't flinch, which she gave him credit for. "Fever. His lungs, mostly. He'd been living rough a long time." He hesitated. "There's more. You're not going to like it."

"Tell me anyway."

"He talked about you. Near the end. He—" Theo stopped, started again, plainly choosing each word like footing on scree. "He believed you hated him. He said his daughter hadn't answered a letter in years, and that he'd earned it, and that the only thing left he could do was make sure the ground got to you anyway. He made me swear the promise before he'd let himself rest. I think he held on those last hours partly out of guilt. Partly out of hope. But mostly, Wren — he died believing he'd lost you for good."

The dining room went on around them. A girl refilled the water glasses. Somewhere in the kitchen a pan clattered and someone swore cheerfully. Ordinary sounds, and Wren sat inside them feeling as though the floor had been pulled out and replaced with air.

"Hated him," she repeated. Her voice came out strange. "I wrote him. Every year. Care of the outfitter at Kettle Falls, care of the assay office, care of every forwarding address I could dig out of anybody. Eleven letters. Maybe more." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I got one letter back. One, when I was nineteen. It said the country was good and the trapping was fair and he hoped I was well. *Hoped I was well.* Like I was a neighbor. So I stopped writing, because what was the use of writing to a man who signed his own daughter 'S. Crane'?"

Theo was very still. "Wren. Did you ever get an answer to any of the others?"

"No. That's the whole point—"

"Did you ever get them *back*?"

She opened her mouth to say something sharp and then closed it, because the question had gone in under her ribs and found something. Undelivered mail didn't vanish. It accumulated. It sat in dead-letter offices and forwarding depots and the drawers of outfitters who moved around. Unless someone collected it.

Unless someone had been collecting it.

"His pack," she said slowly. "When you met him. What was in his pack besides the map?"

"Paper," Theo said. "A lot of paper. Bundles of it, wrapped in oilskin. I assumed survey notes. I took the map and left the rest — he asked me to, said the rest was for nobody." He was already reaching for his coat. "Hark inventoried his effects for the coroner. Everything went into storage at the freight office pending next of kin."

"Next of kin," Wren repeated. She was standing now, though she had no memory of standing. "I'm the next of kin."

"You're the next of kin," Theo agreed, and for the first time in days he smiled, tired and crooked. "Let's go collect your mail."

* * *

The freight office smelled of creosote and canvas, and the clerk — a round, kind-faced man named Ostrowski who had clearly been waiting years for something interesting to happen — produced a crate from under the counter with the reverence of a man unveiling a relic.

"Crane, S., deceased," he read off the tag. "Effects, one crate. Held ninety days per regulation, then auction. You're lucky, miss. Day sixty-one."

Inside, wrapped in oilskin against the damp, were bundles. Dozens of them. Survey notes, yes — pages of elevations and bearings in a cramped, sure hand. Trapline ledgers. A tin star from some long-ago miners' association. And beneath all of it, tied with string gone soft with age, packets of letters.

Letters addressed to *Wren Crane, c/o General Delivery, Ashford.* To *Wren Crane, care of Mrs. Tulliver's Boarding House, Ashford.* To *Miss W. Crane, Ashford Post Office.* Eleven years of them, addressed and stamped and returned, marked in various clerks' hands: *no such party*, *moved*, *unclaimed*. He had written to addresses she'd left behind like breadcrumbs every time she'd moved rooms trying to get cheaper rent, and he had kept writing to each stale address anyway, because it was the last one he had.

And at the bottom, separate, wrapped in its own oilskin: a bundle of letters addressed to no one. Unsent. Dated one per year, every year, on the same date — her birthday.

Wren carried the crate back to the hotel herself, refusing help, though Theo walked beside her the whole way with his hands in his pockets pretending not to watch her stagger. In her room she lit the lamp, sat on the bed, and untied the first unsent letter with fingers that would not behave.

*My Wren —*

*You are nine today. Nine! I tried to imagine it and could not, so I asked Hark's boy how big a nine-year-old is and he held his hand at his shoulder, which cannot be right, you were at my elbow last I saw you.*

*I am writing these even though I don't send them, because Dr. Ferris (not a real doctor, but he pulls teeth) says a man ought to say a thing out loud once a year or it goes rotten in him. Here is the thing out loud: I left. Nobody made me. Your mother was three months gone and I told myself the mountains owed us a fortune and I believed myself, which is the trickiest lie there is, the ones you tell yourself with a straight face.*

*The vein isn't found yet. Or maybe it is and I can't prove it. Either way I'm not coming home until I can come home carrying something worth the leaving.*

*Happy birthday, sprout. Grow mean. It's a hard country.*

*— Your father, who is a fool, but a persistent one*

Wren read it four times. Then she blew her nose on her sleeve, which she would deny forever, and opened the next one.

She read until the lamp burned low. The letters changed as the years changed — shorter, harder, the prose of a man getting old in cold country — but the birthday ritual never broke. Year ten: an account of a grizzly that raided his cache, told with such relish she laughed out loud alone in her room and then cried because laughing felt like betrayal. Year thirteen: *I heard you keep bees now. Bees! Whose child are you? Certainly not mine, mine can't sit still for a minute.* Year sixteen: an apology so clumsy and complete that she had to set it down and walk to the window and look at the streetlights for a while. Year eighteen, the last one, written six weeks before he died:

*Sprout —*

*Hark tells me you've been seen up-country. That you're living on the escarpment like a wolverine in a hat. I have read this news forty times and I still don't believe it, except that of course I believe it, because who else would choose that rock pile but my girl.*

*I know why you won't write. I've known for years. You think I chose the mountain over you. Sprout, I chose wrong, that's true, but I never chose the mountain. I chose a debt. Every year I meant to pay it and come home and every year the proof was one season further off, and now here I am, an old man with bad lungs, and the debt's still sitting there. So I'm paying it the only way left. The ground goes to you. All of it. Whatever it costs me, and it has cost me everything, and I want you to know I'd pay it again.*

*Don't hate me, sprout. Hate the survey, if you have to hate something. It's a better target and it deserves it more.*

*— Your father*

Wren sat with the letter in her lap for a long time. Down in the street a dog barked at nothing. The lamp hissed. She understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that grief had been waiting for her for eleven years, patient as a trapline, and that she had walked past it all this way because she'd been too angry to step where it stood.

Now she stepped into it.

She cried the way she did everything — thoroughly, privately, and with considerable swearing. She cried for the birthdays. She cried for the letters sitting in dead offices while she sharpened her anger like a knife and used it on the memory of a man who had been writing to her the whole time. She cried because he had died in a saloon among strangers believing his daughter hated him, and because the last full sentence he'd written had been a joke about surveys.

Somewhere past midnight there was a knock. Theo, in the hallway, holding two cups of coffee and wearing the expression of a man who had heard crying through hotel walls and didn't know the protocol.

"I brought coffee," he said. "I can also leave."

"Come in," Wren said, and her voice was wrecked and she didn't care. "You knew him at the end. Sit down. I'm going to read you all of them, and you're going to tell me which parts match, and then we're both going to be sad, and then—" she wiped her face with the heel of her hand, "—then we're going to figure out how to take a hillside away from a corporation before their lawyers finish their breakfast."

Theo handed her a cup and sat. "That's the plan, then."

"That's the plan."

* * *

They filed the claim on Monday.

Not the easy way — Pemberton saw to that, materializing outside the land office with his grey smile and a sheaf of objections thick enough to stop a door. Prior corporate interest. Outstanding judgment against the claimant's father. Question of the claimant's residency. Question of the claimant's standing. He laid them out one by one like cards, and Wren listened to all of them, and then she reached into her satchel and dealt her own hand onto the counter: the marker rubbings, the photographs of the spring, the certified copy of the hearing transcript, and — last, laid down gently, like something breakable — the original second survey in Elias Marsh's hand, with her father's annotations running alongside it in grease pencil, dated years before the company claimed anyone had surveyed there at all.

"And this," she said, tapping the last item, "is the estate of Silas Crane, entered into evidence, witnessed by the county clerk, copies filed with the board, the newspaper, and my lawyer."

"You don't have a lawyer," said Pemberton.

"I do now." She nodded past him to where Theo stood talking to the clerk, pointing at boundary descriptions with the calm of a man who had redrawn half this country in his head. "He works cheap. Coffee and spite."

Pemberton gathered his objections with the air of a man re-racking a lost game. At the door he paused again, as he had in the parlor, and this time what crossed his face looked almost like respect. "Your father turned down five hundred dollars a year," he said. "Do you know what that was worth, compounded? He could have lived like a king anywhere else in the territory."

"Yeah," said Wren. "But then he wouldn't have been rich where it counted."

The clerk stamped the filing. The fee was paid — Hark insisted on splitting it, grumbling that it was the first honest investment of his life and he resented it deeply. Outside, the morning was bright and cold, and the escarpment stood up over the rooftops to the north, blue and enormous, its crown still holding snow.

Wren looked at it a long time. *The ground goes to you,* the letter had said. Well. Halfway there. A stamp on a document was not a hillside; the company's lawyers would circle for months, maybe years, and somewhere in a cell Voss was presumably already composing appeals in his neat, terrible handwriting.

But the claim was filed. The record existed. And the source — the spring in the limestone amphitheater where the whole river began — was now, officially, contested ground rather than condemned ground.

"Resurvey starts when?" she asked.

Theo checked his notebook, though she suspected he knew by heart. "Board approved provisional status yesterday. If we can produce corrected boundaries within the month, the protection holds through the investigation." He squinted northward, measuring distances the way he measured everything, in lines and grades. "It's thirty miles of escarpment top, Wren. In thaw season. With a chainman who's never held a chain."

"I held a spear-point on you once. That's basically surveying."

"It really isn't." But he was smiling, and he'd already started walking toward the outfitter's to argue with Hark about mules, and Wren fell in beside him with her father's letters zipped safe in her coat, riding over her heart like a compass that finally pointed somewhere true.

Behind them, in the land office window, the notice went up: *RESURVEY OF THE UPPER WATERSHED — PARTY DEPARTING AT FIRST LIGHT.*

First light. Of course it was. Nobody in this business ever slept past dawn.
