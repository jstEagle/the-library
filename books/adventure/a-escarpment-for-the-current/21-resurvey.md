## 21. Resurvey

The morning the resurvey began, frost still held the alder leaves in its grip, and Wren Crane stood at the head of the crew with a thermos of coffee and an expression that dared anyone to call the weather an excuse.

"Sun's up," she said. "Barely. That counts."

"It counts," Theo agreed, blowing steam off his tin cup. "Technically."

Hark, who had appointed himself cook and quartermaster for the expedition on the grounds that nobody else could be trusted with a stove, looked up from his cast-iron skillet with the air of a man preparing to deliver a verdict. "In my day," he said, "surveyors waited until the frost was off the line pins."

"In your day," Wren said, "you sold a dead man's route to a stranger in a muddy saloon and called it commerce."

"That was *good* commerce." Hark flipped something in the pan that might have been trout or might have been ambition. "Best decision I ever made, as it happens. But I still say frost makes steel brittle."

"Frost makes men slow," Wren said. "There's a difference."

Theo drank his coffee and let them argue. It had become the morning music of the camp, this bickering between the prospector's daughter and the outfitter, and he had learned that underneath it ran something neither of them would name — the particular affection of people who had nearly died together on a river and found, to their mutual surprise, that they liked each other.

The crew was small: six men from the county, two of them former concession hands who had come forward after the hearing with shaking hands and stories they'd been sitting on for years. One of them, a broad-shouldered Swede named Lindqvist, had wept openly when Theo shook his hand on the first morning. "I chained lines for that survey," Lindqvist said. "I knew the numbers were wrong. I told myself it wasn't my business." He had looked at Theo then, not accusingly, which somehow made it worse. "You know how that goes."

"I know exactly how that goes," Theo had said.

So they worked. And the work, Theo discovered, was unlike any surveying he had done in twenty years.

* * *

An honest survey is slower than a dishonest one. That was the first lesson. A falsified map can be drawn at a desk in an afternoon; a true one has to be earned foot by foot, chain by chain, through devil's club and scree and water up to the thighs. Voss had produced his beautiful fraudulent boundary in three days of comfortable lying. The truth was taking them weeks.

But there was a pleasure in it that Theo hadn't felt since his apprenticeship under Elias Marsh — the old mentor whose handwriting had started all of this, whose erased second survey had lain hidden in a cabin while the company built its lies on top of it. Each morning Theo carried both documents in oilskin: Marsh's original, yellowed and foxed, and the clean new sheets on which the corrected lines were going down.

They were redrawing the maps he himself had helped falsify. That was the thing he couldn't get around, no matter how many miles he walked. His signature sat on the boundary paperwork of 1911 like a brand on stolen cattle. Every corrected line he laid down now was also a confession, entered into the record in ink and brass.

"You're doing it again," Wren said one afternoon, finding him standing motionless over the transit, staring at nothing.

"Doing what?"

"The penance stare. You get it when you think nobody's watching. Like a dog that's been caught at the bread."

Theo huffed. "That's a generous comparison."

"It's accurate. Sit down." She dropped onto a granite slab beside him and patted the stone. "Tell me what line you're stuck on."

He unrolled the sheet. The upper river, drawn twice: once in Marsh's hand, once in the company's official version, and the divergence between them was a wedge of land some four miles wide and eleven long — land that had been quietly moved out of the permit zone so the vein beneath it could be logged and claimed before anyone asked questions. Eleven miles of country, erased with a ruler and a steady hand.

"I keep thinking about how easy it was," Theo said. "Not the forgery — I didn't do that part. But the signing. Marsh brought me the boundary package, and I signed where the X was, because he was my teacher and because the train was leaving and because it was easier than reading forty pages of coordinates." He turned the sheet toward her. "A man could spend his whole career being careful about bridges and grades and load limits, and then sign away eleven miles of somebody's country in ninety seconds because his coffee was getting cold."

Wren studied the map. Her father's map, folded soft as cloth from years in a coat pocket, lay against her chest inside her shirt, where she kept it the way other people kept lockets.

"Silas used to say a lie needs maintenance," she said. "Like a bad fence. You have to walk it every season and fix the places it sags." She tapped the official survey. "This fence has been sagging for twelve years. Voss walked it, patched it, threatened everyone who noticed. And it still fell down the moment two strangers compared notes on a sandbar." She glanced at him sideways. "You want absolution, Reyes, you won't find it in a signature. You'll find it here." She tapped the new sheets instead. "Line by line. Same way you lost it."

It was, Theo thought later, the closest thing to a sermon the escarpment country had ever produced, delivered by a woman eating dried salmon with her knife.

* * *

The news came upriver in the third week, carried by a boy on a mule who had ridden two days from the county seat with a newspaper folded in his jacket and an expression of enormous self-importance.

"Board's ruling!" he announced, before his mule had even stopped. "Concession's revoked! All permits suspended pending criminal investigation! It's in the paper!"

Hark took the paper and read aloud, doing voices, because Hark could not read anything aloud without doing voices — the board chairman got a pompous basso, the company lawyer a nasal whine, and Voss's name, when it appeared in the account of his arrest, was pronounced with such relish that Lindqvist laughed for the first time since joining the crew.

"'...the board finds the original boundary documentation of 1911 to be materially falsified,'" Hark intoned, "'and further finds that the source waters of the Kettle River, together with the surrounding escarpment watershed, warrant permanent protection as public domain.'" He lowered the paper. "Permanent protection. You hear that? Somebody's grandfather is going to fish that spring someday and never know it almost belonged to men who burned bridges."

"Bridges," Theo said, "and more than bridges."

Wren took the paper and read the rest herself, silently, her lips moving. When she reached the paragraph about the watershed being set aside pending formal reservation, she stopped, and folded the paper along the crease, very carefully, and put it inside her shirt next to the map.

"Well," she said, in a voice that was suspiciously thick. "Somebody should tell the river."

"Somebody will," Theo said.

They told the river that evening. It was Wren's idea and nobody laughed at it, though Hark pretended to be busy with the skillet. She stood at the bank where the current ran quick and clear over gravel the color of hammered copper, and she read the relevant paragraph aloud to the water, word for word, the way you'd read a letter to someone who'd waited a long time for good news. The river didn't answer. Rivers never do. But the light came down through the pines the way it does late in the day, all gold and long shadows, and Theo thought: if water can be glad, it is glad now.

* * *

The resignation letter took him four drafts.

The first was too angry, full of phrases like *complicit institution* and *culture of convenient blindness*, and he burned it in the stove because anger made a poor foundation for anything you meant to build on. The second was too polite and sounded like a man asking permission to leave. The third he wrote at midnight by lantern light and actually liked, until he reread it in the morning and found it was really addressed to Elias Marsh, dead two years, rather than to the living men at the division office.

The fourth draft he wrote on a flat rock in the sun, with Wren nearby mending net and offering commentary.

"'Dear Sirs,'" she read over his shoulder. "Sirs? You've met those men. They'd sell their mothers' graves for a right-of-way."

"It's a formal letter."

"It's a eulogy. Write it like one." She threaded her needle. "Say what you did. Say what they did. Say thank you for the parts worth keeping, and goodbye to the rest. Shorter than a sermon. Longer than a telegram."

So he wrote:

*Messrs. Aldous & Frey, Division Office, Northern Cascade Railway —*

*I am resigning my position as engineer and surveyor, effective immediately.*

*I helped build your maps for nineteen years, and for most of that time I believed the work was neutral — that a line drawn on paper was only a line, and what happened afterward was somebody else's department. I no longer believe that. Every abandonment order I drafted closed a town's door. Every boundary I signed without reading gave away ground that belonged to someone. I was not corrupt. I was comfortable, which proved worse, because comfort never has to look at itself.*

*I do not write this in bitterness. The railroad taught me precision, and precision, pointed honestly, is a form of respect — for the land, for the people on it, for the truth. I mean to spend the rest of my career pointing it that way. There is a watershed above the Kettle that needs a surveyor who reads every page before signing. I have applied for the position. I expect to be hired.*

*Respectfully,*
*Theo Reyes*

"He'll hate 'I expect to be hired,'" Hark said, reading upside-down from his chair. "Old Aldous hates confidence in the lower orders."

"Good," said Wren.

Theo read it once more, folded it, and slid it into his breast pocket. He did not mail it. Some letters are not messages; they are errands, and errands deserve to be delivered by hand.

* * *

He went down to the county seat alone, on the mail boat, wearing the only coat he owned that didn't smell permanently of woodsmoke. The division office occupied the corner of the freight depot, all varnished oak and brass fittings, and smelled of ink and cigar smoke and the particular dust that accumulates around ledgers nobody opens.

Aldous received him in the inner office, behind a desk you could have landed a canoe on. The old man was smaller than Theo remembered, grayer, but the eyes were the same — quick, appraising, always calculating what a thing cost and what it could be sold for.

"Reyes." Aldous gestured at the chair. "Heard you've been playing woodsman. The papers made quite a meal of it. Voss, the hearing, all that." He laced his fingers. "Messy business. But it's blown over now, mostly. Which is why I'm glad you came. There's a section chief's post open in the southern district. Good pay. Your reputation's actually *improved*, if you can believe it — the papers like a whistleblower. We could use a man the public trusts."

"That's why I'm here," Theo said. He took out the letter and laid it on the desk.

Aldous read it. Theo watched him read it, watched the expression move from professional courtesy to puzzlement to something colder and more careful, the way ice forms on still water.

"You've written this in anger," Aldous said at last. "Understandable, after your ordeal. But feelings cool, Reyes, and careers—"

"I wrote it in October," Theo said. "On a rock. In the sun. With a woman mending nets beside me telling me to make it shorter than a sermon." He stood. "I'm not angry, Mr. Aldous. That's the whole point. I spent nineteen years being comfortable instead of careful, and I'm done with it. The railroad drew its lines through other people's lives and called it engineering. I'm going to spend the rest of mine drawing them the honest way."

Aldous looked at the letter a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he sighed — a real sigh, tired, almost human.

"Nineteen years," he said. "You were the best boundary man I had. Do you know why?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Because you never loved the railroad, Reyes. You loved the *country*. The rest of us loved the railroad. That's the difference, and it's the reason you're sitting there resigning and I'm sitting here signing the paperwork." He pulled a form toward him. "Where do I send your final pay?"

"Care of Hark's Outfitting, Upper Kettle Landing," Theo said. "And Mr. Aldous — thank you for the section chief's offer. Truly."

"Don't thank me. Go survey something." Aldous signed the form without looking at it. "And Reyes — the man who replaces you will love the railroad. God help whatever valley he's assigned to."

Outside, the afternoon had gone amber, and the depot clock struck four with the fat, contented sound of machinery that has never once questioned itself. Theo stood on the platform a moment, lighter than he had been in twenty years, and watched the evening freight pull out — cars he would never ride, schedules he would never keep, a whole humming apparatus of lines and erasures carrying on without him.

Then he turned his back on it and went down to the river landing, where the mail boat waited to carry him home.

* * *

Home. He tried the word out on the trip upriver and found it fit better each mile.

The others had gotten ahead of him in his absence, and he caught the camp at dusk, pitched now on the bench above the spring itself — the limestone amphitheater where the Kettle rose cold and certain out of the dark of the escarpment. The company stakes were gone, pulled and stacked for burning. In their place, fresh cedar posts marked the proposed reserve boundary, and on the tallest of them Wren had nailed a board with the words SOURCE COUNTRY — SURVEY IN PROGRESS painted in her blunt, uncompromising hand.

She met him at the landing with a lantern, because the light was going, and because — though she'd deny it under oath — she liked seeing boats come up the river.

"Well?" she said. "Did you deliver it?"

"Delivered. Signed. Filed." He climbed out onto the gravel. "Aldous says I never loved the railroad."

"Huh." Wren considered this. "Was he insulted?"

"I think it was the nicest thing anyone at that office ever said to me."

Behind her, up the slope, Hark's voice boomed across the water — something about beans and about how if Reyes expected supper he'd best hurry, because the crew ate like timber wolves and there were rules of succession. Lindqvist laughed. Somewhere a fire crackled, and the spring murmured its one endless syllable beneath everything, the sound the whole country was built around.

Wren held the lantern higher, studying his face. "So that's it," she said. "Nineteen years, ended with a letter."

"Not ended," Theo said, looking past her, up the darkening slope to where the new boundary posts caught the last of the light. "Corrected. Line by line." He shouldered his pack. "Come on. I want to see how much of the east boundary you finished without me — and I warn you now, if your chaining's sloppy, I'm making you run it again."

"Sloppy," Wren repeated, with dangerous calm, turning up the trail. "*Sloppy.* My father chained that ground for six years, Reyes, and I learned at his knee—"

"—at his knee, I know—"

"—and I have *never* run a sloppy line in my life—"

Their argument went up the hill ahead of them, into the firelight, where the country that had nearly been stolen waited patiently to be measured — truly measured, at last, by people who loved it.
