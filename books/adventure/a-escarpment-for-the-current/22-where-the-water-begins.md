## 22. Where the Water Begins

The ice went out of the river on a Tuesday, with a sound like a rifle shot and then a long, groaning sigh that Wren swore she could feel through the soles of her boots from half a mile away.

She was mending the fence around her father's claim when it happened — the claim that now had her name on the paperwork instead of his, filed in the county seat in ink that had cost her three dollars and a small argument with a clerk who kept saying *Crane* like he'd never heard the word before. She stood up straight, hammer forgotten in her hand, and listened to the river break its winter silence.

"About time," she told it. The river did not answer. Rivers never did. But she had come to think of that as one of their better qualities.

The spring came on fast after that, the way springs do in escarpment country — all at once, as if the land had been holding its breath since October and finally decided to exhale. Trilliums came up through the last gray drifts of snow along the north bank. The alders along the portage trail budded out in a haze of green so bright it looked wet. And every morning the air smelled of thawing limestone and running water and, faintly, of woodsmoke from Hark's cabin, which had gone up again board by board on the same foundation where it had always stood.

Wren worked the claim the way her father would have wanted it worked — which is to say stubbornly, alone for most of each day, talking to herself and to the mule she'd traded two good pelts for and named, after considerable deliberation, Mule. She sank test pits along the vein's true line, the one Silas Crane had walked thirty years before anyone else knew it existed. She marked each one with a stake and a ribbon and, because it made her feel less foolish than she otherwise would, a few words spoken aloud to a man who couldn't hear them.

"Pit nine," she said, brushing dirt off her knees. "Six feet to bedrock, no color yet. You'd have hated this pit, Pa. It's boring as sin."

But some mornings, when the light came down the amphitheater wall just right and caught the water rising out of the limestone, she stopped working altogether and just stood there, letting it be beautiful, letting it be hers, letting it be his.

Those were the mornings she didn't talk.

* * *

Theo arrived at the source on the first Thursday of April, carrying a surveyor's transit on his back and a set of new maps under his arm, and found Wren sitting on the flat rock above the spring eating cold beans out of a tin pan.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early," Theo said. "The appointment isn't until May."

"The season's started. That makes you late." She scooted over on the rock to make room, which was as close to a welcome as she issued to anyone, though she'd been leaving the fire built up in the cabin stove on days she expected him, which everyone understood meant something warmer.

He sat, set the maps between them, and looked out at the spring.

It hadn't lost its power over him. He doubted it ever would. The water rose out of the limestone floor of the amphitheater in columns so clear they were nearly invisible, welling up without hurry from whatever deep aquifer fed them, gathering themselves into a pool the color of sky, and then spilling over a lip of rock to begin the long journey downriver — past the unrunnable gorge, past the burned bridge's black stumps, past Hark's landing and the drowned waystation and all the country he had once helped erase from the record. Every drop of it, beginning here.

"You've got that look," Wren said.

"What look?"

"The look you get when you're about to say something surveyor-ish and expect me to be impressed."

"I am a surveyor. It's a legitimate profession."

"It's measuring things and writing numbers down."

"That *is* measuring things and writing numbers down," Theo said with dignity, "and this watershed depends on it." He opened the top map and smoothed it flat on the rock, weighting the corners with stones. "New base map. First honest drawing of this river in forty years."

Wren leaned over it. She had learned to read his maps by now — not the way he read them, with the whole grammar of contour lines and benchmarks coming alive in his head, but well enough to find things. She put her finger on the spring, drawn there in fine ink with a small circle around it and a notation beside it in Theo's careful hand: *SOURCE — PERMANENT. PROTECTED BOUNDARY COMMENCES.*

"They printed it," she said.

"The county printer did. Mr. Aldous himself ran the press. He said—" Theo paused, and Wren recognized the particular pause he used when he was about to quote someone and found it mattered more than he wanted to admit. "He said it was the first map he'd printed in eleven years that he'd want his grandchildren to see."

Wren looked at him sideways. "You practiced that sentence on the walk up, didn't you."

"I may have rehearsed it slightly."

"To the trees?"

"The trees are very supportive."

She laughed — the real laugh, the one that had taken all winter to come back after the fever and the letters and the long grief of learning who her father had been underneath the absence. Then she looked back at the map, at the circle around the spring, and her laugh quieted into something steadier.

"It's real now," she said. "On paper. Signed and stamped."

"It's real," Theo agreed. "And I'm the man responsible for keeping it that way." He said it lightly, but they both heard the weight under it, because they both knew what it had cost — the hearing, the testimony, the moment he'd held his own twenty-year-old signature up against Voss's forgery and watched the board's faces change; the resignation delivered in person to a superintendent who had shaken his hand and said, with genuine bewilderment, *but you were good at your work, Reyes.* As if the work and the truth were separate ledgers that never needed reconciling.

"I know," Wren said simply. "That's why I let you sit on my rock."

* * *

Hark reopened his outfitter post the same week, with a fresh-painted sign that read HARK'S — OUTFITTING & GUIDING — RESERVE HEADQUARTERS BY APPOINTMENT, the last line added at Theo's suggestion and painted in letters twice the size of the others because Hark maintained that if a thing was official it ought to look like it.

The post had changed. There was a proper dock now, rebuilt from the timbers of the raft they'd ridden downriver with their lives, which Hark had insisted on salvaging against all sense. "Good wood," he'd said, thumping it. "Been through hell already. Nothing left to frighten it." There was a rack of maps for sale — Theo's maps, printed and folded, selling briskly to fishermen and geologists and one professor from the university who wept openly over the contour lines of the upper gorge. And there was a ledger book behind the counter in which Hark recorded, with the gravity of a man signing treaties, every party he outfitted into the reserve.

The first entry, dated the day the sign went up, read: *C. Party of one. Canoe, provisions, 12 days. Warned re: gorge. Will not listen. Good.*

"He wrote 'good' about me?" Theo had asked, reading over his shoulder.

"He writes 'good' about everybody who comes back," Wren said. "It's his highest compliment. You should see what he writes about the ones who don't."

"Don't what?"

"Come back," said Hark, not looking up from the ledger, "or don't listen. Same word. Saves ink."

There was a fourth chair at Hark's table now, and a fifth mug, and nobody had ever formally arranged any of it. It had simply happened, the way these things do — Theo staying late after a day of running lines, Wren coming down from the claim with a brace of grouse or a report of trespassers, Hark grumbling about the price of flour while setting another plate. By mid-April it was understood that Thursdays were supper nights, and that arguments about the reserve's boundary markers were to be conducted after the beans were finished, and that whoever cooked did not wash up, a rule Hark enforced with a sternness he applied to nothing else in his life.

It was at one of those suppers, in the middle of an argument about whether the protected boundary should follow the high-water mark or the geological seam of the limestone (it should follow the seam; Theo had the data; Wren maintained that Theo's data had a bias toward things that were easy to measure), that Hark cleared his throat and produced a letter from inside his shirt.

"Came up on the supply boat," he said. "Addressed to the Surveyor of the Protected Watershed. Fancy title. Took me a minute to figure out who they meant."

Theo wiped his hands and took it. The paper was heavy, official, embossed with the seal of the Watershed Commission. He read it once quickly, then again slowly, and felt something turn over quietly in his chest.

"Well?" Wren demanded.

"They've confirmed the appointment. Permanent position. Full authority over surveys within the reserve boundaries." He set the letter down. "They want me to hire an assistant. Train someone local."

"Huh," said Wren, in the tone of a person filing something away.

"And there's a second item." Theo tapped the page. "The commission's asked me to lead a public expedition this summer. Up the full length of the river. Documented, mapped, published. They want schoolchildren to be able to read about the source. They want it in the newspapers." He looked up. "They want the story told, Wren. All of it. Including the part about how it almost wasn't."

The fire popped. Out the window, the river went on talking to itself in the dark, the way it had for ten thousand years and would for ten thousand more.

"Well," Hark said at last, reaching for the beans. "You'll need provisions."

* * *

On the last morning of April, Theo went up to the spring alone.

He hadn't planned it as a ceremony. He'd come up to check the benchmark he'd set at the amphitheater's rim — a brass disk set in living rock, the fixed point from which every measurement in the reserve would forever be taken — and to take a final set of readings before the summer field season swallowed him whole. Routine work. The kind of work he'd done a thousand times on a hundred dying lines, except that this time the line wasn't being closed. This time it was being opened, and kept open, on purpose, for good.

He finished the readings, closed the notebook, and stood for a while without moving.

Below him the spring rose and rose, patient as anything, asking nothing of anyone. The trilliums nodded along the rim. Somewhere downslope a wood thrush was running through its song, trying phrases the way a man tries keys in a lock.

Theo took off his pack and sat down on the flat rock — Wren's rock, though she'd stopped claiming it sometime in March, in the way of people who have stopped needing to claim things — and let himself remember.

Silas Crane, feverish in a muddy saloon, pressing a grease-stained map into his hands. *You swear it. On whatever you've still got faith in.* And Theo, who had thought his faith was spent, who had believed himself a man whose whole career was a long act of erasure, swearing anyway — because the old man's hands were shaking and there was no one else in the room.

He had kept that promise. Wren had the map, framed now above the mantel in the cabin she'd built on her father's claim, next to the rubbings and the photographs and the single unsent letter she'd chosen to frame rather than file away. Promise kept, witnessed, done.

But standing there above the water where the river began, Theo understood that there had been a second promise buried inside the first, the way the vein ran buried under the falsified line. A promise he hadn't known he was making when he swore to carry a dead man's map upstream. A promise to stop erasing. To draw the line where the truth was, and hold it there, and stay to defend it — not because an institution required it, but because the ground itself deserved an honest witness.

*I'm keeping that one too,* he told Silas silently. *You should know. In case word travels wherever you are.*

The spring said nothing, in its vast and ancient way. But the light came down the amphitheater wall and struck the water, and for a moment the whole basin filled with a soft blue radiance, and Theo Reyes — surveyor, keeper of lines, resident of the escarpment country, home — sat on a rock in the sun and felt the last knot inside him loosen and let go.

Footsteps on the trail. He didn't turn. Only one person walked that trail like that, quick and certain, with a dog trotting ahead of her that she absolutely had not been allowed to keep and had kept anyway.

"Wren," he said. "You're late."

"For what? There's no appointment."

"There's always an appointment," Theo said, making room on the rock. "This one's for the rest of our lives. Try to be punctual."

She sat down beside him, shoulder warm against his shoulder, and together they watched the water rise out of the earth and begin its long journey to the sea — every drop of it starting here, every mile of it mapped now, every mile of it safe.

Below them, far downriver, a canoe rounded the bend at Hark's landing, riding low with gear, and a figure stood up in it and waved both arms over its head like a man signaling a train.

Theo squinted. "Is that—"

"Professor Aldous's students," Wren said. "First field class of the season. Hark said they'd be up today." She paused. "One of them is going to fall in the shallows. They always do. We should probably go watch."

"We should probably go help."

"We can do both."

They got up together and started down the trail toward the river, toward the landing, toward the noise and the work and the whole unfolding life of it — and behind them, unhurried, the spring went on giving the river its beginning, the way it always had, the way it always would.
