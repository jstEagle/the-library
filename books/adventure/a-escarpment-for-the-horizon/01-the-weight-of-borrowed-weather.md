## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Weather

The storm came up the escarpment like something with an appointment to keep, and Zephyrine Sterling was already waiting for it.

She'd been on the ridge since noon, her camera rig lashed to a tripod that had cost more than she could afford in the year she could still afford things. Below her, the river town of Porte-Clair spread along its bend, tin roofs going silver as the light died. Above her, the sky had turned the color of a bruise deciding where to settle.

"Come on," she murmured, watching the anvil cloud stack itself against the high country. "Be beautiful. Be worth money."

That was the whole religion now: be worth money. Three years since the last network contract, two years since the creditors started calling themselves optimists—a joke among themselves, apparently, because they still believed in her future the way you believe in a horse that keeps throwing riders. Eighteen months since she'd run far enough upriver to become a rumor with a camera. Porte-Clair didn't ask questions. It was a town built on what came down the water and what went back up it, and nobody there looked too hard at either direction.

Storm footage sold. That was the arithmetic she lived by. A good mesocyclone over the escarpment, shot from the right angle with the right glass, could buy four months of invisibility. She'd gotten good at reading the sky again—better than she'd ever been, because now it wasn't passion driving her, it was hunger, and hunger is a sharper instrument than love.

The first gust hit at 4:17, warm and smelling of iron. She felt it through the soles of her boots before she heard it in the grass, and she smiled despite herself, because whatever else had been taken from her, this hadn't. The storm didn't know about her debts. The storm didn't care who she owed or why. It just came, enormous and honest, and for the twenty minutes it took to cross the valley she could pretend she was still the woman who used to chase weather for networks instead of pawnshops.

She got the wall cloud. She got the rotation, tight and mean, spinning dust off the escarpment's shoulder. She got the moment the rain curtain dropped all at once, a gray waterfall swallowing the far hills—and she was framing the lightning when she saw the boat.

Not on the river. On the ridge road, which was absurd, except it wasn't a boat, it was a long black car riding the dirt track toward her with the calm of something that had never once needed to hurry.

Zephyrine's stomach did what it always did when a black car appeared near her: it counted exits.

There was one road down. The escarpment trail behind her, steep and slick, useless with the rig. She stood very still and kept filming, because running confirmed guilt, and because some stubborn animal part of her was tired of confirming anything.

The car stopped thirty meters away. A woman got out.

She was maybe sixty, dressed in a linen suit the color of bone, and she walked across the wet grass without looking down once, as if mud were a problem other people had. Two men stayed by the vehicle—one broad and quiet, standing with the patience of furniture. The other leaned against the fender and watched Zephyrine with the flat professional interest of someone pricing an object.

"You're filming," the woman said when she got close. Not a question. Her voice carried under the wind without strain, the way certain voices do, voices that have spent decades being listened to.

"I'm working."

"Are you." She looked at the rig, then at Zephyrine, and her gaze had weight, physical weight, like a hand on the sternum. "Zephyrine Sterling. You were better known once. *Atmosphere*, the documentary series. The Kansas season. I watched it twice."

"It's available on exactly no platforms anymore."

"Which is why I watched it twice. Before it vanished." The woman extended a hand. Her grip was dry and precise. "Odile Brant."

Zephyrine knew the name the way you know the name of a disease common to your region. Brant Capital. Brant Riverine Holdings. Half the barges on the lower water flew her flag, and the other half wished they did. In Porte-Clair, people said her name quietly, not from fear exactly—from the particular respect owed to weather.

"You hold paper on me," Zephyrine said.

"I hold several people's paper. Yours I bought eighteen months ago from a man in the capital who was tired of waiting." Odile glanced at the storm building behind them, unbothered by the first fat drops starting to land. "You've been paying eleven percent interest in footage and silence. It ends today. One way or another."

Lightning stitched the horizon. Zephyrine didn't flinch, though she wanted to. "One way is you take my camera and sell it, which gets you maybe four hundred dollars of your forty thousand."

"The other way is work." Odile turned slightly, and the quiet man by the car came forward. Up close he was younger than he'd seemed—young twenties, maybe, with a face that gave away nothing and hands that looked like they'd done everything. "This is Tomas. He'll be on the crew. So will you."

"Crew for what?"

"A rescue." Odile said the word carefully, tasting it. "Six weeks ago, an expedition went upriver past the last mapped tributary. Archaeological survey. Eleven people, led by Dr. Anders Halvorsen—a name you may know, if you follow the journals. They stopped transmitting. Satellite phone dead, radio dead, beacon never triggered. The families are loud. The insurers are louder. And I financed the expedition, which makes me loudest of all."

"So send professionals."

"I am sending professionals. Amara Okonkwo pilots the boat; she knows the upper water better than any map does. Yusuf Adeyemi runs medical. Tomas handles logistics." A pause, thin as a blade. "And I need someone who can document. If we find survivors, the world will want to see them found. If we find bodies—" she shrugged, elegant even in the rain, "—the world will want to see those too. Either way, the record matters. Your debt clears on completion. Forty thousand dollars, gone. Clean slate."

Rain ran down Zephyrine's neck. She thought about the arithmetic, because arithmetic was safe. Six weeks' pay, effectively, minus the fact that the last three expeditions past the tributary had come back short-handed or not at all. Minus the fact that Odile Brant did not spend money on sentiment, and a rescue mounted six weeks late, by a financier, in the dry season, was either desperate or dishonest, and Odile Brant did not look like a desperate woman.

"Why me?" Zephyrine asked. "You could hire anyone. You could hire someone grateful."

"I could hire someone grateful, and they would film what I tell them to film." Odile smiled, and it was almost warm. "I want someone with nothing left to lose and a reputation for honesty. The combination is rare. You're the only one I found."

"That's not a compliment."

"It's an inventory." She stepped closer, and the rain chose that moment to thicken, drumming on the camera's rain cover, hissing in the grass. "Understand me, Ms. Sterling. I bought your debt because I buy things that are useful. But the optimists you owe—they meet Thursday. All of them, together, in one room, with documents. Some of them have been patient. One of them has not. If you're not on that boat when it leaves, I stop being patient on your behalf, and you can negotiate with the gentleman who has not been patient directly. He has a different style than mine."

Zephyrine looked at the storm. It was a gorgeous thing, the whole western sky organized around a single rotating heart, and she had been filming it for ten minutes and already knew the footage was the best she'd shot in two years. It would sell. It would buy her until spring. And in spring there would be another storm, and another sale, and another Thursday somewhere with men who weren't patient, and the wheel would turn and turn and she would grow old on a ridge filming borrowed weather for people who owned the paper on her lungs.

Invisibility had failed. That was the truth of it, arriving the way truths do—not dramatically, but all at once, like the rain. She had run upriver and hidden in a town that didn't look at strangers, and it hadn't worked, because debts don't need to find you. They just wait where you're going.

"When does the boat leave?" she said.

"Tomorrow, dawn. North dock." Odile was already turning back toward the car, satisfied, a woman closing a file. "Bring the camera. Bring nothing else you love."

Tomas lingered a half-step behind his employer. He looked at Zephyrine—not at the rig, at her—and said, in a voice so quiet she almost missed it under the rain: "Wear boots that dry fast."

Then he was walking back to the car, and the car was turning around on the narrow track with impossible smoothness, and Zephyrine stood alone on the escarpment with forty thousand dollars of weather rotating in front of her and no reason left to film it.

She filmed it anyway. Habit, or defiance, or the simple animal comfort of doing the one thing she still knew how to do. She got the lightning strike at 5:03, the bolt coming down clean and white onto the far ridge, and through the viewfinder she watched the storm walk away north—upriver, toward the unmapped water, toward whatever had swallowed eleven people whole.

Toward whatever Odile Brant actually wanted found, or wanted buried. She didn't believe in rescues mounted by financiers. She believed even less in coincidences named Halvorsen.

But dawn comes whether you sleep or not, and the north dock would be there, and the boat, and the pilot who knew the upper water, and the medic, and the quiet young man who'd told her to wear boots that dried fast—as if he expected her feet to get wet, as if he expected her to go deep enough for that to matter.

Zephyrine broke down the rig with hands that wouldn't quite stay steady, and started down the escarpment in the dark, carrying the best footage of her life and the worst decision of it, both of them heavier than they should have been.

Behind her, the storm went on without her, honest and unpaid, going wherever it pleased.

* * *

The north dock at dawn smelled of diesel and wet rope. The boat was smaller than she'd feared and uglier than she'd hoped—a shallow-draft river runner with a patched hull and a radio antenna mended with tape. A tall woman with close-cropped gray hair stood at the helm checking gauges, and a compact man with kind eyes and a medic's bag was stowing crates below. Tomas sat on the gunwale, already aboard, already waiting, wearing boots that looked like they'd dried fast many times.

The pilot looked up as Zephyrine came down the planks, and her eyes went from the camera bags to Zephyrine's face and back, unhurried, cataloguing.

"You're the documentarian," she said. Not warmly.

"I'm the debt," Zephyrine said. "The documentarian comes free."

Something moved at the corner of the pilot's mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where one might live. She jerked her chin at the deck.

"Stow your gear. We cast off in twenty minutes, and after the last tributary there's nowhere to stop that isn't a guess."

Zephyrine stepped aboard. The engine caught on the second try, coughing, then settling into its low patient rhythm, and Porte-Clair began to slide away behind them—tin roofs, fish smoke, the Thursday room full of patient men, all of it receding into morning haze.

Ahead, the river ran north into country no map bothered with, and the water was already dropping.
