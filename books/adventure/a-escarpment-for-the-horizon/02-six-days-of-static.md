## 2. Six Days of Static

The boat was called *Mercy of Small Debts*, which Odile Brant apparently found funny, and it smelled of diesel, mildew, and someone's abandoned ambition to smoke fish below decks.

Zephyrine spent the first hour cataloguing exits. The pilothouse had one door and a window that might open if you broke it. The stern deck offered water—fast, brown, indifferent to whether you could swim. She'd grown up around storms; she knew exactly how much a river could take from a person and how little it gave back.

"You're going to want to sit down for this," said the pilot.

Amara Okonkwo stood at the wheel with her forearms resting on it like she was leaning on a fence at the end of a long day. She didn't look at Zephyrine when she spoke. She looked at the river, which was doing what rivers do: pretending to be simple.

"I stand fine."

"Sure. But Brant didn't tell you what we're actually doing, did she? She told you *rescue mission*. That word does a lot of work." Amara adjusted the throttle a centimeter. "Six days upriver to the last known coordinates of the Halvorsen expedition. Nine people. They went quiet on the fourteenth."

"Quiet how?"

"Off." Amara finally turned, and her eyes were the flat gray of weather coming in from a long way off. "Not dead-air dead. Not mayday-then-nothing. Off. Like someone set the radio down and decided they were finished with it."

Yusuf Adeyemi appeared in the pilothouse doorway with two tin mugs of something that steamed like coffee but smelled like punishment. He handed one to Zephyrine without asking if she wanted it, which told her he was the kind of doctor who treated patients whether or not they consented.

"Drink that," he said. "It's terrible. It'll keep you honest."

"Is that medicine?"

"That's breakfast."

Tomas sat on the engine housing aft, cleaning a rifle with the unhurried tenderness of a man grooming a horse. He hadn't spoken since the dock. When Zephyrine had boarded, he'd looked at her once—a long, level look, like a man reading the first line of a document he already knew the ending of—and gone back to his work.

She took the mug and drank. Yusuf was right. It was terrible. She felt more honest immediately.

* * *

The radio logs lived in a binder that Amara kept wedged under the chart table, and by the second evening Zephyrine had read them three times.

They were transcriptions, typed by some functionary in Odile Brant's office, timestamped and annotated. The Halvorsen expedition had checked in nightly at 21:00 local, six seconds of squelch, then position, then status. Routine as a heartbeat. Zephyrine read the heartbeats:

> **LOG 11 — 21:00:07 — HALVORSEN EXPEDITION**
> Position: 9°41'S, above third cataract. All nine accounted. Fuel at sixty percent. Morale high. Dr. Okafor says tell Brant the mosquitoes are winning. Next contact tomorrow.
>
> **LOG 12 — 21:00:04 — HALVORSEN EXPEDITION**
> Position unchanged. Found something today. Halvorsen is beside himself. Will explain when we're back in range. All well. Next contact tomorrow.
>
> **LOG 13 — 21:00:09 — HALVORSEN EXPEDITION**
> Position unchanged. All well. Next contact tomorrow.

Then nothing. Nine days of nothing, until Odile Brant bought a boat and a debt and a storm chaser.

"It's the middle log," Zephyrine said, on the third morning, laying the binder open on the chart table while Amara ate rice with one hand and steered with the other. "Log twelve. *Found something.*"

"Archaeologists find things. It's their whole personality."

"They don't go silent over it. Look at the pattern. Log eleven is forty-one words. Log twelve is thirty-three. Log thirteen is twenty-two. They're getting shorter."

"So they got tired."

"People don't get tired of checking in," Zephyrine said. "I've listened to distress traffic for eleven years. Pilots, sailors, chasers caught out in cells they shouldn't have been near. Nobody stops answering unless they're dead, disabled, or—" she paused, because the next word sounded insane even inside her own head, "—or they don't want to be found."

Amara chewed. Swallowed. Looked at her with something that wasn't quite respect but was at least no longer contempt.

"You think they went dark on purpose."

"I think the transcript reads like a man deciding what not to say. Read twelve again. *Will explain when we're back in range.* Back in range of what? They were transmitting fine. Range isn't a thing that runs out overnight on flat water."

"Unless the radio broke."

"Then log thirteen would say so. These are careful people. Halvorsen ran three expeditions before this one without losing so much as a crate." Zephyrine tapped the page. "Something happened between twelve and thirteen. Something they all agreed, together, to stop talking about."

Amara was quiet for a while. The river talked instead—the long brown syllables of it against the hull, the occasional slap of a fish, the far-off complaint of a kingfisher.

"My grandmother used to say there's a place upriver that keeps its people," she said at last. "I always thought it was a story about drowning."

"And now?"

"Now I think my grandmother never wasted a word in her life." She flicked the throttle up half a notch. "Get some sleep, Sterling. Tomorrow we pass the last mapped tributary, and after that nobody's grandmother helps us."

* * *

On the fourth day the rain found them, because rain always found Zephyrine eventually—it was the one relationship in her life that had never required negotiation.

She stood on the bow with her camera bag zipped shut and let it hit her, warm and sideways, while the escarpment rose ahead out of the murk like the spine of something sleeping. Lightning walked along its crest, unhurried, showing off. Her hands itched for the camera. Eleven years of instinct said *that cell is beautiful, that cell is worth money,* and eleven years of creditors said *you can't eat footage.*

"You're smiling," Yusuf observed, materializing beside her with a towel she hadn't asked for.

"I'm not."

"Your face disagrees." He draped the towel over the rail between them, an offer rather than an imposition. "You know what I did before this? Trauma surgery, city hospital. Everyone always running. Everyone always bleeding on a schedule." He watched the lightning with clinical interest. "This is better. Out here the bleeding is optional."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"It's supposed to be conversation. I find people talk easier when they're wet and miserable." He turned to her. "Brant owns you. That's obvious. What's not obvious is what she thinks you're for."

Zephyrine watched the storm instead of answering.

"I've done two trips for her," Yusuf went on, mildly, terribly. "Medical support, both times. Both times the mission changed shape halfway upriver. First time we were looking for a missing survey team; we found them in a bar two hundred kilometers from where we were told to look. Second time—" he shrugged, "—second time I stopped asking what the mission was and started asking who benefits. You should try it. It's very clarifying."

"And who benefits this time?"

"That," said Yusuf, "is the question you should be asking the quiet man with the rifle."

* * *

So she tried Tomas.

He was on the stern deck that night, after the rain, while Amara anchored in the lee of a bluff and the sky cleared down to a spill of stars so dense it looked like the river had been poured upward. Zephyrine brought him coffee—real coffee, hers, hoarded—and sat down on the engine housing a meter away, which was as close as you got to Tomas without an invitation.

He cleaned the rifle. She waited. She was good at waiting; storms had taught her that too.

"The logs," she said finally. "You've read them."

"Yes."

"What do you see?"

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he set the rifle across his knees and looked out at the black water sliding past.

"I was bought once," he said. His voice was low and even, a voice built for saying things only once. "A long time ago. A man paid my family's debts and I did a thing I am not going to describe to you, and afterward the debts were gone and I was still here, and the thing was still done." He picked the rifle back up. "So when I read those logs, I don't see a mystery. I see nine people who found out what they were working for, and made a choice about it. And I recognize the choice."

"You think they chose silence."

"I think silence is the most expensive thing a person can buy," Tomas said, "and somebody upriver has already paid for it." He glanced at her then, briefly, and she saw that the flatness in his face was not emptiness but discipline—the expression of a man holding a door shut from the inside. "Whatever you're looking for, Sterling. Decide fast what it costs. Brant always knows the price before you do."

* * *

Day five. Day six. The tributaries thinned out on the charts until the map simply gave up, the inked lines dissolving into blank paper the way a voice dissolves into static.

Zephyrine spent the evenings reconstructing the transmissions a final time, reading them aloud to the pilothouse because Amara claimed she thought better with a voice in her ears. Forty-one words. Thirty-three. Twenty-two. And then, on the sixth night, with the escarpment looming close enough that its shadow fell across the deck hours before sunset, she noticed what she'd missed every time before.

"Amara. Log thirteen. Play it again."

Amara keyed the recorder. Yusuf leaned in the doorway. Even Tomas came to stand at the window, arms crossed, listening.

> **LOG 13 — 21:00:09 — HALVORSEN EXPEDITION**
> Position unchanged. All well. Next contact tomorrow.

"Twenty-two words," Zephyrine said. "But listen to the hand. Logs eleven and twelve were dictated—Halvorsen talking, casual, jokes about mosquitoes. Thirteen is different. It's being *read*. Someone reading off a list. You can hear the eyes moving."

"So?"

"So 'all well' isn't a status report. It's a password. It's what you say when someone else is standing behind you, listening, and you need them to hear exactly one thing." She looked at the recorder, at the small green light pulsing on it like a slow heartbeat. "They weren't reporting in. They were signaling through. To anyone patient enough to count the words."

The pilothouse was quiet except for the engines and the river.

"Signaling what?" Yusuf asked.

"That they're alive," Zephyrine said. "And that they don't want to be rescued."

Ahead, where the last mapped tributary bled into blank paper, the river bent around a shoulder of black stone and disappeared—and on the far bank, small in the failing light, a fire burned that had not been burning an hour ago. One plume of smoke, straight up in the windless air.

Someone had seen them coming.

And someone, it seemed, wanted them to know it.
