## 3. Where the River Forgets Its Name

The last mapped tributary entered the river from the east at a place the charts called only *Confluence 9*, as if whoever drew them had run out of conviction before they ran out of river. Amara Okonkwo cut the engine to idle and let the current carry them past it, watching the brown water braid together like two ropes being spliced.

"That's it," she said. "That's the edge of the world, officially speaking."

Zephyrine Sterling stood at the bow rail with a handheld anemometer in one fist and her hair whipping sideways. She'd been up since four, checking barometric readings against the satellite feed that faded in and out this far from any tower. "Wind's backing around to the northeast," she said. "Pressure's falling faster than the forecast said."

"Everything out here falls faster than the forecast says," Amara said. "Water. Pressure. My patience."

Tomas laughed once from the stern, where he was cleaning a rifle he never seemed to fire. Yusuf didn't laugh. The medic sat on the cooler with his medical bag between his boots, reading the radio logs again for the fourth time, and when he looked up his face had the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Six days of static," he said. "Then nothing. You know what I keep coming back to? There's no distress pattern. No mayday, no pan-pan. Halvorsen's last transmission is a weather report. A *routine* weather report." He tapped the page. "People who are drowning don't file weather reports."

"People who are hiding do," Tomas said.

Nobody answered that, which was its own kind of answer.

Amara pushed the throttle forward and took them across the line.

* * *

She had piloted this river for nineteen years, and she knew every family that lived off it the way other people knew their cousins. The Vermeuls at the fish camp. Old Sipho at the fuel dock, who charged tourists double and gave her diesel at cost because she'd pulled his grandson out of a flooded culvert in '09. The river was not empty. It was full of people who simply preferred not to be counted.

So when she saw smoke rising from the reed flats below the second bend, she eased off the throttle and raised a hand in greeting before she even saw who it was.

It was Marta Vermeul's daughter-in-law, standing hip-deep in the shallows with a net over one shoulder, and when the boat came alongside she did something Amara had never seen her do: she stepped backward, away from the waterline, putting distance between herself and them.

"Marta won't guide you," the woman said, before Amara could ask. Her eyes went down the length of the boat—Tomas, the rifle; Zephyrine, the instruments; Yusuf, the bag—and came back to Amara with something like grief in them. "Not you either, Okonkwo. Nobody will."

"We're looking for the Halvorsen expedition," Amara said. "Six people, upriver three weeks. We just want to bring them home."

"They're already home," the woman said, and turned back to her nets.

Amara held position for ten seconds, then twenty, the way you do when you're deciding whether to push. The woman didn't turn around again. Upstream, the river bent north into country Amara had charted only from the air—green going gray-green going green-black, the escarpment rising on both sides like the walls of a throat.

"The place that keeps its people," the woman said, still facing the water. "That's what my grandmother called it. She wouldn't say more, and neither will I. Turn back while the river still knows your name."

Amara opened the throttle and did not turn back.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar that wasn't on any chart, and Amara stood first watch because she didn't trust anyone else to read the water.

The river here behaved wrong. She'd noticed it all afternoon: the current stuttering where it should have run smooth, eddies spinning counterclockwise against everything she understood about the channel's shape. Somewhere ahead there was a rock garden or a submerged shelf, something big enough to rearrange the whole river's manners, and no survey had ever flagged it because no survey had ever come.

"You're frowning at the water like it owes you money."

Zephyrine dropped onto the gravel beside her, a tin mug in each hand. She handed one over without asking. Coffee, burnt, the way boat coffee always was.

"I'm frowning at the water because it's lying to me," Amara said. "Current's stuttering. Eddies are backwards. Something under the surface is bigger than anything on my charts, and my charts end tomorrow anyway."

"Barometer's still falling," Zephyrine said. "If it keeps this rate, we get real weather inside forty-eight hours. Not rain. Weather."

Amara glanced at her. On the boat, the woman mostly kept to the bow, filming clouds, logging numbers, saying little. But she'd been the one who spotted the snag field at the second bend before the sonar did, and she'd read the shoaling water through the afternoon glare and told Amara to favor the left bank, and the left bank had been right. Twice now. Amara hadn't thanked her. Gratitude, this far upriver, felt like a currency you shouldn't spend early.

"You're good at this," she said instead. "The sky part."

Something moved behind Zephyrine's face—not pleasure, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a debt she hadn't expected anyone to notice. "I used to be paid for it," she said. "Now I'm blackmailed into it. Same skill set."

"Odile Brant doesn't blackmail people who aren't worth blackmailing."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "She doesn't." She drank her coffee and looked upstream, where the escarpment had gone purple with dusk. "You've run this river your whole life. Do you believe what that woman said? About the place that keeps its people?"

Amara considered lying, decided against it. "My grandmother had a word for the headwaters too. She used it the way you'd use a name for God—carefully, and mostly in the negative. *Don't go there. Don't name it. Don't need it.*" She shrugged. "River families survive by knowing which doors stay shut. I always figured it was flood stories. Bad ground, bad memory, dressed up as spirits so children would listen."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting on a gravel bar that isn't on any map, drinking bad coffee, three days past the last person who'll admit this river exists." She turned the mug in her hands. "And I keep thinking about Yusuf's arithmetic. Six people go silent, and they don't call for help. Either they can't—" she nodded at the escarpment, the dead zones where the radio ate every signal they sent— "or they won't. And I honestly don't know which one scares me less."

Zephyrine was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "When I chase storms, there's a moment—the hook echo, when the rotation tightens—where you know the tornado has decided where it's going, and you're either ahead of it or behind it, but you're not in charge anymore. That's what today felt like. Passing Confluence 9." She looked at Amara. "We're not in charge anymore."

"I was never in charge," Amara said. "I'm the pilot. I just decide how we die."

But she said it lightly, and Zephyrine almost smiled, and for a moment the isolation loosened its grip—not gone, just bearable, the way fear is bearable when someone else is holding half of it.

* * *

The old family came at dawn.

Amara heard the pole-boat before she saw it—a soft rhythmic knock of wood on wood, the sound of someone poling a flat-bottomed skiff through shallow water with a lifetime's practice. By the time she got her boots on, the skiff was thirty meters off the gravel bar, holding position in the current with impossible ease. Two men in it, old and older, faces seamed the way driftwood is seamed.

The younger of the two—he might have been seventy—raised an open hand. Not a wave. A halt.

"Okonkwo," he said. His voice carried flat across the water. "You passed my cousin's nets yesterday. She sent me. Go home."

"We can't," Amara called back. "There are six people upriver who stopped answering their radio. Families are waiting on them."

The old man was silent for a while. The current knocked his skiff gently against itself, and he stilled it with the pole without seeming to move.

"Three weeks ago another boat came through," he said. "Big one, generators, white men with machines that beeped. They asked us the same thing you're asking—for guides, for safe passage. We said no." He paused. "They found someone else. A boy from the fuel dock, hungry enough to say yes. They paid him well." Another pause, longer. "He hasn't come back."

Yusuf appeared at Amara's shoulder. "Come back from where? What happened to him?"

"He walked into the place that keeps its people," the old man said, "and the place kept him." He looked at each of them in turn, and his eyes lingered on Zephyrine, who had come to stand at the waterline with her anemometer hanging forgotten from her wrist. "You have storm-eyes," he said to her. "You watch the sky like it talks to you. Listen to me the way you listen to it. The river ahead does not forget anything. It remembers everyone who enters, and it does not give them back, and the ones who return"—he shook his head slowly—"the ones who return are not the same ones who left."

"Sir," Amara said, "we have contracts. Obligations. People downstream waiting."

"Yes," the old man said. "That is how it always begins. With obligations pointing upriver." He poled the skiff around, unhurried, a man who had all the time the river would give him. Over his shoulder, as the current took him, he said one more thing:

"When the water starts going down, go down with it. Whatever you find up there—whatever keeps you—the dry season will not negotiate."

Then he was gone around the bend, and the river closed behind him like a door.

* * *

They ran all morning into country that got wilder and quieter by the mile. No more fish camps. No more nets drying on poles. No more cutbanks with cattle paths worn into them. Just the escarpment closing in, green walls rising on both sides until the sky was a ribbon overhead, and the river running deep and slow and strange, stuttering over whatever lay beneath.

At noon the satellite feed died completely. Amara watched the signal bars drop to zero and felt the last thread to the mapped world go slack.

Zephyrine stood at the bow, reading the sky. She'd been reading it for an hour without speaking, and finally she said, "Cloud deck's building over the escarpment. Organized. If this holds, we get our storm in two days, maybe less."

"Good news or bad?"

"Depends," Zephyrine said, "on where we're standing when it lands."

Ahead, the river bent north one final time, and the escarpment opened around it like a gate, and Amara brought the boat through into water no chart had ever named.

Half a mile on, Yusuf, who had the binoculars, went very still.

"Smoke," he said. "Upstream. And it's not a campfire." He passed the glasses to Amara. "Somebody's burning something big."
