## 4. Smoke Without a Fire

We smelled the camp before we saw it. Woodsmoke, faint and wrong—old smoke, the kind that has soaked into stone and stays there long after the fire dies.

"Two days," Yusuf said, lifting his face to the current like a man tasting wine. "Maybe three."

Amara cut the engine and let us drift. The river had narrowed here, running fast and shallow over red gravel, and on the left bank a shelf of flat ground rose above the waterline, screened by fig trees. That was where they'd camped. You could read it from fifty meters: the geometry of people who knew what they were doing. Tent lines taut. A kitchen fly pitched downwind of the sleeping area. A solar array angled at the morning sun.

Nobody was there.

Tomas went in first, the way he always did, moving low and quiet with his weight forward, and Amara and I followed because standing still on an empty riverbank felt worse than walking into whatever this was. Yusuf stayed with the boat. He said it was protocol. His hands on the tiller said otherwise.

The camp was intact. That was the first thing—the thing that made the skin on my arms prickle under the equatorial heat. I've spent enough seasons around disaster sites to know what abandonment looks like. Abandonment is chaos. Abandonment is gear scattered by wind or animals, food torn open, the small archaeology of panic. This wasn't that. This was order. This was people who had stood up one morning, looked at everything they owned, and walked away from all of it.

The tents were staked and zipped. Inside the largest one, sleeping bags were rolled—not stuffed, *rolled*, with the care of soldiers—with headlamps placed on top of each one like punctuation. A satellite phone sat in its charging cradle, screen dark. Next to it, a laptop, closed. A camera bag worth more than my truck had ever been worth leaned against the tent pole, unzipped, lenses nested inside like eggs.

"They didn't run," Amara said behind me. She'd gone very still, the way she does when the river shows her something she doesn't like. "You don't leave your glass if you're running. You don't leave your comms."

"No," I said. "You leave them if you're not coming back for them."

She looked at me like I'd said something obscene. Maybe I had.

The kitchen told the same story in a different voice. A water filter hung from its stand, cartridge still wet when I squeezed the housing—a day, maybe two. Cans stacked in their crates, labels facing out. A bag of rice, opened, folded shut with a clip. And on the folding table, three enamel plates with food still on them, gone stiff and gray under a skin of dust. Beans. Cassava porridge. One plate held a half-eaten mango, its flesh shriveled to leather.

I stood over those plates for a long time.

People who flee don't stop eating. People who are taken don't leave their plates arranged neatly beside their bowls. These were people who had been mid-meal, mid-conversation, mid-life—and then simply decided, all together, without alarm, that they were finished here. Not finished with the meal. Finished with the camp. As if the camp itself had become the thing they no longer needed.

"You're filming," Tomas said. It wasn't a question. He'd appeared at my shoulder without sound, which is his particular talent, and he nodded at the camera hanging against my chest. Its red light was on. I hadn't remembered switching it on.

"I'm always filming," I said. It was true. It's the closest thing I have to a nervous system.

He grunted and moved off toward the tree line, and I turned the lens slowly across the tables, the tents, the plates, and understood, with a cold professional clarity, that this footage would never sell. There was nothing here the news could use. No wreckage, no blood, no grief you could point a camera at. Just absence, arranged with terrible courtesy.

The journal page was Halvorsen's. We found it—or part of it—in the second tent, weighted down under a rock on top of a field desk, as if he'd meant someone to find it. One page, torn cleanly from a bound notebook, folded twice. Amara read it first, standing in the tent doorway with the light behind her, and her face did something I had never seen it do in six days on the water: it emptied out entirely, all that skepticism and river-hardened competence just draining away, leaving something younger underneath.

Then she handed it to me without a word, which frightened me more than anything else that day.

Halvorsen's handwriting started controlled and got less so as the page went on, the letters climbing uphill, the ink pressed harder:

*Day 41. The terraces are older than we thought. Older than anything. I keep writing the dates and crossing them out because they can't be right, and then Ines runs them again and they come back the same. Four thousand years before the oldest site in the valley. Someone built stone steps up this escarpment four thousand years ago and nobody knows. Nobody has ever known.*

*But that's not why we're staying. I need to write this plainly because I know how it will look from outside. We are not prisoners. We are not lost. The radio works—I checked it again tonight, deliberately, to be sure. We could call out any time we choose.*

*It's the settlement. They came down on the third night, seven of them, and they didn't threaten us and they didn't welcome us either. They watched. And then an old woman touched the ground where our fire had been and said—we think this is the translation, Marcus's phrasebook is imperfect—that the place keeps its people. That we had already been kept. That we should stop pretending we were going anywhere.*

*And the strange thing, the thing I will be mocked for if anyone reads this, is that she was right. Not mystically. Practically. Every reason I had to go home has quietly stopped applying. The grant, the department, the divorce—all of it looks small from up on those terraces. I have spent thirty years digging in other people's countries for other people's purposes, and this is the first time the ground has asked me a question instead of answering mine.*

*We are staying. All nine of us. If anyone reads this: we are not missing. We are found. We simply chose to stay found somewhere else.*

*E.H.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, because Amara asked me to, and hearing it in my own voice changed it somehow—made it less like evidence and more like testimony.

"This is a ransom note written backwards," she said finally. "This is a hostage letter where the hostages are fine and the kidnapper is a hillside."

"Or it's exactly what it says it is."

"Don't." Her voice sharpened. "Don't do that thing where you romanticize it because you've got a camera and a hunger for meaning. Nine trained professionals don't walk away from their satellite phone because an old woman gave them a vibe. Something happened here. Something we haven't found yet."

"Maybe," I said. "Or maybe something happened to *them*. Somewhere else. Before."

She stared at me. Downriver, Yusuf called something and neither of us answered.

* * *

We searched until the light went copper and then gray. Tomas worked the perimeter in widening circles and found the trail almost immediately—it was impossible to miss once you knew to look for it, a path worn smooth up through the fig trees, climbing toward the escarpment's shoulder. Nine sets of boot prints, recent, heading up. None coming down.

At the base of the path, someone had planted a stick in the ground and tied a strip of blue cloth to it. It moved in the evening wind like a small flag of surrender, or invitation. Tomas studied it for a long moment and then stepped back from it carefully, the way you step back from a snake you've decided not to kill.

"They went up there," he said. "On purpose. Walking."

"That's what the journal says."

"The journal says what whoever wrote it wanted believed." He turned to look at me, and in the failing light his face was unreadable, which from Tomas is itself a kind of statement. "I've seen people walk away from their lives before. I've helped some of them do it. It never looks like this. It never looks *tidy*."

"What does it look like?"

"Burned," he said. "Running. Fear. Somebody paid, somebody chased. This—" he gestured at the camp, the plates, the rolled bags, the blue cloth stirring on its stick "—this looks like people who were given something. And I don't trust gifts."

We made camp that night in the expedition's abandoned tents, because Amara refused to sleep on the boat with the trail leading away from it, and none of us argued. Yusuf cooked over the old fire ring, using their wood, and apologized to nobody in particular while he did it. The stars came out hard and close. Somewhere above us on the escarpment, wind moved through stone, a sound like slow breathing.

I lay in Halvorsen's tent with the journal page under my palm and tried to feel what I was supposed to feel. Dread, probably. Dread was the professional response. Nine people gone up a mountain, leaving their phones and their futures behind—every instinct I'd honed chasing storms said *something is wrong here, something is very wrong.*

But dread wouldn't come. What came instead was worse, or better, or both: recognition. Because I knew what it was to look at your own life and find that every reason holding it together had quietly stopped applying. I knew about walking away. I'd done it myself, two years ago, badly, in debt, in the dark. The difference was that Halvorsen and his eight had walked *toward* something. Upward. In daylight. Leaving their plates arranged as if they expected to eat again soon—just not here.

Something up that path had made nine strangers stay. And reading that page, feeling the smooth worn stone of the trail under my boots that afternoon, I understood that it wasn't danger. Danger leaves mess. Whatever lived up there in the dark above our fire had offered them something the whole world below hadn't: a reason.

I slept better than I had in years, and hated myself for it.

In the morning, the blue cloth on its stick was gone. So was the stick. The path stood open, bare, patient.

Above us, faint on the wind, we heard it for the first time clearly: voices. Many voices, high and layered, drifting down from the terraces like birdsong—and beneath them, unmistakable even at this distance, the sound of children laughing.

Yusuf reached for the radio. Amara caught his wrist and held it.

"Not yet," she said, eyes fixed on the treeline. "Not yet."
