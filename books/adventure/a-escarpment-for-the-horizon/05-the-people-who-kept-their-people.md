## 5. The People Who Kept Their People

Yusuf had stitched enough wounds to know the difference between a body that was dying and a body that was hiding. The first kind told you everything. The second kind told you nothing, and watched you while it decided.

The woman who stepped out of the cane brake at dawn was the second kind.

She stood at the edge of the clearing with a machete held low and easy, barefoot on wet grass, and she looked at each of them in turn—Amara at the bow line, Tomas with his hands loose at his sides, Zephyrine half-raised out of her crouch—and when her eyes reached Yusuf's medical bag, they stayed there a moment too long.

"You're the doctor," she said. Not a question.

"Medic," Yusuf said. "Who's hurt?"

"No one." She said it like a man reporting good news he didn't trust. "That's what I came to tell you. No one is hurt. No one is lost. You can turn around."

Behind him, Yusuf heard Zephyrine exhale through her teeth. "Halvorsen's team," she said. "You're one of them. Marta—Marta Osei, geophysicist. Your last transmission was day eleven."

The woman's jaw tightened. "You have our logs."

"We have your logs," Odile Brant said, stepping forward from the stern where she'd been silent all morning, "and we have a contract to bring you home. All of you. Alive or otherwise—the paperwork doesn't specify."

Yusuf watched the word *otherwise* land. Watched Marta Osei's grip shift on the machete, not toward them but toward the treeline, toward whatever was behind her.

"You should understand something," Marta said quietly. "We stopped answering because we chose to. And we will choose again."

* * *

They followed her anyway. Of course they did. Yusuf had known from the moment Odile hired him that this mission would end not with a rescue but with an argument, and arguments required everyone in the same room.

The path climbed away from the river through country that shouldn't have held a path—limestone shelves and hanging vines, switchbacks so old their stone steps had worn into smooth green curves. Someone had maintained these steps for a very long time. Someone had carried children up them, and bodies down them, for longer than any map Yusuf had ever studied.

Zephyrine walked beside him, filming nothing, which told him more than any footage could. For six days she'd kept the camera up like a shield, narrating the world into manageable pieces. Now she carried it holstered against her hip and looked with her actual eyes.

"How far do you think?" he asked.

"Older than us," she said. "Maybe older than the idea of us."

At the top of the final rise, the trees opened, and Yusuf forgot, briefly, that he was a professional.

The settlement sat in a fold of the escarpment like a secret kept by the rock itself. Stone houses with living roofs. Terraced gardens climbing the slope in green steps. Smoke rising thin and unhurried from a dozen chimneys. And people—dozens of them, maybe a hundred—turning to look at the strangers on the ridge with expressions Yusuf recognized from triage tents: not fear, exactly. Assessment.

Among them, unmistakable even at distance, were the pale sunburned figures of the Halvorsen expedition. Six of them. Standing among the settlement folk not as captives. As neighbors.

"They're healthy," Yusuf said, mostly to himself. He raised his binoculars anyway, ran them over faces and postures the way he'd been trained. "Look at their shoulders. Look how they're standing. Nobody's guarding anybody. That's not Stockholm syndrome, that's—" He lowered the glasses. "That's a community."

"That's my problem," Odile said behind him.

* * *

Dr. Ingrid Halvorsen was sixty-one years old and looked like she'd spent eleven days eating well and sleeping deeply, which was its own kind of accusation. She met them in the open square between two stone houses, and she shook each of their hands—each, including Tomas, including Odile—with the firm courtesy of a woman greeting guests she had not invited and did not intend to apologize for.

"You came upriver on a rescue contract," she said. "Paid for by Meridian Holdings. By Aldous Vane."

Odile's face did something very small and very controlled. "You know our financier."

"I know what he paid for." Halvorsen turned and gestured, and the crowd in the square parted—not dramatically, just the ordinary parting of people making room—and she led them along a stone lane to the edge of the terraces.

Yusuf had seen the journal page Zephyrine found at the abandoned camp. *Stone terraces, pre-ceramic, possibly older than anything in the hemisphere.* Reading about it had been one thing. Standing on it was another.

The terraces fell away below them in long arcs, agricultural steps cut into the escarpment's shoulder, and every wall was faced with fitted stone that bore no tool marks he could see—no iron, no steel, nothing but patience. Water channels still ran along their crests, fed by springs, feeding gardens that had been feeding people since before the word *garden* meant anything in any language anyone in his profession spoke.

"Fourteen hundred years of continuous habitation," Halvorsen said. "Minimum. The carbon dates are conservative. These people's ancestors were engineering water here while Europe was learning to boil things." Her voice stayed level, but Yusuf was a medic; he knew a pulse when he heard one. "And they never appear in a single colonial record. Not one. Do you understand what that means? They were never conquered. Never contacted. They simply kept themselves, up here, above the maps."

"And Vane funded you to find them," Zephyrine said.

Halvorsen turned to look at her, and something passed between the two women—recognition, maybe, of a certain kind of person who reads contracts twice.

"He funded me to find them," Halvorsen said, "so that they could be made to have never existed. Read your own charter, Ms. Sterling. Section nine. All cultural materials encountered are the sole property of Meridian Holdings. Section twelve. Site documentation is classified. There is no section for preservation. There is no section for *people*. Because officially, there are no people. A site with no descendants can be sold. A ruin with no heirs can be strip-mined for its artifacts and its water rights both. Vane didn't send me to discover this place. He sent me to inventory it before erasing it."

The square had gone quiet around them. Settlement elders stood at the edges, listening to words they clearly understood even in a language they may not have spoken.

"I refused," Halvorsen said simply. "I went dark. We all did. Eleven days ago I transmitted one final message, and then I let the world believe we were dead, because a dead expedition leaves no salvage claim." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "And now, Ms. Brant, you've arrived with a rescue contract signed by the man whose fortune depends on us staying missing. So. Rescue us."

* * *

Yusuf spent the afternoon doing what he was for: checking the expedition members over, one by one, in the shade of a stone house while children watched from a safe distance with the frank appetite of children everywhere watching something interesting happen.

They were all fine. Better than fine. The botanist's chronic knee had improved on terrace stairs. The grad student had gained weight. Yusuf wrote *NAD*—no acute distress—six times in his log, and the absurdity of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Six patients, zero injuries, one catastrophic diagnosis that belonged to none of them.

He found Zephyrine sitting alone on the terrace wall above the square, camera in her lap, lens cap on.

"You're not filming," he said.

"No."

"Why?"

She was quiet a while. Below them, the settlement moved through its evening—water carried, fires lit, a language rising that sounded like rain deciding where to fall.

"Because some things break when you point a lens at them," she said finally. "I've spent ten years selling other people's weather. Filming floods I drove away from. And every time, I told myself the camera made it honest—that if I recorded it, I wasn't responsible for it." She turned the camera over in her hands. "This isn't weather. This is people who figured out how to stay. And if I film it, I make it footage. Footage gets owned. Section nine."

Yusuf sat down beside her. "Vane will come for it regardless."

"Then let him come to witnesses, not archives."

From below, Tomas's voice rose, low and level, calling them down. Odile wanted everyone in the square. She had a proposal, she said. She used the word *generous*.

Yusuf stood and offered Zephyrine his hand, and as they walked down together toward the firelight and the waiting faces, he thought about triage again—about how the hardest calls were never the ones where someone was bleeding. They were the ones where everyone looked fine, and the only wound was the decision itself, and no one had yet agreed to make it.

Odile was smiling when they reached her. Yusuf had seen that smile before, on relatives in hospital corridors, right before they asked him to stop trying.

"Sit down, all of you," she said. "I'm going to save every one of us."
