## 6. A Debt Paid in Silence

Tomas had heard Odile Brant lie before. He'd heard her do it to bankers, to journalists, to a customs officer in Belém who'd held their cargo for eleven days and walked away believing he'd been paid in full. She lied the way other people breathed—without effort, without apology, without ever quite letting you see the moment it happened.

But he had never heard her lie this gently.

"Nothing found," she said, standing in the center of the settlement's main terrace with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman inspecting a vineyard she intended to buy. "That's the report. The Halvorsen expedition is located, deceased or dispersed—we'll determine which once we've interviewed them privately. The site is unremarkable. Erosion gullies, some recent masonry of no archaeological significance. The land rights revert to the consortium per the original grant, and the salvage contract closes out clean."

The morning light came down the escarpment in long amber bars, and it fell on everything Tomas wished it wouldn't: the terraces stepping up the hillside like the ribs of something enormous, the smoke rising thin and blue from cook fires that had been lit here for longer than any nation on the map, the children—children—who had come out of the doorways to stare at the strangers with guns and radios.

"Clean," Amara Okonkwo repeated. She stood with her arms crossed, pilot's jacket unzipped, and her voice had the flatness Tomas had learned to read as fury under pressure. "You're asking us to look at this—" she gestured at the terraces, at the stone channels that still carried water after four hundred years, maybe six, nobody knew yet, "—and write *nothing found*."

"I'm not asking you to look at anything," Odile said. "I'm telling you what the report says. There's a difference, and your employment contract is very clear about which of us writes reports."

Yusuf Adeyemi was quiet beside Tomas, the way he always went quiet when something hurt him. The medic's eyes moved across the settlement—the elders seated on the low wall, the young ones half-hidden behind them, Halvorsen standing apart with his ruined boots and his face like a man watching his own funeral being scheduled—and then came to rest on Zephyrine Sterling.

Tomas watched her too. He'd been watching her since the boat, since the static-filled nights when she sat up reconstructing the expedition's last transmissions with the patience of someone defusing a bomb. She stood now at the edge of the group, camera bag over one shoulder she hadn't opened, and her expression was the worst thing on the terrace: not anger, not horror, but recognition. She was doing arithmetic. He could see it. Debt against conscience, running the numbers the way she'd run the storm cells, looking for the gap she could fly through.

Odile turned to her last, deliberately, the way a card player saves the ace.

"Sterling. A word."

* * *

They spoke by the river, out of earshot—or what passed for earshot, because Tomas did not give them earshot. He drifted to the waterline under the pretense of checking the boat's mooring lines, close enough to hear, far enough to be furniture. It was a skill he'd perfected years ago, back when he'd been the one standing where Zephyrine stood, listening to the same offer shaped slightly differently.

"You owe two hundred and forty thousand," Odile said. No preamble. She never used preambles with people she'd already priced. "Accrued interest included. I hold the paper, so we can skip the theater about negotiating with your creditors. Here's the number: zero. You walk off this boat with nothing owed to anyone. Clean credit, clean name, no optimists with baseball bats finding your mother's address in Recife."

Zephyrine said nothing. The river slid past, brown and patient, already lower than it had been three days ago. Tomas noted that too. He noted everything; it was the only inheritance his father had left him.

"In exchange," Odile continued, "you help us clear the site. Equipment, records, anything Halvorsen dug up or wrote down. It goes on the boat, it goes downstream, it goes into a shredder and a landfill. You don't have to hurt anyone. You don't even have to lie, really—you just have to be somewhere else while other people do the lying for you. It's the easiest money you will never make."

"And if I say no?" Zephyrine asked.

"Then you say no." Odile smiled, and it was almost warm. "I'm not going to threaten you, Zephyrine. Threats are for people who don't understand leverage. You understand leverage better than anyone I've met—you chased storms for a living. So here's the weather report: take the deal, and by Friday you're free. Refuse, and you're free anyway, except two hundred forty thousand people-shaped kilograms of debt are still strapped to your back, and the men holding the rope are not as patient as I am. Same river either way. One of them has a waterfall."

Tomas closed his eyes briefly. *Same river either way.* She'd said nearly those exact words to him, nine years ago, on a dock in Santarém, when he'd owed a different kind of debt to a different kind of man and she'd bought the paper for pennies on the real. He'd taken the deal. He'd helped clear a site too—not this one, a smaller one, a fishing cooperative's land claim that dissolved into a mining concession—and he'd told himself the same things she was probably telling herself right now: *it would happen anyway. Someone else would do it. At least I'll be free.*

He was free. That was the joke. Freedom was exactly what it looked like from the inside: a smaller cage with better lighting, and Odile Brant holding every key.

* * *

He found Zephyrine that evening at the edge of the camp, where the terraces began their climb toward the escarpment wall. She was sitting on a low stone wall older than the language she thought in, turning her phone over in her hands though there was no signal for a hundred miles and they both knew it.

"You're going to take it," he said.

She didn't startle. He'd noticed that about her—she registered people the way she registered weather, early and without drama. "Are you here to tell me not to?"

"No." He sat down on the wall, an arm's length away, leaving the space between them honest. "I'm here to tell you what taking it costs. Because Odile will sell you the deal and never the invoice."

Zephyrine looked at him. In the last light she had storm-chaser's eyes, he decided—eyes built for reading systems, for seeing the rotation in a cloud before anyone else felt the wind. "You took one," she said. Not a question.

"Nineteen years old. My father borrowed against our boat and died owing more than the boat was worth. Odile bought the paper. She offered me the same arithmetic she offered you." He kept his voice level. This story had been told so many times inside his own head that saying it aloud cost almost nothing, which was its own kind of damage. "I said yes. I helped move some papers, intimidate some fishermen, burn a ledger. Small things. Everything is small when you're doing it."

"And then?"

"And then I was free, and I discovered that freedom purchased that way isn't yours. It's hers. Every year since, when she needs someone to stand quietly in a room, she calls me. Not because I'm loyal. Because I'm *bought*, and we both know it, and knowing it means I can never testify, never refuse, never leave. She didn't erase my debt, Sterling. She *became* my debt." He looked out at the river, darkening now, running visibly thinner over the stones. "Two hundred forty thousand sounds like a chain around your neck. It isn't. It's a leash, and it's shorter than you think."

Zephyrine was quiet for a long time. Below them, in the settlement, someone had begun to sing—a low, unhurried melody, a work song maybe, or a lullaby, something that sounded like it had been sung on this hillside since before maps. The children's voices wove through the adults'. Yusuf's laugh rose once from the fire, and Halvorsen's answering baritone, and Amara's reluctant near-smile that Tomas could hear even without seeing.

"They fed us," Zephyrine said finally. "We showed up armed and lying and they fed us. The elder—Mama Iyalode—she put the bowl in my hands herself and she looked at me like she knew exactly what I was deciding." She turned the dead phone over again. "That's the invoice, isn't it? Not the shredder. Her face, every time I remember that I took the money."

"That's part of it."

"What's the rest?"

"The rest is that you'll spend the rest of your life watching the horizon for her weather." He stood, because the light was gone and there was nothing left to say that experience hadn't already carved into him. "I'm not telling you to be noble. Nobility is expensive and it doesn't keep you warm. I'm telling you that I've been on the other side of this wall for nineteen years, and the view never changes, and you strike me as a woman who needs the horizon to mean something."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, the first he'd heard from her that wasn't armor. "You're a strange man, Tomas."

"I'm a bought man. It leaves strange hours."

* * *

He was halfway back to the fire when he saw it: Odile's satellite phone pressed to her ear, her back turned, her voice pitched low and fast in a register he'd only heard twice before. Reinforcements. The second team, upstream, the one she'd mentioned once and never explained.

And beyond her, past the firelight, the river lay exposed and gleaming in the moonlight—wide gravel flats where water should have been, boulders standing bare that had been submerged a week ago, the current shrunk to a brown thread in a channel built for a much larger river.

The dry season had come early. Tomas had grown up on rivers, and he knew what an early dry season meant: boats stranded, timetables broken, and everyone on this escarpment—crew, expedition, settlement, and whatever Odile had coming down from upstream—locked together until the water returned.

He looked at Zephyrine, still sitting alone on the old wall, still turning the phone in her hands like a woman waiting for a signal that would decide her life.

Whatever she chose, she was going to choose it soon. And the river, which had brought all of them here, was preparing to make sure none of them could leave.
