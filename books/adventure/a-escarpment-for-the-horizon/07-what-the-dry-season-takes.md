## 7. What the Dry Season Takes

The river began lying to us on the third morning after the settlement.

I'd been measuring it without meaning to—old habit from a decade of reading water for storm surge and flood stage, except now I was reading it backward, watching it shrink. The gauge stone below the landing had two brass rings set into it by some surveyor dead a century, and when we'd arrived the water lapped the upper ring. By dawn it touched the lower one. By noon it had dropped below both, exposing a shelf of mud glazed like fired clay, and the *Meridian* sat in her own reflection with six inches to spare beneath her keel.

"Amara," I said, crouching at the rail. "Come look at this."

She came. She looked. She said nothing for a long moment, which from Amara Okonkwo was the loudest thing she could have said.

"That's not forecast," she finally managed. "Dry season drop is maybe four centimeters a week up here. That's—" She did the math with her eyes, not her hands, because her hands were busy gripping the rail. "That's three weeks of drop in three days."

Upstream, then. Something upstream was taking the water.

Yusuf appeared with his medical bag over one shoulder, because Yusuf carried that bag everywhere the way other men carry worry beads. "The elders are saying the same thing," he reported quietly. "Old Halima stood on the bank this morning and called it *the river holding its breath*. She says it's happened before. Says there's a year—the word they use translates roughly as 'the year of walking'—when the whole channel went to mud and everyone moved."

"Moved where?" I asked.

"Wherever they could. On foot."

Tomas materialized the way he always did, soundless, a man built out of shadow and economy. He looked at the exposed mud shelf, then upstream, then at me. "Odile's second team is coming downriver," he said. "If the water's dropping, it's dropping for them too. They'll be walking soon enough."

That was the sentence that rearranged my whole understanding of our situation. We weren't stranded alone. We were stranded *together*—with whatever Odile Brant had sent upriver behind us, carrying permits and demolition charges and the particular patience of men who are paid by the result.

* * *

By afternoon the argument had burned through every possible version of itself.

Halvorsen wanted to move the evidence inland immediately—crate the records, the samples, the photographic plates of the terraces, and bury them somewhere only the settlement knew. His expedition members, gaunt but stubborn, were already packing. The settlement elders refused to abandon the site; it was theirs, had been theirs since before maps, and Halima said something in their old language that Yusuf translated with visible difficulty: *"You do not carry your grandmother out of her house because strangers are rude."*

Amara wanted to run the *Meridian* downstream while there was still water to float her, take the evidence out fast, get ahead of the story. Tomas pointed out, gently, that Odile's people would simply follow the boat down and meet it at the mouth with lawyers instead of guns, which would be worse.

And I stood in the middle of it, holding a radio logbook, feeling the strange vertigo of being the person everyone was looking at.

"We're not going anywhere," I said finally. "None of us. Not tonight."

They all turned. Even Halima, who understood no English, seemed to turn.

"The river's going to keep dropping. Whatever's damming it upstream—a slide, a sandbar, sabotage, I don't know—it's not clearing tomorrow. Amara can't take the boat through the shallows we already mapped, let alone what's coming. And if Odile's team arrives tonight or tomorrow, they arrive on foot, tired, wet, and angry." I looked at Tomas. "And you told me once that your people work best when their targets are scattered and afraid. So we don't scatter. And we don't act afraid."

"And what do we do?" Halvorsen asked. "Sing songs?"

"We make dinner," I said.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about enemies: they eat the same food.

We built the fire on the high bank above the stranded boats, in the lee of a fallen fig tree, because Halima directed its placement with the imperiousness of a woman who had been siting cooking fires since before I was born and saw no reason to defer to anyone's tactical instincts. Yusuf and two of the settlement women produced a stew from the expedition's rice stores and the settlement's dried fish and a handful of greens I couldn't name, and the smell of it drifted down the bank like an apology the river owed all of us.

We ate in a circle because there was nowhere else to put thirty-odd people.

I ended up beside one of the descendants, a young man named Ekwueme who had spent our first days at the settlement watching me with flat suspicion and who now handed me the ladle without comment. Across the fire, Tomas sat shoulder to shoulder with one of Halvorsen's surveyors, both of them silent, both of them eating, which between those two constituted a friendship. Amara argued engine specifications with a settlement elder who had once worked diesel barges downriver and knew more about fuel contamination than she did. Halvorsen was showing children photographs of the terraces, and the children were correcting him—gently, the way you correct a guest—on which ancestor had laid which course of stone.

Nobody had decided to become neighbors. It was simply happening, the way weather happens.

"You're staring," Tomas said, appearing at my elbow with a tin cup of something strong.

"I'm documenting," I said. "It's what I do."

"What you see?"

"People," I said. "Being people. It's rarer than you'd think."

He followed my gaze across the fire. For a long time he didn't answer, and I remembered what he'd confessed to me in whispers two nights ago—that Odile had bought him exactly the way she was trying to buy me, that he'd taken the deal twenty years ago and had been paying for it ever since in a currency he couldn't name.

"My mother used to say," he said finally, "that a shared fire makes liars uncomfortable. That's why powerful people eat separately. Private dining rooms. Sealed cars." He turned the cup in his hands. "When Odile's people come down that bank tomorrow, someone should make them sit here first. Before anything else. Make them eat."

"You think that changes anything?"

"I think," Tomas said, "that I have done terrible things for money, and not one of them was done on a full stomach, sitting next to a child, looking at a fire. There's a window. It's small. But it exists."

Downstream, far off, a night bird called three times and stopped, and the silence after it felt less like peace than like held breath.

* * *

They came at first light, as Tomas predicted—eleven men and a woman, descending the dry riverbed in single file with the weary, deliberate gait of people who had walked farther than their contract promised. I watched them come from the *Meridian*'s deck with binoculars while behind me the camp woke slowly, stew pots already breathing steam.

Their leader was a compact man with a government lanyard around his neck and a document case chained to his wrist. When he reached the bank he unrolled a tarp with theatrical precision, laid out papers, and announced to no one and everyone that he possessed demolition permits for the structures on the terrace site, issued under a land reclamation statute, countersigned, stamped, legal as morning.

Halvorsen went down to meet him. I stayed back, filming, doing the thing I was actually good at: watching.

What I saw was eleven tired people look past the permits at the fire pit, at the stew, at the children who had come out of the tents to stare at the newcomers with frank, unafraid curiosity. I saw the leader's eyes flick to those children and away again, quickly, like a man touching a hot stove. I saw one of his crew—an older man, gray-stubbled—accept a cup of tea from Halima without quite deciding to, his hands moving on their own.

And I saw the leader check his watch, and say something to the man beside him, and the man beside him glance upstream toward the terraces with an expression I recognized from a decade of chasing storms.

It was the look of a man estimating how long he has before the sky comes apart.

Because the sky *was* coming apart. I'd felt it for two days—a pressure behind my teeth, a heaviness in the windless air, the particular green-black bruise color gathering over the escarpment rim where no cloud had any business being. The dry season doesn't end politely. Somewhere upstream, above whatever had stolen our river, the atmosphere was loading itself like a spring, and when it released, everything that water had borrowed would come home at once—all of it, down this channel, in hours.

The demolition team had permits. What they didn't have was a hydrologist.

I climbed down off the boat and walked toward the fire, where enemies and neighbors sat in the same circle pretending not to notice each other, and I made a decision that would cost me everything I had left.

"Everyone listen," I said. "The river's coming back. Tonight. All of it."
