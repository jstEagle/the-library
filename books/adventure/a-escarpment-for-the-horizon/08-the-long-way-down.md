## 8. The Long Way Down

Amara Okonkwo had run the Kessu River for eleven years, and she knew its moods the way she knew her own hands. She knew the brown water of the wet season and the green glass of October. She knew where the sandbars migrated and which snags were honest about their position and which ones lied.

So when she waded out at dawn to check her soundings and found the channel marker she'd set the night before standing in eight inches of water, she understood that the river had stopped being a river at all. It was becoming a road—a bad one, going nowhere they wanted to go.

"Zephyrine," she called back toward the bank, not turning around. "You'd better come see this."

The storm chaser came down the slope with that loose, unhurried walk of hers, like gravity was something she'd negotiated a personal arrangement with. Amara had spent most of the trip deciding whether she trusted this woman. The verdict kept changing. A person who ran toward storms for money was either very brave or very broken, and Amara had learned those two conditions looked identical from the outside.

Zephyrine crouched at the waterline, studied the marker, then looked upstream to where the *Marta-Kay* sat listing gently in mud that hadn't been mud three days ago. Her hull was beached as thoroughly as a whale.

"How long do we have before she's high and dry?"

"She already is." Amara pulled the sounding pole up. "The forecast said the drop would level off by now. It hasn't leveled off. Either the gauges are wrong or the dry season is running ahead of schedule, and I don't bet on gauges."

"So we're stranded."

"We're *parked*." Amara let herself say it plainly, because everyone else on this expedition had developed a talent for euphemism and someone had to keep the ledger honest. "Odile's second team is coming downriver with permits and machinery. They'll have shallow-draft barges. We have a boat that floats in memory only."

Behind them, the settlement was waking—smoke from cook fires threading up through the fig trees, children's voices carrying across the terraces. Forty-one people who had spent four hundred years being invisible, and one archaeologist's crates of evidence that could make them visible forever, for good or ill.

Zephyrine stood there a long moment, watching the water shrink around the boat's keel. Then she turned and looked east, at the escarpment—the great stacked wall of stone that held the horizon up, honeycombed with shadow where the caves opened like dark mouths.

"The elders," she said. "They know a way over. Through the rock."

"They've never told anyone the way," Amara said. "That's rather the point of it."

"They're going to have to tell us."

* * *

The council happened under the great fig tree, and Amara sat at the edge of it because she was neither crew nor settlement nor expedition—she was the pilot of a dead boat, which made her a kind of ghost.

Old Sefu spoke first, in the trade tongue the elders used with outsiders, his voice dry as the riverbed. "Our grandmothers walked that path when the soldiers came. Our mothers walked it when the loggers came. Every time, the caves kept us, and every time, we sealed them behind us so no one could follow." He looked around the circle, at Halvorsen, at Yusuf, at Tomas standing apart with his arms folded, at Zephyrine. "Now you ask us to open them and lead strangers through. You ask us to trust you with the only road we have ever owned."

Halvorsen started to answer—the archaeologist couldn't help himself, he answered everything—but Zephyrine put a hand on his arm and stopped him, and Amara felt something shift in her chest, some small recalibration of judgment.

"You don't owe us the path," Zephyrine said. "I want to be clear about that. What Odile did to your people—what the people who paid him did—we haven't earned forgiveness for it, and we won't get it by asking nicely. But those machines are coming, and the river can't carry us away anymore. So I'm asking you to choose between trusting us and fighting alone. And I'm telling you what I know about fighting alone: it works right up until it doesn't."

An old woman named Mireille, who Amara had seen only once before, sitting alone at the edge of the terraces like a heron, spoke without opening her eyes. "The storm woman talks like someone who has been left behind before."

"I have," Zephyrine said.

Mireille opened her eyes then, and looked at her for a long time. "My grandmother led thirty-one people into the caves in the year the surveyors came. Eleven came out the other side. The mountain takes a toll even from friends." She rose, joints cracking like green wood. "But the mountain has never taken a toll from people who walked it together. Bring your crates. Bring your sick. Bring your boat pilot, if she can climb."

"I can climb," Amara said, surprising herself with how fast she said it.

* * *

They spent two days preparing, and Amara discovered that being useful was its own strange comfort. She stripped the *Marta-Kay* of anything portable—fuel, rope, the radio, the medical stores—and helped the settlement's men build drag-litters for Halvorsen's crates. The stone samples went in padded boxes. The photographs and journals went in oilcloth. The core drill sections, the ones that proved human hands had shaped those terraces centuries before any map showed anyone living here, got wrapped in blankets like infants.

Tomas worked beside her in silence for an hour before he finally spoke.

"You think she can pull this off?" he asked. "The cave route. With forty-one people and a storm chaser leading them."

"It's not Zephyrine leading them. It's the elders." Amara cinched a knot and tested it. "She's just the one who asked the right question."

"That's what leadership is," Tomas said, and there was something in his voice Amara couldn't read—regret, maybe, or envy, or the particular sadness of a man who had once been bought and knew exactly what he'd sold.

On the third morning, Mireille stood at the mouth of the first cave with a torch, and the exodus began.

* * *

The caves were nothing like Amara had imagined. She'd expected tunnels; she got cathedrals. The passages opened into chambers tall enough to swallow the fig tree whole, their ceilings hung with flowstone that caught the torchlight and threw it back in amber. In one chamber, the walls were marked with handprints—small ones, children's, pressed in ochre, generations deep, overlapping like leaves.

Yusuf stopped there and didn't speak for a full minute. When he moved on, he touched the wall lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder.

The pace was brutal. Litters had to be hauled up chimneys on ropes. Twice they crossed underground streams on stones slick as ice. The elders moved through the dark like it was a room in their own house, calling turns and hazards in their own language, and Amara followed the sound of Mireille's voice the way she'd follow a channel marker—because it was the only thing between her and the bottomless.

On the second day underground, Zephyrine fell into step beside her during a rest halt.

"You've gone quiet," Amara said. "For you, that's alarming."

"I've been listening." Zephyrine nodded upward, toward the stone above them. "Water. There's a stream running somewhere close, and it's louder than it was yesterday. If the dry season's pulling surface water down through the karst, these caves could flood from below. Fast."

"Should we tell Mireille?"

"I already did." Zephyrine smiled faintly. "She told me her grandmother timed the crossings by the sound of that stream. Said if it sang loud, you hurried." She looked back down the passage, at the line of litters and lanterns stretching into the dark. "Four hundred years of knowledge, Amara. Odile wants to erase a people who wrote their history in stone and song because paper burns too easy to prove them wrong."

"Then we'd better get it all out the other side."

They moved again. And near dusk of the third day, the darkness ahead turned grey, then gold, and they came out onto a ledge on the far side of the escarpment with the whole river valley spread below them—the shrunken river a brown thread, the abandoned camp a scatter of pale dots, and beyond it, far downstream, the smoke of Odile's advance camp rising thin and patient against the sky.

They had beaten the machines overland. Amara allowed herself exactly ten seconds of relief.

Then Zephyrine, standing at the ledge's edge with binoculars raised, went very still.

"What?" Amara said.

"There's a second column of smoke." Zephyrine passed her the glasses. "East ridge. Above the cave mouth we came out of." She lowered the binoculars, and Amara saw her face go flat and cold, the face of a woman reading weather she'd hoped wouldn't come. "They didn't follow us through the caves. They didn't need to. Someone showed them the old logging road over the top."

Tomas appeared at her shoulder, breathing hard from the climb, and looked where she was looking, and Amara watched the color leave his face.

"That road's been washed out for years," he said quietly. "There's only one man alive who could guide trucks up it."

"Who?" Amara demanded.

Tomas didn't answer. He just looked at Zephyrine, and whatever passed between them needed no words at all.

Below them, on the thread of brown river, the first barge horn sounded—long, low, and much closer than it should have been.
