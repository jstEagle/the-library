## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The Vell River had moods the way old men had opinions—stubborn, layered, and best learned by listening rather than arguing. Ivo Ashgrove had spent eleven years learning to listen.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with one hand on the brass rail and the other loose at her side, reading the water the way a priest reads scripture: not for what it said but for what it almost said. A sandbar announced itself long before it showed its spine. First came the change in current's voice—a hush where there should have been chatter, a smoothness that was really a lie stretched over gravel. Then the color shifted, brown going to olive going to a pale green-gold that meant the bottom was rising up to meet you. Then, if you were careless or new or drunk, the smell of silt churned by someone else's hull grinding to grief.

Ivo was none of those things. She cut the *Kestrel*'s throttle to half and swung wide around the hush, giving the bar more room than she strictly needed, because the river forgave caution and never forgave pride.

"Bar's moved," called Tam from the deck below, where he sat with his boots dangling over the cargo hatch, shelling sunflower seeds into the current. He was nineteen, all elbows and appetite, and he'd been with her two seasons now—the longest any deckhand had lasted, which she suspected was less loyalty than the fact that she paid on time and asked no questions about his past, in exchange for him asking none about hers.

"I see it."

"You always see it." He cracked a seed between his teeth. "One day I'm gonna find a bar you don't see, and I'm gonna laugh so hard I fall in the river."

"One day you'll find a bar you don't see," Ivo said, "and you'll be swimming home to your mother in Corvane Landing."

He grinned at that, unbothered. The boy had no ghosts worth mentioning, which made him easy company and slightly exhausting, like a dog that had never been kicked.

The morning fog was burning off in rags. Downriver, the town of Lowmarsh assembled itself out of the mist in pieces—first the grain elevator, then the bell tower of the parish church, then the tin roofs catching light like coins thrown on green felt. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight gray columns. Somewhere a mill wheel turned with its patient wooden groan. It was, by every measure, an ordinary morning on the Vell, and Ivo loved ordinary mornings the way other people loved feast days: fiercely, and with the knowledge that they could not last.

She brought the *Kestrel* alongside the Lowmarsh landing with a touch so gentle the fenders barely sighed. Three crates of salted fish out, four bales of wool cloth in, a passenger—a thin woman with a carpet bag and a cough who paid her fare in small silver and didn't meet anyone's eyes. Ivo liked her immediately.

"Name?" Ivo asked, pencil ready over the manifest.

"Does it matter?"

"No." She wrote *passenger, one* and closed the book.

That was the whole of her philosophy, inscribed daily in cheap pencil: names mattered to people who wanted to find you later.

* * *

Lowmarsh took two hours. Ivo did her business the way she did everything—cash, exact, no credit given or received beyond what the Syndicate already held against her, which was considerable and growing. She bought lamp oil, rope, a sack of flour, and coffee that tasted like burnt acorns and cost like saffron. She paid the harbormaster his fee and watched him write *captain of the Kestrel* in his ledger without a surname, as he had for six years, because she'd paid him a small quarterly sum to keep it that way. Everyone along forty miles of the Vell knew her as Ash, or Captain, or that quiet woman off the riverboat. Nobody living could tell you her family name, and she intended to keep it that way until she died, at which point it would stop mattering entirely.

There were no photographs of her anywhere. She'd burned the last one—her father's, taken when she was nine, both of them squinting in sunlight on the *Kestrel*'s foredeck—eleven years ago, the night before she ran. Sometimes she missed it with an ache that felt physical, a phantom limb: the shape of his arm around her shoulders, the river behind them gone silver. But a photograph was a hook, and hooks were how they found people. She had learned that lesson in a courtroom, of all places, watching a man point at an image of her father and say words that were lies, while the Corvane family solicitor smiled like a man watching a well-executed maneuver.

Ivo Ashgrove had been convicted of theft at seventeen. Her father had died in prison two years later, of a fever the prison doctor hadn't bothered to treat. And the name Ashgrove—which had once meant something good, meant charts and soundings and a man who drew the river true—had been dragged through the mud of three counties until even Ivo couldn't say it without flinching.

So she became nobody. It was the safest thing she'd ever done, and the loneliest.

She was loading the last crate herself when Tam appeared at her elbow wearing the particular expression he wore when something had gone wrong and he wasn't sure how much to say.

"Captain. There's a man on the boat."

"There's often a man on the boat."

"He's sitting in your cabin. Said to tell you he'd wait." Tam swallowed. "He's got a Harrow pin on his collar. The little iron nail."

Ivo's hands went still on the crate. The Harrow Syndicate's badge was a bent iron nail, because their founder had begun life as a debtor himself and liked to remind everyone that debts, like nails, held things together whether you wanted them to or not. She saw that pin perhaps twice a year, and each time it appeared, it took something from her.

"All right," she said. "Finish the load. Don't come below unless I call."

"Aye, Captain." He hesitated. "You want me to stay close?"

It was a kind offer, and it touched her more than she let show. "Stay on the dock. If I shout, run for the harbormaster."

"And if you don't shout?"

"Then mind your seeds."

* * *

The man in her cabin had made himself comfortable, which was itself a message. He sat in her chair, behind her desk, his hat set square on the corner of the chart table, his boots—one boot crossed over one knee—showing a shine that spoke of money spent on appearances. He was maybe fifty, heavy through the shoulders, with a face like a closed ledger: nothing entered, nothing visible. When she came down the companionway he looked up with the unhurried attention of a man who had already decided what she was worth.

"Captain." He gestured at the cabin around him. "Nice boat. Smaller than I expected."

"It does the work it needs to do."

"That's what they all say right up until it doesn't." He produced a leather folio from inside his coat and laid it on the desk, opening it toward her with one finger. "Merrit Stave. Regional collections, Harrow Syndicate. You'll forgive me for making myself at home; my legs aren't what they were, and yours is the first dry seat I've seen since dawn."

Ivo remained standing by the door. "What can I do for you, Mr. Stave?"

"You can sit down, for a start. This isn't a short conversation, and I hate talking up at people."

"I'll stand."

Something flickered across his face—not anger, exactly. Recalculation. He turned back to the folio.

"Your account," he said. "Principal of four hundred crowns, drawn five years ago against hull repairs. Interest compounded quarterly per the standard instrument. Payments received: irregular. Total outstanding as of this morning—" he turned the folio fully toward her, and there it was in neat clerk's ink, "—six hundred and twelve crowns, fourteen shillings."

Ivo looked at the number. Six hundred and twelve. Two years ago it had been five hundred and forty. The debt grew the way mold grew in a damp hold: slowly, patiently, in the dark, no matter what you did.

"I've paid down two hundred in the last eighteen months," she said. "Every crown on time when I promised it."

"And we appreciate it. Genuinely." Stave folded his hands over his stomach. "But here's my difficulty, Captain. The Syndicate has restructured. New men upstairs, new thinking. They've directed regional collections to accelerate recovery on all legacy accounts. Yours is flagged." He tapped the folio. "Flagged accounts are to be resolved within ninety days or collateral action begins."

"Collateral action." She kept her voice flat. "Meaning the boat."

"Meaning the boat."

The word *no* rose in her throat and she swallowed it, because arguing with a collector was like arguing with the tide. Instead she made herself do arithmetic aloud, calmly, the way she read water. "Six hundred and twelve crowns in ninety days. My margins run thirty crowns a month in a good season, after fuel and fees and wages. You know that. Your office knows that better than I do—you've had my books audited twice."

Stave nodded, unsurprised, almost gentle. "I do know that. Which is why I'm here in person instead of sending the usual letter. Letters get ignored. Faces don't." He leaned forward, and for the first time his voice dropped into something quieter and worse than menace: reasonableness. "The Syndicate will consider alternative arrangements. There are parties upstream who pay well for reliable captains who ask no questions. High-value runs. One season of those clears your account and puts coin in your pocket besides."

"What kind of runs?"

"The kind where the cargo is sealed and the manifests are blank." He spread his hands. "Before you refuse on principle, Captain—and I can see you winding up to refuse—consider what principle costs at six hundred and twelve crowns. Consider that the *Kestrel* is the only asset attached to your account, and that when we take her, we sell her at auction, and auction prices on the Vell this year are pitiful. You'd still owe us the difference. You'd owe us the difference standing on a dock with empty pockets."

The cabin was very quiet. Through the overhead hatch Ivo could hear Tam's footsteps crossing the deck above, light and quick, and beneath them the lap of water against the hull, the oldest sound in her life. They would take the boat. Of course they would take the boat. The *Kestrel* was not merely her livelihood; it was the last thing her father had built with his own hands, plank by plank, and the only place left in the world where she existed under no one's authority but the river's.

"How much do these sealed cargoes pay?" she heard herself ask.

Stave smiled, and it transformed his face into something almost warm, which was the most frightening thing he'd done yet. "Now we're having a conversation. Enough. More than enough. You'd be surprised how generous certain parties are with discretion." He closed the folio and stood, taking his hat. At the foot of the ladder he paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at her with an expression she couldn't parse—something almost like regret, or perhaps just indigestion.

"Ninety days, Captain. I'll come back downriver at the end of the season either way. Think about which version of me you'd rather see." He settled the hat on his head. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I'd rather you took the runs. Clean debt is boring work. Chasing boats is not."

Then he was up the ladder and across the deck and down the gangway, and the *Kestrel* was hers again, and somehow the cabin felt smaller than before he'd entered it.

* * *

They made way that afternoon, running downriver toward Landfall Point with the current helping and the wind quartering fair. Tam handled the lines without being told. The passenger with the cough slept in the bow shadow, her carpet bag clutched to her chest even in sleep, and Ivo found herself watching the woman and wondering what name she hadn't given, what she was running from or toward, and whether she too had a number written in somebody's folio that grew in the dark.

Six hundred and twelve crowns. Ninety days.

She could do it. Barely, if the season held, if the locks didn't raise their fees again, if the engine didn't fail, if nothing went wrong. But nothing ever simply went wrong on the Vell; things went wrong in cascades, one failure pulling the next after it like ducklings. And Stave's offer sat in her mind like a splinter. Sealed cargo, blank manifests, parties upstream. Parties upstream meant Corvane country. Everything upstream meant Corvane country, eventually—the family owned the locks, the mills, the warehouses, the newspapers, the magistrates, and, though no document would ever say so, the river's memory itself. Working for them would clear her debt and bury her deeper at the same time. Debtors could be released. Employees of the Corvanes were kept.

The cliffs of Landfall Point rose ahead in the late light, red stone streaked white with bird lime, the river bending around their feet in a long green curve. Ivo had passed this point four hundred times. She knew every seam of it, every fold, the way the shadows pooled in the hollows at dusk.

Which was why, when the tide gauge at the point showed a reading lower than any she'd seen in eleven years—an extreme low, the kind the almanac called a hundred-year water—she noticed, and slowed, and looked.

Below the cliffs, where the water should have been twelve feet deep against the rock, bare stone glistened in the failing light. And in that stone, revealed for the first time in living memory, was a dark archway. A cave mouth, man-cut by the regularity of its edges, breathing out cold air that smelled of salt and old paper and something else—something like dust, and like time.

Tam came forward to stand beside her at the rail, following her gaze. "Huh," he said. "Never seen the tide out that far."

"Neither have I."

"What do you figure's in there?"

Ivo didn't answer. The compass hung at her neck on its cord, as it had for eleven years—her father's compass, the one relic she'd kept, the one thing the court hadn't thought worth confiscating. She had never questioned it. It pointed north, more or less, the way compasses did, and she'd worn it the way you wear a dead parent's ring: for the weight of it, not the function.

Except that now, as the *Kestrel* slid past the exposed archway, she glanced down at it out of habit—and stopped.

The needle wasn't pointing north.

It was trembling, straining against its pivot, swinging in slow, deliberate arcs away from north entirely—away from the sea, away from downstream, away from everything sensible—and settling, again and again, toward the cliffs. Toward the dark archway in the wet stone. Toward whatever lay inside, waiting in the cold, with her family's name engraved on the case she wore against her heart.

"Captain?" Tam said. "You want to put in at the point tonight, or push on to Millbrook?"

Ivo watched the needle swing. Watched it settle. Watched it strain toward the cave like a dog toward a door.

"Put in at the point," she said.
