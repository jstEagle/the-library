## 2. What the Tide Gave Back

The tide at Landfall Point had a reputation the way certain men have reputations—earned slowly, in whispers, mostly by people who'd never seen it happen themselves. Old-timers at the Fishhook Tavern claimed that once every generation the sea pulled back far enough to show you what it had taken, and that anyone fool enough to walk out onto the revealed ground came back changed or didn't come back at all. Ivo Ashgrove had heard the story a dozen times over seven years of running the Vell River, and she had filed it where she filed all such stories: under things said by people who needed the world to be more interesting than it was.

But on the morning the water withdrew like a held breath finally released, she stopped filing and started watching.

She had tied up the *Marrow's Grace* at the old stone jetty below the cliffs to wait out a fog that sat on the river like wet wool. The fog burned off by mid-morning, and when it went, it took the horizon with it—or rather, it revealed that the horizon had moved. The shallows east of the point, which even at low water stayed knee-deep and brown with stirred silt, were simply gone. Exposed. A vast apron of glistening sand and ribbed mudflat stretched out toward the sea, pocked with stranded starfish and the dark lace of weed, steaming faintly in the cold sun as if the whole seabed were exhaling.

Ivo stood at the rail with her coffee going cold in her hand and felt something turn over in her chest that had nothing to do with wonder.

She was not a woman given to omens. She had spent seven years teaching herself not to believe in them, because a woman who believed in signs started believing she deserved them, and a woman who believed she deserved things started wanting them, and wanting was how you got caught. Her life fit inside the hull of a forty-foot riverboat: cargo runs between the valley towns, cash paid and cash received, no surname on any manifest, no photograph anywhere, no debts except the one she couldn't escape. She was nobody from nowhere, and nobody from nowhere survived.

And yet.

She climbed down the ladder to the jetty, boots ringing on wet stone, and stood looking at the exposed seabed for a long time. The cliffs at Landfall Point rose sheer above her, sixty feet of layered shale the color of old bruises, seamed with the nests of cormorants that croaked down at her like disapproving clerks. At their base, where the water should have been lapping, there was instead a long shadow—a horizontal darkness in the rock face, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging kelp, that she had never seen before in seven years of mooring at this jetty in every season and every state of the tide.

A cave. Or the mouth of one.

Her first instinct—the good instinct, the trained instinct—was to go back aboard, make coffee properly, and pretend she hadn't seen it. Curiosity was a debt too, and she already owed the Harrow Syndicate more than she liked to count. But her boots were already moving across the mud, and the mud made a sound beneath them, a deep sucking kiss with every step, as though the ground itself were reluctant to let her pass—or eager.

* * *

The seabed was a country unto itself, and Ivo walked through it like an explorer in a drowned kingdom. Tide pools lay scattered like dropped coins, each one holding its own small weather: a crab the size of her thumbnail waving its claws at nothing, anemones with their tentacles furled tight as fists, a school of silver fry trapped in a basin no wider than a hat, turning and turning in their shrinking world. The smell was enormous—iodine and rot and salt and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a locked room.

The kelp curtain hung across the cave mouth in thick ropes, and when she pushed through it, the fronds slid over her shoulders with a weight like hands.

Inside, the light changed. It came in low and green-gold through the water still pooled at the entrance, laying wavering stripes across stone. The cave ran back into the cliff farther than she could see, its floor shelving gently upward, dry beyond the first ten yards—dry, which meant the sea did not own this place entirely, which meant it had been here longer than the sea's patience. The air tasted of salt and dust and, faintly, of paper.

Paper. In a sea cave. Her mind snagged on it like a nail on cloth.

She had a lantern in her coat pocket—she always carried a lantern; a river pilot without light was a corpse waiting for an appointment—and she thumbed it alive. The flame steadied, and the shadows pulled back, and Ivo Ashgrove saw what the tide had given back.

The passage opened, twenty yards in, into a chamber that had been made rather than found. The walls were dressed stone, fitted close, and along one side ran a shelf carved from the living rock, and on the shelf, ranked like soldiers or saints, stood row upon row of lead-sealed cylinders, each stamped with a crest she recognized the way you recognize a face from a wanted notice: the Corvane arms, the river and the tower, the motto worn nearly smooth but still legible—*THE RIVER REMEMBERS ITS OWN.*

Beside the shelves, leaning against the wall as though set down by someone meaning to come right back, stood framed portraits. Dozens of them. Oil paintings cracking with age, daguerreotypes furred with salt-bloom, photographs curled in their mats. Men and women in the stiff dress of three generations past, and then two, and then one. All of them bearing the same heavy jaw, the same hooded eyes. Corvanes. A dynasty laid out in a row like evidence at a trial.

And below the shelf, in a long wooden case with brass fittings gone green, lay documents. She knelt and lifted the lid, and the smell that rose out of the case was the smell of her father's boat cabin, of his charts and his logbooks, of the year before everything ended—and for a moment the vertigo was so complete that she put her hand flat on the stone floor to steady herself, as if the cave itself had tilted.

There were deeds. There were letters bound in ribbon gone brittle. There was a ledger with a spine cracked white, its pages dense with a clerk's careful hand, columns of figures and names, and beside some of the names small annotations in a different ink, a different hand: *paid.* *silenced.* *gone.* There were maps of the valley, older than any she'd ever charted by, showing towns she knew—and towns she didn't, towns that appeared on none of the charts sold in any chandlery from Landfall to the headwaters, as though they had been unwritten from the world.

One of the maps had a corner folded back, and beneath the fold, in pencil faded almost to nothing, someone had written: *Hallowford Parish, 1,140 souls, removed.*

Removed. As if a parish were a stain.

Ivo sat back on her heels in the green half-dark and listened to her own breathing, which was too loud, and to the sea outside, which was beginning its slow return, a distant mutter growing by degrees into a voice.

This was not treasure. She understood that immediately, and the understanding frightened her more than gold would have. Treasure could be sold. This could only be *used*, and things that could only be used were dangerous in a way that money was not. Whoever had sealed this chamber had sealed away not wealth but memory—not the family's memory, but everyone else's. Every name in that ledger annotated *gone* was a person the Corvanes had removed from the record, and this room was the only place left in the valley where they had ever existed at all.

She should leave it. Seal it, or let the tide have it back, walk away, stay nobody from nowhere.

Her hand, acting on some older authority than her training, reached into the case again.

At the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a small object the shape of a heart if a heart were made by a machinist. She unwrapped it with fingers that had begun, without her permission, to shake.

A compass. Brass, heavy for its size, its glass clouded but unbroken, its case engraved around the rim with a single word:

**ASHGROVE.**

* * *

She did not open it at once. She sat with it in both hands, in the lantern light, in the smell of salt and dead paper, and let the word do its work.

Ashgrove. Not a common name. Not a name she had spoken aloud in seven years, not a name written anywhere, not a name attached to any debt or dock or lock on the whole length of the Vell. She had buried it so thoroughly that sometimes, waking in the dark of the *Marrow's Grace*, it took her three full breaths to remember she had ever been anything but Ivo, captain, no surname, no history, no photograph.

Her father had owned a compass. She remembered it from childhood—a brass thing he kept in his breast pocket and consulted even on water he'd run a thousand times, tapping its glass twice before he read it, a little ritual she'd mocked him for. *The river doesn't need your needle, Papa. The river knows where it's going.* And he'd laughed and tapped the glass again and said, in the tone he saved for the truest things, *It's not pointing at the river, girl. It's pointing at where the river comes from. Everything worth knowing is upstream.*

He had died upstream. That was the official version, anyway—the version that had come back down the river without him, wrapped in the language of courts: theft, flight, drowning, disgrace. A mapmaker who stole from his employers and paid for it with the river. She had been sixteen. She had inherited the conviction along with the grief, because in the valley the sins of a father were a daughter's address, and so she had done the only sensible thing: she had erased herself as thoroughly as the Corvanes erased their parishes. No surname. No photographs. No past.

And now the past had washed up at her feet, engraved with her name, in a chamber full of other people's stolen histories, and the vertigo of it was not fear exactly. It was the sensation of standing on ground that turns out to be moving—of learning that the solid floor of your life was current all along, and has been carrying you somewhere the whole time without your consent.

Because this compass had been in the cave. Which meant her father's compass had been in the cave. Which meant either her father had been in the cave—

Or someone had brought it here after he died, and left it where the tide would one day give it back.

She pressed the release catch. The lid sprang open with a click that sounded obscenely loud in the silence.

The needle swung. Wobbled. Settled.

Not north. Not anywhere near north. It pointed steadily, confidently, upriver—back the way she had come, toward the hills, toward the high country where the Vell rose out of the earth in places no chart named, toward the gorge where her father had drowned twenty years ago. The needle trembled against its pivot like a finger straining toward something, and Ivo looked at it for a long time, and the cold of the cave crept up through her boots and into her bones, and she found she was not afraid.

That was the strangest part. Seven years of practiced anonymity, seven years of flinching from her own reflection in shop windows, and now, sitting in a secret room stuffed with the evidence of everything the powerful had buried, holding the one object in the world that proved her father had been something other than a thief—she felt, under the grief, a fierce and unfamiliar steadiness. Like a lock finally finding its key. Like a river reaching the fall line and knowing, at last, which way to pour.

Outside, the sea had begun to speak in earnest. Through the cave mouth she could see the water sliding back across the flats, fast and silent and total, erasing the starfish and the pools and the kingdom of mud, restoring the world to the shape everyone agreed it had. In an hour there would be no cave mouth visible at all. In an hour the sea would keep this secret the way it kept all its secrets.

Ivo wrapped the compass in the oilcloth and put it inside her coat, against her chest, where she could feel its small weight with every heartbeat. She took the ledger marked with the annotations—*paid, silenced, gone*—and one of the maps with the folded corner, and rolled them tight, and stowed them the same way. The rest she left. She was a smuggler's daughter and a debtor and a ghost, and she knew better than to travel heavy.

At the cave mouth she paused once and looked back at the shelves of sealed cylinders gleaming in the lantern light, ranks and ranks of them, a library of everything the valley was never allowed to know. *Another day,* she thought, and did not know yet whether she meant it as a promise or a threat.

Then she waded out through the rising water, pushed through the kelp curtain one last time, and turned up the river road toward the lights of the tavern at Landfall, walking fast, with her father's compass beating against her ribs like a second heart.

She did not see the man on the clifftop who had watched her go in, and who now turned away and began, unhurried, to walk toward town—to the tavern, where he would ask, in a mild voice, whether anyone strange had come through lately asking about the caves.

She did not see him. But the tide had seen her, and the tide tells everything eventually.
