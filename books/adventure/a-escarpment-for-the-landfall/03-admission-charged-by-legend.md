## 3. Admission Charged by Legend

The Salt Lark sat where the Vell bent wide and slow around Landfall Point, a tavern built of driftwood and stubbornness, its porch posts bleached the color of old bone. Ivo tied off at the public mooring rather than her usual private berth—her usual instinct was to be seen as little as possible, but the tide that morning had left her hands shaking, and shaking hands needed something stronger than river water.

She counted out coins at the bar. Cash, always cash. No name given, no tab opened, no credit extended, because credit was a thread and every thread could be pulled.

The tavern-keeper was a broad woman named Maren with forearms like mooring rope and a face weathered into something between kindness and caution. She poured without being asked twice—a measure of dark spirit distilled from marsh barley—and set it down on a coaster of cork gone soft with years.

"Rough morning on the water?" Maren asked.

"Long night," Ivo said. It wasn't a lie. She had not slept. The compass sat in her coat pocket like a second heartbeat, and every time she shifted her weight she felt it press against her hip, patient and insistent, pointing at nothing in this room except perhaps at her.

Around her, the Salt Lark filled with the evening's first crowd: lockmen off shift, eel-fishers with their hands still smelling of mud, two carters arguing amiably about axle grease. The stove ticked. A dog scratched itself under a table. Ordinary sounds, ordinary lives—and yet Ivo felt as though she were listening to all of it from underwater, from inside that cave, from behind the sealed door where the past had been stacked in careful rows waiting for someone foolish enough to open it.

"Landfall Point's been strange lately," said an eel-fisher near the window, loud enough for the room. "Tide went out farther than my granddad ever saw. Farther than the farthest mark on the pilings."

"Farther than the farthest mark on the *church*," someone corrected, and there was laughter.

"My cousin swears he walked out past the old wreck," the eel-fisher went on. "Says there's a cave down there under the cliffs. Says when he got close his lamp guttered like something didn't want him looking."

"Your cousin also swears he once wrestled a sturgeon," said the carters' loser.

"He *did* wrestle a sturgeon."

"And lost."

"Lost *with dignity*."

More laughter, easy and warm, and Ivo drank her drink and kept her face arranged into the mild boredom of a stranger passing through. But beneath the boredom her pulse ticked upward. They were talking about her cave. Her cave, which she had found by accident and left in haste, sealing the door behind her as best she could with driftwood and denial.

"The Corvane Archive," said an old man in the corner who hadn't spoken until now. He was mending a net with fingers knotted like the twine itself, and he spoke without looking up, in the tone of a man who has told a story so many times it has worn smooth as river stone. "That's what my grandmother called it. The Corvane Archive, under Landfall Point. She said the family put everything down there. Everything they wanted drowned."

"Drowned how?" asked one of the carters, half interested.

"How do you drown a paper?" The old man pulled a knot tight. "You don't. You drown the *place*. You build your locks and your levees and your pretty towns upstream, and you let the water do the forgetting for you. My grandmother said the Corvanes never burned a record in their lives. Burning leaves ash. Ash can be read." He held up his tangled hand. "Water leaves nothing."

"That's a fine story for men drinking," said the barmaid's assistant, a girl of maybe sixteen, wiping down the counter near Ivo with more energy than the counter required.

"It's a fine story because it's true," the old man said. "Or true enough. Every valley's got a story like it. Ours just happens to have a family rich enough to make theirs stick."

"What else did your grandmother say?" This from a young lockman, leaning forward, genuinely curious—the kind of curiosity that made Ivo's skin prickle, because curiosity was a current too, and currents carried things downstream.

The old man considered his net. "She said the archive keeps a ledger. Not of debts—" he nodded toward the Syndicate's collection notice tacked discreetly by the door, the one Ivo had been avoiding reading closely all evening "—of *sins*. Every wrong the family ever did, written down fair and square, because the old Corvane who started it was superstitious. Thought if the sins weren't written somewhere, they'd come back up the river like dead fish. So he wrote them all down and hid them where no one would go looking."

"And where's that?"

"Where the water begins," the old man said, and finally looked up, and for one uncomfortable moment his eyes seemed to pass over Ivo and pause, the way a fisherman pauses when his line ticks. "Upstream. Where nobody goes."

The room laughed again, softer now, the laugh of people who enjoy a legend precisely because they don't believe it. Someone ordered another round. The dog fell asleep. The stove ticked.

Ivo finished her drink and set the glass down with deliberate steadiness.

*Where the water begins.*

Her compass needle had not pointed north since the cave. She had told herself it was broken, or magnetized by some vein of iron in the cliff, or simply old and mad like everything else her father had left her. But the needle swung steady and sure whenever she checked it, and it swung always in the same direction: upriver. Toward the source. Toward the place her father had gone and not returned from, twenty years ago, leaving her a name that meant thief in three counties and a compass that couldn't find north.

She paid Maren and stepped out onto the porch, where the evening air came off the river cool and brown-smelling, thick with silt and willow. The Vell moved past in the dark, broad and unhurried, carrying its cargo of secrets seaward. Somewhere out there, below the cliffs, below the tide line, a sealed chamber held portraits of faces she almost recognized and documents bearing a seal she had seen only once before—burned into the memory of a night when men with lanterns had come to her childhood house and taken her father away.

She leaned on the porch rail and breathed, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the drink settling wrong.

Behind her, through the window, she heard the old man resume his mending, and heard him say, quietly, to no one in particular:

"Course, my grandmother also said the archive calls to certain folk. Folk with a grudge and a reason. Said it waits for them like a trap waits for a foot."

* * *

Maren had kept the Salt Lark for nineteen years, and in nineteen years she had learned that the most dangerous thing a tavern-keeper could possess was a good memory. So she cultivated a bad one, carefully, the way other women cultivated gardens. She forgot faces. She forgot names. She forgot who owed her what and who had cried into whose beer and which lockman was sleeping with which widow. Forgetting was a service she provided along with the ale, and the valley loved her for it.

But some faces stuck anyway, like burrs in wool.

The woman at the end of the bar—cash payer, no name, river-callused hands, a way of sitting with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door—had a face Maren knew from somewhere other than here. It took her a while to place it, because the notice had been old even when she'd seen it, and faces change over ten years, especially faces that are trying to change.

It had been pinned to the customs post at Lock Nine, back when Maren still ran cargo upvalley instead of drink downvalley. A printed sheet, sun-bleached to the color of weak tea, with a woodcut portrait that flattered no one: a young woman, sharp-boned, dark-eyed, wanted for questioning in the matter of stolen Corvane property. The name beneath it had been smudged by rain, but the word above it was clear enough, because it was printed twice, in bold, the way officials print things they want remembered.

ASHGROVE. ASHGROVE.

Maren had stood in front of that notice for a long time, longer than curiosity required, because her own father had been an Ashgrove man—or rather, had worked *for* an Ashgrove, the mapmaker, the quiet one who charted the upper Vell back when charting the upper Vell was still allowed. She'd been a girl then. She remembered the day the riders came for him too, though the mapmaker's daughter had been the one they took, in the end, or so the story went. Stories thinned as they traveled, like soup stretched over too many bowls.

And now here was a woman with that same sharp-boned face, drinking alone, paying cash, saying nothing, while the whole tavern chattered about caves and archives and sins written in ledgers—as if the valley itself had decided, after twenty years of silence, to start talking all at once.

Maren polished a glass that didn't need polishing.

She should say something. That was the thought that kept surfacing, patient as the tide. Warn her. Tell her the notice might still be live, that notices like that didn't die, they just went dormant, hibernating in some customs clerk's drawer until someone with money and a grudge came asking. Tell her that a man had been in this very tavern six days ago, a soft-spoken gentleman in a good coat, asking questions about the low tide and the cliffs at Landfall Point, offering silver for any fisherman who'd walk the flats and report what he found.

Tell her she was already hunted.

But Maren had kept her tavern for nineteen years by knowing which silences to keep, and there was another silence underneath this one, older and heavier: the silence of everyone in the valley who had watched the Corvanes erase things and had said nothing, because saying something got your wharf burned or your son conscripted or your name added to whatever list the family kept. Nineteen years of profitable, cowardly, necessary silence.

She looked at the woman's back—at the set of her shoulders, the wary angle of her head, the way her hand kept drifting to her coat pocket as if checking on something small and vital—and Maren thought of her father coming home from survey trips with river mud to his knees and stories he'd only tell after the third drink, stories about a spring in the high country where the water came up clean out of the dark earth, cold enough to hurt your teeth.

He'd died believing that water mattered. Believing maps mattered. Believing that whoever controlled the record controlled the river, and whoever controlled the river controlled everything that lived along it.

The woman finished her drink, paid in exact coin, and left without a word to anyone. Through the window Maren watched her go down to the moorings, moving with the particular care of someone who has recently discovered the ground is not to be trusted.

Maren set down the glass.

Then she reached beneath the bar, into the drawer where she kept the things she officially didn't have—an unlicensed scale, a letter she'd never sent, a key to a cellar no one rented—and drew out a folded square of paper, sun-bleached to the color of weak tea, that she had torn from a customs post eleven years ago for reasons she had never fully explained to herself.

She smoothed it flat on the bar. The woodcut stared up at her, younger and harder than the woman who had just walked out. The rain-smudged name. The doubled word.

ASHGROVE. ASHGROVE.

"Well," Maren said to the empty glass, to the ticking stove, to nineteen years of careful forgetting. "Damn it all."

She folded the notice away, untied her apron, and went to find her least drunk stable boy, because there was a boat at the public mooring that would be casting off before dawn, and there were things a woman with a bad memory ought to say quickly, while she still could.

* * *

Ivo cast off in the blue hour before sunrise, when the river ran silver-gray and the mist stood on the water in slow columns like the ghosts of drowned trees. She had slept two hours aboard, fitfully, the compass wrapped in oilcloth and stowed in the bilge as far from her body as the boat allowed—which was not far, and did not help.

The trouble was that she couldn't stop thinking. Sleep required a certain agreement with the world, a truce, and she had broken hers last night in a tavern full of laughing strangers who had no idea they were describing her life back to her.

*A ledger of sins. Where the water begins.*

*The archive calls to certain folk. Folk with a grudge and a reason.*

She worked the boat out into the channel and let the current take the first mile, standing at the wheel with her collar up, watching the banks slide past—willow and alder, a heron frozen in the shallows, the ruins of an old towpath drowning slowly in reeds. Normal river. Honest river. A river that had never lied to her, whatever the legends said about the family that owned it.

Owning a river. She turned the phrase over. You could own a boat, a debt, a mistake. But a river? The Corvanes owned the locks, the tolls, the charts, the customs posts, the printing houses that produced the valley's single newspaper. Own the record, her father used to say—she hadn't understood, as a child, why he said it with such bitterness—and you own everything the record describes.

At the bend below Gullwing Reach she finally gave in, hauled up the oilcloth bundle, and unwrapped the compass in the gray light.

The brass case caught the dawn and threw it back dull gold. Engraved around the bezel, in letters worn nearly smooth: a name. Her name. The name she hadn't spoken aloud in ten years, hadn't signed, hadn't answered to, had buried so deep she sometimes startled when strangers addressed her as *miss* as though she were anyone at all.

ASHGROVE.

The needle trembled once, steadied, and pointed—not north. Not toward the sea, not toward any honest pole. Upstream. Up the long brown reach of the Vell, past the locks and the drowned towns and the country where the maps went thin, toward the high silent hills where, according to a drunk eel-fisher's grandmother, the water began and the sins were kept.

"You're not broken," Ivo said aloud. Her voice sounded strange on the empty deck, rough with disuse and sleeplessness. "You're working exactly as designed. That's worse."

Because a broken compass was junk, and junk could be thrown away. But a compass that pointed where her father had died—that was a summons. That was a hand reaching up out of the sealed chamber, out of the drowned dark, closing around her ankle.

She wrapped it away again and stowed it, and was turning back to the wheel when she saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke—chimney smoke rose lazy and domestic from the farmsteads along the bank. This was different: a thin, purposeful column rising from the point behind her, from the direction of Landfall Point itself, where the cliffs dropped to the tide flats. And beneath it, faint across the water, carried on the morning air the way sound carries over calm water, she heard voices. Several voices. Shouting, but not in alarm—in the organized cadence of men coordinating a search.

She raised her father's spyglass, an old brass tube she'd owned longer than she'd owned anything else, and put it to her eye.

Figures on the flats below the cliffs. Six, seven of them, spread out in a line, walking the exposed rock where the freak tide had fallen and not yet risen. Two held lamps against the pre-dawn gloom. One crouched at the base of the cliff, at the place where—Ivo's stomach dropped slowly, sickeningly, like an anchor going down—at the place where her driftwood seal had been pried apart and lay scattered on the stones like the ribs of something eaten.

And standing apart from the searchers, on a shelf of dry rock above the spray line, a figure in a good coat, arms folded, watching the cave mouth with the stillness of a man who has all the time in the world and intends to use it.

Even at this distance, even through the trembling lens, Ivo recognized the posture. She'd seen it yesterday evening, or its twin: the soft-spoken gentleman Maren's stable boy had described, arriving at the Salt Lark an hour after she'd left, asking after a woman traveling alone, showing a sketch that the stable boy—bless his loyal, frightened heart—had sworn looked like nobody at all.

They hadn't found her documents. She'd taken those aboard the first night, wrapped in waxcloth inside her ballast. But they'd found the chamber. They'd found the empty chamber, and the scattered portraits, and the dust-free rectangle on the shelf where a brass compass had waited twenty years for a hand to lift it.

And they would know, the way the old man's grandmother had known, that the archive called to certain folk. Folk with a grudge and a reason. Folk with a name.

Ivo lowered the spyglass with hands that had stopped shaking, strangely, completely, the way a river goes still above a weir.

Ahead of her the Vell ran north-by-northwest into the morning haze, wide and brown and patient, all the way up to the hills where the water began. Behind her, on the flats, men in good coats were climbing toward the cliff path, and one of them had turned, and was looking—not searching, *looking*, deliberately, methodically—at every boat on the water between Landfall Point and the horizon.

She put the wheel over and let the current carry her upstream, into the mist, toward the locks and the tolls and the country where the maps went thin.

Whatever she held, she understood now, she had never picked it up by accident. It had been waiting for her the way the tide waits for the moon. And the family that had drowned it under Landfall Point had spent twenty years making sure that no one named Ashgrove would ever live long enough to answer.
