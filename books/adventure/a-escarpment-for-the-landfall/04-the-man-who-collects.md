## 4. The Man Who Collects

Silas Fen had boarded a hundred boats in his working life, and he had learned to read them the way other men read faces.

This one—the *Kestrel*, moored at the Harrow Syndicate's lower wharf with her lines drawn tight and her deck scrubbed raw—told him things her captain would never say aloud. She was small, shallow-drafted, built for the shoals above Lock Nine where deeper hulls went hungry. Her paint was old but applied in even strokes, no drips, no haste. Her brass fittings were polished but not new, polished by a hand that wanted the boat to look poor without actually being neglected. And there, tucked beneath the gunwale rail where most captains kept nothing but rope-ends and rust, a coil of fresh hemp line, expensive grade, coiled with a precision that spoke of either vanity or fear.

Fen stood on the wharf a moment longer than he needed to, letting the morning do its work on him. The Vell ran brown and slow past the pilings, carrying its usual freight of silt, sawdust, and secrets. Gulls argued over something dead on the mudflats. Downriver, the Corvane grain elevator stood against the sky like a headstone for an entire valley's independence—*CORVANE & SONS, EST. 1811*, though everyone knew the sons were dead or scattered and only the name remained, the way names did when they were fed well enough.

He climbed aboard without asking permission. That was part of the ritual. Collectors who knocked became guests, and guests could be refused.

The deck was empty. He waited, hands folded over the ledger he carried—a real one, the Syndicate's, bound in oxblood leather with the accounts of forty-seven debtors inked inside—and let his boots learn the rhythm of the hull. She rode light. Lighter than she should have, if her ballast tanks were full. He filed that away the way he filed everything: in the quiet room behind his eyes where he kept the second ledger, the one no employer had ever seen.

Footsteps on the dock. Not hurrying. Good sign, or a very good performance of one.

Ivo Ashgrove came up the gangplank with a canvas satchel over one shoulder and an expression Fen had catalogued long ago as *carefully ordinary*. It was a remarkable face for how hard it worked at being unremarkable—weathered, plain-spoken, eyes the grey-brown of river water in October. He'd seen her photograph once, years ago, on a notice that should have followed her out of the capital and hadn't. The woman in the photograph had been younger and angrier. This woman had learned what anger cost.

"Captain Ashgrove." He used the name deliberately. Not *Ash*. Not *captain* alone. Names were levers; you showed people you had theirs.

"Fen." She didn't offer a hand. "You're early."

"The tide was early." He said it mildly, watching her face for the flicker. There it was—not much, a tightening at the corners of the mouth, gone in half a breath. Most men would have missed it. Fen made his living not missing it. "Payment schedule says the fifteenth. Today is the eleventh. I like to arrive before problems do."

"Problems don't keep schedules."

"No," he agreed. "But I do. It's the only thing separating me from the weather." He set the ledger on the deck box between them, open to her page. The figures were neat, his handwriting a collector's handwriting—small, upright, impossible to dispute. "Four hundred and twelve crowns outstanding. You've been current for six months. That's unusual."

"Is it?"

"Most debtors are either late or desperate. You're neither. You pay early, you pay cash, you never ask for extensions, and you've never once invited me to drink." He looked up at her. "Do you know what that tells me?"

"That I want this finished."

"It tells me you're saving up for something. Or running toward something. Or—" he turned a page he didn't need to turn, "—running from something, and want your accounts clean before you go. Clean accounts travel better than dirty ones."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere aft, water shifted in the ballast tank—that soft, heavy slosh again, and again too full, unless she'd taken on extra weight recently, which captains did for exactly two reasons: storm ballast, or cargo they didn't want inspected.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said. "The Vell is my living."

"The Vell is everyone's living. That's the trouble with rivers. They belong to whoever owns the locks." He closed the ledger and left his hand flat on the cover, a gesture that was not quite a threat and not quite anything else. "Which brings me to why I'm really here."

"Payment."

"Payment," he confirmed, "and a question. The Syndicate has an interest in a property matter down at Landfall Point. A cave, apparently. Exposed by the spring tides. Our client"—he watched her eyes, and her eyes gave him nothing, which was itself information—"wants to know if anyone's been poking around in it. Salvage rights are murky. The family that holds the foreshore is particular about trespass."

"Corvane," she said. Flat. Testing him.

"Corvane," he agreed. "Particular is their whole personality. So. Have you been down to Landfall Point lately, Captain?"

She met his gaze the way good liars and honest women both do—straight on, unhurried, letting silence do some of the work. "I run cargo. Grain, salt, lamp oil. I don't poke around caves."

"No," Fen said. "You wouldn't. You'd just happen to be moored near one." He smiled, thin and professional. "It's all right. I'm not the Inquisition. I collect debts. But my client pays me a retainer for observations, and observations are like debts—they compound. If you found something down there, Captain, something old, something with seals on it, you'd want to think carefully about who you told. Because the Corvanes don't buy salvage. They erase it. And they've been erasing things on this river since before your grandfather drew breath."

"You speak fondly of your employers."

"I speak accurately about them. Fondness is a different ledger entirely." He picked up the ledger, tucked it under his arm. "Your payment's due on the fifteenth. I'll take it today if you have it. Saves us both a trip."

She hesitated—then went below and came back with a canvas pouch, counted out four hundred crowns into his palm coin by coin, making him witness every piece. Professional. Careful. The coins were worn, mixed mints, the currency of someone who dealt in cash because cash left no trail. He counted it back anyway, slowly, watching her watch him.

"Twelve short," he said.

"Twelve goes with next payment."

"Twelve always goes with next payment, until one day it doesn't, and then it's forty, and then it's a lien on your hull." He put the coins away. "Keep it current, Captain. The river's getting narrow this year. Narrow water, narrow margins."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's arithmetic." He moved to the gangplank, then stopped, one hand on the rail, and gave her the thing he'd come all this way to give her—the thing that wasn't in any contract. "One more observation, free of charge. Last week a man came through Harrow asking after you. Not a Syndicate man. Soft shoes, city coat, asked questions at the tavern about the caves at Landfall Point and about a woman captain who'd been seen near them. Grey-eyed fellow. Chews mint leaves—you'd smell it on him before you saw him."

Something moved behind her face then, deep, like a fish turning under ice. "Did he leave a name?"

"They never do." Fen stepped down onto the planking. "But he paid for his drinks with a Corvane house chit. So when you're counting your friends on this river, Captain, count slowly."

* * *

He walked the length of the wharf without looking back, which took discipline, because looking back was a habit of men who doubted themselves and Fen could not afford doubt. At the harbormaster's shack he settled the mooring fees on three other vessels, noted the depth gauge on the channel buoy—it had dropped another hand's width; the drought upstream was strangling the middle reaches—and bought a meat pie he didn't want from a vendor whose stall sat conveniently within sight of the *Kestrel*. He ate it standing, slowly, and watched.

A boy came off the *Kestrel* an hour later with a canvas satchel and walked, not ran, toward the tavern district. Fen marked the direction. Maren's place, most likely—the Ferryhouse, with its crooked chimney and its keeper who remembered everything and repeated almost nothing. Almost nothing. That *almost* was where empires leaked.

He finished the pie, brushed the crumbs from his coat, and opened the oxblood ledger to a page near the back where the Syndicate's accounts ended and the paper was still blank. Then, standing in the lee of the harbormaster's wall, out of the wind, he uncapped his pen and wrote in a small, compressed hand that no clerk would mistake for bookkeeping:

*11th of the Flood Month. Kestrel at Harrow wharf. Ballast tanks riding heavy — cargo unmanifested or documents. Captain denies Landfall. Denial clean but delayed half a second — she has thought about how to answer before I asked. Boy sent to Maren's with satchel. Mint-chewer passed through last week per plan. She will move soon. Question remains: does she know what she carries, or only that it frightens the family?*

He paused, pen hovering. Then added:

*If she knows — protect her. If she doesn't — teach her fast.*

He blotted the page with sand, closed the book, and felt the familiar double weight of it settle against his ribs: one ledger for the men who paid him, one for the dead.

Because Silas Fen kept accounts the way rivers kept silt. Everything that washed downstream eventually came to rest somewhere, and he had spent nineteen years making sure it rested with him. Every drowned village. Every petition burned before it reached a magistrate's desk. Every name struck from the valley rolls by men in Corvane livery who called it *administrative correction*. His brother Tomas's name was among them—struck from the parish rolls of Wethering Ford the same year the dam charts redrew the town out of existence, the same year the letters home stopped coming back answered. Fen had been twenty-two, already collecting for the Syndicate, already learning that the Corvanes paid the Syndicate to look the other way, and he had done the arithmetic every young man does: survive now, settle later.

Nineteen years of *later*. The interest alone was monstrous.

* * *

That evening he reported to the factor at the Harrow counting-house, a damp man named Pellwick who wore his loyalty to the family like a borrowed coat, two sizes too big. Fen gave him the payment figures, the standard observations, the weather. He did not give him the ballast tanks. He did not give him the boy and the satchel. Those entries lived only in the back pages, in the small compressed hand, compounding quietly.

"She's sound," Fen said. "Current for six months. No flight indicators."

Pellwick sniffed. "The client thinks otherwise. There's been a communication from the house. They want closer surveillance on the Ashgrove woman. Apparently she may have come into possession of materials relevant to family interests."

"Materials," Fen repeated.

"Documents. Papers. The client was not specific." Pellwick smoothed his coat across his stomach. "There's talk of sending the grey man down again. Properly, this time."

Fen kept his face still, which was the hardest work he did all day. "And if the woman cooperates? Surrenders whatever she has?"

"Then she keeps her boat and her freedom." Pellwick smiled with the bottom half of his face. "The client is generous with cooperation."

"And if she runs?"

"Then the grey man runs faster." Pellwick slid a sealed envelope across the counter. "Your retainer's increased. The client values your... thoroughness."

Fen took the envelope, weighed it, pocketed it. Outside, the evening had come down blue and cold over the harbor, and the lamps along the wharf were lighting one by one, each flame a small stubborn argument against the dark. Upstream, beyond the locks, beyond the drowned parishes and the patrolled shallows, the river ran on toward its source through country he had not seen in nineteen years—country where his brother's town still lay under thirty feet of engineered water, holding its streets and its church bell and its dead in a silence the maps had been ordered to call *reservoir*.

He stood on the counting-house steps a long time, listening to the water move in the dark.

Then he went back to his room above the chandler's, unlocked the floorboard under the bed, and took out the second ledger proper—the true one, thick as a Bible, bound in grey sailcloth, filled edge to edge with nineteen years of names and dates and crimes. He turned to a fresh page and wrote at the top, in the small compressed hand:

*The Ashgrove woman.*

Below it, after some thought:

*Her father reached the source. They called him a thief. I helped carry the warrant.*

He had never written that sentence before. Nineteen years, and his pen had always found a way around it. Now it sat on the page in wet ink, undeniable as a body.

Fen blotted it, closed the book, and pressed his palms flat against the grey cloth as if taking a pulse.

Down at the wharf, a lantern kindled aboard the *Kestrel*, one warm square of light in the dark hull. He watched it through the window until it went out, and made his decision the way he made all of them—slowly, in ink, with the interest accruing.

Tomorrow he would file his report: subject cooperative, surveillance reduced, no further action recommended. It would buy her a week. Perhaps two.

After that, the grey man would come, and the river would narrow, and Silas Fen would have to choose—at last, with nineteen years of compounded interest—which ledger he intended to die holding.
