## 5. Compass Points Upstream

The compass did not point north.

Ivo discovered this at dawn, on the third morning after the tide gave back the cave, when she carried the thing out onto the deck of the *Magpie* to see what daylight would make of it. She had spent two nights telling herself it was a keepsake and nothing more—a brass trinket engraved with a name she had spent twenty years trying not to say aloud, ASHGROVE in small serifed letters worn nearly smooth by someone's thumb. Her father's thumb, most likely. The thought of it had kept her awake with her lamp burning until the oil ran dry.

But trinkets pointed north. This one did not.

She stood at the rail with the compass cupped in both hands and watched the needle swing—slowly, deliberately, with the patience of something that had been waiting far longer than she had—until it settled pointing upriver. Not north. Not east toward the sea. Upstream, into the hills, into the country where the Vell came down out of stone that no chart in Landfall bothered to map.

She turned. The needle turned with her, patient as a dog on a lead, holding its bearing against the current.

"Damn you," she said quietly, and meant it in several directions at once: the compass, her father, the river, herself for ever learning to want anything.

* * *

She should have thrown it back into the water. That was the sensible thing, the Ivo-Ashgrove-of-the-last-twenty-years thing. Take the drowned man's compass and drop it off the stern somewhere past the second bend, let the Vell have it the way the Vell had everything else—her name, her father, her face on a notice she'd once torn from a tavern wall so fast she'd splintered the nail of her ring finger. Sensible people did not keep objects that pointed at things.

Instead she sat down on an overturned crate amidships, set the compass on her knee, and opened the oilcloth bundle she'd carried up from the cave.

The documents were drying badly. She had spread them across every flat surface below decks, weighted at the corners with candlesticks and the iron skillet, and they curled as they dried like leaves deciding to become autumn. Most were water-swollen past reading, their ink bloomed into grey flowers. But some had been sealed—wax-stoppered tin tubes, the kind surveyors used—and those had survived whole. It was one of these she unstoppered now, working the wax loose with her thumbnail.

Inside was a surveyor's field journal, bound in oiled canvas, and the handwriting on the first page stopped her breath.

She knew that hand. She had learned her letters copying it. The tall ascenders, the way he crossed his sevens twice, the little hook he put on the tail of every *g*—her father, Tomas Ashgrove, mapmaker of the upper Vell, had written this page, and the date beneath it was the spring before he died.

*Survey commissioned by E. Corvane, per contract,* the entry read. *Headwaters approach via the Millwright road. Note: the Corvane agent insists all elevations be recorded "as amended." I record them true and keep my own copy.*

Her own copy. Of course he'd kept his own copy. He was the sort of man who kept his own copy of everything, who believed—naively, fatally, beautifully—that ink was a kind of honesty. She could hear his voice saying it: *A map is a promise, Ivo-girl. Whoever draws the map decides where the bridges go.* She had been nine years old and thought he was talking about cartography.

She read on, hunched over the crate while the river muttered against the hull and the morning fog burned off the water in slow rags.

The journal covered eleven days. Eleven days moving upstream through country she half-recognized from childhood stories—the flooded parish, the old mill towns, the gorge with its sheer limestone walls—and then, on the twelfth page, the entries stopped being about elevation and soundings and became something else. Something hurried. The handwriting tightened.

*Found the station. Not a lockhouse, not a depot—it's an archive. They have ledgers going back a hundred years. Floods they caused and blamed on weather. Parishes erased. A massacre at Hallowford that appears in no other record on earth except theirs, because theirs is the only record anyone is allowed to consult. They don't hide the truth. They curate it. There is a woman here whose job is deciding what happened.*

*I am going to copy the master ledger. If I do not come back, the compass will find it. It always points at what matters. Your mother thought I was mad to have it made. Tell Ivo—*

The sentence ended there. The next page was torn out—not ripped in haste but cut cleanly, with a blade, by someone who wanted a specific passage gone. The remaining pages were blank.

Ivo sat very still. A kingfisher went down the river like a thrown blue dart, and she watched it go because watching anything else was impossible just then.

*Tell Ivo—*

Tell Ivo what? The rest of the sentence was in a drawer somewhere in a fortified station at the top of the river, filed and catalogued by people whose profession was deciding what sentences got to be finished.

* * *

She worked through the rest of the day, and the work steadied her the way work always had. There were four more sealed tubes. Two held Corvane correspondence—dry administrative evil, requisitions and denials, a letter instructing a lockmaster to "detain the surveyor Ashgrove without formal charge; see that his papers are found irregular." One held a child's drawing, done in charcoal on butcher paper: a river seen from above, winding through hills, with a small star drawn at its headwaters and, beside the star, in a grown-up's careful hand, the word *HERE*. The last tube held a single sheet of official record, and this was the one she read three times, because it was the one that lied.

It was a tribunal finding, dated twenty years past, stamped with the Corvane seal in wax gone amber with age:

*Tomas Ashgrove, surveyor, having entered restricted headwater lands under color of lawful commission and there committed theft of proprietary documents, is adjudged a thief and fugitive. All professional credentials revoked. His maps are recalled and destroyed as fraudulent. Reward offered for information leading to his apprehension. Let it be noted throughout the valley that the man Ashgrove acted alone, for gain, and that no grievance of his has any foundation in fact.*

No grievance of his has any foundation in fact.

They had written him into a thief the same week they wrote him out of the world. And the valley had believed it, because the valley had no other version available to believe. Every map, every record, every gossiping tongue at every landing said the same thing, because one family owned the saying of things. Her father hadn't died a disgraced man. He had died a *corrected* one.

Ivo folded the tribunal sheet along its original creases, very precisely, and put it away, and found that her hands were steady and her eyes were dry and there was a feeling in her chest like a lock mechanism turning over—something rusted beginning, reluctantly, to move.

* * *

That evening she climbed to the wheelhouse and took down her charts.

She kept them rolled in a brass tube lashed under the pilot's bench, and they were good charts—her own work, surveyed over fifteen years of running the Vell, corrected in pencil a hundred times. She had always prided herself on their honesty. Now she unrolled the upper reaches and looked at them with new eyes, and saw how much white space there was. Above Lock Nine the river narrowed and the detail thinned. Above the drowned parish her lines grew sparse and apologetic. And above the gorge—the gorge where the journal's entries stopped—there was nothing at all. Blank vellum. The conventional sign for *no data*, which on every chart printed by the Corvane Survey meant the same thing: *nothing to see here, and you didn't look.*

Except her father had looked. And the compass was looking now, its needle lying along the river's course like a finger laid on a line of text.

She measured it properly, the way he would have. Took the bearing off the needle at dusk, when the first stars were out and the magnetic interference of the day's heat had settled. Checked it against her chart, against the lie of the land upstream, against the little charcoal star on the child's drawing. The numbers agreed. Whatever force moved that needle—and it wasn't magnetism, or not only; she'd held a lodestone near it once and the needle hadn't so much as trembled—it wanted the headwaters. It had maybe always wanted the headwaters. Maybe it had been pointing there, patiently, through twenty years of her keeping it in a drawer under spare clothes, and she had simply never asked it a question.

Or maybe it had only started pointing since the tide gave back the cave. Since the archive surfaced. As if the river itself had decided the time for waiting was over.

Below her, the *Magpie* rode at anchor in a backwater eddy, her riding light doubled against the fog. Somewhere downstream, behind her, were Landfall and the syndicate office and the life she had built out of smallness and cash payments and never giving anyone her surname. Ahead of her, upstream, were Lock Nine and its inspections, the drowned parish, the gorge, and whatever waited at the source with her father's unfinished sentence in it.

And between her and all of it, somewhere on the river, quite possibly already asking questions at Landfall taverns, was whoever had come looking for the cave.

Maren's voice from the tavern, casual as spilled salt: *Funny thing—a gentleman was in last week, asking after the Point. Wanted to know if the low tide had turned anything up. Offered coin for word of anything found.* She had said it lightly, pouring Ivo's cider, not meeting her eye. Maren had recognized her—Ivo had seen the recognition happen, a flicker behind the barkeep's tired eyes, the ghost of a twenty-year-old notice passing across her face—and Maren had said nothing about it, which was its own kind of message. But the gentleman asking after the cave was not a message. It was a warning shot.

They knew the tide had given something back. Which meant they had known about the cave all along, and had merely been waiting for the sea to open it—or they had watchers on the cliffs, or informants among the gulls-and-gossip crowd of Landfall harbor. Either way, the arithmetic was simple. She had documents that could unmake the Corvanes. The Corvanes had spent a hundred years making sure such documents did not exist. The distance between those two facts would be crossed by men with boats, and soon.

She could run downstream instead. Out to sea, down the coast, a new river in some other valley, a new name, start the invisibility over again from zero at forty-one years old. The thought arrived on schedule, the way it always did, wearing its sensible coat.

Then she looked at the compass. The needle lay steady on its upstream bearing, indifferent to her, to the fog, to the whole architecture of her caution. Pointing at the place where a sentence waited to be finished. *Tell Ivo—*

"He tried," she said aloud to the empty wheelhouse. "He got eleven days up that river and a knife in the dark for it, and he still got the compass out somehow. Still got it to me."

Because that was the part she kept circling back to, the part that felt less like history and more like instruction. The compass had reached her. The journal had reached her. Twenty years in drawers and dead men's pockets, and all of it had washed up at her feet inside a week, as if the river had been holding it in trust. Her father had failed—but he had failed *forward*, leaving a trail a daughter could follow, and the only unforgivable thing she could do now was treat that trail as a coincidence.

Grief was a current. She had known that since she was a girl watching floodwater take fences and livestock and once, horribly, a wedding party's hired barge. You could stand on the bank and mourn what it carried off, or you could get in the boat and use it. The same water that drowned the parish would carry her upstream if she read it right. Grief, read right, was propulsion.

Ivo rolled the charts, lashed the tube, and went below to prepare.

* * *

She worked until well past midnight, and the work was a litany, each task a word in it.

The documents went into oilskin, double-wrapped, sewn into a canvas satchel that had once held sounding leads. The originals she would keep on her body; copies—she made copies, laborious, by lamplight, of the worst of the accusations, enough that losing the originals would not be losing everything—went into the hollow of the ballast keel, wrapped in tarred cloth, because a wise runner never kept all her cargo in one hold. She took on water, hardtack, lamp oil, the short-barreled fowling piece her father had left her along with everything else, though she hoped with a tired, practiced hope never to need it. She paid her anchorage fee in cash at the harbormaster's window and signed the ledger, as always, *I. Marlowe, master of the Magpie, coastwise trader*—and paused, pen hovering, aware for the first time in twenty years of what the false name cost her. Then she wrote it anyway. Invisibility was still worth something. Just not everything. Not anymore.

At grey dawn she weighed anchor.

The *Magpie* came about sweetly, caught the early ebb's last push, and swung her bow upstream. The Vell took her the way it took everything—without comment—and the first locks and landings began to slide past, gray shapes in gray mist, chimneys and crane arms and the small human smoke of breakfast fires.

In the wheelhouse, the compass sat in its usual place on the shelf above the binnacle, where a proper compass would have been. Its needle lay along the channel ahead, unwavering, pointing at Lock Nine, at the drowned parish, at the gorge, at the blank space on every map in the valley, at the finished sentence.

Ivo Ashgrove—Marlowe, today; Ashgrove again, perhaps, someday, if the river allowed—put the wheel over two points to hug the deep channel and drove her boat into the current.

Behind her, far down at Landfall, she imagined she could hear it already: a gentleman asking questions, coin changing hands, a description of a woman with a sailor's stride being written down in a neat hand. Ahead of her, the fog was lifting off the water in long banners, and the river ran on toward its source, carrying everything it carried.

"Keep pointing," she told the compass. "I'm coming."
