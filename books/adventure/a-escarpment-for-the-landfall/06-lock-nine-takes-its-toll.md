## 6. Lock Nine Takes Its Toll

The river told her about Lock Nine before she saw it.

Ivo had learned to read the Vell the way other people read faces—the way it sulked before a storm, the way it went glassy and secretive when something waited downstream. For two days now it had been telling her *slow down*. The current thinned as if the water itself were being counted somewhere ahead. Driftwood that should have swept past her instead circled in eddies, patient and trapped. And the birds had changed: no gulls working the shallows, no herons stalking the reed margins. Just crows, sitting along the wire that ran from the lock tower toward the hills, all facing upstream, all watching.

She killed her engine a mile out and let the *Kestrel's Grace* drift, and while she drifted she worked.

The documents lay in their oilskin bundle on the deck beside her—her father's letters, the Corvane shipping manifests with their columns of names struck through like throats, the survey maps of the drowned parish, and the compass, which sat in its brass housing pointing stubbornly upriver no matter how she turned it. She had slept badly for two nights with these things aboard, dreaming of doors opening. Now, with the lock's stone towers rising gray out of the morning haze ahead of her, she stopped dreaming and started hiding.

The ballast of an old riverboat is a country unto itself. Under the deck planks, between the iron pigs and the gravel sacks that kept the *Grace* honest in a crosswind, there were gaps a hand could slip into and a bundle could vanish behind. Ivo pried up three boards amidships, working fast, her breath loud in her own ears. She wrapped the oilskin in a spare canvas tarp, tarred the seams, and wedged it down between two ballast stones where the damp was already black and permanent. Then she scattered bilge grit over the boards and reseated them, and poured a cup of actual bilge water across the seams so the smell would be right. A man searching a boat smells first and looks second, and no inspector alive would put his nose deeper into that stench than he absolutely had to.

The compass she did not hide. That surprised her, when she caught herself doing it—tucking it into her coat's inner pocket, against her ribs, where her heartbeat could reach it. It made no sense. It was the single most dangerous object on the boat, a piece of brass engraved with a name that had been erased from every ledger in the valley, and she was carrying it straight into a Corvane checkpoint like a fool carries a lit lantern into a powder store.

But she couldn't leave it in the ballast. Her hands refused. When she imagined the bundle down there in the dark going without it, something in her chest pulled tight as a hawser in a flood. So: against her ribs, under her shirt, warm from her skin. If they found it, they found it. Some part of her—the part that had been awake ever since the tide went out at Landfall Point and showed her the cave—was almost curious to see what she'd do if they reached for it.

That part of her frightened her more than the lock did.

* * *

Lock Nine sat in a narrows where the Vell pinched between two shoulders of granite, and it wore its newness like a uniform too big for it. The old lock had been timber and moss; this one was cut stone and fresh mortar, with a crane arm painted Corvane blue and a signal mast flying the family's colors—a white heron on black, wings folded, which always looked less like heraldry to Ivo than like a warning about what happened to things that flew too high.

Every vessel was being taken through in single file. She could see them queued below the gates: a coal barge, two timber rafts lashed together, a fish-buyer's skiff, a pleasure launch with canvas covers over everything. On the lock apron stood men in gray coats who were not lockmen—you could tell by how they stood, weight even on both feet, hands loose and ready, the way men stand when their work is people rather than rope. One of them carried a long iron rod with a hooked end. A sounding probe. For probing.

An old woman selling eels from a barrel near the towpath watched the inspections with the flat patience of someone who had watched many things here and expected to watch many more. Ivo marked her and marked the barrel and marked the distance, because a riverboat captain learns to know where the exits are even in a place with only one way through.

"Vessel, name and port," called a lockman from the lower gate, clipboard in hand, rain spotting his ink.

"*Kestrel's Grace,* out of Landfall Point," Ivo said. "Master's name Ash." The lie came smooth as water off a hull. She had been Ash for six years, Ash to harbormasters and chandlers and the Harrow Syndicate's clerks, and the name had worn grooves in her by now, comfortable as a tiller handle.

"Ash." He wrote it. "Cargo?"

"Ballast and general freight. Running light upriver to take on a grain contract at Hallowford."

He waved her in, and the *Grace* slid into the lock chamber like a whale into a net, and the gates closed behind her with a sound Ivo felt in her teeth—a boom of oak and iron, final as a verdict.

Water began to rise around the hull. That was the trap of locks, she thought, watching the stone walls climb past her gunwales: for ten minutes you belonged entirely to whoever held the gates. No current to carry you off, no wind worth naming, no bank within jumping distance and forty feet of wet masonry below. A floating room with one door, and the door was theirs.

A whistle shrilled from the apron above. Two of the gray-coated men came down the iron stair to the lock wall, and one of them—tall, dry-eyed, with a voice like a file drawn slow across soft metal—said, "Captain. Routine inspection. Cut your engine and open your spaces."

"I've got nothing but ballast and bad coffee."

"Then this will be quick." He smiled without any part of his face agreeing to it. "We're looking for documents. Papers, books, sealed correspondence. Anything unregistered."

The word *documents* went through her like cold water through a seam. Not contraband, not weapons, not smuggled brandy. Documents. They knew something existed, then—or suspected—and they were sifting the whole river for it the way her father used to sift sand for gold, patiently, certain it was there.

Her heart should have been hammering. It took her a moment to understand that it was—but not the way it used to. Six years ago, when a syndicate man had once grabbed her wrist in a Landfall tavern, fear had been a fist closing on her throat, all heat and blindness. This was different. This was cold and clear and strangely wide, as though someone had opened windows in her skull. She could see everything: the probe-man's boots, the wet iron of the ladder, the eel-seller's barrel, the angle of the sun on the upper gate, the exact spot where the lockman with the clipboard was standing too close to the edge. Fear hadn't paralyzed her. Fear had *sharpened* her, the way a whetstone sharpens—not gently, but thoroughly.

Well, she thought. There's a thing to know about yourself. Better to learn it here than never.

"Coffee first?" she said aloud, and was already moving to the galley as she said it, because motion was confidence and confidence was permission. "It's terrible but it's hot."

The tall man considered her. "Name?"

"Ash. Told the gentleman at the gate."

"Told him. I'm asking." His eyes were pale river-fog eyes, and they did not blink enough.

"Ashgrove," she heard herself say, and felt the compass go hot against her ribs like a coal, and watched his face for the flicker—and there wasn't one, because of course there wasn't. Nobody remembered the name. That was the whole point of the Corvanes. They had spent sixty years teaching the valley to forget, and the forgetting had been so thorough that she could walk her own true name through their checkpoint wearing it like a common coat. "Ashgrove. Family name. My father piloted this stretch before me."

Something moved behind the fog of his eyes—not recognition, but the reflex of a man checking a list he keeps in his head. "Ashgrove," he repeated, tasting it. "River folk, are you."

"Born on the water. Will likely die on it, if the coffee doesn't get me first."

One of the younger agents snorted despite himself, and the tall man's gaze flicked sideways at him with quiet reproof, and in that half-second of divided attention Ivo handed the moment her whole attention, the way you hand a line to someone leaning too far over the rail. People, her father had taught her, are locks. Every one of them has a gate, and every gate has a mechanism, and most mechanisms are vanity.

"And who holds the honor of commanding this fine piece of engineering?" she asked the tall man, nodding up at the crane arm, the new-cut stone, the signal mast. "I've locked through nine of these dams and this is the cleanest work on the river. Whoever built this understood that a lock isn't a wall—it's a handshake. Most of them feel like being swallowed. This feels like being *received*."

The fog-eyes narrowed a fraction. "The lockmaster is Master Quillon. He designed the expansion himself."

"Did he, now." She let admiration settle into her voice like silt into still water, layer by layer. "You can tell. Amateurs build for the river. A man like that builds for the *boats*, and for the people in them. I'd count it an honor to tell him so myself, if he's the sort who takes an interest in traffic."

"He takes an interest," the tall man said, and there was something almost wry in it, the weariness of a subordinate who has watched a vanity operate for years. "Finish your coffee, Captain Ashgrove. We'll search your spaces while you drink."

* * *

They searched well. That was the worst of it—they were good, and she watched them be good, and she drank her terrible coffee and made herself look bored instead of attentive, because attentive was a scent and dogs of that kind caught scents.

The young one went through her cabin: mattress lifted, sea chest emptied, the false bottom under the chart drawer—she'd left it empty on purpose, a decoy with nothing in it, because an inspector who finds a hiding place finds it empty feels clever and moves on satisfied. He found the decoy. He smirked at it, privately, the smirk of a man adding a small victory to his day, and Ivo wanted to kiss him.

The tall one walked the deck slowly, reading the boat the way she read the river. He paused at the ballast hatch. Of course he did. The bilge smell rose up to meet him and he recoiled half an inch—an inch of dignity, a half-inch of disgust—and Ivo watched him weigh the cost of kneeling in filth against the probability of reward.

"Ballast and gravel," she offered. "And whatever the river puts in a bilge. You're welcome to wade in it. I'd offer boots but I haven't got any that'd fit you."

He looked at her for a long moment. Down in the dark under the deck planks, wedged between iron and stone, wrapped in tarred canvas and swimming in the ripe chemistry of six years of standing water, her father's letters waited. She kept her face pleasant and vacant, a woman with nothing in her head but grain contracts, and inside she was a held breath, and inside *that*—deeper than the breath, deeper than the fear—was that strange cold clarity again, steady as a star sight.

"Probe," the tall man said.

The other agent brought the iron rod, and Ivo's stomach dropped through the hull and into the river, because the probe would go into the ballast, and the ballast was deep, and the hook on the end might catch canvas—

But the tall man didn't use it on the ballast. He used it on the deck seams, walking the length of the *Grace* and sounding the caulking like a doctor percussing a chest, and then he stood the probe upright against the rail and looked at her with those fog eyes and said:

"You flatter easily, Captain."

"Beg pardon?"

"The lockmaster. You were laying it on thick. Either you want something or you're afraid of something." He smiled his face-disagreeing smile. "Which is it?"

"Both," Ivo said, and laughed, and the laugh was real, which saved her, because it startled even her. "I want a grain contract and I'm afraid of the Syndicate's interest rates. Neither of those is your business, but both of them make a woman generous with compliments."

He studied her a moment longer. Then, unexpectedly, he inclined his head—a small, precise bow, the bow of a duelist acknowledging a touch. "Master Quillon will be pleased to receive you at the upper gate. He receives everyone. It's the price of building something worth boasting about." He gathered his men with a tilt of his chin. "Clear vessel. Pass through."

The gates boomed open ahead of her onto the higher reach of the river, and the *Grace* rose on the last foot of water and floated free, and Ivo stood at her wheel with her heart finally allowed to beat again, each stroke landing like a hammer on an anvil, ringing.

* * *

Master Quillon received her on the upper apron, exactly as promised, in a coat with more braid than a admiral's and a mustache groomed to the geometry of a drafting compass. He was perhaps fifty-five, pink-faced, delighted, and lonely in the particular way of men whose achievements exceed their audience.

"*Kestrel's Grace*," he read from his clipboard before she could speak. "Landfall Point. Light cargo, bound for Hallowford grain. Captain—" he peered— "Ashgrove. Any relation to the elder Ashgrove? Pilot on this reach, oh, twenty years back? Before my time, but the old lockkeepers still speak of him."

The world tilted very slightly on its axis. Ivo kept her hand easy on the wheel and her voice level, and beneath the level voice the cold clarity spread its wings.

"You knew my father?"

"By reputation only. They say he could run the narrows at Hallowford in fog." Quillon sighed with genuine feeling. "Half the men they praise that way died proving it, of course. But the other half earned it. Which was he?"

"He earned it," Ivo said. "He just ran out of river eventually. They all do."

"Well said." Quillon beamed and gestured at his works with both arms, a priest displaying relics. "Well. You'll want to compliment the masonry, I imagine. Everyone does."

So she did. She gave him twenty minutes of it, and it cost her nothing because ninety percent of it was true: the cut stone *was* beautiful, the gate mechanisms *were* elegant, the crane arm *was* the finest on the reach. She asked questions a builder's soul aches to answer—how he'd solved the ice shear problem, why he'd angled the upper approach against the spring current—and Quillon answered them all, glowing, his gray-coated escort shifting from foot to foot in exquisite boredom. And when she finally cast off, he pressed a paper into her hand: a pass, signed, waiving inspection at Locks Ten and Eleven.

"For a pilot's daughter," he said grandly. "The river remembers its own, Captain. Even if the ledgers don't."

Ivo froze for half a heartbeat—half a heartbeat too long—and Quillon's brow began its small confused journey downward, and she covered it with a laugh and a wave and let the current take her out into the upper reach.

Only when the lock was a gray smudge behind her did she let herself shake.

*Even if the ledgers don't.* An old man's throwaway courtesy, meaning nothing, meaning everything. Somewhere in some record—Quillon's own memory, a lockkeeper's tale, a page nobody had gotten to yet—her father's name survived. The Corvanes had erased him from the archives, but they couldn't erase him from the river. The river remembered its own. She put her hand inside her coat and touched the compass, and it pointed upstream, faithful as a pulse, toward the source, toward the gorge, toward whatever her father had found and died keeping.

Ahead, the valley narrowed. The crow-lined wires gave way to pine ridges, and somewhere beyond them, Odile's people were watching the water—though she didn't know that yet, wouldn't know it for days. What she knew was simpler and colder: the gray coats were inspecting every hull on the Vell for papers, which meant the Corvanes knew the archive existed, which meant the hunt had started before she'd even understood she was prey.

She fed fuel to the engine and turned the *Grace's* nose upriver, into the narrowing gold of afternoon.

At Lock Ten, tomorrow, she wouldn't need to hide anything. She had a pass.

She'd need to hide herself.
