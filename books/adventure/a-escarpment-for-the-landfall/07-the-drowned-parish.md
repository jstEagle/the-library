## 7. The Drowned Parish

The fog came off the Vell like something exhaled, and Ivo Ashgrove steered into it gladly.

She had run the river above Lock Nine for two days now without a chart she trusted. The Corvane surveys showed a channel that had silted over a decade ago; the syndicate charts showed nothing at all, as if the upper reaches of the Vell were nobody's business and no one's home. So she read the water the way her father had taught her before the word *father* became a wound: by the lean of the current against the pilings, by the color of the foam, by the way the mist sat heavier over deep water than shallow. The river was honest even when the maps were not. That was the first thing it had ever taught her, and the only lesson that had never failed her.

*The Vesper* moved through the white silence with her engine throttled to a murmur. Behind Ivo, the cave documents lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the ballast stones, and in her coat pocket the brass compass turned its stubborn needle upstream, upstream, always upstream, indifferent to north as a saint is indifferent to flattery.

She smelled the parish before she saw it.

Woodsmoke, yes—every riverside town burned wood—but under it something else: wet plaster, drowned lime, the sweet-rot smell of a house that has stood half-submerged for years and never quite died. Then the fog thinned, and there it was.

A church spire rising out of the water.

Not beside the water. Out of it. The stone tower stood hip-deep in the Vell where no tower should be, its base furred with green weed, its bell arch dark and dripping. Around it, rooftops broke the surface at angles like ribs—a whole town tilted and sunken to the second story, windows staring up through brown water at a sky they had not asked to see. Here and there, on the higher ground along the eastern bank, newer structures clung: shacks and lean-tos and houses built from salvaged doors, a settlement grown like scar tissue over the wound.

Ivo brought the boat alongside a rotting wharf that had once, clearly, been well inland. The mooring posts were so tall she had to stand on her cabin roof to loop the line. The water lapped at what had been a second-floor balcony. A child's chalk drawing still ghosted across the boards, faded to almost nothing: a boat, a sun, a stick figure waving.

She stood there a long moment with her hand on the rail.

*What happened here?*

She had grown up on this river. She knew every landing from the delta to the gorge, or believed she did. There was no town on any chart called—she squinted at the weathered sign nailed crookedly above the old balcony, letters bleached but legible—**SAINT ELMER'S PARISH**. No such name anywhere between Lock Nine and Hallowford. And yet here it was, drowning in plain sight, big enough to have had a church with a bronze bell.

The compass needle trembled in her pocket, pointing past the spire, upriver, patient as guilt.

* * *

The living part of Saint Elmer's climbed the hillside in terraces of salvage. Ivo walked up through it with her collar up and her cap low, though she was learning that disguise meant less here than it did downstream. The people who came out to look at her did not look like people checking for strangers. They looked like people checking whether she had come from the government.

An old man mending a net by the path didn't greet her. A woman hauling water gave her a nod so brief it was nearly a warning. Dogs watched her from under porches with the flat patience of animals that had learned not to bark at anything.

She found the tavern by following the smell of frying fish. It had been, in another life, the ground floor of a granary; the great timber beams still overhead still bore iron hooks for sacks. Now they held drying nets, bundles of herbs, a hammock where a boy slept through the noon noise. The room was warm and dim and full of the particular quiet of a place where everyone knows everyone and no one trusts anyone new.

Conversation dipped when she entered. Rose again, carefully, when she took a stool at the end of the bar and ordered stew and whatever passed for beer.

"You're off the river," said the barkeep. Not a question. He was a heavy man with forearms like mooring rope and a face that had decided long ago to give away nothing for free.

"Down from Lock Nine."

"Nobody comes down from Lock Nine." He set the bowl in front of her anyway. "Lock Nine sends people away. Or doesn't let them go further." He wiped the bar in slow circles. "You'll want fuel. We've diesel, such as it is. You'll want to buy it tonight and be gone by morning, because that's how everyone does it, and we'd all like to keep it that way."

"I'm not looking for trouble."

"No one ever is." He leaned closer, voice dropping. "But trouble lives upstream of us, miss, and trouble floats down. You want my advice? Don't ask about the spire. Everyone who asks about the spire ends up asking other questions, and questions are how parishes drown twice."

Ivo ate her stew and said nothing, which was its own kind of fluency. But the barkeep's words worked in her like a splinter. *How parishes drown twice.* She turned the phrase over while the fire crackled and the nets dripped overhead, and she understood that she had already decided to ask.

It was the compass that made her do it. She told herself afterward it was caution—she wanted to know if anyone here had seen the Corvane seal, wanted to know if the family's reach extended this far into their own blank space on the map. But truthfully, in the way one is truthful only with oneself, alone, at night: she was tired of carrying a mystery in her pocket. She wanted someone to tell her what it was.

The old woman was in the corner by the fire, and Ivo had marked her on entering the way a sailor marks a rock: not dangerous, just fixed. She was small and dry as driftwood, wrapped in a shawl the color of pond water, and she had been watching Ivo with an attention that had nothing vague in it. When Ivo rose to leave, the woman spoke.

"Show me your hand."

Ivo stopped. "Ma'am?"

"The hand with the thing in the pocket. The thing that's been pulling at you since you walked in like a fish on a line. Show me."

The room had gone quiet again, but differently now—not hostile, merely attentive, the way a congregation attends. Ivo could have walked out. Every instinct trained into her by ten years of running said *walk out, walk out, walk out.* Instead she crossed the room, crouched by the fire, and opened her hand with the compass in it.

The old woman did not touch it. She looked at it the way one looks at a face across a grave.

"Well," she said softly. "The mapmaker's daughter."

The words went through Ivo like cold water through a hull.

"My father," Ivo said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, "was a hydrographer. He surveyed the middle Vell. He died a thief."

"He died a surveyor," the old woman said. "The theft was done to him." She lifted her eyes. "Sit down, girl. You've come a long way to sit on a stool, and you'll want to hear this sitting down, because it will take the legs out from under you either way."

* * *

Her name was Bethel Quill, and she had been the schoolteacher of Saint Elmer's Parish, back when Saint Elmer's Parish existed.

"It existed," she said, "the way you exist. On the ground. In the record of God, if you believe in one. Four hundred souls. A church with a bronze bell cast in Harrowford. A schoolhouse—" she touched her own breastbone briefly, "—with my name over the door. We had orchards on the bluff. We had a ferry landing, and a chandler, and a man who made the best eel traps on the Vell, and none of it, none of it, is written anywhere now. Go to the provincial archive in Corvane City and ask after Saint Elmer's. They will show you a map of the valley with a smooth green wash where we lived, and the wash will say *floodplain, uninhabited,* and the ink will be fifty years old, and it will have been drawn *before* the flood, because they drew it before, deliberately, so that afterward there would be nothing to contradict."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked. Ivo realized her hands had gone cold around the compass.

"There was a dam," Bethel said. "You've seen the weirs below Lock Nine—the little ones, the mill weirs. This was to be the great one. The Corvane Consolidated Water & Light Company bought the valley's future with paper nobody could read, and when the last holdouts wouldn't sell—the ferryman, the chandler, my deacon—they didn't burn us out. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be pointed at. They waited for the spring melt and they opened the coffer gates early, at night, three weeks ahead of any schedule ever filed. No warning siren. The telegraph lines had been cut for 'maintenance' the day before. The water came down the valley in the dark like a wall with a grudge."

"How many?" Ivo asked. Her mouth was dry.

"Officially? None. Unofficially—" Bethel's voice did not break; it simply went flat, the way water goes flat before a storm. "We counted thirty-one missing in the first week. Some were found. Some were found eventually. My sister was not found at all, and neither were the Aldous twins, who were nine." She looked into the fire. "And then the company men came around with compensation schedules, and the schedules paid for land that was, according to every map in the province, worthless floodplain, and men signed because their children were hungry, and signing was the last act of Saint Elmer's Parish. After that we were a rumor. After that we were *uninhabited.*"

"And no one—no one downstream remembers?"

"Remembering requires a record, child, and records require someone willing to keep them against pressure. The newspapers took Corvane advertising. The county clerk's office took Corvane patronage. The church diocese took a handsome donation and reassigned our priest to a coastal parish where he drank himself quiet within two years." She shrugged, a small dry motion like a leaf turning over. "Grief needs somewhere to live. Ours lives here." She gestured with her chin at the room, the hillside, the spire standing in the river. "In the water. Where they put it."

Ivo looked down at the compass in her palm. The needle swung gently, insistently, toward the headwaters, and for the first time she saw it not as a clue to her father's fate but as an accusation aimed at everything.

"Why does the compass matter to you?" she asked. "You said *mapmaker's daughter* like you knew him."

Bethel smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ivo had ever seen, worn smooth like a stone in a current.

"Your father came here. Twenty-two years ago, maybe twenty-three. He was younger than you are now, and he came with instruments and a notebook and a hired skiff, and he spent nine days sounding the flooded valley and photographing the high-water marks on our church—which, you will note, are still there, girl, still cut into the stone, because stone keeps better testimony than clerks. He interviewed everyone. He wrote down names. He sat where you're sitting and he cried into his beer, quietly, like a gentleman, and then he said—" her voice shifted, taking on another man's cadence, and Ivo's heart clenched so hard she had to grip the stool, "—he said, *They can drown the parish, Mrs. Quill, but they cannot drown the survey. Water finds its level, and so does the truth, given time and a channel.*"

"He was here," Ivo whispered. "He was here, and I never—"

"He asked us to keep things for him. Papers. Copies of what he'd gathered, sealed, in case anything happened to him upstream. We kept them eleven years. Then the collectors came—one of them wearing a Syndicate badge and the other wearing nothing at all but a very good coat—and they searched every house on this hill, and they found nothing, because we had already sent them downriver in pieces, inside eel traps, inside coffins, inside hymnals, to people who owed this parish their lives and hadn't forgotten it." She tapped the arm of her chair. "So somewhere on this river, mapmaker's daughter, pieces of your father's work are sleeping in other people's keeping. And the fact that you're holding his compass means the rest of him is still upstream. Which means the Corvanes have not won yet. Which means—"

She stopped. Her eyes had gone past Ivo's shoulder, to the window, to the dark.

Ivo turned.

Out on the black water, near the drowned spire, a lantern moved. Not a fishing lantern—fishing lanterns swing, dip, wander. This one traveled in a straight, unhurried line, the way a man walks when he is certain of where he is going. It paused at the foot of the old wharf where *the Vesper* lay tied. It hung there, motionless, for the length of ten heartbeats.

Then it went out.

"That'll be the second one this month," Bethel Quill said quietly behind her. "The first one asked my neighbor about a woman traveling alone. Tall. Dark hair. Cap pulled low." The old woman's hand closed over Ivo's wrist, dry and strong as root. "Girl, whatever you carry—it isn't only your father's ghost anymore. Someone else wants it found. Or wants *you* found. And come morning, you'll need to decide which of those frightens you more."

Upstairs, the boy in the hammock turned over in his sleep. Out on the river, the fog closed over the place where the lantern had been, and the drowned bell in the drowned spire hung silent in the dark, keeping, as it had for thirty years, the only record the Corvanes had never managed to edit.
