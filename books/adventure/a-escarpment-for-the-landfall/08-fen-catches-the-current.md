## 8. Fen Catches the Current

Silas Fen had learned long ago that a river keeps no secrets; it only postpones them.

He stood on the towpath above Lock Seven with his hands in the pockets of a coat too heavy for the season, watching the *Vesper*—no, she'd repainted her, the name was different now, something plain and forgettable, *Marrow* or *Mole* or some other small brown word—riding low against the current. Even at this distance he could pick her out of any traffic on the Vell. The way she stood at the wheel was the tell: not leaning, not lounging, but braced, as if the river were something to be argued with rather than ridden. Most pilots leaned into their boats like lovers. This one steered like a woman crossing ice.

She had passed through Lock Six at dawn, paid her toll in coin counted out flat on the lockmaster's palm so no one could see how thin the stack was, and asked no questions. That was discipline. That was a woman who understood that curiosity was a debt that always came due.

Fen had been sent to collect debts for eleven years, first for the Harrow Syndicate and then—for the last four, though the Syndicate's men did not precisely know it—in service of whoever paid the Corvane account. He was good at it because he understood the arithmetic of fear. A man who owed money would run from a fist but walk toward a ledger. You didn't chase debtors. You let them see the page, and they came to you.

But Ashgrove—Ivo Ashgrove, though the papers said *Ivo Marsh* now, and before that *Ivo Kell*, and before that nothing at all—was not running from money. She was running from a name, and names were heavier than gold, and gold was what she owed.

He turned up his collar and began to walk. The towpath would carry him faster than she could steam against the current, and there was a shortcut over the ridge at Miller's Bend that would put him ahead of her by nightfall. He had done this three times now since Landfall Point. She had never once seen him. That was the craft of it: to be the current itself, unseen, everywhere, always going the same direction she went.

What troubled him—and he permitted himself, once a day, in the hour after dusk, to actually feel the trouble rather than merely note it—was that he had stopped being certain which direction *he* was going.

* * *

The permit office at Hallowford was a converted chapel with a damp smell and a clerk named Prewitt who wore his authority like a borrowed coat. Fen arrived an hour before the *Marrow* was due, laid two silver crowns on the counter, and watched Prewitt's eyes do the arithmetic of corruption, which was the only arithmetic he truly trusted.

"Vessel coming upstream," Fen said. "Brown hull, single stack, pilot travels alone. There's a question about her cargo manifest."

"There's always a question about someone's manifest," Prewitt said, pocketing the crowns with the smoothness of long practice. "What am I looking for?"

"Nothing. That's the point. You're looking for nothing, and finding it takes time." Fen slid a third crown across. "A day's delay. Inspection of ballast, hold, and personal effects. Thorough. Documented."

Prewitt wrote it down. Fen watched him write it, and felt the familiar cold satisfaction of a mechanism engaging—the small gears of petty officials turning exactly as designed, each man doing his modest damage for his modest price. This was how the Corvanes governed the valley. Not with riders and torches, though they kept those for emergencies, but with clerks. With delays. With the thousand small frictions that could grind any traveler's journey into powder without ever leaving a mark you could point to in a court of law.

By tomorrow noon, Ivo Ashgrove would be tied up at Hallowford while Prewitt performed his theater of thoroughness. By tomorrow evening, the Corvane agent at Lock Nine—a soft-bellied man named Quill whom Fen despised with a purity he usually reserved for himself—would know her position, her heading, and her schedule. And by the day after, if the Syndicate moved when they were supposed to move, her boat would be collateral and her options would be the width of a plank.

It was clean work. It was the work he was paid for.

He walked out of the permit office into a rain just beginning, fine as sifted flour, and stood under the chapel's broken eave and did not move for a long moment.

Because here was the thing he had not written in any report: the ballast inspection would find the documents.

He knew what she carried. He had known since Landfall Point, since the morning after the tide fell out farther than any tide in living memory and gave back a cave that the Corvane family had spent sixty years pretending did not exist. His employers' instructions had been precise: *determine whether the woman located the chamber. Do not recover the materials. Report.* They wanted knowledge before action. Edmun Corvane had built an empire on knowing things before anyone else knew them, and he treated information the way a miser treats coin—counted, hoarded, never spent until the spending bought more than it cost.

So Fen's report, drafted in his careful hand on the night ferry from Landfall, had read: *Subject located the cave. Subject removed documents of unknown quantity. Subject's destination consistent with upstream traffic. Recommend interception at Lock Nine.*

All true. All accurate. And all of it missing one fact, the way a map can be perfectly drawn and still leave off the one road that matters:

He had seen what she took.

He had been in the cave. Before her. Three days before the tide gave it back to the world, when he'd come down at extreme low water on his own initiative, following a rumor he had carried for twenty years like a stone in his boot. And he had stood in that sealed chamber among the drowned portraits and the salt-stiffened ledgers, and he had opened one book at random, and he had read his brother's name.

Not in the way names usually appear in ledgers—not as debtor, not as asset. As a line item. An expenditure. *Drowned Parish resettlement: incidental losses, 214 souls, cost of suppression 1,900 crowns, press coverage contained.* Two hundred and fourteen souls, and Tam Fen was soul number ninety-one, listed between a ferryman and a schoolteacher, his death recorded not as a crime but as a *cost*, filed under *contained*.

Fen had sat down on the wet stone floor of that cave and held the book and read that line perhaps forty times, and somewhere around the thirtieth reading he had understood that he was not reading about his brother's death at all. He was reading about its erasure. The drowning itself had been an accident of engineering, or near enough—bad gates, bad weather, bad luck. But this page, this tidy column of figures, this was the murder. Someone had decided that two hundred and fourteen deaths were a rounding error, and someone else had made sure no one downstream ever learned the parish had existed, and the second someone had signed his work, and the signature was *E.C.*, and Fen had traced those letters with his finger until the salt haze blurred them.

Then he had closed the book, put it back exactly where it lay, climbed out of the cave, and reported to his employers that the site remained undiscovered.

That lie had cost him nothing yet. Lies of omission were the cheapest currency in the valley; everyone dealt in them. But he understood—he was a collector, he understood debts better than any man alive—that a lie was a loan, and interest compounded, and someday the whole balance came due at once, usually at the worst possible moment, usually in front of witnesses.

* * *

He caught up with the *Marrow* at Miller's Bend, where the river narrowed and sulked between shale banks, and where the fuel depot—a Syndicate operation, nominally, though the Corvanes owned the paper it stood on—kept its drums behind a fence that a determined child could breach.

Her boat was moored below the bend, taking on water at the standpipe, and she was up at the depot arguing with the attendant. Fen heard the argument from thirty yards: her voice level, the attendant's voice climbing, the familiar liturgy of scarcity. *No stock for private vessels. Syndicate contract holders only. Try Hallowford.* And her reply, quiet enough that the wind took most of it, but he caught the shape of it: she had tried Hallowford, and Hallowford had suddenly developed an inspection backlog, and wasn't that curious, and wasn't it also curious how every depot from Landfall to here had gone dry the same week.

She knew. Not everything—but she knew the river was thickening around her, that the small frictions were not accidents. Good. Fear sharpened her; he'd seen it in the set of her shoulders as she'd come through the locks, in the way she'd begun sleeping aboard instead of at taverns. A frightened quarry was a predictable quarry. A despairing one simply stopped.

The attendant turned her away. Fen watched her walk back down to her boat with her hands loose at her sides—not clenched, deliberately unclenched, a woman rationing her own anger—and he waited until she was aboard and the lamp in her cabin window showed small and yellow through the rain.

Then he walked to the depot's side gate, where the fence met the drainage ditch and a board had rotted loose since the spring floods, and he slipped inside, and he rolled a drum of diesel across the mud to the gap in the fence, and he left it there. Half-hidden by the ditch grass. Visible to anyone walking the towpath at dawn who happened to look down, which a woman short on fuel and long on watchfulness certainly would.

He did this the way he did everything: without hurry, without fingerprints, without a single motion wasted. And then he stood in the rain beside the drum for a moment longer than the work required, and asked himself the question he asked every night now, the one that was wearing a groove in him like water wears a groove in limestone:

*Which ledger is this entry in?*

Because he kept two now. He had kept two for weeks without admitting it, the way a man keeps drinking without admitting it, and the fiction had finally collapsed somewhere on the stretch between Lock Five and Miller's Bend. Ledger one belonged to his employers: the reports, the sabotage, the permits strangled in their sleep, the slow closing of the river around Ivo Ashgrove like a hand around a throat. Every entry in that book was honest, professional, and damning.

Ledger two had no employer. It lived in a oilcloth packet sewn into the lining of his coat, and it was written in a hand smaller than his own, cramped, almost furtive, and it recorded a different kind of accounting. *Hallowford permit office: bribed to delay subject. Entry logged.* *Fuel denied at Miller's Bend. Fuel provided at Miller's Bend. Balance: even.* *Lock Nine agent Quill: receives payments from Corvane household, skims forty percent. Weakness: vanity, gin, a mistress in Tarn Row.* Page after page of it. Names, prices, weaknesses, arrangements—the entire invisible architecture of the valley's corruption, the thing he had spent eleven years learning from the inside because he was the kind of man who could not stop noticing how the machine worked.

He told himself it was leverage. A collector's instinct: never destroy an asset, even one you hate. Someday the ledger would buy his life, or his freedom, or simply the pleasure of watching Quill swing.

He told himself that. But he had begun, lately, to suspect the truth, which was that the second ledger was not leverage at all. It was testimony. It was the thing you wrote when you had already decided, in some cellar of yourself where the lamps never went out, that someday you would have to stand up in front of somebody—God, a tribunal, a mob, it hardly mattered—and say *here is what I did, and here is what I knew, and here is the whole of it, weigh me.*

His brother had drowned at nineteen in a parish that officially had never existed, and Silas Fen had spent twenty years collecting other people's debts because the alternative was collecting his own.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. From the ridge above Miller's Bend, Fen watched her find the drum.

She came up the towpath at first light with a canvas sack, moving with the economy of a woman who had already inventoried everything she owned and found it wanting. At the drainage ditch she stopped. He saw her look at the drum. He saw her look along the towpath in both directions, then up at the depot, then at the tree line where he sat motionless behind a screen of gorse. Her gaze crossed within ten feet of him and moved on.

Then she did something he had not predicted. She did not take the drum immediately. She crouched beside it, and she studied the ground—the mud, the grass, the fence board—and Fen realized with a cold slide in his stomach that she was reading the scene the way he would read it. Looking for the story of how the drum had come to be there. And the mud, thank whatever gods bothered to watch over liars, said nothing, because he had approached along the ditch itself, in the water.

After a long minute she laughed. It was not a pleasant sound. It was the laugh of a woman who has just confirmed that the world is exactly as rigged as she suspected, and who has decided to be amused rather than broken. She took the drum's handles and began dragging it toward her boat, and halfway there she paused, looked back at the depot, and raised her voice just enough to carry across the water:

"Much obliged!"

The word sailed up the bend and found no one. But Fen, behind his gorse, felt it land on him like a hand on the shoulder.

*She knows,* he thought. Not that it was him. But she knew the fuel was a message, the way a flower on a grave is a message. Someone in the machine was leaking. Someone was feeding her just enough to keep her moving, and she was smart enough to ask why, and the answer—the answer he could not give her, not yet, maybe not ever—was that the machine had a man inside it whose brother was soul number ninety-one on a page marked *contained*, and that man had decided, without ever quite deciding it, that she was going to reach wherever she was going.

Because he had read the documents she'd copied from the cave—or rather, he'd read the ones she'd left behind, the duplicates, the discards, the pages too damaged to keep. He knew what she carried now. And he knew, with the terrible clarity of a man who has spent his life reading other people's obligations, what she intended. She wasn't running anymore. Running was what you did when you wanted your old life back. She was going upstream, toward the source, toward the place where the river began and the lying ended, and every mile of it she was becoming harder to stop and more dangerous to catch.

His employers would want her taken at Lock Nine. The Syndicate would want her boat. Edmun Corvane, when he finally understood what she carried, would want considerably less polite things.

And Fen, walking down through the wet gorse toward the towpath, keeping to the ditch, keeping to the shadows, keeping both his ledgers balanced, understood that the day was coming soon—days, not weeks—when he would have to choose which book he appeared in.

Behind him, far downstream, the *Marrow*'s engine coughed, caught, and settled into its working rhythm, and the sound carried up the valley like a pulse. Ahead of her, past Hallowford and the drowned country, the hills rose dark above the river, and in those hills, though Fen did not yet know it, people were waiting who remembered the seal on the documents in her ballast—who had spent thirty years keeping a rebellion alive in the only place it couldn't be erased: in the memory of the few who had survived it.

The current carried them all the same direction. For now.
