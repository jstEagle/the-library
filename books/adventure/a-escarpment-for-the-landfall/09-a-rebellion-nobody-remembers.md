## 9. A Rebellion Nobody Remembers

The mule track left the river at a place where the water forgot itself—a side channel gone to reeds, a broken weir, and beyond it a path so steep the Vell seemed to fall away beneath Ivo's boots like something she was abandoning rather than leaving. She climbed through birch scrub that whispered with each wind off the valley, and by the time the sun stood two hands above the ridgeline she could no longer hear the current at all. The silence of the hills had a different texture than the silence of the river. The river's silence was patient. This one felt like held breath.

She had been walking since before dawn, having tied off the *Magpie* below the drowned parish in a slough only she and the old woman knew about—half-sunk herself now, that town, its church spire rising from the floodwater like a finger raised in permanent objection. The woman, whose name Ivo still didn't know, had pressed a heel of black bread and a strip of dried eel into her hands and said only: *The millwrights went up. If anyone remembers how to hide you, it's them.* Then she had turned back to her nets strung across a second-story balcony, drying over water that should never have been there.

Ivo climbed. The compass rode in her breast pocket, and twice she took it out just to feel its wrongness—the needle swinging not north but steadily upstream, toward the headwaters, toward whatever waited where the Vell began. It swung even here, on dry ground, among trees. As if the source pulled at iron the way grief pulls at the living.

Near midday she found the first sign: a boundary stone, older than any survey marker she'd seen on the river, carved with a symbol she recognized from the wax seals in the cave—a wheel within a gear, a gear within a wheel. Someone had tried to chisel it out decades ago. They had failed. The stone kept its emblem the way scarred skin keeps its shape.

She was still crouched over it when the whistle came.

Not a bird. A bird doesn't repeat itself in threes, doesn't come from two directions at once, doesn't stop the moment you freeze. Ivo straightened slowly, hands visible, heart going like a paddlewheel.

"Turn around," said a voice above and behind her, "and walk toward the smoke."

* * *

They came out of the hillside the way water comes out of limestone—everywhere at once, from cracks she hadn't known were there. Four of them, then six, materializing from behind gorse and out of a hollow that had looked like nothing. They were old, most of them, or middle-aged with old eyes; two carried shotguns with barrels worn bright from handling, and one, a wiry man missing three fingers on his left hand, carried nothing but a coil of rope he handled like a rosary. Their clothes were hill-clothes, undyed wool and oiled canvas, and none of them asked her name.

"Smoke," repeated the nearest, a broad woman with a face like a river boulder. "Walk."

The path they took her down didn't exist until they walked it—switchbacking through bracken, along a ledge no wider than her shoulders, under an overhang furred with moss—and then opened without warning into a bowl of land cupped between two ridges, where a stream ran cold and quick over white stones and a cluster of low buildings huddled against the slope as if trying not to be seen. Smoke rose from a chimney built of fieldstone. Laundry hung between two dead apple trees. Goats regarded her with the flat contempt goats reserve for everyone.

And everywhere, the seal. Carved into lintels. Branded into gateposts. Painted, faded to a ghost, on the door of what looked like a smithy: the wheel within the gear.

"Millwrights," Ivo said quietly.

The boulder-faced woman stopped. Turned. Looked at her for a long moment with an expression Ivo couldn't read—hunger and suspicion fighting for the same ground.

"Nobody says that word," the woman said. "Not out loud. Not for forty years." She jerked her chin toward the largest building. "But you'll say it to her. Come on."

* * *

Odile Vasseur received her in a room that had been a mill office once—you could still see the outline on the wall where a ledger shelf had stood, a pale rectangle in the smoke-dark wood, like the negative space of everything taken away. Now there was a table, two chairs, a lamp burning fish oil, and maps. Maps everywhere. River charts so old their soundings were in a superseded system, valley surveys with towns marked on them that Ivo knew did not exist anymore, and one enormous chart of the upper Vell, hand-drawn, annotated in three different inks, with the headwaters circled and circled again until the paper had nearly worn through.

Odile herself was small and dry and precise, seventy if she was a day, with white hair cut short as a man's and hands that lay flat on the table like tools set down deliberately. Her eyes were the color of the stream outside—clear, cold, and moving fast over hidden rock.

"Sit," she said, and did not sit herself. "You came up the millwright track. Who sent you?"

"The net-mender at the drowned parish. The one who—" 

"I know who she is. What did she send you *for*?"

Ivo set the compass on the table.

It was, she would think later, the right move and the wrong one, made for the right reason and nearly the wrong one. Because Odile looked at the brass casing, at the engraving—ASHGROVE, in the same hand that had signed the documents in the cave—and something moved across her face like weather. And then she picked up the compass, weighed it, and set it down again with a click that sounded like a small door closing.

"Ashgrove," Odile said. "The mapmaker."

"My father."

"The mapmaker who went up the river twenty years ago with our help and came back as a thief in every record the Corvanes own. The mapmaker whose name we were ordered never to speak again, because speaking it got people questioned, and questioning people got people disappeared." Odile leaned forward. The lamp threw her shadow huge against the pale rectangle on the wall. "So. You'll understand when I ask you plainly: are you his daughter, or are you bait?"

The question landed like a boathook. Ivo sat with it. She thought about lying—she was good at lying, she'd been lying for fifteen years, lying was load-bearing in her life—but something about this room, this woman, these maps of erased towns, made lying feel like tracking mud across a clean floor.

"I don't know," she said. "I might be. I've been followed since Landfall Point. There's a debt collector named Silas Fen who works for the Harrow Syndicate and maybe for other people too, and he's been one lock behind me the whole way upriver. So yes. Maybe I'm bait. Maybe I'm bait without knowing it. But I didn't come here to be anything. I came because my father died trying to reach the source, and because I found—" she stopped. Started again. "Because the tide gave back a cave full of everything the Corvanes ever erased, and one of the things in it was my family's name, and I have spent half my life being nobody on purpose, and I am done with it."

The room was silent except for the lamp's small combustion and, somewhere outside, a goat expressing opinions.

"You found the cave," Odile said. Not a question. A test.

"I found the cave. Documents. Portraits. Sealed correspondence with the Corvane crest on it. Enough paper to hang them a hundred times over, if paper could hang anyone, which it can't unless someone reads it." Ivo met the cold clear eyes. "You recognize the seal. That net-mender said the millwrights went up. So you tell me what I'm carrying, and whether it's worth dying for, because I've been asking myself that since Lock Nine and I keep getting different answers depending on how scared I am."

For the first time, something that was almost warmth crossed Odile's face. Almost. It might have been indigestion.

"She talks like a boat captain," Odile said—to the room, to the doorway, where the boulder-faced woman and the rope-handed man had silently gathered. "That's either good or very bad."

* * *

They tested her for three days.

They tested her the way the hills test everything: slowly, with weather. On the first day they gave her work—splitting firewood, hauling water, holding the far end of things—and watched how she complained. (She didn't. Complaining was for people who believed someone owed them comfort.) On the second day they let her sit in on a council of sorts, seven elders around the table in the old mill office arguing about seed grain and a roof, and watched whether she spoke when she shouldn't. (She spoke once, about load weights on the mule track, and then shut up.) On the third day they put her alone in a room with the great chart of the upper Vell and told her to memorize nothing, and she understood it was the opposite of what it seemed, and studied anyway, and admitted afterward that she had studied, and saw in Odile's face that this—admission—had been the actual examination all along.

In between, they fed her barley soup and hard cheese and stories, doled out in fragments like rations.

She learned that the Millwright Uprising had been neither spontaneous nor small. Sixty years ago, when the Corvanes had begun buying up the mills and rewriting the water rights—lowering the weirs upstream to starve the downstream towns' wheels, flooding the parishes they wanted cleared—the millwrights had organized. Not with muskets, mostly. With records. They had been, of all things, an archive movement: clerks and engineers and parish scriveners who copied deeds and ledgers and shipping manifests, who built duplicate registries in hidden rooms, who believed with a ferocity that seems naive only in retrospect that paper truth could stand against moneyed power.

"They almost won," said the rope-handed man, whose name was Perrin and who had lost his fingers not to machinery but to frostbite during the winter the riders burned the valley farms. "There were four hundred of us keeping books in cellars from Hallowford to the gorge. For two years the Corvanes couldn't sell a fraudulent deed because somewhere there was a true copy."

"What happened?"

"What always happens." Perrin turned his mug slowly on the table. "They bought one of us. Just one. A boy who ran messages between cells. He sold the locations of the registry rooms, and in one week the riders burned eleven cellars and forty-one people went into the gorge, and the ones of us who lived learned the lesson the Corvanes wanted taught: that memory has a price, and poor people can't pay it." He looked up. "We kept a few copies anyway. Out of spite. Spite keeps better than hope."

"And the seal? The wheel in the gear?"

"The millwright's mark. Every guild man had it on his tools. When we went underground it became—" he smiled without humor, "—a password written in wood and stone. Anyone who knows it is either family or a very good liar."

* * *

On the fourth evening Odile summoned her to the mill office alone, and Ivo knew the shape of the thing before it began, the way you know weather coming in your bad knee.

"Show me your father's route," Odile said, turning the great chart around. "From what you've reconstructed. From the cave papers and whatever he left you. Show me where he went."

Ivo hesitated. "I don't have his route. I have a compass that points upstream and a name on a wall of carvings I haven't seen yet. Everything else is guesswork."

"Guess anyway. Out loud. Where a man with a compass like that would go, starting from Landfall, twenty years ago."

So Ivo guessed, tracing with one finger: upriver past Lock Nine, past the drowned parish, hugging the east bank where the current slackened; through the hill country by the old drove roads; down to the river again at Hallowford—

"No," Odile said sharply. "Not Hallowford. Never Hallowford. The ford was watched even then. Here." Her own finger took over, dry and certain, following a line west of the river through country Ivo had never charted. "The gorge. The Gorge of Names, we called it, before it was dangerous to call it anything. Your father came through here—we carried him partway, my brother and I, with his instruments in oilcloth. He had letters of introduction from the uprising's last cell. He had the compass. He had—" she paused, and Ivo watched the pause happen, watched the old woman's hands flatten on the table, "—he had more courage than sense, which is the family trait of everyone who ever came up this river thinking the truth was a place you could walk to."

"You helped him," Ivo said. "You got him to the gorge."

"I got him to the gorge." Odile's voice did not change at all, and that was how Ivo knew the sentence cost her. "And then Corvane riders came up the drove road behind us, faster than they should have, faster than possible unless someone had talked, and I made a decision. I scattered. I led my people west into the high country and I left him at the gorge mouth with a boy named Tam and a promise we'd come back for him in three days." The lamp hissed. "We went back in five. Tam was dead in the shallows. Your father was gone. No body. No blood enough to account for a body. Just—" her finger stopped on the chart, on a mark Ivo hadn't noticed, a tiny inked X with a date beside it in faded brown, "—this. His last camp. His instruments were stacked neatly beside it. Neatly, do you understand? Men who die running don't stack their instruments."

Ivo's throat had gone tight. "He laid them down. He meant to come back for them."

"He meant to go on. On foot, into the headwaters, alone, knowing riders were behind him." Odile finally sat, folding down onto the chair like a collapsed instrument. "And I have spent twenty years telling myself that scattering was the right call, that one life wasn't worth forty, that the Corvanes wanted us to follow him and die with him. Twenty years of doctrine, girl. My doctrine is: nobody touches the source. We survived by forgetting. We will survive by continuing to forget."

"But you don't believe it anymore," Ivo said quietly. "Or you wouldn't have shown me the X."

The silence stretched long enough for the lamp to gutter once. Outside, the stream ran over its white stones, indifferent and constant, the way truth is supposed to run and rarely does.

"I believe," Odile said at last, "that the doctrine kept forty people alive for twenty years, and that doctrines are like dams—they hold until they don't, and what they hold back gets bigger the whole time." She looked up, and her eyes were not cold now; they were tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "Here is what I can give you, Ashgrove. Guides—Perrin and Sorrel, the boulder-woman, they'll take you as far as the high pasture above the gorge. Safehouses—there are three between here and Hallowford, marked on no chart but mine, and I'll copy the marks for you tonight. Food, rope, oilcloth for your papers." She held up one finger. "And one piece of knowledge that you cannot get anywhere else in this valley, and that you will need, because without it you'll die at the last mile the way your father nearly did."

"The source is guarded," Ivo said. It wasn't a guess. She'd felt it coming down the whole length of the conversation like a change in pressure.

"The source is *staffed*," Odile corrected. "There is a station at the headwaters. Has been for sixty years—it was built with uprising labor, did you know that? Our own people, starving after the crackdowns, hired to quarry stone for the thing that would bury us. It looks like a supply depot. Weather station, they call it on the official charts, if it appears at all. It is not a weather station. It is where the Corvanes keep their archivists, and their archivists decide what has happened." She let that sit. "Floods that never officially flooded. Famines struck from the harvest rolls. Whole parishes unmade. It happens there, at the top of the river, where the water is born clean and the record is born dirty. Your father understood that. It's why he went. It's why they couldn't simply let him arrive, and couldn't simply shoot him on the road where witnesses might wonder."

"So he arrived."

"Or he was taken inside. Or he died at the threshold. No one living knows." Odile rose, went to the great chart, and began copying its secret marks onto a scrap of oilcloth with a stylus pressed hard enough to score. "What I know is that the station takes deliveries twice a season from Hallowford, that its garrison rotates yearly, and that in sixty years no one who went in uninvited has come out to tell the design of the place." She finished, folded the oilcloth, and held it out. "Take it. Take the guides. Take the food. And take this last thing, which is free because it's worthless to anyone sensible: the moment you step onto the high pasture, you stop being a woman running from her debt and become a woman the Corvanes must kill. There is no invisibility past that line. Whatever you're carrying in your breast pocket, decide now whether it's worth becoming visible forever."

Ivo took the oilcloth. It was heavier than paper had any right to be.

"It's worth it," she said. "It was worth it twenty years ago, to a man stacking his instruments neatly in the dark. I'm just late."

Something in Odile's face finally, fully broke—not into tears, but into decision, which for some people is the harder breaking. "Then you leave at dawn," she said. "Before dawn. And Ashgrove—one more thing. The syndicate men were asking at the crossroads hamlet yesterday. Three of them, with a requisition order for any vessel answering to a gray riverboat with a patched sternwheel." Her eyes flicked up, sharp. "They described your boat, girl. Which means they know your boat. Which means while you've been splitting our firewood, they've been walking down to your slough."

Ivo stood very still. Below the hills, invisible over the ridgeline, the Vell ran on in the dark toward the drowned parish, toward the slough, toward the *Magpie* sleeping at her moorings with everything Ivo owned in her hold—and three men with a requisition order were already on the road down.

"Dawn," Ivo said. "Then I'll need to be fast."
