## 10. The Price of Passage

They came at dawn, the way debts always do—not with violence but with paperwork.

Ivo heard the engines first, two of them, idling low against the current in that patient way boats idle when they have nowhere to be and all the time in the world to not be there. She was on the *Kestrel's* aft deck with a tin cup of chicory going cold in her hands, watching mist lift off the water in ropes, and she knew before she saw the hulls what they were. The Harrow Syndicate ran gray boats with black rub rails and never painted a name on either. You were meant to know them the way you know a debt collector's knock—by the shape of the silence after.

She set down the cup. There was no point running; the river narrowed above Millbrook into the shallows she'd threaded the night before, and below lay Lock Nine and its Corvane inspectors, who had let her through once by the grace of a lockmaster's vanity and would not be fooled twice. The *Kestrel* sat in the pool between like a coin dropped in a drain.

The first boat came alongside without a word exchanged. Ropes went over her rail. Boots on her deck—four men, then five, moving with the unhurried competence of people who had done this a hundred times and been thanked for it more often than resisted. At their center walked a woman Ivo had hoped never to see again: Vess Halloway, the Syndicate's river agent for the middle valley, a broad-shouldered, gray-tempered woman who wore a ledger chain around her neck the way other people wore saints' medals.

"Captain Ashgrove," Vess said.

"I don't use that name."

"You do now." She unclasped the ledger, wet her thumb, turned pages until she found what she wanted, and held the book out flat so Ivo could see the entry. "Two hundred forty crowns outstanding. Interest compounded monthly since the spring. Your last payment was sixty days ago, and your last payment was short." Her eyes came up, flat as slate. "You've been hard to find, Captain. Boats that are hard to find make lenders nervous."

"The river flooded in April. Half my runs drowned."

"Half your runs drowned," Vess repeated, without inflection, and Ivo understood that the words had already been weighed and found weightless. That was the thing about the Syndicate: they did not deal in reasons. Reasons were what the river did. Debts were what people did.

One of the men had drifted aft, toward the engine housing, and was looking down into the bilge with too much interest. Ivo's stomach tightened. Under the floor plates, wrapped in oilcloth inside the ballast keel, lay the documents from the cave—the Corvane papers, the portraits, the brittle sheaf of things that proved a family had eaten a valley and picked its bones clean. And beneath those, in a smaller wrapping, the brass compass that pointed upriver and refused, with a kind of stubborn dignity, to point anywhere else.

"Your man doesn't need to inspect the ballast," she said. "It's ballast."

"Everything is something else, on this river," Vess said mildly. "That's why we weigh it." But she waved the man off, and he straightened, and Ivo let one breath out slowly through her nose. Either Vess hadn't come for the papers or she didn't know they existed. Both possibilities were survivable. Only one of them was comfortable.

"Here is where you are," Vess said, closing the ledger with a sound like a small door shutting. "The Syndicate takes the vessel. Effective now. It stands as collateral against the full balance plus recovery costs. You have three days."

"Three days to do what? Swim?"

"Three days to bring me two hundred forty crowns, or title to whatever asset replaces the boat." Vess looked past her, at the *Kestrel*—at the patched rail Ivo had re-caulked herself, at the wheel her father had turned before her, at the little brass bell worn smooth by thirty years of thumbs—and something almost human crossed her face, there and gone like a fish shadow under a bridge. "I've taken a lot of boats, Captain. I'll tell you what I tell everyone. The boat isn't yours anyway. It belongs to the water. You just borrowed it longer than most."

They worked fast. Lines cast off, fenders positioned, the gray boat nudging the *Kestrel* away from the bank with the tenderness of thieves handling stolen goods. Ivo stood on the mud and watched her whole life float ten feet off shore, and the worst part—the part that lodged under her ribs like a splinter—was how quiet it was. No shouting. No chains. Just the ledger, the arithmetic, and the river accepting another vessel into its indifferent keeping.

Vess paused at the rail as the boats drew apart. "There's a mule track leaves Odile's camp heading north along the ridge," she said, conversationally, as if remarking on weather. "Steep, but it beats walking the flood road. If a person were inclined to walk."

Then the mist took them, and Ivo was alone on the bank with a tin cup, a satchel, and three days.

* * *

She went up to Odile's camp because there was nowhere else, and because pride, she had learned, was a luxury item priced beyond her current means.

The Millwright survivors kept their settlement in a fold of the hills where the trees grew old and the paths forked meaninglessly on purpose. A boy of perhaps twelve saw her coming and vanished ahead of her, so that by the time she reached the first smoke, Odile was already waiting outside the longhouse with her arms folded and her face arranged into an expression Ivo had come to think of as the toll gate.

"They took the boat," Ivo said. No point dressing it.

Odile studied her for a long moment. "And you came here instead of drowning yourself, which shows sense. Come in. There's barley tea. It tastes like punishment, which builds character."

Inside, the fire was low and the room smelled of resin, wool, and decades. Ivo sat on a bench worn into two shallow hollows by generations of sitting, and told it plainly: the seizure, the three days, the sum. She left out nothing except the cave and the compass, which stayed against her ribs in their oilcloth, warm as a second heartbeat.

"Two hundred forty crowns in three days," Odile said when she finished. "On foot."

"On foot."

"And what will you do in three days? Rob a mint?"

"I'm going upstream," Ivo said. "To the source. The money was always a story I told myself so I could keep moving. The boat made the story easier. The boat's gone. The story stays."

Something shifted in the old woman's face—not softening exactly, but a recalculation, the way a lockmaster recalculates when a vessel draws deeper than her papers claim. "You understand what's at the source."

"I understand enough to be afraid of it. That's usually where understanding starts."

Odile snorted, which Ivo had learned was the local dialect for approval. Then she stood, joints cracking like green wood in a hot stove, and led her out behind the longhouse to a lean-to where a mule stood regarding the world with profound and ancient disapproval.

"This is Brother Constable," Odile said. "He is stupid, vindictive, and sure-footed on scree, which makes him the finest creature I have ever known. He knows the ridge track better than I do, and I cut half of it. He will carry your pack and refuse every instruction you give him, and if you listen to him instead of yourself, he will keep you alive." She pressed a rolled bundle into Ivo's arms: a bedroll, a waxed cloak, a bag of dried fish and hard bread, a flint, a coil of good rope. "The track follows the ridge above the flood road for two days, then drops to the gorge country. After that—" she stopped, and her jaw worked as though chewing on something that had been stuck in her teeth for twenty years, "—after that you're beyond anything I can help you with."

"Why help me at all?" Ivo asked. "You tested me for days. You still watch me like I'm a lit fuse."

"Because the Corvanes took my river too," Odile said simply. "Different year. Same family." She turned back toward the longhouse, then paused. "Captain. When you reach the gorge—if you reach the gorge—you'll want to ask me about a man who walked that track before you. Don't. Not yet. Ask me when you come back down, if you come back down. Some answers keep better than others."

* * *

Brother Constable lived up to his reputation within the first hour, refusing a perfectly good stretch of trail in favor of a goat path that switchbacked up through bracken and loose stone, and Ivo, having no standing to argue, followed.

Walking, she discovered, was a language she barely spoke.

Her body knew the river the way a bell knows its rope. Twenty years of reading current, of feeling a shoal through the sole of her boot on the deck planks before her eyes ever found the broken water, of knowing a lock's mood by the sound of its sluices—all of that was useless here. On the ridge there was no current to read. The land did not flow; it merely endured, in folds and shoulders and sudden drops, and it expected her to negotiate every yard of it on her own two feet like some prehistoric ancestor. By midday her calves burned with a deep, mineral ache that no day at the wheel had ever produced. By late afternoon her boots—river boots, deck boots, wrong boots—had raised blisters in places she hadn't known she owned.

But there was this: from the ridge, she could finally see the river entire.

She had spent her life inside it, the way a fish spends its life inside water, and had never once seen its shape. Now it unspooled below her to the south—a brown ribbon stitching together the valley, threading the towns she knew like beads on a string, each one with its landing, its lock, its little church spire, its little lie about who it belonged to. Somewhere down there was Landfall Point and the cave, sealed now behind the returning tide. Somewhere down there floated the *Kestrel*, being weighed and assessed and entered into a ledger. Somewhere down there, in a town called Hallowford that appeared on no map printed in the last thirty years, thousands of people had once lived until a dam turned their parish into a lake, and the Corvanes had edited the event out of existence so thoroughly that even grief had nowhere legal to live.

She stopped on a bald shoulder of rock where the wind came clean off the high country, and she took out the compass.

It sat in her palm, brass gone soft-edged with handling, engraved with a name she had spent half her life pretending wasn't hers. The needle swung, settled, and pointed north-northwest—upstream, always upstream, toward the forbidden headwaters where the original ledger waited and where her father had walked this same track, or one like it, twenty years ago, carrying the same inherited stubbornness in a younger body.

"Dad," she said aloud, to the wind, feeling foolish and doing it anyway. "You should've warned me about the blisters."

The needle trembled, as needles do, and held.

She thought about what Vess had said—that the boat belonged to the water and she'd only borrowed it. There was a cruelty in it and also, unexpectedly, a freedom. Every mile she'd ever run had been shaped by the *Kestrel*: which towns she could reach, which locks she could pass, which debts she could outrun. The boat had been her invisibility, and her invisibility had been her prison, and both of them were gone now, floating gray-handed downriver toward an auction block. What remained was a woman on a mountain with a mule, a compass, a satchel of forbidden paper, and three days ticking down like water out of a cracked hull.

It was, she admitted to the wind, the freest she had felt since the trial.

She laughed—a short, startled bark of a laugh—and Brother Constable turned his head and regarded her with one liquid eye, clearly concluding that his new charge was unstable, which was fair.

* * *

The second night she camped in a hollow below the track, a fire no bigger than two fists, eating dried fish and listening to the dark. The ridge country had its own sounds—owl, wind, the tick of stone cooling—but underneath them she kept hearing engines that weren't there, and boot heels on a deck that wasn't there, and once, distinctly, a voice saying *two hundred forty crowns* in a voice that was not Vess Halloway's but colder.

Someone else was asking about the cave. Maren had told her that, weeks ago, in the tavern at Landfall, with her eyes doing the talking her mouth wouldn't. Someone had come before Ivo ever found the tide's gift, asking after a sea cave below the cliffs. Which meant the Corvanes—or their enforcer, the one the rebels called the Quiet Man—had known the archive existed, had maybe always known, and had been waiting for the tide or a fool or a daughter to open it.

And the Syndicate had found her anyway, twice, on a river with a thousand miles of hiding in it. Coincidence was a luxury she'd stopped believing in somewhere around Lock Nine.

She fed the fire a stick and watched the sparks climb. Three days. Two hundred forty crowns. A boat she couldn't reclaim and a name she couldn't bury and a needle that pointed, with terrible patience, at everything she'd lost and everything she might still become.

Above her, the stars wheeled slow over the gorge country, and somewhere in that black fold of hills, carved into living rock, was a wall of names—including, she now knew, her own family's. ASHGROVE, cut deep, among the disappeared. Her father had died trying to reach the source, and everyone downstream called him a thief, and the record—the official record, the only record anyone was allowed to read—said so forever.

Unless someone went and changed it.

She slept badly and woke before dawn with the decision already made, the way rivers make their courses: not all at once, but inevitably, and then all at once.

* * *

The track dropped into the gorge country on the third morning, and the world changed character around her like weather.

The ridge oaks gave way to pine and shadow. The air cooled and thickened. Below and to the east, she could hear water moving through stone—a deeper note than any river she'd known, a bass rumble like the valley clearing its throat—and the compass needle, which had held steady for two days, began to swing in small, eager arcs, as if the thing it sought were close enough to smell.

Brother Constable stopped so abruptly that Ivo walked into his rump.

Ahead, where the track bent around a shoulder of cliff, a figure stood waiting in the shade of a leaning pine. Not armed, or not visibly. Dressed in traveling gray, hands loose at his sides, face composed into an expression of professional regret that Ivo recognized the way you recognize a storm front.

Silas Fen. The Syndicate's collector. The man who had boarded her boat in the spring, counted her cargo with too much care, and asked one question too many about where she'd been the night the tide fell.

"Captain Ashgrove," he said.

"I don't use that name."

"I know," Fen said. "That's rather the problem." He glanced up at the pines, at the empty track winding away north, and when he spoke again his voice was lower, pitched for her alone, and it carried something under it that she had never heard from a Syndicate man before.

"We need to talk about your father. And about what Odile did at the gorge—twenty years ago, the night he died."

The compass needle shivered against its glass, pointing north, pointing home, pointing at the truth.
