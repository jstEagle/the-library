## 11. What Odile Buried

The mule track above the river was older than any of them, and Odile Vetch had spent forty years pretending she didn't know where it led.

She walked it now in the blue hour before dawn, her boots finding the ruts without looking, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. Behind her came the Ashgrove girl—no, the Ashgrove woman, Ivo, thirty years old and carrying her father's compass like a coal cupped in both hands—and behind Ivo, the two young ones, Pell and Sarn, who had never known the valley before the Corvanes taught it to forget itself. The mules followed on their lead ropes, patient as stones.

Odile did not look back. Looking back was a discipline she had failed at exactly once in her life, and the cost of that failure was still carved into a gorge wall two days' walk upstream.

*You will take him as far as the narrows,* she had been told. *No farther. The source is not for touching.*

She had agreed. She had even meant it.

The track climbed through birch scrub gone silver in the early light, and the smell of the river rose up to meet them—the green rot of the backwaters, the cold mineral breath of the current. Odile breathed it the way other people prayed. Forty years of breathing it. Forty years of telling the young ones: *the uprising is dead, the archive is a story, and the source is a story about a story. We keep the seal. We keep the names. We do not go.*

Doctrine. That was what guilt looked like when you let it harden long enough. Not a wound—a wall.

"You're quiet," Ivo said, drawing level with her. The woman walked like a boat-hand, rolling slightly with some internal tide, her eyes always moving—upstream, down, across. Reading water that wasn't there.

"I'm always quiet."

"You were quieter yesterday."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. "Yesterday we hadn't started climbing toward the thing I swore never to climb toward."

Ivo said nothing to that. She was learning, this one. Learning when silence was a question and when it was a courtesy. Her father had been the same way—Tam Ashgrove, the mapmaker, with his ink-stained fingers and his terrible patience, who could wait out a storm or a lie with equal calm.

Odile's stomach turned over, the way it did every time the name surfaced. She had thought she'd drowned that feeling decades ago, weighted it and sunk it in the deep pool below the gorge. But grief was buoyant. Grief always came back up.

They made camp at midday in a hollow below an outcrop, where a spring seeped from the rock and the birches closed overhead like a held breath. Pell built a smokeless fire of dry alder, because Pell was good at fires and bad at everything else, and Sarn saw to the mules. Odile sat with her back against the stone and watched Ivo unroll the cave documents on a groundsheet—those brittle, tide-swollen papers she had pulled from the Corvane family's drowned vault, evidence of a century of erasure.

And there it was again. The seal. The millwheel crossed with the quill, pressed into wax gone the color of old blood.

The Millwright Uprising's own mark, stolen and inverted by the family that crushed it.

"You've seen this before," Ivo said. It wasn't a question either. "At the camp. When I showed you the first folio, your face changed. You knew it."

"I know it," Odile said.

"Then tell me what it means. All of it. Because I've read these papers until my eyes bleed, and they tell me what the Corvanes *did*. They don't tell me why the seal is ours."

Odile looked at the fire. At the girl. At the country rising around them, fold over fold, all the way to the high gray shoulder of the headwaters where the river came out of the earth like something being born or something escaping.

"Build the fire higher," she said. "This is a long story, and I'm only going to tell it once."

* * *

"We were not always smugglers of memory," Odile began. "We were millwrights. That was the whole of our ambition—wheels, gears, sluices, the honest arithmetic of water and grain. When the Corvanes began buying the locks, we built the resistance the way we built everything: to last. We kept records. That was our weapon and our pride. Every bribe, every burned tenement, every man they disappeared—we wrote it down, copied it three times, hid it in three places. We called it the Ledger of Grievances, and we believed—I *believed*—that a thing written down could not be unwritten."

She turned her hands over, palms up. Old hands, rope-scarred, steady.

"The seal was ours. The millwheel for the mills, the quill for the record. When they broke us—when Edmun's father rode the valley with fire and took every wheel apart bolt by bolt—they kept the stamp. Trophy. And then something worse: they learned what we had known. That whoever holds the record holds the past, and whoever holds the past owns everyone living in it. They didn't just destroy our ledger, girl. They *became* it."

Sarn had stopped fidgeting with the mule tack. Pell had stopped feeding the fire. Even the mules seemed to be listening.

"So the archive in the cave—" Ivo started.

"Is theirs. Their sins, their erasures, sealed away where the tide could guard them better than any lock. And the master ledger, the original, the one book they can't afford to lose—" Odile's voice went flat, the voice she used for prices and funerals, "—is at the source. Where your compass points. Where your father went."

The word landed between them like a dropped anchor.

"How do you know he went?" Ivo asked quietly.

"Because I took him."

* * *

The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope a wood pigeon called, three notes, like a question asked twice and abandoned.

"It was twenty-two years ago," Odile said. "I was younger than you are now. Harder, too—or I told myself so. Tam Ashgrove came to us through the ferry network, a mapmaker with a ruined name and a theory. He'd found discrepancies. Old survey marks that didn't match the official charts, parish boundaries that moved overnight, a village called Hallowford-that-wasn't. He'd traced the pattern the way water traces a valley: patiently, downhill, toward the lowest point. Toward the source. He believed the original ledger was there, and he believed—if he could reach it, copy it, carry it out—that the valley could be freed the way we'd once dreamed. Not by burning anything. By *reading aloud*."

"He talked like that," Odile said, and heard her own voice soften despite everything. "Like reading could be a flood."

Ivo had gone very still. The compass lay in her lap, and her thumb moved over its engraving—ASHGROVE, the brass worn bright at the letters—back and forth, back and forth, a woman touching a scar.

"We outfitted him," Odile went on. "Food, rope, the mule track, the safehouses. I guided him myself as far as the high pasture, because I was the only one who knew the way past the wardens' patrol line, and because—" she stopped. Started again. "Because I wanted to see it. The source. I was Millwright-born; the source was in our oldest songs. *Where the water begins, the debt begins.* I wanted to stand where the river stood up out of the ground."

"And?"

"And three days out, Corvane riders found us." The words came out of her like splinters coming out of flesh, slow and tearing. "Not by accident. Someone sold us. I've had twenty-two years to work out who, and I still don't know, and that not-knowing has eaten more of me than the knowing would have. But they found us—six riders, at dusk, in the narrows where the track pinches against the gorge wall and there is no cover and no escape except down."

She looked at her hands again. They were steady. She hated how steady they were.

"There was a split in the track. One way along the gorge rim—open, exposed, a day's run to the next safehouse. One way down, to a ledge above the river, hidden under an overhang, where a man could wait out a search. I knew both ways. Tam didn't. I was his guide. That was the entire covenant of the thing: *I know the country, you don't, do what I say.*"

"What did you say?" Ivo asked. Her voice was careful, the way you're careful walking on river ice.

"I said *down*. I put him on the ledge with half the food and the compass, and I told him I'd draw the riders off along the rim and come back for him in two days." Odile finally raised her eyes. "And then I drew them off along the rim, and I ran, and I did not come back."

Pell made a small sound. Odile lifted one hand—not quite a command to silence, but near enough.

"Not that night. Not the second night. On the third day I told myself the search would still be wide. On the fourth, that the snowmelt had flooded the ledge path. On the fifth, I told myself he'd have gone on alone, because Tam Ashgrove was exactly the kind of man who'd go on alone, and maybe he had, maybe he was fine, maybe—" She shook her head. "On the sixth day I stopped telling myself things. And I never went back to look. Not to the ledge. Not to the narrows. Not for twenty-two years. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I chose the uprising over the man. I chose the doctrine—*never touch the source, never spend lives on the old dream*—and I built that doctrine out of my own cowardice and called it wisdom. Every year I preached it harder. Every year the preaching got easier, because the alternative was going up that track and seeing what was left of what I'd done."

The hollow was silent. The spring seeped. The fire settled into itself.

Ivo set the compass down on the groundsheet, deliberately, the way you disarm someone.

"They branded him a thief," she said at last. "That's the story I grew up inside. Tam Ashgrove, who stole Corvane survey documents and drowned in the gorge running from the law. My mother died believing it. I went to prison believing a version of it. I have spent my whole adult life being the daughter of a thief." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it—it stayed level as a millpond, and you could hear the depth underneath. "And you knew. You knew when you tested me at the camp, when you made me swear my motives, when you gave me guides and safehouses. You knew what happened to my father, and you let me walk into this blind."

"Yes," Odile said.

"Why? Why tell me now?"

"Because we're two days from the gorge, and you'd have found the names anyway." She nodded upstream, toward the high country. "The walls down there—they're carved. Everyone the Corvanes disappeared, everyone the record lost. The Millwrights started it, before the end; the families kept it after. Your father's name is on that wall, girl. ASHGROVE, cut deep, with the others. And beside it—" her throat worked, "—beside it there's a message. Scratched small, low, where you'd have to kneel to read it. I've never knelt. I couldn't make myself kneel. But the ones who tend the wall have described it to me, and I've carried the description for twenty-two years like a stone in my boot."

Ivo's hand had closed over the compass again, knuckles white.

"*The source remembers everything,*" Odile said. "That's what it says. That's what he scratched into the rock while he waited on a ledge for a guide who wasn't coming. He wasn't cursing us. He wasn't even afraid, far as anyone can tell. He was leaving a message forward. For whoever came after." She exhaled, long and shaking. "For you, I think. Though he couldn't have known it. He just trusted that the river would carry someone, someday, who needed to hear it."

* * *

Ivo stood. Walked to the edge of the hollow and stood looking downstream, toward the country she'd come from, the drowned parish, the locks, the sea. Her shoulders were rigid. The wind moved her hair and she didn't seem to feel it.

Odile let her stand. Some things could not be answered while sitting by a friendly fire.

When Ivo turned back, her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear.

"I ought to leave you here," she said. "I ought to take Pell and Sarn and the mules and the seal and everything you've given me, and finish this without you, and let you keep your doctrine and your wall and your twenty-two years of not kneeling."

"Yes," Odile said. "You ought."

"But you came." Ivo's jaw tightened. "You could have sent me with the young ones and stayed behind your palisade with your rules. Instead you're on this track, walking toward the place you swore off, at your age, on your knees' worth of bad hips. So either you're lying to me about everything, or you're trying to carry something back that you buried. Which is it?"

Odile found that she was crying—quietly, without drama, the way a roof leaks, having held for decades. She hadn't wept at her husband's funeral. She hadn't wept when the last of the old millwrights died. She wept now, in front of a stranger who was not a stranger, because the question had cut clean to the bottom of her.

"I want to kneel," she said. "At the wall. Beside his name. I want to read what he wrote with my own eyes instead of carrying it secondhand like contraband. And then I want to walk you past the wardens' line, because I'm the only one alive who knows the gap in the patrol, and if you die in those narrows because I stayed home—" she wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, "—then the ledge wins. Then the cowardice wins forever. I won't give it that."

Ivo studied her for a long moment. The firelight moved on her face, and Odile watched her weigh it—all of it, the betrayal and the confession and the forty years of hardened grief standing in front of her offering its last service.

"My father trusted you," Ivo said finally. "Right up until the split in the track, he trusted you. Maybe after it too—he waited, didn't he? Three days on a ledge, and he waited, because he believed you'd come back." She picked up the compass and stowed it inside her coat, against her heart, where she kept it. "So I'll trust you the same amount. Right up until the next split in the track. And Odile—"

"Aye."

"If there's a next split, and you choose wrong again, there won't be a twenty-two-year grace period. There'll just be me, and I'm my father's daughter."

Something in Odile's chest loosened—painfully, like a knot cut from rope under tension—but loosened. "That," she said, "is the fairest offer I've had in two decades."

She kicked soil over the fire and stood, and the four of them took up the lead ropes, and the mule track bent upward through the birches toward the high country, toward the narrows, toward the gorge of names.

It was Sarn, youngest, who broke the silence an hour later, pointing back the way they'd come with a sudden sharp motion.

"Smoke," he said. "Downriver. Past the drowned parish. A lot of it."

Odile shaded her eyes and looked, and her heart went cold and old and certain all at once. That was no farmer's burn. That was a pursuit fire—riders signaling to riders, line of sight to line of sight, up the whole length of the valley.

They had found the trail. And whatever else happened at the gorge, they would not be alone when they reached it.
