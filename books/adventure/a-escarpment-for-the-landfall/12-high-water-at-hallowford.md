## 12. High Water at Hallowford

The valley road at Hallowford was built on a promise the river never made.

Ivo saw it from the ridge above the town as the light went copper and long: a single causeway running between two arms of water, cobbled, raised a man's height above the fields, the only dry way east toward the gorge country. On either side of it lay the old flood meadows, and beyond them the Hallowford weir—a low gray spine of masonry across the river, older than any of the Corvane locks downstream, with its row of sluice gates rusted shut for as long as anyone living could remember. The town huddled under it like a dog beneath a table, trusting the hand that fed it.

She had walked three days since the Syndicate took the *Vespera* off her at Millbrook landing, and her boots had learned a new language of complaint. Odile's mule track had delivered her to the hills, then abandoned her to hedgerows and drainage cuts and the long wet grass that soaked her trousers to the knee by midmorning every day and never quite dried. The compass rode in her breast pocket against her heartbeat, its needle holding steady on the source the way a held breath holds.

Fen had not caught up to her. That was the thing she kept turning over like a stone in her palm, looking for the crack in it. A man with his resources, his permits, his knowledge of the river's every vein—he should have had her in irons two days ago. Instead she'd found fuel cached at a derelict lock cottage, wrapped in oilcloth with no note. Instead the checkpoint at Tarn Bridge had been, impossibly, unmanned. Either he was playing a longer game than she could see, or—

Or he was keeping two ledgers, and one of them had her name in it.

"Stop woolgathering," she told herself aloud, and started down toward the causeway.

* * *

Hallowford smelled of woodsmoke and eels. She came into the town along the mill leat, past drying nets hung like funeral banners, past children skipping stones off the weir's apron where the water slid through green and glassy. Nobody looked twice at a woman in river clothes with mud to her thighs; half the valley looked like that this time of year. Anonymity, she had learned, was less about hiding than about being unremarkable, and three days of walking had made her excellently unremarkable.

She wanted three things: bread, boot grease, and news. She got the first two from a baker whose forearms were white with flour to the elbow, and the third came free, pressed on her whether she wanted it or not.

"You'll not be crossing tonight," the baker said, wrapping the loaf in paper gone soft with steam. "Riders come through at noon. Corvane livery, six of them, went up the valley road like they owned the stones under it." He said it the way people in the valley said everything about the Corvanes now—flatly, without heat, the way you reported weather or a death. "They asked after a woman traveling alone. Description fit nobody, they said. But they posted a man at each end of the causeway all the same."

Ivo's hands did not shake. She had trained them out of it years ago, on nights when the *Vespera* rocked and men with ledgers stood too close. But something behind her sternum went tight and bright, the feeling she'd begun to recognize as fear sharpening itself into usefulness.

"Which end has the weaker man?" she asked.

The baker looked at her properly then, flour-dusted and tired-eyed, and she watched him decide—watched the small moral machinery turn behind his face—before he said, "The east end. Young fellow. Keeps looking at the water instead of the road." A pause. "Weir's been groaning all day. Old as it is, it doesn't usually talk."

* * *

She bought the boot grease anyway. You took what the day gave you; her father had taught her that, back when he was still a mapmaker and not yet a name carved somewhere she hadn't found. Then she took a room over the eel-smokery because hiding in the hills while riders held the road would mean circling north into country she didn't know, losing days she didn't have. Three days. The Syndicate's clock was already running on the *Vespera*, and every hour she spent being cautious was an hour closer to losing the last thing that had ever been hers.

From the smokery window she watched the causeway as dusk came down. The west-end rider sat his horse like a sack of meal, bored, his lantern lit early. The east-end rider—the young one—had dismounted and stood at the causeway rail looking down at the water exactly as the baker had said, and there was something in the set of his shoulders that Ivo recognized from the inside: a man doing a job his stomach had objections to.

The weir groaned again. Long, low, mineral, like a door in a house nobody lived in.

She was still watching when the second lantern appeared on the west road—not rider-borne but carried in a man's hand, swinging with a walk she knew. Broad-shouldered, unhurried, coat collar up against the river damp. Silas Fen came into Hallowford the way he came into everything: as if the place had been expecting him and ought to be grateful.

Her heart performed a slow, cold revolution. So. Not an ally after all—or not only. He'd let her run easy for three days and now he'd arrived to close the trap personally, because the Corvanes paid better than patience.

Except.

Except the fuel in the oilcloth. Except the empty checkpoint. Except the way he'd stood in her cabin a lifetime ago, in chapter one of this ruin, and looked at her chart of the upper river with an expression she'd filed away as professional interest and had never quite managed to stop thinking about.

She watched him speak to the west-end rider. Watched the rider straighten in his saddle. Watched Fen hand over a folded paper—an order, a warrant, her description in official ink—and then walk out onto the causeway alone, lantern high, and stand there in the middle of it like a man taking the measure of a bridge before deciding whether to burn it.

Then he turned his head, slowly, deliberately, and looked directly at the smokery window.

He couldn't have seen her. The room behind her was dark; the glass would hold nothing but lamplight from the street. But he looked the way a man looks when he knows exactly which window matters, and he raised two fingers off his lantern handle—not a wave, not a salute. Something smaller. A signal with no codebook she knew.

*Wait until full dark,* it said, or she decided it said, because wanting a thing shapes what you see.

* * *

Full dark came down like a lid. No moon; the clouds had been stacking in the northwest all evening, a bruised and towering country of them, and the air had gone thick with the smell of rain that hadn't started yet. The river rose a hand's width against the weir's apron, muttering. In the smokery below, the old smoker worked late, and the smell of alder smoke crept up through the floorboards and made Ivo think, absurdly, of her father curing fish on the *Vespera*'s deck when she was small enough to sit inside a bait barrel.

She went down the outside stair at the hour when even dogs give up arguing. The compass in her pocket. The documents from the cave sealed in oilskin against her spine, flat as a second skin. A knife at her belt and no illusions about using it well.

The causeway was forty yards of exposed stone between the town's edge and the east meadow. She meant to cross it fast and low, past the young rider if he dozed, into the willow scrub beyond, and take the drover's path north along the leat. Simple. The kind of plan that works right up until the world changes its mind.

She was halfway across when the weir screamed.

Not groaned—screamed, a tearing metallic shriek from the gatehouse, and every hair on her arms stood up. She spun. Downstream, at the western gatehouse, a figure moved in the spill of a shuttered lantern: broad-shouldered, coat collar up, both hands on the great iron wheel of the sluice gear, hauling it around with his whole body, his whole history, his whole weight.

Fen.

And the gate began to open.

Water came through the gap the way a verdict comes through a courtroom door. First a tongue, white and hissing, licking across the apron. Then a shoulder of it, waist-high, shoving driftwood and fifty years of silt ahead of it. The river had been backing up behind those closed gates for generations, patient as a creditor, and now it had an account to settle. The whole weir shuddered. Somewhere in the masonry a crack ran with a sound like ice going out in spring.

On the causeway, the young rider's horse reared screaming. Lanterns bloomed in windows across the town. And from the west road came the sound Ivo had been dreading since noon: hooves, many, coming hard.

Six riders. Corvane livery black as spilled ink even in the dark, and at their head a man whose voice carried across the water with the flat authority of someone who had never once been interrupted.

"FEN! Step away from that wheel!"

Fen did not step away. He put his back into the gear another quarter-turn, and the gate ground higher, and the flood meadows began to drink.

Ivo understood it all in one vertiginous instant, the way you understand a chart at a glance: the Corvane riders had planned to hold the causeway and pen her against the weir. But the valley road east ran along the base of the far meadow, and the far meadow was about to be a lake, and the only dry way out of Hallowford was about to be no way at all—for them. For everyone. Unless—

Unless the eastern gates opened too, and the water passed *through* instead of pooling, and the old engineers' design did what it had been built to do a century before the Corvanes taught the river to kneel: spread the flood thin, spare the town, open a channel.

The eastern gatehouse stood thirty feet from her, across the heaving causeway, its wheel green with a century of neglect.

She ran.

The water hit her at the causeway's midpoint like a living thing, thigh-high and furious, trying to take her feet out from under her, carrying a fence post that clipped her hip hard enough to whiten her vision. She grabbed the causeway's mounting block, hauled herself along it hand over hand, breath torn out of her in rags, and the cold was so total it burned. Behind her, hoofbeats on stone. Ahead, the gatehouse ladder, weed-slick, impossible.

She climbed it anyway. Fear sharpened rather than paralyzed—that was the lesson Lock Nine had taught her, and she leaned on it now like a crutch, hands finding holds because they refused to do anything else. At the top, the wheel. Seized. Rusted into its housing like a tooth into a jaw.

She threw everything onto it. Nothing.

Below and behind: "Hold the causeway! Don't let her reach the—" and then the west rider's voice cutting off, because the west meadow had become a sea and the road along it had become a rumor, and the lead rider's horse went to its hocks in water and refused, sensibly, to go further.

Ivo set her boots against the gatehouse frame, gripped the wheel, and pulled with her legs, her back, her grief, her father's name, twenty years of a stolen life—and the rust cracked. A sound like a knuckle popping in the ear of God. Then the wheel turned, grudging, screeching, a hand's width at a time, and the eastern gate lifted, and the river found its second voice.

The two currents met in the middle of the weir pool and the whole structure rang like a struck bell. Water tore through the eastern arch and fanned out across the lower channel in a broad, shallow sheet—exactly as the dead engineers had intended—carving a moving corridor of brown water between the causeway and the far bank. Not deep. Fast. Passable for a woman who had grown up reading water the way other people read letters.

For a moment, in the chaos, in the roar, in the lantern-light staggering across the flooded town, Ivo looked downstream at the western gatehouse.

Fen stood in the wreck of his own lantern, soaked to the bone, and the lead Corvane rider had a pistol leveled at him across thirty feet of churning water, and the rider was shouting something the river ate whole. And Silas Fen—debt collector, double ledger-keeper, the man who had haunted her wake for weeks—looked at the pistol, and then deliberately, unhurriedly, smashed his own lantern against the gatehouse wall and stepped forward into the open, arms slightly wide, letting them see his face. Letting them *know* him.

Whatever he shouted back, she heard only fragments through the flood: "...done collecting... tell Edmun Corvane his accounts are open..." and then, pitched to carry, aimed like a thrown rope across the water at her:

"THE LOWER CHANNEL, ASHGROVE! IT'LL HOLD IF YOU GO NOW!"

A shot cracked. Chipped stone near his head. He didn't flinch—didn't even turn—just stood there being the loudest thing in the valley so that every eye, every horse, every gun stayed on him while she went.

Later, she would understand it completely: he had chosen the moment when capture and defection became the same act, so there could be no walking it back, no quiet arrangement, no returning to the Syndicate with apologies. He had burned his own boat, in the only way a man like him could.

She slid down the ladder's far side into the lower channel.

The current took her like a debt collector takes a debtor—thoroughly, impersonally, without malice. She rode it. Feet up, chin high, the oilskin packet pressed to her spine, the compass a small hard heart against her chest, shooting the brown flood between drowned fence posts and startled eel traps until the channel spat her out into the willow scrub on the far side, gasping, scraped, alive, and free.

Behind her, Hallowford blazed with lanterns and shouting, and the weir sang its broken song, and somewhere in all that noise a man was either dying or running, and she could not go back to learn which without dying herself.

She went north, into the hills, dripping, laughing once—a short, wild, involuntary bark of a laugh at the sheer audacity of the night—and then walking, fast, while the promised rain finally arrived and fell on her like applause.

* * *

She found him at dawn, four miles upstream, sitting on a flat stone above the leat with his boots off and his coat spread to dry, wringing out his socks with the methodical care of a man who had done worse things to his body than get wet.

He looked up. For a long moment neither of them spoke. The river talked instead, loud and liberated below them, running through gates that hadn't opened in a hundred years.

"They'll hang us both," Ivo said finally.

"They'll try." He wrung the sock again. "I've read their warrants. They're very thorough about procedure and very vague about outcomes." He nodded at the stone beside him. She sat. Her body announced every one of the last twelve hours, itemized and invoiced.

"Why?" she asked. It wasn't gratitude talking, or not only. It was the question she'd been carrying since the fuel cache, since the empty checkpoint, since a lantern raised two fingers in the dark. "You had a good position. Quiet work, steady pay, everyone afraid of you. Men don't throw that in the river for a stranger."

Fen looked at the water for a while. When he answered, his voice had none of its collector's smoothness in it; it was just a man's voice, worn at the edges.

"I keep records," he said. "It's the trade I'm actually in. Debts, mostly. But debts leave trails—payments, bribes, settlements—and trails lead places the Corvanes paid me to stop looking." His jaw worked. "My brother kept a ferry at the parish they drowned. Ashmill. Before the dam, before the maps lost it. He wrote me letters for years asking me to look into the compensation rolls, and I told him I didn't mix family with business." A pause like a dropped anchor. "Then the parish went under, and the letters stopped, and the compensation rolls turned out to have been edited. By professionals. People like me."

Ivo said nothing. There was nothing to say that the morning wasn't already saying.

"So when your cave surfaced," Fen went on, "and the Corvanes started spending money like drowning men throwing off ballast—I realized somebody had finally found the master book. And I thought: everything I've collected in twenty years, every scrap they made me gather and file and forget—it all means something if it lands next to yours." He turned and looked at her straight, and his eyes were the flattest, most honest things she'd seen in years. "I'm not your friend, Ashgrove. I'm your corroborating witness. Don't confuse the two."

"Wouldn't dream of it," she said. But she found she was smiling, and after a moment, so—barely, unwillingly—was he.

He reached into his coat and drew out a leather notebook, swollen with water, pages warped but legible, and set it on the stone between them like a card played face-up.

"Two ledgers," he said. "Time we compared them."

Far downstream, faint on the wind, Hallowford's church bell began to ring—not alarm, she thought, not quite. More like a town counting itself awake.
