## 13. Two Ledgers

The fire was small on purpose. Ivo had built it in the lee of a fallen sycamore where the smoke would tangle with the mist coming off the water, and she'd ringed it with wet stones so the light wouldn't leap. Old habits. Ten years of running taught her that firelight carried farther than sound, and sound carried farther than regret.

Fen sat across from her with his coat folded under him and his long legs stretched toward the flames, and he was doing the thing she hated most about him: waiting. He could wait like stone waited. He'd been a debt collector too long to flinch at silence; silence was his native country.

Above them, the hills of Hallowford were black against a sky rinsed clean by the flood. The valley road was still underwater somewhere behind them—she could hear it, a vast slow breathing of displaced river finding its new shape—and somewhere downstream, Corvane riders were picking through the wreckage of their own sluice gates, looking for the man who'd opened them and the woman who'd walked out through the channel he made.

"You should eat," Fen said finally.

"I'm eating."

"You're moving food around your plate." He nodded at the tin where her beans had gone cold in their grease. "I've watched you do it for three days. It's how you think. You push things into patterns."

She looked up at him. Firelight did strange kindnesses to his face, softened the scar over his eyebrow, made the hollows of his cheeks look like something other than hunger. She reminded herself that this man had boarded her boat twice with a ledger full of her debts and a smile full of knives. She also reminded herself that yesterday he had cut open a Corvane sluice gate with his own hands while the water tried to take his arm off, because she asked him to—or maybe not because she asked. Maybe for reasons of his own that she hadn't yet priced.

"Show me," she said.

He didn't pretend not to understand. That was another thing about Silas Fen: he never wasted motion on pretending. He reached inside his coat—the old reflexive flinch rose in her chest anyway, ten years deep—and brought out a book.

It was nothing like the Corvane ledgers from the cave. Those had been beautiful objects, vellum and gilt, bindings stamped with the family's tide-wheel crest, built to be admired before they were believed. This was a cheap composition book, its cover soft as cloth from handling, its spine held together with waxed thread and what looked like fishing line. The corners were rounded with wear. It had lived close to a body for a long time.

"How long?" she asked.

"Eleven years." He set it on his knee but didn't open it yet. "Since my brother."

"Odile told me some of it."

"Odile tells everyone some of it. It's how she tests whether they flinch." He turned the book over in his hands, once, like a man checking a coin for clipping. "The rest is mine to tell or not tell. I'm telling it now because after Hallowford there's no version of me that goes back. You understand that. I burned the bridge with the whole Syndicate watching. Vess will have my name on a slate by morning, and Corvane will have it on a worse one."

"I know."

"So." He opened the book.

* * *

What was inside stopped her breath.

Not beauty—there was no beauty in it—but order. Page after page in a small, ruthless hand: dates, names, sums, places. Drowned Parish, 14 families removed, compensation paid to none, records amended to show voluntary sale. Millwright Uprising, casualties listed as four, actual count gathered from parish registers and grave-diggers' talk: thirty-one. Lock Nine tolls, collected above statute since year such-and-such, remitted upstream, signed initials she recognized from the cave documents. And beside each entry, in the margin, sources—a lockman's drunken confession, a widow's letter kept twenty years, a shipping manifest that contradicted an official report.

"You cross-referenced," she said. Her voice came out strange. "You built corroboration."

"I'm a collector," Fen said. "Debt is just a story people tell about what they owe. I learned early that the story only holds if the numbers hold. So I check numbers." He turned a page. "When they sent me after you, I thought it was routine. A debtor with a fake name and a fast boat. Then I heard the word *cave*, and I started keeping two books."

"Two?"

"This one." He tapped the composition book. "And the one I keep for them. Every claim, every delinquency, every name they wanted frightened. I've been writing both sides of every transaction for eleven years, Captain Ashgrove, because a man who works for monsters either stops seeing them or starts taking notes. I took notes."

She reached across the fire. He hesitated—one heartbeat, two—then passed it to her.

The paper felt warm from his knee. She turned pages slowly, the way she'd turn pages in chart-house archives when she still had a reputation to protect, and what she found made the cave documents change shape in her memory. Alone, the Corvane archive was a family's sins written in a family's hand—deniable, dismissible, the rantings of a dead mapmaker's daughter with a grudge. But here, in a debt collector's cheap notebook, were the same dates arrived at from the other direction. The same drowned parish. The same erased uprising. Independent lines converging on the same truth like tributaries finding the main channel.

"Together," she said slowly, "this isn't evidence anymore. It's a record."

"It's a record," Fen agreed. "That's the word that frightens them. Anyone can call a thing a lie. Nobody knows what to do with a record."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope an owl asked its one question and got no answer. Ivo sat with the book in her lap and felt the weight of it—not the paper weight, the other kind, the kind her father must have felt at the gorge with the source a day's walk away and Corvane riders at his back. The weight of being the only person alive who knew.

Except she wasn't alone anymore. That was the difference. That was everything.

* * *

Silas Fen watched her read his life's work and braced himself the way he always braced: for dismissal, for laughter, for the flat look of a person deciding a thing was worth less than he'd believed.

It didn't come. She read like a pilot reads water—looking for the deep channel, the hidden snag, the place where the surface lied. When she looked up, her eyes had that quality he'd first noticed on her boat, the one that had made him report back to Corvane with careful vagueness for six months: she saw the whole river at once.

"There's a problem," she said.

"Name it."

"The problem isn't them." She closed the book but kept her hand flat on its cover, the way you'd calm a spooked animal. "The problem is that I've been sitting here for an hour thinking about what I could *do* with this."

Fen said nothing. Waiting was his craft.

"Think about it." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved on her face like current. "Every lockmaster on the Vell takes Corvane money. Every magistrate. Every newspaper that prints the weather and the shipping news and nothing else. If I walk into Landfall tomorrow with your book and the cave papers, half the valley will call it forgery and the other half will be too afraid to repeat it. But if I *don't* walk in anywhere—if I keep it—if I hold it—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "A lockmaster who won't take my boat through for free today would take it through for free if he knew I had a page with his signature on it. A magistrate who convicted me on perjured testimony would reopen the file if I owned the letter that proved the perjury. I wouldn't have to threaten anyone. I'd just have to *have* it. Everyone would know I had it. That's enough. That's more than enough."

The words hung in the smoke.

"That's what they do," Fen said quietly.

"I know what it is."

"I want to hear you say it."

She laughed, short and without any humor in it, and threw a twig into the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "Fine. It's what they do. It's exactly what they do. Edmun Corvane doesn't rule the valley with riders—he rules it with a vault. He decides what's true. He decides which floods happened and which famines were weather and which men were thieves. And I sat in that cave holding my father's compass and I thought, *good God, all that power, and it's just paper.* Paper I could carry. Paper I could use." She looked at him across the flames, and her jaw was set the way it got when she was steering into weather she couldn't avoid. "Do you know what nobody tells you about being wronged? They tell you about justice. They don't tell you that the wound makes a shape, and power fits that shape perfectly. Like it was cast for it."

Fen nodded slowly. He had expected this—had, in fact, been waiting for it the way a lockman waits for the spring surge, knowing the river will do what the river does. Eleven years of collecting other people's debts had shown him every way a soul can owe.

"My brother," he said, "died in the Drowned Parish."

"I know."

"No. You know the fact. Let me give you the rest, because I've had eleven years to learn what the rest does to a man." He fed the fire a stick, deliberate, giving his hands work. "Tomas was a lockman's son. Good with numbers, better with boats. When the dam went in, the Corvane agents came through with papers showing the parish had sold voluntarily—all proper, all signed. My mother signed because the alternative was a magistrate who'd already decided. Six weeks later Tomas was dead—fever, they said, in the resettlement camp, though the camp had no doctor and the well water ran brown. And here is the thing, Captain. There is no record my brother ever lived. No census entry after the amendment. No grave marker—the graves were flooded too. For eleven years I have been the only proof my brother existed." He met her eyes. "So believe me when I say I understand the temptation better than you do. I have dreamed about owning the vault. I have drawn maps of it in my sleep. There were years when the only reason I didn't go upriver myself was that I knew what I'd become at the end of that walk."

"And what's that?"

"A smaller Edmun Corvane." He said it plainly, without drama, the way you'd state a draft depth off a chart. "A man with a monopoly on memory. It starts as justice—I'd only correct the lies, I'd only right my own wrongs. But a monopoly doesn't stay polite. The day comes when someone wrongs *you*, and you reach for the vault instead of the law, and the day after that someone merely inconveniences you, and the vault is right there, and it opens so easily, and every time you use it the world reshapes itself around your preference until you can't remember what anything cost." He gestured at the dark hills, the invisible drowned road, the whole wounded valley. "They didn't start as villains. I've read enough of their oldest papers to know they started as people who'd been hurt and decided hurt gave them title."

Ivo was quiet a long time. The river breathed below them. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, rougher, honest the way things get honest at two in the morning near water.

"When they took my boat," she said, "I lay awake on Odile's floor doing arithmetic. Not escape arithmetic. *Power* arithmetic. Three days, they said. Three days to pay or lose the *Vesper*. And I caught myself thinking: if I had the master ledger, I wouldn't need three days. I wouldn't need money at all. I'd send one page to the Harrow Syndicate and one page to the lockmasters and one page to the magistrate who convicted me, and the whole river would reorganize itself around my convenience, and I'd tell myself it was justice because most of it would *be* justice—that's the trap, Fen, that's the beautiful rotten heart of it. Most of it would be justice. You could feed the valley truth for a decade and call yourself a servant of the light, and every single page would serve you first."

"Yes."

"And then what am I?" She spread her hands. "A captain with a vault. A queen of paper. My father died rather than touch it—no. That's not true. He died reaching for it, and I'll never know if he meant to burn it or use it, and I have spent every mile of this river telling myself he meant to burn it because I need him to have been better than I am tonight."

"He was better than you are tonight," Fen said. "So am I. So is everyone, at two in the morning, before they decide." He leaned forward. "But listen to me, because I've collected debts from better people than either of us. The temptation doesn't go away. It waits at the source with the ledger. It'll be standing next to you when you open the cover. What matters is what you build *before* you're strong enough to be corrupted—because if you build it alone, you'll own it, and if you own it, it owns you. But if you build it so it belongs to everyone—"

"—then no one can steer it," she finished.

"Then no one can steer it. Including you. Especially you."

Ivo looked down at the composition book in her lap, at eleven years of a dead man's brother keeping faith in cheap ink, and something in her chest unclenched—not all the way, not cleanly, but enough. Enough to breathe past.

"Then we don't take it to the magistrates," she said. "We don't take it to the newspapers—they're bought. We don't take it to the Syndicate, we don't trade it, we don't bargain with it." She was talking faster now, the pilot's cadence, reading the channel as it opened. "We put it in the water. The mail steamer runs the whole river twice a week—every landing, every lock, every town from the source to the sea. If copies come down with the current, on the current, arriving everywhere at once, there's no one left to bribe. You can't buy back a thing that's already in ten thousand hands. You can't edit a memory the whole valley shares."

Fen stared at her. Then, slowly, he began to laugh—a dry, rusted sound, like a gate opening after years shut.

"The river delivers," he said. "Of course. The one courier in the valley Corvane doesn't own."

"They own the locks. They own the landings. They don't own the water." She stood, and the firelight fell away from her, and for a moment she was all silhouette against the stars and the black moving shine of the Vell—just a shape at the edge of the light, the way she'd lived for ten years, except now the invisibility was a choice and the choice had a purpose. "Sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we make for the headwaters. Odile's mule track meets the old survey road above the gorge, and if the wardens patrol the way she says they do, we'll want daylight hours and dark nerves."

"And the temptation?"

Ivo paused at the edge of the firelight. When she answered, she didn't turn around.

"It's coming with us," she said. "It sits in the vault with the ledger. Which is exactly why I can't be the one who decides alone. Neither can you. When we open that cover, Fen, there has to be someone standing behind us ready to say *no*—someone who owes us nothing." 

She looked back over her shoulder, and the ghost of something that was almost a smile crossed her face.

"Luckily, we know a woman in the hills who's spent forty years perfecting the word."

* * *

Two ridges east, where the mule track bent around a shoulder of granite, Odile Vasseur sat with her back against cold stone and counted the distant fireglow through the trees, and waited for morning, and sharpened the answer she had been carrying for forty years.
