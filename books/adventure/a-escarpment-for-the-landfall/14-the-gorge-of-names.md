## 14. The Gorge of Names

They came down to the gorge in the last grey hour before dawn, when the mist lay on the river like a sheet drawn over a body.

Ivo had walked for three days now—three days of mule track and scree and Odile's silence—and her feet had stopped complaining somewhere past Hallowford and settled into a dull, obedient ache she could almost ignore. The river had been their companion the whole way, sometimes beside them, sometimes below them, sometimes only a sound, a breathing in the dark under the land. But here it changed its voice.

Here it roared.

The track bent around a shoulder of rock and the trees fell away all at once, and there was the gorge: a wound in the earth a half-mile deep, walls of black limestone going up and up into the fog, and at the bottom the Vell compressed itself from a broad brown river into a single furious ribbon of white. The noise came up out of it like something alive. It got into Ivo's chest. It got into her teeth. She stood at the rim with her hands on her pack straps and felt the sound pass through her the way a bell passes through a struck hammer.

"Vell's Gorge," Odile said behind her. The old woman's voice was flat as slate; it always was, but here it seemed flatter still, pressed thin by the roar. "Your father called it that too. Not the name on any map. His own."

Ivo didn't turn around. She was looking at the far wall, where the fog was lifting off the stone in slow rags, because she had seen something there—or thought she had. Pale marks. Rows of them. Like stitches in the cliff face.

"What are those?" she asked.

Odile came up beside her. Fen hung back, as he always did, a few paces to the rear, his hand resting easy near the knife at his belt—not threatening, just present, the way he'd been present since the sluice gates at Hallowford, since the moment he'd chosen which ledger to keep. He looked up at the wall too, and Ivo saw his jaw tighten.

"Names," Odile said.

* * *

There was no bridge. There hadn't been one since before Ivo was born, if Odile was to be believed, and Odile was believed about very little else these days, so Ivo believed this too. Instead there was a path—a ledge cut into the near wall of the gorge, no wider than a coffin laid sideways, descending in switchbacks toward a shelf halfway down where the names were. The Millwrights had cut it, Odile said, in the years after the uprising, when the gorge was still a place people came to grieve rather than a place they'd been forbidden to go. Corvane riders had sealed the upper trail decades ago. They had never found the lower one. The river kept its secrets better than any conspirator.

The descent took two hours. Ivo did not look down more than twice, and both times regretted it. The rock under her boots was slick with spray; the air grew colder as they went down, and wetter, until every surface wept and her hair hung in damp ropes against her neck. Fen went first, testing each foothold with a patience that surprised her—he moved like a man who had done hard climbing before, in some life he never spoke of—and Odile came last with the mule, which refused the first switchback and the fourth and the ninth, and each time stood trembling against the wall while Odile murmured to it in a language Ivo didn't know until it consented to live a little longer.

Halfway down, the ledge opened onto the shelf.

It was wider than she expected—a natural terrace perhaps forty paces across, sheltered by an overhang so that the spray fell short of it and the stone stayed dry. And the wall above it, rising sheer for a hundred feet, was covered in names.

Not painted. Carved. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, chiseled into the limestone in every size and style of hand—some deep and confident, some shallow as scratches, some clearly the work of children, letters wobbling like drunkards. They climbed the wall in columns and spirals and great ragged blocks, overlapping, crowding, older ones half-erased by newer ones cut over them, so that the whole face of the cliff looked like water frozen mid-boil. Moss filled some of the letters. Lichen had taken others, staining them orange and green. Near the top, unreachable, someone had carved a line of text Ivo couldn't read from below, but she could guess at it, and the guessing closed her throat.

She walked out onto the shelf and the roar of the river seemed suddenly distant, held off by the overhang, and in that strange acoustic pocket she could hear the wind moving through the letters, a low hooting over hollows in the stone, like breath drawn across the mouths of bottles.

"How many?" she asked. Her voice sounded small.

"No one counted," Odile said. "Counting would have meant stopping." She lowered herself onto a flat stone with the groan of an old hinge. "After the uprising they came through the valley with lists. Every millwright, every sympathizer, every man who'd sold them flour or hidden them a night. Some were taken. Some were drowned in the dam cuts. Most simply vanished—house burned, family scattered, name struck from the parish rolls. The Corvanes didn't kill the record, you understand. That came later, when they learned better. In those years they killed the *people*, and left the record standing, thinking grief would keep quiet." Her mouth twisted. "Grief doesn't keep quiet. Grief climbs down into gorges with hammers and chisels."

Fen had stopped at the edge of the shelf. He wasn't looking at the wall. He was looking at the floor of it—at the stone underfoot—and Ivo followed his gaze and saw that the shelf itself was carved too, names worn nearly smooth by generations of boots, so that they walked on the disappeared without being able to help it.

"My brother's is here," Fen said quietly.

Ivo turned. He hadn't spoken of his brother since the fire at Hallowford, since the night they'd compared ledgers and she'd seen, for the first time, what he carried under all that careful bookkeeping: a boy from the drowned parish, a flood that official maps said had never happened, a debt collector who had spent twenty years collecting other people's debts because his own could never be paid.

"You've been here before?"

"Once. When I was young. Before I took the Syndicate's coin." He crouched, brushing grit from the floor with two fingers, gentle as a man touching a sleeping child. "I couldn't find it then either. There are too many." He straightened. "That's the point of it, isn't it. You come looking for one name and you find ten thousand, and your grief gets lost among them, and then it isn't yours alone anymore. It belongs to the wall."

Ivo turned back to the names.

She hadn't let herself think it yet—the thought had been building in her chest since the drowned parish, since the old woman with the river-mad eyes had gripped her wrist and said *the mapmaker's daughter*—but now, standing under ten thousand carved dead, she let it rise. Her father had come here. Her father had stood on this shelf. And if the stories were true—if Odile's guilt and the cave documents and the compass all pointed the same direction—he had come here on his way to die.

She began to walk the wall.

* * *

She searched for four hours.

The light came slowly, filtering down through the fog in pale shafts that crawled along the cliff face, illuminating one column of names and then another, so that the wall seemed to reveal itself by choice, section by section, like a face turning toward her. She read as she went. She couldn't help reading. *Marta Voss. Aldous Crane. The three Pell brothers, carved side by side by a hand that shook. A name that was only a first name, Edda, with nothing after it, as if whoever cut it had run out of time or knowledge or hope.* Some entries had dates. Some had small carved marks beside them—a star, a bird, a key—that must have meant something once, a code of grief shared among the carvers. One name had been carved and then deliberately defaced, gouged over so violently the stone had split, and Ivo wondered what story lived in that violence, and decided she did not want to know.

Her legs ached. Her eyes ached. The letters swam.

"Ivo." Odile's voice, from behind. "Sit. Eat. The wall has waited forty years for you; it can wait another hour."

But Ivo couldn't sit. There was a pressure in her, a current, the same current that ran the compass needle upstream, and it would not let her rest while the name might be anywhere on this wall, in any column, in any century of grief.

She found it in the sixth hour, in a column near the shelf's eastern edge, where the overhang shadowed the stone and the carving was fresher—fresher than everything around it, cut within the last twenty-five years, within the span of her own life.

**ASHGROVE**

Two words beneath it, in a hand she knew.

She knew it the way you know a voice through a wall. The strong downstrokes. The little hook he put on the R, the same hook she'd inherited without ever knowing where she'd gotten it, the same hook that appeared on the shipping manifests she'd forged and the false names she'd signed in tavern registers across three hundred miles of river. Twenty years of running, and her own handwriting had been carrying him the whole time.

Beneath the name, in letters smaller, deeper, cut with a blade rather than a chisel—hurried, she thought, hurried the way a man works when he knows the riders are coming:

**THE SOURCE REMEMBERS EVERYTHING.**

Ivo put her fingers in the grooves of the letters.

The stone was cold. That was all. Cold stone, cold air, the river roaring somewhere below like the sea at the end of the world. No warmth lingered in the carving; twenty years is long enough to cool anything. But her fingers stayed there anyway, tracing the A, the S, the hooked R, following the path his blade had taken, and she understood—with a clarity that arrived not as thought but as fact, the way you understand falling—that this was the closest she would ever come to touching him again.

She had been nine when he went upriver. She remembered the morning of it only in pieces: the compass pressed into her hands, too big for a child's palm; his beard rough against her forehead; the smell of tar and river water on his coat. He'd told her he was going to settle a debt. He'd told her to mind the boat. He'd smiled, and the smile had been wrong, and she had known even at nine that it was wrong, and she had let him go anyway because she was nine and he was her father and fathers came back.

He had come back as a name on a wanted notice. *Thief. Forger. Corvane property absconded with.* She had spent her whole adult life believing the notice, or pretending to believe it, because believing it was easier than believing the alternative—that he had died for something, and that the something had swallowed him whole, and that she might be next.

Now she stood under his name, in a gorge full of names, and the wanted notice burned away like fog.

"He wasn't a thief," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The acoustic pocket caught her voice and gave it back to her strangely, doubled, as if the wall were answering. "He was a courier. He was carrying the truth downstream and they caught him doing it."

"Yes," Odile said.

Ivo turned. The old woman had risen from her stone and stood at the edge of the shelf, leaning on her stick, looking up at the ASHGROVE column with an expression Ivo had never seen on her face—something rawer than guilt, older than doctrine.

"You knew he carved it."

"I carried him here." Odile's voice was gravel. "From the hills, after the ambush at the ford. He was shot through the leg and losing blood, and he made me promise—" She stopped. Started again. "He made me promise that if he didn't reach the source, someone would. Someone with the compass. I sat on this shelf with him for two days while the fever took him, and he talked about you. About the boat. About how you'd steer her through a lock gap sideways if the mood took you." A dry, terrible almost-laugh. "He was proud of you. He was proud of the wrong things and the right things, all of them, everything."

"And then you buried the promise," Ivo said. "For twenty years. You built a doctrine out of it. *Never touch the source again.*"

"I built a grave out of it," Odile said. "There's a difference, girl, and you'll learn it someday, though I pray you learn it cheaper than I did." She came closer, stick tapping the carved floor, tapping on the worn-smooth names of the dead. "I told myself the archive was poison. That anyone who drank from it became a Corvane. Maybe that was true. Or maybe I just couldn't bear to send another Ashgrove up that river to die for a piece of paper." Her eyes, wet and fierce, fixed on Ivo's. "So tell me why I'm wrong. Tell me why this time is different. Because I am too old to bury another one."

Ivo looked back at the wall.

Ten thousand names. Her father's among them, small and stubborn, holding its little sentence like a lantern. *The source remembers everything.* Not a warning. She saw that now. Not a curse, not a Corvane threat scrawled by a dying man. An instruction. A bequest. The whole estate of a murdered mapmaker, left to his daughter in two lines of scratched stone: *they cannot erase what everyone carries.*

"They're editing us," Ivo said slowly. "Right now, today, in that station at the headwaters, there are clerks deciding which floods happened and which didn't. Which famines get recorded and which get filed away until the last witness dies. My whole life I thought power was the gunboats and the lock tolls and the Syndicate's interest rates. But that's just the muscle. The muscle doesn't matter." She touched the carved A one more time, then took her hand away. "The memory matters. Whoever keeps the ledger owns the valley. And right now the ledger belongs to them."

"So you'll take it," Odile said. "And become them."

"No." The word came out of her with the force of the river below, and she heard it and trusted it, the way she trusted a channel she'd steered a hundred times. "You don't take a spring and hide it in your house, Odile. You break the dam. Everyone drinks, or no one does." She looked at Fen, who had come to stand at her shoulder, silent, his ledger-heavy eyes moving between the two women. "We don't destroy it. We don't keep it. We give it away. All of it. To every landing on this river at once, faster than they can burn it, in more hands than they can buy."

Fen exhaled slowly through his nose. "The mail steamer," he said. "Corvane's own packet boat runs the whole valley twice a week. Locks, landings, every town from Landfall to the headwaters." Something kindled behind his careful face—the first thing Ivo had ever seen him look hungry for. "If copies went out with the mail..."

"Their own river," Ivo said. "Their own boat. Their own delivery schedule." She turned back to the wall one last time, to the hooked R, to the sentence underneath. "He started this. I'm just finishing the route."

Odile was quiet a long time. The wind hooted through the ten thousand mouths of the dead. Then the old woman shouldered her stick, and her spine, for the first time in three days, straightened fully.

"The source station changes its watch at dusk," she said. "There's a supply road on the west bank. Carts go up twice a week, and the guards never search a cart driven by someone they know." She looked at Ivo, and the look was an apology twenty years late, and a surrender, and a blessing, all three. "Get some sleep, mapmaker's daughter. Tomorrow we make you a clerk."

Above them, far up the black wall where no climber could reach, the fog thinned for a moment around the unreadable carved line at the top of the gorge, and Ivo—who would wonder about it for the rest of her life—did not see it.

Below, the river ran on toward the source, carrying everything.
