## 15. Wardens of the Source

The river did not begin the way rivers are supposed to begin.

Ivo had spent her whole life believing that a river's source would be a spring—a modest, holy seep of water rising out of moss and stone, the kind of thing you could cup in your two hands. She had imagined kneeling at it. She had imagined her father kneeling there before her, twenty years gone, drinking from the first clean water.

What she found instead was a wall.

It rose out of the headwater valley like something quarried rather than built, grey granite dressed in white render, three storeys tall and longer than the lock-house at Nine, with a slate roof pitched steep against the mountain snows. Behind it the Vell emerged from a limestone throat in the hillside—she could see the actual mouth of the river, a black arch maybe forty feet across, gushing cold and glass-clear into a stone basin—and the station had been set directly across it, astride the birth of the river, the way a man might stand in a doorway.

*Warden Station One*, said no sign at all, because there were no signs. The building announced itself by existing. Everything else in the valley leaned away from it: the pines stood back a respectful hundred yards, the mule track bent around its grounds rather than through them, even the birds—Ivo noticed it with a creeping unease—seemed to fly around the roof rather than over it.

"Supply wagons go to the east gate," Odile had told her on the last night of the mule track, crouched over a charcoal sketch of the station drawn from memory thirty years stale. "Twice a week. Flour, lamp oil, paper." She had looked up at that, and her eyes had been flat as slate. "More paper than food, if you can believe it. They eat less than they write."

Now, standing in the treeline with a borrowed clerk's coat buttoned to her throat, Ivo believed it.

The coat had come from Odile's stores—a dead man's coat, she suspected, though Odile hadn't said so and Ivo hadn't asked. It was good wool, dyed the undyed-looking brown of government issue, with brass buttons worn smooth by someone else's thumbs. Under it she wore her own clothes and, sewn flat against her ribs inside a linen band, the documents that mattered: three pages of the drowned parish's true survey, the Millwright Uprising petition with its Corvane seal, and the compass.

The compass was the problem. She could not leave it behind—it was the one object in the world that pointed where she needed to go, and besides, leaving it felt like leaving her father twice. But it was brass and heavy and unmistakably not a clerk's possession. In the end she had wrapped it in oilcloth and buried it in the flour sack she was carrying, down among the meal where a casual inspection would find nothing but bread's future.

"You don't have to do this part," Fen had said, the night before, in the low voice he used when he was trying not to argue. "Odile's people could get a man inside."

"They'd get a man caught," Ivo said. "No one's looking for a woman clerk. That's the whole shape of my life, Silas. I'm invisible. Let me spend it for once."

He hadn't liked it. He had stood at the edge of the firelight with his ledger under his arm like a breviary and watched her with an expression she couldn't name—not worry exactly, something older and more resigned—and finally he'd said, "When you're inside, look at what they throw away. Archives tell you more by their rubbish than their shelves."

She thought about that now as she joined the tail of the supply train winding up the valley road: four mule carts, six drivers, and herself, walking beside the third cart with her flour sack over one shoulder and a bill of lading copied out in Odile's careful hand. The other drivers were hill farmers who took Corvane coin twice a week and never met each other's eyes on the road. That suited her fine. Silence was a currency here, and everyone was rich in it.

The east gate was iron-banded oak in a granite arch, and it opened before they reached it—opened smoothly, silently, on well-oiled hinges, which frightened her more than a challenge would have. A guard stood either side, in grey coats with no insignia, carrying carbines slung muzzle-down. Neither looked at the carts. One of them looked at *her*, briefly, the way a man looks at weather, and then away.

Inside the walls, the first thing that struck her was the quiet. Not silence—the station hummed and clattered like any working place, cartwheels on stone, a bell somewhere marking the hour—but a *curated* quiet, a quiet that had been arranged. No dogs. No children. No music. The buildings were laid out in clean ranks around a central yard, and every window faced inward toward a flagpole flying a plain white banner, and the whole geometry of the place said: *we are watching each other.*

A receiving clerk met them at the granary steps—a young man with ink-stained fingers and the pale, unlined face of someone who worked indoors forever—and checked the bills of lading against his own list without once looking up.

"Flour to stores. Oil to the lamproom. Paper—" he weighed the ream-bundles in his hands, frowning faintly, "—paper goes direct to Registry. Don't take it to stores. Registry is the long building, north range, green door. Ask for Second Warden Hesk."

"I'm new," Ivo said, in the flat accent of the lower river, apologetic, slightly stupid. "First run. My brother usually drives."

"Mm." He stamped the lading without reading it. "Registry takes everything straight now. New directive since spring. Nothing sits overnight." And he waved her on, already turning to the next cart, and Ivo walked north across the yard with a hundredweight of paper on her shoulder and her heart going like a paddle wheel.

*Nothing sits overnight.* As if the paper were perishable. As if words spoiled.

* * *

The green door was unlocked. Inside, Registry was warm, lamplit, and smelled of glue and hot wax and something faintly sweet she couldn't place until she saw the sealing stove in the corner—a small iron furnace with a rack above it, and hanging from the rack, dozens of paper packets sealed in dripping wax, cooling in rows.

Second Warden Hesk turned out to be a woman of about sixty with spectacles on a chain and the manner of someone perpetually interrupted. She glanced at Ivo, at the paper, at the flour dust on Ivo's sleeve, and sighed.

"You're the new driver's girl. Put it on the trolley. Wash your hands first—there's a basin. Flour in the folios brings mice, and mice bring—" she made a vague gesture that seemed to encompass catastrophe, "—*losses*."

Ivo washed her hands. The water came from the river itself, she realized; there was a channel cut through the floor, lined with glazed tile, and the headwater ran through the building beneath their feet, cold enough that the room felt chilled from below. The station drank from the source. Of course it did. It sat on the throat of the Vell like a cork.

While she stacked reams onto the trolley, Ivo let her eyes work the way Fen had taught her without ever teaching her—casually, in glances, cataloguing. Registry was one long room, and along one wall ran shelving of the kind she knew from the cave at Landfall Point: document boxes, ledger spines, bundles tied with tape. But the far wall was different. The far wall held *empty* boxes. Hundreds of them, labelled and dated, years ahead—boxes waiting to be filled, each with a typed slip pasted inside the lid describing what would go there.

She drifted closer, trolley squeaking, and read the slips.

*1849 — Harvest Returns, Upper Valley Parishes.* A box for records not yet created, dated decades back? No—she looked again. The dates were recent and future both. *Spring Quarter — Lock Incidents, Nine Through Twelve.* *Autumn — Dam Maintenance Reports, Hallowford Cut.* And then, on a shelf by itself, apart, a single box whose slip read only:

*Hallowford — Correction File.*

Correction. Not record. Not report. *Correction.* Her skin went cold. Somewhere in this building was a file whose entire purpose was to decide what the drowning of a parish would be *called*.

"The trolley doesn't go there," Hesk said, without looking up from her desk. She hadn't raised her voice. She didn't need to. "That wall is archivists' access only. Drivers' girl, flour, trolley, north door. Am I understood?"

"Yes, ma'am. Sorry, ma'am."

But as she wheeled the trolley past, Ivo saw what lay open on Hesk's own desk, and it stopped her breath for a full stride.

It was a ledger page, fresh-inked, still smelling of the pen. A table of entries. Each line had three columns: *Event. Disposition. Authority.* And the events—

*Fever, Millbrook Landing (eleven dead). Disposition: entered, cause amended to 'bad well-water.'*

*Lock riot, Nine (two dead). Disposition: omitted.*

*Hallowford inundation. Disposition: entered as 'engineering failure, no negligence found.'*

And beside each entry, in the third column, a signature or a stamp. *Authority.* Someone had signed off on what the fever meant. Someone had decided the riot had never happened. It wasn't censorship as Ivo had always imagined it—men with torches burning books in a square. It was bookkeeping. Quiet, orderly, thorough. The horror of it was the horror of a tidy desk. These people didn't destroy the truth. They *edited* it, the way a careful editor trims a manuscript, and what remained read smoothly and was mostly lies.

They weren't hiding the flood. They were deciding what the flood would say about itself.

"Driver's girl." Hesk again, sharper now. "North door."

Ivo went. At the door she paused, as if confused, and asked the question she'd carried up the whole mountain: "Ma'am—who decides all this? The dispositions?"

Hesk looked at her over the spectacles for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—not suspicion. Weariness. The weariness of a woman who had spent forty years trimming manuscripts and knew exactly what she was.

"The Archivist decides," she said. "Everything goes to the Archivist. Now go."

* * *

The rest of that day, Ivo delivered supplies.

It was the best cover she could have asked for and she hated how well it fit her. A clerk with a trolley was furniture; she passed through corridors, stairwells, a mess hall, a map room, and was seen by perhaps forty people and registered by none of them. She learned the station's rhythm the way she'd learned the Vell's currents—by feel, by repetition. The bell rang the hours. The guards changed at six and midnight. The archivists kept hours like monks: up before dawn, at their desks by lamplight, silent at meals.

And everywhere, the editing.

In the map room she watched a draughtsman redraw the valley above Hallowford, carefully omitting the old parish roads, smoothing them into blank green countryside, while beside him another man transcribed the new map onto fresh plates. The old plates went into a crate marked for the furnace. In the mess hall she heard two junior wardens arguing softly about whether a miller's death at Twelve should be recorded as accident or self-harm, with the bored tone of men debating a rule of grammar. In a corridor she passed an open door and saw, framed on the wall, a portrait of Edmun Corvane at perhaps fifty—silver-haired, kind-eyed, the benevolent father of the valley—and beneath it a plaque: *HE KEEPS THE RECORD TRUE.*

She wanted to laugh and vomit at once. *True.* The word sat there like a knife left in a loaf of bread.

By late afternoon she had found what she needed: the stairs. Registry's north end held a private stair behind a door of black oak, guarded by a single sentry, descending—descending, not ascending—which meant the vault was *below*, sunk into the limestone beside the river's throat, deeper than the foundations, down where the water's sound would come up through the rock like a pulse. The Archivist was down there. The master ledger was down there. Her father had died trying to reach a room she was now mopping floors above.

At dusk she took her supper standing in the yard, hard bread and cheese from the drivers' ration, and watched the lamplighters work the windows, and let herself feel, for one controlled minute, the enormity of what she'd understood today.

She had come up the river thinking the Corvanes ruled by force—by enforcers like Fen, by bought locks, by riders at Hallowford. Force was real; she'd bled from it. But force was only the hand. This place was the *memory*. Every flood they'd caused became an engineering note. Every massacre became bad well-water. Every family erased became a blank box on a shelf, waiting patiently to stay empty forever. You couldn't fight a sword. But you also couldn't fight a *pen*—not with a sword. The valley had lost every fight for a century because the Corvanes wrote the histories afterward, and the histories always won.

Her father had understood that. That's why he'd come here. Not for revenge—for the *record*. For the original ledger, the one written before the editors got hold of it, the one with the truth still bleeding on the page.

And somewhere below her feet, behind black oak and one bored sentry, an Archivist she'd never seen was keeping it.

The bell rang eight. The lamps in the archivists' wing began going out, one window at a time, until only the north range stayed lit—that long room above the vault where Hesk trimmed the world to fit. Ivo finished her bread, brushed the crumbs from the dead man's coat, and picked up her empty trolley.

One more delivery tonight, she told herself. Paper to Registry. Driver's girl, flour, north door.

Except this time she wouldn't stop at the north door. This time, when the bell rang midnight and the sentry at the black oak changed shift and yawned through the long minute between one pair of eyes and the next, she would be standing in the dark of the stairwell with her hand on the rail, listening to the river breathe beneath the stone, one flight above the room where everything her family had bled for waited in the dark.

She wheeled the trolley toward the light, and the water under the floor sang its one cold note, like a voice clearing its throat before it spoke.
