## 16. The Reading Room

The corridor narrowed as it descended, and the air changed the way air changes when a river runs beneath stone—Ivo felt it against her teeth first, a cold mineral breath, then in her ears, a pressure like diving. The supply clerk's gray smock hung loose on her shoulders. She had stolen it off a hook by the loading bay along with a hand truck stacked with paper crates, and for two hours she had pushed that hand truck through the bowels of the Wardens' station, nodding to guards who looked through her the way people look through weather.

Nobody looks at a clerk. That was the whole architecture of this place, she was beginning to understand. The Corvanes had built their fortress not on walls but on tedium—on the assumption that no one would ever want to walk into the most boring building on the river.

The hand truck's wheel squeaked at every third revolution. She had counted. Two hundred and forty corridors of gray stone, and the squeak keeping time like a metronome for her heartbeat.

The last door was iron, and it was unlocked.

That stopped her. Every other door on this level had been locked, warded, chained, or watched. This one stood ajar with the casual confidence of a thing that has never needed defending. Above the lintel, in letters so old they had gone green with verdigris, a single word: *READING*.

Ivo left the hand truck in the corridor. She took the compass from her breast pocket and held it low against her thigh, its needle trembling toward whatever lay beyond, and she went through.

* * *

The room was larger than the chapel at Hallowford and quieter than the bottom of the Vell in winter. Shelves rose from floor to vaulted ceiling in ranks, thousands of them, each crammed with ledgers bound in leather the color of dried blood, spines cracked, gilt flaking. Lamps burned in brass sconces without flickering—the air was too still to stir them. A long reading table ran down the center under a skylight of thick glass, and rain moved across it in slow silver sheets, though Ivo could hear no rain. The sound arrived somewhere else, in some other part of the station, filtered through so much stone it became a rumor.

And there, at the far end of the table, sat an old woman.

Ivo's hand went to the knife at her belt before her mind caught up. The woman did not look up. She was writing—a fountain pen moving across a ledger page in small, patient strokes—and beside her elbow sat a cup of tea gone cold enough to grow a skin, and a saucer of biscuits untouched long enough to go soft.

"You're late," the old woman said.

The word landed strangely. Not *stop*, not *who goes there*. Late—as if Ivo were expected, as if there had been an appointment made decades ago and everyone had simply agreed to wait.

"Late for what?" Ivo kept her voice flat, the voice she used at toll gates.

"For the work." The pen finished its stroke. The woman set it down with care, aligning it parallel to the ledger's edge, and only then raised her head. Her eyes were milk-pale, clouded with cataract, but they fixed on Ivo with terrible precision, the way a blind man's face turns toward warmth. "Sit down, Ashgrove. Your tea is also cold. I'm afraid everything here is cold. It's how we keep things."

"How do you know my name?"

"The same way you know yours." The old woman gestured at the shelves with a hand mapped in liver spots and old ink stains. "It's written down. Everything is written down. That is rather the point of the room."

Ivo did not sit. She moved instead along the wall, keeping the table between them, and let her eyes do what they had learned to do in the cave at Landfall Point: read. The nearest shelf held ledgers whose spines carried dates going back before her grandmother's grandmother. Some were swollen with damp, some scorched at one corner, one wrapped in oilcloth and tied with wire as if it had tried to escape. There were drawers beneath the shelves, hundreds of them, labeled in a clerk's careful hand: *FLOOD, UPPER VELL, 1871 — SUPPRESSED*. *FAMINE, MILLWRIGHT PARISH — RECLASSIFIED AS BLIGHT*. *DISPLACEMENTS, DAM PROJECT — SEE: NOTHING TO SEE*.

Her throat tightened. Here it was again, the vertigo from the cave, but deeper now, colder—the sense of standing inside a machine built to unmake the world and remake it smaller.

"You're the Archivist," she said.

"I am an archivist. There have been many." The old woman smiled, and it transformed her face into something almost kind. "Forty-one years, in my case. I came here young, believing I would curate history. I discovered I would be its jailer instead. Or perhaps its smuggler. One decides rather slowly, over decades, which of those one is."

"Smuggler?"

"Come and see." The woman rose—slowly, one hand braced on the table, and Ivo saw now how thin she was, how the smock hung off her like sailcloth off a spar. Her breathing had a wet rattle at the bottom of it, the sound Ivo knew from river men who'd spent forty years in coal smoke. Whatever was killing her was well along. "Mind the step. And don't touch the master ledger until I tell you. It bites."

* * *

Behind the last rank of shelves, hidden where the skylight's light failed, stood a second room within the room—a cage of iron mesh, and inside the cage, a furnace.

It was cold now, swept clean, its grate black and innocent. But Ivo knew furnaces. She knew what a grate that size could consume in a night. Beside it, on a steel cart, lay a ledger unlike the others: massive, chained shut, its cover stamped with the Corvane seal—the tower above the water, the same sigil that had been pressed into wax on the cave documents, into the lock plates at Lock Nine, into the minds of everyone who had ever decided what the valley was allowed to remember.

"This," the Archivist said, resting her palm flat on the chained cover, "is the master ledger. The original. Every crime the family committed, entered in the family's own hand, because Edmun Corvane's grandfather believed—correctly—that a record no one can find is more valuable than no record at all. You cannot be blackmailed by a rumor. But a proof, held close, held secret—" she tapped the chain, "—that is a scepter."

"And the furnace?"

"Is for the day the family decides even they shouldn't hold it." The old woman's voice did not waver, but her hand did, just slightly, a tremor of age or something older than age. "Every Archivist swears the oath: if the station falls, burn the ledger before it changes hands. Forty-one years I have kept that oath coiled in me like a snake. And for forty-one years I have done this instead."

She turned back into the reading room, and Ivo followed, and the Archivist began pulling ledgers from the shelves—not the blood-bound originals but cheap copies, schoolroom copies, pages typed and hand-copied in dozens of different scripts, some recent, some yellowed with decades. She stacked them on the reading table with the decisiveness of a woman who had rehearsed this moment so many times it had worn a groove in her.

"Copies," she said. "Made in secret, over forty years, by every Archivist since the second one. We were meant to be the eraser. So we erased nothing. We copied everything. Each of us added our own hand to the stack and passed the burden to the next. Do you know how a lie survives, Ashgrove? Not by strength. By maintenance. Someone must sweep out the truth every morning, or it grows back like grass through a floor. For four generations, the Corvanes paid us to sweep. And every night, in this room, we planted it again."

Ivo stared at the stack. Hundreds of pages. Floods. Famines. The drowned parish she had slept in, listed by name, street by street, the number of the displaced entered in a neat column that ended with a total and, beneath the total, in a different ink, a single marginal note in an old clerk's hand: *God forgive the arithmetic.*

"Why?" Ivo's voice came out rougher than she intended. "Why keep it? Why not just burn it and be free of them?"

"Because burning it frees no one but them." The Archivist lowered herself into her chair, breathing hard from the small journey, and studied Ivo with those milk-pale eyes. "A destroyed truth becomes a legend, and legends can be managed. But a *read* truth—" she laid her hand flat on the stack, "—a truth read aloud in a market square by a hundred mouths at once cannot be managed by anything on this earth. I have known that for forty years. What I have not known is who would carry it out. An Archivist cannot. I am the machine, child. The machine cannot testify against itself."

"Then who?"

"Someone with the compass," the old woman said, "and the grudge."

The compass. Ivo's hand closed around it in her pocket before she could stop the motion, and the Archivist smiled—saw, of course she saw, blind as she was.

"My predecessor described it to me, and hers to her. A brass compass, Corvane-made, that points not north but to this place. Given, generations ago, to a river family the Corvanes wronged so thoroughly they thought they had erased them entirely. They gave the man a compass as payment for his silence. They did not know he was a mapmaker. They did not know a mapmaker understands that a compass is a promise of direction, and direction is a form of debt." The old woman leaned forward. "Your father came here, Ashgrove. Twenty-two years ago. He stood where you are standing."

The room tilted. Ivo gripped the edge of the table. Rain slid down the skylight, silent, endless.

"He was alive when he reached the gate," the Archivist went on, gently now, the way you speak to someone standing in deep water. "They turned him back at the outer checkpoint—he never got past the yard. But he shouted through the fence. He said he had seen the gorge, and the names carved there, and his own among them, and that he would come back with witnesses. The guard commander wrote it all down, because everything gets written down here; that is our curse and our craft. Would you like to see the entry?"

Ivo could not speak. She nodded.

The Archivist drew a slim volume from the stack and opened it to a ribbon marker, and turned it toward her, and there it was in faded clerk's ink:

*22nd of Thawmonth. Male traveler, approx. 40, claims name Ashgrove. Turned away at outer gate. Subject states: "The source remembers everything. Tell them I'll return with the whole valley behind me." Subject departed downstream on foot. No further record.*

No further record. Five words that contained a whole death, somewhere between this gate and the river, in the dark, alone, carrying a message no one had ever delivered—until his daughter walked into this room smelling of coal smoke and river water with his compass in her pocket.

Ivo sat down. She found she had no memory of deciding to. The chair was hard and cold and real, and she held onto its reality the way a drowning woman holds a spar. She did not cry. Something in her had gone past crying into a stillness like the center of the room itself, where the lamps never flickered.

"He waited," she said at last. "You've been waiting. All of you. For what—for someone to just *walk in*?"

"For someone with the compass and the grudge," the Archivist repeated. "Those were my predecessor's words, and hers before her. It sounds like prophecy. It is really just arithmetic. The compass finds the room. The grudge survives the journey. Most people with a grudge lack the compass. Most people with the compass—" she paused, "—lack the will to use it. Using it costs everything, you understand. The moment you carry these pages out of this room, you become the most dangerous person in the valley, and the Corvanes will spend any sum, break any law, drown any town, to stop you. There is no invisibility after this. There is no quiet life piloting some anonymous boat under a borrowed name. I want you to understand that before you touch the ledger. I have watched clerks and couriers come and go for forty years, and I have asked myself about each one: does she understand the price? You are the first who has ever gotten this far. So I ask you plainly, Ashgrove, daughter of the mapmaker: do you understand the price?"

Ivo thought of the boat. Of the ballast where the documents rode, of Odile holding the mule track, of Fen somewhere behind her with his two ledgers and his cut sluice gates, of the gorge full of names and her father's scratched message—*the source remembers everything*—and of the choice waiting at the end of all of it, the one she had already begun to feel taking shape in her like a channel forming in a flood: not to destroy, not to keep, but to give it away, all of it, everywhere, at once.

She thought about being invisible. About how it had never once, in eleven years, actually kept her safe. It had only kept her small.

"Yes," she said.

The Archivist studied her for a long moment. Then she drew a small key from around her neck on a cord gone black with age, and reached across the table, and unlocked the chain on the master ledger with a click that sounded, in that perfect silence, like a lock opening on the whole valley's history.

"Then we haven't much time," the old woman said. "Edmun Corvane is coming upstream. My last courier reported riders at Hallowford two days ago, and the old man does not travel anywhere slowly. When he arrives, he will burn this room and everyone in it—you especially. So we will copy what must be copied, we will pack what must be packed, and you will tell me, while we work, exactly how you mean to get a library out of a fortress." Her clouded eyes glinted, and for the first time Ivo heard something in the dry voice that might, in another life, have been mischief. "I have forty-one years of suggestions. I warn you: none of them involve leaving quietly."

Outside, the rain kept sliding down the skylight, and somewhere below, faint as a pulse through stone, Ivo could swear she heard the river—the true river, the first water, gathering itself in the dark to begin its long run toward the sea, carrying everything it carried toward every landing, every market, every ear.

She pulled the master ledger toward her and broke its seal.
