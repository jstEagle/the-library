## 17. Corvane Comes Upstream

Edmun Corvane had not ridden a horse in eleven years, and his body reminded him of it with every mile of the headwater trail. The saddle found the same bruised places it always had; the cold coming off the river found the same old gap beneath his collar where the wind had always gotten in. He was sixty-three years old and he had built an empire on paper, and paper did not care how sore a man's knees were.

The gorge narrowed around them until the riders had to go in single file, boots squelching through snowmelt that seeped from the walls like the rock itself was weeping. Above them the cliffs rose sheer and grey, and carved into them—Edmun kept his eyes forward, but he knew they were there—were names. Thousands of names. His grandfather's generation had called the place the Gorge of Names with a kind of contempt, as if naming it were enough to contain it. Edmun had ordered the road rerouted twice to keep travelers away from it. The names remained.

"Archivist's station by nightfall," said Captain Vess, riding at his shoulder. She was a broad woman with a face like a closed ledger, and she had served the family since before Edmun's hair went white. "The old woman's still alive, they say. Still keeping the books."

"The books," Edmun said, and tasted something sour. "Yes."

He had come upstream for three reasons, and he had arranged them carefully in his mind the way he arranged everything, in columns, in order of precedence. First: the vault. Second: the girl. Third: the fire. But underneath all three, unarranged, uncolumned, sat a fourth reason he refused to look at directly, the way he had refused to look at certain documents for forty years. He was seventy miles from any town that would recognize him. He was closer to the source of the Vell than any Corvane patriarch had been since the dam years. And the water here ran so clear over its stones that he could see every one of them, each pebble distinct, nothing hidden, nothing blurred—and it made him feel the way church had made him feel as a boy: exposed, and small, and furious about both.

They reached the station at dusk.

It was less a fortress than a lie built in stone—a long low building fitted into the throat of the valley, slate roof green with moss, chimneys smoking gently as if it were only ever a waystation for surveyors. The Corvane crest above the door had been painted over decades ago with the neutral grey of the River Authority. That was the genius of the thing, Edmun thought, dismounting badly and hiding how badly. Not locks. Not guns. Boredom. No one climbed seventy miles up a gorge to raid a supply depot.

Inside, the lamps were lit and the hall smelled of lamp oil, dust, and—faintly, unmistakably—of ink. Fresh ink. Someone had been writing tonight.

The Archivist met him in the reading room.

She was smaller than he remembered, and he had only seen her once before, twenty years ago, when his father brought him upriver to be shown what the family actually owned. Not the mills. Not the locks or the tolls or the judges. This. A room lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, each spine dated, each date a year of the valley's life. The old woman stood among them like a caretaker among graves, tiny and upright, her hands folded, her eyes pale and clear as the headwater itself.

"Patriarch," she said. No bow. There had never been bows between the Corvanes and their Archivists; some ancient arrangement, older than Edmun, held that this room answered to no one. His father had hated it. Edmun's father had raged at her predecessor once, in this very room, and the old man had simply opened a ledger to a marked page and let him read, and after that there were no more rages.

"You know why I've come," Edmun said.

"I've known why you'd come for thirty years." She gestured to a chair—the good chair, the reader's chair, worn smooth by generations of Corvane hands. He did not take it. "You've lost the river, Edmun. Locks are refusing your tolls. Your steamer crews walked off at Hallowford. Half the valley is reading aloud in market squares from pages I copied out myself, forty years ago, against the day someone came asking." Something moved in her face that was almost, terribly, a smile. "I'm an archivist. I keep duplicates."

Vess shifted behind him, hand near her sidearm. Edmun raised two fingers: still.

"The girl," he said. "Ashgrove. Where is she?"

"Below. In the vault, with her collector friend and whatever remains of Odile Vask's people. They arrived yesterday. They've been reading." The Archivist tilted her head. "She reads the way her father read. Fast, and angry, and then suddenly quiet, in the middle, when something lands."

Something landed in Edmun's chest too, at the name. *Her father.* Twenty years ago a mapmaker named Ashgrove had come up this same trail with a compass and a grudge, and had gotten as far as the gorge, and had been turned back—or worse—and the whole affair had been tidied into a single line in a single ledger: *thief, apprehended, drowned.* Edmun had written that line himself. He had been younger then, and eager, and his father had been dying, and the line had seemed like housekeeping.

"Bring her up," he said.

* * *

They brought her up at lamplight, and Edmun saw immediately that he had imagined her wrong.

He had expected a fugitive—wild-eyed, cornered, clutching her evidence like a mother clutches a child. Instead the woman who came into the reading room moved like a captain on her own deck: unhurried, watchful, hands loose at her sides. She was river-brown and wind-chapped, with her father's long jaw and her father's habit of looking slightly past a man's eyes to the horizon behind him. Behind her came the debt collector, Fen—Edmun knew his file, knew the double employment, suspected the double ledger—and behind Fen, filling the doorway, two of Odile Vask's hill people with rifles they were not pretending to hide.

"Captain Ashgrove," Edmun said.

"Lord Corvane." Her voice was even. "You're a long way from your desk."

"I could say the same." He gestured to the ledgers. "Though I suppose you've felt at home here."

Something flickered across her face—not anger. Grief, quickly governed. He catalogued it. Grief was leverage. Everything was leverage; that was the first lesson his father had taught him, before the second lesson, which was that leverage was cheaper than violence and lasted longer.

"Sit," Edmun said, and this time he took the reader's chair himself, because the room noticed such things, and because his knees had begun to shake and chairs were merciful.

She did not sit. She leaned against the edge of a table instead, arms folded, close enough to touch the ledgers, far enough from him. Fen stayed by the wall. The hill people stayed in the doorway. The Archivist withdrew to her corner with her folded hands, watching, always watching, and Edmun understood suddenly that he had never once, in sixty-three years, been watched the way this room watched him.

"You burned my boat," Ivo Ashgrove said.

"My riders burned a vessel registered to a false name carrying stolen property." He said it mildly. "I'm told the Syndicate has already seized what was left of it for debt. You have had a difficult month, Captain. Let me make it simple."

He laid it out the way he laid out everything: plainly, in order, without ornament, because ornament invited suspicion and suspicion slowed transactions.

"First. Your father. Tomas Ashgrove, mapmaker, convicted of theft in the spring of the dam year. The conviction is false—I signed the warrant knowing it, and I can sign the reversal now. By morning his name will be clean in every record the family controls, and the family still controls more records than you'd believe. Second. Your debts. The Harrow Syndicate holds paper against you totaling four hundred and twelve marks. I hold the Syndicate's senior notes—I have for six years; that's how I knew where to send Silas Fen. One signature dissolves them. Third." He paused, and gave her the truth, because the truth was the most expensive gift he had and therefore the most persuasive. "Third. You. The warrant from Landfall, the old wanted notice Maren Oduya keeps folded under her bar. All of it. Gone. You walk out of this valley invisible again, Captain. Clean name, clean father, clean hands, no debt, no hunters. You buy a boat under your own name and run passengers down the Vell until you're old."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, faintly, he could hear the river working at the rocks, patient as a clerk.

"And in exchange," Ivo said quietly.

"In exchange you stop. You leave the ledgers here. You leave the copies wherever they are—they'll circulate, they'll fade, a scandal is a season and the valley forgets faster than it floods. You don't broadcast. You don't testify. You go back to being no one, and the Corvanes go back to being what we have been for a hundred years, which is the river's memory." He spread his hands. "That's all it is, Captain. Silence. I'm not asking for your loyalty or your love. Just your silence. It's the cheapest thing you own and I'm offering a fortune for it."

And here was the thing he had not planned for, the thing outside his columns: he meant it. Every word. He watched her weigh it and he recognized the weighing, because he had done it himself at twenty-six, in this room, offered a smaller version of the same bargain by his own father. *Take the clean line, boy. Take the tidy ledger.* He had taken it. He had spent forty years taking it, one line at a time, until the tidy ledger was all that remained of him.

Ivo Ashgrove looked at the ledgers behind her. Then at him.

"My father came up here," she said, "with a compass that pointed at this room. He died in the gorge with your riders behind him, and you wrote *thief* next to his name, and for twenty years my mother told everyone her husband drowned honest because the alternative was telling everyone he died a criminal." Her voice did not rise. That was the frightening part of it. "You're offering me exactly what you took from him. A clean line in somebody else's book."

"I'm offering you your life back."

"No." She pushed off the table and stood straight, and for a moment the lamplight caught the brass compass hanging at her neck, and Edmun saw the engraving on it—ASHGROVE—in his grandfather's hand, and understood that the compass had never pointed north, that it had been pointing here, home, all along, waiting in a sea cave for the tide to give it back. "You're offering me a smaller cell with better furniture. My silence isn't mine to sell anyway." She nodded toward the doorway, toward the hills, toward the whole length of the wounded river. "It belongs to everyone whose name is carved in that gorge. Ask them."

"They'll say yes," Edmun said. "People always say yes, right up until they say nothing at all. Memory rots, Captain. I've watched it rot for forty years. Give it ten and your broadcast is a tavern story. Give it twenty and it's a legend nobody believes. But a signature—" he tapped the reader's table, "—a signature lasts."

"Then you should know," said the Archivist from her corner, in the mild tone of a woman correcting a date, "that the master ledger left this room an hour ago. Downriver. On your own mail steamer, with your own crew, who volunteered." She smiled properly then, and it was the most terrible thing Edmun had seen in forty years of terrible things. "I keep duplicates, Patriarch. But I don't keep originals. Originals belong to the river now."

Edmun sat very still.

Then he rose, and his knees held, and he heard his own voice come out flat and cold and young, his father's voice, the voice he had sworn at twenty-six he would never use. "Burn it," he said to Vess. "The vault. The copies. The station. Everything."

"There's nothing here to burn that matters," the Archivist said. "But burn it if it helps you sleep."

Vess looked at him—at him, not to him—and did not move. Behind her, one of the hill people worked the action of his rifle, softly, like a man clearing his throat.

Ivo Ashgrove was already walking to the door. At the threshold she stopped, and did not turn all the way around, and gave him the last of it over her shoulder like small change.

"The steamer makes Hallowford by morning. After that there's no bargain in the world big enough. Ride down and see, Lord Corvane. See what the river carries."

She went out. Fen followed. The lamps guttered in the draft, and Edmun Corvane stood alone in the reading room among a hundred years of true things, and heard, rising faintly from below—from the vault, from the gorge, from the whole patient length of the Vell—the sound of water already moving toward morning.
