## 18. Everything the River Carries

The master ledger weighed less than I expected.

That was the first thing I understood as I lifted it from the reading room's cradle of oiled silk—less than a hymnal, less than a child. Forty years of erased towns and drowned parishes and names struck from every map in the valley, all of it bound in grey leather gone soft as skin at the corners, and it fit against my ribs like something I'd been carrying for years already. The Archivist had pressed it into my hands with the last strength in her arms, and her fingers had been cold as river stones, and she had said: *It was never meant to be kept. It was meant to be read.*

Behind us, the vault corridor filled with smoke that smelled of lamp oil and old paper. Edmun Corvane's riders had reached the outer station. We had minutes, maybe fewer.

"Tell me you have a plan," Fen said. He had the ledger's duplicate pages bundled under one arm—three months of his own hand, corroborating line by line—and a pistol he hadn't fired yet, which surprised me more than anything else about him.

"I have a river," I said.

* * *

The Corvane mail steamer *Vigilant* sat at the source landing like a fat black swan, its hold stacked with sealed dispatch cases, its single funnel breathing thin grey smoke into the cold morning. Two crewmen dozed on deck. The pilot, an old man named Hask whose face I knew from twenty years of passing him at locks, stood at the wheelhouse window watching the riders come down the switchback road with torches still lit against the dawn fog.

I boarded across the gangplank at a walk, because running draws eyes, and set the ledger on the chart table in front of Hask before he could decide what I was.

"You're Ashgrove," he said. Not a question. "The thief's daughter."

"The mapmaker's daughter," I said, and watched him flinch, because the second name meant something to him that the first did not. "Hask. In your hold there are dispatches going downriver tonight. Lock reports, tariff notices, Corvane correspondence. I need your boat until the last lock."

"And if I say no?"

"Then they burn this station with me in it, and you carry their ash downriver instead of the truth, and nothing changes." I put both hands flat on the chart table so he could see they weren't shaking. They were shaking. He was kind enough not to look. "I'm not asking you to fight anyone. I'm asking you to run water."

Fen came up behind me with the bundle of his own ledger and began, without ceremony, tearing the dispatch cases open and sliding his pages inside among the legitimate freight. Hask watched him do it. Then Hask turned back to his wheel, spat over the rail in the direction of the approaching riders, and rang the engine bell twice.

"We cast off in four minutes," he said. "I've run this river forty years. I know where the current is honest."

* * *

The plan was simple, which is the only kind that survives contact with Corvane riders.

The *Vigilant* would run downstream through the night, stopping at every lock and landing—the way she stopped every week, unremarkable, expected. At each stop, a copy would go ashore. Not one copy. A dozen. To tavern boards and church doors and lock walls and the hands of ferrymen and millwrights and anyone who could read, with instructions to read aloud what they couldn't keep silent. Odile's people were already moving down the valley on foot and mule-back, seeded through every town between here and Landfall, waiting for paper. By the time Edmun Corvane understood what was happening, the story would be everywhere and nowhere, owned by everyone, impossible to burn.

We had copied the ledger three times over in the reading room while the Archivist slept her final sleep in her chair. Three hundred pages each, Fen's hand and mine and Odile's, cramped and furious, ink-stained to the wrist. It had taken everything the night gave us. My fingers still ached. I thought of my father doing this same work alone, twenty years ago, with no one to help him carry it, and I did not let myself think further than that, because the gorge was behind us now and Odile was holding it, and thinking too hard about either would crack me open at the wheel.

Because that was the other part. The part I hadn't said aloud yet.

The *Vigilant* could outrun riders along the water. She could not outrun them forever, and she could not outrun the telegraph wires that ran the valley road. Sooner or later—probably at Lock Nine, probably sooner—a gate would close, or a chain would go across the channel, or a rifle would find the pilothouse. The river's own current would carry our bundles past the barriers; we'd weighted them in oilcloth, built to float free when cut loose. But the boat, and the woman at her wheel—

That part I'd decided alone, at the chart table, while Fen argued with a stuck dispatch case. Some cargoes are worth the vessel.

* * *

We ran the upper reaches fast, white water chattering under the hull, the gorge walls falling away behind us green and dripping. Twice I saw riders pacing us along the cliff road, torches guttering in daylight, horses lathered and furious at the pace. Once a shot cracked off the rocks above the funnel and Hask didn't even turn his head.

"They'll telegraph ahead to Nine," he said. "Chain across the channel, most likely. Or they'll scuttle something in the narrows below it."

"Then we stop short of Nine and cut loose everything," I said. "Every bundle. Let the current do the delivery."

"And you?"

I checked the lead line. Twelve feet, clean bottom, the good deep channel hugging the eastern bank. "And me whatever's left."

Fen appeared in the wheelhouse doorway with oil on his hands and something in his face I'd learned to read as arithmetic. He'd been doing sums since the vault. I'd seen him do them at Hallowford too, standing at the sluice gates with the whole valley road flooding behind him, deciding in one breath which side of his own ledger he wanted to die on.

"There's a back channel below Nine," he said. "Mill race. Silted half shut since the dam went in, but a light hull could take it at high water. It comes out below the lock, past the chain."

"It hasn't been run in fifteen years."

"No," Fen agreed. "It hasn't."

Hask snorted. "You want to take a mail steamer through a dead mill race."

"I want to take *her* through it," Fen said, meaning me, meaning the truth, and I felt the old vertigo again—the tide-cave feeling, the ground opening to show me what had been underneath all along. "I'll take the bundles down to the narrows in the skiff and cut them loose at the chain myself. You two take the race."

"Why you?" I asked, though I knew.

"Because I've spent eleven years collecting debts for men who erase people," Fen said. "Let me spend one hour delivering the opposite." He was already climbing down the ladder to the skiff, and he did not look back, and I understood that looking back was a luxury he had decided, finally, he could afford to skip.

* * *

Lock Nine came into view an hour after noon, and it was worse than telegraphed.

They hadn't strung a chain. They'd brought the steam tug *Warden* up from the lower pool and moored her square across the channel, guns visible on her deck, Corvane pennants snapping wet in the wind off the water. Behind her, the lock gates stood open like a held breath. On the lock wall, maybe thirty men with rifles, and among them—I knew the silhouette, the set of the shoulders, the way he held his hat rather than wearing it—Edmun Corvane himself, come upriver to watch his property come home.

Hask slowed the engine to a crawl. The *Vigilant* drifted. Downstream, the narrows waited, white-flecked, and beyond them the silted mouth of the mill race, invisible unless you knew to look.

"He'll offer terms," Hask said quietly. "He always offers terms. That's how he's ruled this valley—never a battle, always a bargain."

Edmun Corvane raised a speaking trumpet. His voice carried across the water the way money carries, smooth and entitled and absolutely certain of being heard.

"Ivo Ashgrove. You have something of mine."

I took the trumpet from Hask's hook. My hands were steady now. Fear had done its work on me days ago, out at Landfall Point, kneeling in a cave full of my family's ghosts; what was left was something cleaner.

"I have something of *everyone's*," I called back. "Forty years of your family's sins, Mr. Corvane. Your floods. Your famines. The parish you drowned and struck from the maps. Every name your archivists ever cut out of the record. And as of one hour ago, it's in thirty towns, in two hundred hands, and growing."

For the first time in my life I watched a powerful man become small. It happened in the shoulders first. Then the trumpet lowered an inch.

"Lies decay," he said. "Paper burns. Come aboard my tug, give me the original, and I will clear your father's name in writing, dissolve your debt, and you may live out your life on this river in peace. This is the last generous thing I will ever say to you."

And God forgive me, some animal part of me leaned toward it—the way a drowning woman leans toward any shore. *Ashgrove, cleared. Debt, dissolved. Peace.* Twenty years of looking over my shoulder, ended in a sentence. I felt the temptation the way you feel a current under calm water, pulling somewhere you must not go.

"The last generous thing," I repeated. "From the man who burned the station this morning with a dying woman in it." I turned to Hask. "Full ahead. Eastern bank. Hard as she'll bear."

The *Vigilant* surged. Rifles opened up along the wall, spitting sparks off the funnel, punching holes in the pilothouse glass around our heads. Hask drove us at the narrows with the calm of a man who had decided long ago exactly how he intended to leave this world, and I threw the wheel over at the seam of the current where the mill race's mouth hid behind a fold of brown water, and we went in.

* * *

I will not pretend I remember it clearly. There is a kind of terror that erases itself even as it happens, and running a dead channel at flood stage in a stolen steamer is that kind.

What remains is pieces. The race walls closing in, moss-slick stone close enough to touch on both sides. An old sluice gate rotted through, hanging like a broken jaw—we went under it with a foot to spare and the sound of it scraping the deckhouse like a scream. Water coming aboard in tons. Hask laughing, actually laughing, a wild old man's laugh, ringing the bell the whole way through as though announcing us to every ghost of the drowned parish. The channel bending twice, three times, each bend a coin flip, each coin flip landing heads. And then—suddenly, absurdly—open sky, open water, the lower pool spreading out below Lock Nine silver and calm, and the *Vigilant* wallowing in it half-full, alive, her hull groaning but holding.

Behind us, at the lock, chaos. The *Warden* trying to turn in the narrows. Riders plunging down the bank toward the race mouth, finding it impassable, screaming at each other. And cutting loose from the *Vigilant*'s stern davits ten minutes earlier, just before the narrows—Fen, in the skiff, with every remaining bundle, drifting down toward the barrier under the guns with oilcloth parcels stacked around his boots like sandbags made of truth.

Through the shattered glass I watched him do it. He never hurried. He untied each parcel and slid it into the current with the unhurried care of a man laying flowers, letting the river take them—one to the lock wall, where a young lockhand waded out waist-deep to catch it; one to the ferry landing; one snagged on a willow root where children would find it by evening. The rifles on the *Warden* swung toward him. I screamed his name across the water, uselessly, the sound torn away by wind.

Fen looked up at the guns. Then he deliberately, slowly, raised his own ledger—the original, his eleven years of secret keeping—over his head, and dropped it into the river too, into the current, into everyone's hands at once, and the gesture broke something in the men on the *Warden*, because no one fired. You cannot shoot a man who has just surrendered his weapon to the whole world.

Then the *Warden* found her turning room and came for the skiff, and Fen bent to his oars, and the chase swept downstream and out of my sight, and I had a wheel to hold and a sinking ship to argue with.

* * *

She died well, the *Vigilant*. We grounded her on the shingle below the old ferry crossing, and Hask and I got ashore with the last copy—the master ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest—and stood in the shallows watching her settle as the Corvane riders finally rounded the bend on the far bank, too late, their torches pointless in the afternoon light.

"She was a good boat," Hask said.

"She was their boat," I said. "That was the point."

Downstream, faint on the wind, I could hear bells starting. First one church, then another, then a third—Odile's people, doing what they'd promised, ringing every bell in every town the moment the first bundle touched shore. The sound rolled up the valley like weather. On the far bank, the riders had stopped. Their horses stamped and blew. Somewhere among them Edmun Corvane was shouting orders, and the orders were getting thinner, and thinner, and no one was moving to obey them, because half those men had grown up in the drowned parish and had just heard its name spoken aloud for the first time in twenty years.

Hask put his hand on my shoulder, briefly, like a man touching a hot stove on purpose.

"Go on, then," he said. "Deliver it."

So I ran. Along the shingle, up the ferry road, the ledger against my ribs, my lungs full of fire, toward the lights of the town coming on early in the dusk—and behind me the *Vigilant* settled onto her beam ends with a long sigh, and ahead of me the bells kept ringing, and ringing, and ringing.

I burst through the market doors into a crowd that turned as one body to look at me—a riverwoman, soaked, bleeding from a cut on her brow, holding a book like a lantern—and I held it up over my head, and the room went silent enough to hear the river.

"My name is Ivo Ashgrove," I said, "and this is everything the water carries."

* * *

They read until midnight. Then they read until dawn.

By the time the sun rose over Lock Nine, the ledger was no longer a book. It was a hundred voices in a market square, a schoolteacher copying pages by lamplight, a priest who had buried drowned parishioners under false names weeping over the true ones. It was couriers on the morning boats, Odile's mule trains already moving south with fresh copies sewn into grain sacks, ferrymen poling bundles across to farms that had never once been told why their grandfathers' land went under the water. The Corvane name was still on the toll houses and the lock gates and the tariff notices, but it had begun to sound, everywhere, like a word in a language nobody spoke anymore.

I stood on the landing at first light, wrung out, ash-smudged, owner of nothing—not a boat, not a cave, not a hidden name—and watched the current carry the last loose pages downstream toward towns I would never see.

Somewhere downriver, Silas Fen was still running. Somewhere upstream, Odile still held the gorge against riders who no longer believed in what they were riding for. And somewhere in the crowd behind me, a boy of perhaps ten tugged my sleeve and asked, in the particular merciless clarity of children, whether it was true—that his town had been real, that it had been taken, that the book said so.

I knelt down in the mud of the landing so we were eye to eye.

"It's true," I said. "And now it's yours. All of it. That's what the river was carrying—it was carrying it the whole time. We just finally listened."

He took the page I handed him very seriously, the way you take something heavy, and ran to show his mother, and I stayed kneeling a moment longer in the cold, listening to the bells roll down the valley like a tide that had finally, after forty years, changed its mind.

Far downstream, past the last of the locks, a horseman was already riding hard for the coast—to the newspapers, to the capital, to whoever in the wide world had never heard of the Vell River and would hear of it now. The story was leaving the valley. There was no gate left to close against it.

But Edmun Corvane, I would learn by evening, had one move left in him—and it wasn't a rider, or a gun, or a bargain.

It was fire.
