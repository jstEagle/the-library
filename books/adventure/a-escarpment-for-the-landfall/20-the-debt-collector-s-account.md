## 20. The Debt Collector's Account

The Harrow Syndicate kept its paper in a warehouse on Coalmer Row, in a room without windows, because the men who ran it believed that debts, like mushrooms, grew best in the dark. Silas Fen had been coming to that room for eleven years. He knew the smell of it—lamp oil, mildew, and the sweet rot of ink going brown on cheap paper—and he had always told himself the smell was honest. Money was money. A debt was a debt. The ledger did not care who wept when the collector knocked.

He had stopped believing that somewhere between Hallowford and the gorge, on a night when he had put an iron bar through a sluice gate and felt, for the first time in his adult life, like a man instead of an instrument.

Now he stood at the counter with the Ashgrove file open before him, and the clerk—a narrow man named Pellwick with ink-stained cuffs and no curiosity whatsoever—waited for him to sign the transfer of claim.

"Syndicate's taking the boat," Pellwick said. "Standard seizure. You'll carry the notice to the impound yard yourself, since you know the vessel."

Fen looked down at the page. *ASHGROVE, I.* Principal, interest, penalties compounded quarterly with the arithmetic cruelty the Syndicate reserved for people who had nothing to compound it from. Eleven years of numbers. He had written half of them himself. He had stood in doorways reciting them to women holding babies, to old men with shaking hands, to a riverboat captain with her father's jaw and her father's habit of looking at you as if she were already writing your name down somewhere.

"Pellwick," Fen said. "Fetch me the lamp."

"The lamp? You've got light enough."

"The lamp," Fen said again, and set his hand flat on the file.

Pellwick fetched it. He was that kind of man; he would have fetched anything short of a confession. Fen took the little brass oil lamp in one hand, tipped it, and let the flame walk across the top sheet.

Paper burns faster than you'd think, when it wants to. The numbers curled first, blackening at the edges like leaves touched by frost in reverse, then went up all at once with a sound like a held breath released. Fen fed the whole file to the flame, page by page, and watched eleven years of compound interest become smoke that rose and flattened against the ceiling and had nowhere else to go.

"What," Pellwick said, very quietly, "are you doing."

"Settling an account."

"That's—the Syndicate will—"

"Yes," Fen said. "They will."

He let the last corner of the last page burn down to his fingers before he dropped it into the iron tray, and the pain was small and clean and he was grateful for it, the way a man is grateful for any proof that he still has skin and nerve and something left to lose. The tray smoked. The room smelled, briefly, of something other than mildew and rot.

"You could have just torn it up," Pellwick said.

"Tearing is private," Fen said. "I wanted witnesses."

There were none, of course, except Pellwick, who would tell the story anyway, because telling stories was the only power a man like Pellwick had. By nightfall the whole of Coalmer Row would know that Silas Fen had burned a claim in the Syndicate's own counting room. By morning they would come for him.

Good, he thought. Let them come while the smoke is still fresh. Let there be no gap in which I might change my mind.

He walked out past the shelves of files—thousands of them, thousands of names, each one a small dark room with someone inside it—and he did not look back, because looking back was a habit belonging to a man who expected to return.

* * *

The tribunal convened in the market hall at Weirbridge, which had been a fish market until the reading, and then a courthouse, and then nothing at all for two days while everyone argued about what it ought to be. There was no law for what had happened on the Vell. There was no precedent for a valley discovering, all at once, over the course of a single week, that its own history had been edited by the family that owned it. So they built the thing out of what they had: a magistrate from downstream who had never taken Corvane coin, three elders chosen by lot from the towns along the river, and a table borrowed from the fishmongers, scrubbed raw but still smelling faintly of brine.

Fen testified on the fourth day.

He had dressed in his good coat, the grey one, and found when he put it on that it hung differently than it used to—heavier in the shoulders, or perhaps he was simply lighter. In the hall, the benches were full. He saw faces he knew: lockmen, ferrymen, the women from the drowned parish who had traveled upriver to sit in the front row with their hands folded like churchgoers. Odile sat apart, near the wall, arms crossed, watching him with the expression of a woman grading an exam she expected him to fail.

The magistrate read the charges against Edmun Corvane into the record—flood, famine, massacre, and beneath all of them, the quieter crime that made the others possible: the editing. Then she looked up at Fen.

"State your name and your trade."

"Silas Fen. Collector. Eleven years for the Harrow Syndicate." He paused. "And four of those years, in practice, for the Corvane family. They bought my employers' paper during the drought years. After that, some of my collections weren't collections at all. They were errands."

"Errands," the magistrate repeated.

"Watching. Reporting. Carrying lists of names upstream to men who wanted to know which river folk remembered too much." He said it plainly, because he had rehearsed plainness for weeks, walking the towpath alone, saying the words aloud to the water until they stopped tasting like ash. "I am not here as a witness against the Corvanes only. I'm here against myself."

A murmur moved through the benches. The magistrate let it run its course.

"Tell us about your brother."

It was the question he had dreaded and the one he had asked for. He gripped the rail of the witness stand and found the wood damp under his palms, the fish-market ghost of it, and he began.

"My brother Tomas drowned in the parish of Saint Aldhelm's Reach, though you won't find that name on any map printed after the dam. He was nine. My mother had refused to sell the house, so the Corvane agents raised the water level early—before the compensation came, before the moving carts, before anything. They called it a scheduling error afterward. There is a document in the master ledger authorizing the early release. I have seen it. I copied it out myself, two years ago, when I first understood what I was carrying around in my head."

He told them about the ledgers. His own, the secret one, kept in cipher in the lining of his coat for four years—every crime he had witnessed or abetted, every name, every date, cross-checked against the archive pages Ivo Ashgrove had carried out of the vault. He had brought it to the tribunal in a canvas satchel, and he set it on the table now, and the sound it made was small and final, like a stone settling at the bottom of clear water.

"I kept two books," he said. "One for the men who paid me. One for God, or whatever you call the thing that watches when nobody's paying. The second book is yours now. All of it. Including the parts that hang me."

The magistrate turned pages for a long time. The hall was so quiet Fen could hear the river through the walls, running the way it always ran, indifferent and patient.

"These entries," she said at last. "The collection at Millbrook, where you broke a man's arm."

"Broke it and reported him as resistant. He lost the use of two fingers. His name is Jorun Hale. He lives above the chandlery at Millbrook landing. I have already paid him restitution—I went to him before I came here—but payment isn't absolution, so I want it entered in the record regardless."

"The woman at Lock Six whom you evicted in winter."

"Entered. Her name is Petra Voss. She stayed with cousins. Her children missed a season of school."

"The notices you posted for Ivo Ashgrove, when the Corvanes wanted her found."

Fen's throat tightened. He had known this entry was coming, and it was the hardest one, harder than his brother, because the others were done and this one had almost been.

"I posted them. And when I found her, I didn't take her. I left fuel where she'd find it and falsified my reports for six weeks. That's not a defense. It's a man deciding late. But it should be weighed alongside the rest, because I want the record accurate, not flattering. If this valley is going to start remembering things truly, it can begin with me."

He looked out at the benches then, and found Ivo near the back. She had come despite everything—the burned boat, the burned bridges, the new fame she wore like wet clothes. She didn't nod. She didn't smile. She just watched him with those level eyes, and after a moment she gave him the smallest tilt of her head, the way one pilot acknowledges another passing in fair water, and that was worth more to him than acquittal.

The tribunal deliberated for two days. When they returned, the magistrate stood and read the finding in a voice that carried to the back wall:

"Silas Fen: guilty of complicity in the crimes enumerated, in the counts and degrees described in his own testimony and confirmed by his own records. Sentenced to two years' labor in the service of the river towns—rebuilding the drowned parishes' memorial rolls, restoring the erased maps, and standing witness wherever the record requires a living voice. Restitution to continue as begun. The tribunal notes, for the record, that the sentence would be longer but for the prisoner's voluntary surrender of evidence against himself, and that justice which accepts only confessions convenient to the confessor is not justice but theater. Mr. Fen, do you accept?"

Fen stood. His legs were steady, which surprised him; he had expected to shake.

"I accept," he said. "And I'd ask the tribunal to enter one more thing."

"Enter it."

"When I was a boy, my brother asked me why the river flooded our town, and I told him I didn't know, because I didn't. Nobody knew. That was the design—not the flood itself, but the not-knowing. Everything I did for eleven years was a way of keeping people from knowing. Two years of labor won't unbreak Jorun Hale's fingers or raise Saint Aldhelm's Reach. But if I spend them making sure the next child who asks gets an answer—" his voice caught, and he let it, and let the hall hear it catch— "then the account balances closer than it does today. Not square. Closer. Some accounts never square. You just keep paying."

The gavel came down once, softly, like a wave closing over a stone.

* * *

They gave him a cell in the old tollhouse at Weirbridge for his first night, more ceremony than necessity, and he slept better in it than he had slept in years—inns had never agreed with him; a bed without bars had always felt like a lie. In the morning a constable walked him down to the landing, where a work barge waited to carry him upriver to the first of the drowned parishes, where the memorial rolls were to be carved.

Odile was there. Of course she was there. She materialized the way she always did, out of the crowd's blind spot, and fell into step beside him on the planks.

"Two years," she said. "You got two years. I buried a man in the gorge and got forty."

"You got forty of carrying it," Fen said. "That's heavier."

She snorted, but she didn't disagree. They walked a while in silence. The barge crew loaded tools behind them—chisels, mauls, crates of blank slate hauled down from the hills.

"They're saying the Corvane lands are being divided," Odile said. "Parcels going back to the parishes. Edmun's in custody downstream, waiting on whatever passes for a trial these days. And they're saying—" she glanced sideways at him, sly as a heron— "that a certain ferrywoman has applied for her license under her true name. First time in fifteen years anyone's signed *Ashgrove* to a piece of official paper and meant it."

"So I've heard."

"Heard, or been told by the woman herself, at your sentencing, where she sat through every hour of it?"

"She mentioned it," Fen admitted.

"Mm." Odile stopped at the gangplank and looked up at him, and for the first time since he'd known her there was nothing guarded in her face, only the tired, hard-won thing underneath. "My grandfather said a debt paid honestly is a plank you can build on. He was a millwright, so he'd know about planks. Go build, collector. And when the rolls are carved at Saint Aldhelm's Reach—"

She stopped. Her jaw worked once.

"—put my name on the list of helpers. Small letters. Bottom of the page. I owe that town too, and I'm done pretending otherwise."

"It'll be the first line I cut," Fen said.

He boarded. The barge pushed off into the current, and Weirbridge slid away behind him—market hall, fish-smelling courthouse, the crowd on the banks, and among them, at the end of the pier, a tall figure in a pilot's coat raising one hand, not waving exactly, just acknowledging the passage, fair water, see you downriver.

Fen stood at the rail with the wind off the water and the sun climbing, and he took out the small notebook he had bought that morning—blank, every page blank, the first empty ledger he had owned since he was nineteen—and on the first line he wrote, in careful block letters:

*Saint Aldhelm's Reach. Names to be restored: 214. Beginning with T. FEN.*

The barge rounded the bend, and the drowned country opened ahead of him, green and quiet under its shallow water, waiting to be remembered.
