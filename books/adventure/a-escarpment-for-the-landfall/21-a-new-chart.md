## 21. A New Chart

The ferry was called the *Maren's Kindness*, which had been a joke at first and then, somehow, wasn't.

Ivo Ashgrove stood at her wheel in the gray-gold light of an autumn morning, six months out from everything, and watched the Vell come down to meet her the way it always did—unhurried, brown-green, carrying silt and leaves and the smell of cold stone. The boat beneath her feet was nothing like the *Larkspur*. It was forty feet of honest timber with a flat bow for loading cattle and crates, a single deck, a wheel that fought her a little in the current because no one had gotten around to truing it since the war of paperwork that followed the Corvane collapse. It carried twelve passengers on a good day, thirty if they didn't mind standing shoulder to shoulder with their neighbors' chickens.

It was the smallest command she'd ever held, and the first one she'd ever held in her own name.

*Captain I. Ashgrove*, said the paper laminated behind the wheel, issued by the new River Tribunal, signed by a clerk whose handwriting trembled with the excitement of a young man doing something unprecedented. There had been no precedent for overturning a conviction by popular acclamation rather than appeal. There had been no precedent for much of anything that happened last spring.

She still caught herself checking the bank for riders. Old habit, like flinching at a door slam. But the banks these days showed only what banks should show: willows going yellow, herons standing their patient vigil in the shallows, farmers hauling in the last of the squash, children running the towpath alongside her wake and shouting her name—not *captain*, not *hey you*, not the false names she'd worn like borrowed coats for eleven years, but *Ashgrove*. They knew her here. That was the whole miracle of it, repeated daily: they knew her, and knowing her cost them nothing, and it cost her nothing either.

"Morning, Captain!" called old Tull from his fish weir as she slid past, and raised two fingers off his hat brim.

"Morning, Tull. Water's low."

"Water's always low in October. Water's always something." He grinned through his beard. "You'll make your landing fine."

She would. She always did now. That was the other strange thing—the ease of it. For eleven years every approach to every lock had been a small arithmetic of fear: who was watching, what name to give, whether the man on the dock had seen her face before, on paper, under the word WANTED. Now the locks waved her through with a nod. Lock Nine's new lockmaster—a round, cheerful woman named Petra who had replaced the vain old loyalist within a month of the readings—had taken to waving Ivo down just to gossip, and once made her stay for lunch, and sent her off with a jar of pickled onions she hadn't asked for and couldn't refuse.

The valley had changed the way a river changes after a flood: not all at once, but everywhere, and permanently.

* * *

The readings had started, as far as anyone could fix a beginning, at Hallowford, where Fen's cut sluice gates had washed the road clean of Corvane riders and the mail steamer *Corvane's Grace*—renamed within the week, over Edmun's howling objections, to *Grace* plain and simple—had grounded herself against the landing with her hold full of paper.

A schoolteacher had been the first. A thin, nervous woman named Besset who taught the children of Hallowford their letters and their numbers and, until that morning, a history in which the Millwright Uprising had never occurred and the drowned parish of Saint Alder's had never existed. She had stood in the market square with a ledger page in her shaking hands and read aloud the true account of the dam: the evictions, the drowned church bells, the four hundred families moved off land their grandfathers had cleared, and the order—signed, dated, filed—that no map printed thereafter should show the parish at all.

She read for two hours. When she finished, nobody cheered. That was the thing Ivo remembered most clearly, later, when people asked her what it had felt like. Nobody cheered. They stood very still, the whole square of them, the way water stands still before it decides which way to run. And then an old man walked to the center and said, in a voice like dry sticks, "My mother is in that parish," and sat down on the cobbles, and wept, and the square knelt around him, and when they rose they were different people.

After that it spread downstream faster than any rider could carry it. Every lock, every landing, every market square and chapel porch. The copies went out on the current itself—Ivo had loaded them into the mail steamer's mail cases, addressed to every town the river touched, franked with the Corvane seal so that no clerk would dare delay them—and the river delivered them the way rivers deliver everything, faithfully and without judgment.

Edmun Corvane had ridden downstream with forty men to stop it, and found that forty men cannot arrest a valley. At Marlow Bend the militia simply declined to mount guard for him. At Two Fords the toll collectors returned his coin. By the time he reached Landfall, his own household staff had testified before the Tribunal, and the great house on its bluff stood open to the public for the first time in its history—not sacked, not burned, though Ivo would be lying if she said no one had wanted to, but opened, its rooms full of citizens walking slowly through the evidence of how they had been governed.

Edmun himself had gone quietly, in the end, which surprised everyone including, Ivo suspected, himself. There had been no battle because there had been nothing left to fight for. You cannot rule people who remember. He lived now under house arrest in the east wing, writing his memoirs, which the Tribunal would seize and publish unedited the day he finished, and which everyone understood would be the final document in the archive he had spent his life curating: the confession of the last archivist, entered into evidence by his own hand.

* * *

The cave at Landfall Point had been sealed in June.

That had been Odile's doing, mostly, or Odile's insistence, and Ivo had gone along with it because Odile was right, though it had cost her something to admit it. The tide-revealed chamber below the cliffs—the drowned archive, the first ledger, the portraits of three centuries of Corvanes staring out of their gilt frames with eyes like fish—could not be left open. Not because the family might come back for it; there was no coming back from what the valley now knew. But because a secret, even a liberated secret, kept in the dark keeps working on people. Ivo had felt it herself, those weeks she'd carried the documents in her ballast: the way knowledge hoarded becomes a kind of wealth, and wealth becomes power, and power becomes exactly the thing you pulled it out of the dark to destroy.

So they had copied everything worth copying—the Tribunal's clerks worked through the winter, and the full record now sat in open shelving in the converted granary at Hallowford that everyone already called the Reading Room, though the name had migrated and grown capital letters—and then, on a low spring tide, they had sealed the cave.

Not hidden. Sealed. There was a difference, and Odile had been fierce about the difference. They built a stone-and-lime wall across the chamber mouth, mortared it properly, and set into it a slab of river slate on which the masons cut words Ivo had helped choose:

HERE LIES WHAT WAS BURIED. IT IS READ NOW. LET IT REST.

And below, smaller, because Odile had insisted and Ivo had not argued:

FOR THE MAPMAKER, WHO REACHED THE SOURCE.

They had all come for the sealing—Odile and her hill folk, Fen in his first month of freedom, Maren from the tavern with a bottle of the good stuff, half the town of Landfall, and a surprising number of strangers who had traveled days just to stand on the shingle and watch. The tide had come in while they worked, patient as ever, and by the time the last stone went in, the sea was already taking possession of the wall, sending its first exploratory fingers into the mortar joints, claiming the place back for itself.

Ivo had put her palm flat on the wet slate before she left. The stone was cold and alive with salt. *Finished with,* she'd thought. Not forgotten. Finished with, the way a wound finishes healing—not the same as unwounded, but no longer open.

She hadn't been back. She wasn't sure she needed to be. The sea would keep it better than any keeper.

* * *

"Mind the gangplank," she called down to her afternoon passengers, a dozen of them: two sisters from Marlow Bend going to market with baskets of late apples, a Tribunal clerk with his nose in a brief, a young couple who spent the entire crossing holding hands and looking at each other instead of the river, which Ivo considered a waste of a river but not her business. An old man with a cane. A boy of maybe ten, traveling alone with a sealed letter and an air of tremendous importance, who planted himself at the rail near the wheel and did not move for the whole crossing.

"You're really her?" the boy asked, somewhere past the middle of the channel, when the sisters were arguing about apples and the clerk was arguing with his brief. "Ivo Ashgrove?"

"I'm really her."

"My da says you burned a boat." His eyes were enormous. "A whole boat. On purpose."

"I did burn a boat. It wasn't mine at the time, which made it easier." The *Corvane's Grace*—no, the *Grace*—had gone up at the Gorge of Names with the riders' pursuit scattered behind her and Odile's people holding the heights, and Ivo had watched eleven years of invisibility burn off her in about twenty minutes, and had felt, absurdly, lighter. "It was worth it. Don't tell him I said arson was worth it."

"He says you're the reason we got the new schoolbooks." The boy said it accusingly, as if she'd personally inconvenienced him with history. "The old ones were shorter."

"The old ones were lies. Lies are usually shorter."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then nodded once, satisfied, and turned back to the rail, and Ivo put her hand on the wheel and felt the familiar small joy of it—the wheel answering, the current leaning, the far bank sliding closer at exactly the speed she chose. Eleven years she had piloted stolen hours under stolen names, and the skill had been the same skill, but the feeling was not the same feeling. Fear had been the ballast of her old life. Take it out, and the boat rode higher than she'd known a boat could ride.

* * *

Fen came aboard at Two Fords on the Thursday crossing, which had become a thing he did, though neither of them had ever arranged it.

He looked better than he had a right to look, considering. The Tribunal's reckoning had taken a year off his life in fees and testimony—he had stood in the granary at Hallowford for nine days and read his own ledgers into the record, both of them, the Syndicate's accounts and his private catalogue of Corvane crimes, page after page in his small exact hand, naming names, dating sins, refusing every opportunity to soften—and what remained of him seemed, if anything, more solid. Complicity paid down like a debt. He still dressed like a funeral, but the coat was newer, and he smiled now in a way that suggested practice.

"Captain." He tipped his head at the wheel. "You've got a smudge on your chart."

"I know. The boy drew a fish on it Tuesday. I've decided to keep the fish."

"A navigator who tolerates unauthorized cartography." He settled onto the bench beside the wheel, produced from his coat the flask he pretended she didn't know about, and poured two measures into tin cups without asking, which was also a thing he did. "Any word from the hills?"

"Odile sends a runner monthly. They're rebuilding the mill at Harrow Fell. Properly, this time—water rights in the villagers' names, registered with the Tribunal." Ivo drank. The whiskey was terrible and warming. "She says the gorge path is clear, if I ever want to walk it again."

"Do you?"

The question sat between them the way real questions do, patient, requiring nothing.

She did want to. She had wanted to since August, when the first hard frost had come down off the hills and the river had turned the color of hammered pewter, and she had stood at her window in the room she rented above Maren's tavern—she still lived above the tavern; the ferry paid enough now, and she liked hearing voices through the floor—and caught herself studying the upstream dark the way she used to study weather. The source was a two-day walk from the gorge, three in bad conditions, along a track only Odile's people knew. She had stood at its headwaters once, in the worst week of her life, with gunpowder smoke still in her hair and her father's compass in her hand, and she had left in such a hurry—downstream, toward the burning, toward the deliverance—that she had never done the thing she'd promised herself she would do there.

There was a wall of names in the Gorge of Names. ASHGROVE was carved among them, in her father's own chisel-work, beside a message scratched shallow and urgent: *the source remembers everything.* She had read it by torchlight with Corvane riders at her back, and she had wept for perhaps four seconds, and then she had run, because running was what survival required.

She had never gone back to finish reading. To stand there with nothing at her back and take the whole of it in. To say whatever needed saying to a dead man who had reached farther than she had and been erased for it.

"After the season closes," she said. "The ferry runs through November. After that—" She looked upstream, where the water came from, where the water had always come from. "After that I owe myself a walk."

Fen nodded slowly, and refilled her cup without being asked, and said nothing, which from Fen was a benediction.

* * *

The last crossing of the day brought her home in the blue hour, when the willows went to shadow and the first lamps of Landfall pricked the dusk like slow stars. She tied up at the town landing—her berth, assigned by the harbormaster, permanent, hers—and went through the closing rituals that had become liturgy: wheel lashed, hatches dogged, logbook signed.

*I. Ashgrove, Master.* Her signature at the bottom of the day's page, in ink, in a book that would be kept, that anyone could ask to see. Records had frightened her once. Now she made one every day.

Maren was waiting at the foot of the ramp with her shawl over her head and a basket on her arm, because Maren had appointed herself Ivo's supply line and would not be dissuaded. "Bread, cheese, and the eggs you like. And a letter came for you. Postmarked Hallowford."

"Hallowford." Ivo took the envelope. Few people wrote to her; fewer used post. "Who from?"

"Tribunal clerk, by the look of the seal." Maren's eyes narrowed with the particular sharpness of a woman who had once recognized a wanted face across her own bar and chosen silence, and had never stopped being interested in consequences. "Bad news travels by post. Good news travels by mouth. So I'd brace for bad."

But it wasn't bad. It was better than bad, stranger than bad. The clerk's hand was the same trembling-excited hand that had signed her license, and the letter was short:

*Captain Ashgrove — The Tribunal has concluded its survey of the upper watershed. The fortified station at the source is to be decommissioned; the Archivist's records transferred to the Reading Room at Hallowford by spring. The question before the Tribunal is the disposition of the site itself. Some members favor demolition. Others propose a garrison. One member — I will not say which — has proposed that the station be given to you, to hold in trust, or to unmake entirely, as you judge fit. The Tribunal requests your attendance at the winter session. We are told you are the only living person who has stood in that room and understood what it was for.*

*Respectfully,*

*Clerk Second-Class Aldous Bream*

Ivo read it twice, standing under the landing lamp with moths ticking against the glass, while Maren watched her face with the patience of a woman who had waited out worse.

"Well?" Maren said.

Ivo folded the letter along its crease and slid it inside her coat, over her heart, where the compass used to ride. Upstream, beyond the last lamplight, the Vell ran dark and certain toward the hills, carrying snowmelt and memory and the first cold of the coming winter down from the place where it began—where it was beginning, even now, even tonight, drop by drop out of the living rock.

"They want me to decide what happens," she said, "where the water starts."

Maren snorted. "About time somebody competent got the job." She handed over the basket. "Come inside. You'll want supper before you go deciding anything."

Ivo followed her up the ramp, but at the top she stopped, once, and looked back at the river—her river now, charted honestly end to end, every sandbar and sunken parish and drowned bell marked plainly on the new charts they were printing in Hallowford, charts with no blank spaces left, except one.

Except the source. Which was hers to fill in.

She went inside to supper, and after supper she took out the old brass compass—her father's, recovered from the vault, its needle still swinging faithfully toward the headwaters whenever she let it—and laid it on the table beside the letter, and began, in the firelight, to draw the route she would walk.
