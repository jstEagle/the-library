## 22. Where the Water Begins

The mule track was gone.

Not ruined—gone, the way a sentence is gone once it's been spoken, absorbed into the air it was made of. Ivo stood at the base of the gorge with her pack straps cutting into her shoulders and looked up at where Odile's people had once kept a path, and found only scree and gorse and the memory of switchbacks she had climbed twice before: once in terror, once in triumph. The rebellion had pulled its ladders up behind itself on purpose. *Let no one come to the source who does not need to,* Odile had said, the last time they'd spoken, her voice like gravel poured slow. *And let no one stay who wants to own it.*

Ivo needed to come. She did not want to own it. She hoped that was enough.

She started climbing at first light, when the gorge still held its night-cold like water in a cup. Her boots knew the way even where the way didn't exist—heel here, toe there, the ledge below the overhang where you pressed your back to stone and let the river's breath move past you from below. Two years since the burning of the boat. Two years since the mail steamer went downstream heavy with copies and light with everything else, since Hallowford's sluice gates roared open under Silas Fen's hands, since Edmun Corvane rode up to burn a vault and found it already emptied into ten thousand hands. Two years, and her body still remembered every hold in this rock the way a tongue remembers a chipped tooth.

The gorge walls rose around her, gray-gold in the early sun, and the names were still there.

They had not been erased. That had been the fear, in the first months after—when Corvane loyalists still held three locks and the tribunal was nothing but a barge and a borrowed gavel—that someone would come upriver with chisels and fire and take the carved names off the stone the way the family had taken everything else off every page for a hundred years. But the riders never came. Or rather: they came as men, not soldiers, and they stood in front of ASHGROVE and MILLWRIGHT and the nine hundred others with their hats in their hands, and some of them wept, and none of them lifted a tool against the rock. You cannot chisel out what the whole valley has already read aloud.

Ivo stopped at the wall where the names ran deepest, where the old rebels had cut letters a hand-span tall so they'd outlast argument. She found the one she always found. She didn't touch it anymore. In the beginning she'd traced it every time, over and over, until the groove went smooth and dark with the oil of her finger, and then one day she understood that she was wearing her father down to nothing, polishing him away grain by grain, and she stopped. Now she just stood before ASHGROVE the way you stand before a grave that is also a door.

"I brought it," she said. Her voice fell into the gorge and came back smaller.

Above her, faintly, the sound of the source: not a roar, not yet, but a gathering—a long inhale of stone.

* * *

Her father had been a mapmaker before he was anything else, before he was a thief in the record and a dead man in fact. He had drawn the Vell River the way other men loved women: obsessively, inaccurately at the margins, beautifully everywhere it mattered. Ivo had grown up on his charts, tracing the blue ink line from Landfall Point all the way to a small starburst at the top of the page marked only *the spring*—as if the whole river, all its locks and landings and drowned parishes, all its barges and bribes and bodies, could be reduced to a place where water simply decided to begin.

He had walked this track twenty-six years ago with a satchel of blank paper and a compass that pointed not north but here. He had gotten as far as the second bend. Corvane riders had gotten further.

Ivo climbed through the morning. The sun cleared the eastern rim and poured down the gorge in a single golden sheet, and the stone gave back heat like a held breath released. Gorse bloomed yellow in the cracks. A hawk turned circles somewhere above the rim, riding the thermal that rose off the warm rock, and Ivo envied it its indifference. Once—she could admit it now, in the privacy of her own skull—she had wanted that indifference. There had been a season after the broadcast, after the tribunals, after the reading of names in market squares from Landfall to the headwaters, when she had lain awake in a rented room above Maren's tavern wishing she could go back to being nobody. No name anyone knew. No face on any notice. Just a woman and a boat and a debt, invisible as fog on the morning water.

But invisibility, she had learned, is only safety if everyone else stays visible. Anonymity was just another kind of archive—one entry, one person, sealed. And she had spent too long watching what happened to valleys that let other people do their remembering for them.

By midday she reached the shelf where the track had once bent inland, and she allowed herself the luxury of sitting, and ate bread and hard cheese and an apple gone soft on one side, and looked down the whole shining length of the gorge toward the country she had helped unwrite and rewrite. Far below, a thread of silver: the Vell, already wide enough down there to carry barges. Somewhere along it, a modest ferry with a new coat of green paint made its honest runs between two honest landings, piloted by a woman whose name was painted on the bow in plain white letters for the first time in her adult life.

*I. ASHGROVE.* It had taken her a week to stop flinching when passengers read it aloud.

* * *

The last hour was the hardest and the quietest. The gorge narrowed until she could have thrown a stone across it, and the walls closed overhead into a slot of blue sky, and the air changed—it cooled, and sweetened, and took on that mineral smell she had no word for except *beginning*. Water sounded differently here. Downstream it talked, argued, sang work songs; up here it whispered, the way people whisper in a room where something important is sleeping.

She came around the final shoulder of rock and there it was.

The spring did not look like much. It never did. A seep at the base of the northern wall, where the mountain's bones showed through like knuckles through skin—a dark wet stain spreading across pale stone, feeding a pool no wider than a millpond, clear as glass and cold as conscience. From the pool, a rill no deeper than her hand slid away southward, gathering itself, becoming a stream, becoming a creek, becoming—twenty miles and a hundred years later—the Vell River, with all its cargo of human doing.

This was where it began. Not the water. Everything else.

Ivo set down her pack and stood a while without moving, because the place deserved that, and because part of her ritual now was the standing. The Archivist had told her once, in the vault, in the last hour of that long strange night—you must come to the source not to take and not to leave, but to see how little it takes to start a river. One seep. One decision by the mountain to give up what it holds. Everything downstream is just what happens next.

She knelt at the pool's edge and drank. Cold hit her teeth and rang through her skull like a struck bell. Clean water. The first clean water. She had testified about that phrase at the tribunal—*the first clean water*—and watched the commissioners write it down, and understood that some sentences become landmarks.

Then she opened her pack and took out the compass.

It was smaller than she remembered, though it hadn't changed; she had. Brass gone soft-green at the seams, the crystal scratched from twenty years in a sea cave and two more in a coat pocket. On the reverse, engraved in her father's careful hand: *ASHGROVE.* The needle swung inside its housing, restless, alive, and settled—not north, never north—pointing up the last few feet of stone to the seep itself, as it always had, as it always would, faithful to a fault to the one thing it was made to find.

Her father had carried this to the second bend of the gorge and died within sight of what it wanted. The Corvanes had called him a thief for it, had written that lie into every ledger and lockhouse and schoolbook in the valley, and the lie had outlived them all by exactly as long as it took his daughter to carry the truth downstream in a stolen mail steamer. His name was clean now. Officially, legally, publicly clean—the tribunal had restored it in the same session where it dissolved the Syndicate's claims, and Fen had stood in the gallery with his hat off and his own account open on the table beside him, taking his reckoning like a man settling a bar tab he'd been avoiding for thirty years.

But a clean name in a record was paper. This compass was brass and blood and a needle that had never once lied to her.

"I don't need it anymore," she said to the pool, to the seep, to the man who wasn't there. "I know where things are."

That was the whole inheritance, really. Not the archive, not the ledger, not the power she had refused at the moment refusing it had cost her the most. Just this: knowing where things are. Where the bodies were buried, where the floods were ordered, where the maps had been redrawn—and where the water begins, which is to say, where every story starts, which is to say, in the ground, in common, belonging to whoever kneels down to drink.

She waded into the pool. The cold seized her calves, her knees, her thighs—breath-stopping, clarifying, a cold like the truth, unwelcome and unmistakable. At the center, where the seep fed up through the stones in threads of silver, there was a cleft in the rock, deep enough for a hand, deep enough for a compass. Old rebel custom, Odile had called it, or maybe it was older than the rebels, older than the Corvanes, older than names: you leave at the source whatever you can't afford to carry and can't bear to keep.

Ivo held the compass under the water a moment, letting the first clean water fill it, letting the needle spin free in the flood, pointing everywhere and nowhere, every direction at once—which was, she thought, the only honest way for a needle to point when it finally got home.

Then she pressed it into the cleft, into the dark seam of the mountain, and let go.

The brass caught one flash of light and went dim, and the water closed over it, and the seep accepted it the way the valley had learned to accept its history: without ceremony, without ownership, without any intention of giving it back or keeping it for itself. Let some child find it in fifty years. Let them wonder. Let them ask their grandmother, and let the grandmother tell it true, because telling it true was the one law left on this river that everyone actually obeyed.

* * *

She sat on the warm stone above the pool while the afternoon came down the gorge, and she watched the water gather.

That was all. That was the whole errand, the whole pilgrimage, the whole point: to sit and watch the first clean water gather. Drop by drop the seep sweated from the mountain's heart, beaded on the pale stone, ran together, trembled, let go. Each drop joined the pool and vanished into it, indistinguishable, unnamed, and the pool overflowed its lip in a thread of silver that went away south without looking back, toward Lock Nine and the drowned parish and Hallowford and Landfall Point and the sea cave where a different tide had once given a different woman her history back.

Memory was like that, she thought. It gathered the same way. Not in vaults—vaults were just pools that someone had claimed, fenced, taxed, poisoned. Memory gathered in the open, drop by drop, in kitchens and taverns and market squares, in old women's voices and children's questions, in the reading of names aloud on ordinary days for no reason except that the names were true. You couldn't dam it. The Corvanes had tried for a hundred years and all they'd built was pressure. You couldn't hoard it either—she knew, she had stood in the Reading Room with the master ledger under her hands and felt the temptation come up through her arms like current, *keep this and no one can ever hurt you again*—because hoarded memory goes stagnant, goes septic, becomes just another weapon with better manners. The only thing you could do with memory, the only thing that kept it clean, was let it run. Let it belong to everyone. Let it go downstream.

A drop swelled on the stone above the seep, hung, fell. The pool received it. The rill carried it away.

Ivo Ashgrove watched the water begin, and stayed until the light went long and gold in the slot of the gorge, and then shouldered her empty pack—for she had brought nothing up that she meant to carry down—and started home along the river, following it the easy way, the way it flowed, the way everything now flowed: downward, outward, toward every landing at once, past the mouths of towns that were learning to say their own names again, toward the wide bright water where the ferry waited with her name on the bow, plain as day, for anyone to read.
