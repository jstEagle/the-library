## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river told you things before it showed you them. Freya Joubert had learned that in his first season on the Vasseur and had never once been given reason to unlearn it.

This morning the river was telling him about sandbars. Not with signs — there were no signs, not yet, not in this light — but with a mood. The water ran flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of old tin, and where the current should have been talking to itself over the shallows above Kessel Bend, it was silent. Silent water meant deep water had gone somewhere else. Deep water going somewhere else meant the bars were building, shifting, lying to the charts again.

"Low water," Naledi said from the bow, though he hadn't asked her. She said it every morning now, the way other people remarked on weather. She was nineteen and had been on the Magpie for eight months, and she still found the river's moods worth announcing, as if they might be argued with.

"Low water," Freya agreed, and eased the throttle back a notch, and let the old boat drift her nose toward the channel he could feel rather than see.

The Magpie was forty years old if she was a day, a shallow-draught stern-wheeler someone had long ago bolted a diesel engine into, and she wore her history the way an old dog wears its scars: openly, without apology. Her paint was three colors faded into one. Her wheel was worn smooth as river stone where his hands had polished it for ten years. Ten years. He'd bought her with money that smelled of smoke and grief, renamed her for the bird that steals bright things and vanishes, and made himself into a man who delivered fuel drums and flour and engine parts to settlements whose names appeared on no chart worth trusting, and who asked nothing of anyone except to be left alone.

Invisibility, on the Vasseur, was a craft like any other. You learned which landings to tie up at after dark and which to pass in silence. You learned to pay your mooring fees in cash and never in scrip, because company scrip left a trail a man could follow for years. You learned the couriers' schedules so you could be elsewhere when they came through, and you learned the patrol boats' engine notes — the company's fast launches had a particular flat snarl, twin screws turning just slightly out of true, and once you'd heard it you heard it forever, the way you hear your own name across a crowded market.

He had been nobody for ten years, and he had gotten very good at it.

* * *

They took the bend at half throttle, and the bar announced itself exactly as the mood had promised: a long brown shoulder of new sand breaking the surface mid-channel, glistening, arrogant, sitting precisely where the 1911 survey chart insisted there was nine feet of clear water. The chart was wrong. The chart had probably been wrong for thirty years. Rivers redrew themselves every flood season and every drought, and only fools and company men trusted paper.

"Starboard side, past the willow snag," Naledi called, reading the water ahead of him the way he'd taught her — looking for the darker seams, the wrinkled glass where current funneled between hazards. "There's a channel behind it, maybe."

"Maybe." He swung wide anyway, gave the bar the respect it had earned, and felt the Magpie's hull whisper through water that had maybe a foot to spare beneath her plates. Somewhere below his boots the river touched the bottom of his world, and he let it, because that was the arrangement. You didn't fight the Vasseur in low water. You negotiated.

The settlement at Kessel Bend came up on the left bank by degrees: first the drying racks with their silver harvest of fish, then the stilt houses leaning companionably over the water, then the church with its roof of corrugated iron gone rust-red, then the children, who always materialized on the landing before the boat finished approaching, as if the river itself deposited them there. A dozen of them this morning, barefoot on the planks, shouting the boat's name.

*"Magpie! Magpie!"*

Freya lifted two fingers off the wheel — the whole salute, the entire conversation, as far as most people needed to know him — and brought her alongside with a kiss of rubber fender against timber. Old Mama Sesay stood at the head of the landing with her arms folded, which meant cargo today, and cargo meant haggling, and haggling meant an hour of his life spent pretending he wasn't in a hurry, which was its own kind of camouflage. A man who haggled was a man who stayed. A man who stayed was a man who belonged. Nobody looked twice at a man who belonged.

The morning went the way mornings went. Fuel drums rolled down the ramp into Mama Sesay's store shed. Flour and salt and lamp oil came aboard in trade, plus a crate of guavas that Naledi fell upon with the shameless joy of the young. Someone's outboard needed a spark plug; Freya had plugs. Someone's grandmother needed drops for her eyes; Naledi had drops, from a case of medical supplies Freya carried without ever quite explaining why. The sun climbed and burned the tin sky away, and the river turned the green-brown of strong tea, and for a while Freya Joubert was simply a supply captain at a landing, unremarkable, unremarked upon, exactly as designed.

It was almost noon when the courier came.

* * *

Freya saw the launch first — heard it first, the flat snarl of twin screws slightly out of true — while he was still cinching down the last drum strap. His hands did not stop moving. That was the first rule and he kept it: a man who flinched at a company boat was a man with something to flinch about, and he had spent ten years making sure his hands knew nothing, his face knew nothing, his whole body was a locked room with the furniture arranged to bore any visitor.

The launch swung in downstream of the Magpie, all white fiberglass and corporate gunmetal gray, and a young man stepped onto the landing with the particular posture of someone whose authority arrived by radio and would leave the same way. Company uniform: pale shirt, dark trousers, a lanyard with a badge that caught the sun. Behind him, the launch's crewman stayed at the rail and did not pretend to be anything other than what he was.

"Captain." The courier consulted a tablet, then Freya, in that order. "Joubert. F. Joubert, vessel *Magpie*, licensed for cargo, class two inland waters?"

"That's me." Freya set the strap down slowly. "If it's mooring fees, I settled with the district office in March."

"Not fees." The courier held out a sealed folio, heavy cream paper, the kind of envelope that cost more than Naledi made in a week. "A contract. Delivery and resupply, upper river. The Marchetti scientific expedition, currently operating at the escarpment headwaters. They've got a scheduled resupply window coming open and their regular contractor is— unavailable. The company is extending the opportunity to qualified independent operators."

The words were smooth and each one was a stone. *Upper river.* *Escarpment.* *Headwaters.* Three ways of saying the same thing: the one stretch of the Vasseur that Freya had spent a decade not going near.

"I don't run the upper river," Freya said. "Shoals. Gorge. My draught's wrong for it, my insurance wouldn't cover it, and my insurance doesn't exist. Anything else?"

"The compensation is—" the courier glanced at the tablet again, "—considerable. Triple standard rates, plus hazard supplement. The company values reliability, Captain. It remembers reliable operators."

"It remembered my license number off a tablet in four seconds," Freya said. "That's memory enough for me."

Something flickered behind the courier's eyes — not anger; these men weren't paid enough for anger. Recalculation, maybe. He looked at the folio in his own hand as if weighing it, and then, instead of withdrawing it, he set it down on the top of a fuel drum, square in the middle of the deck, deliberate as a man laying a card face-up.

"The offer stands through the end of the month. The escarpment station can arrange pickup at any landing above the falls line." He straightened his lanyard. "There's also a personal enclosure. From regional administration."

"I didn't ask for one."

"No," the courier agreed pleasantly. "You didn't."

He went back down the ramp. The launch backed water, swung, and went away upstream toward the falls line, its snarl fading until the river swallowed it, and the children on the landing resumed their noise as if a hole had opened and closed in the day. Mama Sesay watched the wake flatten out and then looked at Freya with an expression he pretended not to translate: *that one will come back, or worse ones will.*

Naledi came up from the hold wiping her hands on a rag. "Marchetti," she said. "That's the survey expedition, isn't it? The one with the permits? People at Ferro Landing were talking about them last month — scientists, mapping the upper watershed. Everyone said they'd be gone before flood season."

"Mm." Freya looked at the folio on the drum. Cream paper. Wax seal, even — actual wax, the company did love its little theater. "Everyone's gone before flood season. That's what makes it flood season."

"You're not going to take it." It wasn't quite a question. She had learned to read him the way he read the water, and he hadn't decided yet whether that pleased him.

"I'm not taking it." He picked the folio up anyway — because leaving it sitting on his deck was worse than holding it, the way a snag is worse seen than suspected — and weighed it in his hand. Heavy. Heavier than a contract needed to be.

He should have thrown it in the river right there. He knew that later, with the terrible clarity hindsight gives a man, the way you know exactly which rock you should have steered around only after you've hit it. Instead he carried it aft to the wheelhouse, set it on the chart table, and went back to work, and let it sit there all afternoon like a passenger who hadn't paid.

* * *

They ran downriver through the gold end of the day and tied up at dusk in the drowned-forest anchorage below Kessel, where the Magpie sat in six feet of still water among trees that had died standing when some old flood changed the river's mind, their gray trunks rising out of the current like the masts of a fleet sunk at anchor. Freya liked the spot. Nothing company-built could follow him in among the snags, and the dead trees broke the wind, and at night the frogs made a sound like the whole swamp breathing.

He ate. He checked the engine, checked the bilge, checked the anchor light. Naledi turned in early, in the forward bunk, humming something tuneless. And then it was just him and the wheelhouse and the chart table and the folio, and the frogs, and the moon coming up fat and white over the eastern bank.

He opened it because not opening it would have been worse. That was the trap of a thing like this; the company knew it too, or the courier did. A sealed packet sits in a man's sightline all day and does its work without being touched.

Inside: the contract, triple rates, hazard clause, indemnity language dense enough to hide a body in. He skimmed it with the flat attention of a man reading a menu in a restaurant he'll never eat at. And beneath it, a second sheet, smaller, older stock, creased from folding.

A photograph.

He knew before he turned it over. Some animal part of him knew from the weight of the paper, from the decade of silence that suddenly pressed on the wheelhouse like deep water on a hull. He turned it over anyway.

Tomas.

Tomas at twenty-six, standing on the foredeck of a company survey launch, squinting into a sun that had stopped existing eleven years ago. Tomas in a company shirt, and on the breast of it, pinned where a man pins a badge he's proud of, the brass hexagon of the Vasseur Consolidated Navigation & Lands Company. Tomas smiling the way he smiled when he thought he'd finally found the thing he was good at.

Freya put the photograph face-down on the chart table. Then he picked it up and looked again, because that was the other trap, the deeper one, and he fell into it the way he always fell into it, every time, on the rare occasions the past sent him mail.

His brother had been the clever one. That was the family joke, gentle, repeated at every table: Freya had the hands, Tomas had the head. Tomas had gone to the company with his beautiful survey certificates and his beautiful handwriting and come home talking about triangulation stations and datum points, about how the upper river was going to be *mapped properly at last*, about how a man could make a life measuring the world instead of hauling drums of diesel through it. Freya had stayed with the barges. Freya had stayed with the mud. And then one rain-season night the company had sent a letter with a wax seal, and the letter had used the phrase *boating accident*, and the river below the falls had given back a broken skiff and one boot and none of Tomas, and Freya had sold everything that couldn't fit aboard a derelict stern-wheeler, changed the name on his papers, and begun the long project of becoming no one.

Ten years. He had done the arithmetic a thousand times and it never came out different: the company had taken his brother and called it an accident, and Freya had answered by hiding. Hiding well. Hiding so well that the company had needed a decade and a courier with a tablet to find him — and had found him, casually, in four seconds, which meant they had never really lost him at all. Which meant the invisibility had been a courtesy they extended, the way you extend a leash.

The frogs breathed. The moon climbed. On the chart table, Tomas smiled up out of 1973 or whenever it was, wearing a badge, believing in maps.

Below the photograph, in the courier's neat hand, a single line had been added in ink:

*Regional administration believes you may wish to reconsider. Family records are available to interested parties.*

Family records. As if Tomas were a file. As if the boot the river gave back were a footnote. Freya read the line three times, and each time the anger came up in him like silt stirred from the bottom — at the courier, at the company, at the smiling boy in the badge, and last and worst, at himself, the careful man, the invisible man, who had spent ten years steering around the one snag on the river that had his brother's name on it.

He fed the contract to the galley stove and watched the indemnity clauses curl and go to ash. The photograph he did not burn. He told himself that was strategy — keep the evidence, know your enemy — but he slid it into the drawer under the chart table, beneath the tide almanac, face-down, and his hand lingered on the drawer a moment longer than strategy required.

Then he went out into the wheelhouse and stood at the window a long time, listening to the drowned forest creak, watching the moon lie down silver on water that was already, quietly, beginning to fall.

Three days later, at the Ferro Landing fuel depot, the expedition's scheduled radio call came due — and the Marchetti expedition did not answer.

And the day after that, a second courier found him, and this one was carrying triple pay.
