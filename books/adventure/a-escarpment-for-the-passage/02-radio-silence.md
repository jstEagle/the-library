## 2. Radio Silence

The call came at 0400, the way bad news always did on the Vasseur—arriving in the dark, when a man's defenses were down and the river was too quiet to hide behind.

Freya had been awake anyway. Low-water season made him an insomniac; every hour the river dropped another finger's width, and he could hear it happening, the Magpie's hull settling against the mud of her mooring like a tired animal lying down. He'd been sitting in the wheelhouse with a cold cup of coffee and the photograph from the courier's folder, though he'd promised himself he wouldn't look at it again.

Tomas, twenty-six years old, in a company uniform so crisp it looked ironed by machine. Company badge on his chest. That grin—the one that had gotten them both into trouble their whole childhood, the one Freya had spent ten years trying not to remember and failing most nights.

He put the photograph face-down on the chart table as the radio crackled.

*Magpie, Magpie, this is Vasseur Consolidated dispatch, Port Lachance. Come in, Magpie.*

Freya stared at the set. Three weeks since the courier's visit, three weeks of pretending that folder had never been slid under his cabin door. The company didn't call at 0400 for anything good, and they especially didn't call twice for anyone they considered a nobody—which was exactly what Freya Joubert had spent a decade making sure he was.

He let it ring through four cycles before his hand betrayed him and keyed the transmit.

*Magpie receiving. This is early, dispatch.*

*Captain Joubert.* A pause, long enough to be deliberate. *We have an amended contract for your consideration.*

"Joubert." Not the name he'd given the courier, not the name painted—faded, half-scraped—on the Magpie's stern. They knew. Of course they knew. A company that could put Tomas's face in a folder could put any face in any folder it wanted.

*Not interested,* Freya said. *Tell your man to stop wasting stamps.*

*Dr. Marchetti's expedition has missed two scheduled check-ins.*

That landed somewhere under Freya's ribs, in a place he didn't examine. He'd never met Ilse Marchetti—he'd read about her in the gazette clippings Naledi collected, the famous archaeologist, the woman who'd mapped ruins on three continents—but he knew what missing check-ins meant on this river. It meant nothing good, or it meant something the company wanted everyone to think was nothing good.

*People go quiet up there all the time,* he said. *Bad antenna weather in the gorge. Ask any pilot.*

*Two check-ins, Captain. And a third due tomorrow.* The dispatcher's voice was smooth as silted water. *The contract stands at triple rate. Supply run to the escarpment base camp, plus a search sweep along the expedition's last known corridor. Fuel, provisions, medical kit. We'd want you to look for signs of the survey launch.*

Triple rate. Freya did the arithmetic without wanting to: triple rate was a new propeller shaft, was the hull re-plated below the waterline, was a winter where he didn't have to take cargo he hated hauling for men he hated more. Triple rate was also a hook with a lot of line behind it, and he'd watched enough fish come over the gunwale to know how the reel worked.

*I'll think about it,* he said, which was a lie, because he already knew the answer, and then he killed the radio before the dispatcher could hear the lie in it too.

* * *

Naledi was up before dawn, as always, scrubbing the deck rails with a wire brush like the rust might surrender if she bullied it enough. She was nineteen, all elbows and certainty, and she'd come to him two seasons back off a fuel barge out of the delta settlements with no references and a look in her eye that said she'd swim after the boat if he turned her away. He'd hired her because she worked like the devil and asked no questions about his past. He kept her because somewhere along the way she'd become the closest thing to family the Magpie carried.

She straightened when he came down the gangway, brush in hand, and read his face the way she read water—one glance, everything taken in.

"That was dispatch," she said. Not a question.

"Company business."

"Company business at four in the morning." She leaned the brush against the rail. "It's Marchetti, isn't it. Everyone at the depot's talking. Two missed check-ins." Her eyes went bright in the way they did when a thing excited her and she hadn't yet decided whether to be ashamed of it. "They're offering you the run."

"They're offering me a hook."

"So take the fish off the hook and keep the fish." She said it fast, the way she said everything. "Captain. Triple rate. You know what that means for the hull. We could do the plating in Lachance this year instead of never. And—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide to push through the hesitation, because Naledi never left a hesitation unpushed, "—and if something happened to them, someone should go. Someone who isn't company."

"There's no such thing as 'isn't company' on this river anymore. You take their money, you're theirs."

"You took their fuel at every depot from here to the falls."

"That's different."

"It isn't, and you know it isn't." She crossed her arms. She had a way of standing that made her seem taller than she was, feet planted like she expected the deck to argue. "You've been different since the courier came. You barely sleep. You keep looking at that folder."

"I don't keep a folder."

"You keep it under your mattress, Captain. I change the bedding."

Freya looked at her—at the young, stubborn, unbending face of her—and felt the strange vertigo of realizing that someone aboard had learned to see him clearly. Ten years of careful invisibility, undone by a girl with a wire brush.

"The Marchetti woman," he said slowly, "is famous. Famous people have famous friends. If she's dead up there, whoever finds the body gets a name. Names are trouble."

"If she's dead up there and nobody goes, the company writes whatever story it wants and nobody's alive to say different." Naledi shrugged. "You always say the river keeps what the river keeps. Maybe this time it wants to give something back."

"I never say that."

"You say it constantly. It's your third-favorite saying, after 'check the bilge' and 'don't touch my charts.'"

Despite himself—a thing that happened around Naledi with irritating frequency—Freya almost smiled. Then he looked past her, upstream, where the Vasseur narrowed into the haze and the escarpment waited somewhere beyond the drowned forests and the dead channels, a blue-gray wall on the horizon that no honest map bothered to draw.

His instinct said stay hidden. His instinct had kept him alive for ten years, and it spoke now with its usual flat certainty: *Nothing upriver but snags and history, and both of them drown men.* But instincts were built on old rivers, and the courier had put Tomas's face under his door, and the company had called him *Joubert* at four in the morning, and somewhere above the falls a woman who'd gone looking for the truth of this river had stopped answering her radio.

"Prep the forward hold," he heard himself say. "We'll need the crates shifted aft for ballast. And get the searchlight wired—I want it working before we clear the depot."

Naledi's whole face lit like a lamp. "Yes, Captain."

"And Naledi." She paused halfway to the hold. "This is a supply run. In and out. We find what we find, we report what we're paid to report, and we come home. Nothing else."

"Of course, Captain," she said, in the tone of someone agreeing to nothing whatsoever.

* * *

They took on fuel at Kilometre Forty-two, the last depot before the narrows, a floating platform of rotting pilings and corrugated tin run by a one-eyed Fulani named Bakary who'd traded on the river longer than Freya had been alive and remembered every boat that ever passed, including ones that had sunk.

Bakary pumped the diesel himself, slow as syrup, and talked the way he always talked, sideways, watching the gauge instead of his customer.

"Heard you're going up," he said.

"Heard wrong."

"Mm." The pump gurgled. "Two boats went up past the narrows this season. The survey launch in the spring, and a company patrol skiff three weeks ago, running dark, no lights." He glanced up with his good eye. "Running dark on my stretch of river. That's either smugglers or men who don't want to be counted."

"Could be both."

"Could be." Bakary shut off the pump at the agreed figure and wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than it had been cloth. "My cousin's wife's people fish the oxbow below the escarpment. They say the water's been strange. Cold coming down in the warm months, like something upstream changed the way the current sits." He shrugged, a small motion, giving the words nowhere to land. "Old water. Old country up there. My grandmother said the maps stop where the remembering starts."

"Your grandmother sound like Naledi?"

"Your girl has sense. Rare in the young." Bakary took Freya's scrip, held it up to the light out of habit, and gave it back. "On credit, Captain. Pay me when you come down. Dead men tip badly, and I like my customers alive."

It was the closest thing to a blessing the river offered, and Freya took it as one.

* * *

They cleared the depot at midday and ran the narrows through the afternoon, the channel threading between sandbars that shifted with every flood and would shift again before spring. Freya stood at the wheel with the new charts open on his knees—company charts, printed in Port Lachance, accurate to the kilometre mark and then increasingly, politely vague. Past the narrows the contour lines thinned like a receding hairline. Past the oxbow they simply stopped, replaced by a blank wash of paper and a small printed legend: *INSUFFICIENT SURVEY DATA.*

Ten years he'd run this river and never once wondered why the blankness began where it began. Now, with Tomas's photograph folded in his breast pocket and triple-rate scrip in the lockbox, he found he couldn't look at the empty space without hearing Bakary's grandmother: *the maps stop where the remembering starts.*

Naledi worked the deck, lashing crates, testing the searchlight, doing three jobs at once with the restless energy of youth. Twice he caught her at the rail with binoculars, scanning the banks ahead, and once he caught her at the radio—not transmitting, just listening, headphones on, turning the dial through the static in slow increments.

"Anything?" he called.

"Mostly static." She didn't take the headphones off. "I've been sweeping the expedition's frequencies. Marchetti's team ran on 6210 kilocycles, scheduled calls at six and eighteen hundred. I thought maybe—"

"Maybe what?"

She pulled the phones down around her neck. In the fading light her face had gone serious in a way that made her look older, and younger, both at once. "Maybe somebody's still calling. Maybe they can't hear an answer, but they're still calling." She looked upstream, at the first stars pricking out above the escarpment's invisible bulk. "If it were me out there, Captain, I'd keep keying the set until my battery died. Just so someone would know where to look."

Freya said nothing. The engine thudded beneath them, steady as a heart, carrying them upriver into the blank space on the map, toward whatever the river had decided to keep.

At 1800 exactly, Naledi made the scheduled sweep one more time. Static, static, static—and then, buried so deep in the noise that Freya couldn't have sworn to it, something rhythmic. Three clicks. A pause. Three clicks again.

Not words. Not voice. But not random, either.

Naledi looked up at him across the wheelhouse, headphones half-off, eyes wide, and neither of them said what they were both thinking: that someone up there was still calling, and that whoever answered first wouldn't necessarily be a friend.

Freya gave the Magpie more throttle, and the dark water of the upper river slid by, cold and quick, like something hurrying home.
