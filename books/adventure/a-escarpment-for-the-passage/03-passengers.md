## 3. Passengers

The fuel depot at Kessel's Landing was less a place than an argument between the river and the company's ambitions—a shelf of corrugated tin and rusted drums bolted to the last solid bank before the channel narrowed into drowned timber. The Magpie came alongside at mid-morning with her tanks down to fumes and her captain in one of his careful moods.

Freya had been careful for ten years. Careful was a discipline, like steering: you didn't think about it, you simply did it, and it kept you alive. He paid the depot man in cash—never scrip, never credit, nothing that left a name on a ledger—and he topped off his drums while Naledi hosed the mud off the foredeck with the particular enthusiasm of someone who'd spent three weeks listening to him say *we're not going* and then watch him go anyway.

"You could've just admitted you wanted the money," she'd said when they'd left the delta behind.

"I wanted the search fee," he'd said. "The money's incidental."

She'd laughed at that, the way young people laugh when they know they're being lied to and have decided to enjoy it.

Now he stood on the depot's sagging planking, counting crates of diesel into the Magpie's hold, and felt the day change its weather before he saw why. It wasn't the sky—the sky was the flat white glare of the dry season, same as every other morning. It was the depot man, a leathery half-caste named Pieter who'd sold Freya fuel for six years without ever once asking where he was bound. Pieter had gone still behind his counter, the way small animals go still, and his eyes were fixed on the track that climbed away from the landing through the scrub.

A vehicle was coming down it. A good one. Company grey.

Freya's hands did not stop working. That was part of the discipline too. He finished cinching the last drum's strap, straightened, and turned to look with the mild, incurious expression of a man who saw company vehicles all the time, which he did, and had no feelings about them whatsoever, which he didn't.

It was a transport truck, canvas-backed, unmarked except for the dust pattern of hard use. It stopped at the head of the ramp and two people got out before the driver even killed the engine.

The first was a woman. Tall, sunburnt past any cosmetic damage into something geological, wearing expedition khaki gone grey with wear and a week or more of living rough. She moved like someone who had learned recently that her body was a liability and was still negotiating the terms. Her hair was tied back with what looked like a strip torn from a map. She carried nothing but a satchel worn crosswise, and she carried it the way soldiers carry documents—close to the ribs, hand on it even while walking.

The second was a man. Big—not fat, big, the way cargo is big—with skin the colour of the river's deep pools and a face that gave up absolutely nothing. He wore a plain work shirt and trousers, both clean, which after a week in the bush marked him as strange as a wedding suit. He carried two rucksacks without visible effort and set them down on the planking with the gentleness of a man handling eggs or explosives.

"Captain." The woman crossed the ramp toward him. Her voice was low and roughened, an accent from somewhere far north of the river, vowels flattened by exhaustion. "Joubert. The Magpie. You're resupplying the Marchetti expedition."

It wasn't a question, and that was the first thing wrong with her. Nobody at Kessel's Landing knew his business. He bought fuel here the way other men bought sin—quietly, regularly, and without witnesses.

"Depot's open," Freya said. "Fuel's for sale. Passage isn't."

"I'm Dr. Marchetti."

He looked at her. She let herself be looked at, which took a certain kind of nerve, because looking at her meant looking at the split in her lower lip, healing crooked; the rope burn circling one wrist; the way her weight sat all on one hip as if the other leg had recently been asked to do more than it liked.

"The Marchetti expedition," Freya said, "is missing. Three weeks of missed radio calls. The company's paying me a bonus to find out if anyone's alive to feed."

"And here I am," she said. "Alive. Imagine."

* * *

Pieter had retreated into his shed and was very busy with something in there that made no noise. The truck driver stayed behind his wheel, staring straight ahead, a man being paid to see nothing. That left the four of them on the planking in the white glare: Freya, the woman, the silent man, and Naledi, who had come to stand at the Magpie's rail with a coiled hose in her hands and her whole body angled forward like a dog's.

"Your launch is at the bottom of the river," Freya said. "Or your team is. Or both. And you want me to believe you walked out of the escarpment country alone, through forty miles of drowned forest, to the last fuel depot on the navigable river, precisely on the day a supply boat happened to be here."

"Yes," said Dr. Marchetti.

"That's your story."

"That's the outline of my story. The details are expensive. You couldn't afford them."

Behind her, the big man hadn't moved. He stood over the rucksacks with his hands loose at his sides, and his eyes—Freya made himself catalog this, because a decade of invisibility had taught him that eyes were the one thing people couldn't disguise—his eyes were doing arithmetic. Not on Freya. On the river. On the treeline. On the angles of cover along the bank, the way a man looks at a room when he's deciding how many people he could stop from leaving it.

"Who's he?" Freya asked.

"My assistant."

"Name?"

"Okonkwo."

"Does he talk?"

"When there's something worth saying."

Freya wiped his hands on his trousers and looked downriver, back the way he'd come, three hundred miles of brown water and floating forest, and then upriver, toward the escarpment, where the charts went vague and the company's interest went sharp. Three weeks of silence. Triple pay. A photograph of Tomas in a company badge sitting in his cabin drawer like a splinter working toward the heart.

And now this. Either the luckiest woman on the Vasseur or the most dangerous.

"Show me something," he said.

"Something?"

"Proof you're Marchetti. Anyone can be Marchetti. I could be Marchetti."

For the first time, something moved behind her face—not quite a smile; more like the memory of one, surfacing through wreckage. She dug in the satchel, slowly, letting him see every movement, and produced a laminated card. Company issue. Expedition authorization, signed by three names Freya recognized from freight manifests, with a photograph that was unmistakably her, five kilos healthier, taken against a backdrop of survey equipment.

He handed it back. "Laminates can be stolen."

"So can boats. Are you refusing to sell me yours?"

"I'm considering it."

"Then consider this instead." She put the card away and looked at him directly, and up close her eyes were the pale grey-green of shallow water over limestone, and very tired, and very awake. "Tomas Joubert surveyed the upper reaches in '61 and '62. His soundings are still the only ones the company uses above the gorge. He used a green notebook with a brass clasp and he chewed the end of his pencil, and he once told me—" she paused, and something flickered across her face, some recalculation, "—he once told me the river above the falls sang in a key no chart could hold. Is that the brother you don't talk about, Captain? Because you have his jaw, and you flinched when I said his name, and you've been standing with your weight on your back foot since I stepped onto this ramp."

The planking creaked. Somewhere in the shed, Pieter dropped something and swore at it with great feeling.

Freya found that his mouth had gone dry. "You never met Tomas."

"No," she agreed quietly. "I never met Tomas."

* * *

He told himself later that he took her aboard for the money, and he almost believed it, the way a man almost believes the current carried him somewhere he chose to steer.

The truth was messier. The truth was that she knew things about Tomas that Freya himself didn't know—that he'd talked about the river's music, that he'd had opinions about the upper reaches, that he'd existed in the years after his death as anything other than a name on a coroner's report—and Freya wanted those things the way a drowning man wants air, badly enough to stop thinking about what else might be in the water.

Company scrip changed hands—her notes were crisp, high-denomination, drawn on an account that would raise questions at any honest bank, which was perhaps why she was spending them at none. Two hundred for passage to the escarpment base camp. Another fifty, she said, for discretion.

"Discretion about what?" Freya asked, pocketing it.

"About everything," she said. "That's rather the point of discretion."

They cast off within the hour. The truck ground back up the track and vanished, and Pieter emerged from his shed long enough to accept payment and avoid eye contact, and then the Magpie was pushing upstream against the last of the dry-season current, the drowned forest closing around her like a green throat.

Naledi found him at the wheel an hour out. She leaned against the rail beside him, watching the banks slide by, and spoke without moving her lips much, a habit she'd developed from him without either of them noticing.

"She's lying," Naledi said.

"Obviously."

"About all of it?"

"About most of it. The parts she isn't lying about are worse."

Naledi considered this. "The big one hasn't sat down since we left. He's been standing at the stern watching our wake for two hours."

"Counting who might be following."

"Do you think someone is?"

Freya adjusted the throttle a fraction, feeling the engine's note settle, feeling the old boat answer him the way she always did, faithfully, grumbling, hers and his alone. "I think," he said, "that a woman walks forty miles through flooded bush with a split lip and a burned wrist, and the first thing she does at civilization isn't drink water or sit down. It's buy passage deeper into the country she just escaped from. That's not flight, Naledi. That's a return trip."

"So why take her?"

Because she knows things about Tomas. Because triple pay. Because the photograph in the drawer. Because the river above the falls sings in a key no chart can hold, and some traitor part of him had heard that sentence and recognized it as true.

"Because she's paying," he said.

Naledi looked at him for a long moment with her grandfather's patience and her own grandmother's contempt for nonsense, and then went forward to check the lines, and let him keep his lie.

* * *

Evening came down through the drowned forest in bands of copper and violet, the water going mirror-still between the dead trees, so that the Magpie seemed to travel through a doubled world, hull and reflection meeting at the waterline like a seam. Freya anchored in a side lagoon off the main channel—deep water, two escape routes, sightlines he'd chosen a decade ago and never needed until now—and cooked rice and dried fish on the deck stove while the first insects rose in their evening columns.

Marchetti ate like a woman who had forgotten food was optional and was relearning it with concentration. Okonkwo ate little and watched much. Naledi watched them both and pretended to study a knot chart.

"Base camp," Freya said, when the plates were cleared. "That's where you want dropping. The company's camp, under the escarpment."

"Yes."

"You understand that walking into the company's camp is the last thing a missing person should do. Missing persons stay missing. It's safer."

Her fork stopped. For a moment the lamplight caught her face and he saw what lived under the exhaustion—not fear exactly, though fear was in there, layered like sediment. Purpose. Hard, load-bearing purpose, the kind that holds a structure up after everything decorative has burned.

"Captain Joubert," she said, "when we reach the escarpment, I'm going to ask you to do something dangerous, and I'm going to pay you a great deal of money, and you're going to refuse, and then I'm going to tell you something about your brother that will make you wish you'd refused harder. So let's not pretend tonight is simple. Let's just eat."

Silence held the deck. Out in the black water something large turned over and settled, unhurried, indifferent.

"And Okonkwo?" Freya said. "What's his part in the not-simple?"

The big man looked up for the first time all evening. His eyes in the lamplight were calm and old and entirely without apology.

"He reads," Okonkwo said.

His voice was deep and quiet, a voice built for libraries or confessions, and he said it the way other men announce a rank or a crime.

"Reads what?" Freya asked.

Okonkwo glanced at Marchetti. Marchetti gave a single fractional nod, permission granted, and the big man returned his gaze to the dark water beyond the rail, and said nothing further, and picked up his plate, and washed it, thoroughly, at the stern bucket, while the river breathed around them and the drowned forest stood silent as a congregation.

Freya lay awake that night in his bunk with the photograph of Tomas on his chest, the company badge glinting dully whenever lightning-less heat lightning pulsed far off over the escarpment. Below him, in the hold, two passengers slept—or didn't—who had appeared out of the wilderness carrying more trouble than luggage.

He thought about turning back. He genuinely thought about it, running the numbers the way he ran the rapids: depth, current, risk, margin. But the numbers wouldn't close. Every route ended at the same snag: *she knows things about Tomas.*

Toward dawn he heard footsteps overhead, soft, deliberate—Okonkwo, walking the deck perimeter on some private watch—and then, fainter, from the stern, the small scratch-and-whisper of a pen, and he lay in the dark and understood that whatever was being written down aboard his boat, it was being written for someone who wasn't him.

The Magpie swung gently on her anchor. Upstream, days away now, the escarpment waited under its heat lightning, holding whatever it held.

Freya closed his eyes and let the current of sleep take him, knowing—as he'd known since Kessel's Landing, since the photograph, since the first lie he'd told himself about the money—that he was no longer piloting this journey so much as being carried by it.
