## 4. What the River Keeps

The drowned forest began an hour after dawn, and Freya felt it the way a man feels a change in weather—through his teeth first.

The Magpie slid off the main channel and into water that had no business being where it was. Ten years ago this had been dry ground: a shelf of hardwood forest between the river and the escarpment's lower slopes, thick enough that the company had run logging roads through it before deciding the timber wasn't worth the freight. Then the dam at Kessel Bend went up, illegal on paper and permanent in fact, and the river had climbed into the trees and stayed there. Now the trunks rose out of brown water like the masts of a fleet that had gone down standing up, their bark stripped gray by years of rising and falling, their crowns long since rotted to bare black fingers against the sky.

Freya hated this stretch. Not for the danger of it—the channels were actually deep here, deeper than anywhere else on the upper river—but because of what it did to sound. The dead trees killed the echo. A shout went out flat and died without answer. An engine carried farther than it should have, unbroken by any bank, and there was nothing to hide behind except distance itself. On the open river you could read the water. In here the water was just a mirror lying about the sky, and every trunk was identical to the last one, and a man could travel all morning and be unable to swear he'd moved at all.

He kept the throttle low and steered by memory and by the brass compass chained to the wheel binnacle, threading the Magpie down a corridor of drowned oaks that he'd mapped himself over three careful seasons, back when mapping anything still felt safe.

"Depth's good," Naledi called from the bow. She stood with the sounding pole planted like a lance, reading the water ahead of them. "Bottom's soft. No snags till the second bend."

"Keep your voice down anyway."

She glanced back at him, one eyebrow up. She was nineteen and had grown up on a river where shouting across the water was how neighbors talked, and she thought his caution was a superstition, an old man's ritual. He hadn't told her yet that it wasn't a ritual. He hadn't told her that the last time he'd come through this forest, four days ago now, he'd cut his engine twice because he'd heard another engine somewhere behind him, idling, patient, never quite closing the distance.

The company patrol boat from the fuel depot. Or something else. He didn't know, and not knowing was its own kind of cargo, heavy and shifting in the hold.

Ilse Marchetti sat amidships on the supply crates, her field journal open on her knees, writing. She'd been writing most of the morning, in bursts, pen moving fast, then stopping, then starting again. Okonkwo sat opposite her and wrote nothing. He sat the way ballast sits—still, dense, necessary—and watched the banks. He had been watching the banks since they'd left the depot, and Freya had begun to keep count of how often the man's eyes moved from one side of the channel to the other. It was always in threes. Left bank, right bank, left bank again. Like a man counting exits.

"Captain." Ilse didn't look up from her journal. "How far to where you found the launch?"

"Who says I found it?"

"You've been steering toward it since dawn. You haven't checked the chart once." She turned a page. "You found it."

Freya said nothing. The engine muttered under the deck plates. Somewhere off to port, a bird that should not have been there—a weaver, a dry-land bird—called twice from a drowned crown and fell silent, as if embarrassed by its own mistake.

"Two hours," he said finally. "Maybe less if the channel hasn't silted."

"Good." She wrote something else, quick, and closed the journal. When she looked up, her face was composed, professional, a doctor's bedside calm. "There may be bodies. I want to say that now rather than have your deckhand find out by surprise."

"Naledi grew up on the Vasseur. She's seen floaters."

"I don't mean floaters." Ilse's eyes were pale gray, almost colorless in the flat light under the trees. "I mean my people. If the launch is where I think it is, some of them may still be with it."

* * *

They found the launch forty minutes later, and it was worse than bodies.

Naledi saw it first—a pale shape wedged in the shallows where the drowned forest thickened into an actual island, a hummock of original ground the floodwaters had never quite claimed. "Wreck," she called softly, and then, as the Magpie came around the last screen of trunks: "Oh."

The expedition's launch had been a good boat. Freya could see that even now, even in what was left of it. Thirty feet, aluminum hull, twin outboards, the kind of purpose-built river craft a well-funded survey could afford—one of maybe six on the whole Vasseur. It lay heeled over on its starboard side in two feet of water, half sunk in the soft bottom, and it had been shot to pieces.

Not riddled. Shot. Deliberately, methodically, by someone who had known exactly what they were doing. The holes ran in lines along the port side, tight clusters at the waterline, a second row at chest height, a third through the wheelhouse glass. Every hole had been placed. Someone had stood on a stable platform and fired controlled bursts into a drifting boat until it stopped being a boat.

And it had been stripped. That was the part that made the skin crawl up the back of Freya's neck. Bandits took things—that was the whole of banditry, the entire philosophy of it. But these bandits had taken the engines, the radios, the fuel drums, the canvas, the life vests, the tool kit, the flares, the anchor line, even the cleats, judging by the bright unpainted scars on the gunwales. They had left the hull, the seats, a shattered depth sounder, and a single rubber boot floating in the bilge.

Bandits who stripped a boat that clean would have taken the boat.

"Bandits," Ilse said. She was standing at the rail, and her voice was level, but Freya heard the seam in it, the place where the word had been fitted over something else. "River pirates work this stretch. Everyone knows it."

"Everyone knows it," Freya agreed, and let it lie there between them.

He brought the Magpie alongside as close as the shallows allowed and dropped the stern anchor to hold them off the wreck. Naledi was already at the rail with the boathook, and when he didn't stop her, she hooked the launch's bow rail and drew it closer, turning the dead boat slowly so they could read it.

Okonkwo had not moved from his seat. But he had stopped watching the banks. For the first time since the depot, the big man was looking at something specific—at the wheelhouse, at the cluster of holes there—and his face, which had been carved from the same stillness as everything else about him, had changed. Not much. A tightening around the mouth. The look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish.

"There's blood," Naledi said quietly. She was leaning out over the water, one hand white-knuckled on the boathook. "In the wheelhouse. Old blood—it's gone black. And—" She stopped. Swallowed. "There's a handrail bent outward. From inside. Like someone pried themselves out through the glass after."

"Enough." Freya's voice came out harder than he intended. "Back aboard."

She came back. She was a river kid; she knew what a butchered boat meant, and she knew better than to ask questions while standing in the open with her voice carrying across flat water that ate echoes. But he saw her hands shaking as she coiled the boathook line, and he saw her look at Ilse Marchetti once, quickly, the way you look at a stranger who has just become a suspect.

"Six people on the launch when I last saw it," Ilse said. Her pen was in her hand. Freya realized she'd taken out her journal again, was writing, and something about that—the steady script, the tidy hand, the documentation of her own crew's massacre—made his jaw tighten. "Myself and Mr. Okonkwo separated from the group at the second gorge, on foot, to scout a portage route. The others continued by water. When we reached the rendezvous, they weren't there. We waited two days. Then the patrol boats started coming upriver, and we moved."

"Patrol boats," Freya said. "Company?"

"The company has patrols on every river in its concession. That's not remarkable."

"No," Freya said. "It isn't." He looked at the wreck again. At the neat rows of holes. At the stripped fittings and the untouched hull. "Here's what's remarkable, Doctor. Bandits don't shoot a boat full of holes and leave it. A hull that sound is worth more than everything they took off it. They'd have towed it home and sold it whole. Whoever did this didn't want the boat. They wanted the boat destroyed and the destruction blamed on someone poor enough to rob it."

The silence under the drowned trees was total. Even the misplaced weaver had gone quiet.

"That's speculation," Ilse said.

"That's the river," Freya said. "I've lived on it ten years. I know what robbery looks like, and I know what erasure looks like, and this—" he nodded at the wreck, at the black blood drying in the wheelhouse seams, "—this is erasure. Somebody wanted your crew gone so thoroughly that even the story of how they died would be a lie."

Okonkwo spoke then. His first words since the depot, and Freya nearly missed them, they were so quiet.

"Not all of them."

Ilse's pen stopped.

"What?" Freya said.

Okonkwo lifted his chin slightly, toward the island's interior, toward the hummock of dry ground above the waterline where the wreck had drifted aground. "Not all of them are accounted for in the boat. Some got off. Some made it to shore." His voice was deep and slow, each word set down like a stone. "There will be tracks. There may be a camp. There may be—" he paused, and for a moment Freya thought the man was going to say *there may be survivors*, but what he said instead was: "—signs of which direction they went. Toward the escarpment. Or away from it."

"And which way did they go?" Ilse asked, too fast, too sharp.

Okonkwo looked at her for a long moment. Whatever passed between them, it was old, and it was not friendly.

"We'll know when we look," he said.

* * *

Naledi

Naledi had grown up believing the river kept everything. Her grandmother used to say it: the Vasseur forgets nothing, child, it only holds things below the surface until it's ready. Floods brought back what the droughts hid. Droughts brought back what the floods swallowed. Every year the water gave up some new thing—a canoe lost thirty years ago, a cow, a wedding chest, once, impossibly, a church bell—and her grandmother would stand on the bank and nod as if the river were simply returning borrowed property.

So when Captain Joubert sent her across to the island to read the ground, she understood she wasn't looking for evidence. She was looking for what the river was ready to give back.

The hummock was maybe forty meters across, old ground, real ground, shaded by three living figs that had somehow kept their roots above the flood line. She crossed the shallows carefully, boots on, machete loose in her belt, and stepped onto soil for the first time in two days, and the ground felt strange and solid under her, like meeting someone after a long silence.

She found the first sign within five minutes: a boot print in the dried mud near the fig roots. Big. A man's. Then another, smaller, beside it. Two people, walking fast—she could tell from the stride length and the way the prints dug in at the toe. Fleeing.

Then she found the tarp.

It was wedged under a root, army green, company issue—the same gray-green as the tarps on the patrol boats, the same as the ones covering cargo at the depots. Stenciled on the folded edge, half worn away: **VRC SURVEY DIVISION**. Vasseur River Concession. Not bandit gear. Not pirate loot. Company property, abandoned on an island where the company's own charts said there was no island worth naming.

Under the tarp: a rucksack, slashed open and emptied. A water bottle, dented. And a notebook, swollen with damp, its cover gone soft as cloth.

She shouldn't have opened it. She knew that even as she did it. But her grandmother also used to say that a thing the river gives you, you're obliged to look at, or you'll never know why it was given.

Most of the pages had dissolved into pulp. But the middle pages, protected by the folded cover, were legible. Survey notes. Coordinates in a neat engineer's hand. Depth soundings. And in the margins, in the same handwriting but pressed harder, faster, the ink gouged into the paper:

*they are not measuring the river they are measuring US*

*V. ordered the upper sheets burned. I refused. God forgive me, I should have refused sooner.*

*I have seen the source charts. The old ones. They knew. They always knew.*

Naledi's mouth went dry. She read the last legible page three times, because the name at the bottom of it was a name she'd heard the captain say only once, late at night, drunk enough to talk, a name he'd said like a wound he was showing her on purpose so she'd understand why he never talked:

*Tomas.*

She wrapped the notebook in the tarp, tucked it under her arm, and stood very still among the fig roots, listening to the water move through the drowned trees.

Someone had survived this island. Someone had walked off it carrying a company tarp and a head full of terrible knowledge, heading—she checked the prints again, following them to the hummock's northern edge, where they ended at the waterline in a scatter of disturbed mud—heading upriver. Toward the escarpment.

Toward wherever they were going.

And somewhere behind her, faint through the dead air of the flooded forest, she heard an engine. Not theirs. Theirs was behind her, idling low. This one was upstream, beyond the screen of trunks, moving slowly, the way a man moves when he's searching a room.

Naledi waded back to the Magpie fast, the tarp held high, and she didn't call out until she was close enough that her voice wouldn't carry.

* * *

Freya read the notebook by the light of the deck lamp while the others pretended not to watch. The pages crumbled a little under his thumbs. He read the marginalia twice. He read the name at the end of it until the letters stopped meaning anything.

Tomas's handwriting. Tomas's abbreviations, his little shorthand for soundings, the way he crossed his sevens. Freya would have known it blindfolded, in the dark, underwater. His brother had been on this island. His brother—or his brother's ghost in the shape of whoever had copied his field habits—had walked north into the drowned forest carrying the company's secrets, and the company's tarp, and had written *God forgive me* in a margin, and then the river had closed over the rest.

"Captain." Ilse's voice, careful. "That notebook is expedition property. Survey records. I'll need it for—"

"For what?" Freya didn't look up. "For the report you'll file? For the sponsors?"

"For the record," she said, and the phrase hung there, and both of them heard what it really meant. *For whatever version of events survives.*

He closed the notebook and put it inside his coat, against his chest, and looked at her, and she looked back, and neither of them blinked, and the trust between them—which had never been trust, only a transaction wearing trust's clothes—cracked down the middle like ice in thaw.

* * *

Night came early under the drowned trees. Freya anchored the Magpie mid-channel, well off both banks, running a single anchor light and nothing else, and posted the watches in whispers. The distant engine had faded by dusk—upriver, past them, gone wherever it was going—but none of them relaxed. Okonkwo took first watch with a shotgun across his knees that Freya hadn't known he had, and hadn't asked about.

Freya couldn't sleep. Around midnight he gave up trying, pulled on his boots, and climbed quietly into the cockpit to check the anchor rode, and that was when he saw the light.

A small orange bloom, low on the aft deck, behind the fuel drums. The smell reached him a heartbeat later: paper burning, that particular sweet-bitter reek of ink going up with the page.

He moved without deciding to. Ten years of staying invisible had built certain reflexes into him, and one of them was that you never announced yourself to a fire you didn't start. He came around the drums on silent feet and there she was—Dr. Ilse Marchetti, kneeling in the glow of a small flame cupped in a tin plate, feeding it pages torn from her field journal, one at a time, watching each one catch and curl and go to ash before tearing free the next.

She didn't hear him until the light of the deck lamp fell across her shoulders. Then she went rigid, and her hand moved—fast, instinctive—to sweep the remaining pages off her lap and into the fire, and Freya's own hand shot out and caught her wrist.

For a moment neither of them breathed. The flame hissed and guttered. Ash rose between them, turning slowly, settling on the deck like gray snow.

"Let go," she said.

"What are you burning, Doctor?"

"Field notes. Redundant copies. Housekeeping."

"At midnight. In secret. With your back to the wheelhouse." He tightened his grip—not cruelly, but completely. "Show me the last one."

"There is no last one. You're holding my wrist, not my journal."

But her eyes flicked, just once, toward the gap between the fuel drum and the rail, and Freya released her, stepped past her, and picked up the slim black book lying there. It was heavier than a journal should be. Bound in oilcloth. Tied with cord.

"Don't," Ilse said, and her voice cracked on the word, and for the first time since the depot he heard the woman underneath the composure, and she sounded not guilty but terrified. "Captain Joubert. Freya. Please. If you open that, you can't unknow it. And if anyone knows you know—" She stopped. Looked past him, upriver, into the dark where the drowned forest stood like a congregation of the dead. "They shot my crew out of the water and dressed it up as piracy. Do you understand? Six people. Amara was twenty-three. She'd only been with the expedition eight months. They put eleven rounds into that boat and took the engines so the story would hold, and it will hold, unless—" her breath caught, "—unless there's nothing left to burn."

Freya stood with the oilcloth bundle in his hands and the ash drifting down around them, and he thought of a photograph of a dead man wearing a company badge, and a notebook full of a dead surveyor's handwriting saying *they always knew*, and a patrol boat commander who would not exist yet for another day on the river but whose kind he already knew by heart.

Behind them, from the bow, Okonkwo's voice came soft across the dark deck, not turning, still watching the banks:

"Captain. Lights. Two of them. Coming up the channel."

Far off through the black lattice of drowned trees, low on the water, two white lights were sliding toward them, unhurried, side by side, like eyes opening.
