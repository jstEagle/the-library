## 5. The Company's Wake

The engine note changed before Naledi heard it. Freya felt it first through the deck plates—a thin vibration threading under the Magpie's own heartbeat, high and clean where his old diesel was low and rough. Something upstream was running fast water toward them.

He cut the throttle to half and listened.

"Company boat," Naledi said. She'd come up from the hold with a coil of rope over one shoulder and gone still the way animals go still. "That's a turbine whine. Nothing on this river runs turbines but them."

Freya checked the sky out of habit—overcast, low ceiling, good for hiding, bad for being seen from above—and then checked the channel ahead. They were coming down out of the drowned forest stretch, where the river braided wide and shallow around the shoulders of sunken trees, and there was no cover worth the name. A heron lifted off a dead snag two hundred meters out, unhurried, indifferent. He envied it.

Behind him, in the wheelhouse shadow, Ilse Marchetti had stopped pretending to read her journal. Okonkwo hadn't moved at all; he sat the way he always sat, hands loose in his lap, watching the banks as if the answer to some long question might swim past at any moment.

"How fast?" Freya asked.

Naledi shaded her eyes even though there was no sun to shade them from. "Fast."

* * *

The patrol boat came around the last bend at a speed that made the river look slow. It was thirty meters of gray composite, twin screws, a bow that flared spray like a knife through dough, and it carried itself with the particular arrogance of something built by people who had never once needed to ask the river's permission. COMPANY OF THE VASSEUR WATERSHED ran along its flank in letters you could read from a kilometer off. That was the point of the letters.

It slowed as it closed, falling into formation off the Magpie's starboard quarter with a courtesy so precise it was worse than ramming them. A loudspeaker crackled.

"Vessel Magpie, vessel Magpie. This is Patrol Nine. Requesting you hold station. We'd like a word with your master. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," Freya repeated under his breath. He looked at Naledi. "Take the wheel. Keep us steady. Don't volunteer anything."

"And you?"

"I'm going to be a boring man with a boring boat."

Ilse rose halfway from her bench. "Captain—"

"Sit down," he said quietly. "You're my cousin's widow from Kessel Bend, you're carrying seed grain manifests, and you've never been upriver in your life. If you can't manage that story, manage silence." Her mouth thinned, but she sat. Okonkwo's eyes flicked to Freya once—the first time the man had looked directly at anyone in three days—and then back to the water.

Freya went out onto the side deck as the patrol boat slid alongside, close enough that he could have stepped across. Its deck crew wore gray coveralls with no insignia, which was its own kind of insignia. At the rail stood a man in a better-cut version of the same gray, fifty or so, silver at the temples, with the lean, weathered face of someone who'd spent years outdoors supervising other people doing the work. His smile arrived a half-second before the rest of his expression did, like a man who sent his smile ahead to scout.

"Captain Joubert." The man's voice carried easily across the gap between hulls. "Verhoeven. Watershed Security Division. May I come aboard? It's easier than shouting."

Everything in Freya said no. Everything practical said that refusing would only make the visit longer. "Mind the gunwale," he said. "She's old."

"She's beautiful," Verhoeven said, stepping across with the ease of a man who boarded things for a living. "I've admired her since the day she was launched. You keep her well."

"You know my boat."

"I know every licensed hull on this river." Verhoeven turned a slow circle on the afterdeck, taking in the coiled lines, the stacked crates of resupply goods, the rust streaks, the mended rail. His gaze touched the wheelhouse, touched Naledi, touched—briefly, weightlessly, like a fingertip on a scale—the shape of Ilse behind the glass, and moved on without pausing. "And I know every captain. Though in your case, Captain, I feel I should say: I knew you before you were you."

The river filled the silence. Water chuckling against the hull. The turbine whine of the patrol boat idling off the beam.

"I don't know what you mean," Freya said.

"No, of course not." Verhoeven smiled again, warmer now, almost confiding. He produced a folded paper from inside his jacket—not a warrant, nothing so crude, just paper, which could be anything—and held it without offering it. "We're looking for stray personnel. An expedition went quiet upriver some weeks ago. Scientific types. Ill-equipped, frankly, for the upper reaches. There's concern for their welfare." He let that sit. "Dr. Marchetti and an assistant. Possibly others. If they've hailed you, if they've begged passage, if you've seen so much as a dropped field case floating downstream—we'd be grateful to hear of it. Grateful in ways that matter, on this river."

"We're a supply run," Freya said. "Fuel, flour, chain oil, medicine for the Kessel clinics. I don't pick up passengers."

"No," Verhoeven agreed pleasantly. "You don't. You're famous for it." He glanced at the wheelhouse glass again, and this time he did pause, and his smile deepened by a degree that had nothing warm in it. "Your cousin's widow. My condolences. The river takes so many."

"It does," Freya said.

"Mm." Verhoeven refolded his paper along its existing creases, slowly, precisely, and put it away. Then, as if remembering something trivial: "You'll forgive an old colleague's curiosity. When you signed your current license—Joubert, was it—you used a birthdate that belongs to a boy born in Vasseur Port, third district. Mother a net-mender. Brother a surveyor." He watched Freya with the mild attention of a man watching a gauge climb. "The registry clerk who processed that license has since retired to the coast. Comfortably. But records are records. Paper remembers, Captain, even when men are paid to forget."

Somewhere below decks the bilge pump kicked on, its small mechanical cough absurd against the ringing in Freya's ears. He kept his face still because he had spent ten years learning to keep his face still, and it held, and he hated how much practice it took.

"I wouldn't know about any of that," he said.

"Of course not." Verhoeven nodded as though Freya had confirmed everything. He stepped back to the rail. "Well. We won't take more of your morning. We're bound for the escarpment base—there's a great deal of activity up there this season, you may see the lights at night, quite a sight—and we'll be coming and going on this stretch regularly. Do give our regards to the widow." He paused, one foot on the gunwale, and looked back with genuine, terrible friendliness. "Oh—one more thing. Free advice, from one riverman to another. Flood season is coming early this year. The highlands have had rain you wouldn't believe. A boat your age should think carefully about how far up she goes."

Then he was across the gap, and the patrol boat's screws bit, and the gray hull swung away upstream with the insolent grace of something that owned the water it displaced. The wake reached the Magpie a few seconds later, rolling her gently, twice, like a hand checking whether a sleeping thing would wake.

Naledi brought them back on course without being told. Nobody spoke for a long time.

* * *

They tied up that evening in a cutbank willow stand, engines cold, no fire, dinner eaten cold out of tins because Freya didn't want smoke. The overcast had thickened into true dark, the kind the drowned-forest country gave you, where the sky and the water became one blackness and the boat seemed to hang in it.

Ilse came forward to the wheelhouse while Naledi slept in the fo'c'sle and Okonkwo stood his endless watch at the stern rail, a silhouette against marginally less dark.

"He knows your name," she said. Not your boat-name. Your name. She said it flatly, no question in it, and Freya understood that she had heard every word through the wheelhouse glass, sound carrying over water the way it always did.

"He knows a name," Freya said.

"Don't do that. Not to me. I've spent six weeks watching men die politely." She pulled her jacket tighter. In the faint glow of the binnacle light her face was all angles and exhaustion. "You want to know why I burned pages of my own journal two nights ago? Because I've read enough intercepted traffic to know what a man like Verhoeven does with paper. So let's trade honestly, Captain. You're not a supply runner. Supply runners don't flinch when they hear the word *surveyor*. And I'm not a widow from Kessel Bend."

Freya looked at her for a long moment. The river breathed against the hull.

"What did you find?" he asked. Because that was the real question, the one that had been sitting in his chest since the photograph of Tomas in a company badge, since the burned pages, since the word *escarpment* had entered his life like water entering a hull.

Ilse exhaled, and something in her posture gave—not collapsed, but settled, like a load shifting into a truer balance. She sat down across from him uninvited.

"My expedition found caves in the escarpment. Limestone galleries above the spring line, dry, protected—the kind of place things survive in. Carvings. Thousands of them. Not decoration." She spoke carefully, the way people speak when they've rehearsed a thing too many times and are trying to make it sound fresh. "A script. Pre-colonial—older than anything the company's historians admit existed on this river. Okonkwo is a linguist; that's his actual work, not assisting. He's been reading them for months." She glanced aft, at the silent figure at the rail. "There's a treaty carved into that stone. A formal one, witnessed, dated in a calendar we've only partially reconstructed. Between the river peoples—all of them, every settlement from the delta to the headwaters—and the first colonial governor, the one whose name is on the company's founding charter."

"A treaty saying what?"

"Saying the watershed belongs to its inhabitants. In perpetuity. Including the source region—including the water rights the company claims it bought fair and square from a wilderness that was, according to their own maps, empty." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "But here's what matters, Captain. Here's what I need you to understand. The caves contain maps too. Carved copies of the earliest surveys. And those surveys show the upper river exactly as it is. Villages. Fields. Ferry crossings. Trade roads. Forty settlements above the gorge that appear on no chart printed in the last hundred and twenty years."

Freya felt the deck tilt under him, though the river was flat calm.

"They weren't never mapped," he said slowly.

"They were mapped," Ilse said, "and then unmapped. Deliberately. The company's original charter titles rest on a survey—the Kessler Survey—that described the upper basin as uninhabited wasteland. That survey is the legal foundation of everything they own. Every concession, every water right, every eviction. And Tomas Joubert—" she watched him take the blow without blinking, and gave him the mercy of continuing anyway— "Tomas Joubert was the last man to re-certify it. Twenty-two years ago he walked that country with a company transit and a company notebook, and he saw the villages, and he wrote them down, and then he came back and certified a blank."

The bilge pump kicked on again. Freya let it run longer than it needed to, just to have something mechanical to listen to instead of his own pulse.

"He recanted," Ilse said. "Late. Too late. He filed a protest with the surveyor-general's office naming the falsification and the men who ordered it. Three weeks later his boat went down in the delta narrows in weather no reasonable man launches in. The file on his protest disappeared. The clerk who received it retired to the coast." She let the echo land. "Comfortably."

Freya stared at the black water sliding past the hull. Ten years of careful invisibility rearranged themselves in his mind—not as caution anymore, but as complicity. All those seasons hugging the shallows, keeping his head down, keeping the name buried: he had thought he was honoring his brother by staying out of the machine's reach. He had actually been doing exactly what the machine preferred. Invisible men make no protests. Buried names file nothing.

"The company isn't trying to find you to help you," he said at last.

"The company is trying to find me to end me, and to dynamite those caves, and to do it quietly enough that in fifty years even the rumor of them is gone. Verhoeven's division exists for exactly one purpose, Captain. It isn't security. It's erasure. It has been erasing things on this river since before either of us was born—it erased my expedition's radio schedule, it will erase the wreckage of my launch, and given time and money it will erase you." She leaned forward. "You asked me once, the first night, why a woman with my credentials would pay scrip to ride a rustbucket upriver with a stranger. Here's your answer. Because the treaty is real. Because the carvings are real. Because forty villages' grandchildren could own their own water, legally, in court, forever—if the stone survives long enough for a judge to see it."

Above them the overcast shifted, and for a moment the clouds tore enough to show stars, cold and far, reflected double in the river so that the Magpie seemed to float among them.

"And Verhoeven knows who I am," Freya said.

"Verhoeven has known who you are since the day he handed you that contract," Ilse said. "Think, Captain. Of all the boats on all the river, whose brother's file did the courier leave on your deck? Why summon the one man in the delta with a blood reason to hate them? Either they wanted you too frightened to come upriver—" she held his eyes— "or they wanted exactly what they got. You, carrying me, straight up the throat of the gorge, saving them the trouble of hunting."

The engine room clock ticked. At the stern rail, Okonkwo had turned, and though Freya couldn't see his face in the dark, he could feel the man listening, weighing, waiting—as if the whole voyage had been a sentence Okonkwo was translating one word at a time, and the next word belonged to Freya.

Far upstream, past the black shoulder of the drowned forest, a light blinked twice against the clouds. Then again. Steady, patient, industrial. The escarpment base camp, announcing itself to the night.

Flood season early, Verhoeven had said. Think carefully about how far up she goes.

Freya put his hand on the throttle, not moving it yet, just feeling the worn brass under his palm the way he'd feel a brother's shoulder.

"Well," he said to the dark, to the light, to the dead. "I never was any good at thinking carefully."
