## 6. Tomas's Debt

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones, and the river talked to itself in the dark the way it always did—low, patient, saying nothing that could be trusted.

Freya sat with his back against the Magpie's hull and watched Ilse Marchetti across the coals. She had waited until Naledi was asleep under the tarp amidships and Okonkwo had taken first watch at the bow, and then she had said, quietly, "You should know what your brother did."

He had known it was coming. From the moment she'd stepped aboard at the fuel depot with her company scrip and her too-steady eyes, he had felt this conversation waiting for him like a snag under flat water—the kind you don't see until your propeller finds it.

"Tell me," he said, because not asking would have been worse.

She fed a stick to the fire. Her hands were steady. He noticed that about her—everything about her was steady except what lived behind her eyes, which moved like fish in deep water, there and gone.

"Tomas Joubert joined the Vasseur Consolidated survey office eleven years ago," she said. "Junior hydrographer. He was good. Better than good—he had grown up on this river, and the company men who came up from the capital couldn't read water the way he could. They couldn't tell where a channel would shift after flood season, or which sandbar was old enough to build a claim on. Tomas could. So they gave him the upper river."

"The upper river doesn't have charts," Freya said. "That's why it's closed."

"That's why it's *closed*," Ilse agreed. "But it has charts, Captain. It always did. I've seen them. Tomas drew them."

The word went into him like a hook going into a fish—clean, quick, and then the long dragging pain of it.

"You're lying," he said, and heard how thin his own voice came out.

"I'm telling you what's in the company archive under seal number 4-4-1." She turned the stick in the coals. "Three seasons of survey work. Soundings, bank profiles, village locations, water rights claims going back generations—all of it recorded, all of it accurate, because Tomas was honest when he started. And then Verhoeven's office gave him the revision order."

"Revision."

"That's the word they used. The charts were *revised*. Every settlement above the gorge was removed. Every claim, every well, every ferry crossing, every field that depended on the annual flood—the whole living record of the upper watershed, deleted. What remained was a blank marked 'impassable, unproductive, no permanent habitation.' A blank the company could fill with anything it wanted. Concessions. Leases. Titles." She looked up at him through the smoke. "You can't contest a land claim if legally there are no people to contest it. That's what erasure is for. Not ignorance. Erasure is a weapon with a paperwork handle."

Freya found he was standing. He didn't remember getting up. His hands had curled into fists without consulting him, and his breath was coming short and hot, and the river kept talking its low patient talk as though none of this were happening on its banks.

"He wouldn't," he said. "Tomas wouldn't—"

"Tomas did," Ilse said, gentle and merciless. "And then he spent six years trying to live with it, and then he tried to take it back."

* * *

He made himself sit down again. Made himself listen, because some part of him—a cold, clear part, the part that read water—understood that this was the truth whether he survived hearing it or not.

"He filed a correction," Ilse said. "A formal one, through proper channels, with his original soundings attached. He thought the system would work if you used it correctly. He was young like that, even at thirty-one. The correction went up the chain and landed on Verhoeven's desk, and Verhoeven did what the company always does with inconvenient honesty: he scheduled it. Gave it a review date two years out. And in the meantime he had Tomas reassigned to delta work, far from the upper river, far from anyone who might ask him questions, and put a commendation in his file that everyone understood was a leash."

"And the accident," Freya said. His voice sounded like someone else's. Like an old man's.

"There was no storm that night. The weather station at Kiln Bend logged clear skies and light wind. His boat was found inverted below the Serrano flats with a cracked transom that the marine examiner noted—and I've read the report, Captain, I pulled it when I began my research—was 'consistent with impact,' except there is nothing between the Serrano flats and open water to impact. No rocks. No snags. Nothing but company barges running night freight without lights, which the same office declined to investigate." She let that sit in the dark between them. "Tomas told people he was going to recant publicly. He told a journalist. He told a magistrate. Three weeks later he was dead, and the correction file was closed as 'moot, deceased originator.'"

Freya looked at the fire. The fire did not help.

Ten years. Ten years he had carried his brother's death like ballast—grief, yes, but grief with a shape he could live inside. An accident. The river took people; the river had always taken people; that was the bargain every soul on the Vasseur made when they launched a hull. You paid the river's toll and you hated the river a little and you kept working, because the alternative was to stop, and stopping meant drowning in place.

But there had been no toll. There had been no river in it at all. There had been a man in an office with a pen, and a boat with a hole put in it by hands, and a file stamped moot.

"I buried him believing—" He stopped. Started again. "I stood at the grave and I blamed the water. I stood there and I forgave the *water*, Ilse. Out loud. In front of everyone from Kiln Bend to the delta. I said the river gives and the river takes, and we don't get to choose, and that's how we honor them—by staying on it anyway." His laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I eulogized the murder weapon."

Ilse said nothing. She was wise enough for that, at least.

* * *

Anger is a strange current. It doesn't arrive all at once like floodwater; it comes up from underneath, cold first, so you mistake it for clarity.

Freya walked away from the fire because he could not sit still, and paced the shingle bank where the Magpie lay aground for the night, and the anger rose in him in layers, each colder than the last.

At Tomas first. At the falsification itself—not even the death yet, just the work. Six years of surveying, and his brother had drawn the upper river true, and then he had redrawn it false, and Freya tried to imagine the day that happened. Tried to picture Tomas at his drafting table with the honest sheets on one side and the revision order on the other, and the moment where the pencil touched paper and began to delete villages. Had his hand shaken? Had he done it fast, to get it over with, the way you do any ugly job? Had he told himself he had no choice—that refusing meant losing everything, that someone else would do it anyway, that the chart was only paper?

Only paper. Freya had spent his whole life watching what paper did to the river settlements. The depot at Kiln Bend that charged triple rates because no competitor's charter could be issued for a place that wasn't on the map. The fishing families above the gorge who couldn't register their boats, insure their crews, or sue when a company dredge destroyed their spawning beds—because you cannot sue from nowhere, and legally they were nowhere. The schoolhouse at Tamarind Cut that closed for want of a permit nobody could apply for. All of it, all of it, resting on the clean confident lines of a chart his brother's hand had drawn.

At the company next, and that anger was simpler, almost restful in its simplicity. Verhoeven's name Ilse had given him like a key turning in a lock. He turned it over. Commander Verhoeven, who had overtaken them that morning with his courteous hail and his white teeth and his knowledge of a name Freya had buried ten years ago in a different province with different papers. *Captain Joubert,* Verhoeven had said, smiling, and Freya had understood in that instant that his invisibility had never been invisibility at all—only permission. Permission granted by the same office that had erased a river. They had let him hide. They had watched him hide, all these years, the way you watch a dog you might someday need.

And then, coldest of all, at himself.

Because here was the thing he could not pace away from, the thing that followed him down the shingle and back like his own shadow thrown by the firelight: he had known something was wrong. Not the details—but the shape of it. A decade of small wrongnesses, all pointing upstream. The way company men went quiet when the escarpment was mentioned. The way the closure order had come down with unusual speed, unusual unanimity, no appeal process worth the name. The way his brother, in his last year, had started asking strange careful questions over Sunday dinners—*Freya, if a man found out a chart was wrong, what could he do? Who would listen?*—and Freya had answered like a practical man, like a careful man: *Nobody. Keep your head down. Charts are politics and we're boatmen.* And Tomas had looked at him across the table with something dying in his face, and Freya had called it disappointment and poured another drink.

His brother had come to him. Not with everything—but with the door held open, asking to be pulled through. And Freya, loyal to his own safety, loyal to the beautiful doctrine of staying invisible, had told him to keep his head down.

Three weeks later the river—or whatever had been sent onto the river—took him.

"You knew him," Ilse said behind him. She had come to stand at the edge of the firelight, arms crossed against the river damp. "Better than I ever did. I only know the file version. So tell me if I'm wrong about any of it."

"You're not wrong." He said it to the water. "That's what I can't forgive. Not you—I needed someone to say it plainly, and you did. I can't forgive that you're right."

"Do you want to hear the rest?"

"There's more?"

"There's why I'm alive and my team isn't." She came closer, and the firelight showed him her face stripped of its expedition-leader composure, and underneath it he saw exhaustion and guilt wearing each other's clothes. "Your brother left a thread, Captain. When he died, the correction file was closed—but his original soundings were never destroyed. Companies destroy evidence when it's cheap. His was expensive: three seasons of fieldwork, irreplaceable, and Verhoeven is a man who keeps weapons in case he needs them. So the originals sat in the sealed archive. And a year ago, assembling the historical record for my survey, I found a citation in a colonial-era gazetteer that referenced a pre-colonial treaty stone at the source watershed—and the citation had been annotated in a surveyor's hand. Marginalia. Coordinates. A note that said *the stone confirms the old boundaries; see attached; do not attach.*"

Freya turned slowly. "Tomas's hand?"

"I've compared it against forty pages of his archived drawings." She nodded once. "It's his. Sometime before he died, he found the treaty stone—or heard of it—and he understood what it meant. That the erasure wasn't just cowardice and greed. It was a crime with a document at the bottom of it. The old treaty granted the watershed to its inhabitants in perpetuity. If that stone still existed, and could be read, every title the company holds above the gorge is built on sand." Her voice dropped. "He couldn't recant in time. But he left a map for whoever came after. My expedition followed his marginalia. That's why we were really up here—not geology, not botany. We found the caves, Captain. We found the carvings. And that's why there are holes in my launch and why my photographer is dead and why Verhoeven's patrol boat knows your real name. Because Tomas Joubert's last act on this earth was to leave a trail, and the company will kill anyone who walks it."

The river talked. The fire settled. Somewhere up the bank Okonkwo shifted at his watch, silent as driftwood.

Freya stood very still and felt the last layer of the anger finish rising, and it was not anger anymore, or not only. It was direction. Ten years of hiding had been a choice he'd told himself was loyalty—loyalty to Tomas's memory, loyalty to the dead man's wish for his little brother to stay safe, stay small, stay off the company's ledger. But the dead man's last wish, it turned out, had been the opposite of all that. The dead man had spent his final months drawing a treasure map in margins, betting his life's remainder that someone would follow it.

And the company had recruited Freya—hired him, threatened him, dangled his brother's badge in front of him—precisely because they feared that someone would.

"They didn't send me up here to resupply an expedition," he said slowly. "They sent me because they think I'll lead them to whatever's left of yours."

"I believe that's the arrangement, yes."

"And if I turn this boat around—"

"Then they'll know you won't cooperate, and we'll all be dead before Kiln Bend." She said it without drama, the way you state the draft under a keel. "If we go on, we might die anyway. But we might also reach the stone."

Freya looked upstream, into the black where the gorge waited with its white noise of rapids, and past it, days away, the escarpment, and somewhere beneath the escarpment a spring-fed amphitheater he had never seen and somehow already grieved beside.

Loyalty, he thought, had been the word he hid behind. Loyalty to a memory he had gotten wrong. Well. Memory could be corrected. His brother had taught him that, in the end, in the only ink that lasted.

"Naledi can't know any of this yet," he said. "Not the parts about Tomas. Not yet."

"She's been radioing someone, you know," Ilse said quietly. "Short transmissions, after midnight. I've watched her antenna."

Freya closed his eyes. Of course. Of course there was one more debt waiting in the dark.

"First light," he said. "We run for the gorge."
