## 7. First Rapids

The river ended the way a road ends—not gradually, but with an argument. One moment the Vasseur ran wide and brown and reasonable between its banks; the next, the whole channel funneled into a limestone throat no wider than the Magpie was long, and the water went from moving to *committed*.

Freya brought the boat in against the current and let her drift to a stop in the last slack pool, engine ticking as it cooled. Ahead, the gorge walls rose sheer on either side, close enough that he could have spat at both of them. The sound coming out of that throat wasn't the sound of a river anymore. It was the sound of something being swallowed.

"That's it," Naledi said. She stood at the bow rail with one hand shading her eyes. "That's where the charts stop pretending."

She'd been different since the patrol boat—quieter, watchful in a way Freya had filed under *shaken by Verhoeven* and tried not to think about too hard. He'd catch her looking at him sometimes when she thought he wouldn't notice, and there was something in her face he couldn't name. Something almost like guilt. He told himself it was just a young woman realizing the river had teeth.

"Charts don't pretend," he said. "People do." He killed the engine. In the sudden quiet, the gorge roared. "Navigable water ends here. Everything past this pool is winch work."

Ilse came up from the cabin, field jacket buttoned wrong, hair escaping its braid. She looked at the gorge the way other people looked at open graves. "The expedition portaged here," she said. "There's a track on the left bank. Old logging skids, if they're still—" She stopped herself. Freya noticed she did that more and more: starting sentences about what her team had done, then abandoning them like boats cut loose.

"Skids rot," Freya said. "Ropes rot. People rot faster." He turned to Naledi. "Someone walks the bank first. Finds the line we can run, marks every snag, tells me where the winch points anchor. I'm not taking my boat into that throat blind."

"I'll go."

"You'll stay on the boat. I need hands I trust at the winch."

"I've walked worse banks than this."

"It's not about the walking." He kept his voice level. "It's about who's watching my boat while I'm hip-deep in white water with a rope in my teeth. That's you. That's always been you."

Something crossed her face then—quick, gone before he could read it—and she nodded once and went forward to check the winch drum without another word.

Okonkwo appeared at his elbow the way he always did, silent as condensation. The big man hadn't spoken a full sentence in three weeks. He communicated in nods, in pointed fingers, in the occasional single word delivered like a stone set down carefully on a table. Freya had stopped trying to draw him out around day four. But now Okonkwo touched Freya's shoulder—a liberty no one aboard took twice—and pointed at the gorge, then at himself, then at Naledi's back.

"You want to go with her?"

A nod.

"Why?"

Okonkwo considered the question for a long moment. His eyes were on the gorge walls, on the dark seams of cave mouths stitched along the limestone like stitches in a wound.

"Two is safer than one," he said. Four words. It was practically a speech.

Freya looked at the two of them—the deckhand who'd been radioing someone, though he didn't know that yet, and the mute who knew things about Tomas that Ilse only hinted at—and felt the old captain's instinct turn over in him like a fish under a boat: *something in your hold isn't what you loaded.* But the gorge didn't care about his instincts, and neither would the rapids. He needed the bank scouted, and he needed it done by people who could keep each other alive.

"Rope harnesses," he said. "Flare gun goes with whoever's ahead. You mark the safe line with paint. Red means run the boat through, blue means winch and pole, white means don't even think about it. You come back before the light goes, or I come find you, and I will be *unpleasant* about it."

Naledi shouldered the pack. At the gangway she paused, half-turned, and for a second Freya thought she was going to say something—to him, about anything—and then she just said, "Back before the light, Captain," and dropped onto the rocks.

He watched them climb the left bank until the trees took them. Ilse came to stand beside him at the rail.

"They'll be fine," she said.

"Everyone says that right before they're not."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "I mean them specifically. Those two have been circling each other since the fuel depot. Like dogs deciding whether to fight or sleep back to back." She smiled faintly, and it was the first unguarded expression he'd seen on her in weeks. "They'll be fine because they're finally doing something together instead of lying near each other."

Freya grunted. Below them, the gorge ate the river and gave nothing back.

* * *

The bank trail was worse than Naledi had hoped and better than she'd feared, which by now she understood was the standard condition of everything connected to this voyage.

The path—if it deserved the word—clung to the left wall of the gorge forty feet above the water, a shelf of broken limestone threaded with roots. Below her, the river ran white and continuous, a single sustained roar that made speech useless and thought difficult. She'd learned to read Freya's silences over two years on the Magpie, but this was a different order of silence entirely. This was the silence of a place that had never agreed to be known.

Okonkwo moved behind her, sure-footed, carrying most of the gear without complaint. She'd tested him early on the walk—pointed at a stretch of loose scree and said "careful"—and he'd given her a nod so precise it was nearly courteous. Somewhere in the second hour she'd stopped performing caution for his benefit and started actually trusting him, which frightened her more than the drop did, because trusting people was exactly how you ended up owing them things you couldn't pay.

The way she owed the company.

She checked the radio in her pocket for the hundredth time. It sat there against her hip like a tumor. Three transmissions since the depot, short and coded, exactly as instructed: *Magpie upstream, four souls, approaching gorge.* And each time, afterward, the voice on the other end had played her the same recording—her mother's voice, laughing at some family joke two birthdays ago, recorded off a party-line call Naledi hadn't known was tapped—and then said, in a pleasant, patient tone, *We're glad your family is well, Miss Mbeki. Keep us informed.*

Her little brother's school fees. Her mother's clinic debt. A sister with a baby on the way in a settlement whose dock lease the company could revoke with a signature. Verhoeven hadn't threatened her, not exactly. He'd just made sure she understood the arithmetic.

And now Freya was going to find out. She could feel it coming the way you felt weather. He'd caught her looking at him too often. He'd see the radio eventually, or the company would ask her for something she couldn't disguise, and then—

Then what? Then she'd learn whether two years of being treated like family meant anything to a man who'd spent ten years teaching himself to need no one.

Ahead, the shelf widened into a gravel bench, and Naledi stopped dead.

Caves. Dozens of them, honeycombing the gorge wall above the bench—some no bigger than owl holes, others tall enough to drive a barge into. And below the largest, scattered across the gravel like toys dropped by a giant: equipment. Crates stenciled with the Marchetti expedition's mark. A collapsed tent. Rope, still coiled. A aluminum case lying open, empty, its foam lining gouged out in rectangles.

The expedition had come through here. Had camped here. Had—

Okonkwo went past her. He didn't hurry, but he didn't hesitate either, and there was something in the way he moved toward the largest cave mouth that made the hair rise on her arms. Not like a man exploring. Like a man returning.

"Hey." Her voice got lost in the roar. She grabbed his sleeve. "Protocol. We scout together or not at all."

He looked down at her hand on his arm. Then at the cave. Then, last, at her face—and what was in his eyes stopped her breath. It wasn't fear. It was grief, held the way you hold a full cup across a crowded room: carefully, constantly, aware that one stumble ruins everything.

He pointed at the cave mouth. Then he pressed his fist to his chest.

"Yours?" she said stupidly. "You've been here?"

He nodded once.

And then, standing on a gravel bench above a roaring river in a gorge that wasn't supposed to exist, the man who hadn't spoken in three weeks opened his mouth and spoke.

"Not mine," Okonkwo said. His voice was deep and rusted, a hinge unused too long. "But I can read what's inside. That's why they hired me. That's why I'm still alive."

Naledi stared at him. The river roared. Somewhere behind them, the light was beginning to lean toward evening, and the flare gun in her pack suddenly felt very far away.

"How long," she said, "have you been able to talk?"

"Always." He almost smiled. "Silence was the job. Dr. Marchetti's orders, after the launch was hit. She said a linguist who talks gets asked questions, and a man who gets asked questions gets everyone killed." He looked at the cave again. "She was right. They shot the launch because of what I'd already translated. Imagine what they'd do for the rest."

"The rest of what?"

He turned to her fully then, and up close she could see how tired he was, how long he'd been carrying whatever this was alone.

"Come inside," he said. "I'll show you something older than every map you've ever seen. And then you're going to have to decide, Miss Mbeki, which side of it you're standing on—because I watched you check that radio in your pocket, and I count pockets for a living."

* * *

By the time the light went amber in the treetops, Freya had the Magpie rigged at the head of the pool, winch lines run to a granite horn he'd chosen hours ago, and had worn a groove in the deck pacing.

They were late. Not dangerously late, but late enough that the amber was going to red, and red was going to black, and black in a gorge was a place where good captains died of impatience. Ilse stood beside him at the rail, scanning the bank with binoculars, saying nothing, which somehow made it worse.

"There," she said.

Two figures on the gravel bench, small against the cliff. Then smaller, dropping out of sight behind a fold of rock, then reappearing lower, closer to the waterline, moving fast now. Freya raised his own glasses.

Naledi came first, scrambling down the talus with the recklessness of exhaustion, and he saw immediately that something was wrong—not with her body but with her bearing, some structural change, as if a load-bearing wall inside her had been removed overnight. Behind her came Okonkwo, and Okonkwo was *talking*, gesturing at the cliff face, at the caves, at the water, his voice carrying in fragments over the roar, and Freya lowered the binoculars slowly because the world had apparently decided today was the day it rearranged itself.

They reached the boat. Naledi came up the gangway on legs that shook, took the hand he offered without quite meeting his eyes, and stood swaying on the deck like a mast with the wind gone out of it.

"Report," Freya said.

"Bank's runnable to the second bend. Blue markers from there—winch and pole, maybe six hours' work. White past the narrows; there's a falls you can't see from here." She recited it flatly, professionally, and then stopped, and he watched her wrestle with whatever came next and lose. "Captain. There's something you need to hear. From him. Not from me."

Okonkwo stepped aboard. He stood in front of Freya, and the big man seemed to take up more space than he had that morning, as if three weeks of silence had been compressing him and now the compression was released.

"Captain Joubert," he said. "My name is Adaeze Okonkwo. I am the Marchetti expedition's linguist. I can read the script carved in those caves, and in the caves above the escarpment, and I am going to tell you what it says, because tomorrow we start the winch work and one of us may not live through it, and dead men shouldn't keep secrets."

Freya looked at Ilse. She met his eyes steadily, and he understood that she had known—all of it, all along—and had let her man stay silent because silence had kept him breathing.

"Talk," Freya said.

Okonkwo talked. About the carvings. About a treaty older than any chart in any company office, cut into living stone by the river peoples and witnessed by the first colonial governor, granting the source watershed to its inhabitants in perpetuity—in perpetuity, he repeated, the words like stones dropped into still water. About how the company's founding land titles rested on maps that showed the upper river as unmapped wilderness, uninhabited, unclaimed. About how those maps weren't ignorance. They were *instruments*. Someone had surveyed this river honestly once, and then someone else had taken that survey and buried it, and every water right, every dock lease, every debt in every settlement downriver hung from that buried truth like fruit from a poisoned branch.

Freya heard it all standing very still. And underneath Okonkwo's words, quieter, he heard another voice—Tomas's voice, ten years dead, saying *I survey what's there, brother, not what they tell me is there,* and laughing, and Freya had laughed too, back when laughter was free.

"The company sent dynamite for those caves," Okonkwo finished. "I've seen the manifests. When they're done, the treaty exists only in my memory and Dr. Marchetti's photographs. And men with guns are very efficient at reducing memories to two."

The gorge roared. The light died another shade toward black.

Freya found Naledi at the rail, staring down into the dark water, and stood beside her. For a while neither of them spoke.

"You knew some of this," he said finally. Not accusing. Just placing it, the way you place a weight on a scale to see what it does.

"Some." Her voice cracked. "Not all. Captain, I—" She stopped. Started again. "There's things I have to tell you. About the radio. About why I—"

"Tomorrow," Freya said.

She looked up at him, startled, and he saw hope and terror fighting in her face.

"Tomorrow we run the winch," he said. "Six hours of rope and rock and water that wants us dead, and I need every hand on this boat thinking about nothing except staying alive. Whatever you're carrying, you carry it through the gorge first. Then you give it to me. That's not mercy, Naledi. That's seamanship."

She nodded, and wiped her face quickly with the back of her wrist, and went forward to check the winch drum a third time though it didn't need checking.

Freya stayed at the rail. Above him the cave mouths went dark one by one as the light failed, dozens of them, patient as closed eyes. Somewhere in the deepest of them, he thought, a treaty was waiting in the stone, and somewhere downstream a man named Verhoeven was waiting too, and between those two waitings lay six hours of the worst water on the river.

He went to the wheel and woke the engine.

"First light," he said to his crew, to his crew, and discovered the word had grown teeth sometime in the last three weeks without his noticing. "We run the gorge at first light."
