## 8. The Village That Isn't on Any Map

The channel narrowed past the drowned forest until the banks pressed close enough that Freya could have touched the overhanging fig branches with a boat hook. The Magpie's engine dropped to an idle murmur, and the world went quiet in the way the upper river was always quiet—not silent, but hushed, as if the water itself were keeping its voice down. Kingfishers stitched blue along the margins. Somewhere ahead, hidden behind a screen of reeds, something large slipped into the current and left no wake.

"Chart says open water here," Naledi said, holding the survey sheet flat against the wind. She'd been studying it for an hour with the frown she wore when the river and the paper disagreed. "No settlements marked for forty kilometers. No channels either. Just—" she turned it over, as if the missing information might be printed on the back, "—blank."

"The company doesn't chart what it doesn't want to find," Ilse said from her seat near the wheelhouse.

Freya said nothing. He'd learned long ago that blank spaces on charts were never empty; they were only unrecorded. Every sandbar he'd ever struck had been on some fisherman's mental map, every snag known by name to someone who poled these waters at dawn. The official surveys simply hadn't asked.

Okonkwo stood at the bow rail, and Freya noticed his posture change—a slight forward lean, the stillness of a man listening. Since the gorge, since he'd spoken his first full sentences and revealed himself as the expedition's linguist, he'd been slowly reassembling into someone with weight and history. Now he raised one hand, palm out.

"There," he said. "Smoke."

Freya saw it a moment later: a thin gray thread rising from behind a bluff of red earth, too domestic to be a wildfire, too steady to be accidental. His instinct said pass wide, keep moving, stay invisible. A decade of habit pulled at him like current.

But they needed water, and wood, and a safe anchorage for the night. And the patrol boat had vanished two days ago—vanished being not the same as gone.

He brought the Magpie around the bluff with the tide of caution that never left him, and the village opened up before them like a held breath released.

It sat in a bend where the river widened into a lagoon, built on stilts above the flood line: perhaps thirty houses of woven palm and ironwood, a long communal hall facing the water, drying racks hung with fish, canoes drawn up on the mud in neat rows. Children froze mid-game on a platform walkway and stared. A dog began barking and was silenced by a look from an old woman who had already turned toward the long hall, moving fast, as if summoning something.

"They know we're coming," Naledi murmured. "They've known since we rounded the bluff."

"Then we come slow and we come honest," Freya said. He cut the engine and let the Magpie drift the last hundred meters, poling her in himself, hands visible on the shaft. An old habit from years when approaching strangers wrong could cost you your cargo—or worse.

A delegation waited at the landing: three elders, two men and a woman, dressed in the plain work clothes of river people but wearing them with the authority of office. Behind them stood a dozen others, arms folded, faces unreadable. The woman in front was small and straight-backed, her hair gone white in a way that looked deliberate rather than aged, cropped close as a soldier's. Her eyes went past Freya immediately—to Ilse.

"You," she said.

Not *welcome*. Not *who comes?* Just *you*, in a voice like a door closing.

Ilse stepped down onto the landing planks. "Mama Sefu." Her voice was careful. "I hoped—"

"You hoped." The old woman's mouth twisted around the word. "You hoped. Fourteen months ago you sat in my house and ate my fish and drank my palm wine, and you promised. Do you remember what you promised, Doctor?"

"I remember."

"Say it. Say it aloud, so these people hear whether your memory works."

Ilse's jaw tightened. Freya watched her weigh something—the truth against a softer version of it—and choose the truth, which was more than he'd expected of her by now.

"I promised copies of everything we found in the caves. Translations, photographs, the treaty record itself. Documents your people could hold. Proof of what belongs to you." She paused. "I did not return. I am sorry. It isn't enough, and I won't pretend it is."

"Why didn't you return?" The second elder, a heavyset man with a scarred forearm, spoke for the first time. "We sent word downriver twice. We sent our own canoe to the depot at Kilome. Nothing came back but silence."

"Because the company found out what we'd found," Ilse said. "And everything that came after—the attack on our launch, the men lost—I ran. I took the records and I ran, and I told myself I was drawing them away from this place, away from the caves. Maybe that was true. Maybe it was also just running." She met Mama Sefu's eyes. "I owe you more than an apology. I owe you the documents. They're not all with me anymore, but they exist, and I will get them to you if it costs everything else I have."

Silence stretched across the landing. The river slapped softly against the pilings. Freya felt the whole village leaning in, listening, weighing.

It was Mama Sefu who finally moved. She looked at Ilse a long moment, then past her, at the Magpie, at Naledi, at Okonkwo—and last at Freya.

"You are the captain," she said. It wasn't a question. "The one whose boat has passed this bend eleven times in ten years and never once stopped."

Heat crawled up the back of Freya's neck. "That's right."

"We counted," she said. "River people count. We knew your boat before we knew your face—you run dark, no flag, no name painted big enough to read. We wondered what a man was hiding that made him so careful never to see us." She gestured up the walkway. "Come. There is fish and there is talk. You'll take both or neither, but you'll do it under my roof, because out here the mosquitoes make bad witnesses."

* * *

The long hall smelled of woodsmoke and dried fish and generations of meetings. Freya sat on a bench with his crew while the village filled the space around them—elders first, then adults, then children peering through the slatted walls with the shameless curiosity of the young. Food came: smoked catfish, cassava paste, a sour fermented drink that Naledi drank with suspicious enthusiasm and Okonkwo accepted with grave courtesy.

For a while, nothing was said about caves or treaties. Mama Sefu asked about the river instead—where the sandbars had moved, whether the fish were running at the confluence, how bad the low water was below the gorge. Ordinary questions, but Freya understood them as a test, and answered honestly, and felt something in the room loosen by degrees.

Then the scarred elder—his name was Bokassa, though everyone called him Uncle—leaned forward and said, "Tell us, Captain. What does the company say this place is?"

Freya set down his cup. "On the charts? Open water. Uninhabited. Part of the upper reserve."

"And what does that mean, uninhabited?"

"It means no one is recorded as living here. No land title. No registered settlement. No water rights entered in the company's ledger."

"No rights," Mama Sefu said. "Say the rest of it, Captain. Don't be gentle with us; we've had fourteen months of gentle."

Freya looked at his hands. He had spent ten years perfecting the art of not seeing, and now he was being asked to see out loud, in front of sixty people.

"It means," he said slowly, "that as far as any court or company office is concerned, this village doesn't exist. Your houses can be cleared as obstructions. Your fishing grounds can be licensed to someone else. If the company dammed the river above you tomorrow, there would be no legal person here to file a claim. You couldn't sue, because in their books there's no one to sue." He heard his own voice go flat and hard. "I've known that's how it worked. I've watched it happen downstream, village by village, and I told myself it wasn't mine to fix. I'm not going to tell you I'm sorry, because sorry from a man who kept his eyes shut for ten years is worth exactly what it sounds like."

The hall was very quiet. A child somewhere giggled and was shushed.

Uncle Bokassa grunted—an approving sound, Freya thought, though it might have been indigestion. "At least he doesn't lie prettily," he said to Mama Sefu.

"My grandfather fished this bend," Mama Sefu said, and her voice changed, taking on the cadence of recitation, of something told many times. "His grandfather before him. When the governor's men came up the river—long before the company, before anyone alive—they came to the source and they made a stone. A treaty stone. Our people and the governor's people, marks cut in rock: the watershed belongs to those who live on it, forever, both sides bound. My grandmother could trace the signs with her finger. She taught us where each mark stood." She spread her weathered hands. "And then the company came, and their surveyors drew maps, and on those maps there was no stone, no village, no people. Only reserve. Only blank. They erased us with ink, Captain. No soldiers, no burnings—they didn't need them. Ink was cheaper."

"That's why the caves matter," Ilse said quietly. "The original carving still exists. Full text. It proves the erasure was deliberate—that the company's founding titles rest on falsified charts. It's not just history. It's a legal weapon."

"It is a promise," Mama Sefu said sharply. "Your doctor calls it a weapon. It was a promise made to our ancestors, and broken by men who decided paper outweighs stone." She turned to Ilse. "You understand now why we are angry. It is not that you failed to bring copies. It is that you reminded us the promise exists, and then disappeared, and left us knowing again what was taken. Knowing is heavier than not knowing, Doctor. Remember that, next time you feed it to people."

Ilse bowed her head. "There won't be a next time I fail. I give you my word, and I know what my word is worth in this room, which is why I'll back it with the documents themselves or die trying."

"Die trying," Mama Sefu repeated dryly. "You scientists. Everything at the edge of dying." But something in her face had eased a fraction.

* * *

Later, while Naledi helped the village women haul water and Okonkwo sat with Uncle Bokassa over a scrap of bark paper, sketching script-signs and watching the old man's eyes widen at each one, Freya walked the stilted walkways alone.

He stopped at the edge of the village where the platforms ended and the lagoon began. The evening light lay gold on the water. Children raced canoes in the shallows, shrieking. On the far bank, a man repaired a net with the unhurried competence of someone whose family had repaired nets on that exact stretch for centuries.

None of it was on any map. None of it legally existed. One signature in one office, and a dredge could come up the river and unmake all of it, and the courts would call it development.

Tomas had drawn those maps.

The thought arrived without warning, the way a snag arrives under calm water. Tomas, with his careful hand and his love of precision, had sat in a company office and drawn blankness over places like this. Had he known? Had he told himself it was just lines on paper? Or had he known exactly, and carried it, and tried to recant, and died for it in water much like this?

Freya realized Mama Sefu had come to stand beside him. He hadn't heard her approach.

"Your brother," she said.

He turned. "What about him?"

"Uncle Bokassa remembers a company surveyor, years back. Young. Polite. Came upriver twice with the mapping crews." She studied him with old, unfooled eyes. "He asked questions the other surveyors didn't ask. Wrote things in a separate book. The crew laughed at him for it." She paused. "Was that your brother?"

Freya's throat worked. "Probably."

"He gave my granddaughter a pencil once. She still has it." Mama Sefu looked out at the water. "Whatever he did or didn't draw, Captain, remember that a man is bigger than his worst page. And smaller than his best one. Both at once. That's what makes us people instead of characters."

She patted his arm once, briefly, and walked back toward the hall, leaving Freya alone with the gold light and the sound of children and the weight of a question he'd been avoiding since the photograph arrived in that courier's envelope:

If Tomas had tried to fix it—if he'd died reaching for exactly what Ilse was carrying—then what excuse did Freya have left for staying invisible?

Behind him, from the direction of the landing, Naledi's voice rose suddenly, sharp with alarm: "Captain! Boat coming down the lagoon—no lights!"

Freya turned. Far out on the darkening water, a shape moved against the last of the light, low and fast and running silent.

It was flying no lanterns at all.
