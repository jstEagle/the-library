## 9. Verhoeven's Offer

The patrol boat came at first light, when the river was still the color of pewter and the mist hung in the drowned trees like something the water had exhaled and not yet taken back.

Freya heard it before he saw it—the deep, disciplined throb of a company engine, twin screws turning in perfect time, nothing like the coughing, improvising motors of the settlements. He was already on deck with his coffee going cold in his hand, watching the gray channel where the mist thinned. He'd known they'd come back. Men like Verhoeven didn't ask questions once; they asked them until the answers suited them.

"Captain." Naledi's voice from the wheelhouse hatch, low. She'd slept badly—he'd heard her moving on deck twice in the night—and there was a tightness around her eyes he chose, for the moment, not to read too closely. "That's the same boat."

"I know."

"It's slowing down."

"I know that too."

The *Magpie* lay moored against a rotting log boom below the second gorge, in one of the few stretches of dead water wide enough to turn a boat of her size. There was no point running. The patrol vessel could do twenty knots against their nine on a good day, and this was not a good day—low water, the sandbars showing their teeth everywhere, the channel winding like a snake with its spine broken.

So Freya set down his coffee, straightened his collar out of some old habit he despised, and waited to be boarded.

* * *

Verhoeven came up the *Magpie*'s side ladder himself, which was a message in itself. Commanders sent lieutenants to do the climbing. This man wanted Freya to understand that whatever happened next was between the two of them personally, captain to captain, as if they were members of the same guild.

He was smaller than Freya remembered from the first encounter—or perhaps Freya had simply made him larger in the night, the way you make larger anything that frightens you. Neat, gray at the temples, a company uniform without a single crease despite three days on the river. His boots were dry. That was the detail Freya fixated on: the man had been ferried to the ladder in a launch, carried the last ten meters like cargo, and still his boots were dry. A man who never touched the water he commanded.

"Captain Joubert." He pronounced it correctly. Not the flattened river pronunciation—*Joo-bairt*, worn smooth like a stone—but the old Cape Dutch shape of it, precise, archival. "Or should I say—"

"Don't." Freya kept his voice level. Behind Verhoeven, two crewmen in company gray stood at the rail with the studied blankness of men who had done ugly things and been paid well for the doing. Okonkwo sat on the cargo hatch, whittling a piece of driftwood, apparently deaf. Ilse Marchetti was below, behind a locked cabin door Freya had locked himself.

"No?" Verhoeven smiled faintly. "Forgive me. Old habits. When you've spent years reading personnel files, names become—" He gestured vaguely, a small elegant motion. "Sacred. I'll keep yours where I found it."

"You haven't found anything. You don't know what my name is."

"Of course not." Verhoeven turned, hands clasped behind his back, and surveyed the deck with an appraiser's eye: the patched plating, the coiled lines, Naledi standing rigid by the wheelhouse. "Fine old boat. Pre-consolidation hull, if I'm not mistaken. They built them to last, back then. Like loyalties." He let that sit in the air a moment. "May we speak privately, Captain? You'll forgive the imposition. What I have to say won't take long, and I'd rather your people didn't have to carry it."

"There's nothing you can say to me alone that you can't say in front of my crew."

"As you wish." He said it lightly, but Freya caught the flicker of recalculation behind the eyes—the commander adjusting to a variable. "Then I'll be direct. Dr. Marchetti is aboard this vessel. She is a company employee who has suffered a breakdown, abandoned her post, and is withholding property that does not belong to her. Property of considerable scientific and commercial value. I am authorized to recover both the doctor and the property, by any means necessary." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "I would prefer the means to be unnecessary."

"And if they are?"

"Then this becomes a very different conversation, and I'm afraid it happens on a very different boat." Verhoeven's gaze drifted to the mist-shrouded banks, the black mouths of channels no chart admitted existed. "You've seen what the upper river does to people who aren't prepared for it. The Marchetti expedition was prepared. Twelve experienced personnel. We've recovered one of them so far." He said it flatly, without theater, and somehow that was worse than any threat could have been. "I mention it only because I respect you, Captain. You've survived out here ten years by understanding exactly what things cost. So let me tell you what I'm actually offering, before the arithmetic gets away from us."

Freya said nothing. The river said nothing. Even the mist seemed to lean in.

"Your brother's file," Verhoeven said. "Complete. Every page. Including the incident report from the night he died—including the parts that were removed from the version his family received."

The words went into Freya like a hook going into a fish—clean, practiced, deep. He felt his face do something, some small betrayal, and hated it.

"You don't know anything about my brother."

"I know he worked for us. River survey, lower district, six years. I know he was good—too good, in the end, for his own peace of mind. And I know that after the accident, a young man disappeared from the delta settlements. Changed his name, changed his papers, bought a decaying riverboat under a name that doesn't appear in any registry, and spent a decade making himself into nobody." Verhoeven's voice stayed gentle, almost kind, which was the cruelest register it could have found. "Grief does that to some men. Guilt does it to others. I've often wondered which it was with you."

"My brother died in a boating accident."

"Yes," Verhoeven agreed. "That's what the file says. That's also part of what I'm offering—the difference between what the file says and what happened. There are men in this company who would very much prefer that difference stay submerged. I am not one of them. I'm offering to raise it. For you. Personally."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, you deliver Dr. Marchetti and whatever materials she's carrying to our base camp at the escarpment. That's all. No violence implied, none required. She'll be treated courteously. Her research will be reviewed through proper channels—which, I grant you, move slowly, but they do move. And you—" He spread his hands, presenting the future like a menu. "A full pardon for your previous identity. Clean papers. Your brother's file, unredacted. And a commission. Company captaincy, upper river division. New vessel, new charts—" the smile again, brief and white, "—honest ones, even. You'd run waters no one else is permitted to run. You'd stop being invisible, Captain. Isn't that what a man wants, eventually? To be seen?"

For a moment—one long, treacherous moment—Freya let himself see it. The file open in his hands. Tomas's handwriting. The truth about the last night, whatever it was, laid out in ink instead of rumor. His own name spoken aloud on the river without flinching. A boat with an engine that didn't rattle. Charts that told the truth.

It was a beautiful picture. That was the thing about Verhoeven: he painted beautifully.

"The doctor," Freya said slowly, "is a passenger. She paid her fare."

"She stole from her employers and abandoned colleagues who are now dead or missing."

"That's between her and them."

"Everything on this river is between someone and someone, Captain. That's precisely why it needs managing." Verhoeven checked his watch—a small, expensive gesture. "I'll give you until we reach the escarpment confluence. Two days. You needn't answer now. Think about your brother. Think about how long you've carried him, and what it might feel like to set him down." He moved toward the ladder, then paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back with something that in a better man might have been sincerity. "One more thing, freely given. The doctor is lying to you. About what happened to her expedition, about why she's really going upstream, about what she's carrying. I don't expect you to believe me. But when she lies to you—and she will—I want you to remember that I told you the truth about at least one thing today."

Then he was down the ladder, into the launch, across the pewter water, and the patrol boat's engines gathered themselves and carried it away downstream, leaving a wake that rocked the *Magpie* gently, like a hand patting a shoulder.

* * *

They ran the morning slow, picking through the shallows above the log boom, and nobody talked much. Ilse emerged from the cabin when the patrol boat was long gone, pale and composed, and asked what Verhoeven had wanted. Freya told her: personnel recovery, standard procedure. It wasn't the whole truth and they both knew it, and neither of them reached for the rest.

Naledi handled the bow line through the narrows with her usual competence, but she was quiet in a way that had texture to it—not sulking, not fear. Something being turned over and over, like a stone in a pocket. Freya watched her from the wheelhouse and told himself it was the dead photographer, the wreckage, the general weight of the trip. There were simpler explanations for everything, if you worked at it.

Okonkwo sat at the stern through the afternoon, whittling, watching the banks slide past. Once, as the light went long and gold across the drowned forest, he looked up at Freya and said, without preamble: "My grandmother used to say, the company man offers you a chair, count the legs before you sit."

"Noted," Freya said.

"He offered you something," Okonkwo said. Not a question.

"He offered me a job."

"Mm." Okonkwo returned to his driftwood. "Those chairs usually have three legs."

* * *

Night came down thick, no moon, the stars smeared by high cloud. They tied off in a side slough behind a wall of reeds, engines cold, one lantern lit low in the wheelhouse. Ilse slept—or lay still—in her cabin. Okonkwo took first watch without being asked, a habit they'd fallen into that Freya hadn't quite decided how to feel about.

Freya sat in the wheelhouse with the brother's-file-shaped hole in his chest and did not sleep either.

That was where Naledi found him. She came up the steps quietly, wrapped in her jacket against the river damp, and stood in the doorway a while before she spoke. Outside, the reeds ticked against each other in the current. Somewhere far off, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"You're thinking about saying yes."

It landed in the dark of the wheelhouse like a dropped tool. Freya didn't answer immediately, and the silence was its own answer, and he knew it, and she knew it.

"What makes you say that?"

"Because you've been sitting in the same position for four hours. Because you keep looking at the spot where his boat was, like it's still there. Because—" she hesitated, and then, being Naledi, pushed through anyway, "—because when he said your brother's name, something in your face opened. I've worked for you two years, Captain. I've seen you angry, scared, bored, drunk. I've never seen you look like that. Like a door opening onto a room you thought you'd bricked up."

"Naledi—"

"Tomas." She said the name carefully, having clearly rehearsed it. "That's him. The surveyor. The one the photograph was wearing the badge." She came fully into the wheelhouse and leaned against the chart table, arms folded, facing him square. "What did Verhoeven offer you? Actually."

Freya looked at his hands on the wheel, at the brass fittings worn bright by a decade of his own grip. He could lie to her. He had lied to better liars than Naledi and gotten away with it. But the wheelhouse was small and dark and honest, the way boats are at night, and he was tired in a place tiredness doesn't usually reach.

"A pardon," he said. "For a name I buried. My brother's real file—all of it, including what actually happened the night he died. And a captaincy. New boat. Honest charts, he said." He laughed, short and airless. "Honest charts. From that man."

"And all you have to do is hand over a woman who trusts you enough to sleep behind a door you lock for her."

"When you put it that way it sounds terrible."

"It is terrible." No heat in it. Just the flat clarity she brought to everything, the quality that had made him hire her in the first place—that she saw the river as it was, not as it flattered itself. "But that's not what's eating you. If it were just the deal, you'd have refused him on the spot and been angry about it. You're not refusing. You're weighing." She unfolded her arms. "What's in that file, Captain? What do you think happened to your brother?"

And there it was. Ten years of silence, and the question finally asked aloud by a nineteen-year-old girl in a borrowed jacket, and the strange relief of it, like a splinter coming up.

"I think," Freya said slowly, "that Tomas didn't drown. I think he was killed, and I've always half-known it, and I hid from knowing it because if it was an accident, then the world was just cruel, but if it was murder, then somebody did it, and then I'd have to do something about it. And doing something means being visible. Means being a name again. Means—" he gestured at the darkness beyond the glass, at the whole patient river, "—all of this ending. The hiding. The quiet. The only way I've kept myself alive since he died."

"So Verhoeven isn't offering you money."

"No."

"He's offering you your grief. Wrapped up with a noose inside it."

Freya looked at her. "When did you get wise?"

"River does it." But she didn't smile. She pushed off from the chart table and stood close, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing, beneath the reeds, beneath the water. "Captain. Listen to me. Whatever you decide—yes, no, stall him till the confluence and see how it looks in daylight—you decide it knowing one thing. If you say yes to that man, you don't just hand over the doctor. You hand over her, and Okonkwo, and every village up this river that's got no name on any map, and you hand over yourself, the self you built out of the wreck of the other one. There's no partial yes. I've seen people try to sign halfway. The paper doesn't care. It takes the whole hand."

"Naledi." He kept his voice gentle. "Is there something you're not telling me?"

Something crossed her face—fast, gone, like a fish turning under the surface. For a heartbeat he was sure she was going to say it, whatever it was, the thing she carried that had made her quiet all day with that particular textured silence.

"No," she said. "Nothing. Just—don't say yes, Captain. Please. Whatever's in that file, it can't be worth becoming his."

She went out and left him alone with the wheel and the dark and the river breathing against the hull.

Freya sat a long time. He thought about Tomas at fourteen, poling a dugout through the delta channels, laughing at how seriously little Freya took everything. He thought about the photograph in the courier's envelope, the company badge on his brother's chest, the smile that had looked, in retrospect, like a man smiling while standing somewhere he shouldn't be. He thought about a file in a drawer in some company office, pages that knew the truth of the last night, waiting for him like a lamp in a window.

Two days to the confluence. Verhoeven had said it like a gift. It was not a gift. It was a leash measured out to exactly the right length.

Toward dawn, the cloud broke in the east and the first light came down the gorge, gray-gold, touching the water. And Freya—who had spent ten years learning to read the river's smallest signals, the color of a current, the angle of debris caught on a snag—saw something in the shallows downstream that stopped his breath.

Fuel. A rainbow-slicked patch of it, spreading on the slack water, drifting toward the *Magpie* on the morning current.

Their fuel cache—drums lashed under the tarp on the aft deck, enough to reach the escarpment and back—was leaking. Or had been opened. He was on his feet and moving before the thought finished forming, shouting for Naledi, shouting for Okonkwo, as the light rose on the river and showed him, cut into the tarp's canvas in a neat deliberate line, the work of a knife.
