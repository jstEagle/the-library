## 10. The Turncoat

Naledi woke before dawn the way she always did on the river—her body tuned to some clock older than herself—and lay in her bunk listening for the sound that should have been there and wasn't.

The generator.

Freya ran it twenty minutes every morning at first light, charging the batteries, keeping the radio's capacitors warm, a ritual as fixed as prayer. This morning there was only the tick of the hull cooling against the current and the far-off complaint of a fish eagle somewhere up the drowned forest.

She swung out of her bunk, bare feet on cold deck plating, and knew before she reached the engine room.

The smell hit her first. Diesel—not the sharp clean reek of it in a sealed drum but something thinner, sweeter, wrong. She found the hatch open and the bilge lamp swinging on its hook, throwing shadows that moved like things alive.

Both reserve drums had been opened. Not tipped over—opened. A hole punched low in each one with something narrow and deliberate, an ice pick or a marlinspike, so the fuel would drain slow and quiet into the bilge and from the bilge through the limber holes into the river itself. The Magpie sat in forty fathoms of patience above a hundred liters of diesel spread across the water in a rainbow film that was already drifting downstream.

Naledi stood in the doorway a long time.

Her hands were steady. That was the thing she noticed later, the thing she would come back to in the dark hours afterward: her hands were perfectly, terribly steady, because some part of her had known this was coming since the night she first keyed the handset and whispered coordinates into a channel she'd been told was monitored for weather only.

* * *

By full light the whole boat knew.

Freya crouched at the ruptured drums without touching them, reading the scene the way he read water—the angle of the punctures, the absence of splintered wood where a tool would have braced, the fact that whoever did it had knelt here long enough to work two holes clean and cap them after with rags soaked in bilge water to hide the smell until morning. Patient work. Unhurried work. Work done by someone who knew no one would walk past the engine room between midnight and four.

Okonkwo stood at the top of the engine room ladder with his arms folded, and it was his face Freya looked at last, and longest.

The big man said nothing. He never said anything. He stood there with the gray dawn behind him and let himself be looked at, and Naledi watched Freya's jaw work and understood with a sick lurch that the evidence had already done its quiet arithmetic: who aboard could move silently? Who aboard had been awake at odd hours? Who aboard had a reason—a mission, a handler, a war of his own—that none of them fully knew?

"Your kit," Freya said quietly. "Open it."

Okonkwo unslung the canvas satchel without protest. Inside: notebooks dense with his careful hand, a folding lamp, a tin of pencils worn to stubs, a coil of copper wire, spare batteries. And a marlinspike. Standard boat's tool, the kind hung in a bracket by the wheelhouse door—but the bracket was empty this morning, and here it was, and there was a fine black crumb of rubber gasket caught in its point.

Naledi saw Freya see it. Saw him close his eyes for one second, one breath, the way a man does when the river shows him a snag exactly where he feared one.

"I didn't do this," Okonkwo said.

It was maybe the fourth sentence Naledi had ever heard him speak. It landed in the silence like a stone in still water.

"No," Freya said at last, and his voice was flat and terrible. "I don't think you did." He turned then, slowly, and his eyes went down the deck—to Ilse at the rail, to the scummy rainbow on the water, to the wheelhouse where the radio sat with its handset coiled neat as a sleeping snake—

—and stopped on Naledi.

He didn't say anything. He just looked at her, and she felt the look go through her like a gaff hook, and she knew that he knew, or was one question away from knowing, and every plan she had ever made about how this moment would come—bravely, usefully, on her own terms—dissolved like sugar in the rain.

"Naledi," Freya said. "When did you last check the antenna lead?"

* * *

Three weeks earlier. The depot at Kessel's Landing, the night before they'd taken the contract.

She'd been alone aboard. Freya ashore settling accounts with the fuel agent, arguing the price of diesel the way he argued everything—slowly, stubbornly, winning. The radio had crackled its evening weather summary and gone quiet, and Naledi had been wiping down the wheelhouse glass when the handset buzzed.

Not the working channel. The other one—the one Freya kept tuned low, the one he called the ghost band, the frequency old river men used for gossip and song requests and nothing anyone would admit to.

A woman's voice, clipped and pleasant: *"Magpie, Magpie, this is Vasseur Consolidated relay. Confirm your position and heading."*

Naledi had almost laughed. Wrong number. But the voice had said her name next. Her full name. *Naledi Mokoena.* And then it had said her mother's name, and her mother's street, in Sankofa Bend, three hundred kilometers downstream, where the company bought palm oil from every farmer on the left bank and set the price at whatever the farmers could not refuse.

*"We understand Captain Joubert has been offered a contract,"* the voice said. *"We'd like you to help us keep track of it. Nothing difficult. Positions. Landings. Who comes aboard. You'll be doing your family a service, Miss Mokoena. Refuse, and we'll have to assume you're unfriendly, and we'll have to treat Sankofa Bend accordingly. There are inspections. There are levies. There are fires that start in dry seasons."*

She had said yes. God help her, she had said yes in a voice that barely shook at all, and after that it had been easy, horribly easy—one transmission a week, coded as radio checks, coordinates dressed up as drift reports. She told herself it was nothing. She told herself everyone spied on everyone out here. She told herself the expedition was probably fine, that the company just wanted its people back, that Freya would never know and no one would ever be hurt.

Then came the patrol boat, and Verhoeven saying Freya's buried name like a knife laid on a table. Then the wreckage of the launch, shot full of holes. Then Ilse burning her journal pages at midnight. Then the village off the maps, and the elders' fury, and the word *erased* used about living people the way you'd use it about ink.

And last night, a new instruction, the first that had made her hands shake: *"Ensure the vessel is immobilized above the gorge. The captain must accept assistance when it is offered. Do this and your family's account is settled in full."*

She had taken the marlinspike from its bracket. She had knelt in the engine room in the dark with her heart going like a paddle wheel, and she had made the holes, and capped them, and returned the spike to the wrong place—she realized that now, that single stupid error, hung back on the wrong hook—and gone to her bunk and lain awake listening to the diesel whisper out of her captain's boat and into the river, and she had thought: *this is the last thing. After this they leave my mother alone.*

She had believed it the way you believe a sandbar will hold.

* * *

She confessed on the foredeck, because she couldn't do it below, couldn't do it in a compartment, needed sky.

Freya stood with his arms crossed and his face like carved wood while she talked. Naledi made herself look at him the whole way through. She owed him that much at least—to be seen taking it, not to spill it at his boots like something dumped overboard.

She told all of it. The call at Kessel's Landing. The names. The weekly transmissions. The instruction. When she got to the part about Sankofa Bend her voice finally broke, and she hated that, hated the weakness of it, and pushed on anyway.

"They said fires," she finished. "They said it like they were talking about weather. I thought—I thought if I just did this one thing—" She stopped. There was no end to that sentence that wasn't a lie. "I betrayed you. From the second week. Before the patrol boat, before any of it. Everything we've run since Kessel's Landing, they've known where we were because I told them."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere in the drowned trees a dove was calling, idiotically peaceful.

Ilse had drifted near, and Okonkwo stood at the rail with his great stillness, and even they were looking at her now, and Naledi wished the river would take her the way it took everything else, without malice, without memory.

Freya was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough, like a winch taking strain.

"The fuel," he said. "That was them too. Through you."

"Yes."

"Not Okonkwo."

"No." She swallowed. "I hung the spike back wrong. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I let you look at him like that. He's the most honest man on this boat and I let you—" Her throat closed.

Freya turned away. He walked to the rail and stood looking at the rainbow of diesel thinning on the brown water, and Naledi watched his shoulders and tried to read the weather in them and could not.

"You want to know what I'm thinking," he said finally, without turning. "You're thinking I'm deciding whether to put you off at the next landing. Tie you up for Verhoeven's people myself, maybe—turncoat for turncoat, fair trade." A short, bitter breath that wasn't quite a laugh. "Or whether to forgive you, because you're nineteen and they had your mother, and because I've spent ten years teaching you that the only safe thing on this river is to be invisible, and then I broke my own rule and dragged you upstream into this."

"Captain—"

"I'm thinking," he said, "that Verhoeven has known our position every day for three weeks, and we are still alive. Ask yourself why." Now he turned, and his eyes were bright and hard and wet all at once. "Because he doesn't want us dead yet. Because he wants us to arrive. Every report you sent made us more useful to him, not less. Your betrayal bought us passage, Naledi. That's the joke. That's the filthy joke of it. The company has been escorting us upriver this whole time, using you as their pilot, straight toward the caves they mean to blow apart—with us aboard when the charges go, if it comes to that."

The words went through her colder than the river. She hadn't thought of it that way. She had thought of herself as a leak; she had not understood she was also a lure.

"So no," Freya said. "I'm not putting you off. And I'm not forgiving you either—not today. Today you're going to sit down at that radio with me beside you, and you're going to send one more report. Exactly what they asked for. The Magpie is immobilized, fuel destroyed, requesting assistance per instructions." His mouth bent into something that had no humor in it at all. "And then we're going to see what help they offer, and how fast, and from which direction. Because a turncoat who turns twice is worth more than a hundred honest fools, if she turns at the right moment."

Naledi stared at him. "You want me to keep spying."

"I want you to keep *lying*," Freya said. "There's a difference, and it's the width of this river, and you're going to learn to swim in it." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice so only she could hear. "You broke faith with me, girl. That's real, and it doesn't wash off in a day, and maybe it never washes off entirely. But your family is downstream, and mine is dead, and neither of those facts changes what's coming. So here's your penance, and it's heavier than confession: from this hour, everything you send them, you send to save them. You get to carry both weights at once. That's what loyalty costs when you've spent it. Not a clean slate. A double load."

Her eyes were burning. She nodded, once, because speech was beyond her.

"And Naledi." He paused at the wheelhouse door. "If you transmit one word to them that I haven't approved—if you so much as breathe our true position into that handset—I won't punish you. I'll just know, the way I know the depth under a green slick of water. And everything after that, we do without you. Understood?"

"Understood," she whispered.

He went inside. The door clicked shut behind him, gentle as ever, and somehow that gentleness was the worst punishment of all.

* * *

They sent the message at noon, Naledi's fingers on the key, Freya standing at her shoulder reading every character as it went out into the air. *Magpie immobilized above the gorge. Fuel stores sabotaged. Requesting assistance per prior arrangement. Advise.*

The reply came in ninety minutes, which told Naledi more than the words did: someone was sitting on that frequency, waiting, close enough to answer fast.

*Assistance en route. Hold position. Do not attempt repairs. Company vessel will reach you within 48 hours with fuel and escort to base camp. Your account is noted with appreciation, Mokoena.*

*Your account is noted with appreciation.* She read it twice and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very cold. That was how the company wrote about cargo. About barrels. About bodies.

"They're coming to collect us," Ilse said from the doorway. She had heard; of course she had heard; the wheelhouse was the size of a coffin. "Fuel, escort, base camp. They think the sabotage worked. They think we're helpless."

"We are helpless," Freya said mildly. "That's the beauty of it. A helpless boat is the last thing they'll expect to move." He was already pulling charts from the drawer, spreading them over the wheelhouse desk, his finger tracing the brown thread of the river up into the gorge where the navigable water died. "Forty-eight hours. No fuel. No engine." His finger stopped on a faded dotted line running overland along the old logging route, skids and grades drawn by hands fifty years dead. "But we were never a boat crew anyway. We're a hauling crew now."

Naledi looked at the dotted line and understood what it meant: the portage. The overland drag through country littered, if Ilse's face was anything to go by, with the bones of the expedition that had gone before them.

"Captain," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "The logging route—it passes within sight of the old fire tower at Kilometer Forty-One. If I can get to it, there's a relay mast still standing. I could—" She stopped, hearing herself, hearing what she was offering: to climb, alone, toward the thing she'd betrayed them all for. "I could make sure whatever we broadcast from the source actually reaches downriver. Every settlement. Even the ones the company listens to."

Freya studied her for a long moment. Whatever moved behind his eyes, he kept it there.

"Eat first," was all he said. "We break camp at dusk."

Outside, the light was already going amber over the drowned forest, and far downstream—too far to see, close enough to feel—something was coming up the river toward them, patient and certain, expecting to find a crippled boat and a broken crew.

Naledi put her hand flat against the Magpie's deck plating, the way she used to when she was new and scared, and made the old silent promise to the boat, the river, the dead, and the living.

This time, she meant to keep it.
