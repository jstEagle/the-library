## 11. Portage of Bones

The Magpie's engine coughed twice and died with the finality of a coffin lid.

Freya let the boat drift a moment in the green silence of the drowned forest, hand still on the throttle as if the gesture alone might coax it back to life. It didn't. The fuel gauge needle lay flat against its peg like something dead on a road.

"That's it," he said. "That's all she had."

Naledi stood at the bow, one hand shading her eyes against the light slanting through the canopy. "You said we'd make the gorge."

"I said I hoped." He moved aft and pulled the tarp off the reserve drum he'd been lying to himself about for two days. He tipped it. A thin gurgle, then nothing—just a smell of fumes and old metal. Maybe four liters sloshing at the bottom, enough to run the bilge pump and nothing else. Enough to keep them alive, not enough to move them.

Okonkwo crouched near the stern, saying nothing, which was his way. But his eyes went to the eastern bank, where the trees thinned into a corridor of secondary growth—young, straight-trunked, growing over something older. Freya had noticed it an hour ago from the water and told himself it meant nothing. Old logging country. There was logging country all through these reaches, abandoned when the company decided floating timber downriver cost less than cutting it here.

He looked again. Between the young trunks, half-swallowed by vine, he could make out the rusted spine of a rail.

"Skids," he said aloud. "There was a portage line here. Before my time."

Ilse Marchetti came out of the cabin rubbing sleep from her face, and when she saw the dead gauge, something in her posture changed—not surprise. Recognition. As if she'd known this stretch of river would be where their luck ran out, and had been waiting for it the way you wait for a diagnosis.

"The skid road runs to the head of the gorge," she said quietly. "My expedition used it. Coming down."

"Coming down," Freya repeated. "In what?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. Her eyes flicked to Naledi, to Okonkwo, and then away toward the bank, and Freya understood that whatever she was about to say next had been rehearsed and discarded a dozen times since they'd found the wreck of her launch.

* * *

They spent the afternoon getting the Magpie ready to travel overland, which was a sentence Freya never thought he'd construct about his own boat.

The skid rails emerged from the undergrowth like the ribs of some enormous animal—a narrow-gauge logging tramway, laid on rotting ties, running dead straight through the forest toward the sound of water. Somewhere along its length there would be a winch house, or what remained of one. Freya had hauled boats overland before, in his first years on the Vasseur, before invisibility became his craft. He knew the arithmetic: every kilogram mattered, every wheel bearing mattered, and above all the grade mattered, because a boat on skids rolling backward downhill with people under it was not a boat anymore but a weapon.

They cleared brush until his shoulders burned. Naledi worked like someone trying not to think, swinging the machete in flat, vicious strokes, and Freya caught himself watching her—the set of her jaw, the way she checked the treeline between cuts—and made himself stop. He'd deal with what he suspected about Naledi later, or he wouldn't. Out here, suspicion was a luxury. Out here you needed hands.

By late light they had forty meters of track cleared and the first pair of skids bolted under the hull. Okonkwo produced rope and chain from the expedition gear Ilse carried, and together they rigged a bridle off the bow cleat that Freya triple-checked, because the Magpie was the last true thing he owned and he did not intend to watch her tear herself apart on a century-old logging rail.

They camped aboard, tied off, engine cold. Mosquitoes came up off the water in columns. Nobody talked much.

It was Naledi who broke the silence, from the bow where she kept first watch. "Captain. There's a light."

Freya came forward. Through the trees, upslope, maybe three hundred meters along the skid line: a faint orange flicker, steady, too regular to be lightning-struck peat.

"A camp," Ilse said behind him. Her voice had gone thin.

"You know whose camp?" Freya asked.

"Yes." She said it the way you say *yes* to a question you've been dreading. "I know exactly whose."

* * *

They reached it at first light, and Freya made everyone wait at the edge of the clearing while he went in alone, because ten years on this river had taught him that the dead deserve to be approached by one person, quietly, and not by a crowd.

The camp was a dome tent gone gray with mildew, a cook fire long cold, a scatter of equipment laid out with the neatness of someone who expected to come back for it. Camera cases. A solar charger furred with moss. A tin plate with a spoon fused to it by rain and time.

And beside the tent, wrapped in a groundsheet and weighted with stones because the man who did it hadn't been able to dig deep enough in root-bound soil, the photographer.

Freya knelt. He'd seen dead men before—more than a river captain likes to admit—but there is a difference between the dead of accidents and the dead of abandonment, and this was the second kind. The body had been dead for weeks. Weeks, in this heat, and no one had come. No search party. No company patrol. Verhoeven's courteous, murderous wake had passed up and down this river asking after *stray personnel*, and it had never once stopped here.

He found the identification in a waterproof pouch inside the tent. Daniel Osei. Expedition photographer. And beneath the ID card, a notebook—not a journal of observations but letters, unsent, written in a careful hand that deteriorated page by page until the last entries were just names. Names of people downstream. His mother's name, Freya guessed, from how often it recurred.

The last legible entry read: *Ilse said she'd send help from the village. That was eleven days ago. I've stopped believing in the village.*

Freya sat back on his heels and breathed through it. Then he called the others in.

* * *

Ilse Marchetti held herself together for exactly as long as it took her to kneel beside the groundsheet and touch it, once, with two fingers, the way you touch something to confirm it's real.

Then she came apart.

Not loudly—that was the terrible thing. She folded forward over her knees and made a sound Freya had only heard once before, at his brother's funeral, from his own mother: a low, rhythmic keening, grief with nowhere to go, cycling on itself. Naledi went to her and put a hand on her shoulder and Ilse grabbed it and held on like a drowning woman.

"She stayed," Ilse said. "I told her—I told them all—to stay with the records, stay hidden, and I would draw them off. I would take the launch, take the attention, lead them downriver, and circle back in five days. Five days." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Freya had heard in ten years on the Vasseur. "I told myself I was being strategic. I told myself a leader has to survive to tell the story. And then I got downriver and I was afraid, actually afraid, for the first time, and I kept going. I hired you. I paid you in scrip I stole from my own sponsors. I sat in your cabin eating your food and I let you believe I'd been separated from my team by bandits."

"Bandits don't shoot a launch full of holes and strip it clean," Freya said. "Company men do."

"Yes." She lifted her head. Her face was wrecked, stripped, older by a decade than it had been at the fuel depot. "Verhoeven's men. They hit us the night after we came down from the caves. We scattered into the forest with what we could carry. I took the copies—the photographs, the rubbings, Okonkwo's working translation—and I ran, and I told myself the rest of them would be safer without me, because I was the one they wanted. And maybe that was even true. But I didn't come back, Captain. Eleven days. Twelve. I didn't come back for any of them, and Daniel—" her voice broke and reassembled itself into something harder, "—Daniel waited for me. He wrote to his mother every day because I taught him that discipline, that a record must be kept, and he kept his record waiting for me, and I was downriver buying your silence with my sponsors' money."

The clearing was very quiet. Somewhere above, a fish eagle screamed and screamed.

"Is anyone else alive?" Naledi asked softly.

"I don't know." Ilse wiped her face with the heel of her hand, an almost violent gesture, done. "Two of my team were from the settlements. The elders' people. If anyone survived, they'd have gone to ground in villages like the one that sheltered us—villages that hate me now, and have every right to. The other two—" She stopped. Looked at the groundsheet. Didn't finish.

Freya stood. His knees cracked. He looked down at Ilse Marchetti, celebrated scientist, woman who had found the truth of the river and carried it out of the dark, and he felt something he hadn't expected: not anger. Recognition.

"I hid for ten years," he said. "Changed my name. Let the whole river believe Freya Joubert was nobody, from nowhere, owing nothing. You know why? Because my brother died for the same truth you're carrying, and I couldn't afford to care. Caring gets you killed. So I made not-caring into a profession." He crouched so they were eye to eye. "I'm not telling you that to forgive you. I'm telling you so you understand that I know exactly what it looks like when survival wins. And I know it doesn't feel like winning. It feels like this clearing."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. "Your brother," she said. "Tomas. He tried to recant, you know. Did I ever tell you that? Not just privately. He filed a protest. A formal one, through company channels, naming the falsified charts. It went to Verhoeven's desk and Verhoeven buried it, and six weeks later Tomas was dead, and the file was dead, and everyone who'd signed it as witness had been transferred to stations so remote they might as well have been erased themselves." She paused. "Daniel photographed the original survey documents. Tomas's originals. They're in my pack, along with everything else. I have been carrying your brother's work up this river for two months, Captain, and I couldn't even keep one photographer alive."

* * *

They buried Daniel Osei properly, or as properly as the ground allowed. Okonkwo dug for two hours without stopping, sweating through his shirt, saying nothing, and when the grave was deep enough they lowered him wrapped in the groundsheet that had failed to preserve him and Naledi read the names from his notebook aloud—all of them, the mother most often—because someone should hear them spoken in the country where he died.

Then they marked the grave with a length of skid rail driven upright into the earth, and Freya burned the letters? No. He thought about burning them, and instead sealed them in an oilcloth packet and put them in the Magpie's strongbox, next to the contract papers and the photograph of Tomas wearing the company badge. Evidence traveled. Grief stayed. He was learning the difference.

They worked through the rest of the day in a silence that had changed quality—no longer the silence of strangers tolerating one another, but the silence of people who had seen the same thing and could not unsee it. They winched the Magpie onto the first straight run of rail using the ancient hand-crab winch from the collapsed winch house, its gears shrieking like something being murdered, and the boat came up the bank inch by inch, hull groaning, until she sat on her skids among the trees like a shipwreck that had decided to walk.

Freya stood at the bow as they set the first pull, Naledi and Okonkwo hauling on the bridle ropes, Ilse prying ties loose ahead of them, and felt the strangest emotion of the entire journey: pride. Absurd, misplaced pride, in a boat moving at a walking pace through a forest, carrying a dead man's letters and a live woman's confession toward a source that wanted to kill them all.

They made six hundred meters before dark. Six hundred meters, and the gorge still invisible ahead, and somewhere beyond the gorge the base camp, and somewhere beyond the base camp the caves, and somewhere in the caves, if Ilse was telling the whole truth now—and he believed she finally was—his brother's last honest survey, waiting in the dark for a decade.

That night, on second watch, Naledi sat down beside him at the cold fire without being asked.

"Captain," she said. "If we find anything up there—if we find Tomas's survey, or the treaty stone, or any of it—what happens to us? To the people who carried it?"

Freya looked at her in the starlight, this young woman who had begged him to take the contract, who had argued for the money and the adventure, and whom he had watched checking the treeline for two days now with the eyes of someone listening for a radio that wasn't switched on.

"We'll find out together," he said.

She nodded slowly, and he saw her swallow, and he saw her glance—just once, quickly, guiltily—at the canvas satchel where the radio handset slept.

He said nothing. But he lay awake after that, staring up through the canopy, doing the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do: the courier who'd known too much, the patrol boat that had found them too easily, the schedule of calls that had gone silent at exactly the moment a certain voice might have been needed to report their position.

The river had shown him a body today. It was about to show him a traitor.

He could feel it coming down the skid road like weather.
