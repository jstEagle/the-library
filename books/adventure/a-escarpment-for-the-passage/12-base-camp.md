## 12. Base Camp

They came down off the portage trail in the last grey hour before dawn, and the base camp announced itself the way a wound announces infection: by smell.

Diesel first, thick and sweet, hanging over the escarpment's shoulder like weather. Then woodsmoke, and under it something chemical and sharp that Freya couldn't name until they crested the final rise and he saw the flare stack burning off gas at the camp's edge, a ragged orange tongue licking at the dark. Below them, strung along the base of the cliffs like a parasite fastened to a host, lay the company's operation: corrugated roofs, floodlights on poles, a helicopter pad with its net staked down, and beyond the wire, stacked in long ranks under tarps, enough equipment to build a town or bury one.

"Forty men, maybe fifty," Okonkwo said. He had gone quiet again since the caves—no, not since the caves, Freya corrected himself; since the body. Since the photographer with his camera still slung across his chest like a child's toy, lying where the river had left him among the skids and the rotten crates. Okonkwo spoke less now, but when he spoke it was with the economy of a man who had decided words were ammunition. "Rotating watch. Two gates—the vehicle gate on the flats, and the foot gate by the water tower. The foot gate is ours."

"Naledi," Freya said.

She was already crouched over the radio, its casing open, her fingers moving inside it with a confidence that still made something twist behind his ribs. Three weeks of transmissions. Three weeks of her voice going out over the frequencies in coded check-ins while he slept ten feet away, trusting her with his boat, his life, everything he had left. And all of it coerced, all of it fear for a mother and two sisters in a settlement the company could squeeze shut like a fist. He had decided—had chosen, deliberately, the way you choose a heading in fog—to believe her. It didn't stop the twist. Belief and forgiveness were different rivers that only sometimes shared a source.

"They're expecting the resupply run tomorrow morning," Naledi said without looking up. "The *Kestrel*, out of Mile 40 depot. I gave them your call sign because I told them you'd bought the Kestrel's schedule off a drunk pilot at the depot." She glanced back at him, and in the light of the shaded lamp her face was young and old at once. "They'll be watching for a boat coming up the channel at first light. So we don't come up the channel."

"We walk in through the foot gate with the dawn shift," Okonkwo finished. "Ilse goes as cargo."

* * *

Cargo turned out to mean a canvas tarp, a stretcher, and Ilse Marchetti lying under both with a thermometer clenched between her teeth and a fever she did not have. She had argued against it for an hour—argued with the flat, exhausted stubbornness that had carried her this far—and lost, because Okonkwo had looked at her and said, "You are the one person in this camp they will shoot on sight if they recognize your face. Humility is cheaper than burial." After that she'd lain down under the tarp and let them strap her in, and if her jaw was tight Freya pretended not to see it.

The foot gate was exactly what Okonkwo had promised: a plank-and-wire barrier beside the water tower, manned by a single sentry who was more asleep than awake at shift change. What Okonkwo had not promised—or perhaps had simply not known—was the paperwork. A clipboard hung on a nail, and the sentry woke enough to want names.

"Relief crew for the *Kestrel* run," Freya said. He'd spent a decade building a voice that was nothing at all, a voice like silted water, and he used it now. "Boat's coming up the channel behind us. We're early. Boss wanted the medical case seen to before the unloading."

The sentry squinted at the stretcher. At Naledi, who stood with her hands dirty and her eyes downcast in the way of someone who has been yelled at before and expects it again. At Okonkwo, who was carrying the stretcher's front handles and radiating the specific boredom of a man who has done this job too many years for too little pay.

"Sick one?"

"Fever. Depot doctor says quarantine till the med officer looks at her."

That word—*quarantine*—worked on sentries the way a lure works on a lazy fish. Nobody wants to touch a fever case. The man waved them through with two fingers and went back to his stool, and Freya walked into the heart of the machine that had killed his brother, wearing another man's errand like a coat.

* * *

Inside the wire, the camp resolved from silhouette into detail, and the detail was worse than the silhouette.

He had expected a military operation—rifles, sandbags, the bored brutality of hired muscle. There was some of that; armed men moved between the buildings in pairs, and a watchtower swept its light across the flats in slow, metronome arcs. But what struck him, what lodged under his skin like a splinter, was how *administrative* it all was. Filing cabinets under canvas awnings. A mess tent with a chalkboard menu. Typewriters audible through the walls of the largest structure, clattering away in the pre-dawn dark like insects, recording, recording, recording.

"That's the survey office," Okonkwo murmured, nodding toward the clatter. "Where maps go to die."

They were supposed to go straight to the infirmary tent, deliver their fever case, melt into the background until the *Kestrel*'s real arrival created whatever chaos a false schedule could create. But the route took them past a long storage shed whose doors stood open to vent the heat, and Freya looked inside, and stopped walking.

Crates. Dozens of them, stenciled in red block letters: **HIGH EXPLOSIVES — AMMONITE — HANDLE WITH CARE — BLASTING AGENT CLASS 2**. They were packed tight as teeth, row after row, and beside each crate sat its complement of detonating cord and electric caps in padded boxes, all of it stacked with the tidy precision of people who believed absolutely in what they were doing.

Naledi counted under her breath. "Enough to bring down half the escarpment."

"Not half," Okonkwo said quietly. "The mouth. The galleries. Everything that can't be carried out gets collapsed behind them. That's standard practice for—" He stopped himself, but the sentence had already shown its shape. Standard practice for erasure.

Freya stood in the doorway and let the cold move through him. Not rage—not yet; rage was hot and this was something else, something with a longer shelf life. This was the fury of a man reading a ledger. Here is the cost, the ledger said. Here it is in crates, priced, inventoried, signed for. Somewhere in that survey office a clerk had filled out a requisition form for enough explosive to murder five hundred years of carved stone, and somewhere above that clerk a manager had approved it, and somewhere above the manager men in offices in cities Freya had never seen had signed their names without ever smelling the diesel or hearing the river. The machinery wasn't cruel. That was the terrible thing. It was *tidy*. It would erase the caves the way it had erased the villages off the charts—one clean entry at a time, no malice required, just process.

"We should keep moving," Naledi whispered.

He kept moving. But his eyes kept working, cataloguing, the way a pilot's eyes work on unfamiliar water.

* * *

The second shed was locked, and that was what told him it mattered.

Everything else in this camp was casual—tarps loose, doors ajar, the whole compound breathing the sloppy confidence of an operation that believed itself invisible at the end of the world. But this shed had a padlock the size of a fist and a guard dozing in a chair beside it, and when a supply sergeant came to unlock it while they waited out a patrol's passing, Freya saw what was inside, and had to turn his face away so that no one would read it.

Artifacts.

Rows of shelving, floor to ceiling, and on the shelves the contents of a civilization laid out like evidence in a trial nobody intended to hold. Carved wooden paddles with ceremonial heads. Pottery in ranked sizes, the painted patterns worn by centuries of hands. Stone tools wrapped in newspaper. Textiles folded into acid paper. And masks—perhaps forty of them, staring out from the shelves with expressions that had outlasted every empire that tried to own them, each one tagged with a paper label in neat typewritten script: a number, a grid reference, a date.

Grid references. Freya understood, with a lurch, that those numbers were coordinates. Coordinates of villages. Villages that did not exist on any map he had ever piloted by, villages whose people had been told for generations that the law did not know them, could not see them, and therefore owed them nothing—not water rights, not land, not acknowledgment. And here was the proof that the company had always known exactly where they were. You don't catalogue what you haven't found. You don't tag a mask with a grid reference unless a survey team stood in that village and wrote the coordinates down, and then went home and drew the map *without it*.

The theft wasn't the masks. The theft was the map.

"Ilse needs to see this," Naledi breathed beside him.

"Ilse needs to stay on that stretcher," Freya said, "until we're past the infirmary." But even as he said it, the tarp shifted, and Ilse Marchetti's hand came out from under the canvas and gripped his wrist with a strength that fever could not have faked.

"The tall shelf," she whispered. "Third row. The stone tablets."

He looked. On the third row of the tallest shelving unit, lying flat like archived ledgers, were six slabs of dark stone, each one rubbed with white pigment so the carved characters stood out in relief. Rubbed—someone had prepared them for study. Someone in this camp had been *reading* them.

"They're copies," Ilse breathed. "Castings. The originals are still in the cave—they wouldn't risk moving them before demolition. But they've been translating. Verhoeven has had translators working here for weeks. He knows what the treaty says, Freya. He's known since before my expedition ever launched. That's why we were funded—that's why *anyone* gets funded. The company sends expeditions to find the records so it can be the first to destroy them."

The clatter of typewriters seemed suddenly very loud. Freya thought of Tomas at his drafting table, years ago, the lamplight and the smell of ink, drawing a coastline that he knew was false. Had it started like this? A shed, a shelf, a small compromise with a large salary? His brother had not been a villain. He had been a man who took one step onto ground that felt solid and discovered too late that the whole bank was undercut.

"Move," Freya said softly. "All of us. Now."

* * *

They delivered their fever case to the infirmary—a genuine medic, half-asleep, who prodded Ilse's wrist, muttered about depot doctors and their theatrics, and ordered her into a cot behind a screen, which was better than they'd hoped and worse than they needed. It pinned her. Freya left Naledi sitting beside the cot with the face of a worried junior crewman, and stepped outside into the blue-black hour before the generators throttled up, and found Okonkwo waiting by the water barrels with news that rearranged the whole board.

"The *Kestrel* isn't coming," Okonkwo said. "I checked the traffic log posted at the comms tent while you were playing sick-warden. The company cancelled all civilian traffic yesterday. Weather advisory—storm system in the highlands." He paused. "Freya. Storms in the highlands mean the river's coming up. Early. Days early."

Freya looked north, toward the escarpment's black mass against the slightly-less-black sky, and felt the river in his body the way he always felt it, a pressure behind the sternum, a sense of water gathering weight somewhere upstream. Flood season was his oldest enemy and his deepest knowledge. If the highlands were raining, then the gorge below them would become a wall of brown water within two days, and the gorge above them—

Above them was the source. And the caves. And the charges already crated and waiting.

"So Verhoeven's schedule moves up too," he said. "Demolition before the flood cuts the road."

"Demolition," Okonkwo agreed, "possibly tomorrow."

Somewhere across the compound a whistle blew—shift change, the night watch handing over to the day. Floodlights stuttered on along the wire, and in their glare Freya saw, being wheeled across the yard by two sweating crewmen, a low trailer loaded with spools of detonating cord, moving toward the cliff trail. Toward the caves.

He had come here under a false schedule with a fake fever and a decade of practiced invisibility, and none of it mattered anymore, because the timetable had changed and the stone was scheduled to die. Every plan he'd built on the river's patience was now built on sand.

He found Naledi's eyes through the infirmary doorway. She read his face the way she'd learned to read water.

"It's tonight, isn't it," she said. "Whatever we're going to do. It has to be tonight."

Behind her, from the cot, Ilse Marchetti pulled the curtain aside, and her voice came out steady and cold as deep water: "Then somebody had better tell me how we get three people into a guarded cave, past fifty men, before sunrise."
