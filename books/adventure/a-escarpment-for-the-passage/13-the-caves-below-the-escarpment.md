## 13. The Caves Below the Escarpment

Okonkwo had spent eleven years learning to be silent. It was a discipline like any other—breath controlled, hands still, eyes down and moving slowly enough that no one bothered to track them. Silence had kept him alive through three expeditions that ended badly and one that ended in handcuffs at a border crossing he would not name even to himself. Silence was a craft, and he had believed himself its master.

Then the escarpment opened its mouth, and every skill he owned failed him at once.

They went in an hour before dawn, while the base camp still smelled of diesel and cooked porridge and the sentries' attention had gone soft and inward, the way it always did in the last dark hour when the body argued with the mind about what mattered. Ilse had mapped the guard rotations from six weeks of captivity-adjacent observation she described as "fieldwork," which told Okonkwo more about her than any confession had. Freya moved through the camp's margins like a man walking through his own house in the dark—not fast, not slow, just certain, angling between the fuel drums and the generator shed along paths no one else could have known existed.

"How," Okonkwo whispered, as they crouched behind a stack of drill casing.

"Ten years on this river." Freya didn't look back. "You learn where the company builds things. They build them the same way everywhere. Lazy corners."

The cave mouth was nothing—a shadow in the limestone below the third terrace of the escarpment, screened by deadfall that someone had arranged there long ago and let the seasons finish the job. Ilse pulled the branches aside with the tenderness of a woman uncovering a sleeping child. Beyond them, a passage sloped down into absolute black, breathing cool air against their faces. The air tasted of stone and water and something older than either—mineral and vegetal at once, like the inside of the river's own throat.

"Demolition crew is scheduled for the main gallery at oh-eight-hundred," Ilse said. "That gives us—" she checked her watch by feel, shielding the dial— "four hours. Maybe five if the charges are late. Company time runs on company patience."

"And if we're still inside when they're not late?" Freya asked.

Okonkwo heard rather than saw the look she gave him. "Then we'll have bigger problems than being late for breakfast."

He shouldered the lamp and the satchel of notebooks and went first into the dark, because that was the arrangement, and because—and this surprised him, arriving as it did without ceremony—the dark did not frighten him anymore. What frightened him was what waited at the end of it.

* * *

The passage narrowed, then widened, then narrowed again, the way passages do when they have been shaped by water over spans of time that make human history look like weather. Okonkwo walked with one hand on the wall. Limestone under his palm: slick in places, gritty in others, and once—this stopped him mid-stride—deliberately smooth, dressed by tools, a section of wall the width of a doorway ground flat so that hands could pass without catching.

"Here," he said. His voice came out strange in the confined air, flattened and close. "Feel this."

Freya ran his fingers along the dressed stone. Even in the lamplight's spill Okonkwo could see the captain's face change—the wariness thinning into something rawer.

"Somebody wanted people to come through here," Freya said. "Comfortably. For a long time."

"For centuries." Okonkwo lifted the lamp higher. "This is not a cave, Captain. This is a road."

Beyond the dressed threshold the gallery opened like a cathedral that had grown rather than been built. The lamp's beam reached and reached and finally gave up, but in the middle distance the walls rose sheer and pale, and across those walls—floor to ceiling, following the stone's own contours as though the carvers had refused to fight the rock and instead joined it—ran lines of script.

Okonkwo set the lamp down on a natural shelf with hands that were not entirely steady. He had prepared for this. He had spent two years preparing for this: the comparative lexicon he'd built from trade-pidgin survivals downriver, the structural grammar he'd reconstructed from the boundary stones in the delta marshes, the months in Ilse's university basement cross-referencing missionary transcriptions that everyone else had dismissed as gibberish or worse, as decoration. He knew this writing the way you know a language you've resurrected alone—with intimacy and doubt braided together, never certain until the moment of certainty whether the thing you've rebuilt exists anywhere outside your own head.

It existed. It covered four hundred square meters of living rock, and it was waiting for him.

"Okonkwo." Ilse's voice, very quiet. "Is it—"

"It's the treaty." He heard his own voice say it and watched the words leave him like something escaping. "All of it. Not fragments. Not excerpts. The whole instrument, from invocation to seal."

Freya stood with his back to the light, looking up at the walls, and said nothing at all. Which was, Okonkwo was learning, how the captain said the largest things.

* * *

He began at the beginning, because there was a beginning, marked by a band of deeper carving where the first hand had cut the opening formula. He worked left to right, top to bottom, reading aloud in the original and then again in English, and the lamplight made his shadow move across the words as he translated them, so that it seemed the text itself was speaking through him.

"In the year the great water stood high for three seasons"—he paused, checked his notes, corrected himself—"no. Not three seasons. Three years. In the year the great water stood high for three years, and the fish came into the gardens and the gardens drowned, the peoples of the river met the governor who came out of the salt water with soldiers whose skin shone like the bellies of fish."

Ilse was writing. She wrote the way she did everything, fast and total, her pencil keeping pace with him as though she'd been waiting her whole life for dictation.

"The governor's soldiers were many, but the river was greater, and both sides knew it. So the governor did a wise thing, which governors are not accustomed to doing." Okonkwo touched the carved line beneath this, feeling the grooves, the confidence of the chisel strokes. There was humor in it. Actual humor, cut into stone four hundred years ago by someone who expected readers, expected laughter, expected the future. "He sat down on a mat that the elders spread for him, and he took off his shining coat, and he listened for nine days."

"Nine days," Freya repeated softly.

"The number is ceremonial. But listen to what follows." Okonkwo moved the lamp along the wall. Here the script shifted register—from narrative into legal formula, the cadences tightening, each clause nested inside the last like the rings of a shell. This was the part he'd rehearsed most, the part he'd dreamed about, and now that it was in front of him he found he could barely make his voice work. "The waters from the place-of-the-first-spring to the place-where-the-river-forgets-its-name belong to the peoples who drink them. Not to the governor. Not to the governor's children. Not to the crown across the salt water, nor to any company, charter, or covenant that shall hereafter claim descent from the governor's authority. The grant is made to the drinkers of the water, in perpetuity, and it cannot be sold, because the water was never ours to sell; we hold it as the rain holds the mountain. Witnessed by the governor's mark, which is pressed here in lead."

He turned. At the base of the wall, protected by a shallow overhang, a disc of dull metal had been set into the stone, green with age, stamped with a seal he recognized from the colonial archives—the first governor's insignia, the one reproduced on every land-grant document the company's title chain traced back to.

"They have the map," Okonkwo said. "The falsified survey, the erased charts, the paper trail Marchetti's sponsors built their claims on. Every deed they hold descends from this governor's authority." He put his palm flat against the lead seal. It was cold and real and it did not move. "And this governor granted the watershed to the people who live on it. Forever. In terms any court in the world will recognize, because the courts themselves are built on the same legal tradition that drafted this."

The silence afterward had texture. The drip of water somewhere deep in the gallery. The faint hiss of the lamp. Naledi wasn't with them—she was holding the surface position, watching the trail—but Okonkwo thought of her anyway, thought of every settlement downriver, the villages the charts called uninhabited, the communities whose water rights had been dissolved on paper before their grandparents were born.

"So the titles," Freya said at last. Each word came out placed carefully, like footing on wet rock. "Every concession, every lease, every eviction notice—they're all descended from a grant that says the opposite of what the company claims."

"Not the opposite." Okonkwo shook his head. "Worse. The company's charters cite this governor's authority. Their own founding documents invoke him. Which means their entire chain of title rests on a grant that terminates at the first spring and grants everything above it away." He looked up at the walls, all that patient carved certainty rising into the dark beyond the lamplight. "Their maps aren't just wrong, Captain. Their maps contradict their own foundation. The lie has a seam in it, and the seam is cut into stone where no one can burn it."

* * *

They worked. There was nothing else to do with four hours and a cathedral of text. Ilse photographed by lamp, frame after frame, her hands steadier now than Okonkwo had ever seen them. Freya hauled water, held lights, and asked questions that were always exactly the right questions—the questions of a man who navigated for a living and understood that the truth of a channel lies in what the charts leave out. And Okonkwo read.

He read the clauses on fishing rights and flood refuge. He read the provision that any settlement on the river, however small, however new, held the same standing as the oldest—*the water does not count the houses*, the carvers had written, and he laughed aloud when he translated it, a short startled sound that echoed down the gallery. He read the dispute-resolution sections, the annual assembly at the source stone, the clause—his favorite, he already knew it would be his favorite—that required the governor's successors to maintain the road into the caves open and unobstructed, *so that the agreement may be read by any who doubt it.*

"They maintained it," he murmured. "Generations of them. That dressed threshold we came through. Someone was still dressing that stone within the last century. Someone remembered."

"And then someone decided to forget harder," Freya said.

"Yes. And I think I know when." Okonkwo had noticed it an hour into the reading: a break in the record, near the far end of the gallery. The final panels were unfinished—outlines blocked in, some glyphs roughed out and abandoned. And below them, scratched hastily into the stone in a different hand, a shorter text in the trade-pidgin script of the delta. He knelt before it and read it twice before trusting himself to speak.

"This is a warning. Added later—much later, maybe eighty years ago, maybe less. It says: *They came with paper. We showed them the wall. They took the wall's likeness and left. Do not let them take the wall.*"

Ilse lowered her camera. "They surveyed it. Someone came and copied the treaty—"

"And used the copy to know exactly what to erase." Okonkwo felt the shape of it closing around him, cold and complete. Eighty years. The founding era of the company's concessions. A survey team had stood in this gallery with lanterns and sketchbooks, had recorded the treaty in perfect detail, and had carried that knowledge out of the dark and used it—not to honor the grant, but to bury it. To erase the upper river from the charts, dissolve the settlements' standing, and make sure no one would ever come back to read the wall. "They didn't destroy it because they didn't understand it. They destroyed everything around it *because* they understood it perfectly."

Freya had gone very still. When he spoke, his voice had something in it Okonkwo hadn't heard before—not anger, something underneath anger, older. "Tomas surveyed for them."

"Tomas would have seen the company's original records," Ilse said quietly. "If the erasure order traces back that far—"

"He would have known about this place." Freya turned toward the deep end of the gallery, where the lamplight failed and the unfinished panels faded into black. "He would have known it was here."

* * *

They found the second chamber forty minutes later, following a side passage that the dressed-stone road continued to mark, and Okonkwo understood immediately why the demolition charges were scheduled for the main gallery rather than here. This chamber was smaller, lower-ceilinged, hidden behind a bend—and its walls carried not the treaty but the annals. Generations of records: harvests, floods, assemblies, births, disputes settled, names of elders going back further than the script's earliest datable forms. A civilization's memory, kept in the one place its enemies had never thought to look, because the official story said there was nothing here to remember.

It was Ilse who found the niche. She had stepped back to frame a wide shot, and the lamp caught a discoloration in the floor stone—a slab set slightly proud of its neighbors, its edges dressed in the modern way, with steel tools.

"Captain," she said. "This isn't old."

Freya was beside her before she finished. He got his fingers into the seam and lifted, and the slab came up on a bed of gravel someone had laid deliberately to muffle the sound, and beneath it was a waterproof field case, army-green, scuffed, its latches crusted with corrosion but intact.

Okonkwo knew at once what it was. He had seen a hundred such cases in expedition stores. But he watched Freya's face as the captain recognized it, and what moved across that face was not surprise. It was recognition—the terrible kind, the kind that arrives years late.

"That's a company surveyor's kit," Freya said. His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Standard issue. I've seen—" He stopped. Started again. "I've seen one before. On a boat. A long time ago."

Inside the case: instruments, notebooks sealed in oilcloth, and a bundle of papers wrapped in waxed canvas. Freya unwrapped them with hands that were not steady, and Okonkwo, leaning in, saw a surveyor's triangulation log—original, honest, dated—and beneath it, folded twice, a letter.

Freya read the first line silently. Whatever it said, it hit him physically; Okonkwo saw him sway, saw him put one hand flat against the chamber wall among the carved names of the dead, as though the wall were holding him up.

"What is it?" Ilse asked.

Freya didn't answer her. He looked at the letter for a long moment, and then at the name signed at the bottom, and then he looked up at Okonkwo, and his eyes in the lamplight were wet and furious and afraid all at once.

"It's Tomas," he said. "It's my brother's handwriting. And he names Verhoeven."

Above them, muffled through forty meters of limestone, came a sound none of them needed explained: the grind of heavy vehicles arriving at the rim of the escarpment, right on schedule, right on company time.

The demolition crews were early.
