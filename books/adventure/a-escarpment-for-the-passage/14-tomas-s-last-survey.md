## 14. Tomas's Last Survey

The passage narrowed the way grief narrows—gradually, then all at once. One moment the three of them walked abreast with the lamplight spread wide enough to touch both walls; the next, the limestone pressed in and Freya had to turn his shoulders sideways, the lamp held out ahead of him like a small stubborn sun.

Ilse went first. She moved through the dark with the certainty of someone who had walked these corridors before, in another life, when they had still belonged to her team. Okonkwo came last, carrying the second lamp low so that their shadows stretched long behind them, two tall figures and one bent one, walking deeper into the escarpment's throat.

The air changed a hundred meters in. Cooler, damper, tasting of stone and something older—mineral and faintly sweet, like water that had been thinking for a very long time. Somewhere below them, or beside them, or above—the acoustics of the cave made direction meaningless—they could hear the river itself, the true river, the source river, moving through channels no chart had ever drawn.

"Here." Ilse stopped so abruptly that Freya nearly walked into her back.

She was looking at a side chamber, barely a gap in the wall, half-hidden behind a curtain of flowstone the color of old honey. Freya would have passed it without a glance. But Ilse stood before it the way a person stands before a door they've opened once before and never forgotten.

"This is where I left him," she said quietly. "Not here-here. Further down. But this is where he told me to look, if anything—" She didn't finish. Her hand rose to the flowstone and rested there, flat, as if taking its pulse.

Freya set his lamp on a ledge and squeezed past her into the gap.

* * *

He knew it was Tomas's kit before he saw the name.

He knew it by the knots. Their father had taught them both the same rigging—a double fisherman's with a specific tuck, a quirk of an old man who'd learned his ropes on barges nobody else remembered—and there it was, ten years untouched, cinched around a canvas rucksack wedged into a hollow beneath a fallen slab. The canvas was furred with mineral dust. A tin lantern sat beside it, its glass clouded white. And on top of the pack, weighted down with a smooth river stone as if against the possibility of wind ten meters underground, lay a waterproof document case, army surplus, the kind their father had carried charts in.

Freya did not move for a long moment.

Behind him he heard Ilse draw breath to speak and then decide not to. He heard Okonkwo set his lamp down carefully, deliberately, the click of glass on stone loud in the silence. They gave him the room the way you give a man room at a graveside.

His hands, when he finally reached out, were not steady. Ten years of piloting the Magpie through snags and sandbars and company patrol wakes, and his hands had never shaken. They shook now.

The knots gave reluctantly. The canvas exhaled a smell that hit him like a physical blow: rope wax, pencil shavings, tobacco—Tomas had smoked a pipe for exactly one season, trying to be their father, before giving it up—and underneath it all, faintly, impossibly, his brother. Not a smell so much as a memory of a smell, preserved in waxed canvas the way the cave preserved everything, patiently, without opinion.

Inside the pack: a surveyor's transit, wrapped in oilcloth. A leather folio swollen with damp but intact. Three pencils worn to stubs. A folding knife. A photograph of two boys on a boat deck, squinting into sun, one of them laughing so hard the image had blurred—and Freya had to close his eyes at that, because he remembered the day, remembered the heat of the rail under his palms, remembered nothing else about it except that they had been happy and had not known to mark the occasion.

And beneath everything, the document case.

* * *

"It'll keep," Ilse said softly. "If you need it to keep."

"No." Freya heard his own voice come out gravelled. "It's kept long enough."

He carried the case out of the side chamber to where the main passage widened again, a natural alcove where the ceiling lifted and the flowstone fell in frozen cascades around them like the walls of some drowned cathedral. He set both lamps close. The light turned the stone gold and threw their three shadows up the wall, huge and wavering, an audience of giants.

The case's zipper fought him. Then yielded.

Inside, wrapped in a plastic sheet, were two things.

The first was a survey folio—originals, hand-drawn, the linework fine and confident in the way only Tomas's linework had ever been. Freya opened it and felt the floor of the world tilt. Because he knew these charts. Not these exact sheets, but their kind: he'd spent a decade navigating by the company's published versions, and the published versions were *wrong*, subtly, systematically wrong, sandbars shifted, channels misdrawn, whole reaches of the upper river rendered as braided dead sloughs where living water ran deep and clear. He'd cursed those charts for years, blamed the company's sloth, blamed the river's changeability, blamed himself for not trusting his own soundings more.

Tomas had trusted his own soundings. Here they all were. Every reach from the delta to the escarpment, surveyed station by station, depth by depth, with the settlements marked—*marked*, named, houses and docks and water rights noted in his brother's small precise hand—where the official charts showed empty wilderness. The upper river hadn't been unknown. It had been *known*, and then unwritten.

"He finished it," Ilse said. "Three weeks before he died. He sent me a message through a fuel depot clerk—one page, no names, just coordinates and the word *complete*. By the time I could get to him—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "By the time I got there, they'd already called it an accident."

Freya wasn't listening, not fully. He was looking at the second thing in the case.

A letter. Four pages, torn from a field notebook, written in pencil, the handwriting deteriorating from the first page's careful lines to the fourth page's cramped, pressing scrawl that had gone through the paper in places. Dated eleven days before the date on Tomas's death certificate.

He read the first line silently. Then he understood why Ilse had brought him here instead of simply handing him copies in some safe downstream café, and why she had let him walk into this chamber alone, and he hated her a little for it, and loved her for it, because she had known that some things have to be read aloud or they don't take root.

"Read it," Okonkwo said. His voice, still new to use, was gentle. "Out loud. The cave will hold it either way."

Freya cleared his throat. It didn't help. He began anyway.

* * *

*"To whoever finds this, and especially to F.J., if it's you, and if it is, I'm sorry, and I'm not sorry, both at once, which is probably how you already feel about me."*

The lamps hissed. The river murmured somewhere in the dark, patient as arithmetic.

*"You'll want to know when it started. It started small, the way it always starts. Verhoeven—he was just a section chief then, cartography division—came to me with a revision order. Upper reaches. 'Consolidate the hydrology,' he said. Nice words. What it meant was: move the channels, drop the settlements, merge the tributaries until the water rights attached to them dissolved on paper. The communities up here hold their claims by usage, F. Continuous documented use. Erase the documentation and you erase the claim. No court in the country will recognize a village that isn't on a map."*

Freya's voice caught on the next line. He steadied himself against the flowstone, cold and slick under his palm, and kept reading.

*"I told myself it was just paper. That's the trick of it, F., that's the whole machinery of it—it IS just paper, right up until the dredges come, right up until the patrol boats start charging tolls on water that was never theirs, right up until a family gets evicted from land they've farmed for six generations because the map says the map says. Paper is the sharpest thing the company owns. Sharper than any knife they carry."*

*"I redrew four charts before I understood what I was holding. Then I couldn't stop seeing it. Every settlement I'd erased had a face after that. The woman at the confluence who sold us oranges. The kids who swam out to look at our launch. I put my pencil through their lives and called it a career."*

Page three. The handwriting tightening now, the letters leaning forward as if hurrying.

*"I tried to recant. Went to Verhoeven with the originals and told him I'd file a correction with the survey board. He didn't threaten me. That's what I want you to understand, F.—he never raised his voice once in five years. He thanked me. He said, 'Tomas, you're the best surveyor I've ever employed, and the company rewards its best people, and it also remembers them.' Then he asked about you. By name. Your boat, your route, your moorings. He didn't say what he was asking. He didn't have to."*

Freya stopped.

The word *you* sat on the page in his brother's handwriting, and for a moment the cave seemed to tilt around it. All those years of careful invisibility—the false registry on the Magpie, the name shed like a skin, the decade of running quiet and gray and harmless—all of it, and Verhoeven had known anyway. Had *counted on* it. Had built his leverage out of Freya's own hiding, the way you build a wall out of a man's fear.

"You didn't know," Ilse said quietly. "When you refused the contract. When the courier showed you the photograph."

"I thought it was a threat." Freya's voice sounded far away to him. "I thought they'd found out who I was recently. I thought if I stayed small—"

"That's what it's designed to do. Staying small feels like safety. It's actually just staying where they put you."

Freya looked down at the last page. The scrawl had gone nearly vertical, pressed hard, urgent.

*"So I'm doing the only thing left. The originals go in the ground where they taught me to hide things—Iron Gallery, third chamber, under the fallen slab, you remember, we played there when Father ran the supply run. If you're reading this, I didn't make it off the water. Don't believe the report. Don't believe the inquiry. Believe the charts. The charts don't lie anymore; I made sure of that much."*

*"F.—if it's you—I'm not asking you to finish this. I know what I cost you already, making you disappear on my account. Live your life. Run your river. That's enough."*

*"But if someday the river asks you for something—if it ever asks—you'll know what it's asking for. You've always known. You were the one who used to say the river forgets nothing. I laughed at you. You were right. Be right one more time."*

*"Your brother, T.J."*

* * *

The silence afterward was total. Even the river below seemed to have gone still to listen.

Freya became aware, distantly, that he was on his knees. He had no memory of going down. The lamps threw his shadow enormous against the flowstone, a bowed giant, and the letter trembled in his hands—not his hands trembling, he realized, but the paper, and the paper was trembling because his hands were, and there was no longer any point in pretending otherwise.

He had not wept at the funeral. There had been no funeral, only a company telegram and a sealed box of effects and a form to sign acknowledging that the deceased's death was consistent with accidental drowning, and he had signed it with the same pen he later used to register the Magpie under a false name, and he had not cried then because crying would have meant believing it, and not believing it had been the only thing keeping him upright.

Ten years of not-believing came due all at once, in a cave under the escarpment, by lamplight, in front of a stranger and a linguist and his brother's honest charts.

The sound he made was not dignified. It was the sound of a hull taking water, of something long watertight finally failing, and it went on and on, and Ilse crouched beside him and put her hand between his shoulder blades and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the letter hadn't already said better. Okonkwo picked up the fallen lamp and held it higher, so the light reached further down the passage, as if lighting the way for someone still walking toward them out of the dark.

When it was over—when the shaking stopped and the cave came back into focus, gold-lit and ancient and indifferent—Freya knelt in the wet stone dust and found that the grief had not emptied him. That was the surprise. He had expected hollowness. Instead there was something dense and hot sitting where the grief had been, compacted under all that pressure, like coal.

Anger. No—not anger alone. Anger refined into purpose. The difference between a fire that burns a house and a fire that drives an engine.

"He asked me to live my life," Freya said. His voice was wrecked, scraped raw, and he found he didn't care. "Run my river. That's enough, he wrote." He looked up at Ilse, and she leaned back slightly at whatever she saw in his face. "He was wrong. It isn't enough. It was never going to be enough, and he knew it, and he wrote it anyway because he was protecting me even from the grave. Even dead, ten years dead, he's still trying to keep me safe."

"Will you honor that?" Ilse asked carefully. "Or will you honor him?"

Freya stood. His knees cracked. He gathered the folio, the letter, the transit, the blurred photograph of two laughing boys, and repacked the case with hands that had gone steady again—steadier than they had been in a decade.

"I'm going to read these charts," he said, "every reach, every sounding. Then I'm going to learn them the way he drew them, which is the way the river actually is. And then—" He looked down the passage, toward the deeper dark where the treaty carvings waited, where Okonkwo's translation would meet Tomas's survey and become, together, something no company dynamite could unmake. "Then every soul on this river is going to know exactly what was taken from them, and exactly who took it, and exactly where the proof sleeps."

Okonkwo shouldered his lamp. "Then we should keep moving," he said. "The demolition crews start at dawn."

They started down the Iron Gallery, three lamps and a dead man's charts, and behind them the cave held the echo of Freya's voice a long time—longer than it should have, the acoustics carrying it down through chambers none of them had entered yet, as if the escarpment itself were passing the words along, hand to hand, deeper and deeper, toward the stone that had been waiting centuries to hear them.

They had gone perhaps two hundred meters when the light behind them flickered—and died.

All three lamps. At once.

And from somewhere back along the passage they had just walked, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots on stone.
