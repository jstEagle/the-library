## 15. Caught

The light went out of the cave mouth the way a lamp goes out when someone pinches the wick—suddenly, and with intent.

Freya heard it before he saw it: the grind of steel plate against stone, the thud of bolts being driven home by men who knew their work. He was forty meters inside the gallery, lamplight trembling on the carved walls, when the afternoon sun that had been spilling through the entrance narrowed to a blade, then a thread, then nothing.

Okonkwo's hand closed on his arm in the dark. Not panic. Just weight. *Stay.*

Ilse's breathing went quick and shallow somewhere to his left. The lamp they'd hung on a piton still burned, throwing their shadows huge across the treaty wall—the long registers of names, the river rendered as a serpent with its tail at the source, the governor's seal pressed into stone four hundred years ago like a signature on a promise nobody downstream had ever been allowed to read.

"They're not sealing us out," Ilse whispered. "They're sealing us *in*."

"No," Freya said. His voice came out flat and calm, the voice he used for reading water he couldn't see the bottom of. "If they wanted us dead in here, they'd have set the charges first."

Boots echoed beyond the rock. Voices, muffled. Then, absurdly domestic, a knock—three raps, patient, almost polite—against the steel plate.

A voice came through, distorted by stone but unmistakable. Verhoeven's. The same mild, reasonable tone he'd used from the patrol boat's rail two weeks ago, the tone of a man discussing freight rates.

"Captain Joubert. Dr. Marchetti. Mr. Okonkwo. There is no need for anyone to be uncomfortable. The plate opens from our side. You have water down there—I'm told the source seeps through most of these galleries—but I'd rather not test anyone's patience. Dinner is at nineteen hundred."

Silence. Then, because Freya had spent ten years learning exactly how much silence cost and who paid it, he said nothing at all.

Verhoeven seemed to expect it. "Take your time," he said, and the boots receded.

* * *

They gave him an hour before Ilse cracked.

Not tears—that wasn't her crack. Her crack was motion. She began pacing the gallery, lamp in hand, re-reading the carvings she could no longer photograph, muttering register numbers under her breath like a woman counting money in a burning house. Okonkwo sat cross-legged beneath the serpent, notebook open on his knees even though there was nothing left to write that wasn't already in his head.

"They'll move the charges up," she said. "Tonight or tomorrow morning. They won't wait for full demolition protocol—not now, not with us loose in here for six hours. They'll bring the schedule forward and bury the whole lower gallery and call it a collapse."

"You don't know that."

"I know *them*." She stopped pacing. In the lamplight her face looked older than it had at the fuel depot, older than it had on the boat, older maybe than it had ever looked in any photograph. "I hired good people, Captain. Eleven of them. I've accounted for nine. One of those nine is in a company infirmary with a broken ankle and a very convenient case of amnesia. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I did the arithmetic on that launch wreck before you ever fished me off the bank, and I have been doing it every night since."

Freya crouched by the lamp and turned the wick down until the flame was a blue bead. Conserving. A habit from low-water seasons, from nights drifting with the engine off, listening for patrol boats.

"Then we don't wait for dinner," he said.

"The plate's steel, Captain."

"The plate's steel." He let that sit. "But whoever built this gallery needed to get stone out of it. There'll be a working adit somewhere above the entrance—a ventilation shaft, a haul tunnel, something the surveyors never bothered to map because it predates them. Tomas would have known. He walked every meter of this escarpment twice."

Ilse stared at him. "You think your brother left you a door."

"I think my brother left *everyone* a door. It's whether we can find it in the dark that's the question."

Okonkwo spoke for the first time since the sealing. His voice, still rare, still surprising, came quiet as groundwater. "There is air moving. From the north wall. I noticed it an hour ago and thought it was the river." He rose, lifted the lamp, and held the flame toward the far side of the gallery. The blue bead of fire leaned, faint but definite, like grass bending before something unseen.

Freya felt something in his chest that was not quite hope. Hope was expensive. But it was adjacent to hope.

"Get the rope," he said.

* * *

The adit was behind a curtain of flowstone no thicker than a coffin lid, and it broke the way coffins break—badly, loudly, with Freya swinging the boat's spare pry bar until his shoulders screamed and the stone finally sheared along an ancient fracture line and crashed into darkness beyond. Cold air poured through the gap, smelling of wet fern and open country.

Naledi would have made a joke about it. Naledi was three kilometers away at base camp, wearing the company's grudging tolerance like a disguise, and Freya had spent the entire climb into the escarpment telling himself that leaving her behind was protection and knowing, in the honest cellar of himself, that it was also abandonment dressed as strategy.

The tunnel ran thirty meters and opened onto the escarpment's north face, a slot of evening sky above a slope of scree and stunted cedar. Below, terraced into the valley, base camp lay lit like a small vindictive town: generator hum, floodlights, the mess tent, the magazine hut with its red-painted door, and beside it, stacked in neat palletized rows under tarpaulin, enough explosive to take the whole escarpment's memory down to gravel.

Three men between them and the tree line. Two dogs. A radio mast turning slowly against the last light.

"We go down the cedar spine," Freya murmured. "Slow. We're just three shapes the sentries haven't learned to see yet."

"And then?" Ilse whispered.

"Then we steal a boat, a truck, or a miracle, whichever comes first."

They got within sixty meters of the tree line before the floodlight swept, caught the gleam of Okonkwo's lamp housing, and the night tore open with a whistle and a shout.

They ran. Freya drove Ilse ahead of him through the cedars, branches whipping his face, the slope steepening, and for eleven glorious seconds he believed—

The net took him at the ankles.

He went down hard on scree, breath blasted out of him, and hands were on him before he finished falling—professional hands, four men, no anger in them at all, which was somehow worse. They bound his wrists with zip ties that bit to the bone. When they hauled him upright, he saw Ilse face-down under a knee, and Okonkwo standing perfectly still with his hands raised, watching everything, memorizing everything, the way he watched riverbanks.

A flashlight found Freya's face and stayed there.

"Captain." Verhoeven stepped out of the dark with his hands in his pockets, unhurried, a man arriving slightly early for a meeting he'd scheduled himself. "You found the adit. I confess I'm impressed. Tomas never mentioned it."

The name landed like a hook behind Freya's sternum.

"Search them thoroughly," Verhoeven said to his men. "Then bring them to my quarters. The doctor will want to eat; I imagine she hasn't. And Captain—" he paused, half-turned, the floodlight carving his profile into something out of an old coin, "—do try to be civil at dinner. Things go easier for everyone when we're civil."

* * *

They held them separately. Freya knew this because he listened—ten years of invisibility had made his ears into instruments—and what he heard through the prefabricated wall of Verhoeven's quarters was Ilse's voice, rising, then cut off by a door. Okonkwo they took somewhere he couldn't track at all.

His own cell was a converted shipping container: bunk, bucket, a bulb behind a cage. The zip ties came off only after a soldier with kind eyes and dead hands checked his arms for needle marks, which told Freya the company had considered needles and decided against them. That was information too. Dead men couldn't sign statements, and Verhoeven, whatever else he was, liked paper.

Dinner came at nineteen hundred exactly, because Verhoeven was a man who kept his own appointments.

They brought Freya to the commander's quarters—an actual room, salvaged from some drowned settlement upstream, with a real table, real chairs, a lantern turned low, and a bottle of something amber with a label worn blank. Verhoeven sat alone, jacket off, sleeves rolled, looking less like an enforcer than a provincial accountant at the end of a long audit week. On the table between his place and the empty chair opposite: two plates, covered. And a pistol, lying sideways, barrel toward neither man and both.

"Sit," Verhoeven said.

Freya sat. His wrists still bore the tie marks. He folded his hands on the table so the marks showed, deliberately, and watched Verhoeven watch him do it.

"You're angry," Verhoeven observed. He lifted the covers: rice, canned beef, fried plantain, steam still rising. Company logistics, executed flawlessly. "That's reasonable. I'd be angry. But I want you to understand something before the anger does your thinking for you." He poured two measures of the amber liquor and pushed one across. "Everything that has happened to you since the courier handed you that contract—the photograph, the triple pay, Marchetti walking up your gangplank at exactly the right fuel depot—none of it was accident, Captain. None of it."

Freya didn't touch the glass. "You mean luck wasn't involved."

"I mean luck was *manufactured*. We knew where Marchetti would surface. We knew she'd need a boat the company didn't own, a captain with no love for us and no records, someone deniable. There are perhaps four such men on this river. We chose you." Verhoeven leaned back. "Do you know why?"

"Because I'm cheap and desperate."

"Because you're *Tomas's brother*." He said it plainly, without cruelty, which stripped it of all shelter. "We recruited you deliberately. Your file crossed my desk eight years ago—Joubert, T., deceased, next of kin, F., current whereabouts unknown, presumed using an alias on the Vasseur. It took my people four months to find you. Four months, Captain, in a decade of your careful little life. You were never invisible. You were simply uninteresting, and the moment you became interesting, we had you."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a generator changed pitch. Freya found that his hands had not moved, that his face had not moved, that ten years of practice held—even now, even with the floor gone.

"You wanted a brother's loyalty," he said. "To use as a leash."

"I wanted a brother's *motivation*. Loyalty is unpredictable. Motivation isn't. A man whose brother died in company service will do strange things for the company if he believes it honors the dead one. And if it doesn't—" Verhoeven shrugged, a small economical movement. "Then we learn that early, while it's still cheap."

"Tomas didn't die in company service. He died trying to undo it."

"Yes." No flinch. Verhoeven picked up his own glass and turned it slowly, watching the liquor climb the sides. "And that is the tragedy at the center of your life, isn't it? You've spent ten years keeping his memory like a coal in your pocket, warming your hands on it, and the coal was a lie. He falsified the charts, Captain. Every erased village, every stolen water right, every family downriver that can't prove it exists—he drew those lines. With a steady hand. He was *good* at it. And when his conscience finally woke up—too late, and cowardly, and in secret—he died for it in a way that fooled no one who mattered." He set the glass down. "So. The offer. Same as before, but now we're all adults at the table."

He slid a folder across the table. Thick. Freya's own name on the tab—his true name, the buried one—in typed block letters.

"Pardon for the identity. Full expungement. Your brother's complete file, including the original survey, which we have, Captain—we've always had it, sitting in a vault in the capital, worth precisely nothing to anyone but a court that will never see it. And a captaincy. Company marine division, upper river command, once the escarpment question is settled. A house. A pension. A name you can say out loud." Verhoeven folded his hands. "In exchange: Dr. Marchetti signs a statement—her statement, drafted by us, attesting that the cave records are a fabrication she staged for a rival consortium. Okonkwo's translations are surrendered and destroyed. And you captain the Magpie back downriver and tell every settlement that asks that the expedition was fraud, start to finish. That's all. Words. You've always been good with silence; this is just silence with a salary."

"And if I refuse?"

Verhoeven glanced at the pistol, then away from it, dismissive. "The gun isn't the argument. The gun is furniture. The argument is this: at oh-six-hundred the demolition crews put the first charges in the lower gallery, and by noon the oldest legal document on this river is gravel, and there is no copy, no photograph, no witness except three people in containers. If you refuse, that happens, and you live with it, and I will make sure you live a long time. If you accept, the caves still come down—" he spread his hands, honest to a fault, "—I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But the record survives in testimony, in the archive, in *my* custody, and history is long, Captain. Longer than companies. Sometimes the wise play is to keep the truth alive inside the machine that's trying to kill it."

It was a good speech. Freya had to give him that. It was the best kind of lie, the kind built almost entirely out of true things arranged in a false shape.

"You knew about the caves before you hired me," Freya said. "Before Marchetti ever boarded. You knew what she'd found."

"We suspected. She'd filed a requisition for photographic equipment well beyond survey needs. People reveal themselves in their paperwork." Verhoeven smiled thinly. "Why do you think the resupply contract specified the escarpment? Why do you think the patrol boat let you pass at the second rapids instead of turning you back? You weren't escaping us, Captain. You were carrying our cargo for us—Marchetti, the linguist, the whole living proof of the find, delivered to the one place we could close the box."

"And Naledi?"

Something flickered behind Verhoeven's eyes—the first genuine thing Freya had seen in them all evening, and it looked almost like respect. "Your deckhand has been a company asset since the day we explained, gently, what happens to families downstream who inconvenience us. She reported your departure, your route, your fuel state. She's why the scuttled cache happened on schedule." He tilted his head. "Did you think she cried because she was ashamed? She cried because she's known longer than you have how this ends."

Freya stood up. The chair scraped. For a moment the pistol on the table seemed to become the only object in the room, and both men acknowledged it without touching it.

"You've made your speech," Freya said. His voice was level, river-level, deep water over hard bottom. "Here's mine. My brother drew lines that erased people. I've spent ten years ashamed of hiding, and I thought that shame was about him. It wasn't. It was about me. So no. No pardon, no name, no captaincy. You didn't recruit Tomas's brother tonight, Verhoeven. You recruited the wrong Joubert."

Verhoeven regarded him for a long moment. Then he sighed, genuinely, wearily, and reached for the folder, and Freya understood with cold clarity that the interview had been the mercy and the mercy was over.

"Oh-six-hundred," Verhoeven said. "For the charges. I'd sleep, if I were you. It's the last quiet morning this river gets for a while."

The soldiers walked Freya back to his container. Behind the cage-caged bulb he sat on the bunk and did not sleep. He listened instead—to the generators, to the boots, to the distant clank of the magazine hut's red door opening and closing, opening and closing, as men carried the future out in crates.

And underneath all of it, so faint he might have invented it, he heard another sound: rain starting in the highlands, hours early, falling on ground that had already had more than it could hold.

The river was coming up.

Somewhere in the dark, Freya began, silently, to count the hours until dawn—and to wonder whether Naledi was counting too.
