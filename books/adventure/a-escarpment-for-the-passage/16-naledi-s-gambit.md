## 16. Naledi's Gambit

They left her alone.

That was the first miracle, and Naledi did not waste it. She sat on an ammunition crate behind the mess tent, wrists still raw from the zip ties they'd cut at dawn—*we don't tie up our own people*, the corporal had said with a smile that wasn't one—and she let them see exactly what they expected to see: a girl who'd sold her friends and been paid in contempt. She kept her eyes down. She flinched when soldiers passed. She made herself small the way she'd learned to make herself small at fourteen, when the company men first came to her mother's door downstream and asked questions about her father's ferry license.

Written off. That was what Verhoeven's intelligence officer had called her, loud enough to hear on purpose: *the collaborator stays under watch until we ship her out with the next rotation.* But watch meant a man by the gate, not a man by her elbow, because a collaborator was no threat at all. A collaborator was furniture.

Naledi counted everything, the way Freya had taught her in her first week aboard the Magpie. *Count your fuel, count your water, count the seconds between lightning and thunder.* So she counted: eleven crates of detonators stacked under the tarp beside the generator shed, wired in bundles of six, each bundle tagged for a cave section. Two guards on the perimeter wire who changed position every forty minutes and were bored enough to smoke. The helicopter pad, empty since yesterday, its wind sock hanging dead. And the schedule board inside the mess tent, which she'd read upside down while pretending to stare at her boots: **SUPPLY ROTATION 0700 — HELI — AMMO/DETONATORS OUT, MEDS IN.**

Tomorrow morning. Seven o'clock.

She pressed her thumbnail into the old scar on her palm—the one from the winch cable—and thought about her mother's voice on the radio, three weeks ago, thin as thread through the static: *They know where your sister goes to school, Naledi. They know which bus.* The company hadn't needed to threaten her much. They'd only needed to be specific.

And so she had keyed the Magpie's set, night after night, and given them headings. Given them Verhoeven the gorge route. Given them everything except the thing that mattered most, because even drowning, some part of her had held back the cave entrance coordinates, and that half-lie was the splinter she'd built tonight around.

She looked toward the detention hut—a corrugated shed near the north wire, lit by one caged bulb. Behind that door: Freya, Ilse Marchetti, Okonkwo. Three days now. She'd heard a guard joke about it over cards. *Verhoeven wants them talking before the charges go in. After that, doesn't matter what they say.*

After that. The detonators were going into the caves tomorrow, same rotation that brought the meds. Blow the treaty stone, blow the carvings, blow five hundred years of proof, and then—her stomach turned—blow whatever loose ends remained. Loose ends had names. She knew all four of them.

Naledi stood up, brushed off her trousers like a girl stretching her legs, and walked toward the latrine trench along the generator shed. Nobody stopped her. Furniture didn't need stopping.

She knelt at the tarp's edge and lifted it with two fingers.

The detonator bundles were packed in waxed cardboard, each with a twist-wire arming loop and a small brass safety collar, the kind she'd seen the dredging crews use below the falls. You didn't have to destroy them. You only had to make them lie. She'd watched Okonkwo explain it once, oddly, using sugar and salt—*the same crystal, but one dissolves sweet and one won't dissolve at all; the difference is invisible until you commit.*

So she committed. Working by feel in the shadow of the generator's drone, she slipped the safety collars back a quarter turn—not enough to show, just enough that the firing pins would catch and hold when the current came. Then she swapped the leads on the first bundle, crossed the bridge wires on the second, and pulled the initiator caps from the third and fourth and dropped them, one by one, into the fuel drum of waste oil beside the shed, where they sank without a sound into blackness. Her hands shook so badly on the fifth bundle that she bit her own wrist to steady them, and the pain helped, the way cold river water helps.

Six bundles. When she tucked the tarp back down, the stacks looked untouched. They would look untouched tomorrow, too, right up until the moment the caves declined to explode.

It wasn't enough. She knew it wasn't enough. Detonators that failed bought hours, not freedom, and Verhoeven was not a man who spent hours forgiving. Which left the hut, and the wire, and the helicopter coming in at 0700 with its rotors down and every soldier on base watching the cargo sling instead of the treeline.

Naledi wiped her hands on her shirt, walked back to her crate, and sat down again, small and harmless, heart slamming like a loose hatch cover in a swell.

She had one more thing to do before morning, and it was the hardest one. She had to get word to Freya. Because whatever happened at seven o'clock, he could not be surprised by her. If he saw her coming with keys in her hand and thought—even for one second—that it was another betrayal, he wouldn't run. He'd stay in that hut out of sheer stubborn grief, and they'd all die of it.

She'd seen him look at her last night, across the yard, when they marched him past. Not hatred. Worse than hatred. A question he couldn't finish asking.

Tomorrow she'd answer it.

* * *

Morning came gray and low, cloud sitting on the escarpment like a lid.

At 0640, Naledi carried the guard's breakfast tray to the detention hut—she'd volunteered, smiling her collaborator's smile, and the guard had handed over the tray and his boredom together. Inside, the air smelled of damp concrete and kerosene. Freya sat against the far wall, wrists ringed with plastic, beard gone gray-streaked, eyes finding hers the instant the door opened. Ilse was curled on a bench, feverish. Okonkwo stood at the barred vent, motionless, listening the way he always listened.

"Breakfast," Naledi said loudly, for the benefit of anyone listening. Then, barely a breath, kneeling to set the tray down: "Detonators are dead. I killed them. Helicopter at seven. When the rotors spool, everyone runs for the south corner where the wire meets the rockfall—there's a gap, I cut it Tuesday. Freya." She dared his eyes. "I'm sorry. I was never theirs. I was scared, and I was theirs the way a hostage is theirs, and I'm done."

He said nothing. His face did something complicated—grief and fury and something underneath both that might, in better light, have been belief. It was Okonkwo who nodded once, slow, a man confirming a translation.

"Ilse can't run fast," Freya said quietly.

"She won't have to run far. I'll get her out first if I can."

"Naledi." His voice cracked on her name, and she hated how much that cost her, and how much it gave back. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," she lied, and stood, and knocked twice on the door to be let out.

* * *

At 0658 the sky began to chew.

The helicopter came in from the east through the cloud deck, and the whole camp tilted toward it—soldiers turning, radios crackling, the ground crew jogging out with the cargo net. Rotors wound down to idle. The noise was enormous, total, a wall of sound you could hide a stampede behind.

Naledi moved.

She'd taken the hut key from the breakfast guard's belt hook with a stolen fork's worth of misdirection—bumped it loose while steadying the tray, palmed it, prayed. Now she was at the hut door, hands steady in a way that felt borrowed from someone braver, and the lock turned like it had been waiting for her.

"Up," she said. "South corner. Go."

Freya came out of the hut carrying Ilse on his back without being asked, without a word, ten years of river muscle doing what it did. Okonkwo took the rear, moving with that unhurried certainty, scanning the yard. They crossed forty meters of open ground inside the rotor noise, inside the blind spot of every man watching the sling load swing beneath the helicopter—and then the world noticed.

A shout from the tower. A whistle. Someone's voice cracking over the PA, half-swallowed by engine wash: *prisoners—south wire—*

The first shot hit the generator shed above her head and blew splinters across her cheek. The second—

The second found her.

It went through her left shoulder as she shoved Ilse through the gap in the wire, and the sound arrived late, after the force, after the heat, and Naledi went down onto the scree with the sky spinning and the taste of copper and dust. She heard herself make a noise she wasn't ashamed of until later. Hands grabbed her—Okonkwo's, iron and gentle—and then Freya was there, hauling her up by the good arm, his face close enough that she could see every line the last three weeks had carved into it.

"You're shot," he shouted over the rotors.

"It's the shoulder," she shouted back, absurdly precise, because counting things was how she stayed alive. "Muscle. I can run."

He ran her. They went through the gap single file—Freya with Ilse, Okonkwo pushing Naledi ahead of him—and into the rockfall's shadow as bullets sparked off stone behind them. The gorge opened below like a throat, mist rising from the river far down, and sirens were climbing the scale behind them, one siren and then a second, and somewhere Verhoeven's voice was already on the radios, already reorganizing the world against them.

But the detonators were dead. Naledi held onto that as they plunged down the goat track, her arm burning, blood soaking her sleeve in hot pulses. Whatever happened next, whatever the day cost, the caves would still be there tomorrow, and the treaty with them, and Tomas's letter, and the truth the company had come all this way to murder.

Halfway down the switchbacks, Freya stopped in the lee of a boulder, breathing hard, Ilse slumped against the stone. He looked at Naledi—at the blood, at her face—and whatever question had lived in his eyes for three days finally finished asking itself and got its answer.

"The radio traffic," he said. "Every heading I trusted. Every safe anchorage."

"Gone," she said. "You'll have to trust me the old way now. Eyes and lead weight and no shortcuts."

Something moved through his face that was almost a laugh, worn thin as it was. "No shortcuts," he agreed. "That's the whole job."

Okonkwo tore a strip from his shirt and bound her shoulder while Freya scouted the next bend. Below them, the river ran high and brown and angry, swollen early with highland rain, and Naledi watched it and understood, with a clarity that hurt worse than the bullet, what Freya was going to do. There was no going back downriver past the base camp. There was only up.

"He's going to run the gorge," she said to Okonkwo.

Okonkwo tied off the knot and looked at her, and for once his voice was soft. "Yes. And we are going to help him."

Behind and above, the sirens rose to a chorus, and the first searchlights swept the rim of the escarpment through the cloud. The hunt was on. But the Magpie waited at the waterline below, moored where Naledi had begged the guards to let her check on it—*company property now, sir, just maintaining the asset*—fueled two days ago for a supply run that would never happen.

Freya came back from the bend, and there was no hesitation left anywhere in him.

"River's rising," he said. "We're going up."

Naledi gripped her bad arm against her ribs, looked at the brown flood tearing through the gorge below, and stepped onto the trail behind him.
