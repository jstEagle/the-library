## 17. Flood Season Comes Early

The river told them before the sky did.

Freya had spent ten years learning the Vasseur's moods—the way it cleared its throat with a change in current before it ever raised its voice—and by the third morning after Naledi's gambit, the river was shouting. The water in the gorge had gone from green to the color of strong tea. Driftwood that should have been drifting lazily came past the Magpie at a dead sprint, whole trees among it, roots like grasping hands. And the sound. He stood at the wheel in the grey half-light and listened to a sound he hadn't heard since he was a boy: the deep, continuous roar of the Vasseur running hard, a sound like the earth clearing its own throat.

"Highlands," Okonkwo said, coming up beside him. The linguist nodded south, toward the escarpment's distant blue wall and the country beyond it. "Storms up there. You can smell it."

He could. The wind out of the south carried wet stone and ozone and something older underneath, something like turned earth, like the first breath of a thing waking.

Naledi sat on the fuel drums with her arm bound in a sling Ilse had improvised from a shirt, her shoulder wound dressed with the last of their antiseptic. She'd gone grey around the mouth for two days after the escape—blood loss, Freya told himself, though he knew better—but this morning she was watching the water with an expression he recognized because he wore it too. Not fear exactly. Arithmetic.

"How fast?" she asked.

"Fast enough." Freya gauged the drift of a half-submerged log against the bank markers he'd memorized on the way down. "River's come up what—two meters since yesterday? Three overnight if the storms hold."

"That's impossible," Ilse said from the deckhouse doorway. She looked like she'd aged a decade in the caves, her hair streaked with limestone dust, but her eyes were still the sharpest things aboard. "Flood season isn't due for another nine days. I checked the records. Nine days."

"The records were written by people who never went above the falls," Freya said. "The highlands make their own weather. Company charts say flood season starts when the company says it does." He almost smiled. "Turns out the river didn't sign anything."

* * *

They held council over the chart table in the cramped cabin, four heads bent over paper while the hull trembled beneath them with the push of the current. Outside, rain had begun—not the downriver kind that fell straight and soft, but slanting, driven rain with grit in it, blown up the gorge ahead of the water.

Verhoeven's boats were behind them. That was the one mercy. Freya had watched through his binoculars as the patrol craft reached the gorge mouth and stopped there, riding at anchor like dogs refused at a gate. The gorge in flood was suicide for steel-hulled boats with deep drafts; Verhoeven would know it, and his rage at knowing it would be considerable. But Verhoeven had radios, helicopters, men who could portage on foot along the old logging skids they themselves had used coming up. Two days, maybe three, and the company would be above the falls again.

Which meant the question wasn't whether to run. It was which direction.

"We go back down," Ilse said. "Regroup at the unmapped village. The elders will shelter us. We wait out the flood and take the records down when the water drops."

"And Verhoeven waits with us," Freya said. "Or comes down through the gorge behind us once it drops, or sends word ahead and has every station between here and the delta watching for us. We'd be carrying the treaty into a net." He tapped the chart where the village sat, a penciled mark no official map acknowledged. "And we'd be asking those people to stand between us and the company again. They've starved enough for other people's maps."

"So we stay here," Naledi said. "Hide in the upper gorge until—"

"Until what? They'll come. They always come. And we're out of fuel, out of food in four days, and I've got a deckhand with a hole in her." He said it flatly, without accusation, and saw Naledi flinch anyway. Since the confession, since the sabotage of the detonators and the bullet she'd taken freeing them, something had shifted between them—not forgiveness, not yet, but a shared understanding of what coercion cost. He wasn't going to spend her guilt like currency. But he wasn't going to pretend the facts away either.

"There's a third option," Okonkwo said quietly.

Everyone looked at him. The linguist spoke rarely enough that when he did, the room bent toward him like grass toward light.

"The source is two days upstream. Maybe three, in this water." His long finger traced the gorge's convolutions to the amphitheater at the head of it all, where the survey sketches showed the spring-fed basin and the standing stone. "The rapids between here and there are bad. In flood, they're worse than bad—they're new. Every year the water remakes them; no chart can hold them. But." He looked up at Freya. "You've run water nobody charted before. You told me so yourself, the night we winched through the narrows. You said the river doesn't lie. Only maps do."

"I was younger then," Freya said. "And stupid in ways I've mostly forgotten."

"You're forgetting them now," Naledi said, and there was something in her voice—not quite a challenge, not quite a plea. "Captain. If we go down, we lose. If we hide, we lose slower. If we go up—" She stopped, and Freya finished it for her, because the logic was already sitting in all their chests like a swallowed stone.

"If we go up, we reach the source before Verhoeven reaches us. We finish what Tomas started. And the flood that traps us also traps him." He stared at the chart, at the confident ink lines describing water that no longer existed, that had never existed as drawn. "It also probably kills us."

Silence, except the rain on the deckhouse roof and the river's endless roar below.

Ilse broke it. "I've spent six years of my life getting to that stone," she said. "I left my team to die—or to worse than dying—to keep these records moving. I am not going back down that gorge to sit in a village and wait for Verhoeven's patience to outlast ours." Her voice cracked on the last words, and she steadied it with visible effort. "But it's your boat, Captain. Your call. It's been your call since the depot."

That was the trouble with command. Everyone said it was your call right up until it went wrong, and then it was still your call, forever, in the dark, at three in the morning, for the rest of your life. Freya thought about Tomas's letter folded in oilcloth inside his jacket, against his chest, where he'd carried it since the caves. *If you're reading this, I didn't get to say it properly: the river was honest. I wasn't.* His brother had made his choice too late and paid everything for it.

Freya looked at Naledi's sling. At Okonkwo's steady eyes. At Ilse's white knuckles on the chart table.

He thought about the Magpie. Thirty years old, patched twice, blessed once by a priest who'd drowned the following season. Shallow draft. Steel-sheathed bow. An engine that ran rough and refused to quit, which was more than most people managed.

"Naledi," he said. "How's the arm?"

"Useless for winching. Fine for a tiller."

"Okonkwo. Can you read water?"

"I can read everything else. I'll learn."

"Dr. Marchetti. Can you follow orders?"

"No," Ilse said. "But I'll pretend."

Something loosened in Freya's chest that might, in a kinder hour, have been called laughter. He put both palms flat on the chart, over the blank space above the last surveyed bend—the space where the honest maps ended and the lying ones began.

"Then we run it," he said. "Up. Toward the source. We leave within the hour, while the light's still good and before the river gets any more ideas."

* * *

Preparing the Magpie for the run took forty minutes and felt like dressing a body. They stripped everything loose off the decks and lashed it down—fuel drums double-lashed, the winch cable locked, crates of records wrapped in oilcloth and strapped under the deckhouse floor where even swamping shouldn't take them. Ilse's copies of the cave translations went into a waterproof satchel that never left her body. Naledi insisted on working one-handed until Freya physically took the rope out of her fingers and tied the knot himself, showing her how it was done so she could do the next one.

"You're treating me like glass," she muttered.

"I'm treating you like crew. Crew doesn't tear stitches an hour before the worst water of our lives." He cinched the lashing and met her eyes. "You want to make up for the radio? Live. That's the whole payment. Just live, and be here after."

She looked away, jaw tight, and said nothing, but she let him finish the knot.

At the wheel, Freya ran his pre-run ritual the way his father had taught him, back on a different river in a different life: check the rudder posts, check the bilge pumps—both of them, and the backup bucket, which drew a snort from Naledi—check the engine mounts, check the anchor, ready to drop at a moment's notice onto whatever gravel bar mercy provided. Then he stood a moment with his hand on the wheel hub, feeling the vibration of the idling engine climb up through his arm bones, and let himself admit what he'd been refusing to admit since the chart table.

He was afraid. Properly afraid, the old animal fear, cold in the belly. Not of dying—he'd made his peace with the river taking him years ago, some anonymous snag, some drunk night, some ordinary end. What frightened him was the direction. For ten years he had steered only downstream, only away, only toward smaller water and fewer witnesses. Every instinct he owned screamed that the safe move was retreat, that the river rewarded humility and punished pride, that no cargo was worth this.

But the cargo wasn't brandy or machine parts. The cargo was a treaty three hundred years old and a dead man's honesty, and the destination was the place where the river began, and for the first time in a decade Freya Joubert—whoever that name had ever really belonged to—was steering toward something instead of away from it.

"Magpie," he said quietly, to the boat, the way he always did. "Old girl. One more lie the charts tell is that you're not capable of this. Let's prove the charts wrong one last time."

He rang the bell twice. Ahead, through the rain-haze, the gorge narrowed and the water whitened, and the roar rose to meet them like a congregation standing to sing.

* * *

The first rapid nearly killed them inside ninety seconds.

It was a ledge of black rock that on the way down had been a riffle—a chuckling, harmless shelf. In flood it was a standing wave two meters tall with a hole behind it that ate driftwood whole. Freya read it too late to avoid, so he did the only thing left: drove the bow straight at it, engine full ahead, and hit the wave square.

The world became water. The Magpie leapt, hung, and slammed down the far side with a concussion that threw everyone off their feet; the deckhouse door burst open and the river came in across the deck in a green sheet, waist-deep for three seconds, tearing at everything. Freya heard Naledi scream something that was either his name or a curse, heard Okonkwo praying in a language older than any of them, felt the propeller bite air and then water and then air again, the engine screaming—

—and then they were through, wallowing in the boil below, shipping water, alive.

"Bilge!" Freya roared, and Naledi was already moving, one-handed, jamming herself against the pump handle, and Ilse was bailing with the bucket, and Okonkwo had braced himself in the bow well and was reading the water ahead, calling the channel in his precise, unhurried voice: "Right of the sweeper—no, further right—the eddy line bends left past the rock, trust the bend—"

Trust the bend. Freya trusted it. The Magpie answered her rudder like the old girl still had opinions, and they threaded the gap with a hand's width on either side of the hull, close enough that the rock's wet face smeared along the paint.

After that there was no time to think, and that was the gift of it. The gorge in flood permitted nothing but the next ten seconds, endlessly renewed. Freya worked the wheel and the throttle in a language older than speech, reading the boils and the V-shaped tongues of smooth water that marked the channels, avoiding the strainers—trees pinned in the current that would wrap and hold and drown anything that touched them—ferrying across the current's muscle when the channel demanded it, punching through waves that tried to turn them broadside. Twice the river picked the Magpie up and simply moved her, and both times Freya coaxed her nose back downstream-of-the-threat and lived. Once they grounded briefly on a submerged gravel bar that the flood had built that morning, brand-new, uncharted in the truest sense, and Okonkwo was over the bow in the shallows before Freya could shout, hauling on the painter with Naledi pushing from the stern with her good arm, and they came off with a grinding shudder and kept going.

Hours passed. The rain came and went. The light thickened toward evening, and the gorge walls closed in, dripping, festooned with vines that streamed in the wind like the hair of drowned women. Somewhere behind them—days behind, but closing—Freya imagined he could hear engines. Probably imagination. Probably.

In a rare slack stretch above a sharp bend, Naledi appeared at his elbow, soaked to the skin, grinning like a lunatic.

"We're doing it," she said. "We're actually doing it."

"Don't jinx the boat."

"She's not jinxable. She's got us and we're too stubborn to drown." Sobering, quieter: "Captain. There's a pool coming up—I remember it from the scout on foot, the wide one before the last gorge. If we're going to stop for the night anywhere, it's there. Nobody runs this water in the dark. Not you. Not anybody."

Freya weighed it. Every hour at anchor was an hour Verhoeven gained. But every hour of darkness on floodwater was a coin flip with death, and he'd already spent more luck today than a man was owed in a season.

"The pool," he agreed. "One night. Cold camp, no fire, watches in pairs. We run at first light."

* * *

The pool, when they reached it, was barely recognizable as the same water they'd poled through on the way up. It had become a lake, brown and muscular, spinning slowly with debris, the cliffs rising sheer on either side. Freya backed the Magpie into a slot of calm water under an overhang and dropped the anchor onto gravel, and the sudden stillness after hours of violence was so complete it felt like deafness.

They ate cold rice and dried fish and drank rainwater caught in the tarp. Ilse sat wrapped in a blanket, holding the satchel of records against her stomach like a child. After a while she said, to no one in particular:

"My team believed in this. Sørensen. Adeyemi. Little Baptiste, who was twenty-three and took photographs because he said the truth needed a witness with a camera." Her voice was steady in the way of someone who has practiced being steady. "I told myself leaving them was strategy. Drawing the company away from the records. Some nights I even believe it."

"It was strategy," Okonkwo said. "And it was abandonment. Both things are true. The river holds both banks." He said it without cruelty, the way he said everything, and Ilse bowed her head, and no one added anything, because there was nothing to add.

Later, when the others slept in shifts, Freya took the first watch alone at the rail, listening to the flood go by. The rain had stopped. Stars showed in torn patches overhead, and the gorge walls were black cutouts against them. From somewhere upstream came a deep, resonant booming—the water hammering into some cavern in the rock, a drumbeat slow as a heartbeat.

Naledi joined him near midnight, moving carefully around her sling. For a long time neither spoke.

"When I keyed that radio," she said finally, "the first time, back at the beginning—I told myself it was just coordinates. Just weather reports. Just keeping my family breathing." She stared at the water. "Then it was warnings, then it was answers to questions I shouldn't have answered, and every time it was easier, and I hated that most of all. How easy it got." She glanced at him. "Does it ever get easy for you? Hiding?"

Freya considered the question honestly, because she'd earned honesty. "Hiding got easy. That was the problem. Ten years, and one day I realized I couldn't remember what I was hiding from anymore—only that hiding was the shape of my life." He looked upstream, toward the booming dark. "Tomas hid too, in his way. Signed the false charts, kept the salary, told himself family needed providing for. Hid until hiding killed him."

"Do you think we'll make the source tomorrow?"

"I think the river decides that." He paused. "But I think it's inclined to let us. We're finally going the direction it's going."

Naledi huffed—almost a laugh—and went below to wake Okonkwo for his watch. Freya stayed at the rail until the stars wheeled, memorizing the sound of the flood, committing it to the same place in him where he kept the sound of Tomas's laugh and the creak of the Magpie's wheel. Whatever tomorrow cost, he wanted to be able to testify to tonight: that the river had roared, and they had come anyway.

* * *

First light came grey and immediate, the way it does in gorges, night ending all at once like a lamp switched off. They cast off into mist so thick Freya navigated by sound and memory, by the echo of the cliffs telling him where the channel bent. Within an hour the mist burned off and the river showed itself, higher again, faster, and the last gorge opened ahead of them—the final throat of rock between them and the headwaters, water pouring through it in a single braided torrent.

Okonkwo climbed to the bow well and braced himself. Naledi took the stern with the spare tiller, her face pale but set. Ilse lashed herself to the deckhouse frame and put one hand flat on the satchel, the way a believer touches a relic.

Freya stood at the wheel and felt the terrible momentum of the whole enterprise gather beneath him—the flood, the pursuit, the dead, the living, the truth rolling downhill like the water itself—and understood that there was no version of this day that ended with the Magpie turning around. Retreat had died somewhere in the night, quietly, without ceremony. All that remained was up.

"Last water," he said aloud, to the crew, to the boat, to his brother wherever his brother was. "Hold on to something true."

He opened the throttle, and the Magpie leapt for the throat of the gorge just as the echo of engines—real this time, unmistakable, carried downwind from the logging skids above—reached them over the roar.

Verhoeven had found the high road. They had perhaps an hour.

Freya aimed the bow at the white water and gave the river everything she asked for.
