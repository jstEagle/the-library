## 18. The Source

The river stopped being a river an hour before they arrived.

It happened gradually, the way most true things do. The current that had fought them for two days through the gorge began to slacken, then to hesitate, then to simply stand still—water holding water, as if the whole Vasseur had drawn a breath it did not intend to release. The Magpie's wake flattened out behind them. Naledi cut the engine without being asked, and in the sudden silence Freya heard what he had not heard in ten years of river work: nothing. No current hiss against the hull. No snags ticking past beneath the keel. Just the drip of water off the winch cable and, somewhere ahead, a sound like slow applause—the whisper of springs feeding a pool too deep and too calm to show it.

"Captain," Naledi said softly. She was standing at the bow rail with her arm still bound to her chest, her face turned toward the head of the gorge. "Look."

Freya looked, and for a long moment he forgot Verhoeven, forgot the sirens that had chased them up the gorge, forgot even the ache in his hands from two days of poling through white water.

The gorge walls fell away on both sides, opening like cupped hands into an amphitheater of stone. Sheer cliffs rose three hundred feet, streaked green with hanging gardens, their faces seamed with threads of falling water that caught the morning light and broke it into moving color. At the base of the cliffs, dozens of springs emerged—not one source but a hundred, welling up through pale sand and between mossy boulders, each one feeding the great pool below with a silence so complete it felt ceremonial. The water was so clear that Freya could see the bottom thirty feet down: white sand ridged into long dunes by currents no longer there, like a bed someone had made and then left.

And above the waterline, on a shelf of rock at the pool's northern edge, stood the stone.

It was taller than a man, wider than two, its surface dressed smooth by hands centuries dead. Even at this distance Freya could see the carving covered every face of it—rows upon rows of figures, spirals, lines of script that Okonkwo had spent weeks learning to read. It did not look like a monument. It looked like a document. A page set upright in the world, written where no one could take it down.

Ilse Marchetti came up beside him at the rail. She had not slept more than an hour in two days, and it showed in the gray of her skin, but her eyes were wet and bright.

"That's it," she said. "That's the treaty stone. The governor's own surveyors recorded it in 1841. And then—" she made a small, bitter gesture that took in the cliffs, the springs, the whole drowned history of the upper river, "—then it was never mentioned again."

Freya brought the Magpie in slow, using the pole now, feeling for the sandy shallows near the eastern bank. The boat slid over water so transparent it seemed to float on air. He tied off to a granite knob worn glass-smooth by centuries of mooring lines—mooring lines from boats whose names appeared on no chart he had ever seen—and for a moment he just stood with his hand on the rope, feeling how old the wear was.

People had been coming here for a very long time. People who were supposed, according to every map the company owned, not to exist.

* * *

They worked through the morning.

Okonkwo went to the stone first, alone, and did something Freya hadn't expected: he took off his boots. He crossed the last few yards of wet rock barefoot, and when he reached the carved surface he laid both palms flat against it and stood with his head bowed for the better part of ten minutes, saying nothing. Naledi watched him from the rail with an expression Freya couldn't read. Whatever language was cut into that stone, it was clear Okonkwo wasn't reading it so much as answering it.

Then the work began, and it consumed them all.

Ilse photographed everything—every panel, every line, shooting in the raking morning light and again under diffusers rigged from the Magpie's awning when the sun climbed high enough to flatten the carved shadows into nothing. She worked with a feverish precision, logging each frame in a waterproof notebook, cross-referencing against the sketches she'd made during the expedition's first visit, muttering frame numbers under her breath like prayers. When her hands shook too badly to hold the camera steady, Naledi held the light board, her wounded arm braced against her body, and neither woman commented on it.

Okonkwo translated aloud as he worked, and Freya found himself pulled away from the mooring line again and again to listen.

"It opens with an invocation to the river itself," he said, tracing a column of glyphs with one finger without touching them. "Not to any god. To the water. 'You who were before the names of men.' Then the parties: the peoples of the watershed—here they're named by reach, by bend, by spring. Forty-one signatory communities." His finger moved down the stone. "And the other party. The governor, acting for the crown beyond the sea."

"And the terms?" Ilse asked, though Freya could see she knew them; she wanted them spoken into the air anyway.

"The source watershed—all of it, from these springs to the falls at Kessing—is granted to the river peoples in perpetuity. In perpetuity." Okonkwo looked up. "The governor's seal is cut here, at the base. And this clause—" he crouched, brushing lichen from a lower panel with the edge of his palm, gentle as a man touching a sleeping child, "—this clause is the one they will hate most. It says the grant cannot be transferred, sold, or surrendered, because the river is not theirs to give. They hold it in trust. For the water. For those downstream. For those not yet born."

Freya stood very still.

Every land title the company held above the falls—every concession, every forced lease, every eviction carried out on the strength of documents claiming the upper river was empty, unclaimed, unclaimed because unmapped, unmapped because mapped and then erased—rested on the lie Tomas had helped build. And here, cut in stone older than any of it, was the truth, sitting in plain sight, waiting a hundred years for someone willing to look.

"Tomas surveyed this," Freya said quietly. "He stood right here."

"He did." Ilse lowered her camera. "His field notes reference the stone. That's how we found it—he'd marked the coordinates in a survey the company filed and then buried. Someone in their archive kept the original. I paid a great deal of money to see it." She paused. "I think your brother would have wanted it used."

Freya didn't answer. He walked back to the boat and checked the mooring lines that didn't need checking, and let the grief sit where it sat.

By midday they had photographed the entire stone from every angle, made rubbings of the key panels on Ilse's tracing film, and filled forty pages of Okonkwo's notebook with transliteration and translation. Ilse made three copies of everything—one for her pack, one for the Magpie's strongbox, one wrapped in oilcloth inside the spare fuel drum, because she had learned on this expedition what happened to things kept in only one place.

"We should go back down," she said, sealing the drum. "Today. Before—"

"Before what?" Naledi asked.

Freya had been watching the gorge mouth all afternoon. Now he said, flatly, "Before they finish walking."

* * *

Verhoeven's men came overland because the river would not carry them.

Freya saw them first as movement on the southern rim—a flicker of khaki against the grey-green cliff top, maybe two kilometers east where the escarpment gentled into a saddle that a determined party could climb. He counted through the binoculars. Eight men, moving single file, burdened heavy. Rifles slung. One carrying something long and boxed on his back that Freya guessed, correctly, was a radio.

"Eight," he said. "Maybe nine."

"They can't have many more," Ilse said. "The flood took their boats at the gorge mouth. Verhoeven wouldn't risk his whole garrison on a climb like that."

"He doesn't need many," Freya said. "He needs enough to hold us here until the water drops, or until he gets a helicopter in. Either way, we're the ones in the bowl."

They discussed running for it—loading the Magpie and backing out down the gorge through rapids they'd barely survived coming up—but the arithmetic was ugly. The rising river that blocked Verhoeven's boats also blocked theirs, and the pool gave them no current to fight, only the gorge below, which by now would be a brown roaring chute. There was nowhere to run to. There was only here, and the stone, and whatever they could finish before the men on the rim finished descending.

So they dug in.

Not literally—there was nothing to dig, the shore all granite and gravel—but in the way river people dig in, which is to say they prepared the boat. Freya moved the Magpie to the deepest corner of the pool, stern toward the open water, engine warmed, lines cast off but looped ready on the cleats. Naledi loaded the shotgun and the old bolt rifle and stowed them under the wheelhouse cushion where a hand could find them blind. Okonkwo packed the translations into the oilcloth drum and then, at Ilse's instruction, made a fourth copy of the most critical pages and sewed them into the lining of his jacket with fishing line, working calmly while the first of Verhoeven's men came down the scree slope on the far side of the amphitheater, tiny as ants, unhurried, certain.

"They're not attacking," Naledi observed, watching through the binoculars. "Why aren't they attacking?"

"Because they don't have to," Freya said. "We're not going anywhere. He wants us to sit here and think about it. He wants us tired and scared when the talking starts." He handed her the binoculars. "Keep count. If they split into two groups, wake me."

The eight men reached the pool's edge near dusk and made camp four hundred meters south, within sight but out of easy rifle range—a professional distance, chosen deliberately. Freya watched them through the last light: pitching tarps, building no fire, posting two sentries facing the water. Disciplined. Patient. Men paid to wait.

At full dark, a voice carried across the still water, amplified by the amphitheater's stone walls until it seemed to come from everywhere at once.

"Captain Joubert." Verhoeven's voice, unhurried, almost friendly. "Lovely evening for it. We'll talk in the morning. Nothing needs to happen tonight that anyone would regret."

Silence after that, except the springs, whispering their endless applause.

Freya took the middle watch. He sat in the wheelhouse with the rifle across his knees and the pool silvered by moonlight around him, and he looked at the treaty stone rising black against the stars, and he thought about Tomas standing on this same shore with a surveyor's chain and a conscience, and about the letter in the strongbox downstairs naming the man camped four hundred meters away as his brother's murderer.

Naledi relieved him at two. As he crawled into his bunk he heard her murmuring into the radio, low, testing frequencies downriver—Kessing relay, then the delta stations, one by one, listening for carriers in the static. Building the chain. She glanced up as he settled, and in the dark her eyes were steady.

"Tomorrow?" she asked.

"Tomorrow we finish it," Freya said. "Whatever he offers, whatever he does—we finish it."

Above the pool, the treaty stone held the moonlight on its carved faces, patient as it had been for three hundred years, waiting for morning.
