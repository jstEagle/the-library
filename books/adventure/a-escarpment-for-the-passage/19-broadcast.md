## 19. Broadcast

The storm that had driven Verhoeven's men to ground had also soaked the Magpie to her ribs. Freya spent the first hour of light wringing the canvas tarp dry and checking the radio housing with a lamp between his teeth. The set was old—older than Naledi, older than his time on the river—but he'd kept it alive the way you kept anything alive out here: with patience, spare parts scavenged from three wrecks, and a superstition against ever letting it go silent for long.

"Will it reach?" Ilse stood at the companionway, hair still damp, the copied pages of the treaty wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like an infant.

"The set reaches." Freya wiped corrosion from a terminal with a rag gone gray with use. "The question is who's listening, and whether they'll pass it on."

"They'll listen," Naledi said from the wheelhouse doorway. Her arm was bound in a sling made from a torn awning, and she was pale under her brown skin, but her voice was steady. "I told you which stations I called. I know their operators by name. Mama Efuru at Kilometre Forty keeps her set on all night because she's afraid of missing word of her son in the delta. Old Bram at the fuel depot sleeps with his hand on the volume knob. They'll listen." She paused. "They have reason not to trust me. I know that. But they'll listen."

Freya looked at her a long moment. Three days ago she'd confessed to months of coerced transmissions—coordinates, schedules, the shape of their cargo—and he had wanted to put her off the boat in the middle of the drowned forest. Instead he'd taught her how to load the flare gun one-handed, because that was what the river demanded now: everyone useful, grudges postponed but not forgiven.

"You'll make the first call," he said. "Your voice opens it. Then they know it's not me asking them to forgive me. It's you telling them why they should."

Naledi's throat worked. She nodded once.

* * *

Okonkwo had spent the night making the translation sing.

He sat in the wheelhouse now among a drift of paper, the lamplight catching the flat planes of his face, reading aloud in a low voice—not the cave script itself, which no living tongue spoke whole, but his rendering of it into trade language, then into the river creole the settlements actually used. He read it three times, adjusting a phrase here, cutting a clause there, until the treaty sounded less like a museum piece and more like something a grandmother might recite.

"It was never meant to be locked away," he said, setting down his pen. "That is the thing none of us understood—the company least of all. A treaty carved in stone above the source, where every traveler going upriver would pass beneath it. It was meant to be *read*. Aloud. Repeatedly. The carving says so, in its own closing lines: *let this be spoken at every gathering of boats.*" He looked up, and there was something almost amused in his gravity. "We are simply doing what the stone asks."

Ilse laughed—a short, cracked sound, the first laugh Freya had heard from her since the portage. "A four-hundred-year-old broadcast protocol."

"A good one," Okonkwo said. "It has survived worse than a company."

Freya spread the last document on the chart table: Tomas's letter, softened at the folds, the ink feathered where river damp had found it once and been driven back. He had read it alone twice since the caves. Reading it aloud to strangers—to anyone—was another matter. His brother's handwriting was neat, surveyor's neat, each letter placed like a benchmark stake, and the words were the same: *I falsified the charts. Verhoeven ordered it. When I refused to falsify the second survey, he arranged my accident. If you are reading this, I am dead, and the river is still lying about where it comes from.*

"He wrote it to be read aloud too," Ilse said quietly. "He addressed it to 'whoever finds this.' Not to you by name, Freya. He didn't dare hope you'd be the one."

"He hoped anyway," Freya said. "He just couldn't write it."

* * *

They ran the set off the Magpie's auxiliary battery, engine idling to hold the charge, the boat tied to the treaty stone's shadow where the amphitheater of the headwaters cupped the water into something almost glassy. The spring-fed pools below the escarpment gave off a cold breath. Above them the storm clouds were breaking apart into high white shreds, and the light came down clean and hard.

Naledi keyed the microphone. Freya watched her square her shoulders the way she did before running a rapid—committing, past the point of argument.

"All stations, all stations, this is the vessel Magpie, calling the relay chain, Kilometre Forty through the delta. This is Naledi Okoro speaking." She swallowed. "Some of you know my voice. Some of you have reason to hate it. I'm not calling to ask forgiveness. I'm calling because I owe you a debt, and this is the only coin I have. What follows is a treaty. It is real. It is carved in stone at the source of this river, and it says the water belongs to the people who live beside it. The company's maps are lies. We can prove it. Listen, and then say it again on your sets, station to station, until everyone downriver has heard it. Beginning transmission."

She looked at Okonkwo. He lifted the pages.

* * *

Kilometre Forty answered first.

Mama Efuru's voice came through the static thin and wavering and absolutely awake: *"Magpie, Magpie, this is Kilometre Forty, I hear you, girl, I hear you—I thought that voice was a ghost, I thought—" A pause, a breath gathered across forty years of river nights. "Say your treaty. Say it slow. I'm writing."*

So Okonkwo read it slow. The grant of the watershed in perpetuity. The names of the signatory peoples, some of them names that survived nowhere else except in the mouths of elders and in the stone. The governor's seal described in detail, matched against the colonial archives Ilse had photographed years ago and carried folded inside her coat through everything. The closing injunction: *let this be spoken at every gathering of boats.*

When he finished, the silence on the band lasted long enough that Freya checked the transmitter light.

Then Mama Efuru again, and her voice had changed—harder, younger somehow, as if the transmission had run current back up the line into her. *"I have it written. I will read it back now, and you will correct me if I err, and then I will call the fuel depot, and Bram will call the mission station, and the mission will call the ferry landing. Go and eat something, all of you. This will take all day and all night, and it should."*

She read it back without a single error.

* * *

By midday the chain was alive.

It moved the way floodwater moves through a channel system—finding every path at once. Bram at the fuel depot repeated the treaty in his gravel drawl, stumbling over the archaic clauses, refusing help, starting over twice until he had it perfect. The mission station took it down and added Ilse's testimony, which she delivered in a flat, unadorned voice, naming dates, naming the expedition members lost, naming the dynamite crates she had seen stacked at base camp with her own eyes. The ferry landing picked it up and passed it to the delta market stations, where the signal fragmented into a dozen smaller relays, fishing boats with handheld sets, a floating chapel, a dredge crew that had once been company contractors and now, apparently, was not.

Freya stood at the rail and listened to his river talk back to him.

For ten years he had known these settlements the way you know houses whose lights you pass at night—warm squares in the dark, never entered. Now their voices filled the wheelhouse, overlapping, correcting one another, arguing over a disputed clause of the treaty with the ferocity of people handling something that belonged to them. An elder at the ferry landing insisted the third signatory name was pronounced with the low tone, and Okonkwo, listening, closed his eyes and said, "She is right. She is right. The stone agrees with her."

"How can the stone agree with her?" Naledi asked.

"Because she learned it from someone who learned it from someone who learned it when the speaking was still continuous." Okonkwo smiled faintly. "The chain was never entirely broken. The company only convinced everyone along it that they were alone."

In the afternoon they broadcast Tomas's letter.

Freya read it himself. His voice held for the first paragraphs and failed at the signature—he got through *Tomas Joubert, Assistant Surveyor, Second Class* and then could not get through the date, because the date was eleven days before the "accident," and eleven days was how long the company had let a man live after he tried to tell the truth. Naledi's good hand covered his on the table. He finished the letter. Down the chain, station after station repeated it, and more than once the repeating voice broke too, and the next station picked the letter up mid-sentence and carried it onward without comment, the way you carry a heavy thing past a neighbor who is also carrying something heavy.

By dusk, the company's own frequencies had gone strange. Freya swept the bands and found the patrol channel full of clipped, panicked traffic—orders to jam, orders to locate the transmitting vessel, orders that trailed off into arguments, because half the company's own river employees were men hired out of the settlements now hearing their grandmothers' names read aloud as signatories to a treaty. One voice on the patrol band, low and furious, said: *"You can't jam every boat on this river, sir. With respect. You can't even find them all."*

Verhoeven's voice answered, and it was very quiet, and very controlled, and it said: *"Find the Magpie."*

* * *

Night came down over the headwaters, and the transmission did not stop.

They rotated watches—Naledi first despite her arm, then Freya, then Ilse, who turned out to have the best radio voice of any of them, clear and patient, a woman who had spent years explaining things to people who did not want to hear them. Okonkwo sat apart with the original photographs of the carvings, cross-checking every repetition that came back up the chain, and twice he keyed the mic to correct a tonal mark, and both times the correction went downriver and came back absorbed, the treaty polishing itself through iteration like a stone in a current.

Around midnight, something new began to happen.

The settlements stopped merely repeating. They started adding.

The mission station appended the testimony of a fisherman who had been evicted from the upper oxbow twenty years ago on the strength of a company map. The ferry landing appended the record of a water-rights fine levied against a village that had, according to the official charts, not existed. Station after station, the accounts accumulated—decades of them, a sediment of grievances laid down year upon year in the dark, all of it suddenly given a channel. The treaty was the keel. Everything else came aboard on top of it.

"They're building a case," Ilse said, watching the log Naledi kept of every contributing station. "Not just broadcasting. *Documenting.* Every settlement is attaching its own evidence while the whole river is listening."

"That's what the stone intended," Okonkwo said softly. "*Let this be spoken at every gathering of boats.* Not read. Spoken. A treaty isn't paper. It's a thing a people agree to keep saying."

Toward dawn, a new voice came onto the band—an old man's voice from a delta station so small it hadn't appeared on any list, singing rather than speaking, a boat-gathering song in a language Okonkwo had translated from the cave walls only days before. He went rigid, then rose slowly to his feet, one hand pressed flat against the bulkhead.

"That song is in the treaty," he said. "The final verses. It is the song the carving instructs them to sing. No one has sung it in—" He stopped. Started again. "No one has sung it in living memory. Except someone down there remembers. Someone always remembered."

On the band, other voices were picking the song up, raggedly at first, then together, settlement after settlement joining across two hundred kilometres of water, until the chorus came up the relay chain and poured out of the Magpie's little speaker into the cold air of the headwaters, where the actual stone it referred to stood ten metres away above the actual spring, listening, if stone listens, to its own instruction finally obeyed.

Freya found he could not speak. He stood at the rail with the wheelhouse door open and the song spilling past him, and below the treaty stone the source water rose up cold and silver out of the earth, exactly where the liars had said there was nothing, exactly where it had always been.

* * *

The sun came up on the fourth day of transmission, and the chain showed no sign of tiring. If anything it had thickened overnight—more stations, more voices, the repetitions now layered over one another so that on any given frequency, somewhere on the Vasseur, the treaty was being spoken at all times. You could tune anywhere and land inside it.

It was Naledi, on the morning watch, who caught the message that mattered.

*"Magpie, Magpie, this is Kilometre Forty."* Mama Efuru's voice, but formal now, official, the voice of a woman speaking for more than herself. *"Be advised: the delta council has convened by radio. Eleven settlements represented. We have adopted the treaty as read. We have retained counsel in the capital by coastal wire—we paid for the call ourselves, all of us, in coins, you should have seen the table. Our lawyers request copies of the photographs, the letter, and Dr. Marchetti's testimony in full. And Magpie—" a pause, and the formality slipped, just for a moment, "—girl, whatever happens next, you come down this river whenever you please, and every dock from the escarpment to the sea will take your lines. That is council business. Officially minuted."*

Static. Then, from the ferry landing, a second voice: *"Seconded. Minuted."* From the mission: *"Seconded."* From the fuel depot, Bram, gruff as gravel: *"Seconded, and about bloody time."*

Down the chain it went, settlement by settlement, a roll call of docks and landings and floating markets, each one seconding, each one minuting, until the whole river had spoken in one slow rolling voice like a wave traveling shore to shore.

Inside the wheelhouse, nobody said anything for a while. Ilse had both hands over her mouth. Okonkwo sat very straight, as though at a ceremony, which, Freya supposed, he was.

"They own the story now," Ilse said finally. "All of it. There's no taking it back. Even if they sink this boat, even if they bury the caves, two hundred stations have the words by heart."

"They won't sink the boat," Freya said.

He was looking north, up the gorge, where the overland trail from base camp came down to the water. Through the binoculars, small in the morning glare, he could see them: a column of men with packs and rifles, moving fast along the rimrock, and behind them, glinting, the masts of two boats being assembled at the gorge mouth from parts carried overland. Verhoeven had found a way down after all. Of course he had. Men like Verhoeven always found a way down; it was finding a way back up that the river controlled.

But the column had stopped. Stopped and clustered, and passed something hand to hand—a radio handset, a single receiver, passed from man to man down the line of them, each one holding it to his ear for a moment before giving it back.

Listening. All of them, listening, in the middle of their own advance, to the sound of the river learning its own name.

Freya lowered the binoculars.

"Naledi," he said. "Get below and rest your arm. Ilse, secure those copies—they're going to want them off us, and we're going to make them work for every page." He turned the Magpie's bow downstream, toward the gorge, toward the rising water, toward the men on the rimrock who had just heard something they could never unhear. "And somebody tell the chain we're coming down to them. Tell them to keep talking. We're going to need every voice on this river before the week is out."

Below the treaty stone, the source water went on rising, cold and inexorable, feeding the flood that was already moving downriver to meet them.
