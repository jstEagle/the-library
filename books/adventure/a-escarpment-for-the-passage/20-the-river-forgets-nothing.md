## 20. The River Forgets Nothing

The storm came down the gorge ahead of its own sound.

Freya felt it first in the water—a shudder through the Magpie's hull at anchor, a tremor like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Then the air pressure shifted, and his ears popped, and Naledi came out of the wheelhouse with her arm still bound in a sling of torn canvas and said, simply, "It's coming."

They had one night's grace. Maybe less.

The source amphitheater sat above them like a held breath: a bowl of grey limestone perhaps four hundred meters across, ringed by walls the color of old bone, and at its center the spring itself—water rising out of the earth without violence, without hurry, as if it had all the time in the world. Which, Freya supposed, it did. The treaty stone stood on its plinth at the water's edge, older than every name anyone had ever given this country, its carved bands of script running around it like a belt that had never once been loosened.

Ilse had spent the day photographing it from every angle, working with the methodical calm of a woman who had already decided how she wanted to be found if it came to that. Okonkwo sat cross-legged at the stone's base with his notebooks spread around him, transcribing by lamplight, murmuring the syllables to himself the way other men prayed.

And below, two kilometers downriver where the gorge narrowed to a throat, Verhoeven's men were digging in.

Freya stood at the rail and watched the water rise.

* * *

The Magpie rode at anchor off the gravel shoal where the gorge opened into the amphitheater's mouth. It was the only deep water for miles—the spring-fed pool never froze, never dried, never dropped—and Freya had put them there because there was nowhere else. Behind them rose the escarpment wall, sheer for sixty meters. Ahead lay the rapids they'd climbed, now unrecognizable, the rocks they'd threaded drowned under brown churning water. The river had come up nearly three meters since dawn.

"It'll crest tomorrow," he told them, gathered in the wheelhouse while rain hammered the deck overhead. "Maybe midday. The highlands got hammered—Naledi picked up the weather relay before they jammed it. Everything north of the divide is running off at once."

"And Verhoeven?" Ilse asked.

"Has eleven men, two boats, and no way home but through us." Freya laid the chart out on the table, the new one, the honest one, and weighted its corners with mugs. "He knows it too. That's what worries me. A man with no way back stops calculating and starts gambling."

"He tried to buy you twice," Naledi said. "Then he tried to bury you in a cave. What's left?"

Freya looked at the chart. At the throat of the gorge, where the honest survey showed a rock garden the company's maps had always rendered as blank, navigable water. *Tomas knew,* he thought. *Tomas drew those rocks in and they erased them, because erasing them made the river look tame enough to own.*

"What's left is the river," he said. "And he doesn't believe in it. That's the whole man in one sentence."

Okonkwo looked up from his notebook. He spoke rarely even now, but when he did, the words arrived finished, like stones set in a wall. "The treaty has a clause about this," he said. "The flood clause. The first governor asked why the river peoples didn't build weirs at the gorge, control the water. Their answer is carved right here." He turned the page toward them. "*Because the river remembers what was done to it, and repays it in season. We do not stand between a creditor and his debt.*"

Nobody said anything for a moment. Rain drummed. Somewhere upriver, thunder rolled along the escarpment like a barrel going downstairs.

"Naledi," Freya said finally. "Get the radio warm. Low power, short bursts. If we're going to drown, I want witnesses."

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, in the last of the dark, and they came fast.

Freya heard the engines before he saw anything—two of them, running hard against current that should have made such speed impossible, which meant Verhoeven was spending his boats like a man spending money he'd stolen. He woke the others with a hand on shoulders, no voices, and by the time the first searchlight swept the amphitheater's mouth they were all in position: Ilse and Okonkwo flat behind the treaty stone's plinth with the document cases, Naledi in the wheelhouse with the radio key under her good hand, and Freya at the Magpie's stern rail, watching the black water.

The patrol boats came around the bend side by side, floodlights crossing, and the light found the pool and the shoal and the anchored Magpie and stopped, briefly, as if surprised to find the world still here.

A loudspeaker crackled. Verhoeven's voice, amplified and flattened, stripped of all its drawing-room courtesy. "Marchetti! You have until full light to bring the records to my boat. After that I stop being reasonable, and I promise you, madam, you do not want to meet the man underneath."

Freya keyed the Magpie's hailer. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "You're standing in a rock garden, Verhoeven. Tomas mapped every stone of it. Your company erased them off the chart. Ask yourself why a map would lie about rocks."

Silence. Then, closer, quieter, almost intimate: "Joubert. Or should I say—" and he said the other name, the buried one, the one Freya hadn't answered to in eleven years, and hearing it over the water in the dark was like being struck somewhere with no bone to bruise.

"That name's dead," Freya said. "You killed it yourself when you killed my brother."

"I offered him everything," Verhoeven said, and there was something almost wistful in it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "I'm offering you more. Last time, Joubert. The file. The pardon. The captaincy. Bring me Marchetti and the originals, and by Christmas you'll be running company traffic on the lower river with your real name on the door."

Freya looked down at the water boiling past the hull. Three meters up and climbing. He could hear it now—not just see it, *hear* it, a low continuous roar from the gorge like the river clearing its throat.

"You know what my brother wrote in his letter?" Freya said. "He wrote that you told him a map was just a story people agreed to believe. And then you spent thirty years making sure nobody could disagree." He let go of the hailer key, then pressed it once more. "The agreement's over. Come and get us."

* * *

They came at first light, and the river was waiting.

Later, Freya would try to reconstruct the order of it and fail; some things happen too fast and too completely to hold in sequence. But the pieces were these:

Verhoeven split his force. Eight men in the larger boat came straight up the throat of the gorge against the current, riding the flood surge the way a surfer rides a wave, because Verhoeven had looked at the rising water and seen not a threat but a road—an escalator carrying him to his prize. Three men took the smaller boat wide, toward the shoal, to flank the Magpie and cut off retreat that didn't exist.

And the river, which had been rising all night, chose that hour to crest.

Freya saw it before he understood it: downstream, the whole surface of the gorge seemed to *stiffen*, a white line appearing far below and coming up fast, and then the bore hit the pool and the pool stood up. Not gradually. All at once, like a table being tilted. The Magpie surged at her anchor, swung, held. The flanking boat, caught broadside on the shoal's edge, was lifted, rolled, and slammed down onto the gravel in a single motion, spilling its three men into shallows gone suddenly violent.

The main boat made it further. Of course it did—Verhoeven drove it like the machine it was, eight men and a hundred and forty horsepower wrestling the surge, and for maybe forty seconds it actually gained, planing up the face of the flood into the amphitheater's mouth, close enough that Freya could see Verhoeven himself at the wheel, rain streaming off him, teeth bared in something that wasn't quite a smile.

Then the river introduced him to Tomas's rocks.

The first one took the boat amidships. Freya heard it even over the roar—a crack like a rifle, fiberglass and timber folding around granite that had waited ten thousand years and could wait ten thousand more. The boat slewed, took the second rock on the quarter, and began to come apart with a thoroughness that seemed almost editorial. Men went into the water. Life vests, Freya noted distantly, none of them wearing life vests, because men who don't believe in rivers don't believe in drowning either.

The current took them. It took them sideways and down, tumbling them through the drowned rock garden their own company had erased from the charts, and the river handled each of them according to its ancient, impersonal bookkeeping. Two washed onto the shoal alive, coughing, crawling on hands and knees. One snagged on a boulder and hung there, screaming, until a rope thrown from the Magpie—Naledi's throw, one-armed and perfect—hauled him in. The rest the river kept.

Verhoeven went over the falls at the head of the pool.

Freya saw him go: a dark figure swept off the wreck's remains, swallowed, gone—and then there again fifty meters out, one arm windmilling, going under, coming up, going under. The current was dragging him back toward the gorge, toward the throat, toward the long tumble through the rocks that had already taken four of his men, and Verhoeven fought it the way he'd fought everything, arrogantly, expending strength he didn't have against a force that didn't care.

Freya was over the side before he decided to be.

The water hit him like a wall of hands. Cold came up from beneath the flood's brown warmth, spring-water cold, ancient cold, and it closed around him and pulled, and for one lucid second he understood exactly what he'd done and how stupid it was. Then his hand closed on a collar—wet uniform fabric, a shoulder, a jaw—and Verhoeven surfaced beside him, eyes wide and white, and grabbed him with the mindless strength of the drowning.

They went under together. The river rolled them. Freya's world became noise and cold and the animal problem of staying up, and somewhere in it he got an arm around the man's chest from behind and locked it, and turned them both downstream, feet-first, the way you're supposed to, the way Tomas had taught him when they were boys swimming the delta channels—*never fight it, Frey, read it, ride it, the river always wins so make sure it wins slowly.*

They came out on the shoal in waist-deep water, coughing, and Freya dragged Verhoeven up the gravel and dropped him there, face-down, while the flood roared past close enough to touch.

* * *

By noon it was over.

Eight men had gone into the water. Five came out of it—two from the wrecked flanking boat, three fished from eddies downstream by ropes and luck. The rest the river kept, and would keep, and would give back eventually in its own time and its own places, or not. The second patrol boat, seeing the first die, had turned and run back down the gorge and grounded itself on a bar two miles below, where its crew sat huddled under a tarp waiting for a rescue that would have to come overland, weeks from now, if it came at all.

Verhoeven sat on the gravel with his back against a drift log, soaked through, a gash over one eye, watching the water that had beaten him with an expression Freya couldn't read and didn't much want to.

Ilse stood over him with the document cases. Okonkwo stood beside her. Naledi stood at the Magpie's rail, one-armed, keeping watch on the prisoners with the company rifle she'd taken off a drowned man, though everyone including her hoped she'd never need it.

"You can't hold me," Verhoeven said. His voice was hoarse with river water. "Whatever you think you've broadcast, whatever testimony—the company has lawyers, courts, ministers on retainer. You have a boat and a cave painting."

"A treaty," Okonkwo corrected quietly. "Four hundred years old. With the governor's own seal reproduced on it. And a surveyor's original field notes, signed and dated, describing exactly who ordered them falsified." He crouched to Verhoeven's eye level. "You are not facing these people, Mr. Verhoeven. You are facing paper. Paper does not drown."

Verhoeven laughed—a short, wet, disbelieving sound. "Paper. You think paper beats money?"

"No," Freya said. He crouched down across from the man, forearms on knees, close enough to see the blood thinning in the rain. "But money can't swim. I watched yours go under this morning. All of it. Every boat, every gun, every man you brought to own a river you never once bothered to understand." He nodded at the flood. "That's the thing you never got, Verhoeven. You thought the river was property that hadn't been filed yet. It's not. It's a creditor. And it just collected."

For a long moment Verhoeven said nothing. Rain ticked on the drift log. Downstream, the flood was already beginning its slow withdrawal, the white line dropping, the rocks emerging one by one from the brown water like a jury filing back in.

"My men," Verhoeven said finally, and his voice cracked on the second word. "The ones on the bar downstream. They'll starve before anyone reaches them."

"Not before the water drops enough to run supplies down," Freya said. "Three days, maybe four. I'll take them food myself." He stood. "Not mercy. Don't mistake it. I just need you alive."

Verhoeven looked up at him. "Why?"

"So you have to live with it." Freya held his gaze. "Every hearing, every headline, every morning you wake up and remember that the river beat you and a dead man's charts undid you. My brother gets to watch you do it. That's not mercy, Verhoeven. That's sentencing."

He walked away up the shoal toward the Magpie, and behind him he heard Verhoeven say nothing at all, which was, Freya thought, the first honest thing the man had managed in thirty years.

* * *

That evening the storm passed east and the sky over the escarpment cleared to a hard, rinsed blue, and the water began to fall.

Freya stood at the rail with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand, watching the rocks come back. One by one they surfaced—Tomas's rocks, every one of them exactly where the honest survey had placed them, exactly where the company's beautiful lying charts had promised clear water. The river gave them back the way it gave back everything: eventually, and changed, and keeping accounts.

Naledi joined him at the rail, her sling dark with spray. For a while neither of them spoke.

"You went in after him," she said at last.

"Seemed like the thing to do."

"It wasn't. It was the worst possible thing to do, and you did it anyway, and it worked, and I've decided not to forgive you for at least a week." She bumped her good shoulder against his arm. Below them, on the shoal, Ilse and Okonkwo were packing the document cases into waterproof wraps, preparing them for the journey downriver that would end, if the radio relays held, in courtrooms instead of caves.

"The settlements are answering," Naledi said. "All night. Station after station. Kesh Junction, the ferry towns, the delta co-ops—they're repeating the broadcast word for word. Some of them are reading it out in the markets." She paused. "The ones I sold out. They're the loudest."

"How does that sit?"

She considered it honestly, the way she considered everything. "Like a debt," she said. "Which means I know exactly how Verhoeven feels right now." She looked up at him. "Except mine I can pay."

The radio crackled behind them in the wheelhouse. Naledi went to it, listened, and came back with an expression Freya hadn't seen on her before—something between awe and alarm.

"Freya. It's the district magistrate's office. On the relay chain. They're asking for—" she glanced at the paper in her hand, as if she needed to check— "for the captain of the *Magpie*. By name. Both names." She held it out. "They want your testimony. First session of the inquiry into the upper river. They're asking you to bring the originals down yourself."

Freya took the paper. The rain-washed evening light lay gold on the water, on the treaty stone above them, on the rocks standing free of the flood at last. Downstream, two thousand kilometers of river ran toward the delta, through every settlement that had ever lived on it unseen, and every one of them now knew his name and why.

"They asked for both names," Naledi said quietly. "Which one are you going to give them?"

Freya folded the message and put it in his shirt pocket, over his heart, next to the letter he carried there.

"All of them," he said. "I've earned every one."
