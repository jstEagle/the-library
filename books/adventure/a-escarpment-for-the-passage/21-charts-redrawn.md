## 21. Charts, Redrawn

The hearings were held in the customs house at Port Vasseur, a building Freya had spent ten years walking past without once going inside. It had been built by the same company whose titles were now under suspension—its foundations poured with company money, its brass fittings stamped with the company's old crest, a heron swallowing a fish—and for three weeks it filled every morning with the sound of people saying the river's name out loud as if it belonged to them.

Freya testified twice. The first time he brought charts: Tomas's original survey, water-stained but legible, showing the upper river in all its branching honesty—the villages, the tributaries, the water rights marked in his brother's small precise hand. He watched the tribunal members pass it down the table and saw one of them, an older woman from the delta courts, run her finger along a tributary that appeared on no official map and mouth the word *there*.

The second time he brought nothing at all. They asked him about Verhoeven, who sat two tables away in a gray suit with his hands folded, wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic he already knew would not come out in his favor.

"Captain Joubert," the chairwoman said. "Is that your name?"

He had prepared for this question for a decade. He had rehearsed denials in the wheelhouse of the *Magpie* on long empty stretches of river, practiced being no one so thoroughly that being someone felt like a language he'd forgotten.

"It is," he said. "It always was."

Verhoeven did not look up. That was somehow worse than if he had.

* * *

The company's lawyers fought the way floodwater fights a levee—not with strategy but with volume. They produced forty years of deeds, permits, purchase agreements, each one resting on the survey maps Tomas had falsified under orders, each one therefore resting on a lie that had been legal for so long it had grown roots. For a while it looked like the roots might hold. Then Okonkwo took the stand.

He was not a dramatic witness. He stood in his rumpled jacket with his reading glasses pushed up into his hair and read the treaty translation aloud in a flat, careful voice, first in the original script and then in trade tongue, and when he finished he said only: "The document grants the source watershed to its inhabitants in perpetuity. The colonial governor signed it. His signature matches the archives. I have checked it eleven times."

"Dr. Okonkwo," one of the company's counsel said, "you are asking this tribunal to invalidate four decades of commercial title on the basis of carvings in a cave."

"I am asking you to read them," Okonkwo said. "What you do after reading is your affair."

The photographs went around the table then—all two hundred of them, Ilse's documentation, every panel of the carved record lit by lamplight and rendered in merciless detail. Freya had been in the caves when they were taken. He knew what they showed. But watching the tribunal members see them for the first time, watching faces change the way faces change when a river shifts course overnight, he understood that something had already ended in that room. The company just hadn't stopped moving yet, the way a headless snake keeps swimming.

The suspension of titles came down on a Tuesday. By Wednesday the notice boards in every settlement downriver had copies nailed to them, some of them translated into three languages by hand, some of them simply the photograph of the treaty stone with the word *YOURS* chalked beneath it.

* * *

The mapping began before the ink on the suspension order was dry.

They came to the *Magpie* in ones and twos at first—elders from the unmapped village, fishermen from the middle reaches, a schoolteacher from Kessel Bend who had been keeping her own secret atlas of the river for twenty years in a exercise book tied with string. Okonkwo set up a folding table on the Magpie's aft deck and turned it into something between a classroom and a court of record. Every name the communities gave him went into the new chart: every bend, every sandbar, every spring, every grave.

"The company named things after directors," Okonkwo told Naledi one afternoon, inking a reach of river with names Freya had never heard spoken aloud since childhood. "We will name things after what happened there. That is how you make a map that cannot be bought."

Freya stayed out of it mostly. He ran the boat, kept the generator fed, cooked rice for whoever was aboard. But some evenings he found himself standing behind Okonkwo's table looking down at the growing chart, and once—he didn't plan it, it simply came out of him—he said, "That tributary is wrong. It forks again two kilometers up. There's a shoal you can't see at low water." And Okonkwo looked up at him over his glasses and smiled like a man who has been waiting weeks for exactly that sentence.

By the end of the month Freya's corrections were all through the draft chart, his knowledge of the river threaded into it alongside the elders' names and the treaty's boundaries, and he realized one night, cleaning the wheelhouse, that he had stopped thinking of himself as a man hiding in a landscape and started thinking of himself as part of one.

* * *

Ilse never left.

She rented a room above the chandlery in Port Vasseur and turned it into an archive, and when the tribunal adjourned she was there every morning with her boxes of photographs and her field journals—the surviving pages, the ones she hadn't burned—and her testimony, delivered in the same clipped, unflinching voice she used for everything. She did not soften it. She told the tribunal plainly that she had abandoned her team to draw the company away from the records, and she named every member of her expedition, living and dead, and described the last time she saw each of them.

The photographer's family came to hear it. Freya had found the body; he had carried the news. He expected them to hate her, and maybe some of them did, but the photographer's mother stood up at the end of Ilse's testimony and said, "You left them so the truth would survive. My daughter died so the truth would survive. I want the truth to be worth it." And then she sat down, and nobody in the room breathed for a while.

Afterward, on the chandlery steps, Ilse lit a cigarette with shaking hands and said, without looking at him, "I keep waiting for it to feel finished."

"It won't," Freya said. "Rivers don't finish. They just keep going somewhere."

"That's either very wise or very tired."

"Both. Usually both."

She almost smiled. She stayed on the steps until the cigarette was gone, and then she went back upstairs to her boxes, because finishing was not what she had come to the river to do.

* * *

Naledi passed her pilot's certification on a morning of hard blue sky, and the whole waterfront heard about it within the hour because she told everyone, loudly, twice.

Her shoulder had healed crooked—a little less range than before, the doctor said, a scar like a lightning strike across the joint—but her hands were fine, and her eyes were better than fine, and the examiner, a grizzled woman who had flown mail runs up the escarpment for thirty years, signed the certificate and said, "You fly like you're arguing with the aircraft. Keep that. Arrogance lands more planes safely than politeness does."

Naledi wore the certificate rolled up in her shirt pocket for three days. On the fourth day she came down to the *Magpie*, where Freya was re-caulking the port seam, and sat on the rail and swung her legs and said nothing for a long time.

"I radioed them for six weeks," she finally said. "Before any of it. Before the caves, before everything. I keep thinking—if I'd told you in chapter— if I'd told you at the fuel depot, none of them would have died. The photographer. Your brother wouldn't have—" She stopped. Her jaw worked.

"Tomas died years ago," Freya said. "Nothing you did or didn't do changes that. And the rest—" He put down the caulking iron. "Naledi. They threatened your family. You were sixteen when they started. You survived the way people survive, which is badly, and then you fixed it. You blew their detonators and took a bullet doing it. I've known captains with thirty years on the river who wouldn't have had that in them."

"You're just saying that because I'm your deckhand."

"I'm saying it because it's true, and because you're not my deckhand anymore. Pilots don't crew boats." He watched her face fall and let it fall for a moment before he added: "But pilots do need work. And I hear there's a survey going upriver that'll need air support. Someone to scout the gorge ahead of the boat. Someone with a radio and bad manners."

She stared at him. Then she grinned, wide and bright, and jumped off the rail, and punched his good arm hard enough to bruise, which was, in her language, thank you.

* * *

The letter came on company letterhead—or rather, on the letterhead of what had replaced the company, the interim watershed authority, its seal still wet-looking, its bureaucracy still smelling of fresh paint. It was addressed to *Captain F. Joubert*, and it was the first piece of mail he had received under that name in ten years.

*The Authority hereby requests Captain Joubert's services as master and pilot of the inaugural official survey of the Vasseur headwaters, being the first such survey conducted under lawful mandate and in consultation with the river communities...*

He read it four times on the dock. Around him the port went on with its noise—crates, gulls, the diesel cough of the dredge—and the letter got heavier each time he read it, not with dread this time but with something he had to sit down on a bollard to bear.

Officially recognized. His name on the mandate. The upper river, opened honestly at last, with him as the one holding the lead line.

Tomas should have had this. That was the thought that arrived first, sharp as ever. Tomas had drawn honest charts and been killed for it, and now the honor was landing in his brother's lap like a bird that couldn't tell the difference between the nest and the stone beneath it.

But that wasn't quite true either, and Freya was done lying to himself about rivers and brothers both. Tomas's survey was in the new charts. Every line of it. The honor wasn't landing on a stone. It was landing on ground Tomas had cleared.

He wrote his acceptance that evening in the wheelhouse, by lamplight, with the draft community chart spread open on the chart table beside him, and he signed it *Freya Joubert* and felt the name settle onto the page like a boat settling onto calm water.

* * *

They sailed on the first morning of the new season.

The *Magpie* went out light and riding high, her hold full not of cargo but of instruments: survey chains, sounding leads, cameras, crates of blank chart paper, Okonkwo's translation folios wrapped in oilcloth, and a small wooden box that rode lashed in the wheelhouse where Freya could see it from the helm. Naledi flew scout in a battered floatplane borrowed from the authority, her voice crackling down from ahead of them—*shoal reported mid-channel, fork two kilometers up, you were right, old man*—and Ilse stood at the bow with her notebook open even though the expedition she was finishing had been dead for months, because some work you carry forward whether anyone assigned it to you or not.

The settlements turned out along the banks as they passed. Not crowds, exactly—just people, standing at their landings and their fish racks, lifting a hand as the *Magpie* went by. Word had traveled, the way word travels on a river, faster than any boat. By the second day children were running the levees to keep pace with them, shouting the boat's name, and by the third day someone in every village seemed to know that the captain was the brother of the surveyor, and that the surveyor's charts were in the hold, going home.

On the fifth day they reached the drowned forest where they had found the expedition's wreck, and Freya slowed, and they stood at the rail a while and said nothing, and then went on.

On the eighth day the escarpment rose out of the morning mist the way it always did, sudden and total, a wall of stone with the sky balanced on top of it, and Naledi's voice came down from above: *"Gorge looks clean. Water's high but honest. You can make it."*

Freya stood at the helm with the new chart open on his knees—his own corrections inked into it, Okonkwo's names along every bank, the treaty boundary drawn in red where the law now agreed with the land—and he looked up at the escarpment, and at the source beyond it where a wooden box waited in his wheelhouse and a stone waited above the water, and he rang the engine ahead, slow and steady, and began the climb home.
