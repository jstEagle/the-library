## 22. Every Sandbar's Mood

The low-water season came back around the way it always did, patient as a debt collector, and Freya Joubert met it standing at the wheel of the Magpie with new charts open on his knees.

They were strange things to hold. He'd spent ten years learning to distrust paper—every chart on this river was a lie with lines drawn on it, every sounding a rumor, every marked channel a suggestion made by men who had never wet a boot above the fuel depots. He'd navigated by memory and by mood instead. The mood of a sandbar, he used to tell Naledi, tells you more than any survey: how the water stacks against it in wind, how the birds use it, whether the reeds lean upstream or down. A river lies to instruments. It never lies to attention.

But these charts were different. These charts were true.

Okonkwo had drawn them himself over the winter, working from his translations of the treaty stone and from the testimony of elders in eleven settlements that had never appeared on an official map. Ilse had added the soundings her surviving team had taken before the end, corrected by Freya's own decade of running the river blind. The cartography office in the provincial capital—newly staffed, newly honest, its old director gone before a tribunal—had printed them on heavy stock with the source watershed marked in ink that would not fade: *Vasseur Headwaters. Inhabited since before record. Title held in perpetuity by treaty.*

Freya ran his thumb along that line the way another man might touch a scar.

"You're going to wear a hole in it," Naledi said from the engine well, where she was coaxing the port-side motor through its warm-up rattle. Her arm was healed now, mostly. She still favored it when the weather turned cold, and she still rolled her shoulder each morning like a woman testing a door she expected to be locked. "You've looked at that page forty times since dawn."

"Forty-one," Freya said. "I'm checking for errors."

"There are no errors. Okonkwo checked them three times and Ilse checked them twice, and you checked them yourself last night by lamplight like a man proofreading a love letter."

He grunted, which was as close as he came to admitting anything before noon. The Magpie slid out of the delta's brown braided water into the main channel, and the morning opened up ahead of them the way it always did—mist peeling off the current, herons standing their sentry posts on the snags, the far bank a green rumor not yet committed to being land.

It was good water. It was, he thought, the first time in ten years he'd looked at it without calculating who might be watching him look.

* * *

They stopped at Kessing's Landing by midmorning, because the new charts said there was a landing there, and because for the first time in the river's history the chart and the truth agreed.

Kessing's wasn't much—a shelf of hardpan above the flood line, a dozen houses on stilts, drying racks hung with fish that glittered like coinage. Ten years ago Freya would have run past it in the dark, lights doused, because Kessing's didn't exist officially and people who didn't exist officially had learned not to wave at boats that also preferred not to. Now the whole settlement came down to the bank to meet them. Children ran the hardpan shelf shouting the Magpie's name before the boat was even tied off, and old Mama Sefu, who had once sold Freya fuel at midnight and asked no questions, pressed a sack of smoked fish into his hands and refused payment.

"You're the one," one of the children said—not a question either. She couldn't have been more than eight. "You're the one who talked on the radio. My uncle says your voice went all the way to the sea."

"It went all the way to the sea," Freya confirmed.

"Did it go farther?"

"Farther than the sea," he said, and found that he meant it. The transmission had been repeated, relayed, copied, sung back; Okonkwo reported that fragments of the treaty reading had turned up in market songs two hundred miles downriver, set to drum patterns older than any colonial governor. Truth, it turned out, traveled the way water did. You could dam it for a while. You could make it silt up and forget its own course. But you could not, in the end, stop it finding the sea.

Naledi spent an hour at the landing showing the headman's daughter how to read the new chart—the symbols, the soundings, the little notation marks where the treaty stone stood. Freya watched her do it and felt something in his chest unclench by degrees. She'd been the company's ear on his boat once, coerced, terrified, sixteen years old and carrying a weight no one should carry. She'd also, in the end, been the one who pulled them all out of Verhoeven's base camp with a sabotaged detonator box and a bullet in her shoulder. Both things were true. He'd learned, that winter, that both things could stay true without canceling each other out. That was what forgiveness actually was—not forgetting one truth for the other, but holding them together and choosing which one to build on.

"Captain." Mama Sefu again, quieter now, at his elbow. "There's word moving upriver ahead of you. About the hearings."

"I know about the hearings."

"The titles are suspended. All of them. Every lease the company ever held above the falls." She said it carefully, watching his face, and Freya understood that she was waiting to see whether he'd flinch—that some part of the river still half-expected the news to be a trap, or temporary, or too good to survive the season. "Your brother's name came up in the tribunal. They read his letter aloud. Into the record."

Freya looked out at the channel, at the light lying on it like spilled brass.

"Good," he said. It came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "That's where it belonged. In the record."

* * *

Above the falls, the river changed character, and so did the trip.

The lower Vasseur was commerce—barges, fuel depots, the gray machinery of the company's long occupation, though the machinery ran quieter now, its masters gone or awaiting trial. But above the falls the river became what it had always been underneath: drowned forest and black water, channels that wandered like gossip, the escarpment rising in the middle distance with its caves hidden in the limestone like a secret kept in a closed fist.

They passed the wreck of the expedition launch on the second day. Someone had already been there—salvagers under charter from the tribunal, recovering evidence—but the hull still lay on its side in the shallows, shot full of holes, and Freya gave it a wide berth and a moment of silence without explaining why. Naledi didn't ask. Some things the river kept, and some things it let you keep.

On the third day they reached the gorge, and Freya did what he had done once before in terror and now did in something close to peace: he winched the Magpie through the narrows, pole by pole, line by line, while Naledi worked the winch and called the depths. The rock walls rose on either side, streaked white with bird lime, and somewhere high above an eagle turned in a column of warm air, indifferent to tribunals and treaties and the affairs of men.

At the top of the gorge, where the river widened again into the highland shallows, they camped on a gravel bar that the old charts had labeled *uninhabited marsh* and the new charts labeled nothing at all, because some places didn't need labels, only respect.

Freya sat up late by the fire. Naledi slept in the wheelhouse, her bad arm tucked against her chest. Upstream, maybe six hours' run, the river ended—in a spring-fed amphitheater beneath the escarpment's crown, where the treaty stone stood above the water and the whole Vasseur began as a whisper out of the rock.

In the footwell beside him sat a small wooden box, and inside the box, half of everything left of Tomas Joubert.

* * *

He hadn't planned the division. It had simply happened, the winter after the tribunal, when the ashes came back from the crematorium in a tin canister with a government seal on it—Tomas, who had died unnamed in a staged boating accident, finally returned to his family by bureaucratic paperwork, of all things. Freya had sat with the canister on his kitchen table for three nights, unable to open it, and then on the fourth night he'd opened it, and found himself dividing the contents without quite deciding to: half into the wooden box, half back into the tin.

Half for the source, where Tomas's honest survey had waited twenty years in a field kit for someone to find it. Half for the delta, where they'd grown up, where Tomas had learned to read water before he could read words, where he'd first dreamed—God help him—of maps. Of making maps. Of drawing the river truly, before the company taught him to draw it falsely, before the falseness ate him alive from the inside until the only exit it left him was a boat and a night and a lie told about his death.

Verhoeven had ordered it. The letter said so, in Tomas's own hand, found by lamplight in a cave while dynamite waited overhead. Verhoeven had ordered the falsification, and when Tomas balked, Verhoeven had ordered the accident, and then—this was the part Freya still turned over at 3 a.m.—Verhoeven had recruited Tomas's brother to run supplies to the expedition, wearing the dead man's photograph like a hook through the cheek. He'd known all along. He'd chosen Freya *because* of the grief, betting that grief made men obedient.

He'd lost that bet. The river had collected on it instead.

Freya fed the fire another stick and watched the sparks climb. Tomorrow he would scatter the source half. Tonight he just wanted to sit near it a while longer, the way you sit near a sleeping person you're about to wake.

* * *

The amphitheater was exactly as he remembered it, which surprised him. Places that matter tend to shrink on return, worn down by expectation. Not this one. The spring rose through pale sand in the rock basin, clear as glass and cold enough to ache, pooling and spilling and gathering itself into the first shy meters of the Vasseur. The escarpment curved around it like cupped hands. And above the waterline, ancient and lichened and patient, stood the treaty stone, its carved bands of script catching the morning light—hundreds of years of law written in rock by people who had understood, better than any company ever would, that a river belongs to those who live beside it.

Ilse had wanted to come. He'd seen it in her face when he told her his plan—wanted it the way she wanted everything connected to the caves, with that scholar's hunger that had gotten her team killed and nearly gotten them all killed. But he'd said no, gently, and she'd accepted, because she understood too: this wasn't an expedition. This was a family errand.

He waded in to his knees with the wooden box. The cold climbed his legs like a warning. At the base of the stone, where the spring water ran fastest over clean sand, he knelt and opened the lid.

"Tomas," he said. His voice sounded enormous in the amphitheater, bounced off the rock, came back to him smaller. "I brought you home by the honest route. Took me long enough."

He tipped the box. The ash went into the current in a gray ribbon, thinned, swirled once at the stone's base—and then the spring took it, the way the spring took everything, patiently, thoroughly, downstream. Toward Kessing's Landing. Toward the falls and the gorge and the drowned forest. Toward the delta, where the other half waited, so that between the two halves the whole river would carry a piece of him, top to bottom, source to sea.

Freya stayed kneeling until the ribbon of gray dissolved entirely and the water ran clean again. Then he stood, and his eyes were burning, and he let them, because there was no one watching and everyone watching, and both of those were fine now.

"You'd have liked the new charts," he told the stone, wiping his face with his sleeve. "You'd have argued with the soundings anyway. Stubborn bastard."

The eagle from the gorge, or one like it, crossed the amphitheater high overhead and vanished over the escarpment's rim.

* * *

The run back downriver took four days, and Freya spent them with the charts open on his knees, reading the river against the paper and the paper against the river, and finding, for the first time in his life, no daylight between them.

Word moved faster than the Magpie, as it always had. At every landing there were people waiting—not hiding now, not watching from the reeds, but standing openly on the banks, waving, calling his name. *Joubert.* His real name, the buried one, spoken in daylight by strangers and children and old women who'd sold him fuel at midnight a decade ago and never asked why he wouldn't give it. At Ferro Bend they asked him to inspect their new community dock and sign the ledger as witness. At the mouth of the Tchala they asked Naledi to look at a young man's injured hand, because word had traveled that she'd pulled bullets and splints on a rescue run and knew field medicine. At every stop, someone pressed food aboard, or fuel, or news, or simply a hand to shake.

Invisible no longer. He kept waiting for it to feel like exposure—for the old animal instinct to hunch his shoulders and douse his lights and slide past in the dark. It didn't come. What he'd never understood, all those years of careful silence, was that invisibility had never actually protected him. It had only made him alone. A man nobody knows can be disappeared without anyone asking after him—that was Tomas's whole story in a sentence. But a man the whole river knows by name and by reason? Disappearing him would take an army, and even an army would leave witnesses.

Visibility, it turned out, wasn't vulnerability. Visibility was armor, forged out of the one metal the company had never managed to buy: being known.

* * *

They reached the delta on the evening of the fourth day, with the sun going down behind them and the whole broad mouth of the Vasseur lit up copper and rose, the channels braiding apart toward the sea like roots of the same tree.

Freya anchored the Magpie off the old mudbank where he and Tomas had moored their father's skiff thirty-odd years ago—two boys, one leaking boat, a summer that smelled of diesel and mango. The mudbank was smaller now, eaten by decades of floods, but it was there, and the current off it ran the same way it had always run, seaward, tireless, keeping every promise the river ever made.

He took out the tin canister and carried it to the bow.

"Rest of you goes here," he said quietly. "Where we started. Fair's fair—you get the beginning and the end, and I get the middle stretch to run. That's a captain's share. Don't argue."

He scattered the ashes wide, into the last of the light, and the delta current gathered them in among the silt and the fish and the mangrove seeds bound for the sea, and Freya Joubert stood at the bow of the Magpie and watched his brother become the river entire.

Behind him, Naledi came up quietly and stood at his shoulder. She didn't say anything for a while. Then:

"Survey office wired ahead. They want an answer by Friday."

"I know."

"They're offering you command of the first official survey of the upper river. Your name on the commission. *Captain Joubert,* in print, in the record, forever." She paused. "You haven't said yes yet."

Freya watched the sun go down the rest of the way. The first stars came out over the escarpment, faint and far, and the river below them ran dark and certain toward the sea, carrying everything it carried—ashes and treaties and songs and the names of the dead, all of it downstream, all of it homeward.

"Naledi," he said, "get the running lights on. We'll answer them in the morning."

She grinned—he heard it more than saw it—and went aft, and a moment later the Magpie's lights bloomed on the dark water, bright and unhidden, visible from every bank, from every sandbar, from every settlement along a thousand miles of honest river, announcing to anyone who cared to look exactly where she was, exactly who she was, and exactly whose she was.

Freya stayed at the bow a long time, hands on the rail, charts stowed below, nothing left to hide behind and nothing left to hide.

Then he turned the wheel toward the anchorage, and the river, knowing his name, carried him home.
