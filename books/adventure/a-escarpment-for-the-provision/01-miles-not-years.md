## 1. Miles, Not Years

The trouble with measuring your life in miles, Rafael Okonkwo had decided somewhere around the two-thousandth of them, was that miles kept coming. They did not stop. They did not ask whether you wanted another one. A mile arrived, was walked or ridden or surveyed, and then—like a bad debt collector—it simply sent another.

She stood at the edge of a cut that had once been the pride of the Copperline Extension and was now a scar full of birch saplings and rabbit warrens, and she thought: *this is mile 2,041, and it looks exactly like mile 2,040.*

"Miss Okonkwo!" called Ferro, her client's foreman, from down the embankment. He was a round man with a voice like a landslide and the temperament of a man who had never once been surprised by anything, including landslides. "What do you say? Can she be saved?"

Rafael looked at the cut. The drainage had failed years ago; the ballast had slumped into the ditch; the rails were rusted to the color of dried blood. Saving it would cost more than building it new. She knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat.

"It can," she said.

Ferro beamed. "Knew you'd say so."

*It can,* she had said. Not *it should.* There was a difference, and she had been living in that difference for three years now, the way a man lives in a house with a cracked foundation—carefully, and without looking down too often.

She made her notes. Grade, drainage, ballast depth, the lean of the old retaining wall. Her hand moved with the easy competence of someone who had stopped having to think about the work a long time ago, which was precisely why she took it: work that required no thinking left all the thinking room for other things, and other things were what she was trying not to do.

Three years since Ashcombe Bridge went down in the river with eleven men on it. Three years since the inquiry, since the testimony, since Marguerite Voss had stood in that wood-paneled hearing room in her gray traveling suit and said, in a voice like cool water over stone, *"In my professional opinion, the span was under-designed for the seasonal load."*

Under-designed. Two words. Eleven men. One career, ended so cleanly it hadn't even bled.

And one brother, who had been on the bridge because Rafael had signed off on the schedule that put him there.

She closed her notebook. The birch saplings rustled. Somewhere a rabbit expressed an opinion about her presence and departed.

"Mile 2,041," she said quietly, to nobody, and walked back down to the wagon.

* * *

The hotel at Dunlow Junction had once been a fine establishment, according to the brass plaque by the door, which was the only part of it that remained fine. The lobby smelled of coal smoke and old cabbage. The clerk—a boy of perhaps sixteen with the mournful dignity of a much older man—gave her Room 4, which was the room over the kitchen, which meant it was warm, which in October was worth the smell of cabbage.

Rafael had a system for hotels. Unpack nothing but the notebook and the pencil case. Hang the coat where she could see it from the bed. Sleep facing the door. It was not a system born of fear; it was a system born of the knowledge that she might need to leave at any hour, and leaving was easier when everything you owned fit in one bag and lived by the door anyway.

She was eating supper in the dining room—mutton stew, honest if joyless, and bread that had opinions about being chewed—when the telegram came.

She knew before the boy reached her table. She always knew. Telegrams had a way of walking toward her like bad news wearing good boots.

"Miss Okonkwo?" The boy set the envelope down with ceremony, as though it might go off. "From the capital."

"Thank you, Tomás—" 

The name came out before she could stop it. The boy blinked. Rafael felt the floor tilt a half-degree, the way it sometimes did, and steadied herself against the table edge.

"Ernie," the boy said kindly. "But my mum nearly named me Tomás. After an uncle."

"Lucky escape," Rafael said, and gave him a coin, and waited until he'd gone before she opened the envelope.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange thing. Her hands had learned to be steady even when the rest of her was falling, which was either professionalism or damage, and she had long since stopped asking which.

**OKONKWO STOP KESTREL LINE REOPENING WINTER WINDOW NARROW STOP OFFER FIVE THOUSAND PLUS BONUS FOR COMPLETION BEFORE SNOWFALL STOP SURVEY AND SUPERINTEND FULL ROUTE STOP VOSS**

She read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times her eyes had slid off the last word like water off oiled canvas.

Voss.

Marguerite Voss. Voss & Associates, Consulting Engineers, whose letterhead Rafael had spent three years wanting to feed into a furnace. The woman whose testimony had ended her. The woman who had been right about the bridge—or wrong about it, depending on which version of the truth you believed, and Rafael had spent three years carefully believing neither, because both versions led to places she couldn't afford to go.

Five thousand. Five thousand dollars was four years of survey work. Five thousand was a house. Five thousand was—

Was nothing. Was bait. Was Marguerite Voss reaching across three years and a hundred ruined reputations to dangle money in front of the one engineer in the country with a personal reason to tell her to go to hell.

Rafael finished her stew. It had gone cold, but cold stew builds character, as her mother used to say, and her mother had built a great deal of character out of very little food.

She went up to Room 4, hung her coat where she could see it from the bed, and lay down facing the door, and did not sleep.

* * *

The thing about grief—the thing nobody tells you, or rather the thing everybody tells you and nobody believes until it happens—is that it doesn't live where you left it. You bury it in a gorge in the mountains, say your words over it, ride away on the morning train, and you think: *there. Done. Sealed under rock, like everything else in that valley.*

And then three years later a telegram says *VOSS*, one word, five letters, and the grief sits up out of its grave, brushes itself off, and says, *oh, are we talking about the gorge? I love the gorge. Let's talk about the gorge.*

Rafael lay in the dark listening to the kitchen clatter below and conducted, as she did most nights, the argument with herself that never ended.

*You swore off that line.*

I swore off the whole profession, for six months. Then I needed to eat. Surveying closed lines isn't the same as building them.

*You swore off the gorge specifically. You told Okoye. You told anyone who'd listen. "Not the Kestrel Line. Not that stretch." You said it like a vow.*

A vow is just a sentence with better posture.

*He died at Mile Forty-One.*

Stop.

*You signed the schedule.*

STOP.

The kitchen went quiet below. Somewhere down the hall a door closed. Rafael breathed the way the breathing exercises taught her—four counts in, seven held, eight out—and watched the rectangle of lamplight under the door, and after a while the argument subsided into the low mutter it maintained through most nights, like a river under ice.

Here was the truth she circled and never landed on: she didn't know what happened at Ashcombe Bridge. She knew what the inquiry said—that Rafael Okonkwo, resident engineer, approved a design inadequate to the spring load, and that her signature was on the drawings, and that signatures were the whole religion of engineering, the one commandment: *your name means the work is sound.* And she knew that when she'd gone back over her own figures afterward, a hundred times, drunk twice and sober the other ninety-eight, they had been correct. The design was sound. The bridge should have held.

Which left exactly two possibilities. Either she was a worse engineer than she believed—and she did not believe she was a bad engineer; false modesty was a luxury of people with careers—or something else had been true about that bridge that never made it into the record.

She had never found out which. Finding out would have meant staying still, staying near, asking questions of people who had every reason to lie to her. So instead she had done what she did best. She had built herself a life out of departures. Survey a dead line, file the report, collect the fee, move on before the town learned her name well enough to say it with the weight it carried. *Okonkwo? Any relation to the Ashcombe woman?* Every town had someone who asked. Every town got the same answer: she was gone by then, on the road, another mile down, the grief trotting patiently behind like a dog that knew it would be fed eventually.

Miles, not years. That was the arrangement. Years accumulated. Years sat in a chair across from you and asked hard questions. Miles asked nothing. Miles only passed.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of rain that wouldn't commit. Rafael ate breakfast in the dining room—eggs, toast, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in—and the telegram sat on the table beside her plate like a guest who refused to take the hint.

Five thousand dollars.

She did the arithmetic she'd been doing all night, the way other people worried a loose tooth. The Copperline job paid forty a week plus expenses. The season had maybe ten weeks left. Four hundred dollars, minus boots, minus the wagon repair, minus the tax the world levied on anyone foolish enough to have a reputation. Against that: five thousand, plus bonus, for one job. One winter. One line.

One gorge.

"You've been staring at that envelope for eleven minutes," said the woman at the next table. She was elderly, knitting something military-colored, and had the frank manner of a person who considered other people's business a public utility. "My late husband used to stare at telegrams like that. He was a gambler."

"I'm not a gambler."

"No," the woman agreed, snipping her yarn. "Gamblers look excited."

Rafael almost smiled. It surprised her, the almost-smile; those had gotten rare. "It's a job offer."

"From someone you hate."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You're holding your fork like a weapon." The woman wound her knitting with the serenity of a bishop. "Take the job or don't, dear, but decide today. Men and decisions—they spoil overnight, like fish."

"That's women too."

"Oh, certainly." She picked up her basket. "We just pretend otherwise. It's politer."

She left. Rafael sat with her cold tea and the telegram and the arithmetic, and beneath the arithmetic, quieter, the real question, the one she'd been circling for three years like a bird over open water:

If Voss wanted the Kestrel Line reopened—if Marguerite Voss, of all people, needed *her*, specifically, badly enough to offer five thousand dollars—then what did Voss know?

Because engineers didn't hire their enemies out of sentiment. Engineers hired their enemies when their enemies were the best, or when their enemies were convenient, or when their enemies were useful to something larger than the job. And Rafael Okonkwo was not the best anymore—not on paper, not after Ashcombe. Which left convenient, or useful.

Or guilty.

That word arrived without knocking, the way the worst ones did. *Guilty.* What would a guilty woman do, three years on, with her family's fortune behind her and her name clean and her testimony bought and paid for? She might, Rafael supposed, grow tired of sleeping well. She might start hiring the people she'd broken, the way rich men endowed hospitals after making their money in slaughterhouses.

Or she might want something in the mountains. Something at Mile Forty-One. Something buried.

Rafael pushed the plate away. Outside, the first spit of rain ticked against the window, and far to the north, past the rooftops of Dunlow Junction, the foothills stood up against the sky like the backs of sleeping animals, and beyond them, invisible but perfectly clear to her, the escarpment rose—the Kestrel Line's high pass, the Gorge of Provision, the place where her brother had gone into the river with a deed in his breast pocket and come out of the world entirely.

Tomas had carried that deed everywhere. Their father's deed, the provision lands, the paper that proved the family owned what the companies said they didn't. Tomas had laughed about it—*"they'll have to dig me up to get it, Rafa"*—and then he'd gone into the river, and the river kept him, and no one had ever dug.

No one had ever dug.

Rafael looked at the telegram for a long time. Then she folded it along its creases, precise as a drawing fold, and put it in her breast pocket, over the heart, where Tomas used to carry the deed.

"Ernie," she said to the clerk on her way through the lobby. "Is there a telegraph office in this town?"

"Yes'm. End of the street. Closes at six."

"And the northbound coach?"

"Leaves at seven tomorrow, if the ford's passable."

Rain ticked on the windows. Somewhere north, snow was already thinking about the high passes, weeks ahead of schedule, the way weather had been misbehaving all autumn, though she didn't know yet how badly.

She stood in the lobby of the Hotel Dunlow with her coat over her arm and her bag by the door, because it always was, and she felt the old machinery of departure begin to turn—wagon, road, mile 2,042—and for the first time in three years the machinery hesitated. Because this time the road pointed north. This time the road pointed at the gorge.

"Send a reply," she heard herself say. "To Marguerite Voss, the capital."

The boy licked his pencil, waiting.

Rafael looked out at the rain, and the hills, and the ghost of a bridge that should have held.

"Tell her," she said slowly, "the rate is accepted. Tell her I'll want terms in writing. And tell her—" She paused. The boy's pencil hovered. Somewhere in her chest, grief lifted its head and paid attention.

"Tell her I have questions," Rafael said. "And I expect answers at the end of the line."

The boy wrote it all down, asked how to spell *accepted*, and ran off through the rain toward the telegraph office, and Rafael went upstairs to pack properly for the first time in three years—everything, drawers emptied, the room surrendered—because whatever waited at the end of the Kestrel Line, she had a sudden, unshakable feeling she would not be coming back this way again.

By the time the coach pulled out at seven the next morning, the reply had already come back, one line, unsigned:

**TERMS IN WRITING YES STOP BRING YOUR CREW STOP ALL OF THEM WILL BE NEEDED STOP V**

Rafael read it twice as the wheels found the north road, and frowned.

*I don't have a crew,* she thought.

And then, watching the foothills rise ahead of her like a wall between her old life and whatever this was: *God help me. I'm going to need one.*
