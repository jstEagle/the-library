## 2. The Sworn-Off Job

The telegram arrived on a Tuesday, which Rafael would later decide was the first sign the universe had a sense of humor about her.

She was in the telegraph office of Corben's Junction, a town that consisted of a rail siding, a hotel with pretensions, a saloon without any, and a dog named Biscuit who had bitten three conductors and been promoted to local legend for it. Rafael had come in to send her own wire—declining a survey job outside Delano because the client had asked her to work through October, and October was when she moved south, always south, before the cold could settle into her bones and remind her of things.

The operator, a boy of perhaps sixteen with ink on his chin and the solemnity of a man who had seen empires fall via Morse code, handed her the flimsy yellow slip with both hands, like an offering.

RAFAEL OKONKWO STOP KESTREL LINE REOPENING CONTRACT OFFERED STOP FORTY DOLLARS A DAY PLUS BONUS ON COMPLETION STOP MARGUERITE VOSS.

Rafael read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings had produced nothing but a high thin ringing in her ears, like a bridge cable singing in wind.

Forty dollars a day. She made eleven surveying for county commissions, when they remembered to pay her.

Marguerite Voss.

She folded the telegram into quarters, then eighths, then into a small dense square she could have used as a chisel, and walked out into the street, where the dust hung in the air like something the town was reluctant to commit to.

Biscuit watched her from the shade of the water tower with the expression of a creature who knew exactly what she was going to do and disapproved.

"I'm not answering it," Rafael told him.

Biscuit yawned.

* * *

She didn't answer it for four days, which by her own accounting was three days longer than pride required and two days shorter than honesty did.

She kept busy. There was always busy to be kept. She re-inked her good pens. She recalculated a drainage grade for a farmer who'd paid her in eggs and gratitude, both of which she'd accepted because refusing either felt rude. She walked out along the dead spur line north of town until the rails gave up in a fan of rust, and stood there looking at the mountains to the west—the ones she did not look at, generally, the way a man with a bad tooth does not probe it with his tongue—and told herself, firmly, that forty dollars a day was not enough money to buy back her peace of mind, chiefly because her peace of mind was not for sale at any price, having been lost some years ago in a gorge whose name she refused to say even inside her own head.

Three years since Ashcombe Bridge came down. Three years since the inquiry board had sat in its paneled room with its pitcher of water and its stenographer, and Marguerite Voss—then merely Voss, consulting engineer, called as an expert witness—had testified, in a voice like cool water over stone, that the load calculations bore the signature of Rafael Okonkwo and that the figures were wrong.

They hadn't been wrong. Rafael had spent three years knowing they hadn't been wrong, in the way you know your own heartbeat: not as information but as fact, structural, load-bearing. Someone had altered the figures between her desk and the boardroom. She had never proven it. Proof required staying in one place long enough to dig, and Rafael Okonkwo had made a profession of not staying anywhere.

Miles, not years. That was the arrangement. You kept moving so grief couldn't catch you, the way a rabbit keeps moving so the hawk can't fix its position. It worked. Mostly. The trouble with the arrangement was that it also meant joy couldn't catch you either, or rest, or the particular contentment of a garden, or a second cup of coffee drunk slowly on a porch you owned. But those were acceptable losses. She'd signed off on them herself, in effect, the way you initial each page of a contract you're afraid to read all the way through.

On the fourth day she wrote her reply. She was rather proud of it. It ran:

VOSS STOP DECLINED STOP REGARDLESS OF TERMS STOP REGARDLESS OF SUM STOP DO NOT WIRE AGAIN STOP OKONKWO

She paid the boy his two bits and walked out feeling lighter than she had in months. Declining things, it turned out, was excellent exercise. She resolved to do more of it.

That evening, the woman herself stepped off the 4:15 from the coast.

* * *

Rafael was in the hotel dining room, which was generous terminology for a room containing nine tables, a piano nobody played, and a moose head that had been there so long its glass eyes had gone cloudy with resignation. She was eating a steak that had been cooked with great sincerity and no talent whatsoever, when the doors opened and the whole room changed weather.

Marguerite Voss did not enter rooms; she revised them. She was tall in the way that made other tall people feel they'd been exaggerating, dressed in traveling gray that probably cost more than the hotel, and she crossed the floor with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once had to ask where she was going. Half the diners pretended not to look at her. The other half didn't bother pretending.

She stopped at Rafael's table.

"Okonkwo." A nod, precise as a plumb line. "You look well."

"You look expensive," Rafael said, because her mouth had always operated slightly ahead of her judgment, like a locomotive whose brakes were more of a suggestion.

Something flickered at the corner of Voss's mouth—not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "May I?"

"There's a chair."

Voss sat, arranged herself, declined the waiter with a small motion, and let a silence develop, which she then examined from several angles, apparently finding it satisfactory. Rafael cut another bite of steak and chewed it with the deliberate calm of a woman who was not rattled, an impersonation she had been perfecting for three years and could now perform under artillery fire.

"You didn't answer my wire," Voss said.

"I answered it this morning. Check with your office."

"I read it." Voss set her gloved hands flat on the tablecloth. "I found it terse."

"I've been working on terse. It doesn't pay as well as engineering, but the hours are better."

"Forty dollars a day," Voss said, "is not a sum you decline."

"It is if you know me."

"I knew you once." And there it was—a hairline crack in the porcelain, quickly glazed over. "You were the best route engineer on the western division. Better than me, though I'd have shot anyone who said so at the time."

"You said plenty at the time."

"What I said at the time," Voss said quietly, "was what the record shows I said. Nothing more."

"The record," Rafael said, "was written by whoever held the pen."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Across the room, the piano sat in its corner radiating unused potential, and the moose head gazed down at them both with the weary tolerance of a creature that had witnessed a hundred tense dinners and outlived every participant.

"Why are you here, Voss?"

"Because you wouldn't come to me." Voss drew an envelope from her coat—good paper, heavy stock, the kind of envelope that arrived with consequences attached—and laid it on the table between them without opening it. "And because I suspected you'd refuse sight unseen, and I wanted to see your face when you saw what the job actually is."

"I know what the job is. The Kestrel Line. Reopen it before the snows."

"That is the job," Voss agreed. "It is not the whole of it." She turned the envelope over. "The contract runs from the valley terminus to the summit cutting. Forty-one miles of mountain rail, most of it dead these twelve years. Trestles down, tunnels collapsed, grades washed out. Every engineer on the coast told me it couldn't be done in one season. I intend to prove them wrong, and I intend to do it with the only engineer alive who has ever built track through that range faster than the mountain could argue."

Flattery, Rafael noted, delivered with the smoothness of ball bearings. She waited. With Voss there was always a *wait*, a hinge point, and if you rushed it you got the sales pitch instead of the truth.

"The route crosses the Gorge of Provision," Voss said.

The words went through Rafael like a spike driven home in one blow—clean entry, no warning, everything after it ringing.

She put down her fork. Very carefully. It seemed important, suddenly, not to make noise.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"There are no terms." Her voice came out level, which cost her something. "That stretch of line is closed. Not washed out. Closed. By me. By oath, if oaths count for anything, which I grant the last few years suggest they don't."

"Okonkwo—"

"My brother died there." The words came out before she could stop them, and once out, they seemed to fill the room, so that the waiters at the far tables went quiet and even the piano seemed to lean in. Rafael lowered her voice. "Tomas died in that gorge, Voss. He was twenty-six years old. He was carrying the transit case up the east wall when the bluff gave way, and we never recovered—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "We buried what we found. Which wasn't him. Not really."

"I know," Voss said.

"You don't know anything."

"I know he was buried in the slide," Voss said, and her voice had changed—lower, stripped of its drawing-room finish, "because I have spent two years reading every report, every claim filing, every scrap of paper connected to that gorge. Including the land records." She slid the envelope across the tablecloth, an inch, and stopped. "Your family held provision lands in that valley. Four hundred acres, granted under the old act. Your father's name is on the original deed."

Rafael stared at her. "My father sold those lands. Before Tomas was born. He—"

"Your father never sold anything," Voss said. "The lands were seized. Quietly, legally, with paperwork so clean it shines. And the original deed—the one that proves the seizure was fraud, the one your father hid before they could take it from him—was never found. Because your father sent it up that gorge. To Tomas. The week before the slide."

The dining room seemed very far away suddenly, as though Rafael were hearing it through water, through rock, through twelve years of mountain silence. She thought of her father, near the end, hands shaking too badly to hold a pen, saying things that hadn't made sense—*it's with Tommy, it's safe with Tommy, don't let them dig*—and how the family had filed it away as the wanderings of a dying man, the way families do, because the alternative was unbearable.

"How do you know this?" she asked. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

"Because my family financed the original Kestrel Line," Voss said. "And when the provision lands were seized, my family bought them—at auction, at a fraction of their worth, from the men who engineered the seizure. We profited from it, Okonkwo. My father built half his fortune on your family's stolen ground." She said it flatly, without apology and without flinching, which was somehow worse than either. "I am not here to offer you charity. I am here because I want those lands returned, and the only proof that will stand in any court in this country is sitting in a rockslide at mile forty-one, and the only person alive who might be able to reach it is the one engineer stubborn enough, and skilled enough, and angry enough to build a railroad through hell in a single season."

"And you expect me to believe," Rafael said slowly, "that Marguerite Voss wants to give land back to a bunch of farmers her family cheated."

"I expect you to believe I want my name clean," Voss said. "Yours isn't the only reputation buried in that gorge."

Outside, the light was going amber and long, and somewhere down the street Biscuit was barking at the 4:15 as it departed, on principle, for having dared to arrive earlier. Rafael looked at the envelope on the table. Heavy paper. Consequences.

"If I say no again?"

"Then I hire the second-best engineer on the western division," Voss said, rising, gathering her coat, "and he will take forty men into that gorge, and he will find what's there, or he will die trying, and either way it will be done without you." She paused. "I would rather it was done with you. Not only because you're better than he is—though you are—but because whatever is buried at mile forty-one belongs to you more than it belongs to anyone. Including me."

She set a card beside the envelope. "The contract terms are inside. Read them or burn them. But answer by Friday, because the season won't wait, and neither will the snow."

She left the way she'd entered, revising the room on her way out, and Rafael sat alone with her ruined steak and her father's ghost and the word *forty-one* turning over in her chest like a stone in a tumbler.

Mile forty-one. The marker nearest where the east wall came down. Where she had stood, three years older than Tomas and helpless as a child, watching the dust rise off the slide and knowing, with the terrible clarity of the powerless, that the world had just gotten smaller and colder and would never warm again.

She picked up the envelope. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed a cathedral.

"Well," she said aloud, to the moose head, because someone ought to hear it. "Damn."

The moose head offered no opinion. Outside, the mountains stood in the last of the light, patient as creditors, and Rafael Okonkwo sat in a dusty junction hotel holding the map to every road she had sworn never to travel again—and understood, with the sinking certainty of a woman watching her own footprints lead somewhere she'd promised never to go, that she was going to say yes.

The only question left was whether she'd be honest enough, with Voss or with herself, about why.
