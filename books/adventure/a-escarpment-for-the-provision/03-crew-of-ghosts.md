## 3. Crew of Ghosts

The trouble with needing a crew was that Rafael had spent three years making sure nobody needed her.

She'd been good at it. A nod in a rail yard, a coin slid across a bar for a meal she ate standing up, a bed in a boarding house paid for in cash and left before dawn. She knew the names of maybe four people between the coast and the mountains, and two of them were telegraph operators who only knew her as *the woman who pays on Fridays*. Grief made an excellent foreman. It kept the schedule tight, the conversation short, the doors closed.

Now she stood in the doorway of the Kestrel Junction freight office with a pencil stub and a sheet of butcher paper, trying to write down the kind of people she'd need to reopen a dead railroad through a living mountain, and discovered she couldn't remember how to ask anyone for anything.

*One blaster,* she wrote. *One surveyor. One cook.* Then, because the paper looked too much like a plan and not enough like a prayer, she added: *People who don't ask questions.*

She looked at that last line for a long time. Then she crossed it out.

No. That was Voss's way of hiring — buy silence by the pound. If Rafael was going to walk back into the gorge where Tomas died, she wasn't doing it with hired mutes. She was doing it with people who had their own reasons to be there, even if those reasons were ugly ones. Especially if they were ugly ones. Ugly reasons held better than money. Money ran out at the first snow squall. Shame and hope could carry a man all the way to the summit.

She folded the paper into her coat and went looking for ghosts.

* * *

Okoye found her before she found him, which was how it always went with Okoye, and which was, she suspected, why he'd survived forty years of work that killed men in their twenties.

She heard him before she saw him — a low humming from behind the Junction's powder magazine, where the town stored its blasting supplies in a shed that leaned like a drunk leaning on a friend. He sat on an overturned crate in the shade, a small brown man with hands like weathered rope and a face so lined it looked cartographic, as though you could trace his whole history with one finger. He was cleaning a fuse crimper with a rag that had once been white in some previous geological era.

"Rafael Okonkwo," he said without looking up. "Heard your boots from thirty feet. You still walk like you're mad at the ground."

"You still sit next to explosives like they're old friends."

"They are old friends. Old friends are the dangerous ones." He set down the crimper and finally looked at her, and his eyes did the thing they always did — went soft and sharp at the same time, like a man reading a bridge inspection report about someone he loved. "Three years. I sent word after Ashcombe. Twice."

"I know. I didn't answer."

"No," he said mildly. "You didn't."

Rafael sat down on the crate opposite him, because her legs decided the matter without consulting her pride. The magazine's shadow was cool and smelled of sulfur and creosote, a smell that hit her somewhere below thought and opened a door she'd nailed shut. Forty-one miles of grade. Trestle Six. Tomas laughing about the coffee.

"I've got a job," she said.

"I heard that too. Junction's smaller than its gossip, but not by much. Voss's woman came through Tuesday asking after me." His mouth twitched. "I told her I was retired."

"Are you?"

"I am seventy-one years old, my knees sound like gravel underfoot, and the last crew I worked with is buried under a slide outside Copper Bend because the mine owner wanted one more shift out of an unstable face." He picked the crimper back up and turned it over in his fingers. "So yes. Retired. Thoroughly. What's the job?"

"The Kestrel Line. Full reopening, summit to valley, before the snows close the pass."

Okoye hummed three notes, low, the way he did when he was doing arithmetic no one else could see. "That's a winter job being sold as a summer job. Who's selling?"

"Voss."

"Mm." He cleaned a spot on the crimper that didn't need cleaning. "And you said yes to Marguerite Voss. The woman whose testimony ended your career. The woman you once told me — and I quote, because I quote everything, it's how I stay young — 'I would rather push rail cars uphill with my teeth.'"

"People change."

"People change their minds," Okoye corrected. "It's different. It happens faster and means less." He looked at her again, longer this time. "You're not doing it for the money."

"I'm doing it partly for the money."

"You're doing it for the gorge." He said it gently, the way he said everything that hurt. "Mile forty-one."

Rafael's jaw tightened until it ached. "There's something buried out there."

"There's a lot buried out there. That's rather the nature of gorges."

"Tomas," she said, because dancing around it with Okoye was like dancing around a lit fuse. "Tomas is buried out there. And something else besides. And I need a blaster who can put holes exactly where I say and nowhere else, and who won't flinch when I tell him we're working ground that's already taken its tithe from my family."

Okoye was quiet for a while. Somewhere across the yard a locomotive coughed itself awake, and the shed's lean seemed to shiver.

"My wife," he said at last, "is buried under a fig tree in a village I can't reach anymore, because the border moved and I didn't. My sons think I'm dead, or worse, comfortable. I have been invisible for eleven years, Rafael, and invisibility is a fine coat but a poor dinner." He stood, slowly, joints reporting in one by one like a crew checking in. "I'll come. But I want it understood: I'm not coming for Voss, and I'm not coming for your brother, God rest him. I'm coming because a mountain pass is about to be blasted open by people in a hurry, and somebody responsible ought to be standing there saying *no* at the correct moments."

"That's the job description exactly."

"And," he added, gathering his tools into a canvas roll with the economy of a man who owned nothing he couldn't carry, "you'll let me retire properly afterward. No more jobs. I intend to find a fig tree of my own."

"Deal."

He shook her hand, and his grip was still strong enough to make her believe, briefly, that mountains could be argued with.

* * *

Nell Quist was harder to find and easier to convince, which Rafael would learn was the central paradox of Nell Quist.

She located her in the Junction hotel dining room, of all places — not eating, but working. A girl of perhaps nineteen sat at the corner table with a plate of cold biscuits pushed aside and a surveyor's transit disassembled before her on a linen cloth, cleaning each brass piece with a toothbrush and reassembling it with the tenderness other girls reserved for love letters. Her hair was cut short and practical, her sleeves were rolled, and there was graphite on her chin, her knuckles, and improbably, her ear.

"You're leveling that instrument on a table that isn't level," Rafael said.

Nell didn't look up. "The table's off by a quarter degree to the east. I compensate." A pause. The toothbrush stopped. "You're Okonkwo."

"You know me?"

"I know your Ashcombe report. Appendix C. The load calculations on the third pier." Now she looked up, and her eyes were quick and hungry and a little frightened, the eyes of someone who had taught herself everything and trusted none of her teachers. "Everyone says you fudged them. I ran the numbers myself twice. They're right. They were always right. Whatever collapsed that bridge, it wasn't arithmetic."

Rafael stood very still. Three years of hearing *negligent, careless, unfit*, and here was a stranger with biscuit crumbs on her collar handing her the one thing no court ever had: a second opinion.

"Thank you," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"I'm not saying it to be kind." Nell went back to the transit, ears going pink. "I'm saying it because it's true and nobody else will say it, and I've got a theory that truth gets lonely if nobody feeds it." She snapped the last piece home and looked up. "Why are you talking to me? Nobody talks to me. I've been thrown out of two survey crews for correcting the chief's chainage in front of clients."

"Would you do that to me?"

"Obviously. But I'd do it privately first, and give you the chance to pretend it was your idea."

Despite everything — the gorge waiting, the telegram burning a hole in her pocket, the season already turning against them — Rafael felt something in her chest loosen half a turn. "I'm hiring a surveyor for the Kestrel Line. Reopening the whole route. Hard country, wrong season, pay is good and the odds are bad."

Nell's hands went still on the brass. "The Kestrel Line goes through Provision Gorge."

"It does."

"My father worked that line. Original construction, twenty-two years ago. He came home the summer it finished and was never right again — used to stand at the window at night watching the hills like they owed him something. He died when I was twelve. Left me his transit and a box of field notes I can't fully read." She swallowed. "I've wanted to see that country since I was tall enough to want things."

"There's something else you should know," Rafael said. "I'm not just running track. There's — personal business of mine along that route. Things I need to look at. When we get to mile forty-one, there may be days when the line waits and other work doesn't."

Most people would have asked what other work. Rafael watched Nell decide not to, watched her weigh it and pocket it, the way she'd apparently learned to pocket every question that might get her thrown out of another room.

"Wrong season," Nell said instead. "Bad odds. A chief with secrets." She stuck out a graphite-stained hand. "When do we start?"

* * *

Baptiste required no finding whatsoever. Baptiste was simply *there*, the way weather is there, and had possibly been there since before Rafael arrived in the Junction, and would likely be there after everyone else had gone.

He materialized at her elbow on the third evening, in the boarding house kitchen, where Rafael was drinking coffee that tasted like boiled regret and reviewing grades by lamplight. He was an enormous man, six foot four at least, built along the lines of a freight car, with a gray-shot beard and an air of profound unhurried calm. He wore an apron over clothes that had been mended so many times the original fabric existed mainly as a historical concept.

"You eat like a man punishing himself," he rumbled, setting down a bowl of stew she had not ordered and had not seen him make. "This is a sin against the cow."

"I didn't—"

"Nobody orders. I cook, people eat. This is the arrangement of the universe." He folded his arms, which were roughly the diameter of her waist, and studied her with the mild, bottomless patience of a lake. "You are the engineer. The one taking people up the Kestrel."

Word traveled fast in the Junction. Word probably traveled faster than the trains ever had.

"I'm hiring a cook," she admitted. "For a high-country camp. Cold nights, long weeks, beans beyond counting."

"I make excellent beans."

"So I've heard."

"From whom? I have never cooked for you."

"From the entire town, apparently. You're famous."

Something moved behind his eyes then — a flicker, quickly banked, like a man hearing his name called from a house he'd promised himself he'd never enter again. "Fame is a heavy thing to carry up a mountain," he said quietly. "But yes. I'll come."

Rafael set down her spoon. "I haven't offered terms."

"You will. They'll be fair. You strike me as a woman who pays on Fridays." He began stacking dishes that hadn't been dirtied yet, tidying a kitchen that wasn't his with the ease of long habit. "I'll tell you my term. One term only." He paused, and for the first time the calm surface showed depth beneath it, dark and cold. "Whatever happens up there — whatever you find, whatever you dig up, whatever old bones or old papers come out of that rock — you let me help carry them. Don't leave me behind at camp to mind the beans when the real work starts. I've minded the beans through enough of other people's histories. I'd like to be present for one."

Rafael searched his face. There was something there, some weight he carried the way she carried hers — slung low, kept quiet, walked with. A drifter cook who wanted to be present for history. Everyone she was hiring had a reason to be invisible, and every one of them, given half a chance, wanted desperately to be seen.

"All right," she said. "You carry with us."

He nodded once, satisfied, and ladled more stew into her bowl without asking, and she ate all of it, and it was, in fact, an excellent stew.

* * *

They signed the contracts on Friday morning in the freight office, around a desk scarred by fifty years of cargo hooks and coffee rings. Rafael had bought the paper, the ink, and — after some internal debate — a bottle of decent whiskey, because some occasions deserved ceremony even when the occasion was four strangers agreeing to risk their lives for a woman they didn't trust on a mountain that had already killed once.

Okoye read every clause twice, including the ones about hazard pay, and initialed the margins with comments. Nell read it once at speed, caught a typo in the mileage figures, and corrected it in pencil before signing. Baptiste signed with an X, then wrote his full name beneath it in careful, beautiful script — *Étienne Baptiste Oduya* — and Rafael realized she was the only one at the table who'd known him by half a name.

"Four of us," Okoye said, capping the inkwell. "Against a mountain, a winter, and whatever Voss isn't telling us."

"You know about that?" Nell said.

"A contract this generous is either charity or concealment," Okoye said. "Voss has never done charity."

"She saved my career's corpse from total burial," Rafael said. "That's worth something."

"It's worth exactly what it cost her," Okoye replied placidly, "which we will discover in due course."

Baptiste poured the whiskey into four tin cups and raised his. "To the season being wrong," he said solemnly.

"To the season being wrong," they echoed, and drank.

Outside, the light had begun to change — that thinning, sharpening quality the foothills got in late summer, when the air started practicing for winter. Rafael stood in the office doorway with her empty cup and looked north, toward the blue wall of the escarpment, toward the notch of the pass, toward mile forty-one, where her brother lay under stone with whatever else the mountain was keeping for her.

Behind her, Nell was already oiling the transit. Okoye was humming to his fuse crimper. Baptiste was asking the freight clerk, gently but irresistibly, whether the town had any butter worth the name.

A crew of ghosts, Rafael thought. People the world had misplaced, carrying their reasons like contraband, following her up a dead line in the wrong season because none of them could bear to stay invisible one more year.

It was, she reflected, the strangest family she'd ever assembled. And it was missing only one member — the tall, silver-tongued woman whose money had bought them all, and whose reasons Rafael intended to drag into the light before they reached the summit.

As if summoned by the thought, a black carriage came up the Junction road through the afternoon dust, moving fast, drawn by two horses that had clearly never hauled anything heavier than their own opinions.

Rafael set down her cup.

"Company," she said.
