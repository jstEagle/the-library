## 4. Voss's Terms

Marguerite Voss had learned long ago that the best lies were mostly true, and the best confessions were rehearsed in front of a mirror until they bled on cue.

She stood at the window of her private car—the *Corvina*, all brass fittings and walnut paneling, her father's last extravagance before the creditors came for everything else—and watched the junction hotel across the yard. Third floor, corner room, the one she'd paid the clerk to reserve. A lamp burned behind the glass. Rafael Okonkwo was up there, and Marguerite would have wagered the last unencumbered acre of the Voss name that the woman hadn't slept. She'd know that lamp's stubbornness anywhere. It was the same light that used to burn in the engineering tent at Ashcombe, three tents down from her own, burning past midnight while the rest of the crews drank and diced and slept.

Rivalry, in Marguerite's experience, was just intimacy with the tenderness stripped out.

She turned back to the table and looked at the papers spread there for the fourth time that evening, the way a card player looks at a hand she's already decided how to play. The contract, fair on its face—generous, even. The survey maps. The Kestrel Line specifications, annotated in her own hand. And beneath them, in the locked drawer of her writing desk, the one document that was not in the stack.

The court order. *In re: Provision Lands, District of Harrow's Bend.* Her father's signature on the petition. A judge's seal she'd spent two years learning to be ashamed of. And in the body of the order, in the flat, merciless language of the law, the name that had ended everything: *Elias Okonkwo, having been found to have committed fraud in the matter of the original land grants...*

Elias Okonkwo. Rafael's father. The man her own father had destroyed with a document Marguerite now kept like a stone in her pocket.

She poured herself two fingers of brandy, considered it, and poured it back in the bottle. She wanted a clear head. What she was about to do required either a clear head or a very clouded one, and she'd tried the clouded version once already, in a lawyer's office in the capital, and had woken up the next morning with her resolve intact and her dignity in pieces.

*Tell her the truth,* her mother had said, in the last lucid letter before the fever took her. *All of it, Marguerite. A debt hidden is a debt doubled.*

Her mother had never met Rafael Okonkwo. Her mother had not watched that woman testify—or rather, refuse to testify, refuse to explain, stand in that inquiry hall with her jaw set like poured concrete while the commissioners wrote her career into the record. Her mother had not spent three years reading the trade journals just to see what ruin looked like in print.

*Mostly true,* Marguerite reminded herself, and picked up the contract.

* * *

The hotel was the kind of place that smelled of lamp oil, old tobacco, and the particular despair of food kept warm too long. The clerk—bald, incurious, exactly as advertised—gave her the room number without being asked twice. Money did that. Money did most things, which was a lesson the Voss family had learned from both sides of the ledger.

She climbed the stairs slowly. Not from nerves—Marguerite Voss did not permit herself nerves, she'd filed them years ago under *luxuries*—but because she wanted to hear the sounds of the building. A floorboard's complaint. Rain starting against the windows, the first spit of the season's early storms. And above it all, from behind the corner room's door, the small, ceaseless sounds of a person who could not sit still: a chair pushed back, papers shuffled, the creak of someone pacing a room too small for it.

Rafael Okonkwo, measuring a hotel room in footsteps. Some things survived ruin.

She knocked.

The pacing stopped. A long silence, the kind that contained an entire argument conducted at speed. Then: "It's open."

The room was spare and fiercely tidy, which surprised her until she thought about it. Of course. A woman who kept moving would learn to keep nothing that couldn't be packed in ninety seconds. A single valise stood buckled by the door. The bed was made with military corners. On the table, the telegram she'd sent lay flattened under an ashtray, as though Rafael had been trying to press the words out of it—or press them into something she could believe.

Rafael herself stood by the window, and Marguerite felt the old, unwelcome jolt of it—the sheer *physicality* of her. Three years had put gray in the black of her braid and carved the lines around her mouth deeper, but she was still built like something meant to bear load. She had the kind of stillness that made other people fidget.

"Voss." Not a greeting. An identification, like a surveyor naming a hazard. "You came a long way to watch me say no in person."

"I came a long way because a telegram can't hold what I have to say." Marguerite set the contract on the table, squared its corners to the edge with a precision she knew would irritate. "And because you'd have said no by telegram. This requires an audience."

"You assume I'll listen."

"I assume you'll look." She opened the contract to the second page and turned it toward her. "The terms. Read them properly this time, not the way you read the telegram—hunting for the trick."

Rafael didn't move. "There's always a trick."

"Yes," Marguerite agreed pleasantly. "There is. But it's not where you think it is, and if you keep looking in the wrong place you'll miss the one that matters. Sit down, Okonkwo. I'm going to tell you a story, and I've never told it to anyone, and I would prefer not to tell it twice."

Something shifted in Rafael's face—not softening, nothing so generous, but the recalibration of a woman who had spent her life reading terrain. She sat. She did not touch the contract.

Marguerite remained standing, because she'd learned that this story went better on her feet, like a lecture, like a defense.

"My grandfather built the first thirty miles of the Kestrel Line," she began. "Before your father ever surveyed a foot of the provision lands. The Voss money was in the mountain before the Voss name was in the papers. He called it his life's work. He died believing the summit tunnel would open the range and make the family immortal." She smiled, and let it be a thin one. "My father inherited the dream and none of the patience. He leveraged everything to finish it—the line, the lands, the works. And then the provision lands were seized. Do you know how that works, from the inside? One day the family holds forty thousand acres in trust for the railroad. The next day there's a court order, a finding of fraud in the original grants, and the lands pass to the Crown's commissioners, who sell them within the year to—coincidentally—three companies, all of whom had lawyers at the hearing. The financing collapses. The line dies at the gorge. The Vosses go from immortal to insolvent in eleven months."

Rafael's eyes hadn't left her. "I know how seizure works," she said quietly. "My family lived on the other end of it."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "You did. And that, Okonkwo, is why I'm here, telling you this in a hotel that smells like boiled mutton, instead of hiring some broken-down surveyor by mail." She let the silence sit. "I believe the finding of fraud was false. I believe my father manufactured it—or had it manufactured—to escape his own debts, and that the men who bought the lands paid him for the favor. I believe the proof of that is still in the gorge, because my father's surveyor—the one who hid the original grant documents, the true ones—died there before he could move them. Tomas Okonkwo."

The name landed in the room like a dropped tool. Rafael had gone very still, in the way that Marguerite knew—had always known—was not calm at all. It was load-bearing stillness. A structure holding.

"You expect me to believe," Rafael said at last, "that you want to reopen the line out of—what. Family honor?"

"I want to reopen it because the line is the only legal instrument that puts us in the gorge," Marguerite said. "A survey crew can be turned away. A court can be bought—believe me, I've checked. But a railroad contract with Crown authorization is a hammer no land company can stand against. The line gets us there. The documents get us justice. The justice—" and here she made herself say it plainly, because the plainness was the price of being believed, "—the justice restores the provision lands to the families that lost them. Yours among them. Mine among them, though mine I'd sell for the satisfaction."

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. Rain traced down the window behind her, and the lamplight caught the gray in her braid, and Marguerite watched her do the arithmetic that engineers do—not of numbers but of forces. Load, counterload. What holds, what fails.

"Why me?" Rafael said finally. "There are a dozen surveyors who'd take this contract. Better ones, maybe. Ones without—" she gestured at herself, at the whole ruined topography of her last three years, "—history."

"Because no one else alive knows that gorge like you do. Because your name on the survey carries weight the commissioners can't ignore. And because—" Marguerite paused, and chose the true thing, because mostly true was the only kind of true she trusted herself to deliver. "Because I watched you work at Ashcombe. You were the best engineer on that river, and everyone knew it, and when the bridge fell, I knew it hadn't been your fault. I said so. In a deposition. It's in the record, page forty-one, if you ever cared to look."

"I looked," Rafael said. "Page forty-one says the witness Voss 'cannot rule out design deficiencies attributable to the engineer of record.' That's not saying so. That's hanging me with a silk ribbon."

"It was the most I could say and keep my seat on the commission," Marguerite said, and the shame of it was old enough now that it came out level. "I was twenty-six years old and my family was drowning and I chose my seat over your neck. I've had three years to decide what that cost. I'm here to pay it."

She watched Rafael absorb that. Watched her want to believe it—saw the wanting, brief and naked, before the armor came back up.

"And the money," Rafael said. "The figure in the telegram. That's real?"

"Half on signing. Half on reaching the summit cutting. Paid from what's left of the Voss estate, which is to say, paid from the sale of my mother's jewels, my father's library, and my own pride. I am not a rich woman hiring a poor one, Okonkwo. I'm a broke one hiring a brilliant one, and we are both gambling on the same mountain."

For the first time, something that was almost a smile moved across Rafael's face and was gone. "You rehearsed that."

"Every word," Marguerite said. "I rehearse everything. It's why I sound honest."

And Rafael laughed—a short, rough, astonished sound, as though it had escaped custody—and Marguerite felt something in her chest ease that she had not known was clenched. There it was. Three years of journals and depositions and cold print, and the woman behind all of it was just a tired, sharp-eyed engineer in a rented room, laughing once despite herself.

Rafael reached out and pulled the contract toward her at last. Read it. All of it, every clause, the way Marguerite had known she would, the way she herself would have. The rain thickened against the glass. Somewhere below, a train sighed into the yard and went quiet.

"The season's wrong," Rafael said, not looking up. "The monsoon's early. The high pass could close a month ahead of the almanac."

"It could. That's why the pay is what it is."

"And if I find what you say is buried there—and I'm not saying I believe you—and it's not what you say it is?"

"Then you'll have been paid to survey a dead line through beautiful country," Marguerite said, "and I'll have been wrong, which will not be the first time."

Rafael set the contract down. Looked at her for a long, long moment, and Marguerite met it, and did not blink, and hated herself with a quiet, professional thoroughness, because she was very good at not blinking and it had never once made a lie true.

"I'll need my own crew," Rafael said. "Chosen by me. And I want it in writing that no one touches the gorge wall without my say. No blasting, no cutting, nothing. That stretch of line is mine. You understand what I'm telling you."

"I understand completely," Marguerite said, and signed the rider on the spot, in ink, because she had brought a pen for exactly that moment, because she rehearsed everything.

* * *

The *Corvina* swayed gently on its siding that night, and Marguerite sat at her writing desk with the brandy she'd finally allowed herself, and did not drink it.

In the drawer before her lay the court order. She took it out. Unfolded it. Read the name again—*Elias Okonkwo, having been found to have committed fraud*—and felt the old vertigo, the one that came every time she held the document and understood what she was holding. Not just a lie. A *loaded* lie. Because if the fraud finding was false, then the man named in it had been destroyed for nothing—and his daughter had spent three years carrying the weight of a collapse that was never hers, on a name that was already ruined.

And Rafael did not know the order existed.

That was the arithmetic Marguerite kept failing to make come out clean. She had told the truth about the seizure, the forged grants, the buried documents. She had told the truth about her family's ruin, her shame, her page forty-one. All of it true, and all of it pointing the same direction: toward the gorge, toward the cache, toward justice. Rafael would walk into that gorge believing she was digging up her brother and her family's honor both.

She would not know that the first thing the courts would hand her, once the truth surfaced, was a piece of paper naming her dead father a fraud. That before the Okonkwo name could be cleared, it would have to be dragged through the mud one more time—publicly, legally, in the record, forever. That the truth was not one gift but two, and the second one was a blade.

*Tell her,* said her mother's voice, in the letter, in the fever, in the drawer.

*Not yet,* said the other voice, the one that sounded like her father, smooth and reasonable, the voice that had manufactured a fraud finding and called it necessity. *Not until she's committed. Not until she's too deep to turn back. She'll walk away if you tell her now. Anyone would.*

Marguerite put the court order back in the drawer. Locked it. Sat with the key in her hand, feeling its small cold weight, and understood that she had just done the thing her father had done—not the forgery, not the ruin, nothing so grand, but the smaller, more ordinary sin, the one all the rest were built on.

She had decided what someone else could bear. And when.

Outside, the rain came harder, and far to the north, over a range she could not see in the dark, the first snow of an early winter was already falling on the high pass—falling on the summit cutting, on the gorge wall, on a cairn that someone had kept, on everything they were all racing to reach.

Marguerite blew out the lamp and lay down in the dark, and told herself, in her father's reasonable voice, that she would tell Rafael in the gorge. When there was time. When it was safe.

When it was too late to matter.

She was almost asleep when she heard, through the rain, the far-off sound of the junction telegraph chattering—and had the sudden, unshakable feeling that somewhere in the wire's long rattle, the mountain was already answering.
