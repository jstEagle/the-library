## 5. Into the Foothills

Nell Ashby had learned to read weather the way other girls learned to read novels: hungrily, and with a suspicion that the ending would be worse than advertised.

The telegram had come to the last supply depot before the foothills, and she'd read it twice over Baptiste's shoulder while he pretended not to notice. *MONSOON SYSTEM FORMING EARLY OVER THE BAY. LANDFALL ESTIMATED TWELVE DAYS AHEAD OF SEASON. ADVISE ALL HIGH-ROUTE WORK PARTIES TO SECURE CAMP AND EXPECT HEAVY PRECIPITATION.*

"Twelve days," she said.

"I heard you the first time, chérie." Baptiste folded the telegram into a neat square and tucked it into his shirt pocket, which was where he kept things he intended to worry about later. "You read it loud enough for the mules."

Nell was nineteen years old and had been a surveyor's assistant for exactly one season, which made her either the greenest member of Rafael Okonkwo's crew or the only one who hadn't yet found a reason to be invisible. She preferred the first description. She'd fought hard for it. Three depots ago, when Okonkwo had asked—without looking up from her instruments—who could run a level line in her sleep, Nell had said "I can" before her brain finished vetting the claim, and then she'd spent two nights with a borrowed textbook propped against the coffeepot teaching herself what she'd sworn she already knew.

It had worked. Mostly. Okonkwo hadn't fired her, which in Nell's private accounting amounted to a medal.

Now they were moving into the foothills two days ahead of schedule, because Okonkwo had read the same weather report and done something Nell was beginning to understand was characteristic: she'd simply decided the schedule was wrong and replaced it with a better one, the way a carpenter replaces a warped board.

"We make the first bench camp by Thursday," Okonkwo had announced that morning, rolling the map closed with a snap. "After that we climb."

"After that we climb," Okoye repeated, in the tone of a man agreeing to a diagnosis. He was sixty if he was a day, built like a stovepipe, with hands so scarred they looked knitted rather than grown. He was the blaster. He'd introduced himself to Nell their first morning by saying, "I blow things up for a living, small one. Try to stand behind me." She still wasn't entirely sure whether it was safety advice or a joke, and she suspected Okoye liked it that way.

* * *

The foothills did not announce themselves. That was the thing nobody told you. In the drawings in Nell's textbooks they were always dramatic—jagged blue teeth on the horizon—but the real ones arrived gradually, as a change in the light and a stubbornness in the ground. The road tilted. The scrubby trees got shorter and meaner. The mules began making comments.

Baptiste walked at the rear of the column beside the cook wagon, singing something low and mournful in French that Nell was fairly sure was about a woman, or possibly a fish. He claimed it was about both. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a laugh that came out of him like a barrel rolling downstairs, and he fed them like they were royalty who happened to be poor. Nobody asked where he'd drifted from. Drifters recognized each other; it seemed to be a courtesy of the trade.

Okoye rode point with Okonkwo, and Nell had contrived—through the simple method of walking fast—to end up just close enough to hear.

"The river gauge at Kestrel Ford is already up eight inches," Okoye was saying. "In September. That's not rain, that's the mountain clearing its throat."

"Then we don't stop to admire the view." Okonkwo didn't look at him. She never seemed to look at anything she was talking to; her eyes were always somewhere ahead, measuring. "We cut the switchback survey down to essentials. I want grades and bearings, not poetry."

"You never wanted poetry anyway."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her—the first one Nell had seen in six days of working for her—and Nell felt an absurd flush of pride, as though she'd personally caused it.

Here was the truth Nell would have denied under oath: she idolized Rafael Okonkwo. Completely. Embarrassingly. She knew all the stories, even the ones people thought she was too young to have heard. The Ashcombe Bridge. The collapse. The inquiry. The way Okonkwo's name used to open doors and now mostly closed them, quietly, the way a sickroom door closes. Half the railroad men Nell had ever met spat at the name and the other half went silent, and both reactions, Nell had decided, were just awe wearing work clothes.

Because here was the other truth: Rafael Okonkwo was the finest engineer Nell had ever stood next to, and Nell had stood next to eleven of them in one season, counting a drunk who'd surveyed an entire county line through a bottle bottom. Okonkwo read terrain the way Baptiste read recipes—not following it but conversing with it, arguing with it, winning. When she sighted along a valley you could watch her mind lay the track down like thread through cloth. Nell had once seen her correct a grade calculation by eye that Nell spent forty minutes proving right on paper, and when Nell showed her the figures, Okonkwo had glanced at them and said, "Good. Now you know why I was right instead of taking my word for it," which was the most useful sentence anyone had ever said to her.

So yes. Idolatry. Nell carried it around like a coal in her pocket, warming her hands on it.

Which was why what she started noticing over the next two days bothered her so much.

* * *

It began at the mile markers.

The old Kestrel Line had been abandoned unfinished, but the survey stakes from the original attempt were still out there, weathered gray, leaning like tired sentries. Every quarter mile there was a marker post, and every marker post had a number stenciled on it, and every number was a step closer to the gorge.

At Mile Thirty-One, Nell watched Okonkwo's stride hitch. Just slightly. Like a skipped stitch in knitting. She covered it immediately—turned it into a pause to check the grade with her hand level—but Nell had spent a season learning to read terrain, and reading people turned out to be the same skill pointed in a different direction. Something had snagged her. Something at thirty-one.

At Mile Thirty-Four, it happened again, and this time Nell saw her jaw set afterward, the way you set your jaw when you've bitten down on something you weren't expecting.

By Mile Thirty-Eight, Nell had stopped pretending she wasn't watching. Okonkwo's flinch had become almost rhythmic, a metronome of dread ticking down toward some number Nell didn't know yet. And the strange part—the part that lodged under Nell's ribs like a splinter—was that Okonkwo flinched *before* she looked at the marker. Not after. As if she knew what number was coming and her body got there first.

Everybody knew the story about the gorge. Even Nell, who was new, knew it. Tomas Okonkwo, the engineer's brother, killed in the rockfall during the original Kestrel survey, seven years back. Buried up there somewhere, or buried nowhere—that depended on who told it. It was the reason, everyone said, that Okonkwo had refused this contract in the first place. It was the reason, everyone said, that Voss had come to her hotel room in person, and whatever had been said behind that door had changed her mind.

Nell had assumed the gorge story was a wound that had scabbed over. Scars don't flinch. But Okonkwo was flinching, at regular intervals, at numbers painted on sticks, and the coal in Nell's pocket was starting to feel less like warmth and more like the first hot breath off a fire that isn't contained.

* * *

They made the first bench camp by Thursday, exactly as promised, on a shelf of flat ground above a creek that was running high and brown and angry for the season. Baptiste declared it acceptable, inspected the creek, pronounced it full of trout who had "grown arrogant in their retirement," and by dusk the camp smelled of woodsmoke and frying fish and the particular contentment of people who are too tired to be afraid yet.

Nell found Okonkwo at the edge of the shelf, standing alone with the transit case open at her feet, looking down-valley at the country they'd crossed. The evening light was doing kind things to the ridgelines, gilding everything, forgiving everything. Below them, faint in the haze, Nell could just make out a marker post on the old grade. Forty-one, according to the notes. She'd checked without meaning to. She was checking everything now.

She almost turned around. Nineteen-year-old assistants did not interrupt engineers having private moments with landscapes. But the weather report was folded in her pocket like a stone, and twelve days had already become ten and a half, and there was a question scratching at her.

"Ma'am?" she said. "The creek gauge readings. I took them at six, like you asked."

Okonkwo held out her hand without turning. Nell put the notebook in it.

Okonkwo read for a moment. "Up another three inches."

"Yes, ma'am."

"That's a foot since the ford." She closed the notebook and finally looked at Nell, and her eyes in the low light were the color of wet slate. "You're worried."

It wasn't a question, and being seen through that easily stung a little. "Aren't you?"

"I'm always worried. Worry is a tool. You keep it sharp and you use it on problems, not on yourself." She said it mildly, like reciting something she'd had to teach herself the hard way. Then, unexpectedly: "You took the readings twice, didn't you? The ink's different weights."

Heat crawled up Nell's neck. "Yes, ma'am. I thought I'd misread the meniscus the first time."

Something shifted in Okonkwo's face—not a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. "Good habit. Water lies to people who only ask it once." She looked back down the valley, and Nell should have left. It was the moment to leave. Instead she heard herself say:

"Ma'am—can I ask something that isn't about water?"

"You can ask. I reserve the right to answer something else entirely."

"The markers. On the old grade." Nell made herself look at the haze where the post stood. "You—" *Flinch* was the wrong word to say aloud to Rafael Okonkwo. "You pause at them. Each one. I noticed."

For a long moment Okonkwo said nothing, and the creek filled the silence, talking to itself in the dark. When she spoke, her voice was even and distant, like a train heard across a valley.

"Tomas used to count them," she said. "Mile markers. He had a theory that a line wasn't real until someone had walked it end to end counting, because numbers on paper lie but numbers under your boots don't." She picked up the transit case and latched it. "He never finished counting this one."

Then she walked back toward the fire, and Nell stood alone at the edge of the shelf with her heart going like a trip-hammer, understanding suddenly that she hadn't been given an answer. She'd been given a door, cracked one inch, showing a lit room inside—and then closed again, gently, by someone who had clearly closed that door ten thousand times before.

* * *

She told Baptiste about it that night, sitting on the cook wagon's tailboard with a tin cup of his terrible coffee, which he made terrible on purpose, he claimed, to keep the crew humble.

"He counted the markers," Nell said. "His brother. And now she pauses at every one, like she's finishing his count for him."

Baptiste was quiet for a while. He was often quiet for a while; it was one of the things that made people underestimate how much he noticed. "Or like she's dreading the end of the count," he said at last. "Chérie, when you get near the end of a book you love, do you slow down?"

"I suppose."

"There are books a person can't finish," Baptiste said. "And there are books a person can't stop carrying." He tapped the side of his nose, where the steam from the cup had fogged nothing at all. "This mountain has both kinds."

Nell frowned at him. "That's very wise and completely unhelpful."

"Wisdom usually is, at your age." He grinned, barrel-laugh rumbling, and refilled her cup with coffee she hadn't asked for. But as she climbed into her bedroll, she caught him looking down the dark valley toward the gorge, and the grin was gone, and his face in the firelight had an expression she couldn't file anywhere—something older than guilt and heavier than memory, worn smooth like river stone from long handling.

Everyone on this crew, she thought, was carrying a book they couldn't finish. Okoye counted avalanches in his sleep—she'd heard him muttering elevations at midnight. Baptiste had a past he stirred into his stews and never served straight. And Okonkwo—

Okonkwo was walking toward a number.

* * *

Two mornings later they broke camp in fog so thick the mules became suggestions rather than animals, and pushed up the old grade toward the gorge. The air had changed overnight; it had weight now, a wet mineral smell, the monsoon reaching inland with its fingers ahead of its body. Okoye stood at the trailhead sniffing like a hound and said, "Three days of grace left, maybe four," and no one argued with him, because no one ever had.

They passed Mile Forty at midmorning. Nell watched Okonkwo come to it, watched the hitch in her stride, the half-second pause, the jaw. But this time she also saw something new: Okonkwo didn't look back down the line they'd come from. She looked *up*. Up the grade, up toward the wall of the escarpment where the fog tore open for a moment and showed a slice of gray rock and white water far below—the Gorge of Provision, waiting, patient as a debt.

And Nell saw her lips move. One word, maybe two, lost in the wind. A number, perhaps. Or a name.

One mile further on, at the mouth of the old haul road, they found the thing that stopped the whole column: a cairn of stones at the base of the Mile Forty-One marker, waist-high, carefully built—and on top of it, impossible, absurd, heartbreaking—a tin plate holding flowers. Fresh ones. Blue and white, still dewy, picked within the day.

From somewhere. By someone. Up here, where no one was supposed to be.

Okonkwo stood very still in front of it for a long time, and the fog moved around her, and Nell, watching, forgot the weather report, forgot the schedule, forgot everything except the question rising in her chest like water in a gauge:

Who else was counting?
