## 6. The First Trestle

The trestle at Mile Twenty-Nine had been down for eleven years, and it looked it. Rafael stood on the near abutment with her hands in her coat pockets and her hat pulled low and gave the wreckage the long, unblinking stare of a doctor regarding a patient who has been eating nothing but whiskey and spite since the last visit.

"Well," said Nell beside her, notebook already open, pencil already moving. "It's a mess."

"It's a corpse," Rafael agreed. "But corpses tell you how they died."

Below them the river ran green and quick over its stones, indifferent as ever to what men had tried to lay across it. The old trestle had come down in a spring flood the year after the Kestrel Line was abandoned—three spans folded into the water like a dropped hand of cards, timbers fanned out along the bank for a quarter mile, iron bolts gone orange with rust. Somewhere under the silt and the willow scrub, the footings were still there. Footings usually were. Stone was patient that way.

Rafael crouched, laid her palm flat on the abutment stone, and felt for what she always felt for: soundness. The rock rang true. Good. She'd half expected Voss's survey reports to be exaggerated—the woman had never met a number she couldn't improve in the telling—but no. The line was recoverable. Which meant the pass was makeable. Which meant—

Which meant she was actually going to do this thing.

She straightened up too fast and her knee cracked like Okoye's dynamite, and Nell glanced over with the carefully blank expression of a young person pretending not to notice an older person's body betraying them.

"Timber inventory by noon," Rafael said, to cover it. "Okoye, I want the footings sounded before we put so much as a plank near them. Baptiste—" she turned, and found Baptiste already leaning against the supply wagon with a tin cup steaming in his hand, watching the river with the serenity of a man who had located the only pleasant spot within ten miles and intended to defend it.

"Baptiste."

"I heard you the first time, boss." He raised the cup. "Coffee's on. It's terrible. We're out of everything except coffee and regret."

"Then pour three cups of each and get the galley unpacked. A crew that eats well works fast, and I intend for us to work obscenely fast."

Baptiste pushed off the wagon, unhurried, and paused beside her just long enough to say, quietly, "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look of a woman about to do something impressive and then feel bad about it later."

"Go unpack your pots, Baptiste."

He went, grinning, and Rafael turned back to the wreck and let herself, for one unguarded moment, feel the thing she hadn't let herself feel in three years: *want*. Not the numb forward motion of miles, not the grim arithmetic of contracts and wages, but the clean animal pleasure of looking at a broken thing and knowing exactly how to mend it. Her hands knew this work the way they knew their own shape. Eleven years of flood and rot, and still she could read the trestle like a letter from an old friend—*here is where we were careless, here is where we were proud, here is where the water won.*

"All right," she said aloud, to the ruin, to the crew, to herself. "Let's bring her back."

* * *

They brought her back in four days.

Rafael had told Voss six. She'd padded the estimate the way any honest engineer pads an estimate, because weather happens and timber warps and mules are mules. But the weather held—cold, clear, the kind of high blue autumn sky that made the escarpment look painted on—and the timber Voss's advance crews had stockpiled at the railhead was better than advertised, seasoned Douglas fir, straight as scripture. And the crew—

The crew was something else.

Okoye sounded the footings on the first morning and pronounced them "sound as a bishop's conscience, which is to say mostly, where it counts." Then he taught Nell to read the ring of struck stone in about twenty minutes, which should have taken a week, because Okoye had the rare gift of explaining things as though he'd been waiting all his life for someone to ask. By the second day Nell was sounding footings alone and reporting numbers that matched his to the inch, and pretending she hadn't enjoyed it, and failing utterly.

Nell herself turned out to be worth two surveyors. She ran levels in the gray dawn light with her collar up and her fingers going stiff, and when Rafael checked her figures that first night she found them not merely correct but *elegant*—reduced, cross-checked, annotated in a small fierce hand. There was a note in the margin: *Check me. I'm new.* Rafael wrote beneath it, *Checked. You're not new anymore,* and heard Nell read it by lantern light and go very quiet, and pretended not to see her fold the page into her breast pocket like a letter from home.

And Baptiste fed them. This sounds like a small thing. It was not a small thing. Rafael had worked crews that ate cold beans out of tins for weeks and she could chart the exact slope of their morale against the slope of their production, and both went down together into the same dark valley. But Baptiste produced, from a wagon that had allegedly contained only coffee and regret, a stew the second night that made Okoye close his eyes and say grace in two languages, and after that the whole camp moved differently. Men who have eaten well argue less. It was, Rafael reflected, possibly the most important engineering principle nobody taught you.

By the third day they were setting bents in the river shallows—Okoye's charges loosening the old scoured bed, derricks swinging the new timbers, Nell calling angles from the bank with her voice cracking on the cold but never on the numbers. Rafael worked alongside them, sleeves rolled, hammering drift pins with the best of them, and somewhere around midday of the fourth day she realized she was humming. Actually humming. Some half-remembered song Tomas used to butcher on the harmonica, and instead of the usual grief-knife behind the thought there was only the thought itself, warm, like a coal carried in a cupped hand.

Tomas would have loved this crew, she caught herself thinking. And then, immediately, the coal burned her, as it always did. But she held onto it anyway. That was new.

The final span went up on the afternoon of the fourth day—a hundred and forty feet of new fir reaching across the green water to meet the old line on the far side, true and level and singing faintly in the wind. They ran the first handcar across it at dusk, all five of them crammed aboard for no practical reason whatsoever, ringing the bell like idiots, and if Rafael whooped—and she would deny it to her grave—well. There was no one on that mountain to write it down.

That night she sat outside her tent with a cup of Baptiste's coffee—which had improved dramatically once he'd excavated the real supplies—and looked at the trestle black against the stars, and allowed herself the sentence she'd been refusing for a week:

*I can make the pass.*

Ten more structures between here and the summit cutting. Harder ones, higher ones, but none of them impossible, and winter was still—she checked her almanac by habit—two weeks out, maybe eighteen days. Tight. Tighter than she liked. But makeable.

"You did a good thing today," Baptiste said, materializing beside her with the silent competence he applied to everything, including eavesdropping.

"We all did."

"Mm." He drank. "Voss telegraphed the railhead. Wants to know why we're ahead of schedule."

"What did you tell them?"

"That you're a terror and I fear for your soul." He paused. "There was a reply."

Rafael turned. Baptiste handed over a telegram flimsy without being asked, which was unlike him—he was a great one for making people ask. She read it by the ember-light of the cook fire.

*PROGRESS NOTED. HOLD PACE. ADDITIONAL SUPPLIES EN ROUTE TO MILE THIRTY-FOUR SIDING. — MV*

"Additional supplies," Rafael repeated. "We're not short on anything."

"Nope," said Baptiste comfortably. "I counted. Twice. Man can't sleep unless he's counted something."

Rafael looked at the flimsy again. *Hold pace.* Two words doing a lot of work. Voss didn't spend money on things a job didn't need; Rafael remembered that much about her from the old days, remembered rival estimators trading barbs across conference tables while Voss's red pencil hunted every unnecessary dollar like a hawk after mice. It was the one quality Rafael had ever respected in her.

So why ship extra?

"Tired eyes," she said finally, and handed it back. "Probably a clerical error. Probably gravel, or spike stock."

"Probably," Baptiste agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing with nothing.

* * *

The supplies came up on the sixth morning, hauled by a four-mule team driven by a railhead man who wanted his signature signed and his coffee poured and otherwise to be gone before the weather thought about turning. Rafael signed him off at the Mile Thirty-Four siding and then walked the length of the wagons with her tally book, because counting things was how she slept too.

Spike stock: normal quantity. Gravel ballast: normal. Replacement timber: slightly generous, fine, timber warped. Lantern oil, rope, chain—

And then the crates.

They were stacked at the rear of the second wagon, six of them, stenciled in fresh black paint: *DYNAMITE — 50 LB — KEEP DRY — NO FIRE ARMS NEAR*, and beneath the stencil, stamped in wax-red, the mark she'd know anywhere, the mark she'd been seeing on invoices and crates and Voss's insufferable engraved correspondence for fifteen years: a kestrel in descent, wings folded, talons forward. Voss Consolidated Mining & Rail.

Six crates. Three hundred pounds of fresh dynamite.

Rafael stood very still in the cold morning air and did arithmetic, because arithmetic was safe, arithmetic always answered.

A job like theirs—trestles, cuts, one hard bore through the shoulder of the pass—wanted blasting powder, yes. Okoye had drawn against the existing stockpile twice, carefully, and she'd countersigned both draws. She knew to the stick how much explosive the remaining work required, because she had personally written the estimate: another ninety pounds, perhaps a hundred and ten if the summit cutting fought them harder than the surveys suggested. The crates already in their magazine covered that with room to spare.

Three hundred pounds on top of that was not room to spare. Three hundred pounds on top of that was enough to drop every structure they'd built, the gorge wall, and a fair portion of the escarpment itself into the next county.

"Boss?" Nell had come up the siding road with her level on her shoulder, cheeks pink, breath smoking. "Okoye wants to know if you want the new stock in the main magazine or—"

"Who told you there was new stock?"

Nell stopped. Blinked. "You're standing next to it?"

Rafael realized she was. She stepped aside, deliberately casual, and watched Nell read the stencils, watched the girl's eyebrows climb, watched her do the same arithmetic Rafael had just done—young surveyors are all arithmetic, it's why Rafael loved them—and arrive at the same impossible total.

"That's..." Nell counted the crates with her finger, hoping to be wrong. "That's a lot of dynamite, boss."

"It is."

"For a line job."

"It is."

Nell looked up at her, and Rafael saw the question form behind her eyes—the question Rafael had been holding at arm's length since the telegram, since the junction hotel, since Marguerite Voss had sat across from her with that careful face and offered a fortune for a dead line through a living wound. *Why does a woman who counts every dollar ship three hundred pounds of dynamite to a job that needs a hundred?*

"Put it in the far magazine," Rafael said. "Separate from ours. Lock it. I'll keep the key."

"Okoye keeps the magazine keys."

"Okoye keeps *our* keys. I'll keep these." She heard her own voice come out flat and hard, and softened it with effort. "It's not about Okoye. It's about knowing exactly what's where. On a mountain, that's just good practice."

Nell studied her a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she nodded, shouldered her level, and started directing the mule driver toward the far cache, and Rafael watched her go and felt the warmth of the past week develop a draft in it, a cold thread running through the middle of the good work like a crack through sound stone.

Because here was the thing she kept circling and refusing to land on: the trestle had gone up beautifully. The crew was the best she'd ever run. The pass was makeable, the schedule was hers, the work sang. Everything was exactly as it should be.

Except the dynamite.

She took out her tally book and recorded the delivery in her own hand—six crates, three hundred pounds, kestrel stamp, received and secured—and as she wrote she noticed her pen pressing harder than the page needed, carving the numbers in like she wanted them legible to somebody someday, some inquiry, some court. She made herself ease off. Made herself laugh at it, even, alone by the siding, a short ugly laugh that scared a jay off the fence post.

*Paranoia, Okonkwo. The woman's financing your redemption and you're inventing crimes for her.*

But the other voice, the quiet one, the one that had kept her alive through the Ashcombe hearings while better engineers than she came apart under cross-examination—that voice said: *Marguerite Voss has never in her life paid for anything she didn't intend to use.*

At supper that night the crew was loud and easy, flush with the trestle, arguing cheerfully about whether Baptiste's stew contained actual pheasant or only the idea of pheasant. ("Gentlemen," Baptiste said, "a chef must keep some mysteries.") Okoye told a long story about a bridge in the coastal range that had been built crooked on purpose to fool a government inspector, and how the inspector had figured it out anyway because the crooked bridge *sang* in the wind, a wrong note, and the moral of the story, Okoye said, jabbing his spoon, was that timber tells the truth eventually, everything tells the truth eventually, the mountain sees to it.

Rafael laughed with the rest of them. And underneath the laughter she turned the kestrel stamp over and over in her mind, wings folded, talons forward, descending.

Afterward she climbed to the far magazine alone, in the dark, with a lantern, and checked the lock, and checked it again. Through the board seams she could smell the sharp almond ghost of the nitroglycerin, fresh and patient, waiting to be asked what it was for.

She stood there a long moment in the cold, listening to the wind come down off the escarpment, carrying the first thin suggestion of snow.

"Hold pace," she said aloud, to nobody. "What exactly are we holding pace *for*, Marguerite?"

The mountain didn't answer. Mountains never did. But eight miles above them, at the head of the gorge where the maps went quiet, the pass waited with whatever it was keeping, and Rafael went back down to her tent and slept badly, and dreamed of red stamps on white snow.
