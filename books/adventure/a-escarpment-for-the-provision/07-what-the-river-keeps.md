## 7. What the River Keeps

Baptiste had three rules for cooking on a work crew, and he'd learned every one of them the hard way, which was the only way Baptiste ever learned anything.

Rule one: never trust a man who salts his beans before tasting them. Rule two: keep the coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in but weak enough that nobody blames you when their hands shake. And rule three—the one that mattered most, the one he repeated to himself like a prayer every morning while the skillet heated—was this: feed people well enough and they'll forgive you almost anything.

*Almost* anything. That was the word doing all the heavy lifting.

He stood at the edge of the gorge river now, in the blue hour before dawn, with a fishing line in his hands and a lie in his chest, and he thought about how long *almost* could stretch. Three years, as it happened. Maybe four. He'd stopped counting honestly somewhere around the second anniversary of Ashcombe Bridge.

The river ran fast and cold below him, snowmelt from the high pass braided with the first gray light. It was a good river. A working river. It didn't know it had killed anybody, and if rivers knew things, Baptiste figured they kept it to themselves better than men did.

He cast. The line whispered out over the water and settled, and Baptiste settled with it, onto a flat stone worn smooth as a church pew, and he waited the way he did everything else: patiently, hungrily, and with a low-grade hum of guilt running underneath like water under ice.

Breakfast was trout, if the river cooperated. If it didn't, it was salt pork and the last of the flour, and Nell would make that face she made—not a complaint, exactly, more of an apology on behalf of the universe—and Rafael would eat whatever was put in front of her without tasting a bite of it, the way she'd eaten everything for three years, and Okoye would say something dry about how in his village a cook who served pork twice in one week would be exiled to the far hills, and Baptiste would tell him that was fine, he'd pack him a lunch for the walk.

That was the shape of their mornings. He loved it more than he had any right to, given what he was carrying.

The line twitched. Baptiste hauled back gently, feeling the weight, reading it through the rod the way you read a letter from somebody whose handwriting you knew. Not a fish. Too heavy, too dead-feeling, dragging bottom.

"Come on, then," he murmured. "Don't be what I think you are."

But it was, of course. The river gave up its secret slow, the way rivers do, and by the time Baptiste had walked backward up the bank hand over hand, hauling against the current's grip, there it was lying in the shallows at his feet: a length of ironwork, rusted to the color of dried blood, furred with moss, bent into a shape no iron wanted to be bent into.

A truss bracket. He knew it without turning it over. Knew it the way you know your own signature. Ashcombe Bridge had been built out of a thousand of these, and on the day it came down, most of them had gone into the water or into the mud or into the men.

Baptiste stood very still for a long moment, listening to the river.

Then he looked up the slope toward camp, where the smoke of the stove he'd left banked rose thin and straight, where Rafael Okonkwo was probably already awake—she was always already awake, the woman slept like a cat on a hot stove—and he made his decision the way he made all his decisions lately: quickly, so the guilt wouldn't have time to catch up.

He wasn't going to show her.

* * *

Here is what Baptiste had told the crew, when Rafael came around the junction hotel asking after men willing to work the Kestrel Line out of season: that he'd cooked on rail crews for eleven years, off and on, wherever the work wandered. All true. That he liked being paid, and paid promptly. Also true. That he had no particular feelings about the gorge one way or the other.

That was the lie, and it was a good one, because it was mostly true. He had no particular feelings about the gorge. It was the bridge he had feelings about. The bridge, and the day, and the sound—which he could still hear, some nights, clear as a bell over a door—of iron letting go.

He hadn't been on Rafael's crew. That was the part that mattered, the part he'd built his whole careful story around. He'd been on the *other* crew. The Ashcombe contract had gone to two companies working opposite banks, racing each other to midspan, and Baptiste had cooked for the Corvane outfit, the east-bank men, the ones who finished their side early and got their bonus and went home while the west-bank crew—Rafael's crew—kept pushing.

And on the last morning, over eggs, he'd heard his own engineers talking. Low voices. The kind of low that isn't meant for the help, except cooks hear everything; that was the whole trade. Steel deliveries shorted. A splice spec "revised." The chief saying, *Corvane won't sign off on delay, we pour Thursday or we explain to the board why we don't.* And another voice—young, nervous—saying something about load tolerances, and the chief laughing and saying the tolerances were for engineers who planned to be around in ten years.

Baptiste had laughed too. He'd been twenty-six years old and hungry and the eggs needed turning.

Thursday, the east side poured. Friday, the west side crew—Rafael's brother among them—went out onto spans that had been signed off by men who'd already spent their bonus money. And at 4:17 in the afternoon, the iron let go.

He hadn't known. That was true, and it was not nearly enough, and he'd had three years to learn the difference between those two sentences. He hadn't *known*, the way you don't know a thing until it has a body count. But he'd heard. He'd heard and he'd laughed and he'd turned the eggs, and afterward, when the inquiry came around and the report landed on Rafael Okonkwo like a landslide—*engineer error, inadequate inspection, failure of supervision*—Baptiste had read it in a newspaper and felt something turn over in his chest and had done absolutely nothing about it. Because the report said what everybody already believed, and contradicting it would mean explaining how he knew, and explaining how he knew would mean admitting he'd heard the east-bank chiefs talk and said nothing, and besides, who was he? A cook. A drifter. A man whose testimony weighed about as much as a trout skeleton.

So he'd carried it. Eleven years on rail crews taught you how to carry things. You packed them deep, down under the flour sacks, and you fed people well, and you moved along before anybody got curious about what was making the wagon ride heavy.

Until Marguerite Voss's telegram brought Rafael back to this exact stretch of country, and Baptiste—who should have run, who had a gift for running, who'd once left a town in Nebraska between supper and dishes—had signed on. Signed on like a fool. Like a man walking back into the burning house to see if maybe, this time, he could carry something out instead of just standing in the yard.

The bracket lay in the shallows, bleeding rust into the clean water.

"All right," Baptiste told it quietly. "All right. Come on, old man."

* * *

He buried it upstream, past the bend, where the willows grew thick and nobody from camp had reason to walk. He dug with the small folding spade he used for latrine duty—a tool, he reflected, that had seen more honest work than most senators—and he laid the ironwork in the hole the way you'd lay out a body, straightening the bent flange as best he could, tucking moss around it.

His mother would have said words over it. His mother had words for everything: for the first rain of the season, for a good yam harvest, for a stranger at the gate. Baptiste had inherited her appetite and her patience and precisely none of her eloquence, so what he said was:

"You're not going back up. Whatever they decide to do with this valley, you're staying put. You've done your work."

It wasn't much. The river took the rest.

He was filling in the last of the dirt when he heard footsteps on the willow duff and his whole body went tight as a drumhead before his brain caught up and identified the gait: young, quick, slightly pigeon-toed. Nell.

"Mornin'," he called, not turning around, arranging himself into the posture of a man who had simply been digging a hole for reasons. Fishing-related reasons. Men dug holes near rivers constantly. It was practically a hobby.

"Baptiste." She came around the willows with her surveyor's satchel and a look on her face that meant she'd been sent. Nell didn't come find people on her own account yet; she still thought of herself as staff, not crew. "Miss Okonkwo wants to know if breakfast's moving."

"Breakfast moves when the trout do," Baptiste said. "Tell her the river's thinking about it."

"She also wants to know why you're digging."

There it was. The girl had eyes like a transit scope. Baptiste wiped his hands on his trousers, slow and thoughtful, buying the seconds he needed. "Compost pit," he said. "Fish bones and coffee grounds. You want a camp stinking of rot by Mile Fifty? No. You do not. A cook who doesn't bury his scraps is a cook feeding bears, and I have been chased by a bear exactly once, and once was educational."

Nell considered this. She was a girl who considered things, which Baptiste privately counted as the single best quality a person could have, and which he also, at present, found deeply inconvenient.

"That's a good depth for compost," she allowed.

"I'm thorough."

She lingered. He could feel a question assembling itself behind her face the way weather assembles behind a ridge, and he headed it off the only way he knew how. "You eat?"

"I—what? No, I was going to—"

"Sit. There's hardtack in my coat and honey in the tin, and if you tell Miss Okonkwo I let you spoil your appetite, I'll deny it with such conviction she'll believe me over you, because I've had three years' practice believing my own nonsense and you've had, what, nineteen years total?"

That startled a laugh out of her, and she sat, and they shared hardtack and honey on the riverbank while the light came up gold through the willows, and for a few minutes Baptiste got to be nothing but a cook feeding a hungry kid, which was the closest thing to innocent he'd felt in a long while.

"They say she's good," Nell said, around a mouthful. "Okonkwo. Best bridge engineer on the coast, before."

"She's good," Baptiste said.

"You worked with her before? On the big lines?"

And there it was, the question he'd been waiting for since the junction hotel, arriving at last on a quiet morning with honey on its chin. Baptiste chewed his hardtack. Downstream, around the bend, a patch of turned earth sat under the willows holding a dead man's iron, and upstream the river came down clean off the glacier, and between the two of them sat Baptiste with his mouth full, choosing.

"I've worked a lot of lines," he said. "They start to blur. Rivers, bridges, mountains—it's all the same job, really. Keep people fed, keep people moving, try not to let anybody die on your watch." He smiled, and it even reached his eyes, mostly. "Eat your honey."

* * *

Breakfast was trout after all—the river relented around midmorning, delivering four fat ones as if apologizing for the earlier misunderstanding—and the crew ate well, and for an hour or two everything was fine.

Rafael ate standing up, studying the survey plates spread across a crate, and Baptiste watched her the way he'd been watching her all week, with a feeling he couldn't name and didn't want to. It wasn't pity. Pity was cheap and she'd smell it. It was closer to the feeling you get watching somebody carry a load up a hill that you know—*know*, from having stood on that same hill—is twice as heavy as it looks.

Because here was the thing he couldn't stop noticing: the report was wrong. Not cruel-wrong, not ordinary-wrong, but *built* wrong, assembled wrong, the way a bridge can be assembled wrong, with bad steel in the middle of it dressed up to look like engineering. He'd read it forty times over three years, mostly late at night, the way you press a bruise. *Failure of supervision.* Rafael Okonkwo had supervised her crew like a woman guarding a nest of eggs. He'd heard the east-bank men say so, grudgingly, in the weeks after—heard his own chief mutter into his coffee that the west side ran tighter than Corvane ever had, and wasn't it a damn shame about the steel, and then go quiet in a way that had told Baptiste everything and proved nothing.

The report blamed her because blaming her was convenient, and because somewhere above both companies there were men who needed a name to hang the disaster on, and hers was the one within reach.

He could fix that. Probably. Maybe. If he talked. If he told Rafael and Voss and whoever else needed telling what he'd heard over eggs on a Thursday morning four years ago—if he stepped out from behind his skillet and became, God help him, a witness—he might crack the whole rotten report open. And he might also end up ruined, sued, possibly worse, because the men who bought a report once were not men who hesitated to buy silence twice.

Or he could keep carrying it. Feed people well. Move along before anyone got curious.

He was washing the skillet when Okoye drifted over to the fire, the old blaster moving with that unhurried economy of his, and settled onto a crate with the air of a man planning to stay awhile.

"Good fish," Okoye said.

"Thank you."

"In my village," Okoye said, "we believe a man who lies with his mouth is a small sinner. A man who lies with his cooking—" he held up a finger— "that one, we exile."

Baptiste kept scrubbing. "My cooking's honest."

"Your cooking is excellent. That is not the same thing." Okoye watched the fire a moment. He had a way of looking at flames like they were colleagues. "I have buried friends under avalanches, Baptiste. Four of them. And I will tell you what I learned, which is that the mountain does not care what you are carrying. But men are not mountains. Men can tell the weight is there. They can see it in how you walk."

"I walk fine."

"You walk like a mule with a hidden pack." Okoye rose, unhurried, brushing off his trousers. "One day the strap breaks. Better you choose the moment than the mountain does." He paused at the edge of the firelight. "Also, the coffee is perfect today. Whatever you are guilty of, it is not the coffee."

He ambled off toward the powder store, and Baptiste stood alone with the skillet and the smoke and the sound of the river coming down out of the gorge, patient as sin, wearing away the stone one year at a time.

* * *

That evening, the light went copper and then violet over the escarpment, and the crew gathered around the fire the way crews do, drawn by food and habit and the human refusal to sit alone in the dark. Rafael was quieter than usual, which was saying something, her eyes on the dark line of the gorge wall where the next mile of track would climb. Voss had ridden up from the lower camp that afternoon with fresh orders and that smile of hers, polished as brass, and Rafael had taken the orders without a word, and something in the space between the two women had felt, to Baptiste, like a fuse quietly lit at both ends.

He served stew. He passed the bread. He kept rule three working for him, hour after hour, and it worked, the way it always worked, and the crew loosened by degrees—Nell arguing happily with Okoye about whether dynamite or black powder made the better avalanche story, Voss watching Rafael across the fire with an expression Baptiste catalogued and filed away under *later*.

Later. Everything was later with him. Later was where he lived.

It was full dark when Rafael spoke, not to anyone in particular, her eyes still on the gorge wall. "Tomas used to fish that stretch," she said. "Below the bend. Said the trout ran stupid there because the current confused them." A pause. The fire popped. "He'd have liked your trout, Baptiste."

"Yes, ma'am," Baptiste said, and ladled her another helping she wouldn't taste, and smiled his warm, practiced, eleven-years-perfected smile, and underneath it, down where the flour sacks were packed, the hidden load shifted and groaned against its straps.

Upstream, under the willows, the iron slept in its new grave. And downstream, around the bend, the river kept the rest—every bolt, every splinter, every secret the mountain had swallowed—and Baptiste understood, sitting in the firelight with his empty ladle, that the river was not the only one keeping things.

Ten days, Okoye had said that afternoon, squinting at the peaks. Ten days before the pass closed. Whatever Baptiste was going to say, whatever confession he was going to drag up out of his own personal gorge, the mountain was going to choose the moment for him if he didn't choose it first.

He looked at Rafael's bowed head, silver-streaked in the firelight, and began, very quietly, to rehearse.
