## 8. Snowline Rising

Okoye had been reading mountains since before Marguerite Voss was born, and the mountain was lying to them.

That was the trouble with weather. It didn't lie outright. It just told you a pleasant truth today so it could hand you a terrible one next week. The mornings up here had been clear as gin, the kind of blue that made young surveyors like Nell want to sing, and every day of that blue sky was a lie with excellent posture.

He stood at the edge of Camp Three with his tin cup of Baptiste's coffee—too much chicory, not enough of anything else—and looked at the high pass through the gap in the ridgeline where he could see it without pretending he wasn't looking. The summit saddle sat under a cap of cloud that hadn't moved in two days. Clouds that sit still are clouds gathering their strength.

"Beautiful morning," said Nell, coming up beside him with her notebook already out, pencil behind her ear, twenty-two years old and built entirely of enthusiasm and borrowed boots.

"Mm," said Okoye.

"The light on the escarpment is incredible. Rafael says we might make the summit cutting by month's end if the drilling goes clean."

Okoye took a slow sip of the chicory swill and considered how to say what needed saying. He'd been a blaster for thirty-one years. He'd learned that the words you chose before an explosion mattered almost as much as the ones after, and this conversation was going to be a kind of demolition too.

"Nell," he said, "look at the snowline."

She looked. "It's... high?"

"It's *low*. It's been climbing down the mountain for three days. See where the white stops on the north face? Two days ago it was above that band of scree. Now it's below it." He pointed with his cup. "Snow doesn't come down to meet you unless something's pushing it. Warm air off the sea, wet systems stacking up behind the range. That's monsoon behavior, girl. That's monsoon arriving early and hungry."

Nell's pencil stopped moving. "The reports said we had three weeks."

"The reports are written by men in offices who look at maps instead of mountains." He drained the coffee and grimaced at it on principle. "I've buried friends under avalanches, Nell. Four of them. Good men, careful men, men who trusted a forecast. I don't intend to make it five, six, seven, and however many of us are standing in this camp when the pass decides to shut its mouth."

* * *

He found Rafael at the grade site, knee-deep in the cut they were driving toward Mile Forty-Four, arguing with a drill bit that had gone dull and stubborn. She worked like she was punishing the rock, which Okoye supposed she was, though he'd never asked her for what. A person didn't get lines around their eyes like hers from sleeping well.

"Miss Okonkwo," he said. "A word."

"If it's about the powder stores again, I inventoried them myself last night."

"It's about the sky."

That got her attention. She set down the drill and wiped her hands on her trousers, giving him the full weight of her focus, which was considerable. Rafael Okonkwo listened the way other people fired rifles.

"Talk."

"The pass closes in ten days. Maybe less." He held up a hand before she could object. "I know what your schedule says. I wrote half of it. But I've watched that north face all week, and the snowpack up there is loading wrong—wind slabs, layered like bad pastry. One warm storm on top of that and the whole bowl above the viaduct comes down at once. We won't hear it coming. We'll just be gone."

Rafael looked past him at the mountain for a long moment. Okoye had seen her face do many things—contempt, mostly, and lately something gentler when she spoke to Nell—but this was new. This was arithmetic. He could nearly see her running numbers: rail laid, rail remaining, days claimed versus days actual.

"Ten days," she said.

"Nine, if I'm being honest. I'm saying ten because nine sounds like panic."

"And if we stop?"

"We don't stop. We either finish or we turn back, and turning back—" He gestured downhill, where the lower camps had started looking nervously at the river. It had come up a foot in two days and turned the color of strong tea. "Turning back may not be on offer much longer either. That water's rising like it knows something."

Rafael nodded slowly. "Then we go faster."

"That's one option."

"What's the other?"

"Going home," said Okoye. "But I notice neither of us has suggested it seriously, so let's not insult each other."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face. "Get me a revised blasting plan by tonight. If we've got ten days, I want the summit cutting drilled and shot in eight, and I want you to tell me honestly if that's madness."

"It's madness," said Okoye. "I'll have the plan by supper."

* * *

Marguerite Voss did not take the news the way he'd hoped, which was to say she took it like a woman who had expected it and resented it anyway.

They met in the survey tent, Voss behind the camp table with her maps weighted down with stones, and Okoye laid it out plain: ten days, revised schedule, more powder, more men on the drills, and a hard rule that nobody worked the upper grades after midday when the sun warmed the slabs loose.

Voss listened with her hands flat on the table. She had a way of listening that made you feel audited.

"You're certain?" she said.

"I'm certain the way a man is certain who's dug bodies out of snow. Miss Voss, I've run charges on four continents and I have never once told a crew a mountain would close early unless the mountain told me first. It's telling us. Loudly."

"Then we accelerate." She was already pulling the master schedule toward her, red pencil out. "Double shifts on the cutting. I'll authorize the extra powder."

"There's the second thing," Okoye said carefully. "We're burning powder faster than the manifest allows. Even before acceleration. I counted crates at Camp Two yesterday and the numbers don't reconcile. Either somebody's miscounting—and I don't miscount—or there's more dynamite on this line than the job needs."

For just a moment—less than a moment, really, a flicker no faster than a fuse—something moved behind Voss's eyes. Then it was gone, replaced by the smooth administrative calm Okoye had learned to distrust in every company man he'd ever met.

"Clerical error, most likely. The supply agent at the junction was a drunk." She made a note. "I'll audit it myself."

"See that you do," said Okoye, and was mildly surprised at himself for saying it, and more surprised that she let him.

* * *

That evening he sat outside his tent cleaning tools that didn't need cleaning, which was what Okoye did instead of praying. Beside him, wrapped in oilcloth inside his pack, was the thing he'd been building for a week now: his exit.

Not cowardice. He wanted that understood, even if only by himself. He'd left crews before—twice, both times against orders, both times because the mountain said go and the boss said stay, and both times the boss had been wrong and some of the men had been dead by Thursday. A blaster who wouldn't walk away was just a corpse with seniority.

But leaving this crew sat wrong in his chest, and he examined why while he oiled the hinge of a charge crimping tool that had never once stuck.

There was Nell, who reminded him painfully of himself at that age, right down to trusting forecasts. There was Baptiste, who fished the gorge river every evening like a man doing penance to it, and who flinched whenever anyone mentioned Ashcombe Bridge. There was Rafael, carrying grief the way mules carry ore—patiently, endlessly, up slopes no animal should climb. And there was even Voss, who lied with her face but whose hands shook slightly when she thought no one was watching, and hands don't shake over clerical errors.

They were, he realized with some irritation, people he'd decided not to abandon. When had that happened? He'd signed on for wages and silence. Wages and silence didn't keep you up at night counting snow layers.

He looked at the pass one more time. The cloud cap had thickened. Somewhere up in that white bowl, tons of winter were leaning against a fence that wouldn't hold.

"Ten days," he told the mountain. "You give us ten, and I'll forgive the chicory."

The mountain, naturally, said nothing. Mountains never promised anything. That was the one honest thing about them.

He finished the tool, packed the exit kit deep beneath his spare shirt where it wouldn't insult anyone, and went to find Rafael. If they were going to race an avalanche, somebody had to teach the young ones how to read the snow before it read them.

And tomorrow, he decided, he'd have a quiet word with Baptiste too. The cook knew something about the powder count. Okoye had watched his face at supper when the crates came up—the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

Everybody on this line was counting something. Okoye just intended to be the one whose sums came true.
