## 9. The Marker at Mile Forty-One

The morning of the forty-first mile arrived wearing fog like a borrowed coat.

Rafael had known it was coming for three days. She had felt it the way a tooth knows a storm is coming—not in the sky, which stayed stubbornly blue and indifferent, but in her own body, in the way her hands went quiet on the transit and her voice shortened to single syllables. Nell had noticed. Nell noticed everything now; that was the trouble with training a good surveyor. You taught them to read terrain, and then they turned the same skill on you.

"You've been re-staking the same curve twice," Nell said over breakfast, watching Rafael fold the same map for the third time.

"Grade corrections," Rafael said.

"The grade hasn't changed since yesterday."

"Then I corrected it twice. Thoroughness."

Okoye snorted into his coffee without looking up from the blasting log he kept in a ledger so worn its corners had gone soft as cloth. Baptiste, who had developed the uncanny ability of a good cook to be present at every emotional moment while appearing to only care about eggs, slid an extra biscuit onto Rafael's plate and said nothing at all, which was somehow louder than anything he could have said.

The line they were rebuilding ran along the old Kestrel alignment through the lower gorge country, and the old alignment was numbered. Every mile had a marker—concrete posts poured by the original crews thirty years ago, stamped with their distance from the junction. Most had survived. Some had cracked. Mile forty-one stood, as far as Rafael knew, exactly where it had always stood: on a shelf of gray stone above the river bend where the water ran green and deep and slow, as if even the river hesitated there.

Where Tomas died.

She had not been back. Three years, and she had engineered herself around that half-mile of earth the way water engineers itself around a boulder—endlessly, patiently, never once touching it. She had surveyed lines in four territories since Ashcombe Bridge came down and took eleven men with it, Tomas among them, and she had arranged every contract, every route, every season of her life so that no road, rail, or footpath required her to pass within twenty miles of this bend.

And now Voss's money had walked her straight up the throat of it.

* * *

They reached the marker by mid-morning. Rafael had sent the crew ahead around the shoulder of the hill with the stake wagon—told them she'd catch up, told them she wanted to check the drainage culvert at the bend, which was true in the way that most lies are true: there *was* a culvert, and it *did* need checking, and none of that was why she stood alone on the shelf of gray stone with her hat in her hands like a man at a funeral she'd promised herself she wouldn't attend.

The marker was still there. Concrete, knee-high, the numeral 41 worn shallow but legible, furred at the base with lichen the color of old brass. Someone had painted the number recently—a careful hand, white paint gone slightly amber with weather, applied not this year but not long ago either. Two years, maybe. Maybe less.

That was the first wrong thing. Nobody painted mile markers on a dead line.

The second wrong thing was the cairn.

It stood six feet east of the post, at the edge of the shelf where the ground began its long negotiation with the river below. It was not large—knee-high, a dozen stones, river-rounded ones chosen deliberately rather than gathered carelessly, stacked with the patience of someone who understood balance. It was the kind of cairn railroad men built. The kind her father had taught her and Tomas both to build, on the provision land, when they were children stacking rocks in the creek while he talked about deeds and boundaries and what a family was owed by the world.

But it was the top of the cairn that stopped Rafael's breath.

Fresh flowers. Not dried ones, not the papery ghosts of last summer—fresh ones, mountain columbine and something yellow she didn't have a name for, their stems wrapped in a strip of cloth to keep them together, laid carefully against the stones. They had been there days, not weeks. And beside them, tucked into a crevice between two rocks where the wind couldn't take it, a tin plate. A dinner plate, enameled, white with a blue rim, chipped at one edge—the kind that came in sets of six from any dry goods store in the territory.

On the plate, neatly centered, sat a smooth gray stone about the size of a plum.

An offering. A place set at a table for someone who would never come to eat.

Rafael sat down on the cold rock without deciding to. Her legs made the decision for her, the way legs do when the rest of a person has been emptied out.

She had spent three years believing she was the only one who remembered this bend of river correctly. That was the private arithmetic of grief—you assume you hold the whole sum alone. She had built her wandering life on the assumption that Tomas existed now only inside her: his laugh, his terrible singing, his habit of reading survey notes aloud in a mock-serious voice until she threw a pencil at him. Eleven men had died at Ashcombe, and there had been widows and mothers and children scattered across three counties, and Rafael had known that, had sent letters and money when she could, but here—*here*, at the exact place, at the exact stone—she had believed the grief was hers alone, a property with a single owner.

Someone had been coming here for years.

The flowers said years. The repainted numeral said years. The plate, weathered but cared for, wiped clean of dust, said *someone climbs down to this shelf regularly, someone who knows the path, someone who has made a ritual of it.* This was not a grave visited once in guilt. This was a tended thing. A kept thing.

She picked up the plate with both hands, the way you hold something that might break or might bite.

Underneath it, wedged flat against the stone, was a slip of paper folded into eighths, gone soft at the folds from weather and handling. Her heart did something complicated. She almost didn't open it. Opening it felt like reading another person's mail, and she had spent enough of her life being blamed for things she hadn't done to be squeamish about small sins—but her hands were already unfolding it, because hands, too, make decisions when the rest of you is empty.

The writing was careful, block capitals, the handwriting of someone who had learned penmanship late or under pressure:

*TOMAS — STILL KEEPING YOUR SHARE OF THE FIRE WARM. THE RIVER'S LOW THIS YEAR. I TOLD THEM WHAT YOU SAID ABOUT THE CULVERT AND THEY LISTENED, FOR ONCE. — F.*

Rafael read it four times.

*F.*

She turned the name over in her mind like a coin she couldn't identify by feel. Who signed themselves with a single letter? Who knew Tomas well enough to quote him—*what you said about the culvert*—and to report back to him, conversationally, as if death were a separation of distance rather than everything else? Who *told them*?

Not her. She was the *R*. If anyone wrote to Tomas, it should have been her, and she had written nothing, spoken nothing, come nowhere, for three years, because she had been running miles instead of standing still, and the shame of that landed on her now with its full weight. While she had been outrunning this bend of river, some *F* had been climbing down to it with flowers and fresh paint and news from the living world, keeping a fire warm, setting a plate.

She looked out at the river. Green, deep, slow. The water that had taken him—or rather, the water beside which the trestle had failed and dropped him, though the coroner's language had been vaguer, kinder to everyone's sleep. She thought about the culvert. There *was* a culvert at this bend; she had checked it that morning with more attention than any culvert in the territory had ever received, running her lamp along its barrel, finding it sound. Tomas had opinions about culverts. He'd been the junior engineer on the original Kestrel survey, fresh off the provision land and hungry to prove that a farmer's son could read rock better than any city-trained man, and he used to argue with the older surveyors about drainage the way other men argued about cards.

*I told them what you said about the culvert and they listened, for once.*

Her eyes stung. She let them. There was no one to see, and the river had seen worse from her than tears—it had seen her scream herself hoarse from the far bank three years ago, held back by two men whose names she'd never learned, while the water did what water does.

She sat for a long time. An hour, maybe more. The fog burned off. A hawk rode the updraft off the gorge in slow, arrogant circles. Somewhere above, faintly, she could hear the crew's hammering start up on the next stretch—Nell's crew, driving stakes, building the future across the bones of the past, which was, when you thought about it, the entire history of railroads in one sentence.

Eventually she did the things that needed doing.

She wiped the plate clean with her sleeve and set it back exactly as she'd found it, stone centered, facing the river. She added one stone to the cairn—choosing it carefully from the shelf, testing its balance the way her father had taught her—and pressed it gently on top. She took out her notebook, tore a page, and after several false starts wrote in her own careful capitals:

*I'M HERE NOW. I'M SORRY IT TOOK ME THREE YEARS. I'M GOING TO FINISH WHAT YOU STARTED. — R.*

She folded it into eighths and put it under the plate, where the other note had been, so that the two letters lay together like people sharing a bench.

Then she stood, put her hat back on, and became, by visible effort, an engineer again.

* * *

She caught up with the crew at the shoulder of the hill, where Nell had staked out the next curve and was arguing amiably with Okoye about whether a cut through the spur was worth the powder.

"Culvert's sound," Rafael reported. "Shelf above the bend is stable. We can run the alignment along the old grade."

Nell looked at her a beat too long. Rafael had washed her face in the river before walking up—that much housekeeping she'd managed—but there are things water doesn't fix, and Nell was learning to read terrain.

"You were gone a while," Nell said.

"Thoroughness."

"That's twice you've used that word today."

"Then I'm being thorough about it."

To her mild surprise, Nell laughed—a short, startled sound, as if she hadn't planned on it—and let it go. Okoye, who missed nothing that involved explosives or human beings, marked something in his ledger that may or may not have been about powder. Baptiste, arriving with the noon kettle, handed Rafael tea she hadn't asked for and studied her face over the steam with the frank appraisal of a man who judged everything by whether it needed salt.

"You look," he decided, "like somebody who found something."

"I checked a culvert."

"Mm." He poured himself a cup. "Culverts'll do that."

They worked the afternoon. That was the mercy of the work, the thing Rafael had known since she was sixteen and had forgotten during her three years of running: labor is a room grief can't entirely fill. By evening the stakes marched along the old grade in a confident white row, the transit readings agreed with each other, and the line ahead ran straight toward the gorge wall for the first time since the crew had entered the foothills. Tomorrow they'd begin the cut. The day after, if the weather held, they'd cross the bend itself—mile forty-one, the shelf, the cairn—with rails and ties and forty tons of equipment, and Rafael would have to decide whether to tell her crew what stood six feet off the alignment, or keep it the way she'd kept everything else: private, carried, heavy.

She was still deciding when Nell came into camp holding a lantern and a piece of paper, moving fast, with the particular gait of a person carrying news that won't keep overnight.

"Rafael. You need to see this."

"What is it?"

"It's Voss." Nell glanced over her shoulder toward the big tent at the far end of camp, where lamplight showed the shadow of a figure bent over a table. "She thinks we're all asleep. She's got two maps spread out—one's our survey, the one I drew myself, I'd know my own linework anywhere." Nell's voice dropped. "The other one isn't ours. It's old. Hand-drawn. And Rafael—it doesn't mark track at all. It marks something *in the gorge wall*. Something buried."

Rafael stood very still. Behind her, down the dark slope, the river ran green and slow past a shelf of gray stone where a tin plate waited under the stars, keeping a dead man's place at the table.

"Show me," she said.
