## 10. Voss's Other Map

Nell Quill had learned three things about Marguerite Voss in the six weeks since the Kestrel Line had swallowed them all: she took her coffee black and bitter enough to strip paint, she thanked people exactly once and then expected the thanks to last, and she never, ever let a map out of her sight.

That last one had started as a curiosity and hardened into a habit of watching. Nell was a surveyor, and surveyors noticed things about maps the way shepherds noticed things about sheep—when one was limping, when one was hiding something, when one had been somewhere it shouldn't.

The engineering survey lived in the map chest in Voss's tent, rolled in oilcloth, consulted at dawn and dusk. Nell had drawn half of it herself. She knew every contour line like the freckles on her own arms.

But there was a second map.

Nell had seen it exactly twice, both times by accident, both times for less than three seconds. It was smaller than the survey, older, drawn on paper gone the color of weak tea, and it lived somewhere on Voss's person—in the leather satchel she slept with curled against her stomach like a child, or in the false bottom of the document case she carried even to dinner, even to the privy, which Nell felt was taking document security a shade too far.

The first time, Voss had been unrolling it at the map table when Rafael's voice called from outside, and the older map had vanished beneath the survey with a speed that would have shamed a card sharp.

The second time, Nell had walked into the tent to deliver the day's levels and caught Voss with both maps open side by side, her finger tracing a line on the old one—a line that matched nothing in the survey at all.

Voss had rolled it up before Nell could blink. "The levels, Miss Quill?"

"Forty-one and a quarter at the cut," Nell had said, and left, and spent the rest of the evening turning the image over in her head like a stone with something written on it.

Because the line Voss had been tracing didn't follow the gorge floor. It went *up*—up the gorge wall, to a spot where the survey showed nothing but solid rock and a shelf of scree.

* * *

The trouble with noticing things, Nell's mother used to say, is that you can't un-notice them. She'd said it about Nell's father's second hatbox, and she'd been right then too.

For four days Nell carried the stone around in her head, turning it, weighing it, looking for a harmless explanation. Family history, maybe. The Voss family had financed the original Kestrel Line—that much everyone knew; Voss mentioned it the way other people mentioned a coat of arms. Maybe the old map was her grandfather's, a first survey, abandoned. Engineers drew abandoned lines all the time. There was probably a very boring reason the line on the old map went up the wall instead of along the floor.

Probably.

Except.

Except that on the fifth day, coming down from the instrument station in the gray half-light before supper, Nell cut through the camp's edge to save herself two hundred yards of mud, and passed the open flap of Voss's tent, and heard her talking to someone in a voice Nell had never heard her use.

It was low. It was careful. It was the voice of a woman reading something aloud to herself to make sure she had it by heart.

"—forty-one, then the bend, then the twin pines gone but the stump on the north side still stands. The shelf above the third seep. *Below* the third seep, not above, the handwriting makes it plain—"

Nell stopped. She hadn't meant to stop. Her boots stopped for her.

Inside, the voice cut off.

Nell did the only thing her body could think of, which was to keep walking at exactly the same speed, as though she had never paused, as though the mud hadn't gripped her boot with an audible kiss, and she made it around the supply wagon before the tent flap moved.

But she had heard enough. Mile forty-one. The bend. The seeps.

Mile forty-one was where the cairn was. The cairn with the fresh flowers and the tin plate, the one Rafael had found and then said nothing about for a week, the one Nell had watched her boss stand beside in the rain with her hat off like a woman at a funeral.

Someone had been grieving at mile forty-one for years. And now Marguerite Voss was reciting directions to a spot near it, in the dark, alone.

Nell went and found Baptiste, because Baptiste was the camp's confessor by default—he fed people, and people tell their cooks things.

* * *

Baptiste was frying something. Baptiste was always frying something; it was his answer to weather, to grief, to boredom, and to the specific despair of high-altitude cooking, and whatever it was, it smelled like a decision Nell wanted to be part of.

"Sit," he said, without looking up. "You've got a face on you."

"I've always got a face on you. It's attached."

"This is a *new* face. This is a face doing arithmetic." He slid the pan off the fire and pushed a tin plate at her—the good plate, the one with the blue rim, which meant she was in real trouble or real luck. "Eat and confess."

Nell ate and confessed. She told him about the second map, the line up the gorge wall, the voice in the tent reciting mile markers and seeps like a prayer. She watched his broad, calm face the whole time, and she saw something flicker across it when she said *third seep*, quick as a fish under ice, and she'd have missed it if she hadn't been hunting.

"You knew that," she said. "The seeps. You knew."

"I know where water comes out of rocks. It's a gorge. They're everywhere." He said it easily, too easily, the way a man walks on ice he's tested and found thin.

"Baptiste."

"Nell." He turned the thing in the pan with more attention than it needed. "Here's what I know about maps. My father drew them. Coast surveys. He used to say a map is a confession—every line on it is somebody admitting what they want. So before you go accusing Miss Voss of anything, ask yourself what you'd do with a confession that wasn't yours to read."

"That's not an answer, that's a *saying*."

"Saying are answers that have been aged." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were kind and troubled in equal measure. "You like it here, don't you. The crew. The work. The—him." He tipped his head toward Rafael's tent, where lamplight showed a silhouette bent over drawings.

"You mean her, and yes."

"Then think on this. Everything that holds this crew together is holding on by a thread already. Okoye's counting days to the snow. Rafael's counting something else—I don't know what, but it isn't miles. And you're about to go poking at the one thing keeping the money coming." He handed her a second helping she hadn't asked for, which was his way of ending an argument. "Ask your questions. But ask them gentle. Gentle questions get answered. Pointed ones get people put off mountains."

Nell took the plate. "You're a very wise man."

"I'm a very tired cook. Same thing, up here."

* * *

The chance came on the ninth day, and it came, as chances do, dressed as an inconvenience.

A storm cell had parked itself over the western ridges all afternoon, and by evening the rain came down in ropes. Voss's tent—staked on the one patch of ground that didn't flood, because Voss had chosen her own campsite personally, which in retrospect was another stone for the pile—had taken water through a seam, and the document case had to be moved to the supply wagon where the dry goods slept under oilcloth.

Voss carried the case herself, hood up, head down, and set it inside the wagon, and then Rafael called her away—something about the trestle footings, something that couldn't wait, and Voss went, because Rafael was the one person whose summons she never questioned, a fact Nell had filed away and not yet examined.

The case sat in the wagon. The rain drummed. Nell stood in the lee of the wagon with her hand on the latch and her heart going like a stamp mill.

*Gentle questions get answered,* Baptiste had said. But gentle questions required the person you were asking to be present, and Voss was not present, and the case was, and Nell was nineteen and had never once in her life managed to leave a closed box closed. Her mother had a story about her at age four and a locked sewing basket that ended with the word *catastrophe*.

She opened it.

Under the contracts, under the payroll ledger, under the court papers she'd seen before, there it was: the old map, rolled in a sleeve of waxed silk that had no business protecting an abandoned first survey.

She carried it to the lantern hanging by the wagon's tailboard, unrolled it flat on the tailboard itself, and weighted the corners with four bolts.

It was not a first survey.

It was a hand-drawn chart, ink faded to brown, the paper soft as cloth. The gorge was there—the Kestrel Gorge, drawn by someone who knew it intimately, every bend and seep and shelf rendered in a sure, loving hand. But there was no track on it. No grade, no alignment, no mile posts in the engineer's sense. Instead, running along the gorge wall, was a single annotated line, and beside the annotations, small and careful, a set of instructions.

*Below the third seep. Two men can move the stone. What is owed is buried with what was taken.*

And there—there was the thing that stopped Nell's breath. In the corner of the map, in a different ink, a different hand, newer, was a small drawn symbol: a circle with a cross through it, and beneath it, in faded pencil, a name.

*T. Okonkwo.*

Nell knew that name. Everyone on the crew knew it, though almost nobody said it aloud. Rafael spoke of her brother the way she spoke of the bridge collapse—rarely, flatly, and with a stillness that made everyone find urgent business elsewhere.

The map was a treasure chart. Or a grave chart. And it pointed at the gorge wall a half mile above the cairn at mile forty-one, and Marguerite Voss had been memorizing it in the dark.

Nell heard the boots on the wet gravel and did not have time to be guilty.

* * *

"You have thirty seconds to explain why my documents are open," Voss said, "before I decide the explanation doesn't matter."

She had come alone through the rain, hood back, hair dark with water, and she didn't look angry. She looked worse than angry. She looked like a woman watching a wall crack in a dam she'd built.

Nell's mouth, which had never once in her life waited for her brain, said, "Your seam leaked."

"My seam leaked, so you opened a locked case."

"The latch wasn't locked."

"The latch was *latched*. There is a difference, Miss Quill, and it is the difference between a surveyor and a thief."

"I wasn't stealing it. I was reading it." Nell lifted her chin. She was cold, and wet, and caught, and some deep stubborn vein in her—the one that had gotten her through three years of being the only girl in every survey office between here and the coast—decided that if she was going to be hanged she'd be hanged for the whole crime. "It's a grave map, isn't it. Not a survey. It marks a burial in the gorge wall, above mile forty-one. And there's a name on it. T. Okonkwo."

For a moment the only sound was the rain on the oilcloth overhead, drumming like a thousand small impatient fingers.

Then Voss did something Nell didn't expect. She stepped under the wagon's tailboard, out of the rain, and looked down at the map—her map, the one she'd slept curled around for six weeks—and her face did something complicated. Grief and relief and fear, all trying to use the same features at once.

"Who else has seen this?" she asked quietly.

"No one. I came straight from the wagon to— well. I came straight from the wagon."

"Rafael?"

"No."

Voss exhaled, long and slow, and Nell watched six weeks of vigilance loosen by perhaps a single degree. "Then we may yet survive your curiosity." She reached out and touched the corner of the map, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was friendly. "You want the truth, Miss Quill. Very well. A partial one, since you've earned a partial one by breaking into my belongings in the rain like a raccoon."

"I'd argue, but the raccoon part is fair."

"This map belonged to my grandfather. He drew it. The other hand—" she touched the corner, the circle and cross, the name— "was added later, by a man my grandfather trusted. It marks where something was buried in the gorge wall during the first construction. Something that was *owed* to people who never received it. My family's fortune was built on this line, Miss Quill, and then lost when the provision lands were seized, and everything my family was promised—everything that was *taken* from us—is in that wall." She straightened. "It is family history. That is all I will say tonight, and more than I intended to say ever."

Nell looked at her. Voss was very good at looking steady; Nell would grant her that. But her hand, resting on the tailboard beside the map, was white-knuckled, and her eyes kept going to the name in the corner—*T. Okonkwo*—and flinching away from it, the way you flinch from a stove you've already burned yourself on.

"Family history," Nell said.

"Family history."

"Then why memorize it in the dark instead of showing it to your chief engineer? Rafael knows that gorge better than anyone alive. She grew up—" Nell stopped, because she'd suddenly seen it, the whole shape of it, the way you see the second figure in a puzzle drawing. "You haven't shown her. Six weeks, and you haven't shown *Rafael* a map of her own brother's grave."

The rain filled the silence for her.

"Miss Quill," Voss said at last, and her voice was quiet and cold and very controlled, "there are things on this mountain that will break Rafael Okonkwo if they arrive in the wrong order, and I am trying—I am *trying*—to make sure they arrive in the right one. You will not speak of this map to her. You will not speak of it to Okoye, or to Baptiste, though I suspect the cook already knows more than any of us would find comfortable. You will do your levels and you will keep your eyes on your instruments. Can I trust you?"

And there it was, the question, sitting in the rain between them like a coin on a rail.

Nell thought about Rafael at the cairn with her hat off. She thought about Okoye counting days. She thought about Baptiste's fish-under-ice flicker at the word *seep*, and about her mother and the sewing basket, and about how every person on this crew had come up the mountain carrying something they hadn't declared at the trailhead.

"I can keep quiet," Nell said. "That's not the same as trusting you."

"It rarely is," said Voss. "It will do."

She rolled up the map, returned it to its silk sleeve, latched the case, and walked back through the rain toward her tent, upright as a fencepost, and Nell stood under the wagon tailboard with the lantern hissing and her heart still hammering, and thought: *she didn't deny it was his grave. She didn't deny a single word of it. She just didn't say whose grave it is.*

* * *

She meant to keep quiet. She genuinely did. She lasted eleven hours.

It wasn't weakness, exactly. It was that at dawn, at the instrument station above the cut, Rafael handed her the day's target and said, "We'll push past forty-one today, if the rock allows it. Past the cairn." And then Rafael had looked up the gorge at the wall—up at the third seep, Nell realized, up at the exact shelf—and her face had done something Nell had never seen it do in six weeks of landslides and flooded camps and Voss's impossible schedule.

It had gone *afraid*.

Rafael Okonkwo, who rebuilt trestles in record time and stared down weather and once, reportedly, talked a mule off a cliff by sheer force of personality, was afraid of a shelf of rock. And Voss was memorizing the way to it in the dark, and lying about it by half, and Baptiste knew things he wasn't saying, and the snowline had come down the ridges overnight like a lid being lowered on a pot.

Nell set up her instrument. She took her first reading. Her hands were steady, which surprised her.

But all through the morning, as the crew drilled and blasted and inched toward mile forty-one, one thought kept pace with her like a dog beside a cart:

*Everybody on this mountain is walking toward the same wall, and only one of them knows what's buried in it—and she's lying to the rest of us about why.*

By noon the clouds had come down to meet the snow, and the light had gone the color of old tin, and Okoye stood at the edge of camp looking north with his thumbs in his belt, and said, to no one in particular, in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant:

"That's not a squall coming down the valley. That's the monsoon. And it's early."

Nell looked at the sky, and at the map's direction in her memory, and at Rafael's back as she walked the grade toward the cairn at forty-one—and understood that whatever was coming, it was coming faster than any of them had planned for.
