## 11. Storm Front

The rain came in the night like a debt collector who'd finally found the address.

Rafael woke to a sound she knew too well—not the polite patter of mountain weather but the flat, driving roar of rain that intended to stay. She lay in her bunk in the Mile Thirty-Three camp listening to it hammer the canvas roof and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading for two days.

Monsoons were supposed to arrive on schedule. The Kestrel range had kept its calendar for as long as men had been foolish enough to build railroads through it: landfall in the third week of the month, snow closing the high pass by the first hard frost. Every survey map, every almanac, every grizzled old trackman's memory agreed.

This was not the third week. This was nine days early, and it was not asking permission.

She dressed in the dark, boots squelching before she even got them laced, and pushed out into it.

The camp was already awake, or at least already lit—lanterns glowing yellow through sheets of water, figures moving between tents with the hunched, hurried posture of people who had just remembered everything they owned was made of canvas. The creek that had been a pleasant trickle yesterday, musical and harmless, now ran brown and muscular ten feet below the cook tent, carrying branches, topsoil, and what appeared to be Baptiste's entire woodpile.

"My stove wood," Baptiste said mournfully, appearing beside her under an oilcloth he wore like a cape. He watched a split log go spinning past. "Seasoned six months. Oak. It had dreams, Rafael."

"It'll have adventures instead."

"That is not comforting."

"Nothing about tonight is going to be comforting," Rafael said. "Get everyone up. Everything above ankle height. If it isn't nailed down, it goes in the tool car."

* * *

By lantern light they fought the water and mostly lost, but losing slowly counted for something. Okoye moved through the chaos like a man who had done this a dozen times and expected to do it a dozen more, directing Nell and the trackmen with short gestures, saving his voice. They raised the powder stores onto pallets, then onto crates, then onto the flatcar itself when the creek took another foot. They weighted the tent lines with rail scrap. They dug a diversion ditch in the dark, hip-deep in mud, and Rafael stood in it alongside everyone else because she had learned long ago that a crew will dig all night for a leader who digs beside them and maybe forty minutes for one who doesn't.

Nell worked next to her, bailing, hauling, swearing with impressive creativity for someone so young.

"You're smiling," Nell accused at one point, pausing to push wet hair out of her eyes.

"I'm not."

"You are. You're standing in a flooded ditch at three in the morning and you look *happy*."

Rafael considered this. There was probably something wrong with her. But there was a particular clarity that came from a crisis—the mind shedding everything unnecessary, leaving only the problem and the hands. For three years her problems had been internal, shapeless, the kind you couldn't shovel. This one had edges. You could fight this one.

"The rain doesn't care whose fault anything was," she said. "It's restful."

"That's the most depressing thing anyone's ever said to me, and I grew up around surveyors."

* * *

Dawn came gray and mean, and with it came the news from below.

The wire from the valley junction arrived at seven, relayed through the last working telegraph station down the line, and Rafael read it twice because the first time she'd hoped she'd misread it.

LOWER CAMPS FLOODED. TRESTLE FOUR WASHED OUT. ROAD IMPASSABLE. NO SUPPLY TRAINS UNTIL SPRING UNLESS LINE OPENED FROM YOUR END. GOOD LUCK.

She carried the telegram to where Voss stood under the awning of the survey tent, watching the rain with an expression Rafael couldn't read. Voss took the paper, read it, and set it down on the map table with terrible calm.

"Well," she said. "There's our answer."

"There's our *problem*."

"There's our answer," Voss repeated. "We've been debating whether to push for the summit before the pass closed. The debate is over. The pass has closed behind us." She tapped the map, at the upper gorge, where the contour lines crowded together like people sheltering from weather. "Snow's sealing the high route right now, if it hasn't already. The lower road is gone until spring. Which means—"

"Which means the only way out of this range is forward," Rafael finished. "Over the summit. On rails we lay ourselves."

"On rails we lay ourselves," Voss agreed. "In whatever window the weather gives us."

Rafael looked at the map and felt the trap close around her with an almost audible click. She thought of Okoye's warning—ten days, not twenty—and of the fresh dynamite crates stamped with the Voss company mark, and of the cairn at Mile Forty-One with its fresh flowers, and of every suspicion she'd been nursing since the foothills. She did not trust Marguerite Voss. She trusted her less every day.

But none of that mattered this morning, and that was the elegant cruelty of it. Suspicion required options. Options required a way out. And the way out had just washed down the valley along with Baptiste's oak.

* * *

Okoye heard her out without interrupting, which was unlike him, and then sat looking at the fire—he'd gotten one going in the cook tent's iron stove, small defiance against the wet—and said nothing for so long that Rafael started counting.

"You're doing arithmetic," she said finally.

"I'm always doing arithmetic. That's the job." He held up fingers. "Ten days of workable weather, being generous. Eleven miles of grade left, including the gorge wall and the summit cutting. Two hundred pounds of powder. A crew that slept four hours and will sleep worse tomorrow." He folded the fingers down one at a time. "And a man my age, who has personally dug friends out of avalanche slides on two mountains, looking at a schedule that requires us to be on the high viaduct during the first storm of the season."

"So you're saying we should turn back."

Okoye gave her a look of genuine pity. "Turn back to what? I can walk on water, girl, but I can't walk on trestle Four. It's in the river now, arguing with the fish." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I'm saying we should be honest about what we're doing. We're not racing the winter anymore. We're *in* the winter. Everything from here is borrowed time, and borrowed time charges interest." He poked the fire. "But yes. Forward. Because forward has a stove at the end of it, eventually, and backward has a river."

That was Okoye's whole philosophy in one sentence, Rafael thought. Forward has a stove.

* * *

They called a crew meeting at noon, such as noon could be identified in the murk. Forty-one souls crowded into the mess tent and the lee side of the tool car, dripping, steaming, passing around a single pot of Baptiste's coffee, which was strong enough to stand a spoon in and had, per Baptiste, "moral authority."

Rafael laid it out plainly, because they deserved plain. The lower camps were gone. The supply line was gone. Retreat was gone. The choice was to sit at Mile Thirty-Three eating through their stores until spring—which meant hunger by February, and no pay, and no story worth telling—or to drive for the summit while the weather allowed, finish the line, and ride the first train down the other side to warmth, wages, and the rest of their lives.

She expected argument. She got some—a trackman named Ferro wanted to know exactly how much food remained (Baptiste answered with a figure and a face that suggested the figure was optimistic), and someone else asked the question Rafael had been waiting for:

"What if the weather doesn't allow?"

Silence, then. Rain on canvas, filling it.

"Then we hole up in the summit tunnel," Rafael said. "It's bored through two hundred feet of granite. Granite doesn't care about monsoons. We've got powder, timber, and tools. We can wait out anything short of the end of the world, and then come down the far side when it clears."

"And if the far side is worse?" Ferro pressed.

"Then we'll be cold, hungry, and famous," Baptiste offered from the back. "Men have survived on less. Well—cold and hungry, anyway."

Someone laughed, and the laugh broke the tension the way a pick breaks rock—one blow, then the seam opens. The vote wasn't formal; it didn't need to be. It happened in shrugs and nods and the sound of men picking up their coats. By one o'clock the camp was striking itself, packing itself, pointing itself uphill.

Voss found Rafael at the head of the column as the first wagons groaned into motion.

"You didn't mention the tunnel option to me privately," Voss said. "You let them hear it with everyone else."

"They'd have smelled a private arrangement. Crews always know." Rafael glanced at her. "Besides, it's true. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"The tunnel holds twenty people comfortably. We're forty-one." Rafael let that sit. "So we don't stop unless we absolutely have to. Motivation, Miss Voss. The best engineering material there is."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered across Voss's face that might have been respect, or might have been the expression of a woman recalculating an equation whose variables had changed. Then it was gone, smoothed away, and she said only, "Very good, Mr. Okonkwo," and went to her own wagon.

Rafael watched her go and felt the old unease settle back over her shoulders like the rain. Trapped on a mountain with a woman she didn't trust, dependent on her supplies, committed to her schedule. Whatever game Voss was playing, the storm had just taken away every exit—including Rafael's own.

* * *

They made four miles that afternoon, which was heroic and insufficient. The grade steepened. The rain turned to sleet at the two-thousand-foot line, sleet to snow at twenty-five hundred, and by dusk the world had gone white and silent in that way that looks peaceful and means *run*. They camped in a rock cut with walls on three sides, built fires that smoked and spat, and posted double watch, because Okoye said avalanches didn't keep office hours.

Rafael walked the perimeter before turning in, checking the snow stakes, checking the animals, checking nothing in particular, really—checking because walking was thinking and thinking was unavoidable.

Nell fell into step beside her, muffled to the eyebrows.

"Mile Thirty-Six," Nell said. "Five miles from the marker."

"I know."

"Are you—" Nell hesitated. In the lantern light her breath hung in clouds. "When we get there. To Forty-One. Do you want me to—"

"Nell."

"Yes, ma'am."

"I haven't decided what I want. Ask me again when we're five miles past it." Rafael stopped at the cut's mouth and looked up, where the snow came down endlessly out of a black sky, erasing the mountain inch by inch, erasing the way back, erasing everything except the next step. Somewhere above them, at the summit cutting, something waited in the rock—Tomas, the deed, the truth, she still didn't know which, or in what order. Three years of running, and the mountain had simply picked her up and set her down in front of it.

No way back. Only through.

"Baptiste says there's stew," Nell offered.

"Then God is merciful after all." Rafael turned toward the fires. "Come on. Tomorrow we climb."

Behind them the snow kept falling, patient as a creditor, burying the road home one flake at a time.
