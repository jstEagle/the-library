## 12. Baptiste Confesses

The rain came in sideways off the escarpment like it had a grudge, and Baptiste stood in the cook tent doorway watching it try to drown the world.

He had cooked through storms before. He'd cooked through a hurricane in Port-au-Prince harbor, through a blizzard in the Yukon where the flour froze solid and he'd made biscuits anyway out of pure spite. He liked to tell people that weather was just God's way of testing whether you'd seasoned properly. But this rain was different. This rain sounded like the end of something.

Behind him, the camp stove hissed and spat, fighting a losing war against the damp. The lower camps were flooded — Okoye had come down at dawn with water to his knees and a face like a man reading his own obituary, saying the upper pass was already white, already closing, that retreat was a word for other people now. The only way out of these mountains was forward, through the gorge, over the summit. Through the place Rafael Okonkwo had sworn off for three years and then walked back into anyway.

Baptiste stirred the pot. Rice again. He'd promised the crew gumbo when they reached the summit, and God willing he'd keep that promise, though lately God seemed less willing than usual.

Rafael came in at midday, soaked to the skin, her survey satchel held under her coat like a rescued child. She didn't complain about the wet. She never complained about anything, which was one of the things Baptiste liked about her, and one of the things that had been eating him alive for six weeks.

"Smells good," she said, which was a lie, because the rice was rice.

"It smells like patience," Baptiste said. "Sit. Eat. You're thin as a rail spike."

"I'm exactly as thin as I've always been."

"You were thinner in Ashcombe." He said it lightly. He said everything lightly; that was his armor, and it had served him well for sixty-one years. "I remember. You used to come through my mess car and eat two plates and pretend it was one."

Something flickered across her face at the name. *Ashcombe.* She sat down on the bench with her back to the canvas wall and took the plate he offered, and for a while there was only the rain and the wind and the small domestic sounds of an engineer eating bad rice because it was hot.

Baptiste dried dishes. It gave his hands something to do while his heart argued with itself.

* * *

Here is what Baptiste knew, and had known since the day he signed Voss's contract and saw the name of the line: Kestrel. Here is what he had not said, through foothills and trestles and mile markers and storms, through every night Rafael had sat by his fire with her boots off and her guard down, trusting him the way you trust a man who feeds you:

He had been there. Not at the gorge — at Ashcombe Bridge, three years ago, the day it came down. He'd worked the rival crew, the Corvane Construction outfit, the one that got the contract Rafael's crew should have gotten and lost by politics. And he had watched, from the far bank, from behind a cook fire he'd built himself, as the bridge that Rafael Okonkwo had designed and inspected and certified with her own name — watched that bridge fold like wet paper with forty men on it.

And he had known, even then, even before the inquiry, why it folded. Because his own crew chief, a man named Duvall with a laugh like a rusty hinge, had come to the cook fire two weeks earlier and said, quiet, not looking at anybody: *We're using the second-grade steel on the anchorages. Company says so. You didn't hear it.*

Company. Which company? Everyone had assumed Corvane. Baptiste had assumed Corvane too, for three years, and hated himself comfortably, the way you hate yourself for small sins. It was only last month, up here in the cold, when he'd seen the dynamite crates stamped with the Voss mark, and heard Nell talking about Marguerite Voss's family fortune, old money, railroad money, money that had gone into the Kestrel Line and the provision lands both — it was only then that the shape of it had rearranged itself in his head like furniture in the dark.

Duvall hadn't said *Corvane says so.* Duvall had said *the company says so*, and Duvall had taken his orders from a fixer, a smooth gray man named Halloway, who was — Baptiste had confirmed it with his own eyes two weeks ago, watching from the woodpile — currently embedded in this very crew, calling himself a track hand, keeping his hands clean and his eyes busy.

The same Halloway. The same gray man. The same company.

Which meant the steel that killed Tomas Okonkwo and thirty-nine others had been ordered cheap by the same family whose daughter had hired Rafael to reopen the line through the gorge where the boy was buried. Which meant the report that blamed Rafael — the bought report, the testimony that ended her career — had been purchased by the people who needed someone else to carry the weight of their own corner-cutting.

Baptiste had carried this knowledge up the mountain like a stone in his boot. Every mile, it had ground a little deeper. And now the storm had closed the pass behind them, and there was no more *later*. Later had drowned in the flood. There was only now, and a woman eating his bad rice with her dead brother's name written all over her tired face.

"Baptiste," she said, without looking up. "You've been staring at me for ten minutes. Either I have rice on my chin or you're working up to something."

He set down the dish towel. His hands, big hands, hands that had fed half the rail crews west of the mountains, were not quite steady.

"Both," he said. "You've got rice on your chin. And I'm working up to something."

She brushed her chin. Waited.

He sat down across from her. The stove popped. Outside, the rain kept up its work of erasing the world.

"When I was a younger man," he began, "I learned a rule in my mother's kitchen. She said: *a lie told kindly is still a stone in the soup.* You can chew around it for a while. Sooner or later, somebody breaks a tooth."

Rafael set down her fork. "Baptiste—"

"Let me finish, girl, or I'll lose my nerve, and I'm too old to start over." He took a breath. "I wasn't always a drifter cook. Before that I was a drifter cook with a different name and a different crew. Corvane Construction. The year Ashcombe went down."

Her face did something complicated. Not anger — not yet. A kind of careful stillness, like water before it freezes.

"I know," she said quietly. "Nell found the pay stubs in your kit when she was looking for thread. She told me in the foothills. I asked her to say nothing until I decided what it meant."

Baptiste blinked. Then he laughed — a short, broken sound, mostly air. "Of course she did. That girl finds everything. All right. Well. That saves me the first confession and leaves me the worse ones." He rubbed his face. "Ask me why I was there."

"You were cooking for Corvane. That's public record. Half the inquiry witnesses ate your food."

"And did the record say how close my cook fire was to the bridge?"

"No."

"Two hundred yards. Far bank. I saw it come down. I saw the anchorages go first — not the span, not the piers. The anchorages. I knew what that meant, because two weeks before, my crew chief told me we'd swapped the grade-two steel into our own anchorage order, and I thought — fool that I was — I thought that meant *our* bridge. Our half-built abutment on the north approach. I didn't know the orders had been changed again. I didn't know the second-grade steel had been routed to *your* anchorage shipment instead, to hide it, to make the swap disappear into a consignment nobody would inspect twice." His voice cracked on the last words and he let it. "Your inspection was honest, Rafael. Your steel was not. Somebody switched the manifests, and the switch was made to protect the theft, and the theft was not Corvane's idea."

The rain filled the silence. Rafael had gone very pale, but her eyes were steady on him, and Baptiste thought: *she has spent three years believing she killed her brother, and I am about to take that weight off her and put down something heavier in its place, and I do not know if that is mercy or murder.*

"Say it," she whispered.

"The order came from above Corvane. From the financing side. Duvall took his instructions from a fixer — a gray man, soft-spoken, called Halloway. Halloway answered to the family holding the bonds on the whole corridor. The Voss family. When the bridge fell, the same Halloway arranged the inquiry. Bought the engineers. Bought the testimony. Made sure the report landed on the one person whose signature was on the certification, because a dead scapegoat can't be cross-examined but a ruined one can be silenced." He spread his hands. "I didn't know any of it for certain until this mountain. Until I saw Halloway's face in our own camp, wearing our own crew colors. Then every piece fit, and I have been sitting on it like a man sitting on a lit stove, telling myself the burn would fade."

* * *

For a long time Rafael didn't move.

Baptiste had imagined this moment a hundred ways. He'd imagined her rage — the throwing kind, the kind that puts fists through tent walls. He'd imagined tears. He'd imagined her walking out into the rain and him having to go after her, an old fat man chasing an engineer through a monsoon, which would have been undignified but necessary.

What he got was worse. She sat perfectly still, and her face emptied, and then — slowly, horribly — it refilled with arithmetic. He could see her doing it. Three years of grief recalculating. Thirty-nine funerals reassigning their blame. Her father's disgrace, her own exile, the miles she'd put between herself and everything warm — all of it suddenly unmoored from its cause and drifting, looking for somewhere new to land.

"The report," she said finally. Her voice was flat as a level. "They wrote the report before the steel was even cold. I read it. I read it a hundred times looking for the error *I* must have made. The load calculations. The rivet schedule. I memorized my own guilt like scripture." She looked at her hands. "There was no error. There was a purchase order."

"There was a purchase order," Baptiste said gently.

"And Voss knows." It wasn't a question. "Voss hired me. Voss brought me back to this range, to this gorge, knowing — knowing what her family did, knowing what the report cost me—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Why would she do that?"

"That," Baptiste said, "is a question I've been chewing since the crates. And I'll tell you what an old cook knows about rich families, Miss Okonkwo, which is considerable, because I've fed most of them. Rich children either inherit their parents' sins or spend their whole lives trying to buy them back. Marguerite Voss strikes me as the buying kind. But a woman can want to return stolen goods and still lie about why she wants *you* carrying them." He leaned forward. "There's something she hasn't told us. Something in those papers she keeps reading by lamplight. I don't know what. But I know the smell of a kitchen where somebody's hiding an ingredient."

Rafael laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and short, halfway to something else. "An ingredient."

"My mother's rule. Everything comes back to the soup eventually."

Then the stillness broke, and what came out of Rafael Okonkwo was not rage after all, or not only rage. It was vertigo. She gripped the edge of the table with both hands, and Baptiste realized the tent was steady and the floor was steady and it was the woman who was tilting, three years of certainty sliding out from under her all at once.

"If it wasn't me," she said. "If it was never me. Then Tomas—" Her breath caught. "He died for a *manifest entry*. For a few hundred dollars of difference in steel grade. He died so some clerk wouldn't have to explain a discrepancy." She pushed back from the table and stood, and stood, and stood, as if standing were a problem she was solving.

Baptiste rose too, slowly, joints announcing themselves. "Rafael."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't comfort me yet. I need to be angry in the right direction first, and I've been aiming wrong for three years, and I don't trust my aim." She turned to face him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely ferocious. "Halloway. He's here. In this camp. Tonight."

"Yes."

"And Voss doesn't know what he is. Or she does, and she's playing a game I can't see the board of."

"Either way," Baptiste said, "we are standing on her game board, in a storm, with the pass closed behind us and the summit ahead, and the man who helped kill your brother is sleeping forty feet from this tent."

Rafael wiped her face with the heel of her hand — one rough motion, done, filed away, the engineer resuming command of herself by main force. "Okoye needs to know. Nell. We do this carefully, Baptiste. If Halloway spooks, we're forty miles from anywhere with a murderer in the crew and a river in flood below us."

"He's not a murderer," Baptiste said. "He's worse. He's a bookkeeper. Murderers feel something afterward."

That almost got a smile out of her. Almost.

She picked up her oilskin and paused at the tent flap, rain roaring beyond it, and for a moment she looked young and lost and twenty-six years old again, the way she'd looked coming through his mess car at Ashcombe, before any of it.

"Baptiste. Why now? Why not in the foothills, or at the trestle — why carry it this far?"

He thought about lying kindly. He thought about stones in soup.

"Because every day I waited, I got to keep being the man who feeds you," he said. "And the day I told you, I became the man who watched. I'm a selfish old man, girl. I bought six weeks of being loved on credit. The bill came due tonight."

Rafael looked at him for a long moment. Then she crossed the tent and put her arms around him, brief and fierce, rice-and-rain smelling, trembling slightly, and said against his shoulder: "The bill's paid. Now help me collect from everybody else."

She ducked out into the storm. Baptiste stood alone with his cold stove and his lighter heart and his hands that had finally stopped shaking, and through the canvas he heard her boots squelch toward Okoye's tent — fast, purposeful, a woman with a direction at last.

Above the camp, invisible in the rain, the snowline crept down the mountain another foot. And in the track crew's tent, a lamp went out a little too quickly, as if someone inside had been listening.
