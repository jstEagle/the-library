## 13. The Court Order

The rain had found a way through everything.

Rafael stood in the supply tent with her coat still dripping, watching water trace a dark line down the center pole and pool around a crate of blasting caps that Okoye had personally threatened three men to keep dry. Outside, the monsoon hammered the tarps like it held a grudge. Inside, the lantern swung on its hook, throwing Marguerite Voss's shadow long across the survey table—long, and trembling, because the tent flap was still moving where Baptiste had just walked out after saying what he'd said.

*The Ashcombe Bridge didn't fail by accident.*

*His old crew cut corners on your family's orders, Miss Okonkwo. And the report that blamed you was bought.*

Voss hadn't moved since Baptiste left. She stood at the far end of the table with both hands flat on the map case, as if she were holding down something that wanted to fly away. Her hair, usually pinned with military precision, had gone loose in the humidity, one gray strand cutting across her cheek. Rafael had never seen her look anything but armored. Now she looked like a woman standing in a house whose foundations had just been pulled out from under her—and realizing she'd helped pull them.

"Say it again," Rafael said quietly. "I want to hear it from you."

Voss's jaw worked. "You heard him."

"I heard a cook who used to work for a rival crew confess to a crime he didn't commit alone. I heard him say *Voss's family's orders*. That's you, Marguerite. Or your father. I want to know which."

For a long moment there was only the rain, and the lantern creaking on its hook, and somewhere out in the dark the river running high and brown through the gorge where Tomas lay under thirty feet of rock and eleven years of silence.

Then Voss reached into her breast pocket—the same pocket where she kept the contract, the pay schedule, the weather telegrams—and drew out an envelope. Not paper. Oilcloth, tied with string, worn soft at the corners the way documents get when they've been carried against a body for years.

"My father died four years ago," she said. "Did you know that?"

"No."

"He died in a chair by a window, in a house full of lawyers, and not one of them loved him." A short, bitter breath that might have been a laugh. "I sat with him the last week. He talked the whole time—morphine makes talkative men of the careful ones—and do you know what he never once said? *I'm sorry.* He told me where things were. He told me which judges owed us. He told me how much the provision lands would be worth once the rail came through. He gave me instructions like a man dictating a will to his own conscience, and his conscience wasn't home."

She untied the string with fingers that weren't quite steady.

"Baptiste is right about the bridge. I didn't know it then—I was twenty-six, in London, being educated at my family's expense while they cut corners at my family's direction. But I've known for six years. I found the invoices. The inferior iron. The inspection that was paid to pass. And I found this."

She laid the oilcloth packet on the map case between them and did not open it.

"A court order," Rafael said. Her voice came out flat. "What court order?"

"The one that made Ashcombe possible." Voss finally looked up, and her eyes in the lantern light were dry and terrible. "Your father filed the original claim on the provision lands, Rafael. Twelve hundred acres, deeded fair, witnessed proper. My family wanted that land—it sat square across the only viable grade through the escarpment. So my father went to Judge Corliss, and Corliss issued a ruling declaring the Okonkwo deed fraudulent. Fraudulent," she repeated, tasting the word. "He ruled that your father had forged his own inheritance. There was a hearing. There was testimony. There was a man who swore he'd witnessed a signature your father couldn't have made, because the man swearing it had been dead two years."

The tent seemed to shrink. Rafael gripped the edge of the table and felt the wood bite her palms and was grateful for it, because the alternative was that the floor moved.

"They took the land," she said. "And then they took the bridge."

"And then they took you." Voss's voice dropped. "When the bridge failed, the inquiry needed someone to blame who wasn't connected to the financing. Your name was already on the engineering reports—you'd signed off on the load calculations, because you were honest, and honest people sign their work. Corliss wrote the finding himself. Three years ago you lost your license, your crew, and every contract east of the mountains. I testified at that inquiry, Rafael. I read their figures into the record because I believed them. Because believing them was easier than asking why the figures felt wrong."

"You could have asked."

"Yes." No flinch, no excuse. Just the word, bare as stripped rail. "I could have asked. And instead I built a career on the ground your ruin cleared for me. Every line I've run since Ashcombe runs over you. I have told myself for six years that I was going to make it right. I have also told myself that making it right and profiting from it could be the same act, if I was careful about the arithmetic." She shook her head slowly. "My father taught me that arithmetic. It doesn't balance. It has never balanced."

Rafael looked down at the oilcloth packet. Rain drummed. Somewhere beyond the canvas, Nell's voice rose and fell, talking the night watch through the storm, keeping the young ones calm the way Rafael herself should have been doing.

"Tomas," Rafael said. "Tell me about Tomas."

Voss closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she spoke like a woman reciting a confession she'd rehearsed in the dark for years.

"When Corliss voided the deed, your father appealed. The appeal needed the original document—the signed, sealed, witnessed deed itself—to present before the territorial court. Your father sent Tomas to retrieve it. Tomas had hidden it, or been given it to hide—I don't know which, and I have spent four years not knowing, which is its own punishment. What I know is this: Tomas never delivered it. He went up into the gorge with the deed, and he died there, and the land seizure was finalized on the assumption that the document was destroyed or buried past finding." She paused. "My father assumed. My father assumed a great deal. But he hired men to search the rockfall twice, and they found nothing, and so he stopped looking and started building—because the Kestrel Line, driven through the escarpment, would bury the question forever under forty miles of ballast and steel. Possession is nine-tenths. A railroad is the tenth."

"And now you're finishing his railroad."

"I'm finishing his *search*." Voss spread her hands over the map case, over the inked contour lines of the gorge. "The deed is in that wall, Rafael. With your brother. Everything—the proof that clears your father's name, the proof that clears yours, the proof that takes twelve hundred acres out of my family's hands and puts it back where it belongs—all of it is lying in the rockfall at mile forty-one, wrapped in whatever Tomas wrapped it in, waiting for someone with the right to dig."

The lantern swung. Rafael became aware, distantly, that she was crying—not sobbing, nothing so generous, just tears running steadily down her face while she stared at the map, and she let them, because there was no one here she needed to perform strength for except the one person she least wanted to.

"Eleven years," she said. "Eleven years I thought he was just—gone. An accident inside an accident. The gorge took him the way gorges take men, and I put a marker there and I never came back, because coming back meant—" Her voice caught. "I measured my life in miles so I wouldn't have to measure it in years. Do you understand that? Three years since Ashcombe. Eleven since Tomas. And the whole time, the answer to both was sitting in the same hole in the mountain."

"Not the answer," Voss said softly. "The evidence. The answer is what we do with it."

"That's a lawyer's distinction."

"I'm the daughter of a man who bought a judge. I contain multitudes of lawyers." For the first time all night, something flickered across her face that was almost humor—grim, exhausted, but real. "It's how I know the difference."

Rafael wiped her face with the heel of her hand and made herself ask the question underneath all the others, the one that had been climbing her spine since Baptiste walked out into the rain.

"Why tell me? You could have dug for it yourself. You had the map—you've always had the map, don't bother denying it, Nell saw it days ago. You could have taken a few men up the wall in the storm and never said a word. Instead you're standing in a wet tent handing me the noose your family tied. Why?"

Voss was quiet for a moment. Then she turned the oilcloth packet over and pushed it across the table until it touched Rafael's fingers.

"Because my father's last instruction was to find that deed and destroy it," she said. "And I have carried those instructions for four years like a stone, telling myself I'd obey them when the time came, because obedience was cheaper than treason. And then I watched you at the first trestle. And at mile forty-one, when you found that cairn with the fresh flowers and you stood there like a woman struck by lightning and grief at the same time—I watched you, and I understood that I had spent my whole life becoming my father, and that you had spent yours refusing to become the liar they said you were." Her voice roughened. "I am tired, Rafael. I am so tired of carrying a name that was purchased with a forged ruling and a dead man's bridge. If that deed comes out of the wall, it burns my family's claim to ash. It may burn mine too. Good. Let it. I'd rather be nobody with clean hands than a Voss with these ones."

Rafael looked down at the packet. Slowly, she untied the string.

Inside was a single sheet, yellowed, folded twice: a certified copy of the Corliss ruling, with the seal half-faded and the margins annotated in a cramped male hand—her father's? Voss's father's?—and beneath the legal language, in the same hand, a private note:

*Original instrument unrecovered. Believed interred with T. Okonkwo, Gorge of Provision. Do not permit recovery. H.*

Halloway. Even the initial landed like a blow. Rafael remembered the quiet man with the company pin who mended fences and watched everything, and felt the temperature of the tent drop ten degrees.

"There's a man in your crew," Rafael said slowly, "who isn't in your crew."

"Halloway. Yes." Voss's mouth thinned. "My father's fixer. He's been reporting to interests above my father for longer than I've been alive—judges, land barons, men whose names aren't on any deed because they own the deeds' graves. If they learn what we know, they won't send lawyers." She met Rafael's eyes. "They'll send him."

Outside, the rain redoubled, and beneath it—faint, maybe imagined—Rafael thought she heard the crunch of boots moving away from the tent line, unhurried, going nowhere in particular.

She folded the court order along its old creases and put it inside her coat, against her chest, where it lay cold as a rail spike.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we vote. All of us. The line or the dig—Okoye will insist on it, and he'll be right. But Marguerite—" Rafael paused, and found, to her surprise, that the name came easier than it had in fifteen years of rivalry. "Whatever that vote says, that wall comes open. I'm not leaving my brother in there another winter. And I am not leaving the truth in there with him."

Voss nodded once, and for a moment they simply stood on opposite sides of the survey table, two women shaped by the same crime from opposite ends of it—mirror images, one raised to profit from the theft, one raised to pay for it—while the storm leaned on the tent and the river rose in the dark toward the place where Tomas waited with the paper that could burn two families' names to ash and build something new from the ruins.

From outside came Nell's voice, sharp with alarm: "Miss Okonkwo! You'd better come see this—somebody's been at the tool cache!"

Rafael was already moving.
