## 14. Divided Crew

Okoye had been in the blasting trade for thirty-one years, and in that time he had learned one law more reliable than any of Newton's: a charge that is not placed exactly where it ought to be will find its own opinion about the matter. Men were much the same. You could pack a crew tight and tamp them down and hope for the best, but sooner or later somebody's fuse would burn crooked, and then you had a very loud afternoon on your hands.

He could hear it coming now. The mess tent was too quiet.

A quiet mess tent, after a day like this one, was not peace. It was the silence of a powder magazine with a lit lantern sitting beside it.

* * *

The storm had shut them in. That was the plain arithmetic of it. Snow above, flood below, and somewhere between the two, thirty miles of half-built railroad that Marguerite Voss had promised would make them all rich enough to retire to somewhere warm and flat. Okoye had retired twice already. Both times had lasted eleven days. He'd come back because retirement, he'd discovered, was mostly just waiting around to die with better furniture, and he preferred his waiting done outdoors, among people who shouted.

But tonight nobody was shouting. They were all sitting in the mess tent pretending to eat Baptiste's stew — which was excellent, as always, and entirely wasted on them — while the thing Rafael had told them an hour ago sat in the middle of the canvas floor like unexploded ordnance.

There was a deed buried in the gorge wall. With Tomas Okonkwo's remains. Proof that the provision lands had been stolen, proof that Rafael's father had been framed, proof that the Ashcombe report — the report that had ended Rafael's career and started every ghost currently haunting this mountain — had been bought and paid for by Voss's family.

And Voss wanted it. And Halloway's men were still out there in the white dark, wanting it never found. And the pass would close in a matter of hours, or days if the weather turned generous, and the weather on this mountain had never once been generous in the whole history of weather.

Somebody had to say something before the whole crew went up like a misfired shot. Okoye sighed, set down his spoon, and did what he always did when a situation needed structure: he made it worse first, so it could get better after.

"Right," he said. "Everyone stop chewing like guilty goats and look at me."

* * *

They looked at him. Nell first, quick and birdlike, her surveyor's notebook still clutched in one hand as though numbers might save them. Then Baptiste, enormous and slow-moving at the end of the bench, his face doing the thing it did when he was carrying something heavy behind it. Then the rest — the track layers, the mule drivers, the two brothers from the lower valley whose names Okoye had finally learned last week and immediately gotten wrong again. It was Ferro and Ferro. Or Ferra. He'd sort it out at the funeral, whichever came first.

Rafael sat apart, near the stove, the way she always did. Not above them — Okoye had known men who sat above their crews, and they were all dead of being wrong — but apart, the way a keel sits apart from the sails. She hadn't touched her food. Her hands were flat on the table, and Okoye, who had spent three decades reading rock faces for the fault lines hidden inside them, read her hands instead. There were fault lines in them tonight.

"Here's where we are," Okoye said, standing, because a man his age could not deliver judgment sitting down without looking like he was complaining about his knees. "We are sealed in. Above us, snow. Below us, a river wearing its Sunday clothes. Behind us, nothing. Ahead of us, a summit cutting, a viaduct, and about forty hours of work we do not have forty hours to do."

"Thank you for the cheerful summary," said Ferro the elder.

"You're welcome. Now here's what we're actually arguing about, since everyone's too polite to say it." He let his gaze go around the tent. "Half of you want to finish the line. Get paid. Walk out of here with wages instead of a lawsuit. The other half want to take those drills we've been using on rock and point them at the gorge wall instead, and dig until we find whatever's buried in there with Tomas Okonkwo." He paused. "And both halves are right, which is the worst kind of argument there is."

Silence. Even the storm seemed to lean in against the canvas.

Nell spoke first, because Nell always spoke first; it was one of the things Okoye liked about her, in the way you like a spark trap on a detonator — alarming, but better than the alternative. "If we finish the line," she said, "we open the provision lands to the companies. That's what rail does. Rail is how land gets taken. We'd be laying the road for the theft to be finished properly."

"And if we don't finish the line," said Ferro the elder, "we don't get paid, and my brother's daughter doesn't get her operation paid for, and I'll tell you what, missy, righteousness doesn't set bones."

"It's Ferra's daughter?" Okoye asked.

"Ferro," both brothers said together, with the weariness of men who had corrected him a hundred times.

"My apologies. Ferro. I'll carve it into my arm tonight."

That got a small laugh, which was the point. A laugh was a pressure valve. Okoye had defused more arguments with a bad joke than with good reasoning, because good reasoning required people to listen and bad jokes only required them to groan, and groaning was at least a shared activity.

Baptiste rumbled then, low, like a river under ice. "I worked a crew once that finished a job we shouldn't have." He didn't elaborate. He never elaborated. But the tent went quieter, because everyone had heard the shape of the confession underneath it, even if they hadn't heard the words.

* * *

It went on like that for the better part of an hour. Okoye let it. That was the other thing thirty-one years taught you: you don't cut a fuse short because you're impatient. You let it burn at its proper rate.

Arguments rose and fell. The track layer named Sosimo, who had a wife in the foothill town and a debt attached to the wife, argued for the line with real eloquence. Nell argued for the deed with the fervor of the young, who believe justice is a matter of deciding quickly. One of the mule drivers — Okoye thought his name might be Anselm, or possibly it was the mule that was Anselm — pointed out, reasonably, that they didn't actually know where in the gorge wall the cache was, and that digging blind through frozen rock in a blizzard was less a plan than a method of dying with purpose.

Through all of it, Rafael said nothing.

Okoye watched her the way he watched a face of granite he was about to trust with a bridge abutment. She wasn't silent the way a man is silent when he has nothing to say. She was silent the way a charge is silent in its hole — everything packed in, tamped down, waiting on a decision about the firing.

Finally she looked up, and her eyes found Okoye's, and he understood that she was asking him for the thing he'd stood up to give: structure. A frame to hang the decision on, so it wouldn't tear the tent down when it moved.

"All right," Okoye said. "We vote. That's how civilized people settle things — badly, but together. Everyone here gets one voice. Miss Voss gets one too, if she wants it, since she's paying the bills and eating our stew." He glanced toward the corner where Marguerite Voss sat wrapped in a coat three sizes too fine for this mountain, and something passed across her face that Okoye chose, charitably, not to identify. "But understand the terms before you vote, because I'm only running this election once. If the vote says finish the line, we finish the line — all the way, fast, no sulking, no sabotage, no midnight drilling expeditions. If the vote says dig, we dig, and every man who voted the other way still gets his share of whatever we find, because we are a crew and not a parliament. Either way, nobody walks off into that storm alone, because dead men collect no wages and win no lawsuits. Are we understood?"

"Aye," said the tent, in nine different accents.

"One more term." Okoye's voice dropped, and the room came with it. "Halloway's men are still on this mountain. Whatever we choose, we choose knowing there are guns out there that have opinions about the deed. So whichever way this goes, we move like professionals and we watch each other's backs, because the company that sent Halloway does not care how we voted."

* * *

They voted by raising hands, because paper could be stolen and voices could be denied, but a raised hand in a small tent was a fact.

For the line: Sosimo, both Ferros, Anselm-or-the-mule, the cook's helper Piet, and old Wrenn, the track walker, who raised his hand slowly and said, "I've got a granddaughter who thinks I'm a hero, and heroes come home employed."

For the deed: Nell, Baptiste, and — after a long moment — Voss herself, her hand rising like something she was lifting against its own weight.

Seven to three, roughly, depending on how you counted the mule.

All eyes went to Rafael.

She stood. Okoye watched her stand, and it occurred to him, with the clarity that comes to a man late in life and high on a mountain, that he had spent three decades setting charges in stone and had never once seen anything break the way a person breaks and keeps standing anyway.

"I'm going to tell you the truth," Rafael said, "because you've earned it and because lying to a crew in a storm is how crews die. Part of me wants to put a drill through that gorge wall right now. Part of me has wanted to for three years, and I didn't even know it until yesterday." Her voice held steady, but Okoye saw her hands close, once, and open again. "Tomas is in that rock. My father's name is in that rock. Everything they took from us is in that rock, and every hour I spend laying rail toward the summit instead of digging for it feels like walking past his grave to catch a train."

Nobody breathed.

"But here's what I know about trains," she went on. "You can't jump a gap halfway. If we abandon the line now, we lose the viaduct, we lose the summit approach, we lose any chance of getting out of these mountains alive, and Halloway's men pick us off in the drifts one at a time while we dig with rock hammers. The deed does no one any good at the bottom of a grave next to another grave." She looked at Nell, and something softened in her face. "So we finish the line. All the way to the summit cutting. Because the summit cutting is *in the gorge wall*, and the last hundred yards of track runs within forty feet of where Tomas died — and when we drill that final section, we will be drilling into the same face that holds what we're looking for. The line and the deed aren't two roads, Nell. They're the same road. We just have to lay it in order."

Okoye felt the tent change. He'd felt it before, in tunnel crews, in the moment when a hopeless drive suddenly shows daylight ahead — not reached yet, but *aimed at*. Hope with a bearing on it.

"So the vote stands," Rafael said. "We build. And when we reach the summit cutting — the last blast of the job — that charge goes where I say it goes, and what it opens, we open together." She sat down.

Voss was watching her with an expression Okoye had last seen on the face of a man watching another man handle his detonator box. Not distrust, exactly. Something more like recognition.

* * *

Later, much later, when the tent had emptied and the storm had taken over the conversation, Okoye stepped outside to check his stores, which was what he told people, and to smoke his pipe, which was the truth. The cold hit him like a bill collector. Above the camp, the clouds had torn open for a moment, and the stars over the escarpment burned hard and indifferent, the way stars do at altitude, like pinholes in tin.

Boots crunched. Rafael, coatless, which was either bravery or a woman who had stopped caring whether her body continued, coming to stand beside him.

"That speech," Okoye said, around his pipe stem. "Was it true?"

"Which part?"

"The part where the line and the deed are the same road."

Rafael was quiet long enough that the wind filled in for her. "It's true that the summit cutting is in the gorge wall," she said at last. "It's true that the last section runs close to where Tomas died. Whether the cache is reachable from the cutting — I won't know until we're standing in front of the rock."

"And if it isn't? If the cutting misses it by fifty feet of solid granite?"

"Then I'll have led my crew to build a railroad for the people who stole everything from my family," Rafael said, "and I'll have called it strategy." She looked up at the black shoulder of the escarpment. "Ask me tomorrow, Okoye. Ask me tomorrow whether I meant it."

He puffed his pipe and considered her. Thirty-one years of reading faces of rock, and this one he could read fine. There was a crack running through her from the Ashcombe Bridge to this exact patch of snow, and cracks in loaded material go one of two ways: they propagate, or they get filled.

"Well," he said, knocking out his pipe against his boot heel, "for what it's worth, I voted for the line."

"I noticed."

"Not because I don't care about your brother. Because I've buried friends under avalanches, and I know the difference between a rescue and a reckoning. A reckoning can wait twelve hours. A rescue can't." He shrugged into the wind. "Also, I'm owed four months' wages, and I intend to live to be unreasonable about collecting them."

That got the smallest ghost of a smile out of her, which in current conditions counted as a festival.

They stood together a moment longer, engineer and blaster, watching the mountain decide their future at its own pace. Then Rafael pushed off the rail of the supply sled and headed for her tent, and Okoye turned to go check his powder stores properly this time, because tomorrow they'd need every stick of it —

— and that was when he saw the light.

Far up the slope, where no camp was, where no camp should be: a single lantern, moving. Traversing along the contour of the gorge wall, slow and deliberate, the way a man moves when he is looking for something specific in the dark.

Not toward the summit. Toward the wall.

Okoye stood very still in the cold and did the arithmetic he least wanted to do. Halloway's men weren't waiting for them to find the cache.

They were trying to find it first — and they had a head start, and a lantern, and all night.
