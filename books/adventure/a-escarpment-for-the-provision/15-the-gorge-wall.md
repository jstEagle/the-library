## 15. The Gorge Wall

The gorge announced itself before we saw it, the way grief does.

First there was the sound—the river running louder through the narrowing walls, a white noise that swallowed conversation. Then the smell: wet stone and cold iron and something green and ancient that grew in the cracks where sunlight gave up by mid-afternoon. And finally, when we rounded the last shoulder of the escarpment, there it was—the Gorge of Provision, cut two hundred feet down into gray granite, with the river at its throat and the old grade shelf clinging to the wall like a scar that never quite healed.

I had told myself I wouldn't look. I looked.

The wreck site was still visible from the grade, three miles upriver where the old trestle had come down in the flood of '89. There was nothing left of it now but stumps of pilings standing in the water like rotten teeth, and a scatter of ironwork along the banks that the river kept and returned and kept again, patient as a creditor.

"Miss Okonkwo." Nell's voice came soft beside me. She'd been watching me watch it. "We can camp below the junction if you'd rather. Push through without stopping."

"No." I said it too fast, then made myself say it slower. "No. We work through. That was always the plan."

It hadn't been the plan. The plan had been to avoid this stretch of line for the rest of my natural life, and I had been very good at that plan for three years. But plans, like bridges, fail under load you didn't account for.

* * *

We made camp on the grade itself, on the wide shelf where the old construction camps had stood. Baptiste got his cook fire going in the lee of a rock overhang, and the smell of beans and salt pork did what it always did—pulled people together whether they wanted to be pulled or not.

Okoye sat sharpening his drill steels by the fire, the way he did every evening, though the steels were already sharp enough to shave with. He'd been quiet since the vote. Since I chose the line.

"You know," he said, not looking up from his work, "my grandmother used to say that a man who won't visit a grave is not avoiding the dead man. He's avoiding himself."

"I'm visiting," I said.

"Mm." He tested an edge against his thumb. "Visiting and digging are different things. One of them turns the soil."

Baptiste ladled out beans and said nothing, which from Baptiste was a sermon. Nell ate fast, the way she did, and watched me over her tin plate with those quick brown eyes that missed less than any surveyor's instrument I'd ever owned.

I turned in early. I lay in my bedroll listening to the river, and somewhere in the dark I let myself do the arithmetic I'd been refusing for three years: Tomas Okonkwo had been twenty-six years old. He had laughed like our mother. He had died holding a deed to land that was ours, buried under forty tons of collapsed trestle, and I had spent every day since running so hard from that fact that I'd mistaken motion for living.

Tomorrow we drilled into the wall above where he died. Tomorrow the work would take me straight past his grave.

I slept maybe two hours.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, with the river running high from snowmelt up-canyon. We set the first drill steel into the rock face at seven o'clock, and I'll tell you what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't wait politely outside the worksite. It clocks in with you. It picks up a hammer.

The job through the gorge was straightforward engineering—widen the grade shelf where the original builders had cut it too narrow, drill and shoot the face, muck out the spoil, lay rail. Forty-one hundred feet of it. I had walked the alignment twice the evening before with Nell, checking grades and sightlines, and my hands had been steady both times. Hands are loyal that way. They'll keep working long after the rest of you has gone somewhere else entirely.

By nine we were in rhythm. Okoye ran the drills with two of his helpers, the bit chattering into granite in that hungry voice rock makes when it's being argued with. Nell worked the transit, calling corrections. Baptiste hauled water and kept the men fed and watched me with the particular patience of a man carrying a confession he hadn't found the right hour for yet.

At ten-thirty the bit struck a seam of softer stone and the drilling changed pitch, and something about that sound—that sudden hollow note, like a voice cracking—took me straight back to a morning three years ago when I'd stood on the Ashcombe trestle with my brother laughing about the lunch waiting on the far side.

I dropped the chalk I was marking with.

Nell saw it happen. She was sixteen feet away with the transit, and she saw the chalk fall and she saw me stand there looking at the rock face like it was going to answer a question, and she did the thing that made me glad I'd hired her: she didn't ask if I was all right. She just called out, easy as anything, "Taking five, Miss Okonkwo? I could use a check on the back sights anyway."

A lie, generously offered. I took it.

We walked up the grade together until the noise of the drills fell behind us, and we stopped where the shelf widened near the old mile marker—forty-one, the one with the cairn, the one with the flowers someone still brought. The tin plate was there again, fresh this time. Someone had left wildflowers, mountain columbine, blue as spilled ink.

"My brother died down there," I said. I hadn't planned to say it. My mouth simply went ahead without filing the proper paperwork. "Right there. Where the piling stumps are. The trestle came down in the flood with him on it."

Nell didn't say *I'm sorry*. She was too well-raised for the obvious. She just stood beside me and looked where I was looking, giving the place the respect of being seen.

"He wasn't supposed to be on it," I went on, because apparently once the door opened, the whole house came out. "He was the company's land agent's clerk—our father's clerk, really, after Father died. Tomas carried the papers for the provision lands. Our family's land. Deeded to us, sixty years back, and then taken by men with better lawyers and worse souls. He was crossing the trestle with the documents because he thought—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle like that drill hitting the seam. "He thought if he kept them close, kept them moving, they'd be safe. Our mother used to tell him he worried like a hen with one chick. That was the chick. A piece of paper."

The river ran on below us, indifferent, keeping its iron.

"They never recovered him," I said. "Or the papers. The flood took the trestle and everything on it, and the company wrote it up as an act of God, and God has never once returned my correspondence on the matter."

Nell was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "My sister drowned when I was nine. Creek flood, spring melt. Everybody kept telling me she was in a better place, and I remember thinking—I was nine, and I thought—*then why does everybody act like the creek should be forgiven?*"

I looked at her. She was watching the river with her jaw set, and I understood that she hadn't told me this to comfort me. She'd told me because that's how wakes work. You don't console the bereaved; you sit down among them and admit you're one of them.

"The creek shouldn't be forgiven," I said. "Neither should this river. But they don't know they've done anything. That's the hell of it. Water's innocent. It's only ever the men who aren't."

* * *

We went back to work, and here is the strange part, the part I would not have believed if someone had told me: the work got easier. Not lighter—easier. As if the story, once spoken aloud, had stopped being a stone I was carrying and become a stone I was building with.

Through the afternoon I talked. I didn't mean to, but Nell kept asking questions—not prying ones, just honest surveyor's questions—and each one opened another room in the house I'd shut.

"What was he like?"

"Tall. Too tall for his own coordination—he broke his wrist twice falling off the same fence, and our father said the fence was winning the family feud." I steadied the chalk against the rock and marked the next drill line, and my hand was steady doing it. "He read everything. Novels mostly, which Father disapproved of, so he read them inside the almanac covers. He could recite whole chapters of some pirate story while he worked. Drove the other clerks mad."

"Did he want to be a clerk?"

"No. He wanted to be an engineer. Like me." The chalk paused. "That was the joke of it, see. Father sent me to school because I was the stubborn one, and Tomas stayed home because he was the kind one, and the kind one ended up carrying the burden and the stubborn one ended up carrying the guilt. If I'd been home that day—"

"You'd have been on the trestle too," Nell said flatly. "And there'd be no one left to finish this."

I stared at her. She adjusted the transit, all business, as if she hadn't just handed me the one sentence that three years of strangers' condolences had never found.

"That," I said finally, "is the most useful thing anyone's said to me since the funeral."

"I charge extra for it."

"Put it on the invoice."

Okoye drifted over around mid-afternoon, ostensibly to check my drill pattern, actually to listen, and he heard enough that when he left he squeezed my shoulder once, hard, the way he did instead of speaking. Baptiste brought water and stayed leaning on his shovel through a whole story—the one about Tomas teaching himself to whistle through his thumbs and whistling at everything for a month, including Father's eulogy practice, which nearly caused a second funeral.

Even the crew felt it, I think. Men who'd been swinging hammers in silence started talking as they worked—about their own dead, their own rivers, their own fences that had won. A wake doesn't need a church. It needs labor and witnesses and somebody who keeps the food coming, and Baptiste kept the food coming.

* * *

At dusk we shot the first round of holes. Okoye handled the powder with his usual ceremony, checking each charge twice, and before he lit the fuse he looked over at me and asked, quietly, "You ready?"

I looked down the gorge. The light was failing gold along the western rim, and the river below had gone the color of hammered tin, and somewhere under that water were the stumps of a trestle and a boy who whistled through his thumbs and a piece of paper that had cost our family everything.

"Tomas," I said—not loudly, just to the air, just to make it true by saying it—"we're cutting the road through. You'd have hated the paperwork and loved the blasting."

I nodded to Okoye.

The charges went off in sequence, four thumps rolling down the gorge like a drumline, and the rock face shrugged loose thirty tons of granite that fell away from the cut and went down and down, and the echo came back off the far wall a full three seconds later, long and deep, the mountain repeating our name for us.

When the dust cleared, the new cut stood open and clean, and the grade ran forward through solid rock toward the summit, and I stood in it breathing stone dust and feeling—for the first time in three years—not lighter exactly, but *placed*. Set down somewhere. A woman standing in a road she had cut herself, instead of a woman running down roads other people built.

Nell came and stood beside me in the settling dust.

"He'd have liked this," she said.

"He'd have complained about the dust and then liked it," I said.

We laughed, both of us, rough and surprised at ourselves, and the sound went down the gorge and came back changed.

Then the laugh died in my throat, because down the grade, riding hard through the last light with his collar up against the wind, came a rider I knew—Halloway, Voss's man, the one who fixed things—and he was leading two pack horses loaded with crates, and he was coming from the direction of the summit, not the valley.

From the direction of the cache.

He reined in beside us, touched his hat brim, and smiled the smile of a man who has already counted everyone in the room.

"Lady Okonkwo," he said. "Fine work. Mind if I camp? The pass above—it's closed sooner than anybody expected."
