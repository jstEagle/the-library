## 16. Sabotage

Nell Quill had learned to sleep through almost anything on the Kestrel Line. Thunder. Okoye's snoring, which sounded like a rockslide arguing with itself. Baptiste singing hymns to his sourdough starter at two in the morning, softly, the way other men prayed.

What she could not sleep through was the sound of the world ending at a quarter past four.

It came as a crack first—a deep, wrong crack, like the mountain clearing its throat—and then a groan of iron and timber, and then silence so complete she could hear her own heart filing a complaint. She was out of her tent before her boots were fully on, stumbling into rain that had softened to a fine cold mist, headlamps bobbing toward the lower camp like fireflies in a panic.

The viaduct at Mile Thirty-Eight—*their* viaduct, the one they'd finished only three days ago, the one Rafael had walked end to end twice with a level in her hands like a mother checking a sleeping child—was broken. Not damaged. Broken. A forty-foot section of track had dropped into the ravine, rails bent into shapes that made Nell's stomach turn, ties splintered, one whole bent of trestle folded sideways like a card table kicked by a giant.

Rafael stood at the edge of it, lantern raised, saying nothing at all.

Nell had seen Rafael angry. She'd seen her tired, and grieving, and once—briefly, gloriously—laughing until she had to sit down on a crate of spikes because Baptiste's impression of Voss's walking posture was simply too accurate for human endurance. But she had never seen her like this: absolutely still, absolutely quiet, in the way of water backing up behind a dam.

"Nobody was on it," Okoye said, coming up from below with mud to his knees. "Thank every saint there is. The night watchman's shack is up-slope. If it had been—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

"It wasn't the storm," Rafael said finally. Her voice was flat as a rail. "Look at the cut faces on those timbers."

Nell looked. She wished she hadn't. The breaks were too clean. Storms tore things; storms were honest about their violence. These beams had been *helped*.

"Somebody put powder under our track," Okoye said, in the tone of a man confirming a diagnosis he'd already written in his head. "Small charge. Placed by somebody who knew where the bent joints were weakest." He turned slowly, surveying the cluster of tents, the cook wagon, the tool shed, the twenty-odd men and women standing in the drizzle pretending not to stare. "Placed by somebody who sleeps here."

The word went around the camp faster than the news of the collapse itself, arriving everywhere ahead of its speaker: *somebody*. Somebody among them. Somebody who ate Baptiste's stew and complained about the coffee and lost at cards on Saturday nights.

Nell felt the thought settle over the crowd like weather. She watched men who'd shared blankets against the cold last week take half a step away from each other without seeming to notice they'd done it.

"All right," Rafael said. "Everyone back to work on the upper sections. Nobody goes anywhere alone. Okoye—with me."

* * *

By full daylight the rain had quit and the sky did that thing mountain skies do after a night of misbehavior—turned an apologetic, rinsed blue, as if nothing had happened. Nell hated it. It felt like the sky was gaslighting them.

She was supposed to be re-surveying the detour route around the collapsed section, but instead she found herself doing what she always did when she couldn't work: following Rafael at what she believed was a discreet distance and which was, judging by the way Rafael said "You may as well walk beside me, Nell, you sound like a herd of cattle," not discreet at all.

They picked along the wreckage while Okoye crouched beneath it, reading the blast site the way Baptiste read weather.

"Here." Okoye beckoned them under the surviving span, pointing at a scorch mark on a pier stone. "Charge sat right against this footing. Wrapped in something to muffle it—canvas, maybe sacking. Whoever set it knew enough not to blow themselves up, but not much more than that." He paused. "That's the interesting part."

"Interesting," Rafael repeated, in a voice that suggested she would have chosen a different word.

"Ain't one of my crew could've placed this. Too sloppy. And it ain't Halloway's men either—they're rough, but they're rough like barbed wire, not like this." Okoye wiped his hands on his thighs. "This is somebody learning as they go. Somebody nervous."

"Somebody new," Nell said, and then heard herself, and felt her ears go hot. "I mean—I've been keeping the labor roster. For wages. Someone joined at the resupply, eleven days ago. A man named Corrigan. Says he laid track on the Coaster Line."

"You checked him?"

"I checked he could lift things," Nell admitted. "He was very convincing about lifting things."

Rafael almost smiled. Almost. It flickered across her face like a signal lamp with a bad connection. "Where is Corrigan now?"

They found out soon enough. His tent was empty. His bedroll was gone. And tucked under the tin plate where his breakfast should have been was a piece of paper, folded once, weighted down with a stone.

Nell read it over Rafael's shoulder, and by the third line her mouth had gone dry.

*Stop asking where the line goes and start asking why. Ask your employer what she keeps in her map case. Ask her what her father buried in this gorge besides my brother.*

No signature. But the handwriting was careful, educated, the letters upright and disciplined—the handwriting of someone who had been taught penmanship by a person paid to teach it.

"That's not Corrigan's hand," Nell said. "Corrigan signs his time slips like a man signing with his boot. I've seen it."

"No," Rafael said quietly. "It isn't." She stared at the note a long moment, and Nell watched something move behind her eyes—not fear exactly, but the recalibration of a woman discovering the map she'd been using was drawn by the enemy. "Voss," she said. "She wants me to ask her directly. This is an invitation."

"Or it's a trap wearing an invitation's coat," Okoye said.

"With Voss," Rafael said, folding the note into her pocket, "those are usually the same garment."

* * *

Lunch was a grim affair. Baptiste, sensing the mood the way he sensed rising dough, had produced his emergency strategy: cinnamon rolls. There was no crisis known to man or railroad, he maintained, that could survive contact with a proper cinnamon roll, and for twenty minutes it seemed he might be right. The camp ate in wary clusters, and the rolls were excellent, and the paranoia receded to a manageable simmer.

Then Pike—one of the older trackmen, a leathery man who'd been laying rail since before Nell was born—set down his roll, brushed off his hands, and said, conversationally, to no one in particular:

"Heard we got us a ghost in camp."

Every fork stopped.

"Ghost?" said Baptiste, who was many things but never slow.

"Fella I talked to at the resupply. Said there was a man come through asking after the Kestrel Line—not asking about wages, mind, asking about the gorge. Asking what was in the walls of it." Pike shrugged. "Description fit Corrigan close enough to be his brother. Or him."

Nell looked across the fire and saw it happen: twenty people simultaneously remembering every odd thing anyone had ever done. The man who'd asked where the survey maps were kept. The woman who'd been seen near Voss's tent. The fellow who took an unusual interest in the blasting stores—which, in fairness, Okoye pointed out, everyone took an unusual interest in the blasting stores, because Okoye guarded them like a dragon and everyone wanted to know how doomed they were.

By evening the camp had organized itself into a state of armed suspicion punctuated by card games. Men walked in pairs. Questions were answered with questions. When Baptiste ladled out stew, he did it with the wounded dignity of a chef whose dining room had become a crime scene.

"They're looking at each other like the stew's poisoned," he told Nell mournfully, as she scraped her bowl. "Three weeks I've fed these people. Three weeks. One blown-up bridge and suddenly my dumplings are suspect."

"The bridge wasn't your fault, Baptiste."

"I know whose fault it is." He lowered his voice, and for once all the music went out of it. "I just don't know which face it's wearing."

* * *

Nell didn't sleep. She told herself she was keeping watch, which was true, though she was also doing what she did best, which was noticing.

She noticed that Rafael and Voss met behind the supply tent for nearly an hour, silhouettes against a lantern, Voss's hands moving sharply, Rafael's very still. She noticed that afterward Voss stood alone a long time, looking east toward the pass, and that her shoulders—those immaculate, armor-plated shoulders—were not straight.

And she noticed, a little after midnight, a lantern moving where no lantern should be: down by the tool shed, then along the cut bank above the river, moving fast, moving *away*.

Nell woke Okoye, who woke Rafael, and the three of them followed the light's ghost-trail down the slope with pistols they mostly didn't know how to hold and lanterns they held too high. At the riverbank they found the lantern itself, still burning, set carefully on a stone. Beside it, a hole. Fresh-dug, knee-deep, refilled badly.

And half-buried in the spoil heap, glinting in the lantern light: a canvas-wrapped bundle, and inside it, when Okoye peeled it back with the reverence of a man defusing a snake, sticks of dynamite. Six of them. Missing from Okoye's magazine, he confirmed grimly, since Tuesday.

"He was coming back for these," Rafael said. "Or planting them somewhere else. Or—"

"Or he wanted us to find them," Nell said.

They all looked at her.

"Think about it," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her, because inside she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Whoever blew the bridge is good enough to place a charge under a trestle in the dark but careless enough to leave a note? Careless enough to let Pike hear his description at a resupply station fifty miles back? Careless enough to dig a hole by lantern-light on the one night the whole camp can't sleep?" She shook her head. "We're being *herded*, Miss Okonkwo. Every clue we've found today has been found just a little too easily. Somebody wants us suspicious. Somebody wants this crew tearing itself apart before we reach the summit cutting—because whatever's in that wall, they don't want a united crew standing in front of it when we get there."

The wind moved down the gorge, carrying river-smell and old snow. Somewhere upslope, a tent flap snapped like a small gunshot, and all three of them flinched, and Nell hated that they flinched.

"So it ain't Corrigan," Okoye said slowly.

"Corrigan might not even exist. Or he might exist and be exactly as innocent as his terrible handwriting." Nell hugged her arms against the cold. "Whoever it is has been here the whole time. Eating with us. Losing at cards. Complimenting the cinnamon rolls."

"They complimented the rolls?" Baptiste had appeared at the top of the bank, drawn by the light, holding a skillet for reasons best known to himself. His voice was stricken. "*Which one of them complimented the rolls?*"

"Baptiste—"

"Because Hollis said they were dry. Hollis said that to my face. Hollis is either honest or the finest liar on this mountain, and I need to know *which*."

Despite everything—despite the wrecked viaduct and the missing powder and the hole in the dark—Nell laughed. It burst out of her, sharp and helpless, and she saw Rafael's mouth twitch, and even Okoye made a sound that in a better-funded emotional life might have been a chuckle.

But the laugh died quickly, the way laughs did now, and Rafael crouched by the refilled hole, studying it, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet and hard as frost.

"Nell's right. We're being played. So we stop playing." She stood, brushing earth from her knees. "From tonight, nobody knows anything. The real survey maps stay hidden; the ones in my tent are fakes. The real cache location—if Voss and I have worked out where Tomas's papers lie—that stays between four people in this circle and no one else. As far as the rest of the camp knows, we're just laying track and racing the snow like honest fools."

"And the traitor?" Okoye asked.

Rafael looked up the slope, toward the summit cutting, invisible now in the dark but as present to her, Nell suspected, as her own heartbeat.

"We let them think their plan is working," she said. "Right up until the moment it doesn't."

They climbed back to camp in single file, and Nell, last in line, paused at the top of the bank and looked back at the little lantern burning faithfully beside the empty hole—a message left for them, patient as a spider.

Behind them, in the dark of the camp, someone was singing. Low, tuneless, friendly. A lullaby, maybe, or a hymn, drifting from one of the tents like it belonged there.

Like it had belonged there all along.

Nell stood in the cold a moment longer, memorizing the sound, trying to place the voice—and realized, with a chill that had nothing to do with the altitude, that she couldn't.

She couldn't place it at all.
