## 17. The Man from the Company

Marguerite Voss had spent her whole life learning to read men the way other people read weather. Her father had taught her, though he'd never known it—had taught her by example, in the way he'd smile at a creditor on Tuesday and ruin him by Thursday, in the way he'd say *family* the way other men said *collateral*. She'd learned to watch hands instead of faces, to listen for what wasn't said, to notice when a man's boots were pointed toward the door even while his mouth was full of loyalty.

Which was why she'd known about Halloway for six days before she did anything about it.

Six days of watching him ladle stew with Baptiste and laugh at Okoye's dry jokes. Six days of watching him volunteer for the hard jobs, the cold jobs, the jobs that put him alone with the maps. Six days of watching him be exactly the kind of man every crew needed and no crew should trust: competent, cheerful, forgettable. A man you'd struggle to describe an hour after he left the tent.

She'd hired him herself—or thought she had. He'd come to her in Kalimpong with papers that were too clean, references that were too glowing, a handshake that was too firm. She'd been desperate then, short-handed and behind schedule, and desperation was the one door Halloway always seemed to find unlocked.

His name on the crew roster was Aldous Wren. His name in her father's old ledgers—the ones she'd burned through three sleepless nights in Darjeeling—was E. Halloway. And his name in the margin of a letter her father had written eleven years ago, the year the provision lands were seized, was underlined twice: *If it comes to questions, send Halloway.*

*Send Halloway.* Not *hire* him. Not *consult*. Send, the way you sent a dog, or a debt collector, or a fire.

She found him at dusk, down at the timber yard below the camp, checking the stores of creosoted pilings by lantern light. The monsoon had arrived two days early and the rain came down in gray sheets, drumming on the tarpaulins like impatient fingers. He worked alone, sleeves rolled, methodical as a clock, and for a moment she simply stood beneath the awning and watched him and felt the strange grief of it—that this man had eaten her food, laughed at her crew's jokes, carried Nell's pack across a swollen creek without being asked, and all of it had been a performance so long-running he'd probably forgotten where the stage ended.

"You count everything twice," she said.

He didn't startle. That was the first confirmation. Honest men startled.

"Old habit, ma'am." He turned, lantern raised, rain streaming off the brim of his hat. "My father was a shopkeeper. Said a man who counts once is guessing."

"Your father was a shopkeeper," Marguerite repeated. "And mine was a railroad baron. Funny how fathers get into everything."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has been waiting for a meeting he hoped would never come and is almost relieved has finally arrived.

"Miss Voss." He set the lantern down on a piling, unhurried. "You've been watching me for six days. I wondered when you'd work up to it."

"You knew."

"I know most things eventually." He wiped his hands on a rag, folded it, set it aside. Neat as everything else about him. "It's why they keep me."

"*They* being?"

He looked out at the rain, at the black shape of the gorge beyond the timber yard, and when he spoke again his voice had changed—lost its crew-camp warmth, gone flat and professional, the voice of a man reading terms.

"Your father built half the Kestrel Line with borrowed money, Miss Voss. Do you know who lent it?"

"The Provision Land Bank."

"The Provision Land Bank," he agreed, "which was owned by Judge Merrick Ashcombe, whose nephew sat on the land commission that seized the provision holdings, whose brother-in-law bought those holdings at auction for a tenth their worth, and whose son—" he paused, delicately, "—was the engineer whose bridge fell down at Ashcombe Crossing and took your family's fortune down with it."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped rail. "Rafael Okonkwo's bridge."

"The same. Though 'fell' is a generous word for it." Halloway picked up the lantern again, and the light threw his shadow long against the stacked timber. "I was there, ma'am. Not at the crossing—at the dinner after. Eleven men around a table in Calcutta, dividing up what the collapse would clear away. Your father's debts, the provision lands, the Kestrel charter itself. They toasted the dead crew with champagne. All except one of them, who couldn't finish his glass. That was your father. He drank, but he didn't toast. I always remembered that."

Marguerite found she was gripping the edge of a piling hard enough to hurt her palm. "You're telling me my family was ruined on purpose."

"I'm telling you your family was *spent*, the way you spend a coin that's gotten bent. Useful once. Worth melting down now." He tilted his head. "Your father signed things he shouldn't have, to keep things he loved. When the lands were seized, he kept a share of the proceeds. Enough to keep the house. Enough to keep you in school. You knew that part, I think. It's why you're here."

"And the deed."

"Ah." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen yet, because it was kind. "The deed. Yes. The original grant, with the signatures that show the seizure was fraud from the first survey stake. Buried somewhere in that gorge with a dead man, if the stories are true. There are eleven families in this country, Miss Voss, whose names are on documents that deed could unmake. Judges. Barons. One governor. Their grandchildren sit in parliament now. And every one of them has received the same instruction I received: the mountain keeps its secret, or everyone in it does."

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming, patient.

"So they sent you," she said quietly, "into my crew. To make sure the deed stays buried."

"To make sure the *line* gets finished, actually. That's the elegant part." He gestured up the slope, toward the camps, the lanterns, the sound of hammers faint through the rain. "A finished Kestrel Line opens the pass. Opened land gets resurveyed. Resurveyed land gets new titles issued—and new titles supersede whatever paper rots in a gorge wall. The moment your trains run, the deed becomes a curiosity. A historical document. Worth nothing but sentiment." He paused. "Unless someone digs it out first and sends copies to newspapers. Which is why there are four of us, not one. And why the others are less patient than I am."

"Less patient how?"

"Halloway," he said gently, "is the name I use when I'm being paid to talk. The others are paid for different work entirely. The sabotage last week—that was meant to slow you past the snowline, strand the crew, force an abandonment. Clean. Nobody dies in a well-planned abandonment." He met her eyes. "But the storm has changed the arithmetic. If the deed surfaces now, with the line unfinished and the pass closing, there won't be time for clean. There are men in the lower camp tonight, Miss Voss, who have shot people for less than a piece of paper, and they are waiting to hear whether you're an obstacle or an asset."

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the rain.

"And which am I?" she asked. "To them."

"That depends entirely," Halloway said, "on what you decide you are. Which brings us to the question I was sent to ask, before the others lose their patience asking it." He reached into his coat—slowly, showing his hand empty otherwise—and drew out a folded paper, dry despite the wet, kept in oilcloth. He held it out. "Your father wrote this the winter before he died. I was instructed to deliver it only if you ever stood where you're standing now. On the wrong side of the mountain, holding the wrong end of the truth."

She took it. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside was a single page in her father's cramped, slanted hand, the ink browned with age:

*Marguerite—if you are reading this, then you went looking, and I am sorry, because looking is the one thing I taught you and the one thing I prayed you wouldn't do. I took their money. I told myself it was for you. Every compromise has a child at the bottom of it, and mine was you. The Okonkwo man was innocent—I knew it and I signed the report anyway. Whatever you find in that gorge, do not let them make you choose between our name and the truth. Choose the truth. Our name was never ours; it was theirs, on loan, and they will take it back the moment it costs them anything. — Your father, who counted everything twice and got you wrong only once.*

She read it three times. The rain kept falling. Somewhere up the slope, muffled by weather, the crew's evening bell rang, and she thought of Rafael Okonkwo standing in it somewhere, unaware, and of Nell asleep in the survey tent with her maps, and of Okoye counting charges by lamplight, and of how every single one of them was standing inside a story that had been written for them by eleven men around a table in Calcutta, drinking champagne over her father's coffin before he was even in it.

When she looked up, Halloway was watching her with that terrible gentleness.

"Well?" he said. "Obstacle, or asset?"

Marguerite folded her father's letter along its old creases and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where the weight of it belonged.

"My father counted everything twice," she said. "So do I. And I've counted you, Mr. Halloway, and here's what I make: you delivered his letter because some part of you has been waiting eleven years for permission to stop being what they made you. So here's your permission. You're going to walk into the lower camp tomorrow and tell the others the line is nearly done and the deed is nowhere—that I've called off the search myself. You'll buy me three days. And if any of them comes up this mountain anyway—"

She stepped closer, close enough that the lantern lit them both, and let him see what her father had seen in her and feared: the family face, the family steel, worn now for a different purpose.

"—then they'll learn what a Voss does when she's finally spending her own money instead of theirs."

Halloway studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he smiled—a real one this time, tired and crooked, like a lock coming open after years of rust.

"Three days," he said. "I can maybe get you three days. But Miss Voss—you should know the tall one, the blaster, already suspects me. And the Okonkwo woman has stopped sleeping. Whatever you're planning, you'd best plan fast. Because the snow's coming down off that summit tonight, and I don't think it intends to wait for anyone's conscience."

Above them, far up the black shoulder of the escarpment, the wind changed—and brought with it, thin and sharp through the rain, the first smell of true winter.

* * *

Marguerite climbed back to camp alone, the letter against her ribs, the rain turning to sleet somewhere around the second switchback. The lanterns of the crew tents glowed yellow through the murk, ordinary and warm, and she stood at the edge of the light for a long moment, watching Rafael Okonkwo's silhouette move behind the survey canvas—bent over her charts, tireless, haunted, carrying a grief that Marguerite now knew had been manufactured for her, fitted to her like a shackle, by men who had never learned her name.

Tomorrow she would have to tell her. All of it. The frame, the forged report, the eleven men, the champagne. Tomorrow she would stand in front of the woman whose life her family had helped destroy and offer her the only currency she had left, which was the truth, unlovely and late.

Marguerite pulled her collar up and walked into the light.

Behind her, far below in the dark, a lantern flickered in the lower camp—once, twice—like a signal, or a warning, or a man counting something twice.

She didn't look back. But she started walking faster.
