## 18. Race Above the Clouds

The snow began an hour before dawn, and by the time the sky turned the color of old pewter, it had stopped being weather and become a verdict.

Rafael stood at the mouth of the upper camp and watched it come down in fat, deliberate flakes, each one landing on her sleeve with the patience of a creditor. Behind her, the pass they'd climbed through was already filling in. Ahead of them, four miles of unfinished grade clung to the mountainside like a rumor, ending at a viaduct that existed mostly in her head and partly in Okoye's timber.

"Ten hours," Okoye said beside her. He said it the way other men said *amen*. "Maybe eight if the wind stays stupid."

"The wind is always stupid," Rafael said.

"Then we have ten hours." He pulled his mittens on with the grim ceremony of a man dressing for his own funeral, which, knowing Okoye, he had probably already planned down to the flowers. "I've laid rail in worse. But I was younger, and the rail was downhill, and I wasn't being followed by men with rifles."

Rafael glanced back down the grade. Three figures moved through the falling snow, keeping to the treeline, unhurried in the way of people who knew exactly how much time everyone else had. Halloway's men. They had been shadowing them since the sabotage, never closing, never leaving—wolves who had learned that patience kills more surely than teeth.

"They want us to finish," she said. "They want the summit opened so they can walk up behind us."

"And you want to finish because?"

Rafael looked up the mountain. Somewhere above the cloud deck, in a cutting that Tomas had helped survey when he was nineteen years old and full of plans, there was a wall of rock with her family's whole history packed inside it like a letter that had waited eleven years for someone brave enough to open it.

"Because the only way out is through," she said. "And because I'm not going to let those men stand on my viaduct before I do."

Okoye grunted, which from him was a standing ovation.

* * *

She took nine people up. That was all the mountain would carry and all the truth could trust. Nell came because Nell would have come if Rafael had ordered her to stay, and Rafael had learned long ago that some orders are just invitations to disobedience. Baptiste came carrying a sled loaded with hot coffee and, inexplicably, forty pounds of salt pork, on the theory—which he defended with real passion—that no crew ever froze to death within reach of a good breakfast. Okoye came with his blasting kit wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart. The rest were the five steadiest hands in camp, plus Voss.

Voss had appeared at the rope-up with a spike maul over her shoulder and an expression that dared anyone to comment. Nobody did. There is a particular silence that forms around a woman in expensive boots doing laborer's work at altitude, and the crew wore it like a coat.

"You don't have to be here," Rafael told her.

"My family built this line's first trestle with their own money," Voss said. "Whatever else happens on this mountain, I will be standing on it when we top the grade. You may consider that sentimentality or strategy. I've considered it myself and gotten nowhere."

"Fine. But you spike where I tell you to spike."

"I wouldn't dream of otherwise." A pause. "Your viaduct calculations from last night. The load distribution across the third span."

"What about them?"

"They're beautiful," Voss said, as though admitting to a crime. "That's all."

Rafael turned uphill before her face could do anything embarrassing about it.

* * *

The first two miles went fast because terror is an excellent foreman. They ran rail off the sleds in hundred-foot strings, Nell calling grades from the transit while her fingers went white around the tripod, the gang spiking and bolting with the wordless rhythm that crews find either in their first year or never. Rafael walked the front of the work the whole way, reading the ground, adjusting the alignment by eye and memory, and every step took her further above the world.

By midmorning they broke through the cloud layer.

It happened the way it always happens up there—one moment gray murk, the next a horizon like the rim of a bowl, clouds spread below them to every edge of sight, white and slow and utterly silent. The sun came up over the sea of it and turned everything gold. Somewhere beneath all that softness were the flooded camps, the gorge, the whole wet struggling world. Up here there was only light and iron and the sound of hammers.

Nell stopped dead in the middle of the grade and stared.

"Nell," Rafael said.

"I know. I know. It's just—" She gestured helplessly at everything. "We're walking on the sky."

"We're walking on a deadline. Sky's incidental." But Rafael found herself stopping too, one boot on the new rail, looking out at a view that no timetable had ever accounted for. Tomas had described this stretch to her once, sitting on their father's porch with his boots on the railing: *There's a place past the shoulder where you come out above the clouds, Rafa, and you'll think you died and got the good part of the afterlife.* She had laughed at him then. Eleven years later, here she was, collecting on his promise the hard way.

"Baptiste," she called. "Coffee. We're taking four minutes to appreciate the afterlife."

"Five," Baptiste said, already pouring. "The pork needs time."

* * *

The viaduct revealed itself out of the snow like something surfacing from deep water—the great stone piers the original builders had raised and abandoned, standing in a row across a hanging valley, patient as tombstones. Between them, nothing. Four hundred feet of open air where track should be, and Rafael's job, in the next six hours, was to convince three hundred tons of timber and iron to lie down across it and hold.

Okoye surveyed the piers with the expression of a man greeting old comrades. "They poured these foundations in '81. Still true. Still plumb." He knocked one with his knuckle, respectfully. "Better masons than we deserve."

"The falsework frames are staged at the far side," Rafael said. "We run the stringers pier to pier, brace, deck, then rail. It's going to feel wrong the whole time. It's supposed to feel wrong. If it feels right, somebody's cut a corner and I want names."

"And if the wind picks up?" asked Halloran, one of the five, a big quiet man who'd been with them since the foothills.

"Then we tie ourselves to the structure and complain professionally." Rafael looked down into the valley floor, which was, at the moment, a floor of cloud. "Nobody works unroped past the second pier. Anybody who objects to heights may volunteer to object back at camp, where it's warm, and I will think slightly less of them but say nothing."

Nobody went back. She hadn't expected anyone to. This crew, assembled out of ghosts and drifters and one repentant aristocrat, had somehow become the kind of crew that didn't go back, and Rafael felt that fact settle in her chest like a coal—warm and dangerous both.

* * *

The stringers went across in the early afternoon, and it was the finest work of Rafael Okonkwo's life, and she knew it even while she was doing it, which almost never happens. Every joint seated true. Every brace took its load with a sound like a held breath released. The wind rose exactly as promised and they worked roped and leaning into it, shouting numbers, and the structure grew under their hands the way a story grows under a writer's—unhurried, inevitable, one impossible piece at a time.

At the third span, halfway across the void, Nell's voice came up thin over the wind.

"Miss Okonkwo! Down-grade!"

Rafael turned. Below them, small with distance, the three figures had left the treeline. They were climbing the finished grade now, openly, no longer pretending to be anything but what they were. One carried a rifle slung across his back like a tool he intended to use.

"How far?" Okoye asked.

"An hour," Rafael said. "Maybe less. They know this mountain."

"So do we." Okoye spat thoughtfully into the cloud sea. "And we have dynamite."

"Not yet." Rafael watched the figures climb. Her mind was doing two things at once, the way it did best under pressure: laying rail, and laying traps. "They won't rush us on the viaduct. Too exposed, too many of us, and Halloway wants the summit reached clean—he wants witnesses to see the line finished so the company's claim looks legitimate. No. They'll wait until we're at the cutting, until we're tired and scattered and digging."

"Digging," Nell repeated softly. She'd heard the same rumor the rest of them had, passed along in whispers since the confrontation with Voss: that the summit cutting held more than rock.

"That's why they've followed us the whole way," Rafael said. "Not the line. Never the line." She turned back to the work, and her voice came out flat and cold and certain. "So we give them a finished line to follow us up. And then, gentlemen, we discuss what happens to trespassers."

* * *

They topped the grade at 4:40 in the afternoon, with the light failing and the snow coming sideways now, and Rafael drove the last spike herself—a golden spike in spirit if not in metallurgy, ordinary steel driven home with six blows while the whole skeleton crew stood around her in the white roar and made a sound that was half cheer and half prayer.

The Kestrel Line was open to the summit. On paper, in any court that still honored paper, the pass was passable, the claim was exercisable, the provision lands were one signature away from belonging to whoever held the strongest lawyer.

In reality, it was a single thread of rail across a drowned sky, and behind them, coming up through the storm, were men with guns.

"Listen," Rafael said, and the crew crowded close because the wind ate anything softer than a shout. "Halloway thinks he's been chasing us to this spot. He's right. Everything from here on, we chose." She pointed east, where the original survey showed the old summit cutting—Tomas's cutting—half a mile along the grade, screened by a shoulder of rock. "The cache is in that wall. We dig tonight. All of us. And when Halloway arrives—"

"When Halloway arrives," Okoye said comfortably, patting his satchel of charges, "he will discover that this mountain has opinions about visitors."

Baptiste hefted the empty sled frame like a club and smiled his terrible gentle smile. Nell was already coiling rope. Even Voss rolled her sleeves.

Rafael looked once more down the shining thread of rail they'd spun across the clouds, at the dark shapes toiling up it through the snow, and felt the strangest thing: not fear, or not only fear. Underneath the terror was a fierce, singing joy, the feeling she'd chased for three years across a thousand miles of closed lines and never once caught—because it wasn't out there on the miles at all. It was here, at the top of the world, with her brother's grave fifty yards away and her friends at her shoulders.

"All right," she said. "Let's go wake up Tomas."

They started along the grade into the storm—and from the rocks ahead, where no man should have been yet, a lantern flickered once, twice, deliberately. Someone was already waiting at the cutting.

Someone who wasn't Halloway.
