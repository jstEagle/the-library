## 19. The Buried Cache

The snow came at them sideways now, the kind of snow that didn't fall so much as arrive, all at once, like a debt collector who'd been waiting politely on the porch for three years and had finally lost his patience.

Rafael stood at the mouth of the summit cutting and looked up at the gorge wall, and for a long moment she forgot to be cold.

She had known this place existed since she was nine years old. Her father used to trace it on the kitchen table with a butter knife, drawing the escarpment in spilled sugar: *here's the pass, mija, here's where the Kestrel Line will go through, and here—* the knife tapping twice against the wood—*here's where your grandfather says the land remembers what was done to it.* She'd thought it was one of his stories, the kind he told between sips of coffee, half history and half sermon. She'd spent thirty years believing the important things were written down somewhere safe, in courthouses, in registries, in ink that outlasted people.

It wasn't. It was written in rock. And it had been waiting for her the whole time.

"Rafael." Nell's voice came thin through the wind, and the girl herself came thicker, bundled in so many layers she moved like a bear that had learned to walk upright out of sheer ambition. "Okoye says the charges are set on the east face. He also says to tell you that if we're not off this mountain by tomorrow noon, he's going to retire whether or not the rest of us do."

"Tell him retirement requires surviving first."

"I told him that. He said—" Nell paused, visibly deciding how much of Okoye's response to sanitize, "—he said something about engineers and their confidence."

Rafael almost smiled. Almost. Then her eyes went back to the wall of the cutting, forty feet up, where the old survey maps—the ones Voss had finally surrendered two nights ago, pressed into her hands with an expression like a woman handing over a kidney—showed a seam of softer stone running behind the collapsed rockfall. A pocket. A hollow in the mountain, sealed since the year Tomas died.

*He's in there,* she thought, and the thought landed in her chest like a dropped rail.

"You don't have to do this part," Nell said quietly. "I can run the drill."

"No." Rafael pulled her goggles down. "This part is mine."

* * *

Drilling into the gorge wall took four hours, and Rafael spent every one of them talking.

Not to Nell, exactly. Not entirely to herself either. To the rock, maybe. Or to whatever was behind it. The drill bit bit into stone with a rhythm like a heartbeat, and she found that if she kept her voice steady, her hands stayed steady too, so she talked.

"He was twenty-six," she said, over the shriek of steel in granite. "Tomas. Two years older than me, which he never let me forget, even though by the end I was taller, which he also never let me forget, because according to him height was 'just stretching,' and seniority was seniority." The bit sank another inch. "He wanted to be a surveyor. Did I ever tell you that? Everyone remembers him as the one who died on the line, but before that he was the one who *loved* the line. He'd climb up on anything—trestles, water towers, my shoulders once, when we were kids—to get a better view of where the track was going. He said a railroad was just a promise you could stand on."

Nell had stopped pretending to work. She stood in the falling snow with her mittened hands folded, listening the way people listen in church, if churches were cold enough to see your breath.

"When they told me he was gone, they wouldn't let me dig. Said it wasn't safe, said the rockfall was still settling, said there was nothing to find anyway." Rafael's jaw tightened inside her collar. "There was something to find. There was always something to find. Papa knew it. That's why he sent Tomas up here that last week—not for the survey. For this." She nodded at the wall. "Papa hid the deed the only place no court order could reach. Inside the mountain. With his son standing guard over it."

"That's not guard duty," Nell said softly. "That's a tomb."

"Yes," Rafael said. "And I'm three years late paying my respects."

The bit broke through into the hollow at 3:40 in the afternoon, with a sound like a held breath finally released—a soft exhale of trapped air, cold and mineral and old, breathing out of the mountain into the storm.

Rafael shut down the drill. The silence afterward was enormous.

"Okoye!" she called, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, which she chose to blame on the wind. "Light charges. Just enough to open the face. Nobody touches anything until I say."

The old blaster appeared out of the white like a rumor, squinting at the bore holes, then at her face, then back at the holes. Whatever he saw in her expression made him nod once and start working without a word, which from Okoye was practically a eulogy.

* * *

The charges opened the seam at dusk, in a bloom of smoke and powder-white spray, and when the dust cleared there was a gap in the gorge wall barely wide enough for a person—and beyond it, darkness, and within the darkness, shapes.

Rafael went in alone. Nell held the lantern at the mouth; Okoye stood watch on the cutting; Baptiste had come up from the lower camp an hour earlier, unasked, and planted himself at the entrance with his arms crossed like a door that had decided to become a man. None of them offered to follow. All of them understood.

The hollow was small—maybe eight feet deep, six wide, a pocket the mountain had made for itself long before men ever came with maps. And it was not empty.

Her lantern found him first.

Tomas lay on his back on a shelf of stone, and someone had arranged him. That was the thing that undid her—not the bones themselves, not the three years of dark, but the care of it. His coat was buttoned to the throat. His boots were side by side, neat as Sunday. Someone had climbed into this hole in the world, in grief, in secret, and had laid her brother down with dignity, and had come back—she could see it, the fresh-cut flowers gone brown and papery in a rusted tin cup, the same flowers she'd found at the mile-forty-one cairn, season after season after season.

"Oh," Rafael said. It was not a word. It was just the sound a person makes when the floor of their chest gives way.

She knelt. Her knees hit stone and she didn't feel it. She reached out and touched the lapel of the coat, gently, the way you'd wake someone you loved who needed to be somewhere.

"Hey, Tomás," she whispered. "Sorry I'm late. Traffic was terrible. Three years of terrible."

She talked to him while she worked, because she couldn't not, because the silence in that hollow was the same silence that had chased her across three years and ten thousand miles of track, and the only way through it was through it. She told him about the bridge. About Voss. About the crew of ghosts she'd collected, each one stranger than the last. She told him Okoye's retirement plans and Nell's terrible card playing and the time Baptiste had tried to make coffee from pine needles and had nearly ended his own career by mutiny. She laughed once, wetly, at her own joke, and the laugh echoed in the hollow and came back sounding like forgiveness.

Beneath the shelf where Tomas rested, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with wire gone to lace, she found the bundle.

Her father's letters. Dozens of them, in his cramped, furious hand, dating back twenty-five years—correspondence with lawyers, with land agents, with judges whose names she recognized from the newspapers' society pages. Page after page documenting the provision lands: the original grants to the settler families, the court order that had declared her father a fraud, the payments that had flowed afterward to the very judge who'd signed it. Proof, in ink that had outlasted everyone who'd tried to bury it.

And beneath the letters, flat and stiff and sealed in wax, the deed itself. The original. The true one. Signed, witnessed, stamped—the paper that said the provision lands belonged to the families who'd worked them, and that everything done to take them away had been theft dressed up as law.

Rafael sat back on her heels in the dark and held her family's whole ruined history in two hands, and it weighed almost nothing at all.

"Papa," she murmured, "you magnificent, stubborn, impossible man. You hid the truth in a mountain."

*He hid it with his son,* said a voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like Marguerite Voss. *Because he knew no one would dig here but family.*

She was still kneeling there, laughing or crying or some hybrid of both that she'd have denied under oath, when the light at the mouth of the hollow changed.

Not brighter. Wronger. Lantern-yellow replaced by something colder, whiter, swinging in a way lanterns don't swing.

"Miss Okonkwo." The voice came from outside, smooth as oiled rail, carrying easily over the wind the way voices do when their owners are accustomed to being obeyed. "How touching. Do come out slowly, please. And bring the papers."

Rafael closed her hand around the deed.

Halloway. Of course Halloway. The quiet man from the company, the one who'd been in Voss's camp since the foothills, helpful and forgettable as furniture, right up until the night somebody blew the finished track and every finger pointed everywhere except at him. Voss had confronted him two days ago and gotten, by her own shaken account, a smile and a warning. Now the warning had arrived with friends.

She turned toward the light. Silhouetted at the mouth of the hollow stood Halloway, snow gathering on the shoulders of his city coat like the mountain itself disapproving of him, and behind him, fanned out along the cutting's rim, five men with rifles, their barrels pointed down with the lazy confidence of people who had done this before and been paid well for it.

"I said," Halloway repeated pleasantly, "slowly."

Rafael rose. In the lantern light her shadow stretched back into the hollow, over Tomas, over the letters, over the deed warm now from her grip.

"Gentlemen," she called out, pitching her voice to carry, the way she did over blasting grounds, the way that made crews stop and listen. "You should know three things before anyone does anything stupid. One: this mountain is rigged with Okoye's charges, and Okoye is currently somewhere in this storm feeling insulted. Two: copies of what I'm holding are already on their way down the line in three separate pockets belonging to three separate people, none of whom like Mr. Halloway even slightly. And three—"

She stepped out of the hollow into the snow, into the guns, holding the deed up flat against the storm like a small, stubborn flag.

"—the paper in my hand is worth more than every rifle on this ridge, and every man who helps me keep it out of yours is going to be able to look his grandchildren in the eye. Your move, Mr. Halloway. But choose fast. The snow doesn't negotiate, and neither, anymore, do I."

For a moment nobody breathed. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled through the clouds—or perhaps it was the mountain, shifting its weight, waiting to see what its people would do.

Halloway smiled his furniture smile and raised one gloved hand, and the rifles came up.
