## 20. Justice Outside the Law

**Okoye**

The snow came sideways, which was the worst kind. Okoye had learned to read weather the way other men read scripture—by heart, by scar, by the particular way it punished you for not listening—and this storm read like a eulogy. It came off the summit in long white slabs, filled the cutting, erased the viaduct they'd spent two days of their lives laying rail across, and turned the world into a room with no walls and no floor.

He crouched behind the cache cut's spoil heap with a coil of det cord in his coat, warming it against his chest like a sick bird, and thought: *I have buried eleven friends under weather exactly like this.*

Twelve, if you counted the ones you didn't dig up.

Halloway had four men. Okoye had counted them through the whiteout an hour ago, when the wind dropped just long enough to show him shapes moving along the ridge—dark, deliberate, spreading out the way men spread when they'd been paid to do a thing and wanted it done before anyone could think. They had rifles wrapped in oilcloth. They had rope. And they had Halloway at the center of them, walking upright into the storm as if the mountain owed him passage.

Okoye had been a blaster for thirty-one years. People thought his trade was destruction. It wasn't. His trade was *understanding*—reading rock the way a priest reads faces, knowing where the fault ran, where the weight sat, what would hold and what would let go. And right now he understood something about this mountainside that Halloway, a man who had never in his life put his ear against stone, did not.

Above the cache cut, the cornice had been building all day. Six feet of wind-packed snow sitting on a lip of rotten shale, waiting for one good reason.

Okoye could give it one.

The trouble was placement. He'd need to get up-slope, past Halloway's line, plant the charge under the cornice itself, and be somewhere else entirely when it went. In this visibility. With hands he couldn't feel past the second knuckle.

He grinned into the wind. His teeth ached from the cold, and the grin felt like the truest thing he'd done all week.

"Old man," he said to himself, "you are too old for this."

Then he started climbing.

* * *

**Nell**

Nell found the rope first, or rather the rope found her—a guy-line from the old survey camp, half-buried, frozen stiff as a fence rail. She'd been sent down-cut to fetch Rafael's instrument case, which was a fool's errand in this light except that Rafael had asked in that voice she used when she meant *get clear of me*, and Nell had learned enough in four months to obey the voice and disobey the meaning.

Which is how she happened to be fifty yards below the cache cut, hugging the gorge wall, when she heard the click.

Not a gunshot. A *click*—metal on metal, small, wrong. The sound of a rifle bolt being worked under an oilcloth, muffled by snow, no more than twenty feet above her head.

She went flat against the rock and did not breathe. Snow sifted onto her collar. Above her, a shape detached itself from the wall—a man in a company slicker, moving toward the cut where Rafael stood over the opened cache, where Tomas's bones lay wrapped in canvas, where the deed that had ruined two families sat in its oilskin packet like a heart in a box.

They were going to shoot her. Not Rafael—the woman, the engineer, the legend; Halloway would want her alive or at least public—but Nell, the nobody, the girl with the transit case, she could simply stop existing and the mountain would file the paperwork.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with strange detachment, the way you notice rain on a window. Then she noticed something else: the guy-line in her fists. Old wire core, but sound. Anchored to a piton driven into the rock by better men than any of these, thirty years ago, by crews who built things instead of unbuilding them.

Nell had spent her whole life being the youngest person in every room, the one who carried things, the one who was told to wait. She looked at the man in the slicker picking his way down toward the cut, and she thought about Rafael's face when she'd unwrapped the canvas, and she decided she was done waiting.

She paid out the rope across the slope, low, shin-height, invisible under six inches of new snow, and staked it taut between two boulders with the cold-bent tent pegs still in her pocket. Surveyor's work. You spend years learning how a line behaves across broken ground—where it sags, where it sings, where it will catch a boot at a dead run.

Then she picked up a stone and threw it, hard, in the opposite direction, so it cracked off the rocks below the cut.

The man turned toward the sound. He moved fast, boots punching through the crust—

—and the line took him at the ankles the way a rail takes a derailed wheel, and the mountain received him with a soft, enormous *whumpf*, and he slid forty feet down the scree in a white avalanche of his own making, arms pinwheeling, rifle gone, dignity gone, coming to rest against a boulder with the expression of a man rethinking his career.

"Sorry!" Nell called, because her mother had raised her polite. She was already running for the cut.

* * *

**Baptiste**

Baptiste had never hit a man in his life until three years ago, and then he'd made up for lost time in a single afternoon, and he'd been carrying both facts ever since like stones in each pocket. He was the biggest thing on this mountain that wasn't the mountain, and he had spent four months cooking for people who deserved better than beans in a whiteout, and now he stood in the mouth of the cache cut with a cast-iron Dutch oven in one hand and no plan whatsoever, which was, he had found, the condition under which God did His best work.

Two of Halloway's men had come around the shoulder of the cut while everyone watched the ridge. He saw them the way you see anything in a storm—late, and badly. One had a pistol out, levelled at Rafael's back.

There was no time for cleverness. There was only the oven.

He threw it underhand—not at the man, Baptiste had killed enough, thank you, the ledger was full—but *at* him, close, the way you throw a stone into water beside a duck, and the iron struck the scree a foot from the man's boot and rang like the last trumpet, and the man flinched, and the shot went wide, and in that half-second of flinch Baptiste crossed the distance the way only very large calm men can cross a distance, which is suddenly and without negotiation.

He caught the pistol wrist. He squeezed. Something in the wrist made a decision it would regret later. The pistol dropped into the snow. The man swung at him; Baptiste absorbed the blow the way a church absorbs a hymn, and then simply leaned forward, all three hundred pounds of him, and folded the man into the drift like a woman putting linen away.

"You," Baptiste said conversationally, holding him down with one hand while the storm tried to bury them both, "are shooting at a woman standing over her brother's grave."

The man said nothing. Men like him never did. That was the whole tragedy of them—they took money precisely so they wouldn't have to say anything, ever, about anything.

"My old crew cut corners on that bridge," Baptiste told him, leaning closer, shouting over the wind because some things needed saying at volume even if only one man heard. "Voss's family signed the order. I kept quiet three years. I am *done* keeping quiet. Do you understand? I have been quiet my whole life and it has bought me nothing but soup!"

The man, face-down in a snowdrift, receiving the testimony of a weeping cook, made a small sound that might have been agreement.

Behind them, the second man turned to run—and stopped, because the ridge above the cut had begun, very quietly, to move.

* * *

**Rafael**

Rafael heard the cornice go before she saw it—a deep structural groan, the sound she knew better than her own name, the sound the Ashcombe Bridge had made in its final second—and she was already moving, dragging the deed packet inside her coat, pulling Nell bodily behind the cut's rock rib, screaming names.

The mountainside shrugged.

Six feet of wind slab let go above the cache cut and came down the slope in a slow-motion white flood, and it caught Halloway's remaining men exactly where Okoye had promised the mountain would catch them. She saw one go under and roll free, stripped of his rifle, coughing. She saw another simply vanish, swallowed, and did not waste a heartbeat mourning him—the slide was shallow, the run-out gentle, he'd dig himself out angrier and poorer. What mattered was the shape that did *not* run, did not fall, did not vanish:

Halloway himself, standing his ground at the edge of the slide's path with snow smoking off his shoulders, watching the cut with eyes like nail heads. Watching *her*. Calculating, even now, even buried to the knee, whether the arithmetic still favored him.

It did not. But men like Halloway never believed the numbers until the bridge was already down.

"RAFAEL!" Voss's voice, raw, from the left. Marguerite Voss came stumbling through the whiteout with her skirts frozen into boards and her face bleeding where the ice had scoured it, and in her fist she held papers, waving them like a torch. "HALLOWAY! Your contract—you hear me? I hold your contract! Every order you've taken since Kestrel Junction bears my signature, and I am telling the courts, the newspapers, and God Himself that every one of them was *forged!*"

Halloway turned. For the first time, something moved behind the nail heads. Doubt, maybe. Or the colder cousin of doubt, which is accounting.

"You'll hang with the rest of us, Mrs. Voss," he called back. "Your father's name is on the seizure writ same as mine is on nothing. Paper burns. Names burn."

"Not this paper." And here Voss did the thing Rafael would remember for the rest of her life, the thing that redrew the map between them: she held up a document against the snow—Rafael could see the seal even at forty yards, the court's embossed ribbon—and she read it aloud into the storm, her voice cracking and carrying, the court order naming Ezekiel Voss and his fixers in the fraudulent condemnation of the provision lands, the order she had hidden from Rafael at the junction hotel, the last card in her family's crooked deck, played now *against* her own house.

"I bought this truth with everything I have left," Voss shouted. "You don't get to keep it buried!"

Halloway looked at the paper. Looked at the cut, where a dead man's sister stood guard over a dead man's grave. Looked at the slope, where a cook was rising out of the drift with a second Dutch oven, and a girl was coiling rope with shaking competent hands, and an old blaster was picking his way down through the settling snow with the unhurried gait of a man whose work was finished.

Four of his men were disarmed, buried, limping, or all three. The storm was thickening. The pass behind him was gone. And the woman with the seal was reading his name into the wind as though the wind were a courtroom, which, Rafael thought, on this mountain, today, it was.

Halloway reached inside his coat.

Every gun on the mountainside came up—Rafael's revolver, Nell's borrowed pistol, the disarmed man's own rifle snatched up by Baptiste—and for one suspended moment the whole gorge held its breath, and Rafael understood with perfect clarity that whatever happened in the next three seconds would follow her longer than any bridge she ever built.

Halloway drew out—not a gun. A telegram form, sodden, useless. He looked at it. He laughed once, a short ugly bark, almost admiring.

"Engineers," he said. "Always building on bad ground."

Then he turned and walked into the storm, uphill, away from the line, away from the deed, away from the four men he'd brought to do murder and abandoned without a backward glance—and the snow took him the way snow takes everything, gradually and then completely, until there was nothing on the ridge but weather.

Nobody fired. Nobody chased. The mountain had already sentenced him to exactly the punishment he deserved, which was to walk out of the range empty-handed, on foot, in the dark, carrying nothing but his own name—which, Rafael reflected, was more than he'd left anyone else.

* * *

**Okoye**

Afterward, they dug the last of Halloway's men out of the slide—he was fine, furious, and unemployed—and Okoye sat on a powder crate with his frostbitten hands in Baptiste's enormous warm grip while the cook poured tea directly into his mittened mouth like he was foaling a horse.

"You set off a cornice slide," Baptiste said, "in a whiteout, alone, at sixty-one years of age."

"Fifty-eight."

"You are sixty-one and a liar."

Okoye drank his tea. Below them, the camp glowed—three fires, close together, closer than they'd ever bothered to build them, because nobody tonight wanted to be more than a shout from anybody else. Nell was showing Rafael the guy-line trick, demonstrating with tent pegs and a length of string, and Rafael was laughing—actually laughing, a sound most of them had never heard from her, startled out of her like a bird. Voss sat apart with the court order spread on her knees, guarding it from the wet, looking like a woman who had burned down her own house and found it warm.

"The law wouldn't have come for us," Okoye said quietly. "Judges, land barons—half the bench is bought. We knew that going in."

"Yes," Baptiste said.

"So the law failed us."

"It did."

Okoye watched Nell loop her string over Rafael's finger, demonstrating the anchor point, both of them bent over it like conspirators. He watched Voss fold her paper with the care of a woman folding a flag.

"But we didn't fail each other," he said.

"No," said Baptiste. "We didn't."

Okoye nodded slowly, and felt something settle in his chest that he hadn't felt since before the avalanches, since before the counting. Tomorrow there would be reckoning—depositions in a snowbound camp, a dead man's deed to authenticate, a railroad to decide about, and Rafael's face when she talked about charges and the summit cutting had a look on it that Okoye, professional reader of structures, did not like at all.

But tonight, the fires were close together, and the tea was hot, and the mountain had chosen sides.

From the mouth of the cache cut, Rafael rose, brushed the snow from her coat, and looked down at them all with an expression Okoye had seen only once before—on the face of a man about to fire a shot he could not take back.

"Everyone," she said. "I need to talk to you about tomorrow."
