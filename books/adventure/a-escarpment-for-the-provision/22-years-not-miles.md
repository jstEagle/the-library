## 22. Years, Not Miles

The courtroom in Kestrel Falls smelled of pine resin and old paper, and Rafael Okonkwo spent most of her trial watching a spider build a web in the corner of the window frame.

It was, she reflected, the first time in three years she had watched anything for longer than it took to pack it.

The proceedings lasted eleven days. Judge Aldous Merrick was a heavy man with heavy hands and the unhurried manner of someone who had never once been late for anything, including his own opinions. The prosecution—funded, everyone understood, by interests whose names appeared nowhere on any document but whose fingerprints were all over Halloway's seized correspondence—argued that Rafael had destroyed public infrastructure out of spite, madness, or some combination of the two.

Her defense needed less than an afternoon.

The deed itself did most of the work. Nell had copied it four times before they left the mountain—one for the courts, one for the *Kestrel Falls Gazette*, one for the territorial land office, and one that Baptiste insisted on keeping folded inside his hatband, "for luck, and because paper burns but hats float." The original went into evidence, and when the clerk read the provision boundaries aloud—the same boundaries Rafael's father had walked with Tomas the summer before either of them died—a sound went through the gallery like wind through wheat.

Then came Voss.

Marguerite testified for two hours without notes. She laid out her family's financing of the original line, the seizure of the provision lands, the purchased report that blamed a dead man's sister for a bridge her father's contractors had sabotaged. She said the words "my father ordered it" in a clear voice that did not shake, though her hands, folded on the rail, were white as survey chalk.

Rafael watched her from the defense table and thought: *we could have been friends, in another life. In this one we were mirrors, and mirrors only ever show you what you already are.*

On the eleventh day, Judge Merrick removed his spectacles, rubbed the bridge of his nose, and said, "Miss Okonkwo, you destroyed a railroad."

"Yes, Your Honor."

"You destroyed it to prevent a greater theft, using evidence this court has now verified independently." He set the spectacles down. "The court finds you not guilty on all counts. Furthermore—" and here he paused, and Rafael would swear later that the old man almost smiled, "—the court notes that the defendant's blasting was, by the testimony of every engineer who examined the site, technically flawless. The mountain came down exactly where she intended it to. The court has rarely seen such workmanship deployed to such an unfortunate purpose, and hopes never to see its like again."

"Understood, Your Honor," Rafael said.

"That will be all."

Outside the courthouse, there were men waiting with contracts. There were always men with contracts; there had been men with contracts since the day the verdict was read, and there would be men with contracts until the sun burned out. They clustered on the courthouse steps like gulls after a fish cart, and their offers were generous, and their handshakes were firm, and their eyes had the particular shine of people who saw other people as mileage.

A consortium wanted her to rebuild the Ashcombe Bridge. A territorial railway offered her chief engineer of the whole northern division. One earnest young man from a company so new it hadn't settled on a name offered her a partnership, a house, and "a legacy, Miss Okonkwo—your name on every mile of track from here to the coast."

Rafael looked at him for a long moment.

"My name," she said, "spent three years buried under somebody else's paperwork. I'm not eager to put it back in the ground."

She declined them all. It took less time than she expected. Declining, it turned out, was like blasting: if you placed the charge correctly, one word did the work of a hundred.

* * *

The provision land lay in the valley below the escarpment, green in spring the way only stolen-and-returned land can be green—as if it had been holding its breath for thirty years.

The families came back in April. Rafael watched them arrive from the porch of the small house she'd rented in Kestrel Falls: wagons and handcarts and one entire family riding atop a piano lashed to a flatbed, the grandmother playing as they rolled through the gate. Children ran the old boundary lines their great-grandparents had walked, shouting the marker names like the names of saints. An old woman named Iyer, who remembered the seizure and had kept the family's claim papers sewn into her coat lining through three moves and two marriages, stood at the gatepost and wept without any effort to stop.

Rafael did not go down to introduce herself. She wasn't sure what they'd make of the woman who had blown up the pass above their land—even if it had been for them. But Mrs. Iyer found her anyway, because old women always do. She climbed the porch steps with the unhurried authority of weather, looked Rafael up and down, and said:

"You're the one who closed the mountain."

"I am."

"The deed says the north forty is ours again. My grandfather plowed that north forty." Her eyes were fierce and wet. "But nobody in my family knows the first thing about the soil anymore. It's been thirty years. Do you?"

"I know rock," Rafael said. "Rock and grade and drainage. Soil is just rock that's decided to be patient."

Mrs. Iyer considered this. "That's the most encouraging thing anyone's said to me all week. Come down tomorrow. Bring your patience."

So began the garden.

Rafael had not planned on a garden. Gardens required staying, and staying was the one skill her life had never taught her. But she found, that first morning, kneeling in the north forty with her hands in dirt that smelled like rain and beginnings, that her body knew how to be still in a way her mind never had. The earth didn't care about miles. The earth cared about today, and whether the frost was done, and whether you'd watered.

She planted by engineering principles. Rows surveyed true. Drainage ditches cut to a two-percent fall, because water, like grief, will find its own level if you don't give it somewhere better to go. Nell came out on weekends and laughed at her graph-paper planting diagrams, then stole them, then produced improved versions with color coding. Okoye visited once, pronounced the compost heap "the finest demolition project in the valley," and left a crate of dynamite on the porch as a housewarming gift, which Rafael accepted and immediately buried at the far end of the property, where it remains to this day, a small geological secret between neighbors.

Baptiste stayed. He took over the kitchen of the boarding house in town and fed half the returning families for free the first month, working off debts he never named and Rafael never asked about. On Sundays he cooked at her place, and he always made the dish Tomas used to make—black-eyed peas with too much pepper—and neither of them ever said why, and both of them always knew.

Voss did not come to the valley. Rafael heard of her secondhand: testifying before the land commission, dismantling her own family's estate claim piece by piece, selling the Voss holdings to fund restitution for the displaced families. It would take years, the papers said. Years, not months.

Rafael read that line twice and caught herself smiling at a newspaper, which was a thing she had never once done in three years of reading newspapers in a hundred different junction hotels.

* * *

In June, she walked up to the cairn.

The trail from the valley to Mile Forty-One took half a day on foot, and Rafael took it slowly, because she could. That was the strange luxury of it—she could take anything slowly now. No schedule. No telegram waiting. No season closing over her head like a trap.

The cairn stood where she had found it that autumn, beside the mile marker, but it had grown. Someone—she suspected Nell, who denied everything with a face as transparent as window glass—had added a low ring of stones around it, and a small wooden post, and carved into the post, in careful letters:

**TOMAS OKONKWO — HE LAID THE FIRST STONE OF THIS LINE AND THE LAST STONE OF OURS**

Below it, fresh flowers. Wild columbine, which grew only on the high slopes. Someone had climbed for them recently.

Rafael sat down beside the cairn the way she used to sit down beside campfires—settling in, making herself comfortable, planning to stay awhile. She talked. She told him about the trial, and the spider in the courtroom window ("you'd have liked her, Tom, she rebuilt the same web four times after the bailiff swept it away, no complaints, no drama, just got back to work"). She told him about the garden, and the tomatoes that were winning, and the beans that were losing, and Mrs. Iyer's opinion that Rafael weeded "like an engineer, which is to say, ruthlessly."

She told him about the bridge collapse—not the official version, not even Baptiste's version, but hers: the sound it made, the way the river took the crew, the way it had taken her too, in a way, and carried her three years downstream through every closed line and empty junction on the map.

"I kept moving so it couldn't catch me," she said. "Turns out it wasn't chasing me at all. It was just waiting for me to sit down."

The wind moved through the gorge below, the same wind that had blown the day the charges brought the pass down, and it sounded different now. Lighter. Or maybe she was.

She stayed until the light went long and gold, and then she stood, brushed off her knees, and put one stone on top of the cairn—the way travelers do, the way family does—to say *I was here, and I'm coming back.*

Then she walked home. Downhill, unhurried, in no direction in particular except toward supper.

* * *

Spring became summer. Summer leaned into autumn, and the escarpment wore its first snow like a crown it had forgotten it owned, and Rafael noticed one evening, setting the table for Sunday dinner, that she had lived in the same house for a full year.

A year. Not a hundred miles. Not forty-one markers. A year, measured in tomatoes ripened and letters answered and the specific way the maple in the yard turned color at the same week it had last year, so that she'd known it was coming, had *waited* for it, the way you wait for a friend.

Nell came for dinner with news: the territorial commission had approved a memorial line—not a freight route, just a narrow track to the summit cutting, to carry visitors to the site where the truth had come out of the mountain. "They want an engineer to consult," Nell said, studying her plate. "I told them I'd ask."

"No," Rafael said, and passed her the peas.

"That's what I told them." Nell grinned. "I just wanted to watch your face."

Baptiste raised his glass. "To the lady who owns nothing but the truth."

"And a garden," Rafael said.

"And a garden," he agreed. "Which is more than most men own, if they're honest about it."

Outside, the first snow of the season was starting—not the killing kind, not yet, just a few flakes turning in the porch light like moths that had gotten ambitious. Rafael watched them through the window and felt none of the old dread. Winter would come. Winter always came. But she had firewood stacked, and a root cellar full, and people who knew where she lived, and in spring the north forty would need her again.

There was a knock at the door.

Rafael opened it to find a girl of maybe nineteen standing on the step, snow in her dark hair, a carpetbag at her feet and a telegram clutched in one mittened hand like it might bite. Behind her, at the gate, a battered trunk sat in the road beside a horse that had clearly been ridden hard for a long way.

"Miss Okonkwo?" the girl said. "My name is Amara Okonkwo. I'm—I think I'm your cousin. From my mother's side, the ones who stayed east when Grandfather lost the land." She held out the telegram with a hand that shook only a little. "The newspaper story reached us in March. Mama cried for a week. And then—" the girl lifted her chin, and for one uncanny moment she was the exact image of Tomas at that age, the same stubborn set of the jaw, the same fire banked behind the eyes, "—and then I saved up, and I came. Because I heard you're the one who knows the land now. And I want to learn."

Rafael stood in the doorway a long moment, snow drifting past her shoulders, the warmth of the kitchen at her back.

Three years ago, a telegram had come for her too, and it had led her up a mountain and back down into her own life. She looked at this one—at the shaking hand, the road-worn trunk, the jaw like her brother's jaw—and she stepped aside, and held the door wide.

"Well," she said. "Come in out of the cold. We've got years, and I'll teach you what to do with them."
