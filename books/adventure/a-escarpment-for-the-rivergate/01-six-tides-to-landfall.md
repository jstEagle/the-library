## 1. Six Tides to Landfall

The Rivergate gave nothing away for free. Ines Halloran had learned that before she learned to swim, and she ran her boat accordingly: cargo lashed twice, fuel figured to the liter, and every passenger treated as freight that talked.

The Meridian expedition was the best-paying freight she'd carried in two seasons. Nine of them, crated instruments and a skiff on a trailer, bound for the survey grounds above the Escarpment where the river narrowed to a throat and the charts went soft at the edges. Their lead, a hydrographer named Vess with salt-and-pepper stubble and the patient hands of a man who'd spent his life listening to water, had paid half up front without haggling. That alone made Ines suspicious. Honest money on the Rivergate was like still water—something upstream was wrong.

She kept the *Tern* hard against the current anyway, six days of running the drowned trade route with its sunken bollards and church spires standing in brown water like the ribs of something that had swallowed a town whole. The fleet's patrol boats passed them twice, gray hulls riding high and arrogant, and each time Vess came out on deck to watch them go with an expression Ines couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Your fleet," he said the second evening, leaning on her rail with a tin cup of her terrible coffee, "buys a lot of silence."

"The fleet buys everything." She checked a mooring cleat that didn't need checking. "Silence is just the cheapest thing on the list."

"And you sell it?"

"I sell passage." She looked at him until he smiled and let it go.

That was the arrangement, and it held for six days because Ines made it hold. She mapped her exits the way other people said their prayers—at anchorages, at fuel stops, at the long slow bends where a boat could be boarded from either bank. She knew which channels were dredged and which were watched, which harbormasters took fleet coin and which took hers, and where the *Tern* could sit dark and cold if anyone came asking questions about who'd moved what, when, for whom. It wasn't paranoia. It was bookkeeping.

The trouble with the Meridian people was that they kept being kind to her.

It started small. The young one, Ansel, who smelled of engine oil and enthusiasm, helped Dov reseat a cooling line without being asked and didn't mention it after. The botanist, Okafor, saved Pell from drowning in a textbook about riparian succession, and Pell—who trusted nobody above the age of twenty-five—actually laughed twice. And Vess asked her questions like her answers mattered. Not about the river's moods; he could read those himself. About her routes. Her soundings. Whether she'd ever run above the Escarpment.

"Once," she said, and left it there, and he was polite enough not to dig.

On the sixth day they came up over the last reach below the Escarpment itself, and the gorge stood up out of the morning mist like a wall that had been arguing with the sky since before there were names for either. The river went through it in a single white vein. Above it, the survey grounds waited with their unrecorded depths, and the expedition unloaded their crates onto the old quarry dock with the giddy efficiency of people who had finally arrived somewhere worth arriving.

Vess walked her to the gangplank as the *Tern*'s engines ticked themselves cool.

"We've got four months of work here," he said. "Maybe five. I'd rather have your boat on contract than any fleet hull. You know these waters better than anyone we've met."

"You haven't met many people."

"Which is why I want you." He said it plainly, no charm in it, just arithmetic he believed she'd respect. "Room and board aboard, full rate, and a share of anything we publish. Stay for the survey."

For one unguarded second—and it was a full second, long enough that she hated herself for all of it—Ines felt the offer land somewhere under her sternum and sit there, warm. A berth above the Escarpment. Work that wanted her specifically. People who had learned her crew's names in six days when the fleet hadn't bothered in fifteen years.

Then the reflex came, the way it always came, smooth and practiced as a mooring knot. *Needed* was just another word for *owned*, and she had been owned once already by this river, back when she was small enough to lose everything in it.

"I don't do contracts past the Escarpment," she said. "Nothing personal. Nothing runs up there but weather and wrecks."

"Wrecks can be interesting to a survey team."

"Not these ones." She put out her hand. "You're paid through to the dock. Radio me at Landfall if you need reprovisioning inside four tides. After that, you're somebody else's problem."

Vess shook it. His grip was dry and careful, a man used to handling things that broke. "If you change your mind—"

"I won't."

She was thirty feet down the plank when he called after her, and she didn't turn around, because turning around was how people got kept.

"Halloran. Whatever's up here that you're avoiding—it keeps better than it drowns."

She told herself it was just surveyor talk. She told herself that all the way downriver, through three locks and a fuel stop, while Pell watched her from the wheelhouse with those too-young eyes and said nothing, which from Pell was a sermon.

* * *

Two days later, anchored at Landfall waiting on a grain consignment that was late the way everything on the Rivergate was late, the Meridian's channel went quiet.

Not static-quiet. Scheduled-quiet—they'd been dutiful about check-ins, twice daily, Vess's voice dry and precise even through the crackle. Then, on the morning of the eighth day, nothing. At noon, nothing. By evening, the band above the Escarpment was a flat dead hiss, and Ines found herself standing at the radio with her hand on the dial, telling herself she was checking it for atmospheric reasons.

"They're scientists," Dov said from the engine room hatch, wiping grease off his hands with a rag that had been black longer than Pell had been alive. "They probably blew a mast. Or they're deep in some gorge where the signal doesn't carry. Or they got drunk on lab alcohol and sank the antenna in a pond."

"Probably," Ines said.

"You're going to go check anyway."

"I'm going to finish this grain run."

Dov looked at her the way he looked at a bearing he suspected was worn. "Sure."

* * *

The fleet came to her before she'd finished loading.

Two men in gray shore clothes with the particular cleanliness of people who didn't do their own laundry, and a harbor office that smelled of ink and wet rope. The older one laid a folder on the desk and opened it toward her, and inside was a contract with more zeros than anything she'd ever signed, and one clause that mattered more than all the rest.

*Salvage and transport services rendered complete. All records of charter to the Meridian Scientific Expedition to be surrendered or expunged. Charterer acknowledges no further knowledge of, interest in, or obligation to said expedition.*

"Forget them," the younger man said, with the gentleness of someone reciting a kindness he didn't understand. "They were a liability before they ever went upriver. Bad science, worse permits. The fleet's absorbing the situation. You were never involved."

Ines read the clause twice. Outside the office window, a patrol boat slid past on the flood tide, gray and smooth as a knife going back in its sheath.

*Absorbing the situation.* She turned the phrase over and found what was underneath it, the way you find a hull breach by feel in dark water. Nine people. A dry, careful man who shook hands like he was afraid of breaking things. A boy who fixed cooling lines for strangers. Twice-daily check-ins that had stopped mid-schedule, not faded out.

And a fleet willing to pay this much to make sure no one went looking.

"That's a lot of money for bad science," she said.

"It's a lot of money for good silence." The older man smiled. "You've sold it before, Captain. Everyone on this river has. This is just the largest invoice."

He was right, and that was the worst part. She had sold silence her whole life, one charter at a time, because the alternative was sinking and she had a crew to feed and a boat that was the only thing she'd ever let herself love. Selling silence was survival. It was arithmetic.

But the arithmetic had changed. Somewhere between the quarry dock and this desk, nine people had become a number the fleet wanted erased, and Ines Halloran knew exactly what erasure looked like. She'd grown up reading ledgers that had holes in them shaped like ships. Shaped like one ship in particular.

She pushed the folder back across the desk.

"Contract's complete," she said. "Charter records are mine. I keep my own books."

The older man's smile stayed exactly where it was, which told her everything. "Captain. Think carefully. This offer expires."

"So does everything." She stood. "You know where to find me if the fleet needs cargo moved. That's the only thing I sell."

Walking out, she expected to feel lighter. Refusal usually did that—the clean snap of it, the old familiar shape of leaving before she could be left. Instead what she felt was the ache, dull and insistent, of having been needed and having said no, and beneath it, rising like cold water into a bilge, a question she'd been dodging for two days:

Who else was going to go?

Nobody. That was the answer. Nobody went above the Escarpment except weather, wrecks, and her.

Pell was waiting at the dock, sitting on the bollard with knees drawn up, watching the patrol boat across the basin with open dislike. They didn't ask what happened in the office. They just looked up, and in the look was the question Ines had been refusing since the radio went quiet.

Ines looked at the *Tern*, fueled and provisioned, riding easy on the flood. She thought about exits mapped, about the clean life of a woman who was never involved, about a folder full of zeros and a clause that said *forget*.

"Get Dov," she heard herself say. "We're running the gorge tonight."

Pell's face did something complicated and young. "Above the Escarpment?"

"Above the Escarpment." Ines stepped aboard and put her hand on the wheel, on the wood her father's hands had worn smooth before the river took him and every record that he'd ever existed. "Six tides to monsoon. We'll need every one."
