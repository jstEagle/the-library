## 2. What the Sandbar Keeps

The skiff sat on the sandbar like a thing that had given up.

Ines saw it first from the wheelhouse of the *Kestrel's Debt*, a pale sliver against the brown water where the reach bent wide and shallow around Graveling Point. She cut the throttle and let the hull drift the last hundred yards, because there was no need to announce herself to a dead boat. The morning fog was burning off in rags. Herons stood in the shallows like disapproving clerks.

"That's a survey skiff," Dov said, coming up beside her with a wrench still in his hand and grease on his jaw. He'd been under the deck plates since dawn chasing a fuel line that wanted to be something worse. "Meridian markings. That's their tender."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know it shouldn't be here. This is six miles below where they said they'd anchor."

Ines said nothing. She brought the *Debt* alongside with the gentleness she reserved for things that deserved better than they'd gotten, and Pell dropped the fenders over the rail without being told. The boy was quick that way—quick at everything except talking about himself—and Ines had stopped asking questions he didn't want to answer somewhere around Vellum Bend. A runaway was a runaway. The river didn't care why.

The skiff lay heeled over on its beam, half aground, and one look told Ines everything the sandbar had been keeping. The seacocks were open. All of them. Not just the drain plug pulled by some careless hand—the seacocks, brass wheels spun open, which no one did by accident, which no one did at all unless they wanted the boat on the bottom. It had flooded, grounded, and settled here instead, saved from drowning only by the river's own laziness.

"Somebody scuttled her," Pell said quietly. He had gone down into the skiff's cockpit and come back up holding a canvas bag, dry inside because it had floated up under the gunwale as the water rose. "Deliberate."

"I can see that."

"Why would they scuttle their own tender?"

Ines took the bag from him. Inside: a signal flare pistol, a tin of waterproof matches, a chart case with the Meridian expedition's stamp burned into the leather, and—a small cold weight at the bottom—a handheld beacon, the kind survey teams carried as insurance, the kind you buried or hid when you thought someone might come looking for you. Its casing was scratched. Its switch was off.

She turned it over twice in her hands and put it in her coat pocket, and she did not explain why, and neither Dov nor Pell asked, because that was the shape of the *Kestrel's Debt*: three people who had each, for their own reasons, decided not to know things.

"They were scared," Dov said. He'd climbed down onto the skiff's hull and was peering through the flooded cabin glass. "Scared enough to kill their own boat. You don't do that because of weather."

"You don't do it because of bandits either," Ines said. "Bandits take the boat."

"So what does it?"

Ines looked upriver, toward the Escarpment, where the gorge narrowed to a throat of black stone and the Meridian expedition—eleven scientists, their gear, their careful optimism—had gone silent two days ago. She looked downriver, toward the fleet patrol station at Corben's Landing, smoke from its galley just visible above the treeline. And she looked at the skiff, which was an answer written in brass and floodwater, if she was willing to read it.

"It means somebody with authority wanted them gone," she said. "Or wanted them quiet."

The word *quiet* sat in the air between them. On the Rivergate, quiet was currency. The salvage fleet bought it by the ton—paid captains to forget cargoes, paid harbormasters to lose manifests, paid widows to stop asking. Ines had sold her share of it over the years. Not much. Just enough. Enough to keep the *Debt* licensed, berthed, and invisible, which was all she had ever wanted to be.

"There'll be patrols through this reach by afternoon," Dov said. "There always are, after anything."

"Then we're done here." She turned for the gangway. "Pell, cast off that line. We were never alongside."

"We weren't?"

"We passed a wrecked skiff at distance. We noted it. We kept moving." She heard her own voice go flat and official, the voice she used at customs gates, and hated how easily it came. "That's what happened."

Dov looked at her for a long moment. Twenty years he'd been her engineer, twenty years of fixing what she broke and breaking nothing she couldn't fix, and in all that time he had learned exactly one thing about her worth knowing: that Ines Halloran could walk past any door in the world as long as she didn't have to open it.

"And if they ask what we noted?" he said.

"Then we note it."

* * *

Pell had never seen anyone lie to the fleet before.

He'd seen people lie *around* the fleet, the way water lies around a rock—curving, yielding, saying nothing. The dockhands at Corben's Landing lied with their shoulders, hunching small. The toll clerks lied with their eyes, fixed on paperwork. Everyone on the Rivergate had learned the same lesson young: the fleet was the river's weather, and you did not argue with weather. You dressed for it. You waited it out. You kept your head down and your name out of ledgers.

So when the patrol boat came sweeping up the reach at midafternoon—grey hull, fleet pennant snapping, bow gun covered but present the way a fist is covered but present—Pell braced himself on the foredeck and waited for Captain Halloran to do what everyone did.

The patrol boat hailed them. "Cargo vessel, this is Fleet Patrol Six-One. Come about and hold position. We have questions regarding the survey vessel *Meridian*."

He watched Ines step to the rail. She wasn't hunched. She wasn't small. She stood the way she stood at the wheel in bad water, feet planted, weight low, and when she answered, her voice carried across the water level and clear.

"*Kestrel's Debt*, cargo and salvage, bound downriver from Vellum. Holding."

A launch came across. Two fleet officers, one of them a lieutenant with a clipboard and the other a big man whose job was clearly to stand behind the lieutenant and be shaped like a threat. They came up the *Debt*'s side without asking, which was also the fleet's way.

"You passed through this reach yesterday evening," the lieutenant said. "And again this morning. Correct?"

"Correct."

"Did you see the *Meridian*'s tender?"

"I saw a swamped skiff on the sandbar at Graveling Point." Ines gestured vaguely downriver, and Pell felt his stomach drop, because she was pointing *away* from where they'd actually found it, away from the little cove where Dov had towed it and sunk it properly an hour before the patrol arrived, deep enough that no casual sonar sweep would call it anything but riverbottom. "Half under. Looked like someone left her open in the rain. I logged it. I can show you the log."

She could. Because she'd written it—an entry dated yesterday, time approximated, position approximate, *swamped skiff, no survivors visible, no assistance rendered, continued downriver*. Written last night, in ink that was already dry, describing a sighting that hadn't happened yet. Pell had watched her write it by lamplight and had understood, with a slow cold feeling, that Captain Halloran had known the fleet would come before the fleet knew it themselves.

The lieutenant consulted his clipboard. "You ferried the *Meridian* expedition upriver eleven days ago."

"I ferry everybody. It's what I do."

"Their radio went silent two days ago. The fleet is conducting a search." His eyes came up from the clipboard, and they were not searching eyes; they were measuring eyes. "You wouldn't know anything about their movements above the narrows."

"No." Ines said it simply, the way you say *the sky is up*. "I dropped them at the Escarpment basecamp and took a contract downriver. What they did after my keel cleared their anchorage is their business."

"You didn't stay for the survey? They offered, I'm told. Good money."

"I don't linger above the narrows." Something moved behind her face then, fast, like a fish under ice, and was gone. "Bad water. Bad memories. Ask anyone."

The big officer behind the lieutenant shifted his weight, and Pell saw his gaze travel—not to Ines, but to the deck, to the winch, to the fresh scratches on the caprail where a skiff's gunwale had rested during a tow that officially never happened. The officer saw them too. Pell watched him see them. And the officer said nothing, and wrote nothing, and Pell understood that this was how the fleet worked: everyone noticed everything, and noticing was a debt, and debts were collected later, with interest.

"Well," the lieutenant said, closing his clipboard. "If you recall anything further—anything at all—the fleet compensates useful memory. Generously."

"I'll keep it in mind."

"One more thing." The lieutenant paused at the rail, halfway down the ladder, and looked back up. "The *Corriander*. You're listed as next of kin to a Halloran, crewman, lost thirty years ago. The record notes the wreck site as unrecovered, location unknown." A beat. "Location unknown to *us*, that is. If your river work ever turns up anything related, the same compensation applies. More, even. Some wrecks are worth more than others."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Pell counted them.

"My father's been dead thirty years," Ines said. "The river keeps what it keeps."

"Indeed it does." The lieutenant smiled with exactly half his face. "Good day, Captain."

The launch pulled away. The patrol boat swung in a wide grey arc and ran on upriver, toward the Escarpment, toward whatever it was really looking for, and its wake rocked the *Kestrel's Debt* gently, like the river shrugging.

Pell stood at the rail until the sound of the engines faded. Then he went aft, where Ines had already turned back to the wheelhouse, and found her standing over the chart table with the handheld beacon from her coat pocket lying on the glass. Her thumb rested on its switch. Not pressing it. Resting.

"Captain," he said. It was the most he'd said to her in a week.

She didn't look up. "They know we found the skiff. The big one saw the scratches."

"Then why lie?"

"Because the truth is a door," Ines said, "and once it's open, you don't get to close it again." She finally raised her head, and Pell saw that she wasn't afraid—or rather, that she was, and had decided to carry it anyway, the way you carry a heavy crate across a narrow gangplank, one step, then the next, because putting it down meant losing it. "Eleven people are missing up that river. The fleet isn't searching for them. The fleet is searching for whatever made them run. And that beacon in my pocket belonged to people who were scared enough to sink their own boat, which means they hid something, which means the something is still hidden, which means—" She stopped. Breathed. "Which means I'm going up the narrows after dark, and every patrol between here and the Escarpment is going to be told I went home to Corben's Landing."

Pell looked at the chart table. At the route she'd already penciled in, faint, erasable, deniable: the night channel through the gorge narrows, the one no licensed captain ran, the one marked in old red ink in a hand that wasn't hers—*D. Halloran, 30 yrs past*.

His father had run from three different towns before the river took him in, and Pell had learned early that drifting felt like freedom right up until the current chose for you. Watching Ines Halloran stand over that chart, thumb on a dead man's beacon, pencil line running toward the worst water on the Rivergate, he understood he was watching the other thing. The moment the current stops choosing. The moment you put the oar in the water yourself.

"You could still sell them the memory," he said. "The lieutenant said it pays well."

"He did." Ines picked up the beacon and pocketed it, and something in her jaw set like poured concrete. "Get below and tell Dov we fuel at dusk. Full tanks. And Pell—" now she did look at him, straight on, and it was the first time she'd ever looked at him like he was crew instead of cargo, "—if you want off at Corben's Landing, say it now. After tonight, there's no getting off."

Pell thought about the flooded skiff. About the beacon buried like a promise. About eleven strangers who had trusted the wrong river.

"I'm in," he said.

Ines nodded once, and turned to the window, where the Escarpment stood far upriver in the haze—black stone, white water, and somewhere above it, the wreck the fleet had spent thirty years pretending wasn't there.

"Tide turns in four hours," she said. "We go up with it."
