## 3. The Escarpment Road

The gorge did not have a night the way the low river had a night. Down at Rivergate, dark was a soft thing, a curtain drawn over the water, and you could still smell the fish markets and hear the dredge cranes groaning in their sleep. Up here, above the last beacon chain, darkness came down like a lid. The cliffs rose on either side of the channel so sheer and so high that the stars were only a rumor, a thin seam of light directly overhead, and the river itself went black as spilled ink.

Ines ran it by feel and by memory, hands light on the wheel of the *Salt Lark*, engine throttled back to a murmur that wouldn't carry. No running lights. No sonar ping. The fleet's patrol boats had infrared eyes and patient habits, and patience was the one weapon she'd never been able to buy or steal.

"Beacon's still singing," Pell whispered from the bow rail, cupping one hand around the little direction-finder loop they'd rigged from the Meridian's spare parts. "Bearing's holding. Two points off the starboard cliff face."

"I hear it." She didn't, not really—the signal was a whisper buried under the river's static—but she liked hearing Pell say it. The boy had good ears and better nerves, which was more than she could say for most people who'd ever signed aboard her.

Dov's head appeared through the engine hatch, grease to his elbows, hair standing up like he'd been arguing with the machinery and losing. "She'll hold together if we don't ask her to argue with rocks," he said. "That's my professional opinion. Ask me again after the narrows and I might revise it."

"Then we won't ask her to argue with rocks," Ines said.

"That's the whole job, Captain. That's literally the whole job."

She almost smiled. Almost. Her eyes stayed on the black water ahead, where the channel bent left around a spur of rock the old charts called the Deacon's Finger, because it pointed accusingly at every fool who tried to pass it in the dark.

The distress beacon had been buried—actually buried, in a waterproof case wedged into a crevice forty feet up the cliff wall, above any flood line, above anything but a climber's reach. They'd found its location written in cipher on the back of Dr. Amara Voss's tide tables, tucked into the skiff's locker before Ines scuttled what was left of the boat herself, so the fleet would find nothing worth finding. *Insurance,* Voss had called it, back when the expedition was still alive and laughing on her deck, still offering her wine and a place at their table. *Every survey team buries one. You hope nobody ever digs it up.*

Somebody had dug it up now. Somebody had turned it on. Which meant somebody up there was alive, and desperate enough to spend their one insurance policy on a stranger's promise.

*If anyone comes looking, come quiet, come at night, and come alone.*

They were not alone. They were three, in a shallow-draft salvage tug with a hull full of dive gear, contraband fuel, and a lie she'd told a fleet officer with a straight face—and the lie was still warm in her mouth, two days old, tasting like copper.

*No sign of them past the sandbar, Commander. Skiff was empty when I found it. Current takes everything downriver eventually.*

Pell had watched her say it. She'd felt his eyes on her back like sunburn. The boy had run from a fleet orphanage at fourteen and had spent three years learning to read her the way other people read weather, and he knew—she was certain he knew—that she had crossed a line in the sand of her own life and could not uncross it. Before the sandbar, she had been a woman who kept her head down and her ledger clean. After it, she was something else. A woman who lied to the fleet. A woman running upstream toward trouble with her name on none of the paperwork and all of the consequences.

The Deacon's Finger slid past to port, close enough that she could have touched it with a boat hook. The current grabbed the *Lark*'s stern and swung her, playful as a cat, and Ines corrected with a touch of throttle, feathering the wheel, feeling the river's mood through the steel of the hull the way her father had taught her to feel it before she was tall enough to see over the gunwale.

Her father. There he was again, uninvited, sitting in the wheelhouse with her like he always did above the third beacon. The Corriander water lay somewhere ahead of them, though no chart admitted it. Somewhere above the Escarpment, in a stretch of river that official record insisted had never claimed a ship at all, the *Corriander* had gone down with eleven souls aboard, and the fleet's ledgers recorded the event as a clerical error—a cargo manifest misfiled, a route never sailed, a ship that had never existed. She had looked. God, how she had looked, at sixteen, at nineteen, at twenty-five, in port offices and archive houses and once, drunk and weeping, in the memory vaults of a retired harbor clerk who took pity on her and showed her an empty drawer. Erased. Scrubbed. As if grief were a debt the fleet refused to acknowledge, and therefore did not exist.

"You're doing the thing," Dov said quietly. He'd climbed up beside her without her noticing, which was his gift. He handed her a tin cup of something hot and terrible. "The navigating-by-ghosts thing."

"It's just navigation."

"Sure." He leaned against the bulkhead, arms folded, watching the black water with her. "You've been quiet since the sandbar. Quieter than usual, and your usual is already a library."

Ines drank the terrible tea. Below them, the channel narrowed. She could feel it in the way the current quickened, the banks closing in, the echo of the engine changing pitch against stone. The Escarpment Road, the old pilots called this reach—not because it was a road, but because it was the only way through, and roads, unlike rivers, implied permission.

"My father ran this stretch," she heard herself say. It surprised her, coming out. Dov didn't move, didn't look at her, which was why she kept talking. "Before I was born. Before the Corriander. He used to say the gorge at night was the only honest place on the river. No signals, no harbormasters, no fleet. Just you and the water telling you the truth about whether you're any good."

"And is it?"

"Hm?"

"Telling you the truth?"

She considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, which was complete. "It's telling me I'm running cargo I didn't declare to men I lied to, toward a wreck the river says isn't there, to dig up a truth the fleet drowned thirty years ago." A pause. "So yes. Honest place."

Dov huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then: "The Corriander. That's his ship? The one that's not in any book?"

Ines said nothing. The silence said it for her, and Dov, who had served four years on her deck without ever asking about the photograph taped inside her chart drawer—a man on a wheelhouse deck, squinting into sun, one hand raised mid-wave—understood that the silence was the answer and the thanks both.

Ahead, the narrows opened their throat.

* * *

The first blockade was a joke, if you had a gallows sense of humor. Two patrol boats anchored across the widest part of the channel below Harker's Bend, floodlights sweeping the water in slow, arrogant arcs, a boom of chained pontoons strung between them. Ines took the *Lark* up the far side, into the shadow of the western cliff, where the current ran fast and treacherous over a shelf of submerged rock that no sane pilot would attempt at night.

"Shelf's got maybe four feet of water on it," Dov murmured, reading the depth sounder's muted display. "Three and a half, at the trough."

"Then we'll be polite and only use three."

"Ines."

"Dov." She put her hand briefly on his shoulder. "I've run this shelf six times. I've run it loaded. We are, tonight, gloriously empty."

"We are not empty. We're carrying enough guilt to sink us."

"Guilt floats," she said. "Ask any fleet captain."

She killed the engine entirely and let momentum carry them, the *Lark* ghosting up the shelf with the current pushing from behind and gravity pulling from ahead, and for ninety seconds there was nothing in the world but the sound of water on stone and Pell's breathing at the bow, quick and controlled, and the floodlight beam swinging toward them, closer, closer—

—and past. The light swept over the cliff face above their heads, caught a family of roosting birds into indignant flight, and moved on. The *Lark* scraped something soft—silt, only silt—and then the shelf dropped away beneath her keel and they were through, sliding into the deep pool above the boom while behind them the patrol crews smoked and dozed and guarded a river that had already given them the slip.

Pell let out a breath that sounded like it had been held since the sandbar. "Captain," he whispered, "that was the worst thing I've ever loved."

"Wait until the second one."

"There's a second one?"

"The Narrows Gate. Old lock works, fleet checkpoint, searchlights mounted on the lock tower." She checked her watch against the tide tables she'd memorized rather than written down. "But the gate keeper changes shift at slack water, and slack water is in forty minutes, and there's a maintenance culvert on the north side that the fleet sealed on paper but never sealed in fact, because sealing things costs money and pretending things are sealed costs nothing."

"You've done this before," Pell said. Not a question.

"I've done everything before," Ines said. "That's the problem with me."

* * *

The Narrows Gate passed in a long, cold hour of wading through a maintenance culvert with the ceiling close enough to touch, guiding the *Lark* through by hand on lines, Dov cursing in three languages at the rusted sluice gears while Pell stood chest-deep in the outflow watching the lock tower's searchlight rotate its patient circle. Ines went last, as always, and stood a moment in the culvert's mouth with the water pulling at her knees, looking back downriver at the lights of the checkpoint.

From here, going back would be easy. Going back meant the sandbar, the lie, the fat contract she hadn't signed, the small safe life of a woman who ferried other people's futures and never risked her own. Going forward meant the Escarpment, and whatever waited above it—survivors or corpses, truth or trap, and always, always, the place where the charts went blank and the Corriander's grave lay unmarked under water that officially remembered nothing.

"Ines?" Pell's voice, echoing down the culvert. "You coming?"

She came.

Above the gate, the river changed character entirely. The walls fell back, the current gentled, and the channel climbed in long terraces toward the Escarpment itself—that final wall of basalt where the river dropped two hundred feet in a series of falls and locks that even the fleet only maintained half-heartedly. And there, faint but steady now, unmistakable, the buried beacon sang its small stubborn song.

Pell raised the direction-finder loop and turned slowly. "Strong," he reported. "Close. Northeast bank, maybe two miles up. There's—" He stopped. Lowered the loop. "Captain, there's a light. On the water. Just one lantern, moving slow."

Ines took the glasses. Far up the terrace channel, a single yellow point bobbed against the black, moving with the unhurried rhythm of someone who had nowhere to hide and no strength left to run.

"Could be a trap," Dov said quietly. "Fleet's had two days to set one."

"Could be," Ines agreed. She kept the glasses up. The lantern swung, paused, swung again—an old signal, older than the fleet, older than the ledgers. Three swings, pause. Three swings, pause. The riverman's call for help, the one her father's generation had used before radios made honesty optional.

"They know the old codes," she said. "Fleet doesn't teach those anymore." She handed the glasses to Pell and reached for the throttle. "Nobody traps you with your own language."

The *Salt Lark* came up the last terrace with her running lights still dark and her engine barely breathing, and the lantern grew, and grew, and resolved into a figure standing knee-deep on a flooded quarry ramp, holding the light high with both shaking hands—a woman, gaunt, mud to her waist, wearing the torn remains of a Meridian expedition jacket.

Behind her, in the cliff's black mouth, other shapes stirred. More than one. Alive.

And above them all, carved into the basalt where the river bent, half-swallowed by thirty years of moss, Ines saw—for the first time since she was a child, since the funeral that wasn't a funeral, since the drawer that was empty—the letters she had spent her whole life pretending not to look for:

**CORRIANDER MEMORIAL — ERECTED BY THE CREW'S FAMILIES — DEFACED BY ORDER OF THE RIVERGATE FLEET COMMISSION**

Someone had recently cut through the moss with a knife. Fresh letters, scratched crude and deep beneath the old defaced ones, in a hand that shook:

**WE REMEMBER. ASK THE WOMAN ON THE SALT LARK.**

Ines stood at the rail with the engine idling and the beacon singing and her father's name rising out of the dark like a body finally surfacing, and she understood that whatever happened next, she had not come up this river by accident. The river had been calling her home for thirty years.

It had simply needed her to bring witnesses.
