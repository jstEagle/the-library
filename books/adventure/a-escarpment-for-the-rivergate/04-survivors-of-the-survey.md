## 4. Survivors of the Survey

The beacon led them up a side channel so narrow the boughs closed overhead like hands, and Pell thought, not for the first time, that the river had more doors than the maps admitted. The *Corriander's Wake* slid through green shadow, Dov nursing the engine to a whisper, Ines at the bow with a sounding pole, reading the water the way other people read letters from home.

"Quarry ahead," she said. "Kill it."

Dov killed it. The silence afterward was enormous, filled with water dripping off leaves and somewhere, faint and wrong for this deep in the gorge, the sound of hammers.

Pell heard it too. Not machinery—hammers on stone, patient, unhurried, the sound of people building something they intended to keep.

Ines raised a fist. They drifted the last hundred yards, Pell shipping the oars as quietly as she'd ever done anything in her short criminal life, and the quarry opened before them like a wound in the hillside: a great flooded bowl of terraced rock, its lower levels drowned in water the color of bottle glass, its upper rim stitched with tarps and rope bridges and smoke rising thin from cook fires. Someone had made a life here. Three weeks of one, by the look of the scaffolding.

And standing at the rim with a fleet-issue carbine slung across his back and no hand anywhere near it, a man in a fleet coat with the insignia torn off at the shoulder.

"Don't come to the ladder," he called down, voice carrying flat across the water. "I'll bring them out. Halloran—I know your boat. I know your name."

Pell felt Ines go still beside her, the particular stillness of a woman recalculating everything.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Ines called back.

"Ossler. Patrol division, Reach Nine." He said it like a man reading his own indictment. "I'm the reason your expedition stopped transmitting. And I'm the only reason three of them are still breathing."

* * *

He came out to them in a patched skiff, alone, oar in both hands, carbine left behind on the quarry rim where anyone could have taken it. Up close he was younger than his voice—thirty maybe, with river-silt dried white in the creases of his neck and eyes that had the look of a man who'd stopped sleeping in shifts because there was nothing left worth dreaming about.

"You're the runaway," he said to Pell, nodding at her. "Off the *Kindly Fortune*. There's a notice."

"There's notices for half the river," Pell said.

"There were," Ossler said. "There won't be soon. That's rather the point of all this." He looked past her to Ines. "Three survivors. Dr. Amara Voss, two of her students. The others—" He stopped. Started again, flatter. "The others didn't make the walk out. I can take you to the ones who did, if you come armed with patience instead of guns. They've been expecting the fleet to finish the job for three weeks. You'll have to be someone else before they'll be anything but afraid."

"And what are you?" Pell asked, before she could stop herself. "If you're not the fleet anymore?"

Ossler looked at her then, really looked, and Pell saw something underneath the exhaustion that frightened her more than any patrol boat—a kind of terrible clarity, like a man who had done arithmetic he could never unlearn.

"I'm the man who read the orders," he said.

* * *

They came up the quarry ladder single file, and the camp unfolded around them in details that told the story faster than any confession: survey instruments cannibalized into weather vanes, a radio tower built from salvage and hope, garden beds scratched into quarry spoil growing something stubborn and green. People had meant to survive here. People had organized their fear into rows.

Dr. Voss met them at the fire, a small woman with a broken arm splinted from survey tripod legs, and she did not flinch from Ines the way Pell had expected. She looked at her instead with an expression Pell couldn't parse until later—like a person deciding how much weight a rope could hold before climbing onto it.

"Ines Halloran," Voss said. "Daughter of Edmun Halloran."

The name went through the clearing like a dropped lantern. One of the students made a sound. The other turned away.

"My father's been dead eleven years," Ines said, level, and Pell heard the cost of the levelness.

"No," Voss said gently. "That's the thing. Sit down, Captain. Please. This is going to take longer than you want it to, and shorter than you deserve."

She sent the students for a case—aluminum, sealed, wrapped in oilcloth—and while they fetched it she talked, in the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this testimony every night against the dark.

"We weren't surveying fish stocks. We were mapping the Escarpment reach for the fleet's new tow route, and the sonar kept returning anomalies below the old channel—structures, regular geometry, wreckage in formation. Too much wreckage. Wrecks don't travel in fleets, Captain. Fleets do." She drew breath. "We found the massacre site. Forty years back, maybe more—the records are burned, but the hulls tell it. A convoy of refugee barges, sunk together, in a line, by charges set along their waterlines. Not storm damage. Not collision. Set charges, placed by divers, in a pattern."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, the hammers had gone quiet.

"The fleet didn't just sink them," Voss said. "They erased them. Every chart, every ledger, every mouth that remembered. And when our sonar started pulling that history up out of the silt, the fleet sent Ossler's unit to close the site. Permanently."

"They dynamited it," the older student said. He was maybe twenty, with a burn scar down one forearm. "Two nights after we stopped transmitting. They blew the wreck field. Buried it under half the escarpment face."

Pell watched Ines's face across the fire and understood, in a cold slide, that she was watching a woman hear her own death sentence read in someone else's name.

"The Corriander," Ines said. It wasn't quite a question.

Voss nodded slowly. "Your father's ship was moored over the site. Salvage contract—legitimate, or made to look legitimate. He found what we found. And he tried to report it." She reached out and touched the aluminum case as the students set it down. "This is his correspondence. Fleet intercepts. His last transmission, which they answered with demolition charges. The Corriander wasn't lost, Captain. She was removed. And the man who ordered it signed the order that killed my colleagues two weeks ago with the same pen."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned to embers and Voss slept propped against a crate, Pell found Ines at the quarry rim, looking down at the drowned terraces where the light died green.

Pell had grown up learning to read adults the way sailors read weather—out of necessity, aboard a boat where moods were barometric pressure and you learned to reef early. She knew grief when she saw it. She knew the difference between grief and this. This was grief colliding with history, a private wound suddenly discovering it had ten thousand siblings.

"You knew," Pell said quietly. "Some part of you always knew it wasn't the storm."

"Some part of me knew," Ines said. "Knowing and knowing are different rivers." Her jaw worked. "Eleven years I stayed off this stretch of water. Eleven years I mapped every route that avoided the Escarpment and told myself it was economics." She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "The whole fleet knew. The whole fleet has known since before I could swim, and they let me grow up thinking my father was a careless man in a careless storm."

"They're going to bury this too," Pell said. "Us, the data, the doctor. Same pattern. Charges along the waterline."

"Yes."

"So what do we do?"

Ines was quiet long enough that Pell counted her own heartbeats. Then, below them, Ossler emerged from the shadows of the tool shed carrying a second case—smaller, battered, marked with faded naval stenciling—and climbed toward them with the deliberate tread of a man bringing something he should have brought hours ago.

"There's one more thing," he said, setting it down between them. "Recovered from the Corriander's debris field before the demolition. The fleet doesn't know I have it. Her sealed log, Captain. Your father's voice, in his own hand." He looked at Ines with those sleepless, clear eyes. "And the tide tables say you've got six tides before monsoon closes the gorge. The fleet knows you're alive. By tomorrow they'll know where."

Pell watched Ines put her hand on the case, watched her knuckles whiten around a dead man's handwriting, and understood that whatever came next would not be running, and would not be hiding, and would not be quiet.

"How fast can we load?" Ines said.
