## 5. The Fleet's Arithmetic

Ossler came down the quarry path with his rifle slung across his back instead of in his hands, and that small reversal told Ines everything before he said a word.

He was a big man gone soft at the edges, the way river men went soft—not fat, but loosened, as if the water had spent years patiently untying his knots. His fleet grays had been dyed brown with something local, bark or mud, and he walked like a man who had stopped expecting to be shot at and wasn't sure yet whether that was wisdom or a mistake.

"Captain Halloran," he said. "You're the one who ferried them up."

"I'm the one asking what happened to them."

"Then you'd better come inside. And you'd better decide, before you do, whether you're going to be able to leave knowing it." He looked past her shoulder at Dov and Pell, standing among the reeds with the skiff tied off behind them. "All of you. That's not a threat. It's just arithmetic."

The flooded quarry had been a survey camp once—canvas shelters lashed to the rock face, a radio mast leaning like a drunk against the cliff, solar panels angled toward a sky that hadn't given them much. Three survivors. A geologist named Vantrease with a broken wrist splinted from a tent pole; a young hydrologist, Sef, who wouldn't stop looking at the water; and Dr. Amara Iqbal, the expedition lead, who sat on an ammunition crate with her hands flat on her knees like a woman holding herself down in a wind.

They had data. Ossler said it plainly, because he seemed to be a man who had run out of patience for anything else. Survey records. Sonar maps of the riverbed above the Escarpment. And beneath those, older charts—the fleet's own charts, stamped and sealed, pulled from an archive nobody was supposed to know existed.

"The Corriander," Iqbal said, watching Ines's face when she said it.

Ines kept her face still. It was a skill she'd practiced for twenty years.

"You knew the name," Iqbal said.

"Everyone knows the name. It's a ghost story on this stretch of river."

"It's not a ghost story." Iqbal opened a waterproof case and turned it so the screen faced Ines: sonar imagery, grainy but unmistakable—a hull section lying in the silt above the Escarpment, and around it, radiating outward in a pattern no wreck makes naturally, a starburst of fracture lines. Blast patterns. Deliberate ones. "Your father's ship was dynamited after she sank. Someone wanted the site erased. And the reason is in these files—what the Corriander's crew saw, what they tried to report, what the fleet did about it. There were others, Captain. Before yours. The river mouth landings, eleven years back. The convoy that never arrived at Rennick. It's all here. Names. Dates. Fleet channel logs."

Ines heard herself say, very evenly, "And you've been sitting on this in a hole in the ground for how long?"

"Since our transmitter died," Sef said, and his voice cracked on *died* in a way that made it clear the transmitter hadn't failed on its own. "Ossler was sent to make sure we stayed quiet. He decided not to."

Ossler shrugged, a heavy, tired motion. "I've been fleet twenty-two years. I signed on to keep the shipping lanes clear, not to drown scientists and dynamite dead men's bones. There's a line. I found it." He met her eyes. "I'm crossing it now. With your help or without it."

"There it is," Dov murmured beside her. "The arithmetic."

* * *

They held the council in the skiff, because Ines didn't trust walls anymore—not canvas walls, not stone ones, nothing that couldn't be left quickly. The rain had started again, that thin Rivergate drizzle that wasn't weather so much as climate, and it ticked against the hull while Dov laid it out in his flat engineer's way, counting on thick fingers.

"Every berth we've ever used is fleet-licensed. Saltmarsh, Corder's Landing, the fuel dock at Tern—fleet concessions, every one. Our cargo contracts are brokered through Merrow & Sons, who hold the fleet's freight charter. Half the pilots we pay to look the other way at checkpoints are on fleet retainer already—they just don't know we know." He paused. "Our mooring at Rivergate proper is registered under a license my cousin forged nine years ago. If the fleet pulls that thread, we're not smugglers anymore. We're pirates. Every gun on the river gets to be legal against us."

"So we run for the coast," Pell said. She was crouched by the outboard, pretending to check the fuel line, which was fine, because Ines had taught her that a person should always have their hands on something when the conversation got heavy. "There's harbors downcoast that aren't fleet. Free ports. We sell the data quiet—to a news syndicate, a rival company, whoever pays—and we buy new names and a new river."

"And then what?" Ines asked.

Pell blinked. "Then we're safe."

"We're safe," Ines agreed, "and the files go into somebody's vault. Some syndicate sits on them until the story's worth more than the scandal, or some company buries them deeper than the fleet did, and in ten years there's a bar story about the Corriander and a boy who swears his grandfather crewed her." She looked at the rain ticking off the river. "Quiet means it drowns again, Pell. That's the whole trick of the fleet. They don't have to win fights. They just have to wait. Everything sinks eventually if nobody holds it up."

Dov shifted. "You're talking about broadcasting it. Openly."

"I'm talking about making it too loud to sink."

"You're talking about spending everything," Dov said. Not angry—Dov never got angry, he got precise. "Not just the boat. The licenses, the contacts, the twelve years of building a name nobody looks twice at. You're talking about becoming the most famous woman on this river, and the most hunted. I want you to say it plain, Captain, because I've been doing sums since Ossler walked down that hill, and every sum ends the same way. We can't half-do this. Either we vanish tonight and live, or we stand up tomorrow and probably die—but we can't stand up quietly. Quiet isn't a thing this kind of truth knows how to be."

The rain filled the silence. Downriver, somewhere past the narrows, a patrol engine idled—a sound so familiar Ines usually didn't hear it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. She heard it now.

She thought about her father's log, wherever it was, in whatever was left of the Corriander's hull. She thought about how she had spent her whole life navigating around one spot on the river, the way you navigate around a grave, and how the fleet had made even the grief illegal—had taken the wreck itself away, dynamited it, so that she couldn't even mourn in the right place. They hadn't just killed him. They'd stolen the body of the proof.

"They gave me a contract two days ago," she said. "To forget the Meridian. Fat money. I took it. I signed the paper and I took the advance and I told myself it was business." She turned the coin over in her fingers—the advance, still in her pocket, worn smooth. "That was the last time I get to be invisible. I understand that. I'm telling you both so you understand it too, because you still have a choice and I'm past mine. The moment I read those files, I stopped being a woman who runs cargo. Now I'm a witness. Witnesses don't get to pick quiet."

Pell stood up slowly, fuel line forgotten. "So we fight."

"I didn't say fight. I said loud. Fighting's just one way to be loud and it's the way that gets us killed fastest." Ines looked at Dov. "How long to rig the Meridian's survey gear? The broadcast array they were using for the bathymetry pings?"

Dov's eyebrows rose, slow, the way they did when an idea landed in him sideways and he needed a moment to feel its weight. "It's built to push signal through water and rock. Through *anything*. Rigged right, pointed right—" He exhaled. "Two tides. Maybe three if the storm chews the wiring."

"Then we have until tide four to decide exactly where we point it." Ines dropped the coin into Pell's palm and closed the girl's fingers over it. "Buy us fuel at Tern. Pay full price, no favors, no faces. From tonight on, we pay retail for everything, because every favor we ever earned belongs to the fleet now, and I won't owe them one more thing I can't afford."

* * *

They ran upriver in the last grey light to a mooring Ossler knew—an old blasting shed on the Escarpment's lower shelf, fleet-built and fleet-abandoned, which meant it was the one structure on this reach the fleet would never think to search, because the fleet did not look at its own garbage. Iqbal and Vantrease came aboard the *Corriander's* memory—no. Ines corrected herself, gripping the wheel. Came aboard the *Halloran*, her own boat, her own name, the one clean thing she had left.

Sef stood at the rail watching the Escarpment rise ahead of them, black stone going purple in the dusk, and said, almost to himself, "My brother crews a patrol boat out of Rennick. When this goes out—if this goes out—he'll hear it on an open channel."

"Yes," Ines said.

"He might be ordered to fire on us."

"Yes."

Sef nodded slowly, absorbing it, the way the young absorb things—whole, without anesthesia. "Okay," he said. "Okay. Then let's make sure it's worth hearing."

Above them, the first thunder of the monsoon front rolled down the gorge like something clearing its throat, six tides early or right on time, depending on how lucky you planned to be. Ines checked her watch against the tide table burned into her memory and did the only arithmetic that mattered anymore.

Three tides to rig the broadcast. Two to run the gorge. One margin for error, and the river had never once given her that margin for free.
