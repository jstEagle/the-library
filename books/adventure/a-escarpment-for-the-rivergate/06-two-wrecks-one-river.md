## 6. Two Wrecks, One River

The Corriander lay where the river had always meant to keep her, folded into the silt below the Escarpment's second shelf, forty feet down in water the color of steeped tea. Pell saw her first through the glass of the survey skiff's hull camera—a long dark shadow with ribs, like the skeleton of something that had once been fast.

"There," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. "That's her. That has to be her."

Ines didn't answer right away. She stood at the rail of the *Magpie's Daughter* with the mooring line wrapped twice around her fist, looking down at the water as if it were a door she'd spent twenty years walking past without opening. The morning was gray and windless, the kind of stillness the gorge made before it decided anything. Above them the Escarpment rose in wet black terraces, and somewhere up there Ossler and the three survivors were watching the fleet channels from the quarry camp, holding the data like a lit fuse nobody had agreed to light yet.

"Depth's good," Dov said from the engine hatch, not looking at either of them. He'd been quiet since dawn, since Ines had told them where they were going in a voice flat as ballast stone. He'd checked the dive rig twice, then a third time, then said nothing about any of it.

Pell understood why. She was sixteen and she'd been on this boat nine months, long enough to know that Dov went silent exactly when he was most afraid for someone. It was his way of praying, if Dov prayed, which he claimed he didn't.

Ines pulled the dive suit on slowly, each motion deliberate, like a woman dressing for a funeral she'd been avoiding since childhood. Pell helped her with the tank harness because Ines let her—let her hands do it, let her check the seals—and that alone told Pell how far past herself the captain already was. Ines Halloran did not let people touch her gear. Ines Halloran checked her own seals twice and trusted no one's knots but her own.

"Forty feet," Pell said. "Bottom time's generous. You could take an hour down there."

"I won't need an hour." Ines settled the mask against her face, and for a moment her eyes behind the glass were just eyes, tired and young and scared, before the captain came back over them like weather. "Twenty minutes. If I'm not up in thirty, don't come after me. Take the boat and go."

"No," Pell said.

It was the first time she'd ever said no to Ines Halloran. The word hung there between them over the gray water, and Pell braced for the flaying she deserved, and it didn't come. Ines looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind her face—something that might have been gratitude, or grief wearing gratitude's coat.

"Thirty minutes," Ines said. "Then you go."

She went over the side without a splash, the way she did everything, and the river closed over her like a mouth.

* * *

Pell counted the bubbles.

That was the thing nobody told you about waiting on a dive: there was nothing to do but watch the surface, and the surface gave you almost nothing. A boil of foam here. A string of silver beads there, rising from the dark and breaking apart in the air. Each one meant Ines was breathing. Each one meant she was alive, forty feet down, inside the wreck of the ship her father died on.

Dov stood beside her at the rail, arms crossed, saying nothing. After a while he said, "Her father captained this boat."

"I know."

"You know what I know?" Dov shook his head slowly. "I've run this river eleven years with that woman. Eleven years. She never once said the word *Corriander*. Not once. You'd ask her about the Escarpment wrecks, the ones worth money, and she'd give you a list like a ledger. But that name—" He tapped the rail. "That name wasn't in her anywhere. Like she'd cut it out."

Pell watched the bubbles. "Maybe you can cut a thing out and it still bleeds."

Dov snorted, but softly, the way he conceded things. Below them the tea-dark water held its secret, and the bubbles kept coming, steady, patient, counting themselves out like a clock. Ten minutes. Fifteen. Pell found herself whispering the numbers under her breath, an old habit from the sandbar camps where you counted the tide instead of hoping.

At nineteen minutes the bubble pattern changed.

Pell couldn't have explained how she knew. The rhythm broke—two quick bursts where there should have been one slow plume—and then a long gap, longer than any gap should be, and Pell's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the morning. She gripped the rail. Dov's hand came down on her shoulder, heavy as an anchor, holding her without words.

Then the gap ended, and the rhythm came back, wrong now, ragged, a person breathing hard through fear or cold or crying. Pell had seen Ines Halloran do everything: run blockades, stare down patrol boats, stitch a wound in her own arm without flinching. She had never once seen her cry, and she was almost certain she was hearing it now, translated into bubbles, forty feet of river away.

At twenty-six minutes Ines surfaced.

Pell and Dov hauled her over the gunwale together, and she came up the ladder on her own power mostly, and the first thing Pell saw was her hands. They were shaking. Not trembling—shaking, the fine violent tremor of a body that had taken a blow somewhere the suit couldn't cover. Ines sat down hard on the deck plates, pulled off her mask, and just breathed, great raw gulps of air, staring at nothing.

"Ines." Dov crouched by her. "Talk to me. What's down there?"

For a moment Pell thought she wouldn't answer. Then Ines opened the mesh bag she'd clipped to her harness and turned it upside down, and something fell out onto the deck between them with a sound too heavy for its size.

A logbook. Sealed in a brass canister, river-scoured green, the clasp fused shut with two decades of corrosion—but sealed, intact, whole. Stamped into the lid, worn nearly smooth but still legible, was the fleet registration mark of the *Corriander*, and beneath it, scratched by hand with something sharp, in letters small and furious:

**HALLORAN.**

"That's—" Pell started, and stopped, because her voice cracked.

"He sealed it." Ines's voice came out scraped raw, like it had been dragged over gravel. "He knew. He knew they were coming for him, and he sealed the log and weighted it inside the wheelhouse, so it would stay with the ship. So somebody would find it someday." She laughed, one broken note with no humor in it at all. "Someday. Twenty-two years. He waited twenty-two years for me to come back and open his mail."

Dov reached for the canister and Ines's hand shot out—not angry, just absolute—and covered it. "Not here."

"It'll open easier aboard—"

"Not here." She drew the canister back against her chest and held it the way Pell had once seen her hold a live steam line: something dangerous, something that could not be dropped. "The survivors' data says what the fleet did. This says *why my father*. This is the part that isn't history. This is mine."

Nobody touched it again.

* * *

They ran downriver an hour before Ines would speak, threading the slack water along the shelf with Dov at the throttle and Ines standing in the bow with the canister in both hands, facing forward, not looking back at the place where the Corriander slept. Pell worked the depth sounder and stole glances at her captain and tried to understand what she was seeing.

What she was seeing, finally, was the shape of the thing underneath everything.

Nine months Pell had ridden this boat, and in nine months she'd built a picture of Ines Halloran out of the pieces the woman allowed: the ferried cargo, the mapped exits, the contracts taken and refused on some private arithmetic nobody else could see. She'd thought the captain was careful the way river people are careful, the way anyone is who lives by salvage and silence. Careful was a profession. Careful was a craft.

But it wasn't that. Sitting there with the shivering woman in the bow and the dead man's log against her chest, Pell finally understood: Ines hadn't been running *this river*. She'd been running *from* it. Every job upriver, every route plotted wide around the Escarpment, every year of invisibility bought from the fleet—it all bent around one point on the map the way water bends around a stone. The Meridian expedition had gone quiet above the Escarpment, and Ines had gone after them, and Pell had thought it was conscience, or contract, or the ache of being needed. Some of it was. But underneath it, all of it, was a daughter swimming toward the one wreck she'd sworn never to look at, because the truth had gotten big enough that avoiding it cost more than facing it.

"She knew him," Pell said quietly to Dov, when Ines had gone below to wrap the canister in oilcloth. "Her father. She remembers him."

"Course she remembers him." Dov wiped his hands on a rag he didn't need to wipe. "She was fourteen when he went down. Old enough to remember everything and young enough to believe whatever story she was told." He fed the throttle a little more. "Fleet told her the Corriander hit the shelf in flood season. Bad luck, bad charts. And she believed it, because believing it was survivable, and she built a whole life on top of believing it." He glanced back toward the cabin. "And now she's got a brass canister that says the bad luck had a name, and the name signs its orders in triplicate."

Pell looked down at the water sliding past, brown and patient and full of the drowned. She thought about her own running—the town she'd slipped out of at fifteen, the name she'd left behind like a coat on a chair. She'd told herself she'd run from smallness, from a life already measured out. Maybe. But maybe she'd also run from a question she couldn't bear to ask, the way Ines had run from a wreck she couldn't bear to find. Maybe everybody on the river was carrying something sealed in brass, down in the dark, waiting.

Ines came back on deck at the narrows, and her hands had steadied. Her face had changed, though, and the change frightened Pell more than the shaking had. The grief was still there, but it had been forged into something with edges. Ines stood at the bow with the current pulling at the hull and said, to the river as much as to them:

"They left him to drown for trying to report them. That's what's in the canister—I haven't opened it, but I know. I've known in my body for twenty-two years, and now I know in fact." She turned, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "The survivors' data proves the crime. This proves the man. When we surface both of them, the fleet doesn't get to call it rumor. They don't get to call it anything."

"The mouth blockade," Dov said. "Tide four comes tomorrow night, and the monsoon behind it. They'll have every gun on the river between us and the sea."

"I know the arithmetic." Ines looked downriver, toward the distant haze where the gorge widened toward the coast, toward the fleet guns she couldn't see yet but knew were waiting. Then she did something Pell had never seen her do. She smiled—small, fierce, and entirely unafraid.

"So does he," she said, and patted the oilcloth bundle at her hip like a holstered weapon. "He waited twenty-two years to be heard. He can wait two tides more."

Behind them, far upstream, the Escarpment slid out of sight around the bend, and Pell felt the boat's whole heading change—not on the compass, which still pointed faithfully downriver, but somewhere deeper, in the keel, in the crew, in the woman at the bow. They weren't running anymore. Whatever came down that channel tomorrow night, they were going to meet it moving.

The first drops of the early monsoon hit the deck like thrown gravel, and far ahead, at the edge of the haze, Pell caught the flash of fleet searchlights warming up at the river mouth.
