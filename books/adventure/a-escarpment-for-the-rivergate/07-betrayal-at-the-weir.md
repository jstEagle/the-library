## 7. Betrayal at the Weir

The weir announced itself before they saw it—a long white seam of noise across the river, the old spillway shedding the last of the dry-season current in ragged curtains. Above it, the gorge pinched to a throat of black stone, and somewhere beyond that throat lay the Corriander.

Ines brought the *Salt Lark* in slow against the eddy below the structure, engines at a murmur, and studied the ruin with a salvager's eye. The weir had been built a century ago to feed mill races that no longer existed. Its gates were rotted to lace. Its lock chamber, though—the old lift on the eastern bank—still held water, still held walls, still offered the one way through for a boat too heavy to portage and too wide to shoot the broken spill.

"Lock's our road," she said. "Dov, get her nose into the chamber. Pell, fenders."

Ossler stood at the rail with his rifle slung and his hands loose at his sides, watching the high ground the way he'd been watching it since dawn. He hadn't slept. None of them had, not really, since they'd pulled away from the quarry camp with three survivors, one waterproof case of data, and a man who had spent eleven years wearing the fleet's grey uniform and had finally taken it off.

"You don't like this," Ines said quietly.

"I don't like Vess," Ossler said. "Mara Vess, the hydrologist. She's been quiet since the pickup. Not scared-quiet. Calculating-quiet." He turned. "I've seen that quiet before. It's what a man does when he's pricing his options."

"She watched her colleagues die in that camp."

"Some people survive by selling." He said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "I'm not saying she has. I'm saying we shouldn't stop anywhere longer than we have to, and we shouldn't sleep in shifts of less than two."

Ines looked upriver, at the dark throat of the gorge, at the place where her father's ship lay under thirty years of silt and silence. Two tides left before the monsoon closed everything. The arithmetic was simple and hateful: rest and lose the window, or push through exhausted and make mistakes.

"We run the lock tonight," she said. "No stops above the weir until the Corriander. Then we dive, take what we need, and we're gone before the storm hits."

* * *

The lock chamber took them in like a cupped hand. Dov worked the ancient gate winches with a pry bar and blasphemy while Pell kept the *Salt Lark* centered against the slime-slick walls, and Ines stood on the old lock-keeper's platform with a lantern, reading the mechanism her grandfather might have known.

It was almost peaceful. That was the trap of ruins—they let you forget they'd been places where people lived, and where things went wrong.

She heard the engine first. Not theirs. A deep, disciplined throb from downriver, muffled by the weir but unmistakable: more than one hull, running without lights, holding formation. Fleet boats didn't run dark unless they were hunting.

"Ines." Pell's voice, low, from the deck. "Downstream. Three—no, four wakes."

The lantern felt suddenly loud. Ines killed it and the dark came down complete, and in the dark she heard the other thing Ossler must have heard ten seconds earlier: footsteps on the eastern bank, moving fast, more than one pair, and the thin metallic sound of a boat being launched where no boat should be.

"Mara," Ossler breathed beside her. "God help us. Mara."

* * *

They came over the top of the lock wall with floodlamps and grapples, six of them in grey assault kit, and the world turned white and screaming. Ines hit the deck of the platform as the first shots chewed the stonework. Below her, Dov got the *Salt Lark*'s engine howling against the closed lower gate, and Pell was dragging the survivors flat behind the cargo crates, shouting names, keeping count the way Pell always did—*down, down, I've got you, down*—

"The case!" Ines screamed. "Pell! The case goes in the boat or nobody leaves!"

A grenade cracked the upper gate housing; the old timbers groaned. Ossler was already moving—not toward cover but along the parapet, toward the flank the strike team thought was empty, and Ines understood what he was doing about half a second before he did it. He wanted their attention. All of it. For as long as it took.

He stood up in the open with his rifle and fired four shots into the floodlamp array, and the night split into shadows and muzzle flash, and every grey helmet on that wall turned toward him.

"GO!" Ossler bellowed, and it wasn't fear in his voice, it was fury, eleven years of it. "Gate's your problem now, Captain—"

Then the return fire caught him. Ines saw him fold against the parapet, saw him go down behind the stonework still gripping his weapon, and there was no time to know if it was death or wounds because the lower gate was jammed and the fleet team was rappelling into the lock chamber itself and the *Salt Lark* was a steel animal trapped in a concrete box.

"Dov!" Ines slid down the ladder into chaos. "Blow the gate linkage—we do it manually!"

"With WHAT?"

"With the crane weight!" The old locks had counterweights, iron donkeys on chains, meant to balance the gates. "Drop the east counterweight on the linkage—NOW!"

Dov understood machines the way saints understood prayer. He cut the retaining pawl with an axe, and forty tons of iron fell eight feet with a sound like the river's own judgment, and the lower gate shuddered, tore its rusted bolts, and swung open into black water.

The *Salt Lark* shot through. Behind them, the lock chamber filled with grey shapes and gunfire, and Ossler was still back there, and Ines's hands were on the wheel and her whole body was screaming to turn around.

Above the engine roar, Pell appeared at her shoulder, bleeding from a graze along the scalp, holding the waterproof case in both arms like a child.

"Got it," Pell said. "Captain—I couldn't get to Ossler. There wasn't—"

"I know." Her voice came out flat and strange. "I know. Hold on."

* * *

The river above the weir was narrow and fast and blind-dark, and the fleet boats below would need twenty minutes to round the lock or portage the spill, and Ines spent all twenty of those minutes doing something no captain should ever do: looking backward.

Vess was gone. The hydrologist had slipped over the side during the first minute of the attack—Pell found her abandoned life vest snagged on a mooring cleat—and the shape of the betrayal assembled itself with terrible neatness. Vess had counted the patrol schedules from inside the quarry camp. Vess had known the rendezvous point. Vess had traded three colleagues' graves and Ossler's blood for whatever the fleet promised people who came in from the cold.

And the Corriander waited upriver, exactly where Vess knew they were going.

"They'll have a net across the gorge," Dov said. He'd wrapped his burned hands and said nothing else about them. "We can't dive with them coming. We can't outrun them loaded. Captain, tell me the play."

Ines looked at the case in Pell's lap. At the survivors huddled in the stern, hollow-eyed, alive. At the black water sliding past, carrying her north toward the wreck she had spent her whole adult life not thinking about.

Thirty years ago, her father had tried to report the fleet and been left to drown for it. Tonight a man who'd worn the grey for eleven years had stood up in the open so that strangers could live. The river kept taking people she refused to claim, and it was done waiting for her to learn the lesson.

"We dive anyway," she said. "Fast. One descent, one recovery, the log and nothing else. Dov, anchor us off the hull line. Pell—" she met the kid's eyes, "—you're topside. You keep the engine warm and the case dry, and if anything grey comes around that bend, you leave. You hear me? You leave, and you get the truth to the mouth of the river however you have to."

"Not leaving you twice in one night," Pell said.

"That's an order."

"It's a bad one." But Pell's jaw set in the way Ines had learned meant obedience arriving the hard way, and Ines let it stand, because there was no time to argue a child into sense.

* * *

The Corriander's buoy line was where her charts said it would be—the mark she'd logged years ago and never returned to, the lie she'd told herself that it was just another job site. Dov set the hook. The sonar read the hull beneath them, long and broken-backed, settled into the silt like a sleeping animal, and Ines stood on the dive step with the air tank cold against her spine and felt thirty years of careful distance collapse to zero.

Her father was down there. What was left of him. What the river had left of him.

She went down the line into water so black her lamp made a small room around her, and the Corriander rose out of the dark by pieces—rail, davit, the wound in her plating where the dynamite had done the fleet's second, quieter work. Ines swam her length with her heart hammering against the regulator, past the wheelhouse, to the master's cabin, and found the strongbox bolted where her father had bolted it when she was nine years old and the world still made sense.

The log was inside. Sealed tin, waxed canvas, her father's initials scratched into the lid. She took it, and she was turning for the line when the shockwave hit.

It came as pressure first—a fist of water slamming her sideways into the hull—and then the sound arrived, rolling down through the river like thunder underwater, and the Corriander shuddered around her, silt blooming up in choking clouds. Charges. They were setting charges, or dropping them, somewhere above—blowing the gorge walls to seal the channel, to bury the wreck, to bury her, all of it, again.

The line was gone. Snapped, or cut, or simply no longer where it should be, and the current that the blasts had loosed through the broken hull took her like a hand. The log tore out of her grip.

For one full second—one heartbeat of pure animal refusal—Ines Halloran hung in the black water and watched her father's voice spiral away into the dark, and understood that the river was offering her the same bargain it had always offered: let go, go home, forget.

She dove after it.

The current slammed her against a rib of the hull; she kicked off, lungs burning, chasing the pale flicker of waxed canvas through the murk. Her fingers closed on it at the edge of the light, and then there was nothing left in her tanks and nothing left in her arms except the log, and she clawed upward on instinct alone, breaking the surface into rain that had begun without her noticing—the monsoon's first scouts, fat drops hammering the river—and Dov hauling her over the gunwale by the collar while Pell screamed her name.

She came up coughing, doubled over the deck plates, the tin locked in a grip she couldn't feel anymore. Above the gorge, orange light flickered against the clouds where the charges walked down the canyon walls. Somewhere downstream, engines. Ahead, the wreck of her childhood, half-uncovered, half-doomed, and six hours maybe before the storm closed everything forever.

Ines pushed the wet hair out of her face and looked at the tin in her hands, dented, sealed, patient.

"He waited thirty years," she rasped. "He can wait one more hour. Get us off this hook. We're going to open it in front of the whole damn river."

Dov was already at the windlass. And through the rain, from the mouth of the gorge, came the searchlights.
