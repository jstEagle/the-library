## 8. Running the Monsoon

The sky went the color of wet slate an hour before dawn, and Pell knew, before anyone said it, that the storm had come early.

She felt it in her teeth first—a pressure behind her jaw like a toothache that wasn't one—and then in the river itself. The Rivergate had been running high and brown for two days, but now it moved with a different intention, a long muscular swell rolling up from the mouth where the sea was already breathing in. The reeds along the banks bent all at once, the way a crowd turns its head. Even the birds had gone quiet, and Pell had learned on this river that silence from birds meant the world was holding its breath.

Tide four of six. She'd been counting them the way you count heartbeats when you're afraid to check whether they've stopped.

Above her, on the *Corriander's* old deck plates welded onto the hull of Ines's boat—Pell still hadn't gotten used to calling any of it home—the rigging groaned and snapped taut. Dov came down the ladder with a wrench in his fist and oil to his elbows, and he didn't have to say anything either. His face said it. The barometer needle had fallen off the bottom of the dial sometime in the night, and the fleet had come early too.

"Two of them," he said. "Maybe three. Anchored across the mouth like a fence."

"Like a gate," Pell said, "that only opens one way."

Dov looked at her sideways. He did that sometimes, she'd noticed—looked at her like she'd grown another inch without him watching. Six months ago she'd been a stowaway with salt-cracked hands and nowhere to be, and now she was reading blockades out loud like weather. She wasn't sure when that had happened. Maybe it happened the same way the river cut the gorge: not all at once, but every day, a little, until the shape of things changed and nobody could point to the moment.

Ines came up from the wheelhouse last, and Pell watched her the way she always watched her—not obviously, not the way you watch someone you're afraid of, but the way you watch a fire in a hearth, checking that it's still burning.

The captain stood at the rail with the survey case under one arm—the salvaged gear from the *Meridian*, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a coffin lid—and looked south toward the mouth she couldn't see yet but clearly heard. There were engines out there. Big ones. Fleet diesels idling in formation, the sound arriving across the water in slow flat pulses, patient as a debt collector.

"They know we're coming," Ines said. It wasn't a question. Nobody answered anyway.

Since the weir, since Ossler, there had been a new quiet aboard. Grief had come aboard with them like a passenger who paid no fare and took the best bunk, and everyone stepped around it carefully. But underneath the grief there was something else, something Pell could feel gathering the way the storm gathered—dense, electric, unwilling to be polite anymore.

"We should talk," Ines said. "All of us. Before the rain takes the choice away."

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse because it was the biggest room and because it faced forward, and Pell thought privately that Ines had chosen it for that reason—that whatever they decided, she wanted them deciding it looking at the water ahead instead of behind.

Ines set the survey case on the chart table and didn't open it. Not yet.

"The mouth is blocked," she said. "Three boats minimum, probably four by the time we get there. They'll have the channel buoyed with their own marks, and anything that doesn't follow their line gets boarded or sunk, whichever's cheaper." Her voice was level, the voice she used for cargo manifests and tide tables. "Storm hits full force inside six hours. After that, nothing runs the mouth—not us, not them. We'd ride it out wherever we are and come out into whatever river they've built while we hid."

"So we hide," Dov said. "There's the oxbow past Kiln Reach. Deep enough, sheltered enough. We sit out the monsoon and—"

"And what?" Pell heard herself say it before she'd decided to speak. Everyone looked at her. She kept going, because stopping would have been worse. "We come out in five days and the story's dead. The survivors go back to being missing. The log goes back to being a rumor. They've had thirty years of practice making things into rumors." She nodded at the case. "That's the whole reason they dynamited the Corriander's site. Not the massacre—massacres they can survive. It's the proof they can't."

"I know what it is," Dov said, not unkindly. "I'm saying there's a difference between dead and drowned. I'd rather we were the first than the second."

"That's why we vote," Ines said.

The words landed strangely. Pell saw Dov blink; she felt her own spine straighten without permission. Because Ines Halloran did not put things to a vote. Ines Halloran decided, and the crew followed, and that arrangement had kept them alive through blockades and betrayals and a bullet that should have taken all of them. It had also, Pell understood suddenly, been the whole architecture of Ines's life: decide alone, need no one, owe no one, so that losing no one was possible.

But Ossler was sewn into canvas in the hold, and needing no one had not saved him.

"Run, hide, or fight," Ines said. "Those are the shapes of it. Run means the coast, the deep water, sell the data somewhere far and stay bought. Hide means Dov's oxbow and a coin flip on what the river looks like after. Fight—" She paused, and Pell watched her hand rest on the survey case, not dramatically, just resting, the way you rest your hand on something that has become part of you. "Fight means we run the blockade with the broadcast live. Every channel, fleet-wide, everything the Meridian found, everything my father logged before they left him in the water. We make ourselves so loud they can't sink us quietly."

"They can still sink us loudly," Dov said.

"Yes."

"And if the gear floods, or the mast goes, or the storm eats the signal—"

"Then we tried it in front of witnesses," Pell said. "Their own crews. Fleet boats don't run on captains, Dov. They run on pilots and deckhands and engine-men, people like us, people who've been told for thirty years that the Corriander never existed. You can't order a man to fire on his own history once he's heard it."

Dov was quiet a long time. Outside, the first fat drops hit the wheelhouse roof, spaced far apart, like footsteps approaching.

"I came aboard for wages," he finally said. "Quiet work, quiet man. That was the deal I made with myself." He wiped his hands slowly on his rag, though they were clean. "My vote's fight. Because I've been doing the arithmetic since the weir, and hiding is just dying slower, with better scenery."

Ines turned to Pell.

It occurred to Pell, absurdly, that she had never voted on anything in her life. Not where she slept, not where she ran, not whose daughter she had to pretend to be. She had drifted from place to place like flotsam, and then she'd drifted onto this boat, and somewhere between the sandbar and the weir the drifting had become choosing—but she'd never once been asked to name the choice out loud.

"Fight," she said. Her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack. "Not because we win. Because it's ours. All of it—the risk, the truth, the boat. I want it to be ours."

Something moved through Ines's face then, quick as a fish under the surface—something that might have been grief and might have been relief and was probably both wearing each other's clothes. She nodded once.

"Then it's real," she said. "The vote's real. Whatever happens at the mouth—we chose it. All of us." She flipped open the survey case, and the instruments inside caught the grey light, brass and glass and the coiled antenna Dov had spent two sleepless days waterproofing. "Now let's go be loud."

* * *

They worked through the rising rain, and Pell had never known work like it—not salvage work, which was solitary and grim, but this, this braided thing, four hands becoming eight becoming one motion. Dov swayed at the mast foot feeding the antenna stays while Pell sealed the transmitter housing against the water that was coming for it sideways. Ines ran cable through the wheelhouse and called readings over the wind, and when Pell's grip slipped on a wet shackle and the whole stay assembly swung for her skull, it was Ines's arm that caught it, Ines's shoulder that took the blow instead, and neither of them said anything about it afterward because there was no time and because some things don't need saying.

By the time the storm arrived in earnest, the *Meridian's* surviving survey array rode their masthead like a lantern made of defiance, and the transmitter hummed below decks with the Corriander's log loaded, the Meridian's data stacked, the names of the massacre read into memory one by one—Pell had insisted on that, reading them aloud while Dov typed, so that the machine carried voices and not just text.

The river mouth appeared through the rain as a wall of white and a constellation of running lights. Three fleet hulls anchored gunwale to gunwale across the channel, black shapes hunched against the fury, searchlights sweeping the water in long blind strokes.

Ines took the wheel herself. Rain hammered the wheelhouse glass until the world beyond was smeared ink.

"Pell," she said. "Key it."

Pell's hand closed around the transmit switch, cold and slick. Through the glass she could see the nearest patrol boat growing larger, its searchlight swinging toward them, and her heart was going like a drum in a storm, and none of it—not the fear, not the cold, not the wall of steel ahead—felt like drifting anymore.

"On your word, Captain," she said.

Ines Halloran put the bow at the gap between the first two hulls and gave the word.
