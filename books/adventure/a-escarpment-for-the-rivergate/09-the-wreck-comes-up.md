## 9. The Wreck Comes Up

The rain came down the gorge like a second river, falling sideways, falling upward, filling the air so completely that Ines could no longer tell where the storm ended and the water began. The *Corriander's* hull section hung beneath the flagship's tow rig, forty tons of black iron swinging on cables thick as her arm, and through the sheeting rain she could see it turning—slow, ponderous, obscene—the way a body turns on a rope.

They had raised it. Of course they had raised it. The fleet had spent twenty years dynamiting that wreck into silence, and now, with the monsoon hours from closing the mouth and their own pilots in mutiny over the broadcast, they were hauling the evidence up to sink it properly, deep, past any diver's reach, past any sonar's argument.

Pell stood beside her at the wheel, both hands white on the rail. "You can't," Pell said. It wasn't an objection. It was a prayer said backward.

"I can," Ines said.

The *Rivergate* ran at full throttle into the storm, her old engine screaming a note Dov had spent six tides coaxing out of her, a note she had never made before and would never make again. Behind them, rigged to the mast and every spare antenna Dov could find, the Meridian's survey gear burned its signal into the rain: her father's voice, cracked and wet with river static, reading the fleet's orders aloud—the orders he had been left to drown for carrying. The massacre's names, all of them, read by Ossler's recording before Ossler took his bullet at the weir, read now on loop, on every channel, in every language the river spoke.

The fleet's pilots heard it. She could see it in the way the patrol boats broke formation, the way their searchlights swung not toward her but toward each other, uncertain, ashamed.

* * *

The flagship was called the *Vigilant*, which would have been funny if Ines had breath to laugh. She came up the reach through the monsoon's first true fury, riding a swell that lifted her bow and dropped it like a hammer, and the *Vigilant* loomed out of the rain with her tow rig lit yellow against the dark, floodlights blazing down onto the hanging wreck as though lighting a stage.

Ines cut the wheel hard to starboard and then harder back, using the swell, timing it the way her father had taught her to time nothing—he had died before teaching her anything—but the way the river itself had taught her, two decades of running this water, of knowing its moods better than the faces of anyone who claimed to love her.

"Again," Pell said, meaning *please don't*.

"Now," Ines said, meaning *yes*.

The swell rose. She rode it. At its crest, when the *Vigilant*'s tow cables went slack for one half-second—one half-second of mercy in all that iron arithmetic—she put the *Rivergate*'s reinforced bow directly into the tow rig's port anchor point.

The sound was not a crash. It was a sentence ending. Metal tore with a shriek that the storm swallowed whole, and then the cables parted, and the *Corriander*—her father's ship, the wreck the fleet had erased from every ledger, every chart, every memory it could buy—dropped free of the rig and hit the water whole.

Forty tons of truth, floating.

It should have sunk. It was holed, gutted, twenty years dead. But the fleet's own salvage foam—pumped into it for the lift—held it up, held it buoyant, and the current took it, and the rain beat on its deck plates, and it drifted down the reach between the flagship and the *Rivergate* like a coffin refusing burial.

On the *Vigilant*'s deck, Ines saw figures running. She saw the floodlights swing wildly, hunting the drifting hull, hunting the broadcast, hunting something to do that wasn't listen. Because everyone on that deck was listening. Everyone on every boat in the blockade was listening, because there was nowhere in the rain you could go where her father's voice wasn't already inside your head.

*"—repeat, this is Captain Edmun Halloran of the salvage vessel Corriander. I am filing a formal report regarding the events at Marrow Bend, the eleventh of the flood season. There were one hundred and fourteen souls aboard the barges. They were not lost to the shoals. The order came from—"*

Static. Then Dov's patched relay caught it again, and the voice rolled on, patient, drowned, unstoppable.

* * *

The *Vigilant* turned to run.

Ines saw it happen—the great ship putting her helm over, wallowing in the swell, trying to put distance between herself and the drifting wreck, as if distance had ever saved anyone from a name. But the *Vigilant* was old, older than her reputation, and the monsoon found the wound Ines's ram had opened near the waterline and opened it wider. The river did the rest. The river always did the rest.

She went down by the bow, slowly, grandly, her floodlights still burning underwater like lanterns sunk in green glass, and her crew went into the water and the fleet's own patrol boats—the same boats that had swept the reach asking questions that were really warnings—turned and picked them up without being ordered to. Ines watched a patrol boat's captain look straight at the flagship's surviving officers, straight into their eyes, and turn away to pull drowning men from the river instead.

No order to fire was ever given. Or rather, it was given, three times, on the command channel, and three times the pilots answered with silence, and the third silence was louder than the broadcast, louder than the storm, louder than twenty years of bought quiet. Truth too loud to drown. That was what Pell had called it, back at the quarry camp, and Pell had been right, the way runaways are sometimes right about things captains take years to learn.

The *Corriander* drifted. Ines brought the *Rivergate* alongside her, gentle now, gentler than she had ever handled any cargo, and put lines on the old hull, and towed her father's ship out of the battlefield of the flooded reach while the rain came down and down and the broadcast named the dead, all of them, one hundred and fourteen names plus one, the last name spoken in a young man's voice that had been waiting under the water for twenty years to be heard.

Dov came up from the engine room, black with grease and rain, and stood at the rail beside her, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the river hadn't already said.

Pell stood at the bow, holding the line to the *Corriander*, one hand on the cable as though holding the hand of someone crossing a street.

"Ines," Pell said. "Look."

Ahead, through the rain, the river mouth was opening. The blockade boats were parting—not scattering, parting, deliberately, in two clean lines, leaving a lane of black water wide enough for a salvager and the wreck she towed. And standing along the rails of those boats, in the monsoon, in the dark, were pilots and deckhands and engineers, dozens of them, silent, watching the *Corriander* pass.

Not saluting. Watching. Bearing witness, the way you bear witness at a funeral, or a trial.

Ines stood at her wheel and steered her father's ship down the lane between them, and for the first time since she was nine years old, since the night the fleet erased him and paid her mother's silence and taught her that needing people was just another way of losing cargo, she did not look for the exits.

There were no exits. There was only the river, and the rain, and the names, and ahead—dim through the storm, but real, but hers—a harbor with lights burning that nobody had ordered lit.

The tide had two days left before the monsoon closed everything. Enough time, she thought, to bring the *Corriander* home.
