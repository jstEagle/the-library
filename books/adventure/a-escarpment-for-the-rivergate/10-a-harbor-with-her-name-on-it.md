## 10. A Harbor With Her Name On It

The rain quit at dawn on the seventh day, and the river came down off its fury like a beast settling after a long fight. Ines stood at the wheel of the *Salt Lark* and watched the water go from brown violence to a deep, patient green, the way it always did when the monsoon spent itself. Below her feet, lashed to the deck where the survey gear had been bolted down, sat the section of the *Corriander*'s hull—crusted, scarred, forty years drowned—and she had not yet been able to stop touching it.

She'd been doing it all night without noticing: one hand on the rail, then trailing along the hull's edge as she passed, fingertips finding the rivet lines her father's hands might have set or sealed or simply leaned against in some other lifetime. Dov had noticed. Dov noticed everything about engines and nothing about people, which was why he said nothing, only left a tarp over the worst of the damage and a thermos of coffee within reach of wherever she happened to be standing.

The river mouth opened ahead of them, wide as a held breath let out.

And it was full of boats.

Not fleet gunboats—not anymore. Fishing skiffs, salvage barges, the flat-bottomed grain haulers that ran the lower reaches, all of them riding at anchor or drifting in slow circuits, and every one of them flying something white from their rigging. Rags, shirts, sailcloth torn into strips. The signal had gone out on every channel during the blockade run—the *Corriander*'s log, the fleet's own orders read aloud in a dead man's steady voice, the names of the massacre read by Dr. Amara Voss in a voice that shook and did not stop—and the river had heard. The river had answered the only way a river could: by showing up.

"They've been at anchor two days," Pell said, coming up beside her with the binoculars. "Pilots walked off the flagship's sister ships yesterday morning. Walked their captains off at gunpoint, some of them. Ossler's people." She said it carefully. Ossler was three days dead and they were all still learning how to carry that.

Ines took the glasses. At the mouth's northern point, where the customs house had stood shuttered for a generation, she saw figures moving on the roof—a jury-rigged antenna, a flag of white running up a halyard. And below, tied at the customs dock, a line of fleet officers sitting on their own crates under guard of men and women who wore no uniform at all, just river clothes and the particular set of shoulders that belonged to people who had decided something and would not be argued out of it.

"The pilots," Ines said. "They're holding the inquiry themselves."

"Somebody has to. The magistrates are all fleet-paid." Pell lowered her voice. "There's a list, Captain. Voss has it—the *Corriander*'s full crew manifest, reconstructed. Forty-one names. They're reading them out at noon."

Ines put the glasses down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake, had half wanted them to, the way you want a wound to bleed properly instead of sealing over wrong. Forty-one names. Her father among them, and thirty-nine others who had been nothing but arithmetic in a fleet ledger for four decades—*lost with all hands, cause: river hazard*—and now, today, they would be said aloud over the water they died in, by people who had come to listen.

"Take us in," she said. "Slow. We're carrying cargo."

Dov looked up from the engine housing. "What cargo?"

Ines rested her hand flat on the *Corriander*'s hull, and this time she let herself feel it—the cold coming up through the crusted steel, the river's long patience finally ending.

"Passengers," she said.

* * *

They brought the hull section ashore at the old ferry landing below the customs house, using the *Lark*'s boom and every rope Dov owned and a dozen more that appeared from the anchored skiffs as if the river itself had supplied them. It took most of the morning. Halfway through, Ines realized she was no longer directing anything—that strangers were hauling on lines she hadn't rigged, calling adjustments in the loose river cant of the lower reaches, and that the work was going fine without her. That was the strange part. All her life, work going fine without her had meant she was expendable. This morning it meant something else, and she stood on the landing with wet rope burns on her palms and let the something else have her.

Voss found her there. The scientist looked hollowed out, monsoon-thin, but her eyes had a settled quality Ines recognized from people who had finished something true.

"They want to record them properly," Voss said. "Names, ages, next of kin where anyone knows them. A ledger entry that can't be bought back." She hesitated. "There's a page for you, if you want it. Next of kin to Halloran, master of the *Corriander*. You'd sign as family."

The river noise filled the pause—water on pilings, gulls arguing over the mudflats, someone laughing on a barge. Ines looked out at the crowd gathering for the noon reading. Fishermen. Salvage crews. Old women who might have been girls when the *Corriander* went down, holding photographs soft as cloth from handling.

"I spent my whole life making sure nobody knew I was his daughter," Ines said. "It kept me safe. It kept me—" She stopped. *Alone*, the word was, and she wasn't going to dress it up. "I thought the river owed me. Turns out I owed it this."

Voss waited. She was good at waiting; scientists were.

"Yes," Ines said. "I'll sign."

At noon the names went out over the water, read from the customs house steps by Voss and by an old pilot named Sef Karro who had served on the *Corriander*'s sister ship and remembered three of the crew by face. When Halloran, master, was read, the river went quiet—all of it, every skiff, every barge, the gulls even—and Ines stood at the rail of her own boat with Pell's shoulder pressed to hers and did not cry until the last name was done, and then only a little, and only because Pell was crying too and somebody had to keep the tally honest between them.

* * *

The offer came three days later, delivered to the *Lark* at her mooring by Sef Karro himself, who climbed aboard with the unhurried dignity of a man who had arrested admirals last week and considered it no great thing.

"The pilots' council met," he said, settling onto her deck bench like he owned it, which in a way he now did, being the river's voice made flesh. "The Escarpment run needs a charter holder. Someone with the right to run the gorge, maintain the beacons, license the pilots above the narrows. Fleet charter's void, obviously." He produced a folded document, heavy paper, real ink. "We talked about candidates. There weren't many discussions, honestly. Voss spoke for you. So did half the lower reach. So did"—he nodded at the hull section, cleaned now, mounted on timbers at the landing where the whole river could see it—"he did, I expect, in whatever way the dead do that."

Ines took the paper and didn't open it. "You know what I am. I've spent twenty years invisible. No berth, no name on any register, no debts. That was the whole design."

"And how'd that work out for you?" Karro asked mildly.

She almost laughed. Behind him, Pell was coiling lines badly on purpose, listening with her whole body, and Dov had emerged from the engine room with the expression of a man pretending not to hope.

"It got me here," Ines said. "That's what it got me."

"So?"

So. She looked at the document. She thought about the girl she'd been, learning exits before faces, learning that needing people was how they got taken from you. She thought about Ossler, who had chosen the river over the fleet with a bullet through him, and about her father, who had chosen truth over silence and paid the whole price alone because no one had stood with him. She was not alone. That was the difference. That was everything.

She signed it there on the deck, in front of Karro and Pell and Dov and the gulls, and when she handed the pen back her hand was shaking after all, and she let it.

"Well," Dov said, when Karro had gone. "Charter holder. That means paperwork."

"That means we're staying," Pell translated.

"That means," Ines said, "that somebody has to run supplies up to the quarry camp before the dry season, and survey the new channel the flood cut through the narrows, and re-hang every beacon the fleet let rot. And that means—" she looked at her crew, her actual crew, hers, "—we're going to need a bigger boat."

### Coda — what Pell wrote, later, in the margin of the *Lark*'s logbook

First entry under the new charter. Captain says logs should be plain, so:

We are moored at Rivergate proper, at a berth with our name on the post—HALLORAN CHARTER, painted by Dov, crooked, repainted by me, less crooked. The *Corriander*'s names are in the ledger at the customs house and flowers come every week from somebody. Voss is staying to finish the survey. Karro teaches me pilot-work on Tuesdays. Ossler's rifle hangs in the wheelhouse and nobody touches it and everybody sees it.

Captain laughed today. Twice. I counted.

She used to map every exit before we ever tied up. Yesterday she drew the harbor chart herself and marked our berth with a star, like a destination, like somewhere you'd choose. I asked her why the star. She said, *Because we're not leaving.*

The river doesn't owe us anything anymore. We don't owe it either. We just live here, and the work is good, and tomorrow we run the Escarpment with the tide, homebound, lights burning.

Homebound. First time I ever wrote that word and meant both directions.
