## 1. Freight and Rumor

The de Havilland Beaver came off the water at Fort Meridian like an old dog shaking off rain — a shudder, a grumble, and then, all at once, grace. Hazel Okonkwo let her climb, banking south over the sawmill smoke, and felt the familiar loosening in her chest that only happened at about three hundred feet, when the town below became a thing she was leaving rather than a thing she belonged to.

She'd owned the plane eleven years. She'd named her *Josephine*, after her grandmother, who had never once in her life been on time for anything but had always, somehow, arrived exactly when it mattered. Josephine the elder would have appreciated Josephine the aircraft: dented, loud, held together by stubbornness and safety wire, and utterly reliable in the ways that counted.

"Morning, old girl," Hazel said, because talking to your plane was either madness or professionalism depending on which pilot you asked, and she'd decided long ago it was professionalism.

Her manifest that morning read like a prayer list. Insulin for Marta Two-Rivers at Kettle Narrows, packed in the good cooler with the cracked lid. A crate of school supplies for the mission at Loon Lake — forty pounds of pencils, which weighed more than you'd think, children being what they were. Antibiotics for old Sam Deere, who would take them or not take them according to some private theology of his own. And, wedged behind the cargo net, the freight nobody put on manifests: two letters from the bank manager's wife to her sister downriver, a bag of licorice for the Kettle Narrows kids, and gossip, which Hazel carried free of charge and delivered with interest.

That was the arrangement, really. The medicine kept people alive; the gossip made them glad to be. Hazel had learned early that a bush pilot who flew only cargo flew half her route. The other half happened over coffee she didn't have time for, drunk anyway.

The Kestrel River unspooled beneath her, high and brown with spring melt, braiding itself through country so green it looked wet even from a thousand feet. She knew this river the way other people knew their own handwriting. There was the bend where the current ate banks every June. There was the gravel bar where a moose had once stood hip-deep and watched her land as though she were a mildly interesting weather event. There was—

Hazel frowned and throttled back.

There was a helicopter where there shouldn't have been one.

It sat on a cleared pad near the mouth of the Kestrel's north fork, orange and white, beside a cluster of canvas tents and stacked fuel drums. Survey equipment. A man in a hard hat stood in the rotor wash, hands on hips, watching her pass with the proprietary air of someone who considered the entire valley a line item.

Vane Consolidated. They'd been creeping up the river all spring, staking claims, cutting seismic lines through forest that had gone unmolested since the last ice age got bored with it. Hazel had flown their crews before, out of Fort Meridian, back when they were small-time and polite. That was four years ago. Now they had their own helicopters, their own barges, their own security men with earpieces and flat eyes, and a reputation that traveled faster than she did.

*Vane Consolidated: Development You Can Trust.* That was the slogan on the side of their barges. Hazel had once remarked to Sam Deere that any company needing to put *trust* in its slogan was advertising precisely what it lacked, and Sam had laughed so hard he'd had to sit down on his own woodpile.

She waggled her wings at the survey camp — not friendly, exactly. More like a cat flicking its tail. Then she turned upriver toward Kettle Narrows and put them out of her mind, which was, she would reflect later, the last easy decision she'd make for quite some time.

* * *

Kettle Narrows smelled of woodsmoke and fish and, today, of Marta Two-Rivers' fresh bread, which was against all decency given that Hazel was working. She tied up at the dock, handed down the insulin cooler, and was captured within ninety seconds.

"You're too thin," Marta announced, pressing bread into her hands. "Flying makes a woman thin. Look at you. Like a heron."

"I'm robust," Hazel said, mouth already full. Resistance was pointless; it had been pointless for nine years.

"You're bones and opinions." Marta was seventy-three and had delivered half the population of the Narrows, including several who now tried to tell her things. "Sit. Drink tea. Tell me what the river's saying."

The river, that morning, was saying plenty. Hazel settled onto Marta's dock with her tea and gave her the news the way she gave it everywhere, in order of increasing importance: the price of flour in Fort Meridian (scandalous), the bank manager's wife's letters (delivered, contents unknown, but the wife had cried while writing them, which meant either romance or debt), the school pencils (arrived), and then, lower, the way you lowered your voice for the real cargo:

"There's a Vane crew camped at the north fork. Helicopter and everything."

Marta's teacup paused halfway to her mouth. "North fork," she repeated. "That's above the falls."

"That's above the falls."

"Nobody camps above the falls." Above the falls was rough country — escarpment country, cliff and muskeg and whitewater, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful. Trappers went there. One prospector, if the talk was true. Nobody else had reason.

"About that," Hazel said. "I heard something in Meridian, before I took off. Doc Whitfield was telling the postmaster — or pretending not to tell him, which is how he tells everyone everything — that there's a man holed up somewhere up the Kestrel. An old prospector. Been living wild up there since spring."

"Eben Crowe," Marta said immediately.

Hazel blinked. "You know him?"

"Everyone knows *of* him. He trapped the upper river thirty years ago. Good man, strange habits. Kept to himself even before keeping to himself was fashionable." Marta sipped her tea, and her eyes, sharp as fish hooks, stayed on Hazel. "He had a daughter. Went south with her mother after the wife died. He wrote to her every year, I heard. Never got answers, far as anyone knows."

"Why's he hiding out now? From Vane?"

Marta shrugged, which from Marta was a whole paragraph. "Men who know things about rivers sometimes know other things too. Vane's been buying up every claim on the Kestrel for two years. Every claim except whatever patch of rock Eben Crowe is sitting on. And Vane doesn't like loose threads." She refilled Hazel's cup without asking. "Doc says he's sick, too. Whatever he's got, it's eating him."

Hazel looked up the river, where the water went silver around the first bend and disappeared into country she'd flown over a hundred times and never once touched. It occurred to her, not for the first time, that she knew the map of this place better than its heart. She could find every lake by its shape from the air. She couldn't tell you who wept at whose funeral on the ground, except when they told her over tea, and then she carried it, weightless and secondhand, to the next dock.

"You're frowning," Marta observed.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous at altitude."

"It's all I've got." Hazel drained the tea and stood. "I should run. Sam Deere's antibiotics won't deliver themselves, and if I'm late he'll declare modern medicine a conspiracy again and I'll have to listen to the whole lecture."

"Tell him," Marta said, "that conspiracies require competence, and he can't even keep his stove lit."

"I will relay that verbatim and enjoy it enormously."

* * *

Sam Deere's cabin sat on a bluff above Loon Lake, leaning slightly eastward as if listening for something. Hazel landed on the water, rowed ashore in the Beaver's dinghy — Josephine's floats leaked a slow, dignified trickle that she refused to fix on principle, or possibly budget — and found Sam on his porch, whittling a piece of wood into something that was either a spoon or a duck and had been both at various times.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm punctual. Your clock is wrong."

"My clock keeps honest time. It just doesn't flatter anyone." He took the antibiotics, examined the bottle, and grunted, which from Sam was gratitude. "You hear about Crowe?"

"So I'm told everyone has."

"He came through here in April. Bought rice, cartridges, and a new tarp. Paid in gold dust." Sam's whittling knife paused. "Wouldn't meet my eye. Man who wouldn't meet your eye is either ashamed or scared. Eben Crowe never struck me as a man with much shame in him."

"What do you figure he's scared of?"

Sam looked past her, at the lake, at the treeline beyond it where the escarpment rose faint and blue in the distance like a wall at the edge of the world. "Same thing everybody's scared of," he said. "Being remembered wrong."

Hazel waited for him to elaborate. Sam Deere had been elaborating on things for sixty years and had never once started before he finished being cryptic.

"He left something with me," Sam said finally, surprising her. "A package. Said if a woman ever came asking — a woman with a plane and no patience — I was to give it to her."

"A woman with a plane and no patience," Hazel repeated. "That's a narrow description."

"Narrower than you'd think, up here." Sam went inside and came back with a parcel wrapped in oilcloth and tied with fishing line. It was heavy for its size. "Said to tell you—" Sam squinted, retrieving exact words, which cost him visibly. "*If she wants the truth about the source, she'll have to want it more than she wants to go home.*"

Hazel turned the parcel over in her hands. Oilcloth, twine, and under her thumbs, through the wrapping, the corner of something stiff and rectangular. Paper. A lot of paper.

"Did he say why me?" she asked. "I've never met the man."

Sam resumed whittling. "He said the sky-woman sees the whole river and belongs to none of it. Said that makes her the only one Vane can't buy." He glanced up, one eye closed. "His words. Not mine. I'd have said it kinder."

Hazel stood there on the bluff with the parcel getting heavier by the second, which was impossible, since weight was fixed, and yet. Below her, Loon Lake lay flat and mirror-still, holding the whole sky in it, and she thought: *that's what I am. A surface that reflects everything and holds nothing.*

She'd built her life that way on purpose. Four communities, six hundred miles of river, a plane full of other people's insulin and other people's letters and other people's griefs, all of it passing through her hands and none of it staying. It was freedom. She'd told herself it was freedom for eleven years, and mostly she believed it, the way you believe a floor is solid right up until it isn't.

"Hazel," Sam said. "You going to open it?"

"Not here." She tucked the parcel under her arm. "Where's his daughter?"

"Fort Meridian, last anybody knew. Works at the hotel, I heard. Ruth, her name is." Sam's knife whispered along the wood. "And Hazel — Vane's men were at the Narrows yesterday. Asking after Crowe. Polite fellows. Hard hats, soft voices." He looked at her. "They'll be at Meridian by tomorrow, if they're not already."

The wind came off the lake and moved the surface, and the reflected sky broke apart and reassembled, and Hazel found she was already doing the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing: fuel on board, daylight remaining, forty minutes back to Fort Meridian, a daughter named Ruth who worked at a hotel, and a dying stranger upstream who had looked at the whole spread of her life from a mountaintop somewhere and seen the one thing she'd never said out loud.

"Well," she said, to Sam, to the lake, to the parcel, to nobody. "I suppose I'd better find out what I've agreed to."

She hadn't agreed to anything. That was the trouble with promises, she'd learn later. They boarded you long before you signed anything.

She rowed back to Josephine, loaded up, and took off into the long gold light of evening, the parcel riding behind her seat like a passenger with opinions. Behind her the Kestrel ran on toward its hidden source, carrying whatever it remembered, patient as water.

Forty minutes ahead, in Fort Meridian, a woman she'd never met was wiping down tables in a hotel dining room, unaware that her father's ghost had just hired a bush pilot.

Hazel flew toward her fast, and told herself it was just the schedule.
