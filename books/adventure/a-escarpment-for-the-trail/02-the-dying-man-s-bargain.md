## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The storm caught Hazel forty minutes out of Kettle Narrows, the way storms always did — casually, like an old friend dropping by uninvited to eat all your food.

She'd been running the last leg of her circuit with a hold full of insulin, a crate of rifle cartridges for the Marchand brothers who were constitutionally incapable of planning ahead, and a letter from the postmistress at Fort Meridian that smelled faintly of lilac perfume and gossip. The de Havilland Beaver — *Maggie*, because you didn't name a plane after a woman you were sweet on, you named it after a woman who'd put up with your nonsense for thirty years — bucked and wallowed through the gray like a canoe in a washing machine.

"Easy, old girl," Hazel said, hunching over the yoke. "We've been rained on before."

Below her, the Kestrel River unspooled through the valley, brown and swollen, and along its banks she could see the new scars: survey lines cut straight as ruled paper through the black spruce, orange flagging tape fluttering on every third tree, and the tin roofs of Vane Consolidated's fly camps catching what little light the day had left. Three years ago there'd been nothing up that river but traplines and silence. Now it looked like someone had taken a knife to the country and was checking whether it bled gold.

Hazel didn't much care for it. But she'd learned long ago that disliking things was a luxury for people who owned more than one pair of dry socks.

The storm pushed her off her line to Kettle Narrows, and by the time she punched through the far side of it, dripping and swearing, she was twenty miles off course over country she knew only as a green wrinkle on a chart. And there — tucked into a bend of a creek that fed the Kestrel — was a cabin. Log walls, sod roof, woodsmoke standing up thin and crooked against the clearing sky.

She hadn't planned on landing. But the light was going, Kettle Narrows was an hour further, and Hazel Winterbourne had survived this long by respecting two things above all others: weather and fuel gauges. She put Maggie down on the creek's long gravel bar with the particular tenderness of a pilot who knew every rock on it personally, which she did not, and taxied to shore with her heart doing a small drumroll until the floats touched gravel instead of granite.

* * *

The man in the cabin was dying, and he was furious about it.

That was the first thing Hazel understood, before she even saw his face. She heard him through the door — not groaning, but arguing, low and vicious, with somebody who wasn't there. When she shouldered the door open he was propped against the bunk frame with a rifle across his lap, sixty-some years old, grey-bearded, yellowed at the eyes like old paper, and glaring at her as though she were the specific personification of his bad luck.

"Door was unlocked," Hazel said, hands visible, voice easy. "I'm Hazel Winterbourne. I fly the river circuit. Storm shoved me off my line."

"Storm," the man said, as if tasting something rotten. "Vane send you?"

"Do I look like I work for Vane Consolidated?"

He considered her — the oil-stained coveralls, the hair escaping its braid, the general air of a woman who fixed her own engine and resented having to say so. "No," he admitted. "You look like you work for yourself." Then his face went white as birchbark and he folded forward, and the rifle clattered to the floor, and Hazel stopped being a visitor and started being whatever he needed.

It wasn't hard to diagnose. He was in agony, curled around his middle like a comma; his belly was rigid as a drumhead; his skin had gone the color of tallow. Hazel had grown up in mining towns where men drank and ate beans for forty years and then paid the bill all at once. A burst ulcer, or close enough to it. She had no surgeon, no hospital, and the nearest one was a hundred and eighty miles of weather away. She had aspirin, a flask of rye she kept for medicinal bribery, and a satellite phone with one bar of signal if she stood on the woodpile and held her tongue right.

She did all three. The doctor in Fort Meridian listened, asked questions, and delivered the verdict Hazel already carried in her stomach like a stone: even if she flew out tonight, even if the weather held, the man would almost certainly die before they got him to a table. Days, maybe. Hours, if he moved.

"How long have I got?" the man asked when she came back inside. He'd heard enough of the call to do his own arithmetic.

"They wouldn't give me a number."

"They never do. Cowards." He laughed, and the laugh turned into something else, and Hazel held the basin for him while he was sick, and when it was over he lay back and looked at the ceiling beams for a long time.

"Eben Crowe," he said finally. "Prospector. Formerly of everywhere."

"Hazel Winterbourne. Formerly of nowhere in particular."

That earned her half a smile. "You'll do," he said.

* * *

Night came down over the creek, and Eben Crowe talked, because dying men talk — it's the one appointment none of them cancel.

He made her tea in a pot blacker than sin, though he couldn't keep any of it down himself, and he talked about rivers. About the Kestrel especially, and the country above the escarpment where the maps went vague and the survey crews hadn't yet dared to go. He talked about claims staked and lost, about a winter in '71 he kept circling back to and veering away from, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. He talked about a daughter named Ruth, in Fort Meridian, who would be thirty-one now, who liked her eggs burnt and her coffee strong enough to trot a horse across, and who had not answered one of his letters in nineteen years.

"You write every year?" Hazel asked.

"Every year. Same week. Her birthday." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "I figure a man owes a debt whether or not anybody's collecting."

"And she doesn't write back."

"She's got her reasons. Good ones, probably. I chose rivers over her mother, and then over her, and a girl only forgives so many rivers." He looked at Hazel with those yellowed eyes. "You got people?"

"I've got a plane."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Hazel said, after a moment. "It isn't."

Somewhere past midnight, when the pain had ground him down to something quiet and transparent, Eben Crowe dragged himself upright, pulled a battered oilskin packet from under a floorboard, and laid it on the table between them like a man laying down cards he'd been holding for fifty years.

Inside was a map. Not a survey map — a hand-drawn thing, grease pencil on waxed paper, the kind of chart a prospector makes for himself alone: creek bends, a portage marked with three X's, the escarpment drawn as a jagged tooth, and at the top, circled twice, the headwater of the Kestrel River. Around the circle, in letters pressed so hard the wax had torn, four words:

*IT REMEMBERS. BRING WITNESSES.*

"What is it?" Hazel asked. "Gold?"

Eben laughed — a dry, terrible sound. "Everybody asks that. Gold's the bait God uses on fools." His hand closed over hers on the table, and his grip was still strong, which surprised them both. "There's a place up there the river comes out of the rock. Vane's permits cover it. Thirty days left on them, and after that it's his forever, fenced and drilled and paved, and whatever's there stays buried behind company gates till the end of the world. You understand? Thirty days. That's the whole window God's giving us."

"Us," Hazel said carefully.

"The source remembers what Vane paid to forget." He said it slowly, like a man setting each word down on ice. "Nineteen seventy-one. There were men up there — strikers, river men, my friends — and Silas Vane's father decided they were cheaper dead than paid. I ran. That's the whole of my part in it. I ran, and I lived, and I spent fifty years telling myself I'd go back." His breath hitched. "Now I'm going back the way everybody goes eventually. So it has to be you."

"Me." Hazel sat back. "Eben. I'm a freight pilot. I haul insulin and ammunition and other people's mail. I'm not a witness. I'm not a sheriff. I'm nobody's—"

"You're the one sitting here," he said simply. "That's how it always works. It's never the bold ones. It's whoever's in the room."

He pushed the map across the table. "Take it to Ruth. Fort Meridian. Tell her it's from her father, and tell her—" he stopped, and for the first time all night his composure cracked clean through, and Hazel looked away to give him the dignity of the moment. "Tell her I kept writing. Every year. Even when she didn't answer. Tell her that."

"Why me?" she asked again, quieter. "You don't know me. I could burn that map tomorrow and fly to the coast."

"You could," Eben agreed. "But you landed when you didn't have to. You held the basin. You stood on the woodpile for an hour talking to a doctor who told you nothing, just so you'd have something to bring back to me." He smiled, and it transformed his ruined face into something young. "A woman who does that for a stranger will do it for a promise. I'm betting my ghost on it."

Hazel looked at the map. At the circled headwater. At the words *BRING WITNESSES*. Everything in her — the part that had spent forty-one years keeping her bags packed and her heart in escrow, the part that belonged to the sky precisely because the sky never asked her to stay for anything — wanted to say no. Wanted to finish the circuit, file her reports, and let the dead bury their own arithmetic.

Instead she heard herself say: "If I take this to Ruth Crowe, and she tells me to burn it, I burn it. It's hers. Not mine, not yours anymore. Hers."

Eben nodded, satisfied, as if that had been the correct answer to some test she hadn't known she was taking. "Fair terms."

"And if she wants to go up that river?"

His eyes gleamed in the lamplight. "Then you'd best check your fuel gauge, Miss Winterbourne."

* * *

He died three days later, on a Thursday, in the trapper's cabin while Hazel sat with him through the last of it, because she found she couldn't make herself leave. She buried him on the rise above the creek, wrapped in canvas, with a cairn of river stones and a marker she cut with her own knife: EBEN CROWE, PROSPECTOR. HE STOPPED RUNNING.

Then she flew south through clear morning air, the map riding in the breast pocket of her coveralls, heavier than any cargo she'd ever carried — and she caught herself, somewhere over the Kestrel's first rapids, rehearsing what she'd say to a stranger named Ruth, and understanding with a lurch that she was nervous.

Hazel Winterbourne, who'd landed on glaciers, who'd argued with customs officers in three languages, who feared nothing so much as staying put — nervous about knocking on a door.

Fort Meridian rose out of the treeline by mid-afternoon, woodsmoke and church steeple and the post office with its lilac-scented postmistress. Hazel set Maggie down on the water, taxied to the dock, and asked the first face she saw where she might find Ruth Crowe.

The man spat thoughtfully over the rail. "Crowe? Works the boatyard. Hard woman to know." He squinted at Hazel. "What'd Eben Crowe ever do to you?"

Hazel looked downriver, toward the country where the maps went vague.

"It's what he left me," she said.
