## 3. Ruth

Fort Meridian smelled of diesel, spruce smoke, and the particular ambition of a town that had once believed it would be important and had never quite gotten over the disappointment.

Hazel tied down the Beaver at the government dock, where the boards had been replaced so many times in patches that walking across them felt like reading braille. She'd slept four hours in a trapper's bunk with Eben Crowe's grease-pencil map folded into her breast pocket, right over her heart, which was dramatic of her and she knew it. The map weighed nothing. It weighed a ton.

She'd promised a dying man she'd find his daughter. Hazel Okonkwo kept her promises the way she kept her logbook entries — reluctantly, occasionally late, but always, eventually, in ink.

* * *

Finding Ruth Crowe took less than an hour, which surprised Hazel until she understood that Fort Meridian wasn't big enough to hide anyone who didn't want to be found, and Ruth Crowe very much did not want to be found.

The waitress at the Blue Spruce Café — a cheerful woman named Dot who treated gossip as a food group — pointed her fork toward the end of the counter without being asked.

"Ruth? She's the one glarin' at her coffee like it owes her money. Mechanic. Works out of Kowalski's garage. Don't take it personal if she bites."

"I never do," Hazel said. "Biting's just conversation with teeth."

Dot laughed. Ruth Crowe, three stools down, did not.

Hazel took her time looking. She'd learned to read people the way she read weather — fast, from a distance, before things turned ugly. Ruth was maybe thirty, built like someone who lifted engines for a living because she liked them better than people, with sawdust-pale eyebrows and hands scarred in the specific way of a woman who'd been fixing things since she was small enough to lose fingers doing it. She wore coveralls rolled at the cuffs and an expression that suggested the world had filed for divorce and she'd countersued.

"Ruth Crowe?" Hazel said, taking the stool beside her.

"No," said Ruth Crowe.

"Good start." Hazel ordered coffee from Dot and let the silence sit between them like a third customer. "My name's Hazel Okonkwo. I fly freight up the Kestrel. I was at Angus Teller's cabin two days ago, and I met your father."

Something moved behind Ruth's eyes — quick, then slammed shut like a hangar door in wind.

"I don't have a father," she said. "I have a man who shares my surname and my blood type and nothing else. What do you want?"

"He's dying, Ruth."

The coffee cup stopped halfway to Ruth's mouth. Then it completed its journey, and she drank, and set it down, and when she spoke her voice was flat as a frozen lake. "Ulcer?"

"Burst, near as I could tell. He wouldn't let me fly him out. Said he'd made his peace." Hazel hesitated. "He gave me something instead. A map. He asked me to bring it to you."

"A map." Ruth laughed, and there was no warmth in it at all, only rust. "Of course he did. Forty years of rivers and rocks, and the man's final act is to send me a treasure hunt through a lawyer's worth of fine print. Did he say anything else? 'Sorry'? 'I thought about you on your birthday every year for thirty years'? Or was it coordinates?"

"He said the river's source remembers what Vane paid to forget."

That landed. Hazel watched it land — watched Ruth's jaw tighten and her shoulders come up around her ears like a woman bracing against weather.

"Vane," Ruth repeated. "Silas Vane."

"You know him."

"Everybody in this town knows Vane Consolidated. Half the town works for them. The other half sells them groceries." Ruth stood, dug bills out of her pocket, threw them on the counter. "Thanks for the coffee. Thanks for flying all this way to deliver a dead man's homework. But I've got an engine torn down and no interest in finishing my father's sentences."

She was almost to the door when Hazel said, quietly: "He wrote to you. Every year. He told me."

Ruth stopped. Didn't turn. For a long moment the café held its breath — even Dot, wiping the far counter, went very still, the way small animals do when they sense a storm front.

"He told you that," Ruth said finally, "and you thought it would help your case."

"I thought it might hurt," Hazel admitted. "It usually does. That's how you know it's true."

Ruth pushed out into the cold, and the door swung shut behind her, and Hazel sat alone with two coffees and the distinct feeling of having botched a landing she should have stuck.

* * *

Hazel spent the afternoon doing what she always did in towns too small for hotels and too proud for charity: making herself useful until someone fed her. She helped old Manoukian re-cord his woodpile, bought fuel she didn't need from the co-op, and listened to three separate theories about why the moose hadn't come down to the river flats this year. By evening she'd learned everything Fort Meridian knew about Ruth Crowe, which was: best mechanic within two hundred miles; doesn't drink; doesn't dance; came here eight years ago from somewhere south and nobody asks where; and once, famously, told a Vane Consolidated site foreman to take his broken dozer and his attitude and go jump in the Kestrel, in that order.

A woman with a grudge against Vane. Hazel filed that away next to the map in her breast pocket.

She found Ruth at the garage after dark, under a work lamp, elbow-deep in the guts of a snowmobile. Hazel leaned in the doorway and waited. She was good at waiting. It was most of what flying was.

"If you're here about the map," Ruth said without looking up, "the answer's still no."

"It's not about the map. It's about the fact that two men came off the morning barge asking questions about Eben Crowe. Where he'd been seen, who he'd talked to, whether he'd left papers behind. They were wearing Vane jackets and carrying Vane clipboards, and one of them had a smile like a fishhook."

The wrench stopped moving.

"They talked to Manoukian," Hazel went on. "They talked to the co-op. They're working their way through the whole town, polite as church ushers. And here's the part I keep chewing on, Ruth: if Eben Crowe was just a half-mad prospector chasing fool's gold, nobody would care. Nobody sends fishhook smiles upriver for fool's gold."

Ruth set down the wrench. Wiped her hands on a rag with the slow deliberation of a woman buying time. When she finally looked at Hazel, her eyes in the work lamp's glare were the color of river ice — beautiful, and liable to break something if you walked on them wrong.

"How long has he got?" she said. "My father."

"Weeks. Maybe days. Maybe less, if he keeps refusing food the way he was."

Ruth nodded slowly. She pulled a crate over, sat down on it, and for the first time since the café she looked tired instead of armored. "You want to know why I don't answer his letters. Eight years of them, one every Christmas, postmarked from whatever hellhole he's camped in. You want to know."

"I didn't ask."

"That's why I'm going to tell you." She turned the rag over in her hands. "When I was nine, he promised me he'd be home for my birthday. Not Christmas, not New Year's — my birthday, March fourth, because he'd missed the two before that and he knew it mattered. And I sat on our porch in Fort Nelson with a cake my mother made, watching the road, until the candles burned down and my mother cried in the kitchen and the road stayed empty. He was forty miles up some creek because the colors looked good, he said later. The colors looked good." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, Hazel could see her hating it. "So no. I don't care what's on his map. I don't care what Vane paid to forget. A man who chooses colors over his kid doesn't get to hand me a quest at the finish line and call it fatherhood."

The garage was quiet except for the tick of the cooling engine and, somewhere outside, the barge horn on the river, long and low, like the town clearing its throat.

Hazel thought about her own empty apartment above the float base in Yellowknife. The calendar with nothing written on it. The way she'd started saying hello to her plane first when she got home, because the plane was always glad to see her and the apartment never noticed she'd gone.

"I don't know anything about fathers," she said. "Mine died when I was six. But I know something about running. I've been running so long I've got frequent flyer miles on my own life. And I'll tell you what I've learned from altitude, Ruth, which is the only place I ever learned anything: you can put a lot of distance between yourself and a thing, but the thing stays exactly where it is, and eventually everybody runs out of fuel."

Ruth snorted. "That's a terrible metaphor."

"It's a true one. Terrible and true — like most of aviation."

And Ruth laughed. Just once, short and unwilling, but real — and the sound changed the shape of the room.

* * *

They were still sitting in the garage an hour later — Hazel on an oil drum, Ruth back under the snowmobile but no longer really working on it — when the truck pulled up outside.

Headlights swept the open bay door. Two men got out. Vane jackets. One carried a clipboard; the other carried himself like a man who'd done violence professionally and missed it the way other men missed fishing. The fishhook smile belonged to the second one.

"Ladies," he said pleasantly. "Sorry to bother you this late. We're looking for information on a prospector name of Eben Crowe. Last seen upriver. Anybody talk to him lately, maybe hold mail for him, maybe—" his eyes drifted, casual as a hand into someone else's pocket, "—maybe receive anything from him?"

"Wrong Crowe," Ruth said, not getting up. "No relation."

"Is that right." He smiled wider. "Funny, because the ferry operator says different. Says a pilot flew in yesterday asking after Ruth Crowe, daughter of Eben. Funny how stories travel faster than planes these days."

"Funny how ferry operators talk," Hazel said, "when they think nobody's listening."

The smile didn't waver, but something behind it recalculated. "Mr. Vane values his privacy. His company's permits are a matter of public record, but what happens on company land is company business. If Mr. Crowe left any documents — maps, journals, correspondence — Vane Consolidated is prepared to offer fair compensation. More than fair." He let that hang. "Or, failing that, we can discuss less pleasant arrangements. Legal ones. Trespass. Interference with survey operations. There's a lot of law, ladies. You'd be amazed how much of it is on our side."

"Was there anything else?" Ruth said.

"Not tonight." He touched his cap brim, mock-genteel. "But we'll be in town a while. Towns are interesting places. Everybody knows something, and everybody tells somebody, and eventually it all comes together like water finding a low spot."

The truck pulled away. Its taillights shrank down the road toward the river, red as brake lights on a runway, and neither woman moved until the sound of the engine faded into the dark.

Then Ruth stood up, crossed the garage, and held out her hand.

"Give me the map," she said.

Hazel dug it out of her jacket — grease pencil on butcher paper, creased into eighths, smelling faintly of the cabin where a dying man had pressed it into her hands. Ruth unfolded it under the work lamp and went very still.

Because there, in the corner, in Eben Crowe's cramped hand, beneath the penciled contour lines and the little X at the head of the Kestrel, was a single sentence Hazel hadn't been able to make out by lamplight in a trapper's cabin:

*Not gold. Worse than gold. They'll kill for it — they already did.*

Ruth read it twice. When she looked up, the ice in her eyes had cracked clean through, and underneath it was something hotter.

"The permits," she said. "How long does Vane have on them?"

"Thirty days," Hazel said. "After that, whatever's buried up that river belongs to him forever."

Ruth folded the map along its old creases, careful as handling a bird, and tucked it into her coveralls.

"Then we'd better get going," she said.
