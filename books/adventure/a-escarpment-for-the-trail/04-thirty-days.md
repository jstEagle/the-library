## 4. Thirty Days

The map smelled like a man's last three days.

That was the first thing Ruth noticed, once she finally allowed herself to unroll it on Hazel's kitchen table in Fort Meridian — the grease-pencil lines gone soft and smeared in places where a dying hand had sweated against the paper, and beneath it all a faint sourness that no amount of telling herself *he's dead, it's only paper* could quite scrub out of her nose.

She'd said no, originally. She wanted that on the record, even if the only witness was a bush pilot who was currently standing at her counter drinking coffee that Ruth had not offered and had not been asked for.

"I said no," Ruth said. "I want to be clear about that. Historically, I said no."

"Very clear," Hazel said. "You said no with real conviction. Then Vane's men showed up asking about your father, and your conviction developed a limp."

"It didn't develop a limp. It was *outvoted*."

"By what?"

Ruth didn't answer that. She smoothed the map flat with both hands, the way you'd smooth a sheet over someone sleeping, and studied what her father had left her.

It was, on its face, a bad map. Eben Crowe had been a careful man with a transit and a careless man with everything else, and the grease pencil wobbled across the paper like a river itself: the Kestrel drawn in one bold stroke, tributaries branching off in thinner lines, and here and there little annotations in a handwriting she knew better than she wanted to. *Cold camp. Grizzly, do not argue.* A sketch of a split rock shaped like a tooth. A falls marked with three exclamation points, which for her father constituted hysteria.

And at the top of the page, where the river narrowed to a thread and simply *stopped* — the way rivers do on maps, as though they were born full-grown — he'd drawn a small square and written, in letters pressed so hard the pencil had torn the paper:

**THE SOURCE. WHAT IT REMEMBERS.**

"What it remembers," Ruth read aloud. "That's not how sources work. Sources don't remember. They just sit there being wet."

"Everything remembers," Hazel said. "Water especially. It keeps going back."

"That's erosion, not sentiment."

"Same thing, if you're patient."

Ruth looked up at her. Hazel Okonkwo was maybe thirty, wiry, with the particular stillness of a person whose job was mostly waiting for weather. She had a small scar through one eyebrow and the unbothered air of someone who had already decided what she thought about you and found you acceptable, provisionally, the way you find a dog acceptable before it's been through a thunderstorm with you.

Ruth did not find people acceptable. Ruth found people expensive. And yet here she was, at this woman's kitchen table, at seven in the morning, with a dead man's map between them.

* * *

The problem, it turned out, was scale.

Eben had drawn his map the way he'd drawn everything — from memory, from love, and with no regard whatsoever for the fact that other people might need to *find* things. The tributaries were right; Ruth could feel that they were right, the way you can feel a song is right. But there were no distances. No bearings. Just the river's shape and a scattering of landmarks that might have been anywhere along two hundred miles of country.

"We need it against a real chart," Ruth said. "He drew this from the inside looking out. We need the outside looking in."

Hazel disappeared into her back room and came out with an armload of rolled paper, a tin of pencils, and a look of such naked relief that Ruth almost laughed. It was the look of a woman who had been waiting years for an excuse to do the thing she was best at.

"Topographic sheets," Hazel said, spreading them across the table and pinning the corners with the salt shaker, a mug, a tin of gun oil, and a small carved loon that Ruth suspected had sentimental value and was therefore in no danger. "Government survey, 1968, so half of it's wrong, but wrong in a *documented* way, which is better than nothing."

They worked through the morning. Ruth read her father's hand — the shorthand, the private symbols, the way he'd always drawn a beaver lodge as a tiny castle because he'd told her once, when she was six, that beavers were the only honest engineers. Hazel read the terrain. Between them, the two languages began to translate.

"That tooth rock," Hazel said, tapping the sketch. "There's a formation like it here — see the contour lines, how they pinch?" She drew a grease pencil — *his* grease pencil, Ruth realized, taken from the map roll — in a long line down the chart. "If that's right, his cold camp is here, and this portage is here, which means the falls are—"

"Three exclamation points' worth of falls," Ruth said.

"Which on your father's scale is a Class Five with opinions."

Ruth found, to her considerable irritation, that she was enjoying herself. She'd been a surveyor's assistant for two seasons before quitting, and there was a particular pleasure in this work — the puzzle of it, two imperfect pictures of the same country resolving into one — and she'd forgotten, or had decided to forget, how much she loved it. Her father had taught her this. Every skill she was using had come through his hands into hers, and she resented every one of them even as they worked.

It was nearly noon when Hazel went still over the chart.

"What," Ruth said.

"Come here. Look at the concession boundary."

Ruth leaned in. Hazel had penciled in the Vane Consolidated permit lines from a notice posted at the Hudson's Bay post — everyone in the interior had seen them, the neat red rectangles that had been creeping up the Kestrel for three years, swallowing trapping lines and fishing camps and, last spring, the whole of the old Meridian trail. The boundary ran up the river and then, at a certain point, simply ended. Above that line, the map was blank. Not unclaimed. *Unmarked.* As though the surveyors had run out of ink, or nerve.

"The permits," Hazel said. "The new ones. They're posted at the post office — they lapse in thirty days. Renewal's automatic if there's no challenge filed." She tapped the blank space above the boundary line. "And whatever your father found is above this line. Inside the renewal zone. After thirty days, it's his. Whatever's up there — it stops being findable and starts being *owned*."

"Owned by a man who sent two goons to ask me when I'd last heard from my father."

"Owned by a man whose goons asked *me* the same question, and then asked if I'd be flying the Kestrel this season, and then looked at my plane a little too long." Hazel said it lightly, but her hand had stopped moving. "Ruth. I've been flying this country nine years. I know what a man looks like when he's pricing your aircraft for scrap."

The kitchen was quiet. Outside, Fort Meridian went about its morning — the floatplane dock creaking, someone's dog conducting a one-dog war with a raven, the general unhurried industry of a town that had survived every boom and bust by refusing to believe in either.

"Thirty days," Ruth said.

"Twenty-nine, now. We slept late."

"We didn't sleep late. *You* were up at five reading a manual for fun."

"It's a good manual."

* * *

They ate lunch standing up, which Ruth took as a sign of something, though she couldn't have said what. Hazel made sandwiches with the efficiency of a field surgeon and talked with her mouth half full, which should have been repellent and instead was oddly companionable.

"So let's say we go," Hazel said. "Play it out. We go up the Kestrel, we find this source, we find whatever your father found. Then what? You can't file a challenge with a feeling. You'd need something real. Documents. Proof."

"He wrote *what it remembers*." Ruth turned the map over. On the back, faint, was a list in her father's hand — supplies, mostly, rope and cartridges and rice, and at the bottom: *journals, sealed tin, DO NOT leave at camp.* "He kept journals. He always kept journals. If he found something up there, he wrote it down. He wrote *everything* down. He once documented, with diagrams, an argument he had with a marmot."

"Who won?"

"The marmot. It got his bacon and a formal apology." Ruth heard her own voice crack slightly on the word *formal* and covered it by drinking coffee. "The point is — if there's proof up there, he has it, or he tells us where it is. And if there isn't—" She set the cup down. "Then we come home, and in thirty days it's gone, and I spend the rest of my life knowing I had a month and spent it being careful."

Hazel was quiet for a moment. "You understand what you're asking. That's Vane country up there. His crews, his enforcers, his river. Two women in a canoe against a company that eats companies."

"I'm not asking you to come."

"You're not asking me to come," Hazel agreed. "You're sitting at my table, looking at my charts, drinking my coffee, with a face like a kicked saint, not asking me to come."

"I can leave."

"You can." Hazel looked out the window at her plane on the dock — a de Havilland Beaver so old and patched that Ruth privately thought of it as a quilt with an engine. "I've been running this route nine years," Hazel said. "Medicine, mail, gossip, the occasional emergency C-section in a snowstorm. I know every community on this river and I'm welcome in all of them, and I sleep in a different bed every week, and I could not tell you a single one of them is *home*." She turned back. "I've been waiting a long time for a reason to go somewhere on purpose. Your father handed me a map and a dying promise. You're handing me thirty days. That's the best offer I've had in years."

Ruth looked at her. There was a whole conversation underneath this one, and they were both declining to have it, and Ruth found she was grateful for that. Two guarded women, building a bridge out of what they refused to say.

"Twenty-nine days," Ruth said.

"Twenty-eight and a half. We'll need to provision tomorrow, fly to the lakehead the day after, and I want a weather window." Hazel was already pulling a fresh sheet toward her, sketching a route in quick strokes: the flight north, the lakehead, the river. "We go light. We go fast. We don't tell anyone where we're going, and when Vane's men ask — and they will ask — we're doing a routine mail run."

"You're a bush pilot. Do you do routine mail runs up dead-end rivers in the last week before permit renewals?"

"No," Hazel said. "That's what makes it routine."

Ruth laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised sound she hadn't made in months — and something in the room shifted, some load-bearing wall coming down between them, not all the way, but enough to see through.

They worked the rest of the afternoon. By dusk the chart was marked end to end: fuel stops, camps, the falls with their three exclamation points now annotated in Hazel's hand (*opinions confirmed*), and the blank space at the top of the river where the source waited, circled twice, with a date beside it. Twenty-eight days from now, give or take a day for weather and a day for luck.

Ruth rolled her father's map carefully and sat holding it a moment before putting it away. *Take it to my daughter,* he'd said. *Before it's too late.* She had assumed he'd meant the map.

Outside, the last floatplane of the day droned in over the water — and it wasn't the mail plane. It was a company aircraft, white and new, Vane Consolidated's crest on the tail, and it circled the town once, low and deliberate, the way a man walks past your house to let you know he knows where you live.

Hazel watched it land at the company dock with an expression Ruth was beginning to recognize: calm on the surface, arithmetic underneath.

"Twenty-eight days," Hazel said. "I suggest we don't waste the first one sleeping."

They started packing that night.
