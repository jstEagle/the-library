## 5. Upstream

The lakehead was called Tumbling Bay on the survey maps and Nothing Much by everyone who'd ever landed there, which was a short list that now included Hazel Okonkwo twice.

She set the de Havilland down on water the color of hammered pewter, long swells rolling in from the west, and let her drift to the gravel spit while Ruth sat rigid in the second seat with both hands braced against the dash like a woman expecting the plane to change its mind about flying.

"You can unclench," Hazel said. "She's a good old girl. Bites nobody but me."

"She shuddered."

"Everything shudders up here. It's how you know it's alive." Hazel killed the engine and the silence came down around them, enormous and ringing, the kind of silence that made you aware of your own pulse. Somewhere across the bay a loon said something rude and meant it.

Ruth climbed out onto the float and stood looking at the country the way she'd looked at everything since Fort Meridian — like it owed her money. She was thirty-one, built narrow and hard, with her father's same stubborn jaw and none of his softness for rivers. She carried the map rolled inside a length of PVC pipe she'd taped to her pack frame, and she checked it was there roughly every ninety seconds, which Hazel found endearing and also slightly exhausting.

"Which way?" Ruth asked.

"Upstream's north-northwest. Three days' paddle if the weather holds, five if it doesn't, and it won't." Hazel hauled their canoe off the rack where she'd lashed it under the wing — a sixteen-foot Kevlar hull she'd traded two cases of engine oil and a favor to a man in Copper Landing for — and slid it into the shallows. "Your father pick this route?"

"He didn't tell me anything about any route."

"No," Hazel said gently. "But he drew it. Men who draw rivers like this have run them. We'll trust the pencil."

They loaded in silence: packs, paddles, the tarp, the food pail, the rifle Hazel had brought mostly out of habit and partly because of what Vane Consolidated's men had done to Eben Crowe's cabin door back at the trapline — kicked in, turned over, patient as tax auditors. That had been three days ago, and Hazel still felt it between her shoulder blades, the sensation of being watched by something corporate.

The river took them the way rivers do, all business at first — current quick over gravel bars, spruce closing in dark on both banks — then gentler as the valley widened. By the second hour Ruth had stopped gripping the gunwales and started reading the water, pointing out rocks before Hazel called them, and Hazel thought: *there she is.* Eben's daughter after all, whether she liked it or not.

"Used to canoe?" Hazel asked.

"My grandfather's. Before he sold the lodge." A pause. "I was nine."

"That's a long time ago."

"Everything about my father is a long time ago." But she said it without teeth, and Hazel let it lie.

* * *

They saw the first sign of Vane's work that evening, and it stopped them both mid-stroke.

A cut block opened on the east bank like a wound — forty acres of spruce felled and skidded down to mud, stumps pale as bone, a crawler track lying dead in the muck with one tread thrown. Beyond it, stacked in cordwood rows, the logs waited for a barge that hadn't come yet. The smell of fresh-cut resin hung over the water so thickly Hazel could taste it.

"They're not mining," Ruth said slowly. "That's timber."

"Timber pays the bills while they look for whatever they're actually looking for." Hazel nosed the canoe toward the far bank, away from the cut. "Eben's map has us crossing a portage tomorrow that starts somewhere near his old camp. If there's anything left of it."

There wasn't much.

* * *

His camp sat in a bend where the river slowed and deepened, a place any prospector would love — bedrock exposed in the banks, quartz veins threading through it like frost on a window. Eben had built well, once: a log cabin with a shake roof, a cache on stilts, a woodshed. Now the cabin door hung by one hinge. Every drawer had been pulled and emptied onto the floor. The mattress was gutted, straw scattered, the stovepipe bent double. Someone had even taken the axe head off the handle, which struck Hazel as a special kind of thoroughness.

"They were looking for the map," Ruth said. Her voice was flat, but her hands, Hazel noticed, were shaking as she picked through the wreckage.

"They were looking for everything. Map, journals, letters, samples. Anything that told them what he knew." Hazel crouched by the cold firepit outside. Ashes, weeks old — but on top of the ash, boot prints, more than one man, recent enough that the edges hadn't crumbled. "And they'll come back when they realize he gave it away before they got here."

Ruth stood very still in the middle of the ruined room. "He lived like this," she said. "All those years. This is what he chose instead of—" She stopped herself, jaw working.

Instead of me, Hazel finished silently. She'd heard the shape of that sentence before, in her own head, about her own father, and she knew better than to touch it.

"Come on," she said aloud. "We've got light left. Let's find his cache before somebody else does."

It was Ruth who spotted it — a stilted platform half-hidden in alder, and beneath it, scratched into the trunk of a spruce, a small mark: a circle with a line through it, like an eye closed. Eben's mark; they'd seen it on the map. Inside the cache, under a false floor, they found a tin box with dried food gone stale, a spare wool shirt, and — wrapped in oilcloth — a surveyor's brass sample case, heavy as sin.

Hazel popped the latches.

Drill cores. Six of them, sleeved in plastic, each tagged in careful engineer's hand: *K-14, 212m. K-15, 240m.* And stapled to the inside of the lid, a photocopied page stamped in red across every inch:

**VANE CONSOLIDATED MINERALS LTD. — CONFIDENTIAL. DISTRIBUTION RESTRICTED UNDER EXPLORATION AGREEMENT 71-C. UNAUTHORIZED POSSESSION IS THEFT.**

"Sealed," Hazel murmured. "Every core from the whole concession, sealed under confidentiality order. You don't seal gold cores, Ruth. Gold you shout about. You seal things you're ashamed of."

Ruth lifted one sleeve to the fading light. The rock inside was ordinary-looking — grey-green, banded — but at one end, where the drill had bitten deepest, the stone was stained. Dark. Rust-brown in some places, black in others, and threaded through it, unmistakably, fine fragments of charred wood.

"That's not ore staining," Ruth said quietly. She was a nurse; she knew burned material when she saw it. "That's ash. That's *burnt* something, sealed in bedrock at two hundred meters."

Hazel looked at the tag again. *Agreement 71-C.* Nineteen seventy-one. The year, according to every public record Hazel had ever read, that Vane Consolidated had first broken ground on the Kestrel headwater — a ground-breaking celebrated in the company newsletter with photographs of smiling men and one caption Hazel now remembered word for word: *a new era begins.*

"The source remembers what Vane paid to forget," she said. "Eben's words. I thought he meant treasure."

"What else could be at two hundred meters?"

"Nothing natural." Hazel repacked the case with hands that wanted to hurry and forced slow. "Bodies get buried. Sometimes companies bury them deeper. And sometimes the ground gives them back to anybody with a drill."

They cached the cores back under the floor — no sense carrying weight they might have to run with — but Ruth copied every tag number into her notebook, pressing hard enough to tear three pages.

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar downstream, fire small, both of them facing the water out of habit neither discussed. Across the river the cut block showed black against the last light, and beyond it, faint on the wind, came a sound Hazel had been pretending not to hear for an hour: engines. More than one. Somewhere upstream, machinery was still working past dusk.

"Survey crews don't run drills at night," Hazel said.

"Then what is it?"

"Honest answer? I don't know. But Eben's portage starts four miles up, and those engines are between us and it." She fed a stick to the fire and watched the sparks climb. "We can wait for dark and slip past, or we can go wide through the burn on the west side and add a day."

Ruth was quiet a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket and took out the bundle of letters she'd shown Hazel once, briefly, in Fort Meridian — her father's annual letters, unanswered, thirty years of them — and held the top one as if weighing it.

"He wrote that the river above the falls was the most honest place he'd ever been," Ruth said. "I used to think that was him apologizing for not coming home. Now I think it was a warning label." She put the letters away. "Dark. We slip past. I'm done letting other people decide how long this takes."

Hazel grinned despite everything. "There's the jaw. Your father's jaw."

"Don't," Ruth said, but she almost smiled, and above them the first stars came out over a river full of secrets, running north toward whatever Vane had paid to forget.

Somewhere upstream, the engines went silent all at once — and in the new quiet, clear across the water, came the sound of men shouting, and a single shot.
