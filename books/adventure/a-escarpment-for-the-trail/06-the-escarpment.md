## 6. The Escarpment

The river changed its mind three times a day, Ruth decided. In the morning it ran brown and sullen with silt from the cutbanks Vane's machines had opened upstream; by noon it cleared to a green so deep it looked like looking down into the sky's own basement; and by evening it went silver and indifferent, carrying nothing for anyone.

She'd stopped hating it somewhere around the fourth day. That frightened her more than Reeve did.

"Portage starts here," Hazel said, nosing the canoe toward a gravel shelf where the water began to talk in that low, muscular way it did before it committed to violence. She shipped her paddle and listened for a moment, head cocked, like a bird deciding whether a worm was worth the trouble. "Hear that? That's not rapids. That's the escarpment. Whole river going over a wall."

Ruth couldn't hear anything but her own pulse and the whine of mosquitoes conducting their eternal audit. But she nodded, because Hazel had a way of hearing country the way other people heard music, and Ruth had learned — grudgingly, then less grudgingly — to trust it.

They lifted the canoe together. It was heavier than it had been at the lakehead, or maybe Ruth was lighter; five days of oatmeal, dried fish, and adrenaline will do that. The trail along the north bank was barely a suggestion, a deer path gone ambitious, threading through alder that grabbed at their sleeves like beggars who'd heard they had money.

"You know what I miss?" Hazel said, half a mile in, breathing hard but cheerful about it. "Coffee. Real coffee. Not this twig-and-regret business you've been brewing."

"That twig-and-regret business is the only reason you're vertical."

"I'm vertical out of spite. Spite needs no caffeine." She shifted the yoke on her shoulders. "Though I'll admit the spite is flagging around four o'clock most days."

Ruth almost smiled. Almost. Smiling felt like spending money she might need later.

They were two days' portage from the source now, if Eben's map could be believed — and every hour Ruth spent deciphering its grease-pencil hieroglyphs made her believe it more, which was its own kind of grief. Her father had drawn the escarpment as a long, deliberate line, like something he'd wanted to underline twice. *Wall guards water*, he'd written beneath it, in his cramped surveyor's hand. *Go high, go quiet, go at first light.* And then, smaller, almost an afterthought pressed so hard the pencil had torn the paper: *Ruthie, if you're reading this, then you came.*

She hadn't come, for thirty years. She was coming now. The arithmetic of that didn't balance anywhere she could find.

* * *

They saw him on the second morning, through fog so thick it seemed the world had been wrapped in wet wool and forgotten.

Ruth spotted him because he moved wrong. Everything in that country moved right — herons folded themselves up like carpenters putting away tools, moose waded with the patience of old men, even the mist seemed to have somewhere proper to be. But far back along the portage trail, maybe four hundred meters, something detached itself from a spruce and flowed to the next one, and it flowed like intention. Like a man who had done this in worse places than this.

"Reeve," she breathed.

Hazel didn't turn her head. That was the first thing Ruth admired about her, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before saying so: Hazel's stillness under pressure, the way her eyes kept working while everything else went quiet. "Where?"

"Back along the trail. Third bend. He's alone."

"Alone means confident or desperate." Hazel eased them both down behind a deadfall furred with moss, moving like the word *slow* had been invented for her personally. "With Callum Reeve, my money's on confident. He tracked us through the gorge section without losing a day. That's not luck, that's craft."

"What do we do?"

"We do what your father said." Hazel's mouth twitched. "Go high, go quiet, go at first light. Well. It's first light. Two out of three isn't bad."

They climbed. The escarpment rose ahead of them all day, a gray-green rampart dripping with seeps, and they took the goat track up its shoulder with the canoe strapped between them like a stretcher case, trading it back and forth when arms failed. Twice Ruth looked back. The second time, the fog below had swallowed the trail entirely, and she understood with a cold, clean clarity that Reeve didn't need to see them. He needed only to read the country the way they did. There was one way up the wall. They were on it.

"Hazel," she said quietly. "He knows where we are. He's just letting us carry the heavy stuff."

Hazel stopped. For a long moment she stood with her hand flat against the rock, breathing, and Ruth watched her do arithmetic with her face — the plane at the lakehead, the journals waiting above the falls, the fuel of her own body burning down like a lamp.

"He can't take the map from us if we don't have it," Hazel said finally. "And he can't follow us past the falls if there's nothing left of us to follow." She turned, and her grin was the reckless kind, the kind Ruth was beginning to recognize as a load-bearing structure. "We cache the canoe. We cache everything but the journals, the rifle, and enough food to be sorry about later. We go up light and fast tonight, in the fog, and we let him climb a wall behind us for nothing."

"And if he catches us on the open rock?"

"Then we'll have a conversation about his career choices."

* * *

It nearly went wrong exactly the way wrong things do: suddenly, stupidly, in weather.

The fog came off the escarpment top at dusk like a tide, and they used it, moving along the rim on hands and knees where the rock opened up, single file, Hazel leading by touch and memory of the map. Ruth carried the journals lashed against her chest in a waterproof pack, and she had one hand on Hazel's boot heel, and the world had shrunk to a circle two feet wide.

Then the circle filled with a flashlight beam.

It came from below and left — Reeve, or someone, climbing the same shelf they'd abandoned, sweeping the rim in patient arcs. The beam swung toward them and Ruth froze, and freezing was the mistake, because her boot found a slick seam of wet lichen and the whole world tilted.

She slid. Six feet, eight, ten — down a chute of rock, the journal pack grinding between her and the stone, until a stunted pine caught her like a hand catching a falling cup. She hung there, breath knocked to static, and heard above her: voices. Two of them. Reeve and another man, close enough that she could hear the wet click of a radio being keyed.

"—saw movement on the rim, Mr. Reeve—"

"Then stop sawing and start climbing."

Ruth pressed herself into the pine's shadow and did not breathe. Gravel trickled past her ear, dislodged by boots above. Ten feet up, maybe twelve, a flashlight beam probed the chute she'd just fallen down, lingered on the crushed lichen, on the long bright scar of her slide—

—and Hazel dropped a rock.

Not at them. Beside them, twenty feet east, a fist-sized stone clattering down through the fog with a sound like a door slamming in an empty house. The beam snapped away. Boots scrambled east. A voice swore with feeling.

By the time the beam swept back, Ruth was up and moving, and Hazel's hand found hers in the dark, and they went west along the rim at a dead crouch, leaving the canoe, the tent, the spare food, the stove — everything but the journals, the rifle, and Ruth's father's letters — scattered behind them like a shed skin.

They didn't stop until the fog thinned near midnight and the stars came out hard and cold above the escarpment's shoulder, and then Hazel sat down on a boulder and started laughing, silently, helplessly, tears cutting tracks through the dirt on her face.

"What," Ruth said.

"You fell off a cliff, hung from a tree, and your contribution to the escape plan was *hanging there*. Meanwhile I threw one rock and I'm the hero." She wiped her eyes. "I'm putting it in my memoirs."

"The memoirs of a woman who flies a plane held together with prayer and baling wire."

"Baling wire has feelings, Ruthie. Be kind."

But the laugh drained out of her after a while, and they sat in the cold sharing the last chocolate, which Hazel insisted on dividing with geometric precision, and the silence got long, and Ruth found — to her horror — that it was filling up with words.

"My father wrote to me," she said. "Every year. On my birthday."

Hazel said nothing. She was good at that. Ruth was beginning to suspect it was her best skill, better than the flying.

"Thirty-one letters. Maybe more. He sent them care of my mother's sister, and Aunt Vera forwarded them, and I never — I didn't open the last ten years of them. They're in a shoebox in Fort Meridian. Unopened." Ruth turned the chocolate wrapper over and over in her fingers. "I told myself it was because he chose the rivers over us. Over me. And that was true. But it was also because as long as I didn't read them, I didn't have to find out whether he was sorry. Whether he'd even thought he owed an apology. I couldn't risk finding out he didn't think about me at all."

"And now?"

"Now I've been reading his survival caches for a week." Her voice cracked and she let it. "He hid letters in them, Hazel. Dated. One per year, tucked in with the fishhooks and the matches, like he knew someday I'd come up this river even if he'd never see it. Like he was leaving a trail of breadcrumbs for a daughter he wasn't sure existed anymore. And every letter says the same thing in different ways. *I was wrong. I loved you the whole time. I don't expect the door to open.*" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "He died thinking I hated him. And the worst part — the part I can't get past — is that he was mostly right. I did hate him. Right up until I started following his handwriting through the bush, and then it all just turned into missing him, and missing him is so much worse."

Hazel reached over and took the chocolate wrapper out of her hands, gently, and held onto her hands instead. Her palms were rope-hard and warm.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I think belonging works like a campfire. You don't find one. You build one, and then you sit by it. Your dad built his out here and left the coals banked for you. That's more than most people ever get from anybody." She paused, and her voice went quieter, rougher. "I've never built one. I fly over everybody's fire and land at none of them. So I'm maybe not the expert here."

"You're the expert at not abandoning people," Ruth said. "You promised a dying stranger. Look where that got you. Up a mountain, in the fog, throwing rocks at mercenaries."

"Somebody's got to throw them."

Above them, the escarpment's crest stood black against the stars, and beyond it, faint on the wind, came a sound Ruth had only ever known from her father's maps: the long, unbroken roar of the falls, and behind the falls, the quiet place where the Kestrel began.

Below and behind, far down the dark slope, a flashlight beam swept once across the fog and went still. Watching. Waiting for morning.

Hazel squeezed her hand once and let go. "First light," she said. "Go high, go quiet. Let's go meet whatever it is he paid to forget."
