## 7. What the Source Remembers

The portage above the falls took them most of the day, and by the time they crested the last ridge the light had gone the color of weak tea. Hazel came up over the lip of the escarpment with her shoulders burning and her boots full of creek water, and stopped so suddenly that Ruth walked into her back.

"Well," Ruth said. "Huh."

Below them, tucked into a fold of the headwater valley where the Kestrel was born as a spring no wider than a kitchen table, lay a town that wasn't on any map Hazel had ever flown over. Not a ruin, exactly — not yet. A scatter of cabins with their roofs caved in soft as old bread. A collapsed headframe leaning against the hillside like a drunk against a lamppost. A row of poplars planted along what had clearly once been a street, grown tall and crooked in the forty years since anyone had walked beneath them.

"There's nothing on my charts," Hazel said. "Nothing on the forestry maps either."

"Of course there isn't." Ruth's voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when she was holding something down. "Dad drew this place from memory. He said—" She stopped. Started again. "He said some places get erased twice. Once with fire, once with paper."

They went down into the valley single file through the poplars, and the quiet up there was a different animal than the quiet below the escarpment. It wasn't empty. It was *attentive*. Hazel had spent twenty years listening to country for a living — reading wind off water, engine noise off mountain walls — and every instinct she had told her this silence was being maintained by something.

The first grave they found was behind the blacksmith's shack, if the rusted anvil half-buried in moss meant anything. A wooden marker, weathered to gray lace, no name. Just a number: **14**.

The second was beside it. **15**.

Then they crested a small rise and stopped counting, because there were too many, rows of them fanning out from a low ground where the spring fed into a pool, dozens of markers, all numbered, none named, the numbers themselves nearly worn away, as though whoever cut them had wanted to mark the dead without ever having to admit whose dead they were.

Ruth sat down hard on a fallen log. Hazel stood beside her and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Forty years of growth on the poplars. Forty years of moss on the markers. 1971. The year Silas Vane's first strike came in. The year the Kestrel concession was registered, clean and unencumbered, on land that had until then been tied up in a labor dispute nobody could resolve because — Hazel felt the thought arrive like cold water — *nobody left alive to press it.*

"Dad knew," Ruth said. Her voice was very steady, which is how Hazel knew she was crying. "That's why he stayed out here thirty years. He wasn't prospecting. He was *guarding*."

* * *

Eben's cache was exactly where his map promised, under the floor of the last cabin on the street — the one with the bell tower, a chapel or a schoolhouse, its roof open to the sky like a rib cage. The boards came up easy; he'd oiled the hinges of the trapdoor underneath, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas, sealed in tins that had cost him real money, were his field journals.

There were eleven of them. Hazel carried them out into the failing light and opened the earliest one on her knee while Ruth kept watch at the treeline, and the handwriting that marched across the first page was cramped and furious and utterly certain of itself.

*June 14th, 1971.* Eben Crowe, age twenty-four, hired as a powder monkey for Kestrel Consolidated Mining. Wages frozen three months. The men voted to strike. Vane himself flew in — young then, hungry, the ink of the company registration barely dry — and promised arbitration, and brought in outside crews instead, and posted notices that the strike was illegal, which it wasn't, but paper beats people when the paper's got lawyers behind it.

Hazel read fast, skimming, dreading. The entries compressed. July: the company cut the radio line. August: the supply barge stopped coming, and there were two hundred men on the headwater and winter coming early. September: the men voted to walk out downriver, and Vane's foreman — a name Eben had underlined three times, *Reeve, A.*, and Hazel's stomach turned over, because Callum Reeve had a father too — locked the armory and told them the trail was washed out.

*October 3rd.* The entry was short. Eben's hand shook so badly some words were illegible. The men had gathered at the pool by the spring to draw lots for who would go for help. There was a fire. There was blasting powder, misfiled, or not misfiled. Eben wrote the same sentence twice, as if once weren't survivable: *It was not an accident. I saw the fuse. It was laid.*

Two hundred and six graves. Hazel looked up from the page at the rows of numbered crosses going gold in the last light, and understood, finally, why a man had spent thirty years alone above a waterfall.

"He didn't hide the journals," Ruth said quietly. She'd come back and was standing over her, reading upside down, which apparently was a skill you acquired growing up around surveyors. "He *sealed* them. There's a difference. Hiding is hoping nobody finds them. Sealing is waiting for somebody who will." She swallowed. "Me. He was waiting for me. Every one of those letters he sent — 'come see me, Ruthie, come up the river' — he wasn't asking for forgiveness. He was asking for a witness."

Hazel closed the journal gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

"Then we'd better be good ones."

* * *

They made camp in the schoolhouse, because it had walls and because neither of them could have slept among the graves. Hazel built a small fire in the old stove, more for the sound than the heat, and they read the journals by turns, passing them back and forth like communion, and somewhere past midnight Ruth laughed — a cracked, wet, astonished laugh — and showed Hazel an entry from 1974:

*Bought a camera today to photograph the markers before the moss takes the numbers. Realized I don't know how to send photographs to a daughter who won't write back. Bought film anyway. Hope is cheaper than sense and lasts longer.*

"That idiot," Ruth said, wiping her eyes. "That stubborn, sentimental idiot. He photographed all of them. Two hundred and six markers, year after year, in case the wood rotted." She dug into her pack and pulled out the bundle of letters she'd been carrying since Fort Meridian, unopened, and set them on her knee, and this time she didn't put them away again.

Hazel was about to say something — she wasn't sure what, maybe just *that's the whole job, isn't it, carrying things for people who can't carry them themselves* — when Ruth's hand closed on her wrist, hard.

"Lantern," Ruth breathed. "Down the portage trail. Two of them."

Hazel killed the stove. In the sudden dark they heard it clearly: boots on the ridge trail, unhurried, confident, the pace of a man who knew exactly where he was going because he'd been going there his whole life.

The voice that came out of the night was mild and almost friendly, and it was worse for that.

"Miss Okonkwo." Callum Reeve, forty yards out, beyond the firelight. "And Miss Crowe. Your father and I never met, but I've read his file. All of it." A pause, the creak of a rifle sling. "You're standing in the middle of the only thing in this world my employer is afraid of. I'd take it as a personal kindness if you'd hand those tins out here, and then we can all go home and forget this valley has a name."

Hazel looked at Ruth. Ruth looked at Hazel. And in the dark between them passed one of those conversations that doesn't need words — the same one, Hazel realized, that Eben Crowe must have had with himself thirty years ago at this exact spot, alone, with the whole weight of the truth in his hands and nowhere safe to put it down.

"The lakehead," Hazel whispered. "The plane. If we can reach the plane—"

"They'll be watching the lakehead," Ruth whispered back.

"Then we'd better be creative."

She stood up, hands visible, and pitched her voice toward the dark with all the easy cheer of a woman who talked to strangers for a living. "Mr. Reeve! Funny thing — I've flown your boss's survey crews for two seasons and nobody ever mentioned there was a town up here. You want these journals? Come and get them. But fair warning—" she kicked the stove door wide, and the coals flared, and in that heartbeat of light she saw Reeve's silhouette shift, commit, start forward — "they're heavy, and I read fast!"

They went out the back wall of the schoolhouse, through the gap where a window had been, into the poplars, journals strapped tight, running blind down the valley toward the falls and the river and the long desperate road home — while behind them, calm as Sunday, Callum Reeve's flashlight beam swept the graves, counting.

The map had never been about treasure. Hazel had known it since the trapper's cabin, really, and had let herself pretend otherwise, because pretending was easier than believing a stranger would hand you something this heavy.

Now she believed it. Now she carried it.

The lakehead was two days' hard travel north, and her plane was the only door out of this valley — and somewhere between here and there, Silas Vane's men were already choosing where to stand.
