## 8. Reeve's River

The first thing Ruth learned about running white water at night was that it was a terrible idea, and the second thing she learned was that they were going to do it anyway.

"They've got lights," Hazel said, crouched at the lip of the gorge where the Kestrel gathered itself and dropped into the dark. Below them, maybe two kilometers downriver, flashlights swept the water like search beams at a prison break. "We don't. So we let the river do the seeing for us."

"The river doesn't see. The river *drowns*."

"Same thing, if you squint." Hazel was already pushing the canoe toward the current, her voice light in that way Ruth had come to understand meant she was scared out of her mind and refusing to admit it. "Eben ran this stretch every spring for thirty years. His caches are along here somewhere. If he could do it drunk on spruce beer and optimism, we can do it sober and terrified."

"That's not the comfort you think it is."

"It wasn't supposed to be comfort. It was supposed to be true." She grinned, and even in the dark Ruth could feel it — that crooked, reckless grin that had no business being reassuring and somehow was. "Get in the boat, Crowe."

Ruth got in the boat.

* * *

Her father had taught her to read water when she was nine years old, back when he still came home in winters, back before the letters started arriving instead of him. *Don't look at the rocks,* he'd said, standing hip-deep in some northern river with her small hand wrapped in his large one. *Look at where the water wants to go. It always knows. Your job is just to agree with it.*

She'd forgotten that for twenty years. Now, with the canoe hissing through black water and Reeve's lights blooming behind them like angry stars, she remembered everything.

"V-ledge coming up left!" she shouted over the roar. "Hard right, then forgive yourself!"

Hazel forgave herself. The canoe slewed, kissed the ledge with a scrape that Ruth felt in her teeth, and shot through the gap beyond into a long churning trough of a wave train. Water came aboard in cold, personal quantities.

"You've done this before," Hazel yelled, hauling on her paddle.

"I've done this at *noon!*"

"Noon's just night with better lighting!"

They ran three rapids that way, by memory and argument, Ruth calling the lines from a childhood she'd spent pretending hadn't mattered, Hazel throwing the canoe around like it weighed nothing. Behind them the flashlights fell further back, stymied by the same water, unwilling to run what the women were running. Once, distantly, Ruth heard a motor cough and die, and she laughed — actually laughed, breathless and wild, in the middle of a rapid that could kill them both.

"What?" Hazel shouted.

"He'd have loved this! My father! He'd have hated *us* for doing it, and loved it!"

"Sounds about right for Eben Crowe!"

Then the gorge narrowed, and the river stopped being a river and became a decision the river had already made without consulting anyone, and there was no more talking after that.

* * *

She didn't remember the flip. That was the strange part. There was a horizon of foam, a wall of rock wearing moss like a beard, a moment of absolute silence inside the noise — and then cold closed over her like a fist and the world became tumbling, directionless green-black. Her father's voice arrived unbidden: *If you go under, curl up. Let it spit you out. Fighting the water is how it wins.* She curled. The river rolled her like laundry, bruised her against something soft — gravel, blessed gravel — and spat her onto a shoal in the shallows, coughing, alive, alone.

Not alone. Thirty meters downstream, Hazel stood waist-deep holding the overturned canoe against the current with both hands and an expression of profound betrayal.

"We lost the packs," Hazel called.

"All of them?"

"Define 'all.'"

Ruth took inventory as she waded over: the journals were lashed inside Hazel's jacket, waterproofed twice because Hazel was paranoid in the specific way of people who fly small planes over big country. Everything else — food, tent, the rifle, the satellite phone with its cracked screen and its stubborn refusal to find a signal — was somewhere between here and the escarpment, or in the bottom of the gorge, or in the general custody of the Kestrel River.

"So," Ruth said, teeth chattering. "Plan B."

"There's no Plan B."

"Then Plan A continues, but colder and hungrier."

Hazel righted the canoe and looked at her across the black water, and Ruth saw something shift in her face — the humor draining out and leaving underneath it something steadier than courage. "There are caches," Hazel said quietly. "Eben's. He told me about them, that last day. He said if I ever needed anything on the Kestrel, the river would provide, because he'd made sure of it. He said—" she hesitated, and Ruth braced herself, because she knew what was coming, had known since the cabin, since the map drawn in grease pencil by a dying man's shaking hand. "He said his daughter should know where they all were. He said he'd written it all down for her. Every year."

The gorge held its breath around them.

"He wrote to me," Ruth said. "Every Christmas. I never opened most of them. They're in a shoebox in Fort Meridian under my winter boots."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because he told me that too." Hazel pushed the canoe toward the bank. "He said, *she keeps them under her boots so she has to move them every morning and think of me whether she wants to or not.*" A pause. "He laughed when he said it. Not a mean laugh."

Ruth turned away and busied herself with the painter rope, which did not need busy-ing. The river went on talking its endless gravel talk, and somewhere upstream, flashlights were still hunting them, and none of it mattered as much as the image of her father laughing — not a mean laugh — while describing the shoebox he'd never seen but had understood completely, from four hundred miles away, for twenty years.

* * *

They found the first cache at dawn, exactly where the map in Ruth's head — assembled now from fragments her father had pressed into Hazel's hands — said it would be: beneath a split boulder shaped like a sleeping bear, on the north bank where a creek slid cold and clear into the main channel. The tin was rusted at the corners but sealed. Inside: a foil blanket, a brick of pemmican, matches in a waxed jar, fishing line and hooks, a candle stub.

And an envelope.

Ruth sat on the boulder with the envelope in her hands while Hazel built a fire with the competence of someone who considered fire a solved problem. The envelope was soft with age, addressed in her father's blocky surveyor's print: *RUTH. CACHE 4. IF YOU'RE READING THIS YOU'RE EITHER LOST OR STUBBORN. PROBABLY BOTH. IT'S THE CROWE WAY.*

"When did he write these?" she asked.

Hazel fed a branch to the flames. "Cache 4? He redid the tins every fall. Said maintaining them was his religion." She glanced over, careful, kind. "You don't have to read it now."

"I know I don't." She opened it anyway.

*Ruthie. The fish below Cache 4 hold in the eddy on the left side, behind the rock with the orange lichen. They'll take a spinner if you work it slow. I caught my first trout there when I was eleven and I thought about you the whole time, which was strange, because you weren't born yet. I suppose I was practicing.*

*I know why you don't write back. Your mother told me once, before she stopped telling me things. You think the river took me from you. And it did, in a way. But Ruthie — the river is also the only place I know how to be a man worth writing to. Out here I'm patient. Out here I'm brave. In Fort Meridian I was neither. I kept hoping you'd come up and meet the version of me that lived here. I kept hoping until hoping got embarrassing, and then I kept doing it anyway, because that's the Crowe way too.*

*Eat the pemmican. It's better than it smells. Nothing is worse than it smells, actually, but it will keep you walking.*

*Love, Dad.*

Ruth read it twice. Then she folded it along its old creases, put it inside her jacket next to the journals, and ate the pemmican, which was indeed worse than it smelled.

"Well?" Hazel asked gently.

"He says the fish hold in the left eddy." Ruth wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike. "And that I'm stubborn."

"He wasn't wrong about either."

"No," Ruth said, and found she was smiling, wet-eyed and ridiculous in the dawn light. "He wasn't wrong about either."

* * *

They moved downriver through the day, off the water whenever the sound of motors carried, on it when the current ran quiet. They found Cache 6 at midday — more food, a coil of rope, another letter, shorter, funnier, an account of a wolverine that had raided Cache 5 and the elaborate revenge Eben had plotted against it ("I have named him Gerald and declared total war"). By late afternoon they found Cache 7, and Ruth had stopped reading the letters like artifacts and started reading them like conversation, answering aloud, arguing points, catching Hazel watching her with an expression she pretended not to notice.

It was Hazel who heard the motors first, though. She always heard them first.

"Not Reeve," she said, flat on the bank with her ear to the ground like some kind of bush pilot trapper hybrid. "Bigger. Slower. That's a barge engine."

Ruth felt it then too, a deep subsonic pulse through the bedrock, upstream and closing. "Coming downriver?"

"Coming *down*. Which means Vane's not waiting for the permits anymore. He's moving." Hazel was already on her feet, scanning the sky out of habit, and Ruth watched the color change in her face as she understood it at the same moment Ruth did. "My plane's at the lakehead. It's been at the lakehead this whole time, sitting on his route like a—"

"Like a witness," Ruth said.

The barge pulse grew. Somewhere above them, Callum Reeve was still in the woods with his flashlights and his patience, and below them the river ran on toward the lakehead where a yellow de Havilland sat alone on the water, and Ruth's father's letters rustled softly against her ribs when she breathed.

"Hazel," she said slowly. "How fast can this canoe go if we stop caring about the canoe?"
