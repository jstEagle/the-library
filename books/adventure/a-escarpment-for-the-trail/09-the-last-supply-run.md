## 9. The Last Supply Run

The lakehead smelled of diesel and spruce rot, the way it had smelled for thirty years, and Hazel stood on the gravel shore with her father's old binoculars pressed to her eyes and watched the barge come up the narrows like a slow, arrogant thought.

*Vane Consolidated No. 3*, the letters on her bow said, though the paint had faded so the name read more honestly as *ANE O OLI*. She was pushing a heavy wake, riding low with fuel drums and blasting supplies and, Hazel would have wagered her last good spark plug, enough men with bad attitudes to staff a small war.

"She's early," Ruth said. She was crouched beside the canoe, repacking it with the economy of someone who had learned to pack light the hard way. The journals were wrapped in oilcloth inside her pack, pressed against her spine like a second heartbeat. "I thought we had until tomorrow."

"We had until tomorrow," Hazel agreed. "He's moved it up because he thinks we're dead in a gorge somewhere, and he wants to burn the evidence before the permits lapse in—" she checked her watch, an affectation up here where the sun barely set, "—thirty-one hours."

"And we're the only ones who know."

"We're the only ones who *care*," Hazel said. "Which is worse."

The journals sat between them like a sleeping animal. Forty years of Eben Crowe's careful hand. Names. Dates. Photographs of the settlement above the falls, of the graves with no markers, of the drill cores pulled from ground that had never been properly surveyed because the men who'd worked it in 1971 had been worked to death and buried under the tailings. Hazel had read enough of it on the portage to feel it in her teeth. Ruth had read all of it, and had come out the other side with a face like winter granite.

"So we take the canoe," Ruth said. "We go down the Kestrel, we hit the telegraph station at Two Mile, we wire the tribunal. Three days, maybe four. Reeve's crew is scattered upriver looking for us. We might make it."

"Might," Hazel said.

"You have a better idea."

Hazel lowered the binoculars. The barge was maybe four miles out, crawling up against the current, and behind it the lake lay flat and pewter-colored all the way to the point where her plane sat moored at the old float dock — *the* plane, the de Havilland, twenty-six years of dents and patches and one memorable incident involving a moose that Hazel did not discuss in polite company. Her whole livelihood. Her whole identity, if she was honest, which she tried not to be before breakfast.

"I have an idea," she said. "You're going to hate it."

"I already hate it," Ruth said, "and you haven't said it yet. That's usually how it goes with you."

* * *

Here was the arithmetic, and Hazel laid it out the way she'd once laid it out for a nervous passenger over the Mackenzie flats: gently, and with the numbers visible.

Vane was coming to destroy the evidence. The evidence was in Ruth's pack. Therefore the evidence was safe as long as Ruth got out. But Reeve had a radio, and Vane's barge had a radio, and the moment anyone at the lakehead saw a canoe heading downriver they'd have a boat on the water in ten minutes, and the Kestrel below the lake was wide and slow and about as forgiving as a tax auditor.

"Unless," Hazel said, "nobody's watching the river."

"Because?"

"Because they're watching the *fire*."

Ruth looked at her. Hazel watched her work it through — the fuel drums on the barge, the depot shed on the shore with its two thousand gallons of aviation gas, the deadline thirty-one hours out, the plane at the dock. Hazel watched the exact moment Ruth understood, and saw her jaw set against it.

"No," Ruth said.

"Ruth—"

"No. That plane is your whole life. You've told me — what was it you said? — 'the sky is the only place I've ever felt like I had a reservation.'" Ruth stood up, and she was taller than Hazel remembered her being, which was a trick grief does. "You are not going to fly that plane into a fuel depot like some — some *movie*—"

"It's not a movie, it's a plan. There's a difference. In a movie nobody has to fill out the insurance paperwork."

"This isn't *funny*—"

"It's a little funny," Hazel said, and then, because Ruth's eyes had gone bright and furious in the way that meant she was two seconds from either punching something or crying, Hazel sat down on the gravel and told the truth, which was harder.

"I've been flying that plane for eleven years," she said. "And you know what I've been doing the whole time? Delivering things. Insulin, mail, gossip, engine parts, a wedding cake once — don't ask. I go everywhere and I belong nowhere. I land, I unload, I take off before anyone can ask me to stay. That's not a life, Ruth. That's a very complicated way of being alone."

The barge's engine carried across the water, a low industrial heartbeat.

"Then stay," Ruth said. "After this. Stay somewhere. With—" she made an impatient gesture that took in the whole north, the river, the journals, herself, "—with people. You don't have to burn your way out of your own life to prove a point."

"It's not a point. It's a *delivery*." Hazel smiled, and found to her surprise that it didn't hurt. "Eben gave me a map and a promise. The map led to the journals. The journals go to the tribunal. And Vane's whole empire rests on those permits not lapsing with the evidence still in the ground. So: the barge doesn't make it back downriver in time. The depot doesn't fuel the crews. The drill site stands untouched past the deadline, with the graves in it, until the investigators arrive. That's the delivery. That's the last freight run. I've never once delivered something this important, and I'd like to do it properly."

"And you?"

"I'll walk out with you," Hazel said. "Down the escarpment trail Eben marked on his map. Two days. I've survived worse — I once spent four days in Fort Reliance during a fuel strike. You have not lived until you've watched three trappers fight over the last tin of peaches."

Ruth stared at her for a long moment. The barge was three miles out now. Somewhere behind it, faint as a memory, Hazel could hear the float plane's mooring line creak against the dock.

"You'll die," Ruth said quietly. "Or you'll crash and live and be crippled and wish you'd died."

"Probably not," Hazel said. "I've been practicing landings my whole life. This one just has a more exciting runway." She stood and brushed the gravel off her knees. "Besides. If I don't come out, you'll write me up in your testimony as a hero, and I'll never have to hear what you actually say about me at dinner parties."

"You don't go to dinner parties."

"Exactly. My reputation remains pristine."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had three moving parts, which Hazel considered two more than strictly wise.

Part one: Ruth took the canoe and the journals up the eastern shore, hugging the reeds, heading for the old portage trail to the escarpment descent. If the fire worked, nobody would be looking at the water. If it didn't work, Ruth was on her own, and the journals were on their own, and Hazel's part in history would be a brief footnote involving an unexplained aviation accident.

Part two: Hazel flew. Not at the barge — the barge was moving, and moving things were a pilot's vanity, and she was not a vain woman about anything except her landings. The depot shed, though. The depot shed sat on the shore two hundred yards from the barge's mooring point, and it was full, and it was going to be very busy in about four minutes.

Part three: there was no part three. Part three was walking out with Ruth, and it only existed if the universe was feeling generous.

They stood at the canoe a last moment. Ruth put her hand flat against Hazel's shoulder — not a hug, Ruth Crowe did not hug, but the hand was warm and it stayed.

"Eben waited his whole life for someone who wouldn't quit," Ruth said. "He found you. I found you. Don't make me regret the family resemblance."

"I make no promises," Hazel said. "I'm told I'm very hard to regret for long."

Then Ruth was in the canoe and sliding into the reeds, and Hazel was walking alone up the gravel to the float dock, and the plane was waiting for her the way it always waited, patient and dented and hers.

She ran her hand along the cowling. "Old girl," she said. "I'm sorry. I'd have retired you to a museum if I'd had the sense God gives a canoe."

The de Havilland said nothing. Planes never did. That was one of the reasons she'd loved them.

* * *

She took off into the evening calm, wheels up off the floats with the lake glassing away beneath her, and for ninety seconds she let herself fly the way she'd always flown — lazy, loving, banking over the narrows to look at the moose drinking at the delta like she had a thousand times before and would never do again.

Then she turned.

The barge was half a mile from shore. She could see men on the deck, and a crane swinging fuel drums, and at the rail a figure in a long coat who could only be Reeve, looking up, shading his eyes. She imagined him recognizing the plane. She imagined the radio crackling. She imagined Vane, wherever he was, hearing the words *it's the pilot* and understanding, in the last minutes of his empire, exactly what was coming.

"Special delivery," Hazel said.

The depot shed grew in her windscreen, neat and gray and full of everything Vane Consolidated needed to keep operating past the deadline. She put the nose down. The airspeed climbed. The lake came up to meet her, and the shed, and the last eleven years of her life, and she found — this was the strange part, the part she would carry out of the fire and down the escarpment and into every testimony afterward — that she was not afraid. She was *home*. She had spent her whole life belonging to the sky, and the sky had never once asked her to stay, and now, at two hundred and ten miles an hour, four feet off the water, she understood that belonging had never been a place at all. It was a promise kept. It was a map delivered. It was a woman in a canoe in the reeds, counting on her.

The shed filled the world.

* * *

The explosion lifted a column of fire that could be seen, people said later, from the Two Mile telegraph station, forty miles downriver.

Hazel came to on her back in the shallows, thirty yards from the burning wreck of the shed, her ears ringing like a church with opinions. The plane was gone — a scatter of burning debris across the water, twenty-six years of dents and patches turned to sparks. The barge, moored too close, was ablaze from bow to stern, her crew scrambling into lifeboats, her cargo of fuel drums going off one after another like a very rude fireworks display. Somewhere in the smoke, men were shouting about the deadline, and the radio, and the fact that there was no longer enough fuel in the whole concession to move a single drill crew, and that the permits lapsed in thirty hours.

Hazel floated. The water was cold. Her left arm was burned and her ribs felt renegotiated and she had never in her life been so completely, so *specifically* alive.

A canoe slid out of the reeds. Ruth knelt in the bow, paddling like her arms were on a deadline of their own, and her face when she saw Hazel sitting up in the shallows did something Hazel had never seen it do — it came apart entirely, into relief and fury and something underneath both that Hazel decided, right then, to spend a long time learning the name of.

"You *idiot*," Ruth said, hauling her over the gunwale. "You magnificent, impossible *idiot*."

"Package delivered," Hazel croaked.

Ruth pulled her down into the bottom of the canoe, wrapped the spare shirt around her burned arm, and put the paddle in the water. Behind them the fire climbed into the dark, and the deadline ticked down, and ahead of them the escarpment trail waited — two days of walking, one set of journals, and the whole truth of the Kestrel River carried between them like a lantern.

"Can you walk?" Ruth asked.

Hazel tested her legs. They answered, mostly.

"Ask me tomorrow," she said. "Tonight, just paddle."
