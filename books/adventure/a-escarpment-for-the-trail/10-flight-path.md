## 10. Flight Path

The letter came on a Tuesday, which Hazel thought was fitting. Tuesdays were the day nothing ever happened, the day the mail plane ran late and the coffee went stale and the world agreed to be ordinary.

She was sitting in the office of the *Fort Meridian Courier* — a newspaper that consisted of one room, one press older than she was, and a man named Aldous Fenn who smelled of ink and pipe smoke — when the envelope arrived. It had been forwarded three times, corner-stamped by post offices in towns Hazel had never landed in, and it bore the letterhead of the Provincial Commission of Inquiry into Resource Concessions.

"Read it out loud," said Aldous, who had been reading over her shoulder anyway. "I like hearing good news in a human voice."

Hazel cleared her throat. "'Dear Ms. Okonkwo: The Commission hereby confirms receipt of documentary evidence, including field journals attributed to Eben Crowe, pertaining to events at Kestrel Headwater, 1971. All permits held by Vane Consolidated Mining Ltd. are suspended effective immediately pending full investigation—'"

She stopped there, because her voice had gone strange in her throat, and because Aldous had begun to laugh — a great wheezing, delighted laugh, the laugh of a man who had spent forty years printing small news and had just watched something enormous land on his desk.

"Suspended," he repeated, wiping his eyes. "Thirty years of permits, suspended by a paragraph. You know what I'm going to run above the fold?"

"A correction," Hazel said. "You misspelled my name in March."

"I'll run two corrections." He tapped the journal lying on his desk between them — Eben Crowe's handwriting marching across grease-stained pages, patient as sediment. "This is going to the tribunal in Meridian City. Copies everywhere. I've got a journalist coming up from the coast — a real one, with a photographer. They want to interview you."

"No."

"They expected you might say that."

"They'd be right."

Aldous studied her for a moment over his spectacles. "You know," he said, "most people spend their whole lives trying to get their name in print. You crashed your own airplane into a fuel depot rather than let a bad man keep his, and now you won't even take credit for it."

"The plane did the crashing," Hazel said. "I just aimed it."

She left before he could argue, and stood a while on the boardwalk outside, looking down the length of Fort Meridian's single street to where the river glinted between the warehouses. Her de Havilland was still at the bottom of the lakehead, thirty miles upstream, folded into the wreckage of Silas Vane's last supply barge like a bird that had finally decided where it wanted to land. The insurance company had sent two letters, both increasingly terse. She hadn't answered either.

It should have hurt more than it did. That was the strange part. For eleven years that airplane had been her address, her country, her only fixed point in a life that otherwise drifted from lakehead to landing strip to borrowed bunk. She had loved it the way you love a thing that never asks you to stay. And now it was gone, and she was grounded, and instead of grief she felt — she searched for the word while the river light flickered under the boards — *unburdened*.

"Hazel!"

Ruth Crowe came up the street at her usual pace, which was somewhere between a walk and an argument. She was carrying a canvas mail satchel and wearing the expression of a woman who had won something and wasn't sure yet whether to gloat.

"You're not going to believe what the tribunal did this morning," Ruth said.

"Revoked everything."

Ruth stopped dead. "How do you *know* that? I've been standing outside the telegraph office since six."

"Aldous got the letter first. He's insufferable about it." Hazel nodded toward the newspaper office, from which sounds of triumphant typesetting were already emerging. "Suspended pending investigation. Every permit. The whole rotten concession."

For a moment Ruth just looked at her, and then she laughed — a short, startled sound, as if laughter were a language she was relearning. Then she sat down right there on the boardwalk, satchel and all, and put her face in her hands, and her shoulders shook in a way that had nothing to do with laughing.

Hazel sat down beside her. Below them the river went on doing what rivers do, which is everything, always.

"He wrote to me every year," Ruth said at last, into her hands. "Nineteen letters. I found them all, in those caches, and I read every one, and I never — I can't write back, Hazel. There's no address for it anymore."

"There's the tribunal," Hazel said. "They'll enter them as evidence. His words will be read aloud in a courtroom full of important people. Every year he wrote, someone official will hear it." She shrugged. "Seems like answering, after a fashion."

Ruth lifted her head. Her eyes were red but steady. "When did you get wise?"

"About an hour ago. It wears off fast. Enjoy it."

* * *

The hearing in Meridian City took nine days, and Hazel attended exactly one of them.

She told herself it was duty — someone had to identify the journals, confirm the chain of custody, answer the questions lawyers ask in voices like syrup. But the truth was simpler and stranger: she wanted to see Silas Vane's face.

He sat at the respondent's table in a suit worth more than her airplane had been, and he did not look like a ruined man. He looked like a man doing arithmetic — running columns, moving assets behind his eyes, already halfway to whatever came next. When the counsel for the Commission read aloud from Eben Crowe's journal — *"They brought the strikers to the headwater camp in trucks and told them they were being relocated. The river took four days to run clear again"* — Vane's expression did not change at all.

That was the thing Hazel carried out of the courtroom, the thing she turned over for weeks afterward: not rage, not fear. Just arithmetic. A man who had never once, in thirty years, thought of the Kestrel as anything but a column of numbers.

Outside on the courthouse steps, the coastal journalist caught up with her — a brisk woman named Okafor with a notebook full of questions.

"One quote," Okafor said. "That's all I need. Why did you do it? The crash. Most people would have flown home."

Hazel considered the question honestly, which took longer than expected. Down the street, traffic moved; somewhere a trolley bell rang; the city conducted its enormous indifferent business.

"When I was learning to fly," she said finally, "my instructor told me that in an emergency, you don't pick the option that saves the aircraft. You pick the one that saves the pilot. And I spent eleven years thinking the aircraft was the part of me worth saving." She buttoned her coat. "Turns out it wasn't."

Okafor wrote it down verbatim. It ran on the front page under a headline Hazel refused to read, though Ruth read it aloud to her twice, with relish, doing the announcer voice and everything.

* * *

Spring came up the valley the way it always does in the interior — all at once, apologetically, as if it had meant to arrive weeks ago and gotten lost in the fog. The ice went out of the Kestrel with a sound like distant artillery, and with the ice went the last excuse for staying idle.

Hazel was broke. This was a fact she wore lightly, the way she wore everything, but it was a fact: no plane, no contract, an insurance dispute she would likely lose, and a reputation in certain corporate offices as a woman best hired never. She spent April repairing other people's engines and sleeping in the loft above Aldous's print shop, and telling anyone who asked that she was fine, which fooled nobody, least of all herself.

Then, on the first of May, Ruth appeared at the foot of the print shop stairs holding two canoe paddles and a piece of paper.

"River mail route," Ruth said. "Kestrel Headwater to Fort Meridian, twelve stops, government contract. The old runner retired. They need somebody who knows the water." She held out the paper. "They need two somebodies, actually. One to row and one to talk. I applied for us both. You're welcome."

Hazel looked at the paddles. Then at the paper. Then at Ruth, whose ears had gone faintly pink, which Hazel had learned was what happened when Ruth Crowe did something brave and tried to pretend she hadn't.

"You applied for *us*," Hazel said. "Plural."

"It's a two-person route. Regulations." Ruth's chin came up. "Also I don't want to do it alone. Also if you say no I've already told the ministry you said yes, so."

"Well," said Hazel, "in that case."

She took the paddle. It was a good paddle — birch, hand-gripped, the varnish worn pale where a previous owner's hands had held it for years. She turned it over and found initials burned into the shaft near the throat: *E.C.*

Ruth watched her notice. "Dad's spares. They were in the cache with the letters." She shrugged, elaborately casual. "Figured they should work for a living again. He'd have hated them hanging on a wall."

Hazel wrapped her hand around the grip, where another hand had worn it smooth, and found that it fit as if it had been waiting for her.

* * *

The first run of the season began in rain, because of course it did. They loaded the canoe at dawn — mail sacks waterproofed in oilcloth, a crate of vaccine for the mission at Second Bend, a replacement part for the gristmill, forty pounds of flour, and one carefully wrapped parcel of newspapers for the settlements upstream, because Aldous had insisted the people of the Kestrel deserved to read about the tribunal in ink, on paper, the way civilization intended.

Twelve stops. Twelve landings where somebody was waiting — a trapper's wife who fed them bannock and gossip, two old brothers who argued over a misdelivered parcel from 1988, a schoolteacher at Third Narrows who made Ruth stay an hour to talk books. At every stop, people asked about the hearings. At every stop, Ruth told the story straight and plain: the journals, the graves at the headwater, the permits revoked. And at every stop Hazel watched the news move through the crowd like warmth through cold hands.

On the fifth day, running the long flat water below the escarpment, they passed the place where the de Havilland lay. The lake was high with meltwater and perfectly still, and far down in the green you could just make out the shape of the barge, and the smaller shape tangled with it. Hazel rested her paddle across the gunwales and looked for a while.

"Do you regret it?" Ruth asked. "The plane?"

"Every day about four o'clock," Hazel said. "Right around tea. Then it passes." She dipped the paddle. "Do you regret the map? If you'd known what it cost — the running, the fire, Reeve — would you have stayed home?"

Ruth didn't answer right away. She shipped her paddle and let the canoe drift, and looked up at the escarpment wall rising ahead of them, grey and enormous, wearing its spring green like a scarf it had just remembered.

"I regret nineteen unanswered letters," she said. "I don't regret a single rapid since." She glanced sideways. "Besides. My father drew that map to bring me here. Took him dying to manage it, but we got here. Both of us. I think he'd call that delivered."

The current picked up where the lake narrowed into river, gentle but insistent, the way the Kestrel always was — a hand on the small of your back. Hazel set her blade in the water, Eben Crowe's initials under her palm, and pulled.

Ahead, around the first bend, someone was standing on the gravel bar at Stop Six with a sack of outgoing mail and, judging by the way they were waving both arms, news of their own. Ruth laughed and dug deep. Hazel matched her stroke for stroke, and the river carried them forward, twelve stops and a whole summer stretching out ahead like a road drawn by a careful hand —

— and on the wind off the escarpment, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a floatplane engine, descending toward the headwater where no plane had any permit left to fly.
