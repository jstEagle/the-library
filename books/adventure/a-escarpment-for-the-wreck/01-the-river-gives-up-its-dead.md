## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The Greywater gave up its dead the way it gave up everything: grudgingly, and only after it had finished with them.

Emrys Nystrom had learned that about the river in his first week on the salvage line, three years ago now, back when he still believed a man could work his way out of a name. The flood came down from the Karst Escarpment every spring, brown and muscular, tearing fences out of the valley farms and carrying whole barns downstream like toys a giant had grown bored of. And when it receded—slowly, sulkily, over weeks—it left its leavings strung along the banks like the contents of a pocket turned out by a thief: fence posts, door frames, drowned sheep swollen to twice their size, once an entire chapel bell that the village of Lowmarsh paid forty crowns to have hauled out and rehung.

The dead, too, sometimes. Not often. But sometimes.

He stood hip-deep in the shallows at dawn with a hooked pole in his hands and his boots leaking, watching the water drop another hand's width overnight. The line stretched along the gravel bar below the old mill weir—six men and a boy, all of them working for Vetch, who owned the barge and the winch and took half of whatever came up. The morning was grey as the river's name, mist sitting on the water in flat sheets, and Emrys's hands were already cold to the wrist.

"Anything?" called Doss—no, not Doss, that was another life; this one was called Pell, a broad slow man with a beard full of last night's supper.

"Boot," Emrys said, and held it up. Left foot, good leather, no owner. He tossed it onto the pile.

Pell laughed. "That's your fortune, Nystrom. A boot."

It was, at least, more than yesterday's fortune. Yesterday he'd pulled up a rotted door and half a henhouse, and Vetch had looked at him the way Vetch always looked at him, which was the way a man looks at a tool that has begun to rust. Three years on the line and Emrys was still the newest hand, still the one given the worst stretch of bank, still the one whose pay came late and short and without apology. He knew why. They all knew why. *Nystrom.* The name had traveled ahead of him down two countries and across the escarpment like a smell you couldn't wash out of wool.

*Kestrel Pass,* they said, when they said anything at all. *The guide who walked nine clients into a whiteout and came down alone.*

Seven clients. It had been seven. But nine made a better story, and stories were the only currency the lowlands minted reliably.

He worked the pole along the submerged fence line where the current eddied behind the weir stones, feeling through the ash handle for the particular drag of something heavier than driftwood. That was the craft of it—the part nobody thought about, the part that separated a salvager from a man poking sticks in mud. Every object in the river had its own voice through the pole. A log rolled and knocked, hollow and impatient. A carcass gave soft, clinging resistance that made your stomach tighten even after three years. Metal sang—a thin bright shiver up the shaft that you felt in your teeth before you heard anything at all.

Near midmorning, in the slack water under the collapsed mill race, the pole sang.

Emrys stopped. Shifted his grip. Probed again, slower, letting the hook walk along the thing's edge. Flat. Broad. Edged in metal, but not sheet metal—not the tin of a stove or the skin of a boat. This was dense, deliberate, worked. And beneath the shiver of the trim there was a deeper resistance, a deadness, the feel of a thing that did not want to move.

"Vetch," he called. "Something big under the race."

Vetch was up on the barge deck nursing his coffee and his grievances in equal measure. "Big how?"

"Box big. Iron-bound." Emrys leaned into the pole, testing. It didn't give. Whatever lay under the tangle of the race timbers had settled there with intent, or with weight enough to make intent irrelevant. "Maybe a strongbox off one of the packet boats."

"Packet boats don't run iron boxes," Pell said, wading closer. He was right, and they both knew it. The Greywater packets carried wool and salt and pilgrims going up to the shrines, and none of them were rich enough to lose treasure.

Vetch spat over the rail. "If it takes more than an hour to raise, it's mine entire. Line rules."

Line rules. Of course. Emrys nodded and didn't argue, because arguing with Vetch was like arguing with weather, and because some stubborn coal at the bottom of him had already begun to glow. An hour. He could raise almost anything in an hour if the hour was honest.

It took four.

The problem wasn't the box itself. The problem was the mill race, which had folded down across it in the flood like a broken wing, black timbers pinned under stone and silt, and every timber they shifted brought down more of the crumbling race wall. Twice Emrys went fully under, feeling along the box's edge in water so cold it burned, finding the iron bands, counting them by touch. Four bands. Corner fittings of heavier stock. And between the bands, when his knuckles rapped against it, not wood and not iron but something dense and dull and wrong—a sound like striking a coffin lid.

Lead. He knew lead. His father had lined ammunition boxes with it, back when Emrys was small enough to sit inside one and pretend it was a fort. Lead meant one of two things: something valuable enough to protect, or something dangerous enough to contain.

By early afternoon they had the tackle rigged to the weir's surviving crane post, and the whole line—six men and a boy, drawn away from their own stretches by the smell of possible money—stood in the shallows hauling while Vetch shouted contradictory instructions from the barge. The box came up out of the river the way drowned things come up: reluctantly, shedding water in sheets, streaming weed, turning slowly to show its face to the light for the first time in however long the river had kept it.

Nobody spoke.

It was a chest—trunk-sized, longer than wide, built of some dark close-grained wood gone black with soaking—and it was bound in iron and faced in lead, and every surface of it that wasn't running with grey water was stamped with a seal pressed into the corner plates. A bird. Two-headed, wings spread, crowned, rendered in a style too fine for cargo marking and too proud for decoration.

Emrys knew the bird. Everyone past forty knew the bird, and everyone younger knew it from the one place it still appeared: on the faded proclamations nailed inside courthouse porches, the ones announcing the annexation, printed the year Vhaloria stopped being on the maps. Forty years gone. Whole valleys, whole ranges, a nation the size of a thumbprint on the world's hand—absorbed, renamed, redistricted until the word *Vhaloria* survived mostly as a curse and a cautionary tale told to children about what happened to countries that trusted their neighbors.

"That's the double-eagle," the boy said. He was maybe fourteen, Vetch's sister's kid, and he said it the way you'd say *that's a ghost.*

"It's scrap is what it is," Vetch said, but his voice had gone careful. He came down off the barge and walked around the chest twice, hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a horse he suspected of biting. "Lead's worth something. Melt it down. Iron too. Wood's rotten, probably."

"It's not rotten," Emrys said. "Feel it. That's oak that's been under water since before you were born and it hasn't checked. Somebody built this to last."

"Somebody built it to sink, more like." Vetch crouched, squinting at the seal, and Emrys watched him recognize it and watch him decide not to say so. "Scrap," Vetch repeated. "Half shares, line rules, and I keep the fittings."

"The fittings are part of the lead work."

"The fittings are *brass*, Nystrom, and brass is mine, and if you want to stand there arguing about a dead country's luggage you can do it after you've helped me get it on the barge."

They got it on the barge. It took all six of them and the winch and nearly swamped the deck, and when it finally settled with a boom that Emrys felt through his boots, the water that ran off it ran clear for the first time—river-washed, patient, keeping its own counsel.

* * *

They beached the barge at dusk below Vetch's shed, and the others drifted home to their suppers, and Emrys stayed, because staying was free and leaving cost something he didn't have. Vetch set a lantern on a barrel and stood over the chest with a pry bar, and Emrys stood opposite him with another, and for a while neither of them moved. The river talked to itself among the pilings. Somewhere upstream a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

"You know what that seal is," Emrys said.

"I know what it's worth, which is nothing, which means we melt it and nobody hangs for it." Vetch set the pry bar against the lid. "You want first bite or not?"

"I want to know why a nation that got erased off the map was shipping lead-lined chests down a river nobody important sails."

"Because dead men can't answer questions, and neither can rivers." Vetch leaned into the bar. The lid groaned. He leaned harder, face going purple, and the hasp tore loose with a crack that scattered birds out of the willows.

Then he stopped. Stood there breathing hard, looking down into the open chest, and whatever he saw made him take a step back that he tried to disguise as a shift of footing.

"What," Emrys said.

"Get it yourself. Half shares." Vetch wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I've decided I'm not curious."

Which was, Emrys would think later, the most honest thing Vetch had ever said to him, and the most frightening.

He climbed onto the barge alone. The lantern light fell into the chest and lay there on top of what it held, and Emrys looked down at it for a long moment before he reached in, because some part of him—the part that had spent three years learning to read a river through a stick of ash—was already telling him that this was the moment. Not the raising. Not the opening. This one, the looking. Everything after this one was just consequences arriving on schedule.

Oilcloth bundles, tied with waxed cord, stacked with a precision that no flood could have produced and no flood had disturbed. Beneath them, wrapped in oilskin of its own, something small and hard. And tucked against the chest's inner wall, wedged upright as if someone had placed it there in a hurry and meant to come back, a document case of stiff leather, stamped with the same two-headed bird, sealed with a plug of wax the size of a plum.

He lifted the case out. It was lighter than it should have been, or his hands were shaking, or both. The wax was unbroken. Pressed into it, sharp as the day it was set, the double-eagle again—and around the eagle's rim, in lettering worn shallow but legible, words in old Vhalorian script that Emrys could not read and yet understood completely, the way you understand a locked door.

Behind him, Vetch cleared his throat. "Well?"

"Paper," Emrys said. "Sealed paper."

"Paper's worthless. Paper gets people asked questions." Vetch's eyes flicked toward the darkening road, then back. "Take your half of the lead and go, Nystrom. Take it tonight. And if anyone asks what came up under the race, it was a stove. Say stove. Stoves are boring."

"Why—"

"Because I've been on this river thirty years," Vetch said, "and I know what floats down out of those valleys when the floods run high, and it isn't stoves." He picked up his lantern. "Forty years ago they marched everybody out of Vhaloria who wouldn't swear the new oath, and the ones who swore got to stay and watch. You think a thing like that leaves nothing behind? You think the river doesn't remember where the bodies went?"

He walked off up the plank road, and his footsteps faded, and Emrys stood alone on the barge with the chest and the lantern and the sealed case in his hands.

* * *

He meant to leave it. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the world kept of men like him: *he meant to leave it.*

But the shed was dry, and the night was cold, and the case fit inside his coat, and the coat fit inside the lean-to behind Vetch's shed where Emrys had slept for the better part of a year on a pallet of sacking. He sat with his back against the wall and the lantern trimmed low and the case on his knees, and he told himself he was only looking at it. Examining the craftsmanship. Assessing value, the way a salvager assesses everything, because assessing value was the one skill nobody had managed to blacklist him for.

The wax plug came free easier than it should have. He'd tell himself later that it was already cracked, that the river had done it, that forty years of water had done the work—but his thumbnail was on it, and his thumbnail moved, and the seal parted with a small sound like a knuckle cracking, and there was no undoing it after that.

Inside: papers. Dozens of them, folded and refolded, some in the same old Vhalorian script, some in the trade-tongue of the republic that had swallowed the old country whole. Dispatches, by their headings. Military dates. Place names he half-knew from maps that had been redrawn since—valley names wearing their old spellings like clothes from a previous life.

And on top of the stack, separate, wrapped in a page torn from a ledger, a single sheet heavier than the rest. Heavy paper. Notarized—he recognized the ribbon-and-stamp format, the republic used it too, some bureaucratic bones never change. A writ. He sounded the word out silently, not daring to think it fully. A writ of succession, the heading said, in the trade-tongue, though the body of it slid back and forth between languages as if drafted by a clerk with one foot in each country.

He read it twice. Then a third time, slower, moving his lips, because the words refused to behave like words and started behaving like weather instead—something vast moving toward him that he could see coming and could not make stop.

*...the lawful succession devolves upon the child of Ilse Vane, sole surviving issue of the House of Vhalor, whose custody and claim are hereby committed to whomsoever shall recover these instruments...*

The child of Ilse Vane.

A child, forty years ago, would be a man or woman of thirty-nine now. If the child lived. If the child had ever lived, and not died unnamed in some ditch during the purges, or worse—died named, in a record somewhere, in a registry that somebody had kept.

Emrys sat very still. The lantern hissed. Outside, the Greywater went on talking to itself in the dark, patient as ever, giving nothing up for free.

Luck, he thought. This is what luck feels like when it's been away too long. It doesn't feel like luck. It feels like a trap with the door standing open, because you've forgotten what doors look like when they're not.

Three years of short pay and long silences. Seven dead on Kestrel Pass and his name nailed to every guild board from here to the coast. And now, on the one night in three years the river had handed him anything heavier than a boot, it had handed him a sealed claim that could topple a republic—or hang whoever held it, depending entirely on who read the heading first.

He should burn it. He actually got as far as holding the writ near the lantern, watching the flame lean toward the paper's edge, feeling the heat start to crawl.

Then he stopped. Because underneath the fear, honest as a stone in his boot, was the other thing. The hungry thing. The thing that had kept him walking north after the blacklisting, west after the second one, that had kept him taking Vetch's scraps and sleeping on sacking: the animal conviction that somewhere ahead of him was a door with his name on the other side of it, and that all he'd ever needed was one honest piece of luck to find the key.

This was either the key or the trap. He genuinely could not tell which. That, he suspected, was the whole design of it.

Emrys folded the writ along its original creases, returned it to the case, and pushed the wax fragments together in his palm like a man trying to reassemble an eggshell. The seal wouldn't seal again. Everyone who looked would know it had been opened, and everyone who looked would ask by whom.

He blew out the lantern and sat in the dark listening to the river, and it was only as his eyes adjusted that he noticed the last item he hadn't inventoried—the small hard thing wrapped in oilskin, still lying in the chest out on the barge, forgotten in the shake of everything else.

He went back for it. Of course he went back for it.

Unwrapped, it was a compass. Brass-cased, heavy, older than he was, the kind guides carried before liquid-damped faces came in. The glass was intact. The needle swung lazy and true. And around the case's rim, where a maker's stamp might sit, someone had pressed a seal into the brass—

The double-eagle. Small, worn, unmistakable.

Emrys turned it in the dark until the needle steadied, pointing north, pointing upriver, pointing at the escarpment and everything that had been taken from the valleys beyond it. His father had carried a compass like this once. Had carried it over passes that weren't on any map, in years when the borders were being drawn in blood, in the last years when a man could still walk from one country into another without asking anybody's leave.

His father had disappeared into those mountains the spring the purges ended, and come home thin and silent, and never once in the eleven years he lived afterward said a single word about where he'd been.

Emrys sat on the barge with the compass open in his hand and the river running cold and quick below him, and for the first time in three years—for the first time since Kestrel Pass—he was afraid of something other than himself.

Upstream, far off, a light was moving along the bank road. Not a farm lantern. Farm lanterns swing. This one traveled steady and low, the way a man walks when he's reading the ground.

It was coming downriver. Toward the shed. Toward the barge.

Toward him.
