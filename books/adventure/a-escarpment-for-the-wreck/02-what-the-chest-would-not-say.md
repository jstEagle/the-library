## 2. What the Chest Would Not Say

The chest sat in the corner of Emrys Nystrom's rented room like a guest who refused to introduce itself.

He had carried it up from the salvage yard after dark, wrapped in a tarpaulin, hunched under its weight through back streets where the flood mud still shone on the cobbles like old oil. It was not a thing a man wanted to be seen carrying. Lead-lined chests did not come down rivers by accident. They came down rivers because someone had put them there, or because someone upstream had failed to stop them, and either way the arithmetic ended the same: whoever held it would be asked questions he could not answer.

He set his lantern on the table and looked at it for a long time.

The room was four paces by five, above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and river rot. A cot. A stove with one good burner. His gear hung from pegs by the door—rope coiled with a professional's neatness, crampons oiled, ice axe wrapped against rust—the tools of a trade that no longer wanted him. Forty years old and every skill he had fit inside a bag he couldn't afford to replace.

He knelt beside the chest.

Up close, in good light, the workmanship announced itself the way money always does: quietly, and everywhere at once. The bands were hand-forged iron over oak, but the corner fittings were brass, cast in relief, and each one bore the same device—a double-headed eagle, wings spread, heads turned away from each other as if neither wished to look upon what they shared. The stamp had been struck deep into soft metal by someone who expected it to last longer than the nation that ordered it struck.

Vhaloria. He said the word in his head the way you'd say the name of a dead man you'd never met. Erased before he was old enough to read maps. The valleys beyond the Karst Escarpment now flew the republic's grey-and-gold, and the schoolbooks called the annexation *the Consolidation*, which was the kind of word governments use when they mean graves.

He should sell the fittings. That was the sane move. Brass was brass; a pawnbroker in Lowmarsh would give him passage money and ask nothing, and in three days he'd be on a barge south where nobody knew his face or his name, and the chest could become somebody else's problem, somebody else's trap.

He put his hand flat on the lid instead.

The wood was cold in a way the night wasn't. Lead did that—it drank warmth and gave nothing back. He thought of the salvage line that morning, the Greywater dropping six feet in a day, the drowned willows rising black out of the shallows, and then the glint of it snagged in a root wad fifty meters downstream of the ferry crossing, half-buried, patient as a stone. Old Tamsek had been twenty meters away and hadn't seen it. Nobody had seen it but him. First luck I've had in years, he'd thought, standing hip-deep in the current, and even then, even in that first bright moment, some older and colder part of him had answered: *luck is just a hook with better bait.*

He fetched his knife and a pry bar from his kit.

The lock was a plate lock, old pattern, and the keyhole had been filled with wax and stamped—the same double eagle, small as a thumbnail. Not locked, then. Sealed. There was a difference, and the difference was a message: *this is not protection, this is promise.* Whoever closed this chest meant for it to be opened by someone specific, or meant for it to stay shut until the world changed enough to deserve what was inside.

He worked the pry bar under the lid's lip at the hinge side, where the wood was weakest, and leaned his weight into it.

The chest groaned. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog started up and quit again. The seal cracked with a sound like a knuckle popping, and the lid came free with a breath of air that had been waiting forty years to leave—dry, mineral, faintly sweet, like the inside of an old book, like a tomb that had kept its manners.

Emrys sat back on his heels and let the smell wash over him and felt something in his chest tighten that he refused to name. Hunger, maybe. Or fear wearing hunger's coat.

* * *

He laid everything out on the floorboards in a row, the way he'd lay out gear before a traverse, checking each item against the mountain of whatever this was going to be.

Dispatches first. Eleven of them, oilskin-wrapped, tied with cord gone brittle as straw. Each bore the double-eagle stamp in black wax, and each was addressed in a fine, upright hand to names that meant nothing to him—*Colonel-Adjutant Rell, Eastern Approaches; the Custodian of the Aldric Archive; Madame Provost, Vhalen.* He turned them over. Unopened. Every one. Whatever these men and women had needed to know, they had never learned it, and the learning had died somewhere in the water between then and now.

He weighed one in his palm. The paper inside shifted with a dry whisper. He thought about steaming one open over the kettle—he had the patience for it, he'd done subtler work with worse tools—and then set it down unopened, and noticed that he'd set it down carefully, aligned with the others, and understood that some part of him had already decided these documents were not his to damage. Which was absurd. Salvage was salvage. Finders keepers was the oldest law on the river.

Next, a locket.

It was silver, small enough to hide in a closed fist, on a chain fine as fishing leader. The front was engraved with a single letter worn nearly smooth: *I*. He thumbed the clasp and it opened with a click that seemed very loud in the small room.

Inside, behind clouded glass, two curls of hair lay side by side. One dark brown, almost black. One pale gold, the color of winter grass. Two different heads, kept together in a space the size of a coin. Someone had loved two people enough to carry them against her heart, and someone had carried this locket across a border, through a purge, down a river, and delivered it—to whom? To the mud? To him?

He looked at the hair for a long time. Then he closed the locket, more gently than he'd opened anything in years, and told himself the tightness in his throat was the dust.

The third item stopped him the way a cornice stops you—everything fine right up until the snow gives way.

It was a document, folded in thirds, heavy stock, written in ink that had faded from black to the brown of dried blood. At the top, a heading in formal script. Below it, lines of legal language he had to read twice, partly because the phrasing was archaic and partly because his eyes kept sliding off the middle of it, refusing, the way eyes refuse a drop off a cliff edge.

*A writ of succession, notarized and sealed under the authority of the Royal House of Vhaloria, in the final year of the reign.*

It named no throne. It named no title in so many words. What it did, in careful, lawyerly sentences that had survived the country that produced them, was establish a claim—one line of descent, protected and recorded, held in trust. And the person in whose favor the whole architecture rose, the person toward whom every clause climbed like water finding its level, was identified in exactly seven words:

*...the child of Ilse Vane, being living proof...*

The sentence broke off there, continued on the next page. Emrys didn't turn it over. He sat with those seven words while the lantern hissed and the river muttered somewhere below the town, going about its business of carrying things away.

Ilse Vane. He turned the name over. Nothing. It rang no bells, opened no doors. He knew the shape of Vhalorian history the way any child of the borderlands knew it—the royal family shot in the palace courtyard, the story went, all of them, down to the youngest; the ministers scattered; the name itself struck from registers and road signs. Ilse Vane was not a name attached to any story he knew. A cousin, maybe. A lady-in-waiting. Some minor branch the histories hadn't bothered to burn.

A child, though. A child who was *living proof*, according to people with seals and lawyers, in the last year before the end.

He set the writ down and rubbed his face with both hands and became aware, distantly, that his heart was going hard—not fear exactly, not yet, more like the body's early warning system registering a slope that the mind hadn't caught up to. He was a guide. He had spent thirty years reading terrain, and here was terrain: a sealed chest, a dead nation's paperwork, a claim that could pull a republic's foundations down if anyone alive could act on it. And he was sitting alone in a rented room holding it.

There was only one sensible response. Take the brass, take the silver, sink the rest in the deepest hole in the Greywater and walk south.

He reached for the locket again instead.

* * *

It was near midnight when he noticed the compass case.

His father's compass case—brass-cornered leather, cracked along the spine, sixty years old and carried over more miles than Emrys could count. His father had carried it, and his father's father before that, and when the Kestrel Pass reports came back and the blacklist came down and the family name became something people wiped their hands after touching, the compass case was the one thing Emrys had refused to surrender to the bankruptcy assessor. He'd lied about its value. It was the only time in his life he'd lied to an official's face, and he'd do it again.

He kept his spare needle and waxed thread in it. He'd had it open a hundred times that season alone.

Now, kneeling amid the spread-out contents of a dead kingdom, he looked at it lying by his kit bag, and saw it.

The scar.

Every guide's kit carries scars—rope burns, axe nicks, the record of bad weather and worse judgment. On the compass case's lower left corner there was a mark he had known since childhood without ever once examining: a small impressed shape in the leather, roundish, about the size of a thumbnail, that he had always taken for a brand from some long-gone maker or a burn from a stove lid. As a boy he'd traced it with a finger and made up stories about it. Afterward, grown, he'd stopped seeing it entirely, the way you stop seeing your own face.

It was a double-headed eagle. Wings spread. Heads turned away from each other.

He picked the case up with hands that had gone strangely distant, that felt like borrowed hands, and held it beside the wax seal on the nearest dispatch. Same device. Same proportions. Same odd inward turn of the two heads, like a signature too distinctive to be coincidence—and too deliberate to be anything else. The seal on the dispatches had been pressed into wax by a die. The mark on his father's case had been pressed into leather by the same die, or its twin, by someone who had stood close enough to touch.

His father had been a guide on the escarpment for twenty-two years. His father had died on Kestrel Pass in a whiteout with three clients, and the inquiry had found—*negligence, route error, failure to turn back*—and the finding had been repeated so many times, in so many official mouths, that it had worn smooth as river stone, and Emrys had hated it and fought it and finally, in the years since, simply carried it, the way you carry a stone in your boot that you've stopped trying to shake out.

His father had carried a Vhalorian royal seal on his compass case.

Emrys set the case down. Picked it up. Set it down again. The lantern flame leaned in a draft from under the door and righted itself.

He thought: *coincidence.* Merchants' goods got stamped with all kinds of marks; dies traveled; a case bought secondhand in some market could carry anything. He thought it firmly, the way you plant a piton in rotten rock and refuse to test it.

Then he thought: *the child of Ilse Vane.* Seven words. And beneath them, unbidden, rising like cold air off a glacier, another thought he didn't want: *my father guided the escarpment. My father knew the high routes. My father died in the mountains the year after the annexation, on a route no client ever hired him to take.*

He got up and paced the four paces and five paces of the room, twice, three times, and the pacing helped nothing, because the room was too small to hold the question and the question was getting bigger.

Stop, he told himself. You're a salvager with empty pockets. This is a dead woman's locket and a dead government's mail and a lawyer's paper about people forty years in their graves. None of it is yours. None of it wants you. Sell the brass, sink the rest, go south.

But he was already back on his knees at the writ, turning to the second page, reading the clauses he'd flinched from—the trusts, the conditions, the succession of custody—and near the bottom, in the section enumerating the persons entrusted with knowledge of the claim's location and validity, there was a list. Four names. Three were titles: *the Custodian of the Aldric Archive; the Warden of the Northern Shrines; the Royal Notary, by office.*

The fourth was not a title.

The fourth was a name, written in the same fine upright hand as the addresses on the dispatches, and though the ink had faded to brown and the paper had drunk forty years of river damp through lead and oak, the letters stood clear as cut steps:

*Nystrom.*

Just the surname. No given name. But there was only one Nystrom who had ever guided the high escarpment, and Emrys had inherited his hands, his routes, his compass case, and his name.

He sat back against the wall and the wall was cold through his shirt and he let it hold him up. Outside, the Greywater ran on in the dark, patient, carrying its silt and its secrets down to the sea. The lantern burned low. The eleven sealed dispatches lay in their row like sleeping animals, and the locket with its two colors of hair lay closed beside them, and the writ with its impossible fourth name lay open on the boards, pointing at him.

*They're all dead,* he told himself. *Everyone who signed it, everyone it named, everyone it was hidden from. It's a ghost's errand. It can't hurt anyone.*

But the republic had erased a nation, and nations that erase nations do not stop watching the places where the erasure might wear thin. He knew that much about the world. A man who had been blacklisted in two countries on the strength of reports he'd never been allowed to read knew exactly how efficiently institutions could reach across years and borders to tidy a loose end. And he was, as of tonight, holding the loosest end there was.

He should burn it. Right now, in the stove, all of it, and keep only the brass and the silver, and by morning be a poor man with a poor man's clean conscience.

He fed the stove a stick of kindling instead, and watched the fire take, and did not feed it anything else.

Instead he took out a stub of pencil and the back of an old route card, and began, in the small hours, in a hand far less steady than his father's, to write down everything he knew about the Karst Escarpment's northern approaches—which passes closed first, which monasteries still stood, which valleys had changed hands and which had merely changed flags. He wrote until the pencil was a nub and the window showed grey, and when he finally slept, in the chair, the compass case in his lap, he dreamed of a whiteout on Kestrel Pass and a voice calling his name in a language he didn't speak, and woke with the certain, sick, exhilarating knowledge of a man standing on a cornice in the first light of morning, hearing the snow begin to give.

Three days later, needing passage money and telling himself it was only the brass he'd sell, he carried a fitting into the tavern at Lowmarsh—and the tavern keeper looked at it, and then at him, and went white as the escarpment in January.
