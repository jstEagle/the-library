## 4. A Guide Nobody Hires

The town was called Lowmark, which was honest of it, because there wasn't a mark in it worth more than a week's bread. Emrys came down off the Greywater road with the chest strapped to his back and forty-one coppers in his boot, and by the time he'd walked the length of the single street he'd already spent three of them in his head.

Lowmark sat where the river gave up its temper and became something you could ferry across. It had a customs shed with a crooked door, a chandler that smelled of tar and old rope, and two taverns: the Ferryman's Rest, which charged fair prices for bad beer, and the Anvil, which charged unfair prices for worse. He went into the Rest because the Rest had a pawnbroker who drank there on Thursdays, and today felt like a Thursday, though he'd lost track somewhere around the third day of prying at the chest's lock.

He ate first. That was discipline—the kind they teach you when you've been hungry enough times to know how hunger lies. Stew, dark bread, a cup of the bad beer, which was at least honest about being bad. He ate slowly, back to the wall, the chest between his boots under the table like a dog he didn't trust. Around him the tavern filled with ferry men and wool factors and a pair of drovers arguing about a horse nobody in the room had seen. Ordinary noise. Ordinary evening. He let it wash over him and did not relax, because relaxing was a habit he'd surrendered along with everything else.

The brass fittings were the plan. The chest itself was too recognizable—lead-lined, double-eagle stamped, the kind of object that made people ask questions with official voices behind them. But the corner brackets, the hasp plate, the handle mounts: those were just metal to anyone who didn't look twice. Good brass, heavy, Vhalorian work from before the annexation, which meant it was either worthless or worth a great deal depending entirely on who was buying. A pawnbroker on a Thursday night would see scrap. Scrap was what Emrys could afford to sell.

He finished the stew, wiped the bowl with the bread, and carried the chest to the corner where Halvard Penn kept his little table of scales and loupe and the air of a man perpetually disappointed by what people brought him.

Penn was sixty and bald as a river stone, with spectacles thick enough to start fires if the sun ever cooperated. He looked up, saw the chest, and his disappointment arrived ahead of his curiosity.

"Salvage," Emrys said, setting it down. "River find. I'm after the fittings only."

"Fittings." Penn turned the chest with one finger, unimpressed. "Brass runs four coppers the ounce if it's clean. This is green at the seams."

"It's clean under the green."

"Everything's clean under the green." But he was already working the loupe up onto his eye, tilting a bracket toward the lamp, and Emrys watched the man's expression do the small complicated thing that meant the metal was better than advertised. "Vhalorian casting. You don't see this weight anymore. Where'd you say—"

"River find."

"Rivers don't cast brass." Penn set the loupe down and looked at him properly for the first time, and Emrys felt the familiar tightening behind his ribs, the one that came whenever anyone looked at him properly. "This is a sealed chest, friend. Lead-lined. Somebody went to trouble. Fittings come off, but the rest stays with you, and I'll tell you now I don't want the rest anywhere near my table."

"I'm not selling the rest."

"Good. Because I'd pay you to keep it." Penn began unscrewing the brackets with a jeweler's driver, quick and practiced, laying them out on a square of felt. "Fourteen ounces the set, maybe sixteen with the handle mounts. Call it sixty coppers, and I'm being generous because I like your silence."

Sixty coppers. Passage east, food for nine days, a new flint, and the small luxury of not owing the tavern keeper when he left in the morning. Emrys nodded and kept his face still, because wanting things visibly was another habit he'd surrendered.

Penn worked in silence for a while, then said, without looking up, "You're Nystrom."

The name landed in the tavern noise like a coin dropped in a well.

Emrys didn't answer. There was no answer that helped. He watched Penn's hands keep working, steady, unhurried, and understood that the question hadn't really been a question.

"Heard you drowned," Penn said. "Or got arrested. Stories differ."

"The stories usually do."

"Mm." Penn screwed the last mount free and weighed the pile on his little brass scale, adjusting weights with a fingertip. "I knew a Nystrom once. Not you. Older. Guided the high routes back when the escarpment passes still had names instead of numbers." He glanced up. "Your father?"

"My father."

"Hell of a guide, they said. Hell of a guide." Penn counted out coppers in stacks of ten, and his voice went carefully neutral, the way men's voices go when they're deciding whether to step around something or step on it. "You'll want to settle your tab and be gone early, if you take my meaning."

"I take it."

* * *

He should have left that night. Sixty coppers in his pocket, the chest lighter by its fittings, the road east dark and empty and calling. Every instinct he owned told him to walk.

But the Kestrel Pass disaster had taken more than his license and his reputation. It had taken his tolerance for running before he'd finished a meal, and some stubborn animal part of him wanted one hot dinner eaten sitting down, in a chair, indoors, like a man with nothing to hide. So he ordered the stew again, and paid for a room upstairs, and told himself one night was safe.

One night was never safe.

He was halfway through the second bowl when the tavern door opened and let in the cold and a man built like a doorframe, gray-bearded, moving with the deliberate economy of someone whose knees had opinions. He scanned the room out of habit, found nothing interesting, and was turning toward the bar when his gaze crossed Emrys's table and stopped.

Emrys knew him before recognition finished crossing the keeper's face. Torvald Meiss. Fifteen years ago they'd run supply lines together over the low cols—Meiss hauling, Emrys navigating—and Meiss had once talked him out of a cornice traverse in weather that would have killed them both, and Emrys had bought him a bottle afterward and promised to name a route after him someday. The route had gotten named after somebody else. Most things had.

"Nystrom." Meiss said it quietly, but quiet carries in a small room, and heads turned the way heads do. "Emrys Nystrom."

"Keeper." Emrys put his spoon down. "Beer's still bad, I hear."

For a moment Meiss just stood there, and Emrys watched fifteen years move across the man's face—surprise, then something warmer, then the thing that always came after the warmer thing, which was memory, and after memory, Kestrel Pass. He saw it happen. He'd seen it happen a hundred times in a hundred rooms, the exact moment a man's welcome curdled into arithmetic: *this is the one who walked down and the others didn't.*

"Sit," Meiss said finally. "I'll bring you something better than the stew."

"I've got the stew."

"You've got my stew. Sit." And there it was again, underneath—the old warmth, fighting the arithmetic. Meiss waved off the girl at the bar and poured two glasses of the good brandy himself, the dusty bottle from under the counter that Lowmark's better citizens probably believed was decorative. He brought them over and sat down across from Emrys without asking, the way old rope partners sit.

They drank. The brandy was excellent and Emrys hated how much he needed it.

"Kestrel Pass," Meiss said, not cruelly, just getting it over with, the way you'd lance a boil. "You want to tell me what happened? The real version? Because I've heard six versions and none of them sound like you."

"No."

"Fair." Meiss drank. "You know what I heard most? That you turned back at the icefall and sent them up anyway. That you sat warm while—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "That's the version that travels. You know that."

"I know that."

"And it's not true."

"It's not true."

Meiss looked at him for a long moment, and Emrys braced for the next question, the one everyone asked eventually: *then why won't you say so? Why won't you fight it?* And the answer was the same one he'd given for fifteen years, which was that the truth required witnesses, and all his witnesses were dead, and a man alone against an official story is just a man shouting at weather.

Instead Meiss said, "You look terrible."

Emrys laughed despite himself—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening for the first time in years. "I've been salvaging the Greywater."

"That's a job for men with nothing."

"It is."

"And you've got nothing."

"I've got sixty coppers and a chest full of paper I can't read."

It was out before he could stop it, and he watched Meiss's eyebrows climb, and cursed himself silently for the brandy, for the chair, for the whole stupid luxury of sitting down. Meiss leaned forward, voice dropping. "Paper? What kind of paper comes out of the Greywater in a lead-lined box?"

"The kind nobody buys."

"Everyone buys paper, Nystrom. That's the trouble with paper." Meiss drained his glass and sat back, and for a moment he wore the old expedition face, the one that mapped problems aloud. "There were men through here last month asking after salvage crews. Not customs. Not police. Clean boots, wrong questions. One of them had a republic archive pin, the little bronze flame, worn inside the lapel like he thought hiding it was the same as not having it." He tapped the table once. "If your paper's what I think it might be, you don't want to be in Lowmark tomorrow. You don't want to be in Lowmark tonight."

"I've got a room."

"Burn the receipt."

* * *

But it was already too late, and Emrys knew it the way you know weather coming—not from any single sign but from the pressure, the quality of the silence in the room. The heads that had turned when Meiss said his name were still turned, or pretending not to be. The drovers had gone quiet mid-argument. In the corner, a man in a wool coat who'd been nursing the same beer since Emrys arrived had begun, very casually, to finish it.

Meiss saw it too. Fifteen years of keeping a tavern teaches a man to read a room the way a guide reads a sky, and whatever he read made his jaw tighten.

"Back room," he said. "Now. Bring the chest."

In the back room there were flour sacks and a broken chair and a window that opened onto the alley behind the woodshed. Meiss shut the door and lit no lamp, so they spoke in the gray light coming under the door.

"You can't be seen leaving through the front," Meiss said. "Wool-coat will mark you, and whoever he reports to will have the road east watched by morning. Go north instead. Old drover track along the escarpment foothills. It's slower but there's no checkpoint until Harrow Ford, and Harrow Ford doesn't care about anything except sheep theft."

"Why are you doing this?" Emrys asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "Last time anyone in this country saw me, I was the reason four families buried empty coffins."

Meiss was quiet a moment. When he answered, his voice had the flat steadiness of a man stating conditions on a mountain: "Because I guided with you for six years and you never once cut a rope early. Whatever happened on Kestrel Pass, it wasn't that. And because a man who won't defend himself is either guilty or broken, and you were never guilty." He paused. "Broken, maybe. But that heals."

Emrys looked at the floor. There was a reply in him somewhere—something about how broken things heal into different shapes, none of them useful—but what he said was, "The chest. I can't carry it over drover tracks. It weighs as much as sin."

"Then it stays here. Under the floor of the smoke cellar. Nobody finds anything in my smoke cellar; the health inspector fainted in there in '09 and we agreed to part ways permanently." Meiss held out his hand. "The paper. Keep the paper on you. Paper's thin. Paper hides."

Emrys hesitated. Everything in him that had survived fifteen years of disgrace screamed against handing the one valuable thing he owned to a man he'd known before the world ended. But the alternative was carrying lead-lined proof of something across open ground with a watcher on the door, and survival had taught him to make ugly trades quickly.

He handed over the chest. Meiss took it like it was ordinary, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for Emrys in years.

"North track starts behind the mill," Meiss said. "Follow the wall until the wall ends, then follow the creek. You'll hit the escarpment foothills by noon tomorrow if your legs remember their trade." He opened the alley window and cold air poured in, smelling of woodsmoke and snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it. "Nystrom. Whatever's in that paper—it's worth killing for, or those men wouldn't be drinking bad beer in my taproom waiting for you to sneeze. Worth killing for cuts both ways. Remember that."

Emrys went through the window into the alley, and the cold closed over him like water.

* * *

Behind the mill the wall ran crooked and knee-high through weeds stiff with frost, and Emrys followed it north with the dispatches wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, feeling naked in a way he hadn't felt since they took his license. Behind him Lowmark glowed its few lamps, and somewhere in that glow was a man in a wool coat finishing a beer, and by morning the coat would be talking to someone with a bronze flame pin, and by afternoon the story would have grown legs: *the Nystrom boy, alive, broke, carrying something.*

Ahead of him the escarpment stood against the stars, a black seam stitched across half the sky, and the first wind off it carried the mineral smell of high country and, faintly, the promise of snow. His legs remembered their trade. They always did. It was the only part of him that had never been blacklisted.

He walked, and behind him, in a tavern called the Ferryman's Rest, Torvald Meiss blew out the lamp over an empty taproom, and went down to the smoke cellar to hide a dead nation under his floorboards.
