## 5. The Woman with the Locket

Sable Vane had spent eleven years drawing lines that other people erased.

It was the work nobody thanked you for and everybody needed: the border commissions, the disputed watersheds, the mining concessions where a river had shifted two hundred meters since the last survey and suddenly three villages belonged to someone else. She redrew them. She was good at it—precise, patient, immune to the flattery of officials who wanted the ink to bend their way. And every time she finished a map, she signed it *S. Vane* in small letters at the margin, because her mother had told her once that a name on a map outlives the hand that drew it.

Her mother had also told her never to ask about the other name. The one on the locket.

She carried the locket the way some people carry a knife—close to the body, checked by touch a dozen times a day without thinking about it. Brass, worn smooth, the hinge stiff with forty years of pocket-warmth and neglect. Inside, behind clouded glass, a curl of dark hair and half an inscription: *Ilse V—*, the rest eaten away by something that had not been water. She had taken it to jewelers, archivists, one very expensive forger in Corvane's capital who had looked at it for ten seconds and then refused her money and asked her to leave by the back stair.

Half a name. Half a mother. Half a history, with the other half locked in a country that no longer appeared on any map Sable had ever been paid to draw.

Vhaloria. Even thinking the word felt like stepping off a trail edge.

* * *

She tracked Emrys Nystrom the way she'd track anything: backward from the evidence.

The rumors had reached her in Greywater Falls the way rumors did—drunk, contradictory, and mostly true. A salvage man had pulled a chest from the flood debris. Lead-lined. Sealed. Stamped with a double-headed eagle that hadn't flown anywhere in forty years. The salvage man had tried to sell the brass fittings and vanished from town before dawn, which told Sable two things: he knew what he had, and he knew who else knew.

The tavern keeper—a heavy woman named Brannoc with forearms like mooring rope—had given up the rest after Sable bought three hours of her time and didn't flinch at the price. *Nystrom*, she'd said, wiping down a counter that didn't need it. *Emrys Nystrom. You don't want him.* And when Sable asked why not: *Because Kestrel Pass is why not. Ask anyone.* Nobody would say more than that, which was its own kind of answer. In mountain towns, disasters got talked to death. Disgraces got whispered.

He'd gone north on the old drover road toward Thornmere Ford. Sable gave him a day's head start and then closed it in a day and a half, because she had grown up walking surveys with her mother and her legs knew what they were for.

She found him at the ford in late afternoon light, and her first thought—not admiration, exactly, but the professional recognition of one craftsman for another—was that he moved like terrain. Not across it. Like it. He came down the near bank over wet stone without a single wasted motion or hesitation, pack riding high, eyes doing that constant quartering thing she'd seen in guides and soldiers and nothing else. He was younger than his reputation. Thinner than it too. There was a rawness under the competence, the look of a man who had been living badly for long enough that bad living had become a kind of discipline.

He saw her before she meant him to. Of course he did.

She stopped twenty paces off, on the flat gravel bar where the road met the water, and made herself still. Hands visible. Weight settled. Everything about her posture saying *not a threat* while knowing full well that a lone woman intercepting a blacklisted guide on an empty road said nothing of the kind.

"You're following me," he said. His voice was low and roughened, like gravel worked by current.

"I am."

"Since when?"

"Greywater Falls."

His jaw tightened. "Brannoc."

"She was very loyal. It took three hours."

"What do you want?" He hadn't moved closer. He hadn't moved away either, and she noted that—the calculation running behind his eyes, weighing distances, footing, the weight of the pack against whatever he thought she might be carrying. Archivist? Mercenary? Both wore better clothes than hers, usually. She'd dressed down deliberately: field jacket, survey satchel, boots broken in past politeness. A working woman. Which was true, if incomplete.

"My name is Sable Vane," she said. "I'm a cartographer. I work border commissions—redrawing lines when rivers move, when treaties change, when someone with money wants the ink bent." She let that sit. "Three weeks ago I was hired to survey a stretch of the upper Greywater. While I was there I heard about a chest."

Something crossed his face—fast, controlled, but not fast enough. Not for her. She read faces the way she read contour lines; a flicker was a cliff edge, and she'd just watched him fall off one.

"There's no chest," he said.

"No," she agreed. "There isn't. You're right. So we can stop talking about it." She crouched, unhurried, and untied the flap of her satchel. "Instead we can talk about this."

She brought out the locket.

She held it up between finger and thumb so the low sun caught the brass, and she watched him the way she'd watch a seismograph needle. For a moment—two, three—he simply looked. Then his whole body changed. Not dramatically. A settling, like snow deciding whether to hold on a slope. His eyes went from the locket to her face and stayed there, and what she saw in them wasn't greed or fear.

It was recognition. The terrible kind. The kind a man has when he sees something he's seen before in a place he's spent years trying to forget.

"Where did you get that," he said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"It was my mother's."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." She stood, closing her fist around the locket, feeling its warmth against her palm. "That's rather the point, Mr. Nystrom. My mother is dead. Her papers are dead. Her name—" she paused, and heard her own voice go quieter than she intended, "—her name is half-eaten off the inside of this locket, and everything it used to be attached to was erased before I was born. I've spent my whole life drawing borders for countries that exist. I want to know about the one that doesn't."

He looked at the river. The ford ran shallow here, braided channels over pale stone, the water talking to itself in the way water does when it has somewhere to be. When he spoke again his voice had changed—flatter, guarded, a door pulled shut.

"You've got the wrong man. I'm a salvage worker. I pull scrap out of floods. Whatever you think I found—"

"I don't think anything," Sable said. "I know what the double eagle means. I know what lead lining is for—it's for keeping paper safe from water and from fire, which means whoever packed that chest cared about what was inside surviving, which means it matters. And I know that within a day of your find, a republic archivist named Odile Marchetti burned her personal copy of the Vhalorian civil registry and took the north road." She let that land. "You're being followed, Mr. Nystrom. By people whose job is making things disappear. I'm the least dangerous person on your trail, and I'm the only one offering to pay you."

That got through. She watched it get through—the slight shift of his weight, the recalculation. Fear was useful. She hated using it, but she'd learned that men who lived alone in high places trusted fear faster than they trusted money.

"Marchetti," he repeated. "You know that name how?"

"The same way you know the eagle. You listen in places where people forget to lower their voices." She shouldered her satchel. "Here's my offer, and then I'll leave you alone if you want leaving. I need to cross the Karst Escarpment before the snows. To a monastery—Saint Aldric's, on the eastern face. There's an archive there. Pre-annexation records. I want to know what's in them, and I can't get there alone, and every guide with a license will refuse me the moment I say the word *escarpment* in autumn." She held his gaze. "You don't have a license. That's not a problem for me. It might even be a recommendation."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll hire someone worse than you, and probably die, and it won't be your fault." She shrugged. "But you'll still be followed, and you'll still be alone, and whatever's in that chest will still be worth killing for to people who kill professionally. I'm offering the only alliance you're going to get that isn't trying to bury you."

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He built a fire instead—which she understood, eventually, as a form of speech. On the drover road at dusk, with the escarpment's first blue foothills stacked against the sky, a fire meant *we are talking now*. It meant *sit down*. It was the closest thing to hospitality a man like him could offer without admitting he wanted company, and she sat down across from it and accepted without comment, because she recognized the grammar. Her mother had spoken it too.

They ate hard bread and tinned fish and he asked her questions in the sideways manner of a man conducting an interrogation he wanted to seem like conversation. Where had she trained. Who paid for the survey. Did she know the eastern approaches. She answered honestly except where honesty was dangerous, which was a skill she'd inherited along with the locket, and she caught him doing exactly the same thing—answering truthfully about rope techniques and river gauges and lying smoothly, badly, about Kestrel Pass when she finally asked.

"Fourteen dead," she said. "That's what the record says. The record also says the guide survived."

"The record's right."

"Do you want to tell me what happened?"

"No."

"All right." She turned the tin in her hands. "For what it's worth, I don't believe you killed anyone."

"You don't know me."

"No. But I've spent eleven years reading official records, and official records are written by people who need the story to end neatly. Fourteen dead and one survivor reads neat. It reads like someone decided it should." She looked at him across the fire. "Whoever wrote that report wanted you finished, Mr. Nystrom. I'd like to know why. Not tonight. But before we're on the ice together, I'll want to know why."

He stared into the flames for a long time. When he finally spoke, it wasn't about Kestrel Pass at all.

"That seal," he said. "On the chest. The double eagle." His thumb moved unconsciously against the compass case at his belt—an old leather thing, scarred, handled so much it had gone soft. "There's a mark on this. Same stamp. My father's compass case. He was a guide too." A pause, and then, grudgingly, like each word cost him: "He died on Kestrel Pass. The same day."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, the ford kept talking.

"So we're both," Sable said slowly, "carrying dead parents and half a story."

He almost smiled. It was a small, damaged thing, gone as soon as it arrived, but she'd seen it. "Is that what you call it."

"It's what I know it is." She reached into her collar and drew the locket out on its chain, letting it hang in the firelight between them. "My mother never told me where she came from. Never told me her family name beyond *Vane*, which she chose herself—I found that out from a marriage register, which is a fun thing to learn about your own mother. She drew maps. Beautiful ones. And she taught me that a map is a lie you agree to keep telling until enough people agree with you that it becomes true." Sable's voice went quiet. "She died when I was nineteen. Last thing she said was *don't go looking*. Which is, of course, the single most effective thing she could have said to make me look."

Emrys Nystrom watched her across the fire, and whatever armor he'd built through the evening developed, briefly, a crack. "And the hair? Inside?"

"I don't know whose it is. I've never been able to find out. Every expert I've approached either couldn't help or wouldn't." She curled her fingers around the locket again, closing it away. "Forty years ago a country died. Everyone agrees it deserved to, everyone agrees it's better forgotten, and everyone who could tell me otherwise is either dead or hiding. Except—" she nodded at his belt, at the scarred compass case, "—apparently somebody left receipts."

He was silent a long moment. Then: "If I open that chest—if I even admit it exists—there's no road back to being a salvage man. You understand that. The people following me don't send letters."

"I do understand that."

"And you're asking me to walk you into the middle of it anyway."

"I'm asking you to walk me to Saint Aldric's," Sable said. "What's in the middle is already happening to you. I'm just offering to happen alongside."

* * *

They parted for the night—or pretended to. She took a spot upstream among the alders with sightlines to his fire, and she had no doubt he'd chosen his ground with sightlines to her, and there was something almost companionable in the mutual surveillance, two wary animals agreeing to sleep in the same meadow. She lay awake a while listening to the river and turning the locket's chain around her fingers.

Running from names, she thought. That was what they were both doing. He'd buried his under a disaster report and a blacklist; she'd buried hers under a chosen surname and a trade that let her live in other people's geographies. And now a river had coughed up a box with an eagle on it, and both their buried names were clawing at the dirt.

Her mother's voice, unhelpfully, in memory: *Don't go looking.*

*I know, Mama,* she said silently to the dark. *Too late.*

Near midnight she heard him move—soft, deliberate—and pretended not to. An hour later, checking by instinct, she saw his pack by the dead fire and his bedroll abandoned, and her stomach dropped with a certainty that needed no evidence: he'd slipped out in the dark, heading north alone, choosing solitude and danger over a stranger with a locket and too many questions.

Sable lay still for exactly as long as it took to decide.

Then she rose, shook out her boots, and began packing by feel, because she had grown up on surveys and she could break camp blind, and because she had spent her whole life redrawing borders no one asked her to—and Emrys Nystrom, she suspected, was about to discover that the one border he couldn't cross alone was the one he was currently crossing.

By first light she had his trail. He was good. She was better motivated.

The escarpment stood up ahead of them both, blue and enormous, holding its secrets the way it held its weather—and somewhere behind them both, on the roads they'd already walked, other people were reading ashes and loading rifles, and none of them yet knew how short the distance was between a salvaged chest and everything it could burn down.
