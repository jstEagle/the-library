## 6. Terms of Passage

She found him at the ferry landing, of all places, where the Greywater ran slow and brown and the morning mist came off it like breath off a sleeping animal. Emrys was counting coins into the palm of a boatman with a face like a closed fist—passage money, or most of it, scraped together from the brass fittings he'd pried off the chest two nights before—and the woman simply appeared at his elbow, the way certain people do, as if she'd always been standing there and he'd only just noticed.

"You're Nystrom," she said.

He didn't look up. "You're mistaken."

"I'm rarely mistaken. It's a professional failing." She watched the last coin leave his hand. "That's not enough for the crossing."

"It's enough for me to walk around," he said, pocketing what the boatman had pushed back. "I've done it before."

"Emrys Nystrom." She said the whole name deliberately, tasting each syllable, and he felt it land on him like a hand on the back of his neck. "Guided the Kestrel Pass commercial route four seasons without a single incident. Youngest lead guide ever certified on the northern escarpment. Then one winter—" she snapped her fingers, softly, "—gone. Blacklisted in Vhaloria's ghost and blacklisted here. Two countries, one man, no explanation anyone will put in writing."

The mist drifted between them. Downriver, a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry.

"Whoever you are," Emrys said, "you've got the wrong wreck. I'm a salvage man now. I pull dead men's furniture out of rivers."

"And chests," she said. "Lead-lined ones with double eagles on them."

Everything in him went still—the way it goes still on a slope when you hear the first whisper of a slide somewhere above you and every muscle understands before your mind does that the next few seconds decide everything. He turned and looked at her properly for the first time.

She was perhaps thirty, dressed like someone who had learned to dress for weather rather than for rooms: waxed canvas, good boots worn honest, a canvas roll slung across her back that he took for instruments of some kind. Brown hair cut short and practical. Eyes the grey-green of glacial meltwater, and just as cold-looking until you saw the current underneath. She carried herself like a map that had been folded too many times along the same creases.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"No," she agreed. "But your face does, which is more than I got from the tavern keeper, and he talked for an hour." She extended a hand. "Sable Vane."

He didn't take it. The name went through him strangely—not recognition, nothing so clean as that, but a faint wrongness, like a compass needle trembling near iron it can't see. *Vane.* Somewhere in the chest there had been a writ naming a child of Ilse Vane, and here was a woman on a muddy ferry landing handing him that name like it was nothing, like it was a coat she wore because it fit.

"Sable," he repeated.

"My mother thought it was poetic. She was wrong about several things." She withdrew the unshaken hand without embarrassment. "You're leaving town ahead of gossip. I watched you sell those fittings to Ferro the smith at half their worth, which tells me you need money badly and want distance more. So let's not waste each other's morning. I have a proposition."

"The answer's no."

"You haven't heard it."

"Don't need to. Whatever it is, it involves the escarpment, and I don't go up anymore. That's not negotiable, it's not dramatic, it's just a fact about my life, like a limp." He shouldered his pack. "Good luck finding another guide. There are dozens. Most of them haven't even killed anyone."

"That's exactly why it has to be you."

* * *

They ended up walking anyway, because she wouldn't be shaken and he wouldn't run while she watched—it would look too much like fleeing, and he'd spent three years learning the difference between leaving and fleeing, and he refused to blur the line again. They walked the river road north through country gone gold and rust with the turning season, and she laid it out between milestones.

"I'm a cartographer," she said. "Commissioned work, mostly border surveys. Governments pay well to redraw lines nobody asked them to redraw, and I've stopped pretending it means anything. But ten years ago, working a survey in the old Vhalorian valleys, I found something in a monastery archive—a reference to a succession writ. A claim. Sealed, notarized, dated the final year before the purge."

"There is no Vhaloria," Emrys said. "There hasn't been for forty years. The republic holds the valleys, the maps say so, the graves say so. A piece of paper doesn't change that."

"A piece of paper changes everything. That's the entire history of nations, Nystrom—pieces of paper that enough people decided to believe." She walked with a cartographer's stride, even and tireless, eyes always moving over the terrain like she was already drafting it. "The monastery of Saint Aldric sits high on the Karst Escarpment. The monks hid records there during the annexation—whole archives, smuggled up in grain sacks. If the writ's twin exists, it's there. And the passes close with the first real snow. Three weeks, maybe less this year. After that, nothing moves above the treeline until spring."

"So hire a guide when spring comes."

"By spring, whoever else wants that paper will already have it. Or burned it." She glanced at him sideways. "And by spring, I may not be able to get permits. My name opens doors in the lowlands. It opens fewer every year, and none at all up there. I need someone the mountains still remember—even if the towns don't."

"They don't," he said flatly. "That's the point of a blacklist."

"Which is why whoever engineered it spent real effort doing it. You've asked yourself why, haven't you? A clean record, four safe seasons, then ruin—no inquiry, no appeal, just letters from two governments arriving the same week. That's not an accident. That's architecture." She let it sit. "Someone wanted you grounded, Nystrom. People don't spend that kind of effort grounding a man unless they're afraid of where he might fly."

He stopped walking. The river muttered past below the road, carrying its usual freight of silt and secrets.

"You talk like someone who's read my file."

"I read everything. It's how maps get made—out of other people's paperwork." She stopped too, facing him, and for a moment the practical mask slipped and he saw something rawer underneath: a tiredness that had nothing to do with the road. "Here are my terms. Full payment up front—enough to clear every debt you have and buy the crossing you couldn't afford this morning. Permits under my commission; you travel as my survey assistant, invisible and legal. Provisions, equipment, whatever you need. In exchange, you take me to Saint Aldric before the snows. That's all. One archive, one climb, one season. After that we never have to see each other again."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll find someone worse than you, and probably die, and it'll be on your conscience, which I understand is already crowded."

He almost laughed. It startled him, the impulse—his face had forgotten the mechanics of it. "You don't know anything about my conscience."

"I know you pulled a drowned chest out of a river and didn't sell it whole. A man that hungry keeps the fittings and melts the rest. You kept it intact. Why?"

Because the seal matched the scar on his compass case. Because the writ named a child of Ilse Vane and the locket inside held a curl of hair that had made his hands shake. Because for the first time in three years he'd lain awake at night not replaying Kestrel Pass but turning a wax impression over and over in the dark like a stone he couldn't stop rubbing.

"Sentimental value," he said.

"Liar," she said, without heat. "But you're entitled to your lies. Everyone running from something is." She started walking again. "Think about it. I'm at the Drowned Ox until tomorrow noon. After that I go alone, and the mountain decides which of us it likes better."

* * *

He told himself he would sleep on it, which was a lie, because he didn't intend to sleep at all. He took a room at a flophouse near the tannery—the kind of place where the bedbugs paid rent and the landlady asked no questions because questions cost extra—and sat on the mattress with the chest open in front of him, going through the papers again by candlelight.

The dispatches were military, brittle, official. The locket he'd stopped touching after the second night; it did something to him he didn't have vocabulary for yet. The writ he'd memorized. *The lawful succession of the house of Vhalor devolves upon the child of Ilse Vane,* in ink gone the color of dried blood, under a seal he could draw from memory now, though he'd never seen it before the river gave it up—except that he *had* seen it, forty years before he was born, scratched into the underside of his father's brass compass case, a mark his father had called *just an old stamp, boy, from a job long ago*, in a voice that had closed like a door.

His father. Dead eleven years on Kestrel Pass, in the disaster that had ended Emrys's career, his reputation, his right to exist in two countries. And now a stranger with his father's ghost in her surname was offering him money to walk the same ground.

"No," he said aloud to the candle. It flickered, unconvinced.

Because here was the arithmetic he kept refusing to do: Sable Vane was either a fool or a lure, and both possibilities led uphill into weather that killed professionals. And beneath the arithmetic, quieter, a truth he could feel in the old break of his shoulder whenever the air turned: that some part of him had been waiting three years for exactly this offer, the way a wound waits for weather.

He blew out the candle and lay down and did not sleep, and somewhere in the small hours he heard footsteps on the stairs that didn't belong to the landlady—he knew her tread, heavy and halting, one knee gone bad. These steps were light, doubled, careful. They passed his door without pausing, which was its own kind of pause, and continued to the end of the hall.

He was moving before he'd finished deciding to move, boots in hand, silent the way the mountains had taught him. The end room belonged to a tinker who'd checked out days ago. Its door stood ajar, and through the gap came a thin bar of lantern light and the sound of paper being handled—carefully, methodically, by someone who understood paper.

Two men. One held the lantern; the other went through the tinker's abandoned belongings with gloved hands, and Emrys understood with a cold drop of the stomach that they weren't looking for the tinker. They were establishing a base of operations. Which meant the next room they'd search—

He was back in his own room in ninety seconds, heart going like a drill into hard ice. The chest. The papers. He couldn't carry them all; there wasn't time. His hands moved on their own, guided by three years of instinct for what matters when everything must be abandoned: the writ went inside his shirt, against his skin. The locket—God help him, the locket went into his breast pocket. The dispatches he left, the chest itself he dragged to the window and lowered on the bedsheets knotted together, down into the tannery yard's shadow, praying the acid stink of the pits would keep casual hands away.

The door handle turned as he stepped into the wardrobe, pulling its door nearly shut behind him, leaving a finger's width of dark.

They were good. He'd give them that. They entered quietly, swept the room with the lantern in practiced arcs, and went to work—one on the mattress, slitting it open along the seams, one kneeling at the floorboards, testing each one with a thin pry bar. Not thieves. Thieves don't check under floorboards for paper. These were archivists with knives, or soldiers with libraries, and Emrys stood in the wardrobe among hanging coats that smelled of tallow and strangers' sweat, and understood that the choice had been made for him.

If they found the writ on him, he was dead in an alley by morning—or worse, delivered somewhere with soft chairs and hard questions, and the paper taken and burned, and whatever Ilse Vane had bled to preserve would end in a bureaucrat's stove. If they didn't find it tonight, they'd find it tomorrow, or the day after. Men like these didn't stop. Stopping wasn't in their contract.

And Sable Vane was at the Drowned Ox until noon, offering money, permits, legality, and a road that led up and away from all of this—up onto the escarpment, where two men with a lantern and a pry bar would be reduced to what they actually were: lowlanders, breathing thin air, dying competently.

The wardrobe door creaked under his grip. He eased it still.

One of the men spoke, voice flat as slate: "Nothing. Chest isn't here either."

"He'll have moved it. Watch the room. Report to the woman that the target's active."

*The woman.* So there was a her behind these two, the way there's weather behind wind. Someone who read ashes, someone who sent gloved men into flophouses after a salvage diver's leavings. Emrys pressed himself deeper into the dark and did his last piece of arithmetic, the kind you do when the rope's already loaded and the only question is whether you commit:

He could refuse Sable Vane and stay hunted in the lowlands, poor and cornered, waiting for gloved hands to find him. Or he could take the job he'd sworn never to take, climb ground he'd sworn never to touch, toward a monastery full of answers he wasn't sure he wanted—in exchange for distance, money, and one season of being impossible to corner.

It wasn't courage. He wanted that clear, even with himself. Courage would have been staying to fight. This was terrain assessment. This was choosing the route where he had the advantage, and the advantage was altitude, and altitude meant her.

The men finished, set a watcher by the window—Emrys marked his silhouette against the lamplight from the street, noted the angle of sight, the blind spot beneath it—and settled in to wait for a man who was already three moves gone in his head.

He went out through the roof.

The tannery yard received him in its reek and shadow. The chest came up from behind the lime pits heavier than he remembered, and he left it—left it deliberately, wedged under the drain grate where only someone who knew to look would find it, because a man climbing onto the escarpment carries nothing he can't afford to lose, and the papers against his skin were the only cargo now.

Dawn was coming up grey over the rooftops when he reached the Drowned Ox. Through the window he could see her in the corner booth, awake, a map spread before her and a cup of something steaming untouched at her elbow—as if she'd known exactly when he'd come, and had simply been keeping the seat across from her warm.

Emrys pushed open the door. The bell above it rang, bright and cheap, and Sable Vane looked up from her map with the expression of a woman collecting a debt she'd never doubted.

"Well?" she said.

"Terms accepted," Emrys said. "But we leave within the hour. There are men watching my room, and by tonight they'll know I've gone—and whoever sent them will know where we're headed." He sat down across from her, and the writ shifted against his chest like a second heartbeat. "So before you spread that map, you'd better tell me everything you know about a woman who reads ashes."
