## 7. The Third Shadow

The meeting was set for a tannery outside Vellmark, which told Doss Ferrin everything he needed to know about the man who'd hired him. Tanneries stank. Nobody lingered in a tannery. Nobody remembered faces there either, because everyone's eyes were streaming and every conversation happened at a shout over the drums and the vats. A man who chose a tannery for a business meeting had been careful his whole life, or had something to hide, or both.

Doss had been both his whole life, so he recognized the breed.

He arrived early, as he always did, and walked the perimeter with the unhurried gait of a man killing time. Six buildings around a reeking courtyard, stone floors sloped toward drainage channels, one road in and one road out—the road being the problem, but you couldn't have everything. Two of Corvane's men were already posted: one on the loading dock pretending to inventory hides, one across the yard smoking with the studied boredom of a man who'd been told to stand somewhere and look like nothing. They were competent. Not good—good would have meant Doss couldn't spot them—but competent, which meant the money behind them was real.

The money was the only thing about this contract that was.

He found the office above the drying lofts, where the smell changed from ammonia to something older and sweeter, like rotting fruit, and where a man sat behind a desk that had never seen a hide in its life. The man was maybe fifty, grey at the temples, dressed the way lawyers dressed when they wanted to be mistaken for clerks. He had a ledger open in front of him and didn't look up when Doss entered.

"Ferrin."

"Corvane's man."

"I'm Master Quillon. I handle the master's arrangements." The man closed the ledger with two fingers, delicately, as if it might bruise. "Sit."

Doss didn't sit. He'd learned twenty years ago that the first man to sit in a negotiation was the first man to be managed, and he'd been managed enough for one lifetime. He leaned against the doorframe instead, arms crossed, and let the silence do its work.

Quillon looked up. There was no irritation in his face, which was itself irritating. "You came recommended by people who don't recommend anyone. So I'll assume you're as good as they say, and you'll assume we pay what we promise. That saves us both an hour of posturing."

"It saves you an hour," Doss said. "I still want to hear the job."

Quillon opened a drawer—not the one with the ledger—and set three things on the desk. A purse heavy enough that it landed with a thud. A folded paper. And a photograph.

Doss ignored the first two and picked up the photograph.

It was a portrait, the kind done by one of the new street photographers who set up in market squares and charged a copper for a likeness. The man in it was perhaps thirty-five, lean in the way of men who carried their own weight up mountains, dark-haired, with a face that might have been handsome if it hadn't been so tired. He wasn't smiling. He was looking slightly off-camera, at something the lens couldn't see, with an expression Doss had seen before on other men's faces and had spent years learning not to think about. It was the look of a man standing at the edge of something, deciding whether to step off.

"Emrys Nystrom," Quillon said. "Formerly a mountain guide. Currently a salvage worker on the Greywater, or he was until ten days ago. He pulled something out of the river. A chest. We want the chest."

"What's in it?"

"Papers. Old papers. Worthless to anyone but collectors, but our employer is a collector." Quillon said it smoothly, the way a man says a rehearsed sentence, and Doss had heard enough rehearsed sentences in enough wars to know when the rehearsal was covering a hole. "The chest is lead-lined, banded in brass, stamped with a double-headed eagle. You'll know it when you see it."

"And the man?"

"Nystrom may attempt to keep the chest. If he does—" Quillon spread his hands, a gesture of reasonable regret "—he's dangerous. A trained mountaineer, desperate, possibly armed. We'd prefer the situation resolved without complication."

Doss set the photograph down. "Resolved how?"

"I imagine a man of your experience can resolve situations in several ways." Quillon's voice didn't change at all. "We're paying for all of them."

There it was. Doss had taken contracts with teeth in them before—every soldier of fortune had, if he'd worked longer than a season—and he'd learned to price them honestly. But there was a difference between a contract that said *the target may resist* and a contract that said *leave no witnesses* without saying it, and Quillon was dancing right along that line with the grace of a man who'd danced it many times.

"The chest comes back intact," Doss said. "And Nystrom?"

"Comes back intact too, if it can be arranged."

That stopped him. Doss had expected *dead* or *doesn't matter* or the long silence that meant both. He hadn't expected *alive*, and he especially hadn't expected the slight hesitation before it, the half-beat where Quillon checked himself against his instructions.

"He's a salvage worker," Doss said. "You said he's dangerous. Now you say take him alive. Which is it?"

"He's dangerous," Quillon repeated, "and he's valuable. Both things are true. Bring him to us if the opportunity allows. Don't risk the chest to do it." He slid the purse forward. "Half now. Half on delivery. You'll report through me, only through me, and you'll tell no one your employer's name, because you don't know it."

"I know it's Corvane."

"You know that's the name you were given." Quillon almost smiled. "Names are cheap, Ferrin. That's why we spend them freely."

* * *

Doss took the contract back to his band, who were camped in a disused quarry two miles north of town, and laid it out for them the way he always did: plainly, with the ugly parts facing up.

Six of them sat around the fire. Rell, his second, a big quiet woman from the border country who could read a trail the way priests read scripture. The Voss brothers, twins, who fought like one animal with four hands and drank like two. Anselm, the youngest, quick and hungry and not yet old enough to know what the work cost. Petra, who'd been a army medic before her regiment was disbanded and its officers hanged, and who kept her instruments cleaner than her conscience. And old Havel, who was technically retired and technically present anyway, because Havel had been retiring for fifteen years and nobody had the heart to make it stick.

"A retrieval job," Doss said. "One chest, one guide, over the Karst Escarpment before snowfall. Pay is double the going rate."

Anselm whistled. Rell didn't react, which was how she reacted to everything.

"Double rate means triple trouble," she said.

"Means someone doesn't want to be known," Havel said, spitting into the fire. "In my day, when a man paid double, it was because he was ashamed."

"When a man pays double," Petra said, "it's because he wants something done that can't be undone."

Doss let them talk. It was part of how he ran the band—they got to say the doubts out loud, in front of each other, so the doubts didn't go underground and come back up at the worst moment, on a rope, in a storm, with a knife. He'd seen bands die of unsaid things. Said things, you could argue with.

"The client wants the chest intact," he said. "Wants the guide alive if convenient. Dead if not. No witnesses beyond that."

"No witnesses," Rell repeated flatly. "On a public trade route, in harvest season, with pilgrim traffic on the low passes."

"We'll be above the treeline by then. Above the traffic."

"Above the treeline," she said, "where nobody hears anything."

"That's the point of above the treeline."

She looked at him across the fire, and he saw her doing the arithmetic he'd already done: six professionals against one salvage worker and whoever he'd scraped together, easy odds, dirty terms. She shrugged. "I've done dirtier."

Everyone had. That was the whole industry.

They spent the next two days preparing, and Doss spent them watching the photograph.

He pinned it inside his map case, not out of sentiment but procedure—you studied a target's face until you could pick it out of a crowd at distance, until the face became information instead of a person. Dark hair going grey early at the temples. A scar through the left eyebrow, old and clean, stitched by someone who knew their work. Hands in the portrait were hidden, but the shoulders said rope-worn, and the posture said a man used to being responsible for other people's lives, which was its own kind of weapon and its own kind of weakness.

*Dangerous*, Quillon had said. Doss had killed dangerous men and been paid well for it, and none of them had looked like this. Dangerous men looked at the camera. This one looked past it.

On the second evening, Rell sat down beside him without asking, which was her way of asking.

"You've looked at that face forty times," she said.

"It's my job to look."

"My job too. I've looked four times." She nodded at the map case. "What are you seeing?"

Doss considered lying to her, then discarded it. Lying to Rell was like lying to a compass; it worked once and then you were lost.

"There's a file on him," he said. "Quillon gave me a page. Kestrel Pass, eleven years ago. A guided party caught in a slide, nine dead, the guide survived. He was struck off the rolls in two countries. The republic's guild board called it negligence. The northern guild called it criminal negligence."

"And you believe the file?"

"I believe files." He paused. "Usually."

"But?"

But. He turned the photograph over, though there was nothing on the back but the photographer's stamp. "Eleven years ago I did a contract in the northern ranges. Escort work, boring, snowed in at a waystation for six days. There was a rescue while we were there—a party buried on the Kestrel side, and the local guides went up before the weather fully broke, which was suicide, and pulled two people out of a collapse that had already killed seven." He could see it still, if he let himself: the white slope, the small figures moving up it with the particular economy of men who knew exactly how much time they'd bought and exactly how little. "One of them dug a boy out with his hands. Took him most of an hour. Everyone at the station watched through the glass and nobody breathed."

Rell was quiet. "Nystrom?"

"The file says he survived the slide uninjured. It doesn't say he dug anyone out. Files don't say things like that." Doss tapped the photograph. "This face is younger than the man I remember. But the scar's the same. And the way he's looking off-frame—" he stopped himself. "It doesn't matter. It was eleven years ago and half a range away, and half the guides up there dig people out of things."

"Then why bring it up?"

Because it mattered, some stubborn part of him answered, and he stepped on it. "Because Quillon handed me a file that makes this man a coward and a killer, and my memory makes him something else, and I want to know which document is forged. That's all. It affects tactics. A man who digs children out of avalanches fights differently than a man who lets them die."

"How differently?"

"Better," Doss said. "And worse to fight."

* * *

They moved out on the third morning, ahead of the target, because the plan was simple and plans should be: get above him, find the choke points on the escarpment route, and take the chest wherever the terrain favored them rather than him. Quillon's intelligence said Nystrom would move within days—he'd been seen buying passage money's worth of brass fittings in Vellmark, which meant he was broke, which meant he'd take whatever offer came, which meant he'd have company, because men like Nystrom never traveled alone for long. Someone would hire him. Someone always hired a guide with his reputation, if the price was right and the questions were few.

Doss wondered, not for the first time, how Quillon knew so precisely what a blacklisted salvage worker would do next. Intelligence like that cost more than the purse on the desk. People didn't spend that kind of money on worthless papers.

He put the thought away with the others, in the place where he kept the things a professional wasn't paid to think. The place was getting crowded on this contract. He noticed that too, and noted it the way he'd note a creak in a rope bridge—one more weight it would hold, probably, but worth counting.

They made the foothills by evening and camped in a fold of ground below the goat track, and Doss took first watch because he slept badly in new country anyway. From the ridge above camp he could see the lowlands spread out under a rising moon: the river a dull silver ribbon, the towns pricked with lamplight, and somewhere down there a man with a tired face and a scarred eyebrow, holding a chest full of worthless papers that powerful people wanted badly enough to hire six strangers to kill for.

*Dangerous,* Quillon had said, and Doss had written it in his head next to the photograph, and now, watching the dark line of the escarpment cut the stars in half, he found the word wouldn't stay where he'd put it.

Dangerous men were a commodity. He'd been one himself. What Quillon feared wasn't danger—danger could be bought, outgunned, buried. What Quillon feared, in the hesitation before *bring him alive*, in the rehearsed smoothness about worthless papers, was something else entirely. Something the photograph showed and the file hid.

Down in the valley, small as an ember, a light moved along the river road. Northward. Toward the hills.

Doss watched it for a long time, and then he went down and woke Rell.

"He's moving," he said. "Get the others. We climb tonight."
