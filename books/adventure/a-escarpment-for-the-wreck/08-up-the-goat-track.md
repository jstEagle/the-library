## 8. Up the Goat Track

The foothills began where the road gave up pretending.

One hour past the last farmstead the track narrowed to a shelf of packed earth barely wide enough for a mule, and after that it wasn't a track at all but a suggestion—the kind of line a goat would draw if you gave it charcoal and a grudge against switchbacks. Emrys stopped at the transition point out of habit, the way other men checked their watches, and looked back once at the valley: the Greywater glinting through the poplars, the town with its taverns and its gossip, all of it already shrinking into something he could pretend he'd never needed.

Then he turned uphill and started testing her.

He didn't announce it. That was the point. A client who knew they were being tested performed their fitness like a recitation—chest out, breath controlled, eyes flicking to the guide every hundred yards to see if they'd passed. He wanted to see what Sable Vane did when she thought no one was measuring. So he set a pace that was honest on paper and vicious in practice: long strides on the steep sections, no pauses at the false summits, water breaks timed to arrive just as she found her rhythm again. He took them off the main path twice under the pretext of avoiding loose rock, which was true, but also put them on ground that climbed four hundred meters in a kilometer and required hands.

The third member of the party made his opinion of this known within the first hour.

Teodor was a packer from the lowland villages, sixty if he was a day, built like a rain barrel and about as conversational. He had been recommended by the livery stable in half a sentence—"Teo knows the escarpment side, he'll take your loads"—and Emrys had hired him because the alternative was carrying everything himself, and because old men who still worked mountains were worth more than their wages. But Teodor did not approve of Emrys's route choices, his pace, or possibly of Emrys personally, and expressed all three through a continuous low muttering in dialect that Emrys caught maybe one word in five. The word he caught most often sounded suspiciously like *madman*.

Sable, meanwhile, climbed.

She didn't talk while she climbed, which put her ahead of most clients by itself. She breathed through her nose, kept her weight over her feet instead of pulling with her arms, and when the track crossed a scree fan she read it before stepping onto it—watched how the stones sat, tested the angle with one boot, chose her line without being told. Twice she adjusted her own pace on the steep pitches, shortening her stride exactly as the grade demanded, and both times Emrys pretended not to notice and noticed completely.

By midday she had sweat through her shirt and said nothing about it. By the second ridge she had stopped looking at the view, which meant she understood the view was a luxury. And when he called the lunch halt at a spring that ran cold enough to hurt the teeth, she drank, ate half of what he ate, repacked her own gear without spreading it everywhere like an offering, and stood ready before he was.

"You're trying to break me," she said. Not accusatory. Diagnostic.

"I'm trying to find out what I've got."

"And?"

Emrys looked at her—dust to the knees, hair plastered flat, a scrape along one forearm she hadn't mentioned—and gave the only answer that cost him anything. "More than I budgeted for." He shouldered his pack. "Fifteen minutes more. Then we climb until dark."

Behind him, Teodor muttered something that definitely contained *madman*.

* * *

They made camp in a fold of the hillside where a stand of black pines broke the wind, and Emrys built the fire small, the way you do when you're not sure who else is reading the light. That habit had come back to him on the trail somewhere around the second hour—a prickle between the shoulder blades, old and unwelcome, the sense of being watched from below. He'd turned three times and seen nothing but the valley emptying behind them. It didn't mean anything. It also didn't mean nothing. Men who had spent years learning to read weather learned to read the feeling of a valley too, and the valley felt occupied.

Supper was barley and salt pork and tea strong enough to strip paint. Teodor ate with his back against a stone and his boots off, groaning theatrically, and was asleep almost before the light died. Which left the fire, and the two of them, and the question Emrys had been watching her carry all day like a second load.

She asked it the way she did everything—without decoration.

"The Kestrel Pass," she said. "That's where you were guiding. Before."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope a rock let go and clattered down into darkness.

"Yes," Emrys said.

"What happened?"

There were answers to that question the way there were routes up a face: the true line, the easy line, and the line that got you down alive. He had rehearsed the easy line so many times in taverns and hiring halls that it came out smooth as river stone.

"Avalanche," he said. "Late season. We had a window and I took it. The slope above the col released. Eleven people." He fed a stick to the fire. "I survived. They didn't. The inquiry decided I shouldn't have taken the window. Two countries agreed, which is the only time they've ever agreed on anything."

It was a good lie. It had the right shape—hubris, weather, arithmetic of the dead. It was even mostly true, which was what made good lies work. There had been an avalanche. There had been eleven people. He had survived.

What it left out was his father, and the chest, and the reason a man named Corvane had spent two years quietly making sure that every guide's association, every garrison office, every hiring hall from here to the coast had Emrys Nystrom's name on a list with a red line through it. What it left out was that the window hadn't been his idea alone—that there had been pressure from above, from a client whose name he'd never been given, pushing the party onto Kestrel Pass in a season that every local guide knew was wrong. That part he had never told anyone, partly because he couldn't prove it, and partly because saying it aloud meant admitting the disaster hadn't been his sin but someone else's weapon, and he had carried the sin so long it had grown into his spine. Take it out and he wouldn't know how to stand.

Sable watched him across the fire with those cartographer's eyes, the ones that measured distances and discrepancies alike.

"That's not how the reports read," she said.

Emrys kept his face still. "You've read the reports?"

"I read everything. It's my trade. Borders, mostly—but borders are just disasters drawn neatly." She turned her tin cup slowly in her hands. "The Kestrel Pass inquiry file has a gap in it. Three days of testimony, sealed. Nobody will say why. And the guide who filed the initial route plan withdrew it the day before the ascent, then refiled it identical, same ink, same hand. People don't change their minds and unchange them in identical handwriting unless someone's holding the pen."

The fire cracked. Emrys heard his own pulse in it.

"You've done a lot of homework for a woman who needs a mapmaker," he said.

"I've done a lot of homework for a lot of reasons." She looked at him steadily, and he understood that she was doing to him exactly what he'd done to her on the trail all day—testing, probing, finding the load-bearing walls. "You're lying to me about something, Mr. Nystrom. I don't know what yet. But you lie badly, which is interesting, because most men in your position lie well. Practice."

"Maybe I'm out of practice."

"Maybe." She set the cup down. "Or maybe you've never had anyone worth telling the truth to."

She rolled into her blanket then, back to the fire, and left him sitting with that like a piton driven under his ribs. He stared into the coals a long time. Above the black pines the sky had cleared to a hard glitter of stars, and the escarpment stood against them, a saw-blade of darker black, forty kilometers of limestone and ice that his father had crossed with a pregnant woman and never come home from.

*Identical handwriting,* he thought. *Someone's holding the pen.*

She knew more than a mapmaker should. He filed it away next to the prickle between his shoulder blades, and slept badly, and dreamed of snow falling upward.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the best kind of climbing weather and the worst kind of hiding weather, because sound carried. Emrys was up before the others, and while the kettle heated he walked fifty meters along the morning shelf and stood listening to the mountain wake.

That was when he found the cairn.

It was new. That was the first thing—not the shape, which was old as hills, but the stones themselves, pale-fresh where they'd been pried from the frost-heaved ground, no lichen, no weathering. Someone had built it within the last few days, on a shelf that no shepherd used and no climber needed, marking nothing: not a route junction, not a water source, not a summit. Just a stack of rocks, precise as punctuation, placed where a man coming up the goat track from the valley would be sure to pass it.

A signal. Or a marker for someone following.

He crouched beside it and looked downslope, back the way they'd come, at the thin gray thread of their yesterday's trail stitched across the green. Nothing moved. Crows worked the thermals over the valley. A hawk hung motionless over the far ridge like a note held too long.

But the prickle between his shoulder blades had graduated from feeling to evidence, and Emrys Nystrom had survived this long by respecting evidence.

He broke the cairn with his boot, scattering the stones downslope, and walked back to camp wearing the same face he'd worn all yesterday—the bored, competent, slightly cruel guide face—and found Sable awake, packing, watching him with those measuring eyes.

"Something wrong?" she asked.

"Loose rock," he said, and lied to her again, and hated how easily it came now, and wondered which of them was holding the pen.

He kicked dirt over the fire. "Eat fast. Today we gain the tree line—and after that, there's nowhere left to hide except each other."
