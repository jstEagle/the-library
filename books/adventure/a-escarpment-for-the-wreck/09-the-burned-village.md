## 9. The Burned Village

The village announced itself before they saw it.

Sable noticed it first as a wrongness in the map she carried in her head—the one she'd drawn and redrawn since childhood, the one with every contour of the Karst Escarpment inked from survey sheets and secondhand accounts. There should have been a hamlet here. The old Vhalorian charts her mother had kept folded in a tin beneath the floorboards showed a cluster of roofs at the confluence of two streams, a mill, a chapel with a squat bell tower. The new republic maps showed nothing at all. Just blank space, the cartographic equivalent of a held breath.

Then the trail bent around a shoulder of limestone, and the blank space became a place.

"Emrys." She said his name quietly, because the valley seemed to demand quiet.

He stopped beside her, breathing hard from the climb, and looked down into the bowl of the valley without expression. "Ostrovets," he said. "That's what it was called."

"You've been here?"

"No." He adjusted the strap of his pack, and for a moment he didn't look at the ruins but at the ground, as if reading something written there. "My father's route notes mention it. Water, shelter, a day's march from the high col. He wrote it like any other waypoint." A pause. "It isn't any other waypoint."

Sable understood what he meant. From the ridge, the village looked less destroyed than *unmade*—as though someone had lifted it off the earth and set it back down carelessly, at an angle, broken. Roofless walls stood in rows along the stream, their gables still upright, blackened beams jutting into the sky like ribs. The chapel's bell tower had been toppled deliberately, pushed so that it lay across its own graveyard, crushing a score of headstones under its weight. Grass had come up through everything—through doorways, through hearths, through the cracks in the millrace where water no longer ran. Forty years of green softening the edges of violence, but not erasing it. Nothing had been rebuilt. Not one wall raised on another. The republic had annexed these valleys and then simply declined to let them exist again.

"They burned it," Emrys said. It wasn't a question.

"The purge reached everywhere," Sable said. She kept her voice level, professional, the voice of a cartographer describing terrain. "The records say resistance cells operated out of the valley villages. The reprisals were—" She stopped. The word *documented* died in her throat. What was documented was a list. What was here was a chapel lying on its own dead.

"We'll camp in the church," Emrys said. "Walls are sound. Wind can't reach us there. We'll be gone before light."

They descended in silence.

* * *

The church had lost its roof but kept its shape, and inside, the grass grew tall and pale where the light fell through the open rafters. Someone—shepherds, perhaps, or other travelers over four decades—had cleared a corner near the eastern wall and built a fire ring of fallen roof slate. The stones were smoke-blackened. People came here. People always came to ruined churches; Sable had seen it in a dozen borderlands, the way the living were drawn to the architecture of grief even when they didn't share it.

Emrys set about making camp with the economy of a man who had slept rough for years: fire laid small, packs arranged against the wall, the water question solved by pointing Sable toward the stream with instructions to fill well upstream of the village proper. "Old wells are bad news," he said. "And don't drink from the millpond."

She took the canvas bags and walked down through the ruins alone while he saw to the horses—or rather, to the single mule they'd bought in the last town, a gray, opinionated animal named Branc that Emrys treated with more tenderness than he'd yet shown her.

The stream ran clear over white stones. She knelt and filled the bags, and that was when she saw the first of them.

A child's shoe. Leather, rotted soft, half-buried in the bank where forty years of floodwater had worked it loose from somewhere. It lay on its side, toe pointed downstream, as if its owner had simply walked out of it and kept going. Sable crouched over it for a long time. Then she did not touch it. She finished filling the bags and walked back up through the grass, and she made herself look at the doorways she passed—at the iron hooks where coats had hung, at the shelf brackets still screwed to walls that held nothing, at a child's chalk drawing weathered onto a doorframe: a sun, a stick figure, a bird. She made herself look because looking felt like a duty she couldn't name, and because some part of her, some part she kept locked below decks, already suspected why the duty felt personal.

Back at the church, Emrys had the fire going and was heating the flat of bread and drippings that passed for dinner. The mule stood hobbled outside among the gravestones, cropping grass with the indifference of its kind.

"There's a school," Emrys said, nodding toward a long low building across the lane. "Or what's left of one. I'm going to check it before dark. If there's salvage worth carrying, better to know now."

"Salvage," Sable repeated.

"Burned buildings hold things. Paper survives in odd corners if it was sealed away when the fire came through. My father taught me that." He said it flatly, without looking at her, and she caught again—that flicker she'd been catching for three days now—the sense that everything he told her about his father cost him something to say.

"I'll come with you," she said.

He shrugged, which she had learned meant yes.

* * *

The schoolhouse was smaller than she'd expected—one room, presumably, for all the valley's children—and the fire had taken it badly. The roof had collapsed inward, and the interior was a tangle of charred joists and slate, furred over with moss. But the walls of mountain villages were built thick against winter, and thick walls had pockets. Emrys moved through the ruin with a strange gentleness, lifting fallen beams with a pry bar rather than dragging them, setting things down instead of throwing them.

"You've done this before," Sable said.

"River salvage. Wrecked houses after floods. You learn how buildings die." He pried up a section of flooring near what must have been the teacher's desk, and beneath it, in the dry dark between the joists, there was paper.

Not much. A ledger swollen with damp, its pages fused into a brick. A bundle of exercise books, their covers scorched brown. And a wooden box, olive-drab, army-stamped, which Emrys opened with the pry bar's edge and went very still over.

"What is it?"

He turned it so she could see. Inside, nested in straw, was a class register—a teacher's roll book, bound in blue cloth, its spine scorched but its contents dry. Beside it, a small tin cup, a brass hand-bell gone green with age, and a folded flag, small, ceremonial, the kind schools kept to raise on feast days. The flag bore the double-eagle of Vhaloria, its stitching fine, its colors faded to ghost-tints.

"Somebody hid this," Emrys said quietly. "When they knew what was coming. Sealed it under the floor and walked out." He looked around the ruined room, at the moss and the char, and Sable watched him do the arithmetic every guide does instinctively—the weight of objects, the space in a pack, the miles remaining. "We can't carry all of it."

"The register," Sable heard herself say. "Just the register."

He glanced at her, and something moved behind his eyes—not suspicion exactly, but the attention of a man noting a current shift in water he'd thought he knew. "Why that?"

"Names," she said. "Records of people are rarer than flags. Anyone can keep a flag." She heard how it sounded and couldn't tell if it sounded false. "For the archive. If we ever reach it."

He considered her a moment longer than was comfortable. Then he handed her the box.

She carried it back to the church herself, refusing his offer to take it, and sat by the fire with it in her lap while the light failed and Emrys saw to the mule. The blue cloth cover was cool and slightly tacky with age. On the front, in faded gilt, the words: *Ostrovets Valley School — Register of Pupils — Year 31.*

Year 31 of a nation that no longer existed. She had grown up counting years the republic's way, the way everyone did now, the way the maps did. But her mother had counted differently sometimes—muttering dates over her charts, converting in her head, a private arithmetic Sable had always assumed was just an old woman's habit, the way some old women kept their hair in styles from their girlhood. Her mother had left Vhaloria as a girl. Had never spoken of it except in fragments: a valley, a bell, a grandmother's recipe for honey bread, a word—*ostrov*, island—that Sable had thought was just a word until this moment, kneeling in a burned church with a dead school in her lap.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it the way you notice rain starting—as a fact arriving from outside.

She opened the register.

The pages were ruled in columns: name, age, parent or guardian, marks, remarks. The handwriting was a teacher's—firm, slanted, occasionally impatient. The early pages listed names that meant nothing to her. Bren. Kolaric. Vaszary. Children born into a country that would be erased before they finished growing. Some entries had remarks in the margin: *transferred to the gymnasium in Vhalor City.* *Left for the coast.* One boy, aged nine, had beside his name only: *fever, January.* No further entry. The teacher had recorded his death in the same ink she used for attendance, and somehow that undid Sable more than the burned chapel had.

She turned the pages slowly, telling herself she was looking for nothing.

The autumn term of the final year began halfway through the book, and the handwriting had changed—tighter, faster, the ruling of the columns gone slightly crooked, as if the teacher's hand had learned to shake. Attendance thinned week by week. Whole families marked *departed.* And then, on a page dated in a month whose number Sable's mind refused to convert, a name entered in the middle of the roll, in that tighter, frightened hand:

**Vane, Ilse. Age 14. Guardian: none stated. Remark: guest of the valley.**

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the mule shifted and stamped.

Sable read the line eleven times. Her mother's name. Her mother's maiden name, in a dead language's spelling, in a dead school's book, fourteen years old, a guest of the valley, guardian none stated—which meant, Sable understood with a clarity that felt like falling, that by then Ilse Vane had already been running. Already separated from whatever family remained to her. Already the child the whole world was looking for, hidden in a mountain village under a teacher's protection, attending lessons between flights, doing sums in a room that would burn.

There was no further entry after that page. The register simply ended, mid-term, mid-year, the way lives had ended here.

Sable closed the book and held it against her chest and discovered that she was crying—not sobbing, nothing so useful as sobbing, just tears running steadily down her face while her breath stayed perfectly even, as if her body had split into two creatures, one grieving and one keeping watch. She could not have said, if asked, precisely what she wept for. For her mother, yes—but her mother had lived, had escaped, had raised her daughter on three continents and died old in a rented room above a print shop. This grief had no clean object. It was for the girl in the register who had been her mother and whom she had never met. It was for the children with fever in January. It was for a valley full of doorways with hooks for coats that no one would ever hang again. It was for the map-blankness, the deliberate unmaking, the republic's great project of pretending a place had never been.

And underneath all of it, cold and specific, was the thing she could not yet look at directly: the knowledge of what she had done three days ago on the road, when she had intercepted a disgraced guide and hired him with money she'd described as a commission, and had shown him her mother's locket and told him she was tracing her family's borders, and had said nothing—nothing—about the succession writ, about the name *Vane*, about the possibility that the man she'd hired might be tied to her mother's escape in ways she was only beginning to guess. She had spent her life redrawing borders for governments that paid her to make erasure look tidy. She had told herself this journey was different. Now she knelt in her mother's hiding place with proof in her hands, and the difference looked thinner than she could bear.

Footsteps behind her. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, fast, clumsy, and put the register in her pack before turning.

Emrys stood in the doorway with an armful of firewood, silhouetted against the last violet light. His eyes went to her face, to the pack, back to her face. He was not a perceptive man in most ways—she'd catalogued his bluntnesses for days—but he had the animal alertness of someone who'd survived being hunted, and she saw him register that something had changed in the air of the church.

"Find anything?" he asked.

"School records," she said. "Attendance rolls. Names of families." She kept her voice steady. She had lied to border officials in four countries; she could lie to one tired man by a fire. "Nothing important."

He nodded slowly and came in to stack the wood. But as he knelt at the fire, feeding it, he spoke without looking up, in the mild tone of a man discussing the weather:

"My father's route notes say the schoolmaster of Ostrovets hid fugitives in the cellar of the church, the last winter. Before the soldiers came." He set another branch on the flames. "Funny thing to write in a route log. Most men would've just noted the water."

Sable said nothing. The firelight moved on his face.

"He also wrote," Emrys went on, still mild, still not looking at her, "that the schoolmaster had a niece visiting that year. From the city. Nobody in the valley used her real name. Too dangerous." He picked up a stick and drew idly in the ash. "The note doesn't say what happened to her."

The silence stretched. Outside, wind moved through the roofless houses, a long low breath through forty years of absence.

"Tomorrow we climb," Emrys said finally, rising, brushing ash from his knees. "Long day to the tree line. Sleep while you can."

He rolled into his blanket with his back to her, the way he did every night, trusting her exactly as far as the firelight reached and no farther. Sable lay awake long after his breathing slowed, the register a hard rectangle against her spine where she'd tucked it beneath her, listening to the wind comb the ruins.

She had come to the mountains to find her mother's past. She was beginning to understand that she had found instead a debt—owed not by her, but to her, by a man sleeping six feet away whose father had carried her mother over this same escarpment, and who did not yet know it. Tomorrow, on the climb, he would ask her questions. He was already asking them.

And she would have to decide, on some switchback above the tree line, whether the truth was a burden she could finally put down—or whether she would carry it up into the snows and let it crush them both.
