## 10. Marchetti Descends

The lowlands smelled of diesel and river mud, and Odile Marchetti hated both equally.

She stepped off the night train at Greywater Junction with a single leather case, a government credential she was not supposed to be carrying, and a headache that had ridden with her from the capital like a second passenger. The station clock said six-forty. The sky over the river valley was the color of dishwater, and the flood had gone down just enough to show the town its own dirty hem—mud lines on the warehouse walls, debris snagged in the bridge pilings, salvage crews already picking at the leavings like gulls.

She had been awake for thirty hours. It didn't matter. Sleep was a currency she had never learned to spend well.

The Bureau of Records did not send field archivists to river towns. The Bureau of Records filed things. That was the whole architecture of her life: incoming correspondence, catalogued; outgoing directives, stamped; the past, boxed and shelved and made harmless by the simple act of labeling it. Odile Marchetti had spent nineteen years making the past harmless, and she was very good at it, and every year she got a small raise and a smaller window office, and she told herself this was enough.

Then a salvage man on the Greywater had pulled a lead-lined chest out of the silt with a double-eagle stamped on its lid, and somewhere in the ministry's quiet corridors a telephone had rung three times, and Odile had understood, before anyone said anything at all, that the past was not harmless. It had only been waiting.

* * *

The tavern was called the Drowned Bell, which struck her as either very old or very honest, and it stood two streets back from the levee with its timber frame leaning east like a tired man against a wall. She arrived at seven-fifteen, before the morning drinkers had thickened into the afternoon ones, and found the keeper alone behind his bar, polishing glasses that were already clean.

He was a big man going soft, gray at the temples, with the heavy forearms of someone who had once done real work in the mountains and now lifted nothing heavier than a tap handle. He looked up when she came in, gave her shoes and coat the once-over—the city cut of them, the wrongness of them here—and went back to his glass.

"Closed till noon."

"I'm not here to drink." She set her credential folder on the bar, open, the seal facing him. "Ministry of Interior. Records Division."

The glass stopped moving.

"Kestrel Vane," he read, because everyone misread it, the ink had faded that way for forty years, and something flickered across his face—not fear exactly. Recognition's poorer cousin. "Never heard of it."

"That's fine," Odile said. "Most people haven't. I'm looking for a man named Emrys Nystrom. He was in this town within the last week. Tall, dark-haired, mid-thirties, walks like his knees are older than he is. He may have tried to sell brass fittings—chest fittings, marine brass—to anyone who'd buy."

The keeper set down the glass with exaggerated care, the way a man does when he needs his hands occupied so his face can stay neutral. "We get drifters."

"We do," Odile agreed. "This one has a scar through his left eyebrow and a reputation in two countries. Shall I describe what he was selling, or will you?"

Silence. Outside, a cart rattled past on the levee road, and somewhere a dog was losing an argument with a barge horn.

"He came in Tuesday," the keeper said finally. "Bought a bowl of stew he couldn't afford and tried to sell me a hinge. Bronze, dovetailed, salt-pitted. Good work." His jaw worked. "Vhalorian work. I knew that before he said where he got it."

"And you told him—"

"I told him brass is brass and I don't deal in grave goods." The keeper's eyes came up, and there it was, the thing under the neutrality: anger, old and banked like coals. "And then I asked him if he was the Nystrom boy. From Kestrel Pass. And he looked at me like I'd hit him, and he left his stew half-eaten, and he walked out of my tavern and out of my town before the gossip could catch him. So whatever you're hunting him for—" he planted both hands on the bar, and the wood creaked, "—you can write down that he paid for his stew. In full. With coins counted twice. A man counting coins twice isn't a criminal. He's a man who's been robbed so long he doesn't trust arithmetic anymore."

Odile wrote it down. Not because she needed to. Because writing gave people time to say the next true thing, and the next true thing always came.

It came.

"You're the third one asking about him," the keeper said quietly. "There were two men, day before yesterday. Big fellows, matching jackets, said they were insurance investigators. They weren't. Insurance men smile." He wiped his hands on his apron. "They asked the same questions you're asking. Then they asked different ones. Where does he sleep. Who does he drink with. Whether he has family." He met her eyes. "You want to know what I think? I think you people should decide among yourselves whose dog he is before the rest of us get bitten."

Odile closed the folder. "Thank you. You've been helpful."

"I've been careful," the keeper corrected. "There's a difference, and I'd thank the Ministry to learn it."

* * *

She spent the rest of the morning doing what she did best, the thing no one in the ministry valued because it looked like clerical work and was actually something closer to surgery: reconstruction.

From the harbor master's ledger, a salvage permit issued nine days ago to E. Nystrom, salvage line, section fourteen, the bend below the old weir. From the ferry clerk, a ticket purchased and unused—so he hadn't crossed the river; he'd gone up-valley. From a feed store, a purchase record: rope, hardtack, stove fuel, boot grease. Mountain rations. Enough for two, though the clerk remembered only one customer—a detail Odile circled twice, because a man buying food for two who travels alone is a man expecting company, or dreading it.

Up-valley. Toward the Karst Escarpment. Toward the passes that would close with the first snows, which the weather bureau—she had checked from the train, illegally, using a cousin's credentials—predicted for early this year. Very early.

She spread the papers on her inn room's narrow desk and looked at the shape they made, and the shape was unmistakable. He wasn't running anymore. Running men go down-valley, toward ports and crowds and new names. This man was climbing. He had a chest full of dead nation in his possession and he was taking it up into the last place on earth where a republic's writ ran thin, and he was racing the snow to do it.

Which meant he knew what was in the chest.

Or he suspected. Or—and this was the possibility that had kept her awake on the train, staring at the black window—he had opened it, and read it, and was carrying it toward the one archive in the mountains that might still hold its twin.

Saint Aldric's. Of course. Everything came back to Saint Aldric's eventually; it always had, since the purge, since the monks had hidden the registry behind their cliff-hung walls while the valleys burned. Her superiors believed the monastery's holdings had been catalogued and neutralized decades ago. Her superiors believed many convenient things.

Odile lit a cigarette at the window and watched the river carry its freight of silt past the town, and let herself think the thought she was not permitted to think:

*What if the papers are true?*

Not legally true—legality was a matter of stamps, and she dealt in stamps. True in the older sense. What if the child had lived? What if the line had run on, underground, all these years, while the republic paved its roads over Vhalorian graves and taught its schoolchildren that the double-eagle had died childless and deserved to? What if there was a person alive today who was owed everything, and the entire apparatus she served existed to make sure that debt was never called?

She stubbed out the cigarette half-smoked. Sentiment was a luxury item and she had never been able to afford it either.

* * *

The requisition fight took place by telephone, in the garrison commander's office, with the commander present and pretending to review paperwork, and it lasted eleven minutes and cost Odile more than she would admit.

"The answer is no." Deputy Director Havel's voice came down the line thin and official, the way disapproval always sounded from the capital. "The helicopter allocation for your district is reserved for flood assessment. You are an archivist, Marchetti. You file things."

"What I file, Director, is evidence in an ongoing historical-records violation. Article Nine. Unregistered recovery of protected state documents."

"Article Nine covers theft from archives. A chest in a riverbed is not an archive."

"It was an archive," Odile said. "In 1941 it was the provincial registry annex at Vhalgrad, and it was being evacuated when the column was destroyed at the Greywater crossing. Which means the contents are state property under continuous custody law, and the man carrying them up the Karst Escarpment is in violation, and every hour we spend debating jurisdiction is an hour closer to the snows."

A pause on the line. She could hear Havel deciding how much he wanted to know.

"Why the helicopter?" he asked at last. Too casually.

"Because the passes close in days and the ground route takes a week."

"Because," Havel said, "if this goes badly, I need to know whether the Ministry sent an archivist to the Karst Escarpment, or whether Odile Marchetti went hiking. There's a difference in how the newspapers phrase it."

"There's no newspaper story, Director. There's a recovered document and a receipt."

"Then you won't need a helicopter."

She closed her eyes. The garrison commander across the desk had stopped pretending to read and was watching her with the frank interest of a man enjoying theater he was glad not to be in.

"Director," she said, evenly, "I have served the Bureau for nineteen years without a single irregularity. I am asking for one. And I am telling you, as the person who actually reads what comes across my desk, that the contents of that chest do not stay buried. Someone else is already looking for it—I have confirmation of at least two private operatives in the field, unaffiliated with any agency I can identify. If they reach it first, the story that reaches the newspapers will not be about an archivist. It will be about what was in the chest. And then no one in this building will be able to say they didn't know."

Another pause. Longer. When Havel spoke again, his voice had changed—not softer, but warier, the voice of a man stepping carefully in a room where he suspects the floor is painted over a hole.

"What was in the chest, Marchetti?"

"Dispatches," she said. "Sealed. And a succession writ."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow loading a roof.

"Requisition denied," Havel said finally. "Officially. Unofficially—the garrison commander has discretionary authority over flood-assessment flights, and I am instructed to remind you that the Ministry values deniability above efficiency. Do you understand me?"

Perfectly. Deniability meant he believed her. Belief, in the ministry, was the one thing you never admitted in writing.

"I understand completely, Director."

She hung up. The garrison commander slid a flight authorization form across the desk without being asked, already signed, already dated yesterday.

"Flood assessment," he said, deadpan. "Upper valley. Weather window closes tomorrow evening, if you're flying."

"Tomorrow morning," Odile said. "First light."

* * *

That night she sat in her inn room with the door locked and did the thing she had come all this way refusing to do. She built a fire in the little iron stove, took from her case a slim bound volume—the Vhalorian civil registry, 1938–1944, her own working copy, annotated in her own hand over nineteen years—and fed it to the flames page by page.

It was procedure. That was what she told herself. The registry was a redundancy; the master copy sat in the capital vault; destroying a working duplicate was standard counter-intelligence hygiene when an operation went active. Page by page: births, marriages, deaths, the whole administrative skeleton of a murdered country curling and going orange and coming apart into ash. Ilse Vane's entry went early—she watched the ink bloom brown, then black, then nothing. *Fled the province, final year. Disposition unknown.*

Disposition unknown. Forty years, and the republic had never known, because the monks at Saint Aldric's had known and kept silent, and because a guide named Nystrom had carried a pregnant woman over the escarpment and never told anyone what he carried, and because Odile Marchetti's predecessors had chosen not to look too hard at the gaps.

She had burned registries before. Twice, in her career, on direct order, records that embarrassed the state. She had told herself then what she told herself now: paper is not truth; burning paper does not burn what happened. But her hands were steady in a way that frightened her more than shaking would have, because it meant she was getting good at this, and a person who gets good at burning history is a person the history will eventually come for.

The last page she held back. The final leaf, the marginal note in some long-dead registrar's hand: *Child born on the far side of the mountains, spring of the last year. Mother registered under the name Vane. Father's name withheld.*

She held it over the stove until the heat climbed her wrist. Then she folded it small, and put it inside her glove, and buttoned the glove shut.

Her superiors wanted deniability. Fine. She would give them deniability. But certainty—that was hers. Certainty was the one thing she had ever wanted from the job, the reason she had become an archivist at all: somewhere, under all the ash and the filing and the lies, there was a true record, and someone had to read it.

Outside, far up the valley, thunder muttered—or artillery, or just the escarpment talking to itself in the cold. The first snows were coming early. Everyone in this story was racing them now: a disgraced guide, two sets of hunters, and an archivist with a page hidden in her glove and a helicopter warming on the pad at first light.

Odile Marchetti packed her case, laid out her clothes, and slept four hours without dreaming—which she would later understand was the last easy night she would get for a very long time.

At dawn the mountain took her up into the clouds, and the clouds did not give her back quickly.
