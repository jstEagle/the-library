## 11. The Rope Bridge

The gorge announced itself an hour before we saw it.

We heard it first—a low, continuous grinding beneath the birdsong, like a millstone turning somewhere underground. Then the trees thinned and the ground simply fell away, and there it was: the Greywater's wilder cousin, a river that had cut itself a throat two hundred feet deep through black limestone, running white and furious with three days of storm-melt from the high country.

Emrys Nystrom had crossed this gorge eleven times in his life. He had never seen it like this.

"That's not crossable," Sable said flatly. She stood at the lip of the cut, wind pulling her hair sideways, and looked down at the water with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Nothing's crossable," I said. "Some things are crossable anyway."

She gave me a look that said she was keeping a ledger of my aphorisms and would present the bill later.

The bridge was where I remembered it—or where it had been, once. The old Vhalorian crossing sat at the narrowest point of the gorge, where the rock walls pinched together and the river compressed itself into something that sounded less like water and more like argument. Two iron rings were still bolted into the far cliff face, green with forty years of weather. Of the bridge itself—planked walkway, twin hand-cables, the work of monks or miners or whoever had needed to get across before the republic decided nobody should—there remained exactly one cable on our side, anchored into a bollard of stone, sagging out over the void and fraying into the white noise below.

I walked the anchor. Knelt. Put my hands on the cable where it entered the rock and read it the way you read rope: strand by strand. Three broken wires per lay, maybe four. Rust bloom at the socket. But the core was sound, and the anchor itself was set into living stone by people who'd expected their work to outlive them.

"It'll hold," I said.

"You're guessing."

"I'm reading. There's a difference." I stood, wiped rust from my palms. "We rig a tyrolean. Haul ourselves across hand over hand on the good cable, packs clipped to a second line. Nobody touches the planks because there are no planks. It's slow and it's ugly and it works."

"And if the cable fails mid-crossing?"

"Then we find out what the river thinks of us."

Sable looked at the water for a long moment. I watched her do it—not the way tourists look at rivers, hungry and afraid, but the way she looked at maps, cataloguing, measuring, deciding. Whatever she concluded, she kept it behind her teeth.

"Show me the harness knots," was all she said.

* * *

It took two hours to rig, and I spent both of them being quietly, professionally impressed and saying nothing about it.

She'd told me she could climb. Plenty of clients tell you that; what they mean is they once paid for a via ferrata in the Dolomites and someone held their hand. Sable Vane learned knots the way she apparently learned everything—fast, silently, and with an attention to detail that bordered on insult. She tied her figure-eights without being shown twice. She asked why I backed up the prusik instead of trusting the mechanical ascender, listened to the answer, and then did it my way without argument, which is rarer than talent.

"Your turn," I said, when her line was rigged and tensioned. "Feet on the cable, body under the haul line, pull with your arms, walk your feet backward along the wire. Don't look down."

"Everyone says don't look down."

"Because everyone looks down."

She went first, because she insisted, and because I'd learned in eight days of travel that arguing with her cost more time than it saved. She made the crossing in twelve minutes—slow, deliberate, her boots finding the cable like she was proofing a route on paper—and when she unclipped on the far side she didn't celebrate, just turned and braced herself against the second anchor ring to help me tend the line.

I sent the packs across. Then it was me.

I was thirty feet out over the gorge when the wind came.

It came off the glacier fields above us in one long exhale, the kind of katabatic gust that the escarpment produces when the afternoon sun finally gets its teeth into the ice, and it hit the cable and made it sing. Not sway—sing. A high, shivering note, and then a lateral oscillation that swung my boots off the wire and left me hanging full weight on my arms over two hundred feet of churning white.

Below me the river had found a new voice. Up close, at the speed of falling, it wasn't water anymore. It was a place. A destination. The kind of cold that doesn't kill you so much as erase the question of whether you were ever warm.

Don't look down.

I looked down. Habit. And in the half-second my eyes took the river's measure, my right boot found the cable again, and I hauled myself the last twenty feet on fury alone, and came over the lip of the far cliff breathing like a man who'd been underwater.

Sable was crouched beside the anchor, one hand still on the ring. "You stopped," she said. "Mid-span. For almost a minute."

"Gust."

"That wasn't a gust. That was you deciding something."

I started coiling rope so I wouldn't have to answer. She let me not answer, which was its own kind of conversation.

* * *

The trouble came an hour later, and it came from the one direction I hadn't rigged against: behind us.

We'd made camp on the far bank in a hollow of scree above the crossing, planning a dawn start up the goat track toward the high col. I was melting snow for tea. Sable was sketching—she sketched constantly, little topographic ghosts in a battered notebook, and I'd stopped asking what for. The light was going copper and long. It was, briefly, a good hour, the kind you don't trust in mountains because they exist only to be taken away.

The crack came from the near side of the gorge. A gunshot sound, sharp and mineral.

My head came up. "That was the anchor."

"What?"

"The near anchor—" I was already moving, scrambling to the gorge lip. Across the gap, in the dying light, I could see it: the stone bollard where the old cable had been socketed, and a fresh fracture running through it like a lightning scar. The day's heat, the day's cold, the singing cable's vibration—something had found the flaw in the rock, and the rock had answered. The cable's near end whipped loose and lashed once against the cliff face, and then the whole span slithered out over the void and fell, taking forty years of patience with it, and the river swallowed it without changing pitch.

Sable came up beside me. We stood looking at the empty gap between us and everything we'd come from.

"Well," she said. "At least no one can follow us."

"At least." But I wasn't thinking about pursuit. I was thinking about the fact that we now had one way forward and zero ways back, which has a way of clarifying a person's commitments, and about how the fracture in that bollard had been visible if you knew to look, and I hadn't looked, because I'd been reading the cable instead of the stone holding it. Forty years of reading mountains, and I'd checked the thread and ignored the needle.

"We'll go up the track at first light," I said. "It's fine."

"Is it?"

"No," I said, honestly, and something in her face flickered—almost approval, I thought, at the honesty. "But it will be."

* * *

It was not fine.

The goat track climbed away from the gorge and then, treacherously, returned to it—switchbacking down again two miles upstream to a place where the canyon walls leaned together and the old trail had once hopped across on stepping stones drowned now beneath six feet of meltwater. From the high bank we could see the alternative: a detour east that would add four days, four days we did not have, with the snowline already creeping down the peaks like a tide coming in.

"There," Sable said, pointing.

I followed her finger. Halfway along the leaning walls, maybe sixty feet above the flood, a fallen spruce bridged the gap—one of the great old trees from the near rim, uprooted in some past storm, its crown lodged against the far wall, its trunk spanning perhaps fifty feet of open air above the white water. It was grey with age. Its bark had gone to bare wood. It was, by every measure I knew, a death trap.

And it was also, by every measure I knew, the only way across that didn't cost us the season.

"We rope up," I heard myself say. "Individually. You cross last, after I've tested it."

"You'll test it by walking across it."

"I'll test it by walking across it carefully."

"Emrys." She used my name rarely enough that it landed like a dropped tool. "You've been careful this entire trip. It's the only thing you've been. So be careful, and let's go."

I went first. The trunk was worse than it looked—canted downhill toward the far side, slick with morning frost, crowned with stubs of snapped branches that caught at my ankles like hands. Halfway across, with the river roaring underneath close enough to throw spray into my face, I understood viscerally why they say you shouldn't look down, and I fixed my eyes on a knot in the wood three feet ahead and moved one foot at a time, and made the far side, and threw the rope back for her pack, and then for her.

She was ten feet out when the tree moved.

Not much. An inch, maybe two—the whole trunk flexing as her weight passed some hidden pivot point where the root plate balanced on the rim rock. But an inch is a lot when you're standing on a dead tree over a drowning river, and I saw her feel it, saw her weight shift wrong, saw her try to correct—

Her foot skated off the frost-slick bark.

She caught the trunk with both hands. Slid. Caught again. And then she was hanging from a fallen tree above the Greywater's angry cousin, her boots swinging over sixty feet of air and water moving fast enough to punch the breath out of a swimmer before it finished drowning them.

"Sable!" I was already on the trunk, crawling, the rope trailing uselessly behind me because there was nothing to anchor it to. "Hands! Keep your hands locked—I'm coming—"

"Don't—" Her voice cracked apart in the wind. "The tree—it moves—"

"I know. Stay on it."

I got to her in what felt like a year and was probably fifteen seconds. Her fingers were white, clawed into the bare wood, and her eyes when they found mine were the worst part—not panic, exactly, but the specific terror of a person who has always been in control of where her body goes and has just discovered the limits of that empire.

"On three, I lift, you swing your legs up. One—"

The tree shifted again.

I felt it this time, felt the whole span roll under us like a ship, and the world tilted, and the next things happened in the order they happen when everything happens at once: Sable's left hand tore free, her body swung out over the drop, her right hand held for one impossible second and then didn't, and she fell—not straight down, the river's updraft catching her coat, tipping her outward, out past the trunk, out over the white—

I went off the tree after her.

I want to be precise about this, because afterward, lying on the far bank coughing up half the Greywater watershed, I tried to reconstruct the decision and couldn't find one. There was no moment of choice. There was only the arithmetic my body did without consulting me: she would hit the water forty feet downstream of the tree, in the current seam, and the current seam runs to the gorge narrows, and the narrows are a meat grinder. Sixty feet of air. Water like concrete. And me, a disgraced guide with bad knees and a lifetime of swimming in glacial melt, diving off a log into the same equation.

The fall lasted longer than you'd think. Long enough to rotate. Long enough to see the water rise up white and total, and to think, absurdly, of my father's hands on a rope somewhere above me in the dark of forty years ago.

Then the river took us both.

Cold is a strange word for it. Cold is what you feel in your fingertips in October. This was a fist closing around my chest, a detonation inside the skull, the body's every system screaming one wordless syllable. I went down twelve feet, fifteen, the light above me smearing into chaos, and the current grabbed me like a dog shaking a rat and threw me sideways, and I remembered—some ancient training, some voice that was mine and also my father's—ball up. Protect the spine. Let it take you until it tells you where.

I surfaced. The roar was total. Somewhere ahead, a flash of color—her coat, her arm, her hand slapping the water. The seam was carrying us toward the narrows at the speed of a galloping horse.

There was a boulder field on the right margin. I'd marked it from above: a tongue of stones breaking the current, eddy water behind them. Fifty yards. In this water, fifty yards might as well have been fifty miles.

I swam anyway. Not swimming, really—fighting, angling, spending strength I didn't have against a river that regarded my efforts with something like amusement. My shoulder struck a submerged rock and spun me. I came up choking, found her again—closer, God, too close, the narrows' white fangs visible now ahead—and put my head down and swam the hardest twenty strokes of my life.

My hand closed on her collar.

The eddy took us both, slammed us against the boulder field, and I got an arm around her chest and my other hand jammed into a crack in the stone, and the river pulled, and I held, and the holding went on for a length of time I cannot measure and will not try to. At some point my grip began to fail. At some point I understood, with a strange floating clarity, that I was going to lose her and then lose myself, and that this was, in the great ledger of Emrys Nystrom's failures, not even the largest entry.

Then her hand shot up and locked onto a root—a living root, a spruce growing from the scree above the boulder field—and she screamed at me, a wordless raw sound, and screamed again, and her scream found my hands and dragged them upward after her, and we came out of the water the way wreckage comes out of the sea: slowly, in pieces, hauled by four shaking limbs and whatever it is that lives in a person deeper than strength.

We lay on the gravel bar above the floodline for a long time.

The sun came and went behind clouds. The river roared its one note. I was aware, distantly, of hypothermia writing its agenda in my muscles, and made myself sit up, made myself move, because sitting still beside a glacial river is how survivors become statistics. Sable was already moving—shaking, teeth chattering, but moving, stripping her soaked outer layers with clumsy hands, and I dug the emergency shelter and the stove from the drybag that had somehow stayed clipped to my harness, and we got a flame going, and the small blue circle of warmth became the entire world.

It was while she was wringing out her hair that she saw it.

I'd pulled my shirt off to change, and the light caught the scar—the long one, running from the top of my left shoulder down across the deltoid, silver-white and old, the kind of mark that comes from rope burn and rock in equal measure. She went very still.

"That's a rescue scar," she said.

"It's a scar."

"That's a *rescue* scar. Traction injury, rope under load across the shoulder." Her eyes came up to mine, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before—not suspicion, not calculation. Recognition. "I've read a report that describes that exact scar. Kestrel Pass. Twenty years ago. A boy carried down off the ice by his father's partner. The report notes the boy had a recent shoulder wound from the initial fall." Her voice was very quiet. "It noted it because the wound matched the father's. Same fall. Same rock. They were roped together when it happened."

The fire popped. Downstream, the river kept devouring the distance to the sea.

"My father didn't abandon anyone on Kestrel Pass," she said. "Did he."

Every road I had built out of that question—every deflection, every lie, every bitter half-truth I'd fed the world for twenty years—was underwater now, somewhere back in the gorge, spinning toward the narrows.

"No," I said. "He didn't."

She looked at me across the small fire, this woman who had hired me for my shame because my shame was useful, and I watched her decide—visibly, deliberately—not to ask anything else. Not yet. She wrapped the blanket tighter around her shoulders and said only:

"You went in after me. You didn't hesitate."

"Hesitating's how people drown."

"You nearly drowned."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Because the river doesn't care whose fault anything is," I said finally. "It just takes what's in it. Somebody has to be in the business of taking things back."

She was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, stripped of the cartographer's cool precision, carrying something rawer underneath.

"In the reports," she said, "they blamed your father for Kestrel Pass. Everyone did. The inquiry, the guilds, the families. And you've spent your whole life wearing the blame he left you, like it was inheritance." She shook her head slowly. "But men don't dive into floodwater for strangers unless somebody taught them what a guide is actually *for*. And that's not what they wrote about him. That's not what anybody wrote."

Above us, far up the valley, the evening light touched the high peaks, and I saw it—the first white breath of the season, fresh snow on the summit ridge, earlier than any almanac promised.

Neither of us said anything about it. We didn't have to. The mountain had just told us how much time we had left, and it was less than either of us had planned for.
