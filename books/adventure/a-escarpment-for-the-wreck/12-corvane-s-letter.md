## 12. Corvane's Letter

The cache was buried where the map said it would be, which was the first thing about the whole arrangement that bothered Doss Ferrin.

He'd been running contracts for eleven years, and in that time he had learned to distrust anything that arrived too easily. Good caches were hard to find. That was the point of caches. You buried them under a cairn that looked like every other cairn on the ridge, or you sank them in a lake with a bearing taken off two landmarks that wouldn't both exist after the next rockfall. You did not bury them forty paces off the goat track at the mouth of a valley so distinctive it could be identified from a child's drawing.

But there it was. A steel ammunition box, olive drab, no markings, tucked beneath a slab of limestone with a cairn of six stones stacked on top—neat, deliberate, freshly placed. The stones still had damp earth on their undersides.

"Fresh," said Pell, crouching beside it. He was the youngest of the four, all elbows and appetite, and he touched everything like a raccoon. "Somebody put this here this week."

"Somebody put it here for us," Doss said. "That's not the same thing."

He worked the lid open with his knife blade. Inside: dried food, two weeks' worth, good quality. Cartridges in the caliber they actually carried, which meant someone had read the manifest of their equipment closely enough to know what rifles they'd brought into a country where civilians weren't supposed to have them. A medical kit, better stocked than the one Doss carried himself. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, an envelope.

Doss held it up to the light. Heavy paper. A seal of dark green wax, unbroken, pressed with a device he didn't recognize—a stylized bird, maybe, or a key. The address line bore a single word: *Corvane*.

"That's addressed to him," Pell said. "Not us."

"I can see who it's addressed to."

"So we don't open it."

Doss turned the envelope over once, twice. The paper had weight to it, the kind of weight that meant money and consequence. Eleven years of contracts had taught him a simple taxonomy: clients who trusted you sent money; clients who didn't trust you sent instructions; and clients who were afraid sent both, plus a letter they didn't want you to read.

"We're three days behind schedule," Doss said. "We lost the trail at the river crossing and picked it up again by luck. Corvane's man told us the targets were moving slow because one of them is a woman carrying a pack she's not used to. Either that's wrong, or somebody's lying to us about how fast this is moving." He weighed the envelope in his palm. "I don't like operating blind in terrain like this. Blind men walk off cliffs."

"So radio him."

"The radio works when the weather lets it work, and the weather hasn't let it work since the gorge." Which was true, though not entirely the reason. The truth—the reason he'd carry in his chest like a stone for the next several days—was simpler: he wanted to know what was in the letter before he asked permission to know it.

He slit it open with his knife and read standing up, in the wind, while his three men watched him with the particular patience of people waiting to be told what they're doing and why.

* * *

The letter was written in a hand so controlled it looked printed. No salutation. No signature. Just instruction, laid out with the economy of someone who had given orders long enough to stop explaining them.

*The party consists of three. The woman is the priority. The guide is secondary but essential. Under no circumstances is Emrys Nystrom to be harmed. He is to be taken alive, unmarked, and delivered intact to the lowland garrison. If the woman dies in the course of recovery, acceptable. If the guide dies, the contract is void and the balance forfeit.*

Doss read it twice more.

Pell was watching his face. "Bad news?"

"It's specific," Doss said, which wasn't an answer, and Pell knew it wasn't an answer, and for once had the sense not to push.

He folded the letter away and spent the rest of the daylight doing what he always did at a cache: inventory, redistribution, maintenance. Boots checked. Ropes coiled and hung. The med kit split between packs so no single loss would cost them everything. It was ritual work, hands busy, mind free, and all the while the letter sat against his ribs like something swallowed wrong.

*Under no circumstances is Emrys Nystrom to be harmed.*

In eleven years, Doss Ferrin had been hired to do perhaps forty things, and the grammar of the contracts had never varied. Retrieve the object. Remove the obstacle. Silence the witness. Clients paid for outcomes, and outcomes required flexibility, and flexibility meant that names in the paperwork were usually just labels attached to problems. He had never—not once—seen a name singled out for protection. Protection was expensive. Protection was risk. A target you couldn't shoot was a target you had to chase, corner, subdue, and carry, and every one of those steps multiplied the ways the job could go sideways in country like this, above treeline, in autumn, with winter coming down the passes like a debt collector.

So why protect the guide?

Because the guide mattered more than the mission? No—the letter said the woman was the priority. Because the guide was valuable as a person? Nobody paid this kind of money to rescue anybody. Because the guide knew something?

That last one sat best, and sat worst. Doss turned it over while he checked the stitching on Pell's pack straps. A guide who knew something worth protecting—and worth more than the woman, apparently, since her death was *acceptable* and his was catastrophic. A washed-out mountain man, blacklisted in two countries, salvaging scrap off a riverbank. What could a man like that know that a man like Corvane couldn't buy from anyone else?

Unless it wasn't what he knew. Unless it was what he *was*.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a moraine wall, below the snowline but close enough to it that the wind came down off the ice smelling of iron. Doss took first watch because he never slept well the night after reading something he shouldn't have, and because first watch gave him hours alone with his thoughts, and his thoughts needed the room.

The fire was small and screened. Above the moraine, the sky had cleared for the first time in three days, and the stars came out hard and cold, the way they did at altitude—no haze, no mercy, just distance. The escarpment stood up against the sky to the north, a black saw-blade with snow already dusting the high teeth. Somewhere up in that darkness, three days ahead if the reports were right, a disgraced guide was leading a cartographer toward a monastery that officially didn't exist, carrying papers that officially had never been found, pursued by an archivist who officially wasn't operating.

And somewhere behind all of it, pulling strings with heavy paper and green wax, a man named Corvane who wanted the woman dead or alive and the guide alive or nothing.

Doss fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

He thought about the photograph again. It lived in the breast pocket of his jacket, where he kept the things he needed to check often: the picture of Emrys Nystrom, grainy, taken from above and behind, a man bent over a salvage line on a grey river. Corvane's lieutenant had handed it to him in a rented office that smelled of cigarette smoke and floor polish, along with the retainer and the route maps, and had said, *This one is dangerous,* in the tone of a man reciting lines he hadn't written.

Doss had looked at the photograph and seen a tired man in his forties with rope-scarred hands and the posture of someone who'd stopped expecting good weather. Dangerous. Sure. Any competent guide was dangerous in the mountains—that was the whole trade. But the lieutenant hadn't said it like a warning about competence. He'd said it like a warning about identity. Like the danger was in the name itself.

*Emrys Nystrom.*

He said it silently now, testing it against the dark, and felt again that faint snag he'd felt the first time, deep in the back of his mind, like a fishhook caught in cloth. He'd assumed it was nothing. Names echoed. After enough years and enough contracts, every name echoed with some other name, some other job, some other file.

But it kept snagging.

Behind him, the camp breathed. Pell snored with the total commitment of the young. Vess, the oldest of them, slept the way she did everything—efficiently, on her side, knife within reach. Rask was on second watch and would relieve him at midnight without being told twice.

Doss pulled out the photograph and held it near the fire, tilting it so the flame-light moved across the image instead of sitting flat on it. Light in motion sometimes gave up details that static light hid. An old interrogator's trick. He'd learned it from a man in Velka whose methods Doss had admired and whose morals he had not.

The light slid across the photo. Tired face. Grey stubble. A canvas jacket gone soft with age, sleeves pushed up. Forearms roped with old scars—one of them long, diagonal, running from the left shoulder down across the bicep, the kind of scar a man got from rock or ice, healed rough, decades old.

The fishhook set deeper.

A shoulder. A scar. A boy.

Doss sat very still, letting the memory come up on its own terms, the way you let a wild animal come to bait. Twenty years ago—no, twenty-one—he'd been working out of the northern valleys, younger, dumber, taking jobs that paid in cash and reputation. There had been a rockfall season, a bad one, the kind that loosened half a mountainside and everyone's nerve with it. And there had been a rescue—two rescues, actually, in the same week, on the same massif. The first was nothing: a shepherd's kid, a broken ankle, a litter carry down scree. The second—

The second had been a boy.

Kestrel Pass. The name surfaced with the taste of kerosene and cold smoke, because the valley below Kestrel had burned that summer, or part of it had, a lightning fire that filled the drainage with haze for days. A guided party had gone up into it and come apart—weather, rockfall, bad decisions, the usual alchemy of disaster—and when the wreckage was tallied, there were dead, and there was one boy alive, pinned in a collapse of stone near the shoulder of the pass, sixteen years old maybe, seventeen, with a gash opened down his left arm to the bone and his father missing somewhere above him in the rubble field.

Doss had gone up with two others. They'd dug the boy out by hand, through the night, working by headlamp in air thick with smoke, and the boy had never once cried out—not when they moved the stone, not when they splinted the arm, not when they carried him down eight hours of broken ground. He'd lain in the litter looking up at the smoke with an expression Doss had never forgotten and had never been able to describe: not shock, exactly. Something past shock. The look of a person who has understood, all at once and permanently, exactly what mountains are.

They'd never found the father.

The boy's name—Doss reached for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, carefully, so as not to scare it off. The rescue report had it. He'd signed the report himself. *Nystrom.* The family name had meant nothing then. A local name, common enough in the valleys, like a stone among stones.

But the boy's first name had been Emrys.

Doss lowered the photograph and looked at the fire for a long time.

It fit. The age fit—the boy would be thirty-eight now, and the man in the photo could be thirty-eight wearing fifty. The scar fit, if the long diagonal on the left arm was the same wound, and wounds like that didn't grow twice. The silence fit, too, God help him—the way the man in the photo carried himself, the way the file said he'd gone to pieces after Kestrel Pass, drinking, losing commissions, ending up blacklisted after some incident nobody would discuss in detail. Doss had read enough wrecked men to know the shape of one carrying a specific weight rather than a general one. This was a man carrying a pass. A specific pass, on a specific day, with a specific person left under the stones.

So the fishhook came free, and what it had caught was this: twenty-one years ago, Doss Ferrin had pulled Emrys Nystrom out of Kestrel Pass with his own hands, and now he was being paid to hunt that same man across the Karst Escarpment and deliver him—alive, unmarked, *intact*—to a garrison in the lowlands.

And the letter wanted him alive. Not for ransom. Ransom letters explained themselves. Not as a hostage—the woman was the priority, and hostages need the priority alive. Alive for some reason that a man who wrote in printed-looking script and sealed his letters in green wax considered too important to write down even in a private letter to himself.

Doss had a rule, older than any of his professional rules, and it was this: when powerful men want a specific ordinary man kept alive, the ordinary man is not the prize. The ordinary man is the *key*. And keys get used, and keys get copied, and when the lock turns, keys get thrown away—but only ever after they've been worn down to nothing doing it.

He didn't like being the hand that wore a key down.

He banked the fire, checked Rask's watch position with a tap on the shoulder, and lay down in his bag staring at the underside of the tarp. Sleep stayed away, patient as a creditor. His mind kept walking the same circuit: the neat cache, the fresh cairn, the cartridges in exactly the right caliber, the letter that told him everything except the one thing he needed. Somebody wanted this job done badly enough to make it easy, and easy, in Doss Ferrin's experience, was the most expensive thing a client could offer.

Toward morning he finally drifted, and dreamed without images—just the sensation of rope paying out through his hands, faster than he could control, and somewhere above him in the smoke a voice calling a name he couldn't make out, over and over, getting no answer.

* * *

They broke camp at grey light and climbed.

The route Corvane's maps showed cut east of the main track, up a hanging valley and over a shoulder of rotten rock that would let them bypass the monastery approach and come down on the narrows from above—an ambush position, textbook, the kind of ground Doss had chosen himself a dozen times. The maps were good. Too good. Contours accurate to the pace-count, water sources marked with seasonal notes, a spring flagged at the col that, when they reached it, was running exactly as described. Someone had walked this route recently. Recently, and thoroughly, and with an eye for exactly the questions a mercenary leader would ask.

Doss stood at the spring filling bottles and let the others rest, and he thought: *they've done this before.* Not this job—this *kind* of job. Corvane's people had run hunters through these mountains before, or planned to, or rehearsed it. The infrastructure was already here, waiting, like a trapline set in summer for a trapper who hadn't arrived yet.

Which raised the question he'd been circling since the cache: if Corvane had this kind of reach—maps, caches, calibers, timing—why hire outsiders at all? Why not use his own people, his own quiet men, the ones who'd walked these routes and buried these boxes?

Because his own people might be recognized. Because deniability ran outward, never inward. Or because whatever happened to Emrys Nystrom at the end of this job, Corvane wanted no man nearby who might one day be asked about it under pressure—and mercenaries, famously, could not be pressured about what they didn't know.

Fine. Doss could live with not knowing. He'd built a career on it.

What he couldn't live with was the arithmetic. He took out the letter one more time, sheltering it from the wind with his body, and read the sentence again: *If the guide dies, the contract is void and the balance forfeit.*

Void. Forfeit. Not *penalized*. Not *reduced*. Void—as in, the entire transaction unwinds, as in, the money stops mattering because the thing it was buying no longer exists. Men didn't structure contracts that way around assets. They structured them that way around irreplaceables. Around blood.

He put the letter away and looked north, up the valley, toward the high country where the escarpment's teeth held the early snow. Three days, maybe four, depending on the weather and the targets' pace. Enough time to do the job as written. Enough time, also, to learn why a washed-out guide with a twenty-one-year-old scar was worth more alive than a claimant worth killing.

"Doss." Vess, at his elbow, quiet. She nodded back down the valley, south, the way they'd come. Far off, at the limit of sight, a speck in the haze that might have been a bird and was moving wrong for a bird—steady, low, following the drainage. Then it was gone behind a spur.

"Aircraft?" Pell asked.

"Maybe." Doss shouldered his pack. "Could be our archivist friend finally got her helicopter."

"You believe in that?"

"I believe," Doss said, "that everybody in this story wants something, and none of them are telling us what." He set a bearing northeast, toward the col, toward the narrows, toward the place where the maps promised him his ambush and his instincts promised him something else entirely. "Move out. We're ahead of them, and I want to stay ahead of them until I understand what we're actually hunting."

They climbed. Behind them the spring ran on, cold and indifferent, marking a route someone else had measured for them, step by step, like a leash.

And in Doss's breast pocket, over his heart, the photograph rode in its fold of paper—the tired man with the scarred arm, the boy from the smoke, the key—and all day, mile after mile, Doss Ferrin found himself asking the question he already suspected he wouldn't like the answer to:

*Why does Corvane need the Nystrom boy alive?*
