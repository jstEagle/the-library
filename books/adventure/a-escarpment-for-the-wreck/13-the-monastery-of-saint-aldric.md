## 13. The Monastery of Saint Aldric

Sable saw it before Emrys pointed it out, which she counted as a small private victory.

They had been climbing since dawn through a country that seemed to have been assembled by a god with more ambition than patience—limestone fins, hanging meadows stitched to nothing, waterfalls that leapt off the escarpment and dissolved into mist before they ever reached a floor. Then the fog thinned for a moment above the tree line, and there it was: a pale wedge of masonry jutting from the cliff face like a splinter of bone, three hundred feet above the valley and six hundred below the rim.

"Saint Aldric," Emrys said. He said it the way other men said *landslide* or *cornice*—a word with weight in it. "Monks built it in the twelfth century. They wanted somewhere the world couldn't reach them."

"And the world obliged," Sable said.

"For four hundred years." He adjusted his pack straps and didn't look at her. "Then the republic found a use for it."

She had read what use. Everyone who worked with old maps had read fragments—the purge year, the ledgers, the trains that went east full and came back empty. The monastery had sheltered records then, documents smuggled up the goat track by monks who understood that paper could be killed as thoroughly as people. Some of those papers had survived. That was the whole reason they were here, the whole reason she had tracked down a disgraced guide with his father's compass and paid him money she couldn't really spare, and she had not yet told him the largest part of why.

The approach took the rest of the day. The final stretch was a stair cut directly into the rock, switchbacking up the cliff's shoulder, each step worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of sandals and boots. Emrys went first with the rope coiled over one shoulder, testing holds without appearing to test them, and Sable climbed behind him in the rhythm he'd beaten into her over nine days on the track: three points of contact, breathe on the step, never look at how far down it is until you've stopped moving.

She was better at it now than she'd been at the start. She knew he'd noticed. He hadn't said so, because saying so wasn't in his nature, but she'd caught the change in how he roped her—less like cargo, more like a partner. It was a strange thing to be proud of, competence on a cliff, but she had spent her life being competent at tables and drafting boards, and this was the first skill she'd earned with her hands bleeding.

The monastery door was iron over oak, black with age, set in a wall the color of old teeth. Emrys raised the iron ring and knocked—a pattern, she realized. Three, pause, two, pause, one. A knock with grammar in it.

For a long time, nothing. Wind moved through the junipers growing from cracks in the scree below. Somewhere inside the rock, water dripped with the patience of geology.

Then bolts began to move. One, two, a third lower down, and the door swung inward on darkness that smelled of tallow, stone dust, and something green—dried herbs hung in bundles from the lintel.

A monk stood in the gap. He was young, maybe twenty-five, with a tonsure that looked recently and reluctantly acquired, and he stared at Emrys with an expression Sable couldn't parse until she realized it was recognition—not of the man, but of the *kind*. Guides had come here before. His father's kind.

"You're expected," the monk said. Not a question.

"We're not," Emrys said. "But we need shelter, and we'll work for it."

The young monk's eyes flicked past them both, scanning the empty stair, the fog, the way a man checks a road for what might be following. Whatever he saw—or didn't see—seemed to decide him. He stepped back and pulled the door wide.

"The abbot will want to see you anyway," he said. "He always does, now."

*Now.* Sable filed the word away as they crossed the threshold and the door boomed shut behind them, bolts sliding home with a sound like a sentence being passed.

* * *

Inside, Saint Aldric was less a building than a condition of stone. Corridors had been carved from the living cliff and then clad in brick and plaster where the rock allowed; elsewhere the raw limestone simply became the wall, the ceiling, the floor, veined and glistening with seep. Lamps burned in niches every ten paces, and their light made the corridors feel like the inside of a throat. They passed a chapel where forty candles burned before a painted Virgin whose face had been deliberately defaced sometime long ago—scratched smooth by someone who feared icons or loved them too much—and a refectory where a dozen monks ate in silence, and neither Emrys nor Sable was offered food, which told her everything about how the visit would go before it began.

The abbot received them in a cell near the eastern face, where a window had been cut through sixty feet of rock to frame nothing but sky and the drop. He was ancient in the way that certain trees are ancient—not bent so much as *reduced*, pared down over decades to the essential structure. His beard was white and long. His eyes were the surprise: quick, dark, unclouded, the eyes of a much younger man installed behind a ruin of a face.

"You are Nystrom's son," he said to Emrys. No greeting. No offer of a chair.

Emrys went very still. "You knew my father?"

"I knew *of* him." The abbot lowered himself onto a stool with the economy of a man who had learned to spend no motion twice. "Forty years ago a guide came up this stair in the last week before the annexation, carrying a woman under a false name and papers sewn into her coat. He did not give his name. He did not have to. There is only one family of guides that knows our knock." His gaze shifted to Sable, and everything changed.

She felt it happen. She had spent her adult life being looked at and mostly *through*—the cartographer, the contractor, the woman with the neat hand and the neutral surname. People looked at her credentials, her maps, her invoices. This man looked at her *face*, and he looked at it the way Emrys looked at terrain: reading structure, reading history, reading what the surface was trying to conceal.

The abbot's lips moved. Something in the old Vhalorian tongue, soft, almost involuntary—a word, half prayer, half exclamation. And then his right hand rose and traced the cross: forehead, chest, left shoulder, right. An old rite, done slowly, done *at* her.

"What—" Sable started.

"Forgive me." He did not sound sorry. "You have your mother's face. I saw it once, across this room, and I was younger then and thought I would forget it." He folded his hands into his sleeves. "I have not forgotten it."

The silence stretched. Outside the window, the drop breathed its cold air into the cell. Sable was aware of Emrys beside her, aware that he had turned to look at her too, and she kept her own face still with the effort of a lifetime's practice while her pulse hammered against her collar.

"My mother is dead," she said. "Her name was Ilse Vane. She was a schoolteacher from a town that isn't on maps anymore."

"Ilse." The abbot closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he let them be wet without apology. "Yes. That was the name she gave us. Though when she prayed here, in this cell, on the night before my predecessor sent her down the south stair with two monks and a mule, she used another one." He paused. "She asked God's forgiveness for the lie. I have wondered for forty years whether it was a lie."

Sable's mouth had gone dry. "What other name?"

"That is not mine to say." He said it gently, but he said it like a door closing. "It may be written. Things were written, in the last days. My brothers carried the registry chests up from the valley on their backs, three trips each, through snow. Most of what they saved is gone now—sold, stolen, burned by men who came asking polite questions with impolite eyes. But some remains. Below. In the deep archive." His eyes went from her to Emrys and back again, and something in them sharpened, the dread surfacing at last through the wonder. "Which raises the question of why you have climbed nine days through autumn weather to ask me about it. Because you did not come here for shelter. Shelter-seekers do not carry a succession writ's description in their eyes."

"We came for records," Emrys said carefully. "Records concerning Ilse Vane."

"And who sent you?"

"No one," Sable said.

"Everyone," Emrys said, at the same moment, and the two answers collided in the small stone room.

The abbot studied them both for a long moment, and whatever he saw—the mismatch, the tension strung between them like wire—he apparently decided it was honest, because he sighed, a sound like wind through a crevasse, and rose from his stool.

"You will eat tonight," he said. "You will sleep. You will confess nothing further and I will ask nothing further, because walls have heard things in this place that got men killed, and I am too old to be careful and too stubborn to be careless." At the door he stopped, one hand on the iron ring, and looked back at Sable with an expression she would carry up the mountain with her for the rest of her life—grief and awe and fear braided together, the face of a man looking at a ghost that had walked out of a locked room.

"When you sleep," he said, "listen for the bells. We ring them at midnight and again at four. If you hear them rung at any other hour, do not ask why. Take the south stair and do not stop."

* * *

They were given a cell with two pallets, a jug of water, and a lamp with perhaps an hour of oil in it. The window was a slit, and through it the valley lights were coming on far below, tiny and orderly, the republic arranging itself for the evening.

Sable sat on her pallet and discovered her hands were shaking.

Nine days she had held this together. Nine days of lying by omission to a man whose father had died for her mother's secret, a fact she had suspected since the locket and confirmed in the burned village, and had said nothing, because the truth was a bridge and she needed him to keep crossing it with her. Now an old monk had looked at her face and crossed himself, and the omission had grown a second story, and she could feel the whole structure beginning to groan.

"He knew her," Emrys said quietly. He was sitting across the cell, elbows on knees, staring at the floor. "Your mother. He knew her, and he knew my father brought her here, and he's known for forty years." He looked up. "And the first thing he did was look at *you* like he'd seen a resurrection."

"People see resemblances," Sable said. "I have her coloring. It's—"

"Sable." He said her name flatly, without heat, and somehow that was worse than anger. "I've guided enough clients to know when one is managing me. You've been managing me since the road. Every question you've asked about my father has been a question about something else. So I'm going to ask you once, plainly, and you can lie to me if you want, but understand that I will know." He leaned forward. "What did the abbot see?"

The lamp guttered. Down the corridor, a bell began to toll—not midnight, not four. Just a single stroke, the evening bell, calling the brothers to compline, and Sable seized the delay like a drowning woman seizing driftwood.

"It's the office," she said, standing. "We should—we should hear it. It's been forty years since anyone sang Vhalorian rites in this building. That matters. That matters more than—"

Than the truth. Than the locket. Than the name she had carried half her life like a stone in her boot. Than the register in the burned village with *VANE* written in a child's careful hand, which she had pocketed and never mentioned.

Emrys rose too. For a moment she thought he would press it, and she braced herself for the fracture she had been dreading since the rope bridge, since before the rope bridge, since the day she'd put money in a washed-out guide's hand and watched him decide to take it.

But he only looked at her—long, level, unreadable—and said, "After the office, then. Tomorrow. Before we go down into whatever's under this rock."

"Tomorrow," she agreed, and hated how relieved her voice sounded.

They joined the monks in the chapel, standing at the back among shadows older than the republic. The singing was thin—too few voices, too many years—but the language was Vhalorian, the plainsong rising and falling in modes Sable had never been taught and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a lullaby you don't remember hearing. Her mother had hummed it. All her life, working at her maps, humming tunes with no names, and Sable had never once asked where they came from, because asking questions about her mother's past was the one household rule that had never needed to be spoken aloud.

She stood in the dark and listened to a dead nation sing, and wept silently, and was glad of the dark.

* * *

She woke at some black hour to voices.

Not the bells. Voices, low and urgent, in the corridor outside their cell, and beneath them a sound that did not belong in a monastery at all: the scrape of iron, many-footed, on the outer stair.

She was on her feet before she was fully awake, heart slamming. Through the slit window the valley lay dark—but high above, on the escarpment rim, a light was moving. Not a lamp. Too steady, too white. Sweeping along the ridge line the way a man sweeps a room he intends to search.

In the corridor, the abbot's voice, cracked with age and something else: "How many? ... Then wake the brothers. South stair. *All of them.*"

And then, closer, almost at their door, the young monk from the gate, whispering with terrible urgency:

"They're coming up the cliff path. And they're asking for the Nystrom boy by name."
