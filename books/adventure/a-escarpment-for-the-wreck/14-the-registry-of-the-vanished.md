## 14. The Registry of the Vanished

The archive was a throat cut into the cliff.

That was Emrys's first thought, standing in the antechamber while the abbot's key turned three locks older than the republic itself: they had hidden their memory inside the mountain the way a man hides a knife in his boot—close to the body, where no one would think to look. The passage beyond the door was narrow enough that his shoulders brushed both walls, and cold enough that his breath came out in ghosts. Lantern light slid along stone scored with chisel marks, and somewhere below them, so faint he might have imagined it, water moved through the rock like something breathing in its sleep.

Sable walked ahead of him with her lantern held high, and the light caught her face at angles that kept reminding him of someone. He had been noticing it for two days now and refusing to finish the thought. The jaw. The way she tilted her head when reading, exactly the way Ilse Vane must have tilted hers, if the miniature portrait in the locket was anything to go by—and it was; he'd seen it by firelight on the third night, before she'd snapped the case shut as though burned.

"You feel that?" she said quietly.

He did. A draft, steady and cold, rising from below. Moving air meant an opening to the outside somewhere beneath them, which meant the monks had built this place to breathe—which meant it could also be found. Forty years of erasure, and the mountain still remembered how to exhale.

The abbot went first, ancient and unhurried, his lamp throwing his shadow huge against the wall ahead of them like a second, larger man leading the way down. Brother Anselm, he had called himself, though Emrys doubted it was the name he was born with. His eyes were the problem. He had looked at Sable in the gatehouse for one long moment and crossed himself with the mechanical speed of a reflex, and since then he had not stopped watching her sideways, the way a man watches weather coming over a ridge he has to cross anyway.

The stairs went down farther than Emrys liked. Two hundred steps, then a landing, then more stairs, the air thickening with the smell of paper and tallow and cold stone. At the bottom the passage opened into a chamber that stole the words out of his mouth.

It was not a room. It was a vein.

The monks had hollowed a natural cavity and lined it with shelving carved directly into the living rock, tier upon tier rising into darkness above the reach of the lamplight. Thousands of documents lay wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen, stacked in niches labeled in a script Emrys half-recognized—the old Vhalorian hand, the letters that had been beaten out of schoolchildren within a decade of the annexation. Dust hung in the lantern light like snow that had forgotten which way was down.

"They brought what they could carry," Anselm said. His voice was dry as the paper around them. "In the last winter. Before the soldiers came up the valley road. Eleven monks made forty-one trips from the capital. Three did not come back from the last one." He said it without drama, the way he might report the depth of a well. "We have kept them. Not read them. There is a difference, and it has cost us."

"Cost you what?" Sable asked.

"The friendship of every government since." The abbot set his lamp on a ledge. "The republic offered us a stipend in the early years. All we had to do was surrender the collection. My predecessor refused. We have been poor ever since." Something that was almost a smile moved through the wrinkles. "Poverty is a kind of honesty."

Emrys set his own lantern down beside the chest they had carried the whole way—the lead-lined chest from the Greywater, its double-eagle seal cracked but intact. It looked smaller here, among the thousands of its kin. He had spent weeks believing this chest was the treasure. Standing in the vein, he understood it was only a single scale off a much larger animal.

"The writ," Sable said. Her voice had gone careful, flattened, the voice of a woman holding herself very still on loose ground. "The succession writ names 'the child of Ilse Vane.' If there's a registry—if there's any record of who sheltered her, where she went—"

"Then it will be in the Registry of the Vanished." Anselm lifted his lamp and gestured toward the deepest wall, where the niches grew smaller and more densely packed. "Every soul the purge erased. Every name struck from the civil rolls. The monks wrote them back in. That was the whole work, in the end. Not resistance with guns. Resistance with ink."

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the day died somewhere far above them.

Anselm knew the catalog the way Emrys knew a route—by feel, by years, by the accumulated instinct of a lifetime. He moved along the shelves pulling bundles without hesitation, calling out dates and categories in a low monotone. Sable unwrapped each bundle with hands that trembled only slightly, laying the documents flat on a reading table worn smooth by generations of other desperate fingers.

Emrys stood back and let them work, and told himself the cold in his gut was just the mountain.

He had known, in the way a man knows things he refuses to look at directly, that the writ was dangerous. He had known since the night he pried the chest open alone in a rented room that smelled of river rot, since the moment he matched the wax seal against the scar on his compass case—his father's compass case, the one thing of value Aldric Nystrom had left behind besides debts and a reputation. He had told himself it was coincidence. Wax seals were stamped by the thousand. Brass fittings wore the same eagles. The world was full of matching marks that meant nothing.

But the registry would not be coincidence. The registry would be a list.

"Here." Anselm's voice changed. "Registry of the Vanished. Final year entries."

Sable came to stand beside him. Emrys made himself move forward too, one step, then another, until he stood at the edge of the lamplight like a man at the lip of a cornice testing whether it would hold.

The bundle Anselm laid open was bound in black linen, and inside, the pages were dense with columns of names written in a small, disciplined hand. Each entry followed the same form: name, last known residence, date of disappearance or execution, and then—a final column Emrys hadn't expected—a line of testimony. *Seen crossing at Marrow Ford. Sheltered at the millhouse below Tarn Veil. Taken on the road east.* The monks had recorded not just who vanished, but the last flicker of them, the final sighting, the way a lighthouse logs a ship going down.

"Ilse Vane," Sable said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she steadied it. "Find Ilse Vane."

Anselm ran a finger down the column, past names that meant nothing to anyone anymore, and stopped.

"She is here." He turned the register toward the light. "Ilse Vane. Last residence, the Winter Palace quarter. Struck from the rolls in the spring of the final year. And the testimony—" he bent closer, lips moving, and then went still.

"What?" Emrys heard himself say. "What does it say?"

The abbot didn't answer him. He was looking at Sable, and his face had gone strange—not shocked, not frightened, but *confirmed*, the way a surveyor looks when two bearings finally cross.

"It says," Anselm said slowly, "*Fled south in the company of a guide. Pregnant.*"

The word dropped into the chamber and rang there.

Sable's hand came up to her collarbone, to the chain under her shirt, to the locket she had carried across half a country and never once explained. She didn't speak. Her mouth worked once, twice, and what came out was barely sound. "Pregnant."

"There is more," Anselm said gently. "A marginal note, added later, in a different hand. *The guide's name is recorded in the companion volume. Cross-reference Nystrom.*"

The chamber did not tilt. Emrys knew that it did not tilt because he could see the shelves standing perfectly still, the dust hanging motionless, the flames of the lamps burning straight and calm. Everything in the room held its position with perfect discipline except the floor of the world, which opened under him without disturbing a single object on its surface.

"Nystrom," he said.

His own voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing very far away.

"That is your name," Anselm said. It was not a question. He had known since the gatehouse. Perhaps since before the gatehouse. Perhaps the monks had waited forty years for a Nystrom to walk down these stairs, and the crossing of himself had been less a blessing than a salute.

"My father's name." Emrys's mouth was dry. "My father was a guide. On the escarpment. Before—" Before Kestrel Pass. Before the disaster. Before the blacklist that had eaten his life like rust eats iron, slow and total, until there was nothing left to hold weight. "There were hundreds of guides. It's a common enough name in the valleys. It doesn't mean—"

"Companion volume," Anselm said, already moving.

* * *

The companion volume lived in a niche behind an iron plate, and it was thinner than Emrys expected, and heavier than anything he had ever carried.

Anselm set it on the table and did not open it. He stepped back. Later, Emrys would understand why—the old monk had spent his life guarding other people's inheritances, and he knew better than anyone that some doors had to be opened by the person they were waiting for.

Sable reached out and touched the cover, then withdrew her hand and looked at Emrys. "Together," she said.

He nodded, or thought he did.

The pages inside were not a registry. They were a narrative—one account, continuous, written across decades in at least four different hands, the earliest entries faded almost to brown shadow. Anselm adjusted the lamp, and Emrys leaned in, and the past reached up out of the page and took him by the throat.

*In the winter of the final year,* the oldest entry began, *a woman came to the monastery by night, guided across the high country by a man of the valley routes. She gave no title. The brothers knew her anyway; her face was on every wall in the capital. She was five months gone with child.*

Emrys read it again. Read it a third time. The letters refused to rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

*She asked for sanctuary and for silence. The guide argued for taking her farther south, beyond the republic's reach entirely, but the passes were closing and her strength was failing. He stayed three days. When he left, he carried with him certain papers entrusted to him by the household—documents the family wished preserved against the burning. He swore to return for the woman when the thaw came.*

*He did not return.*

*The soldiers came instead.*

Emrys's hands were flat on the table. He watched them as though they belonged to a stranger. They were shaking.

"He brought them here," Sable whispered. She was reading over his shoulder, and her breath was warm against his ear, and none of it registered. "The papers. Your father carried the papers out of the capital and brought them here. That's—that's the chest, Emrys. That's what was in the river. The chest was going *here*. It was always going here."

*The guide was taken on his next crossing,* the record continued, in a newer, harder hand. *He was questioned for nine days. What passed between him and Colonel Corvane's interrogators is not recorded. What is recorded is this: the woman and her child were released. Unharmed. Allowed to disappear into the southern provinces with new names. In exchange for—*

The entry stopped. Someone had scraped it away with a blade, leaving a shallow gouge in the parchment, and beneath the gouge a later hand had written a single line:

*In exchange for his silence, and for the child's erasure. The guide bought their lives with their history. May God judge whether it was treason or love.*

Corvane.

The name hit Emrys like a fall into a crevasse—no warning, no bottom visible, just the sudden absence of everything solid. Corvane. The name Doss Ferrin had let slip around a supply cache fire, careless as a man naming the weather. The name attached to mercenaries and sealed orders and a photograph of Emrys's own face. He had assumed it was about the chest. About money. About some rival claimant's greed.

It was not about the chest.

It had never been about the chest.

"He traded them," Emrys said. His voice came out sanded flat, stripped of everything. "He gave up the papers' location—or he gave up *her*—and they let her live. They let the child live. And everyone agreed to forget. The whole thing was buried, and my father—" His throat closed. He forced it open. "My father carried it for forty years. Alone. Knowing what he'd done. Knowing what he'd saved and what he'd sold."

"And then he tried to fix it," Sable said softly.

Emrys looked at her.

"Kestrel Pass." Her eyes were wet and steady. "Twenty-two years ago. Everyone says you were leading the party. But you weren't leading it, were you? You were sixteen. Your father was leading it. And he wasn't taking clients over Kestrel Pass in autumn for money—he was coming *here*, or coming from here. He was trying to reach this archive, or the cache, or whatever piece of it he still carried guilt about. And the pass took him, and the republic needed a reason for why a famous guide died on a closed route, and they needed his son to never come looking." She reached out and touched the register, lightly, the way you touch a grave marker. "So they blacklisted you. Not because you failed. Because you might *succeed*. Because you're the only person alive who knows his routes."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, wind moved over the monastery roof, and Emrys thought about all the winters he had spent hating a dead man for a disaster he hadn't caused, hating himself for surviving it, hating the name Nystrom every time a permit office slammed its window shut. Twenty years of shame with clean, simple architecture: a storm, a bad decision, bodies on the ice. He had built his whole ruined life inside that architecture.

And it had been load-bearing lies the entire time. The blacklist wasn't punishment. It was containment. He hadn't been disgraced. He had been *filed*—cross-referenced, locked away, a document too dangerous to burn and too dangerous to circulate, shelved in the dark like everything else in this room.

"The child," Anselm said quietly. He had withdrawn to the edge of the lamplight, giving them the privacy of distance. "The record says the child was born the following spring. In the south. The mother took a trade name—Vane was already too dangerous. The brothers lost track of them after that. Deliberately. Some mercies are safer unwitnessed." The old man's eyes moved to Sable, and there was no question in them at all, only a grief so old it had polished smooth. "But the locket you carry, child. The hair inside it. We kept a record of the relics the family entrusted to the guide. Would you like me to describe them?"

"No," Sable said.

Then, immediately, "Yes."

Anselm told her. A silver locket, oval, worked with a border of mountain ash leaves. Inside, a curl of hair tied with thread, and a miniature painted in the last peaceful year. Emrys watched Sable's hand close around the shape of it through her shirt, and watched twenty years of her own carefully constructed architecture—cartographer, drifter, woman with half a name—come down all at once, silently, the way snow sheds off a roof.

Neither of them spoke for a long time. The lamps burned. The dust hung. The dead waited in their shelves with the patience of people who had already survived the worst thing that could happen to them.

It was Sable who finally broke it, and her voice was rough but level. "Your father didn't betray them. Look at it again. Nine days of questioning, and they got *nothing*—not her location, not the child, not the cache. He gave them the one thing he could afford to lose: the papers' public existence. He let them believe the succession was buried so they'd stop hunting a pregnant woman. He traded a crown to save a life." She swallowed. "And then he spent the rest of his life trying to give the crown back, and it killed him on a mountain, and they made his son pay for it."

"They didn't make me pay," Emrys said. "They made me *disappear*. There's a difference, and I'm standing in it."

He turned back to the companion volume, because turning back to the volume was easier than standing in the open space where his identity used to be. The final entries were in the most recent hand—cramped, urgent, dated within months of the annexation's consolidation. He read them fast, hunting, and found what he was dreading:

*A copy of the succession writ was entrusted to the guide, sealed, to be delivered to the heir when she came of age. The guide reported it lost in the Kestrel Pass disaster. The brothers do not believe this. The brothers believe the guide hid it rather than deliver a weapon into a world not ready for it. Search of his effects after his death found nothing. The chest remains unaccounted for.*

Unaccounted for.

Floating in the Greywater forty years later, lead-lined, double-eagled, waiting for a bankrupt salvage diver with a blacklisted name and his father's compass.

"It was never lost," Emrys said. "It was in the river. The flood moved it. Or he hid it in the riverbed knowing the one place no archivist would ever search was moving water." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Forty years. It sat in the Greywater for forty years, and I pulled it out with my own hands because I needed brass fittings to sell for passage money."

"God has a sense of humor," Anselm said gravely. "It is simply that His jokes take decades to land."

Emrys pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. Behind the darkness, everything rearranged itself: the tavern keeper's face when he'd recognized him. Marchetti's agents tearing through his room with methodical purpose, searching not a thief's lodging but a *lead's* quarters. Doss Ferrin's photograph. *Taken alive.* The orders that had puzzled even the mercenaries. Everyone had known what he was before he did. He had been the last man in the country to learn his own name.

When he lowered his hands, Sable was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse—grief and wonder and something guarded underneath, something she was holding back, and he was too hollowed out to chase it.

"We should rest," Anselm said. "The mountain will keep until morning. It has kept this long."

* * *

They gave him a cell with a straw pallet and a blanket that smelled of cedar and time, and Emrys lay awake in it for hours, listening to the water breathe somewhere beneath the stone.

Sleep, when it finally took him, was shallow and full of the register's columns, names scrolling past like a river in flood. He woke before dawn to a sound he knew too well: boots on stone, many of them, and the low clipped cadence of men who had trained together.

He was out of the cell and moving before his mind finished the thought, down the corridor toward the gatehouse stair, and he met Sable coming the other way with her pack already shouldered and her face pale in the pre-dawn gloom.

"How many?" she breathed.

"At least six. Maybe more." He pressed his back to the cold wall. Below them, voices echoed up the stairwell—demands, in the accent of the lowland garrisons. And threaded through them, unmistakable even at a distance, the particular authority of a woman giving orders that others obeyed.

Marchetti. Here. Hours behind them, exactly as he'd feared—but the voices below were wrong for her alone. Too many. Too armed. And beneath the garrison accents, another voice, lower, rougher, one Emrys had heard across a supply-cache fire and never expected to hear again this far south.

Doss Ferrin was at the gate.

And he was not alone.
