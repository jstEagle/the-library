## 15. What My Father Carried

The abbot left them alone with the registry, and Emrys found he could not touch it.

It lay open on the reading table in the archive's cold heart, a ledger bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth at the corners, its pages the color of old teeth. Around them the shelves of Saint Aldric climbed into shadow—forty years of a nation smuggled into a monastery by monks who had committed the only crime that mattered anymore: remembering. Somewhere above them a bell rope creaked in wind. Sable stood on his left, very still, her hand resting on the edge of the table but not on the book, as if even she understood that what waited inside it was not information but a door, and doors only swing one way.

He touched the page.

The ink had faded to brown but the script was firm, monastic, unhurried. *Registry of the Vanished*, the title read, and beneath it, in a later hand: *Those who crossed. Those who were taken. Those who carried others.*

*Carried others.*

His eye went down the columns. Names, dates, routes. The purge had been efficient in the valleys and clumsy in the high country, and so the record of the escapees was really a record of guides—the men and women who knew the goat tracks and the ice chutes and the cols that didn't appear on any map worth trusting. He read their names like a roll call of ghosts. Some he recognized from legend. One he did not recognize at all, because he had spent his whole life refusing to look at it straight on.

*Nystrom, Aldric. Route: the Karst traverse, western approach. Party of two. Subject: Vane, Ilse. Departed [date obscured]. Status: intercepted below Kestrel Pass. Subject escaped westward. Guide detained.*

Guide detained.

Emrys heard his own breathing in the stone silence. Beside him, Sable had gone white, and she was looking not at the page but at him—at his face, as though she'd already known, as though she'd been carrying this knowledge for weeks the way he'd been carrying the chest, without opening it.

"Keep reading," she said quietly. "There's more."

* * *

He had been eleven years old the last time he saw his father pack for the high country.

The memory came back now with terrible clarity, the way drowned things come up all at once when you finally stop fighting the water. Their house below the Greywater, woodsmoke and wet wool. His father at the table by lamplight, checking rope with those thick careful hands, hands that could feel a frayed strand through leather gloves. Emrys had sat on the floor pretending to sleep, watching him coil line, because his father packing for a route was better than any story.

"Where this time?" he'd asked, and his father had smiled—not the easy smile, the other one, the one that meant the answer was complicated.

"West," he said. "High and west."

"Who's paying?"

"A lady." A pause, the lamp flame bending in some draft from under the door. "A lady who needs to be somewhere the maps don't go."

Emrys had thought nothing of it. Children think nothing of everything. His father went out before dawn three days later, and he kissed Emrys's mother on the forehead in the dark the way he always did, and Emrys learned years afterward—from a constable's report, from an inquest nobody attended—that Aldric Nystrom had been arrested eleven days after that departure, at a checkpoint below Kestrel Pass, in the company of a woman whose papers named her Ilse Vane and whose face, according to the single surviving photograph in the republic's files, matched no living citizen of any nation that existed.

They had held him four months. They had let him go.

That was the part Emrys had never understood, the part he had built his whole childhood around not asking. Guides caught running fugitives across a closed border did not get released. They got disappeared, or they got a trial, or they got a wall. His father came home thin and silent and took work on low routes, river salvage, anything below the treeline, and never once spoke of what had happened in those four months, and the family ate, and the family survived, and everyone agreed without saying so that this was the price and the payment both.

Silence bought their lives. Now, standing over the registry with forty years of dust settling through the lamplight, Emrys finally saw the transaction written out in full.

Because there was more. There was a second entry, appended years later in different ink, in the abbot's own hand by the look of it—a marginal note, the kind monks make when they cannot bear to leave a story unfinished:

*The guide returned once, in the winter of the great snows. He asked after the woman. We told him nothing; we had sworn nothing, but fear is its own oath. He asked instead if anything had been left in our keeping—for safekeeping, he said, against a day of return. We denied it. He looked at me a long time and said: then I will have to find where they put it myself. He went toward Kestrel Pass. The mountain kept him.*

The mountain kept him.

Kestrel Pass. The name that had followed Emrys his entire life like a debt collector. The disaster. Four climbers dead, the official report said, through the guide's negligence—through *Nystrom's* negligence, the report said, though the report had been written by men who had never been above the treeline, and every guide in two countries had smelled the wrongness of it and said nothing anyway, because saying something about a Nystrom was bad business. His father had died up there trying to reach whatever the monks had hidden, or trying to reach the woman, or trying to reach both—and the state had needed the death explained in a way that ended questions, so they had written *negligence* and blacklisted the name and let the mountain take the blame alongside the man.

And then, twenty years later, they had done it again. To the son.

Emrys sat down slowly on the cold stone bench, because his legs had informed him they were no longer interested in holding him upright.

* * *

The Kestrel Pass inquiry. He had lived inside that word—inquiry—for six years, and he had never once examined it, because examining it meant admitting it might have been designed.

He remembered the details the way you remember a scar: not the cutting, just the shape. The client, a mining consortium man named Vell, who had hired him through intermediaries and paid too well and asked strange questions about routes. The storm that arrived early—too early, days ahead of every forecast, and Emrys had said so, had put it in writing in the route log, and the route log had vanished between the pass and the inquiry. The fixed lines that had been cut. Not frayed. Cut. He had told the investigators that too, and watched them write down *equipment failure*. The client, Vell, who had turned back at the last camp citing a bad knee and who had never, in all the months of testimony, been asked a single hard question by anyone.

He had come out of that inquiry a murderer in the eyes of the trade, blacklisted in two countries, unemployable past the valley roads, and he had swallowed it because there was nothing else to do with a thing that size. You swallow stones or you choke on them.

But now—

Now he laid the pieces side by side on the table of himself and looked at what they made. A father who had run the heir of Vhaloria over the escarpment and been caught and released, which meant he had traded something, and the only thing he had to trade was silence. A father who had spent twenty years paying that silence back in small coin—low routes, no questions, a boy raised to love mountains but steered gently, always gently, away from the high western country. A father who had finally broken, in the winter of the great snows, and gone looking for what the monks had hidden, and died for it. And a son who had then been handed the same ruin, engineered with the same patience, by people who understood that the chest would surface someday—that rivers give things back, that monks die and talk, that paper outlives oaths—and who had made sure, decades in advance, that the one man alive with his father's route knowledge and his father's blood would be ruined, isolated, discredited. Unbelievable. So that when he found it, no one would believe him.

Or worse. So that when he found it, he would be easy to discredit.

Vell. He said the name aloud in the cold archive and it tasted like copper. He had spent six years hating that man's bad knee, and now he understood the knee had probably been fine, that Vell had been Corvane's instrument or Corvane's creature, that the whole disaster had been a scalpel job dressed as weather. They hadn't killed him. Killing him would have made a martyr and a mystery. They had done something crueler—they had made him worthless, and then they had waited for the current to bring the chest to him, knowing that a ruined man's discovery is a rumor, and a rumor can be smothered.

Rage came up in him like meltwater under a glacier—slow, pressureless, and absolutely unstoppable. It did not make him shake. It made him very calm, which frightened him more.

"They took my name," he said. His voice sounded foreign in the archive, flat and small among all those preserved voices. "Not my life. My *name*. Twenty years ahead of time, they took my name so that when I found this—" he put his palm flat on the registry, "—I'd already be a liar."

Sable sat down beside him. She didn't touch him. She was learning him, he realized; she knew by now that in the first minutes after a collapse you don't put weight on the snowpack.

"I suspected," she said. "About your father. When I saw the seal match the scar on your compass case—I suspected. I've suspected since the road."

"I know," he said. And he did know, and it still hurt, and that was a problem for a colder hour. "Tell me the rest later. Right now tell me what your mother told you. Everything. Because my father carried her over this escarpment, and then he carried her secret until it killed him, and I have been carrying his silence my whole life without knowing it was loaded."

* * *

She told him. All of it—the locket, the half-name, the childhood of moving towns, the mother who drew maps of places she refused to name and burned them each night. And Emrys listened and felt the vertigo of a man whose ground has turned out to be a slope: everything he had grieved, he had grieved wrongly. He had hated his father a little, in the secret place boys keep such hatred, for dying stupidly on a mountain, for leaving behind a stained name like a bad inheritance. And the truth was the opposite thing entirely. His father had died mid-payment. Had died going back for the last of it, the paper proof, the thing that would have freed Ilse Vane to stop running and freed the Nystroms to stop being silent. The disaster wasn't a disaster. It was a delivery attempt.

He laughed, once, and it came out wrong, and Sable looked at him with alarm.

"He carved markers," Emrys said. "On the western route. I've seen them my whole career and thought they were mine-work survey marks—cairns stacked wrong, arrows chiseled at odd angles, a piton rusting out of a wall where no sane line goes. I catalogued them once, for a permit application. They rejected the application. They rejected *me*." He pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "He was marking the way in. Forty years ago. For whoever came after. For—" he stopped, because the next word was *me*, and saying it felt like stepping off a cornice.

For me.

Outside, the light was failing against the cliff face, turning the hanging monastery's windows to orange coins. Somewhere below, in the lowlands, Marchetti was climbing. Somewhere south, Doss Ferrin and his rifles were climbing faster. And somewhere in the high white country above them, along a route no living guide had ever completed, his father's markers waited in the ice like a hand extended across forty years.

Emrys stood. The rage was still there—he could feel it banked in his chest like a live coal wrapped in wool—but layered over it now was something worse and better: purpose with a body count attached. He knew now why the blacklist existed. He knew now who had built it. And he knew that Corvane's people had spent two decades making sure a Nystrom would be too broken to follow this trail—which meant they believed the trail could be followed.

Which meant it could.

"We leave before dawn," he said. "West approach. My father's line."

Sable searched his face. "The route that killed him."

"The route they *made* kill him." He began rolling the registry closed, wrapping it in oilcloth with hands that had finally stopped shaking. "There's a difference. It took me thirty years to see it. I'm not planning to die proving it—but I'm done letting other men write the report."

Above them, in the tower, the old abbot began to ring the evening bell, and the sound rolled down the escarpment like a warning being passed hand to hand through the dark.
