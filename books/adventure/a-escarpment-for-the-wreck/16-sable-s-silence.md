## 16. Sable's Silence

The monastery gave them a cell with two pallets and a window the width of a prayer book, and Sable lay awake in it listening to Emrys not sleep.

He hadn't spoken since they'd come down from the archive. Not at supper, where the abbot's bread sat untouched on his plate. Not when she'd wished him good night through the door of his thoughts. He had taken the lamp to the far corner of the cell and sat with his father's compass case open on his knees, and he had not slept, and neither had she, and the silence between them had grown a texture—dense, mineral, like the rock under the monastery floor.

She should have told him.

She had known for weeks. She had known since the burned village, since her finger stopped on *Vane* in a child's register and she'd closed the book and said nothing while he banked the fire three feet away. She had known longer than that, if she was honest—and honesty was the tax this room was going to collect from her tonight, she could feel it coming up the stairwell with his footsteps—she had known since the locket. Since the hair inside it, dark as her own, and the half-name her mother had given her like a coin bitten to test its metal: *If anyone ever asks about Vhaloria, don't answer.*

Her mother had never once asked her not to answer.

Sable turned onto her side. The window showed a blade of sky going grey over the escarpment, the high country they still had to cross, and somewhere out there Odile Marchetti was climbing behind them with the patience of a woman who read ashes, and somewhere behind *her* were men paid to leave no witnesses. And here, in a stone cell that smelled of tallow and old incense, was the one person alive who could get them across the Karst before the snows—the son of the man who had carried her mother over it.

*That* was why she'd found him. That was the truth she'd dressed up as coincidence on the road, wrapped in an offer of money and permits and a reason, because a man like Emrys Nystrom would never have taken the job if she'd said what she actually wanted: *You are the map. Your father drew you into it without knowing.*

His footsteps crossed the cell. Stopped.

"You knew," Emrys said.

Not a question. His voice was rough with the whole night in it, and quiet, which was worse than shouting. Quiet meant he'd had hours to decide how to say it.

Sable sat up. There was no point pretending to wake. "Yes."

The word fell into the dark and did not break anything, which surprised her. She'd expected shattering. Instead there was only the sound of him breathing, controlled, the way he breathed on bad ground.

"The locket," he said. "In the chest. You saw it before I ever met you."

"Rumors of the chest reached me in Corivo. A salvage man pulling Vhalorian iron out of the Greywater." She kept her voice level because her hands wouldn't be. "I came to see for myself."

"And the writ named the child of Ilse Vane, and you—" He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "You're a cartographer. You redraw borders. You know every extinct name on every old survey. Ilse Vane wasn't hard to find in the records, was she. A woman who fled east in the last year. Married name Vane. Daughter born the following spring."

"No," Sable said. "It wasn't the records."

"Then what?"

"My mother told me stories." She heard how it sounded even as she said it—thin, evasive—and hated herself for it, and made herself go on anyway. "Bedtime stories about a country that didn't exist anymore. About a valley with a white monastery on a cliff. About a guide who carried her over the escarpment when soldiers were burning the passes behind them. She never said his name. She called him *the man who owed nothing*, which I thought was a compliment until I was old enough to understand it was the opposite."

Emrys struck a match and lit the lamp. The light came up yellow and unflattering, and she watched him take inventory of her face the way he took inventory of a slope—reading the load, testing for hollow places.

"She told you about my father," he said.

"She told me about a guide. It took me years to connect the guide to the name Nystrom, and when I did, I found a disgraced mountain man blacklisted in two countries, drinking his way down the Greywater salvage lines." She held his eyes. "And I hired him."

"Why?"

"Because you were the only person alive who might know your father's route."

"Don't dress it." His voice didn't rise but something under it did, pressure against stone. "You didn't hire me for my charm. Say the rest of it."

Say the rest of it. Fine. She had spent thirty years saying less than the whole thing; she could spend one night saying more.

"The rest of it," she said, "is that I needed a guide whose father had walked my mother over that escarpment, because I believed—I still believe—that route exists in no archive, on no map, in no head but yours. Buried things survive in bodies, Emrys. That's the whole science of what I do. Borders die on paper and live in footpaths. My mother's country died forty years ago and lives in a bedtime story and a locket and—" she gestured at the compass case in his lap, "—in whatever your father carved into the rock before Kestrel Pass killed him. I couldn't ask the archives. The archives burn. So I asked you."

"You used me."

"Yes."

The word landed between them and stayed there, honest and ugly, and she refused to flinch from it because flinching would make it worse. He set the compass case down on the pallet with exaggerated care, the way he handled everything when he was angriest, and stood.

"How long?" he said. "Since the road? Since the tavern? Or since the village—since you found her name in that school register and looked right through me?"

"The road," Sable said. "Mostly. I suspected at the tavern. I *knew* at the village."

"At the village." He nodded slowly, twice, a man confirming a fracture line he'd already mapped. "I told you about Kestrel Pass at the village. I lied to you about it, badly, and you sat there knowing—knowing what? Knowing my father died on that pass trying to reach whatever your family left in the hills? Knowing my whole disgrace might be built on top of it?"

"I didn't know that part."

"You didn't *ask* that part!" And now it did rise, finally, the pressure finding its vent, and the monk passing in the corridor outside paused and moved on. Emrys lowered his voice with visible effort. "Every day on that mountain I've handed you another piece of myself. The pass. The rescue scar. My father's habits, his cairns, the way I read weather because he taught me to read weather. And every day you took it and filed it and said nothing, because it was useful. Because I was useful."

"Yes," Sable said again. "All of that is true."

He stared at her. She understood, distantly, that most people in this conversation would have defended themselves by now—would have produced the mitigating circumstances, the fear, the loneliness, the fact that she'd been hunting her mother's ghost across half a continent since she was nineteen years old. She felt all of those things pressing at the back of her throat. She swallowed them.

Because he was right, and because a partnership built on softened truths would not survive the ice above them, and because her mother had taught her exactly one thing worth teaching: *a lie kept warm will hatch.*

"I tracked you for four months," she said. "I learned your routes, your debts, the shape of your pride. I chose the day you'd be desperate enough to say yes and proud enough to pretend it was about the money. I let you believe you were doing me a favor." Her voice cracked on the next part and she let it. "And then you went into a flooded gorge after me without hesitating, and I lay on that riverbank thinking: now tell him. Now, while he's wringing out his shirt. And I didn't. Because telling you would have cost me the guide, and I told myself the mountains would do it for me, that we'd reach the archive and the registry would speak and I'd be innocent of it. Let the dead confess. Let the paper take the blame."

"That's cowardice."

"It's the family trade," she said. "My mother ran from a country rather than claim it. I run from sentences rather than finish them. At least I'm consistent."

Something moved in his face—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever—but recognition, the grim fellowship of two people who had both built their lives on avoidance and met at last in a stone room where avoidance had run out of terrain.

He sat back down. Picked up the compass case. Turned it over in his hands.

"My father bought our lives with silence," he said quietly. "That's what the registry says. He guided her out, he was caught, and he kept his mouth shut and they let him live. And I grew up in the shadow of a disgrace I thought was about a storm on Kestrel Pass, and it turns out it was about *this*. About her. About a writ naming a child who was supposed to be ash with the rest of Vhaloria." He looked up. "Did you know that too? When you found me—did you know what my blacklist was?"

"No." She said it fast and firm because it was true and true things deserved speed. "I knew your father guided someone east. I didn't know the price. I didn't know about Corvane. Emrys—if I had known someone engineered your ruin to keep you from this, I'd have told you on the first day. That I swear on the locket."

He considered her for a long moment. Outside, the wind had changed in the night; she could hear it working at the shutters from a new quarter, colder, with a fine dry hiss under it that she'd learned on a hundred surveys and dreaded on every one.

"Snow," Emrys said, listening. "Early. Days early."

"Is that a change of subject?"

"It's a change of everything." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "Here's where we are, Sable. In three days, maybe four, the safe pass closes. After that there's my father's route or nothing, and his route killed him. Above us there's a mercenary band that hasn't shown itself in two days, which means they've cut ahead. Behind us there's an archivist who doesn't quit. And in this room there's a guide who just learned his whole life was somebody else's cover story, and a client who knew more than she said from the first mile." He looked at her, and the anger in him had settled into something harder and more durable. "So I need to know, right now, before we rope up tomorrow: is there anything else? Anything you're still carrying that I'm going to find out about at fourteen thousand feet? Because I can work with a liar who's finished lying. I can't work with one who isn't."

It was the fairest question anyone had ever asked her, and it deserved better than instinct. She made herself think. The school register. The locket. The bedtime stories. The letter—no. Not the letter. She had never opened her mother's final letter; it lived in the lining of her field jacket, sewn there at seventeen, and she had carried it through nine countries and never once broken the stitches, because some doors you keep shut until you're strong enough for what walks out. But that was hers. That was weight, not deception—or was that exactly the distinction a liar would draw?

"There's a letter," she said. "From my mother. Unopened. I've carried it since I was a girl. I don't know what's in it."

"You've never—"

"I couldn't." Her hands closed on her knees. "It's the last thing she ever wrote me. While it's sealed, she can still be anything. Alive, almost. Once I open it, she's finished."

Emrys was quiet for a moment. Then he picked up his father's compass case and set it on the pallet between them, deliberately, the way a man lays a card down.

"Then we understand each other," he said. "We're both carrying letters we haven't opened. Yours from your mother. Mine from my father. We'll open them when the mountain says so, and not before, and until then—" he exhaled, long and slow, the sound of a man repacking a load he'd have preferred to drop, "—you don't lie to me again. Not to spare me. Not to protect the plan. If you think something, you say it, even if it costs us the day. Especially then. On my father's route, a withheld observation kills people. Treat the truth like terrain. All of it, always."

"All of it," Sable said. "Always."

"And one more thing." His eyes came up, and there was something new in them—not warmth, not yet, but the cessation of cold, the first degree above freezing. "Your mother. Ilse. What was she like? When she told the stories. Was she afraid?"

Sable blinked. Of all the questions, that one.

"She was careful," she said. "Careful the way water is careful, finding every crack. But when she talked about the escarpment—about the crossing—she wasn't careful. She was *alive*. Like it was the one country she never renounced." She smiled despite everything. "She used to say the mountain was the only witness who never testified."

Emrys huffed—almost a laugh, strangled at birth. "Sounds like she knew him. My father. He used to say the escarpment keeps no ledger." He glanced toward the window, where the grey had thickened and the first flakes were starting to come down past the cliff—small, dry, unhurried, the vanguard of something much larger. "Well. It's keeping one now."

Sable followed his gaze and felt the cold arrive through the window slit, precise as a summons. Snow on the third morning of autumn. Mercenaries ahead. An archivist behind. And somewhere below them, hours at most, the sound of engines that no monastery bell would cover—

Down in the valley, faint but unmistakable, a helicopter.

Emrys was already on his feet, snuffing the lamp.

* * *

They were dressing in the dark when the abbot's knock came—three slow raps, and then, through the door, in accented lowland speech, the words that dropped the floor out of the morning:

"A woman has climbed the stair," Father Aldric's successor said. "Alone. No soldiers. She says she has come to warn you—and she says you will want to hear it before the men behind her arrive."
