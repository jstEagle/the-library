## 17. Marchetti at the Gate

The mule died at dawn, two hours below the monastery, and Odile Marchetti shot it herself because the hired handler could not bring himself to do it. She put the round behind its ear with the same economy she used for everything—no wasted motion, no ceremony—and stood a moment in the cold while steam rose off the animal's neck and the handler looked anywhere but at her.

"Take the panniers," she said. "We walk from here."

They had been walking, in one form or another, for six days. Up out of the lowlands where the republic's paved roads ended and its authority began to thin like altitude; up through the burned village where she had knelt in the ash of a schoolhouse and read the charred spine of a register without opening it, because opening it would have made what she was doing real; up through goat tracks and scree fields and a defile so narrow she had to turn her shoulders sideways, all of it following the spoor of three people who moved through this country like water finding cracks.

Emrys Nystrom. The name had been a file number when she started. It was becoming something else now, though she would not yet have said what.

The monastery appeared the way it must have appeared to every traveler for four hundred years: suddenly, impossibly, hanging off the cliff face above the valley throat like a swallow's nest built by something larger than birds. Grey stone against greyer rock, a single thread of smoke rising straight in the windless morning. There was no road to it. There was a stair cut into the living cliff, two hundred steps that the monks had carved before the republic existed and which the republic, in forty years of occupation, had never bothered to close—not out of tolerance, Marchetti suspected, but because no bureaucrat had ever believed anyone would want to go up there.

That was the republic's whole genius, she thought, climbing. Not cruelty. Incredulity. The conviction that the past was a room nobody would bother to enter.

She had entered rooms like that her entire career. She was the woman who read ashes—the one they brought in when a provincial office mysteriously caught fire the week before an audit, when a land registry burned the night before a disputed inheritance came to court. Ash held its shape, if you knew how to look. A page did not burn evenly; it burned in the order the fire found it, and the order told you what someone had wanted destroyed first. She had reconstructed death lists from the shadows of letters pressed into scorched paper. She had once identified a forged will from the way the ink had aged wrong around the signature alone.

It was a talent for reading what other people tried to erase. Which meant, though she had never framed it to herself this way until this climb, that her whole life had been spent in service to erasures—cataloguing them, confirming them, filing them into the archive where the republic kept its finished work. She was very good at proving that things had been destroyed. She had never once been asked to prove that destroying them had been wrong.

The handler set down the panniers at the base of the stair and refused to go further. She paid him double without argument and sent him down the mountain, and then she climbed alone, her breath sawing, her legs burning, thirty years old and feeling every one of them, while the valley dropped away beneath her and the wind came up the escarpment carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it.

* * *

The gate was oak banded with iron, older than any nation currently on the maps, and it opened before she knocked.

"You have been walking since the village," said the monk who opened it. He was young, broad-shouldered, with the calloused hands of a man who did masonry as well as prayer. "Brother Anselm says you stopped to read the schoolhouse ash. You knelt there eleven minutes."

"Your brother has good eyes."

"Our brothers have been watching this road for forty years," the monk said. "Waiting to see who comes up it." He stepped aside. "You are the second party in two days. You are the first one who came alone."

The word *alone* landed on her with more weight than he could have known. She thought of her requisitioned helicopter sitting grounded at the garrison airfield under a paperwork hold her own superiors had placed—*deniability*, the deputy director's aide had said, smiling, meaning *we will not be seen to know what you do*. She thought of the two soldiers she'd been assigned who had received orders, halfway up the mountain, to return to the valley. She had let them go without protest, because she had understood what the orders meant: whatever happened next, the republic wanted no witnesses wearing its uniform.

So yes. Alone. That was the design.

"I need to see your archive," she said.

"And I need to know what you intend to burn."

The question was asked mildly, the way a doctor asks where it hurts. Marchetti met the young monk's eyes and found nothing hostile in them—only a kind of tired precision, the look of a man who had inventory-counted his own doom for four decades.

"I don't know yet," she said, and was surprised to find it was the truth.

He nodded slowly, as if that too were an answer he'd been expecting. "Then come in out of the wind. Brother Anselm will want to talk to you. He talks to everyone. It is how we confess here—we make the visitors do it."

* * *

The abbot received her in a cell barely larger than her office in the capital, though her office had a view of the ministry courtyard and this one had a window full of nothing but sky and the sheer drop of the escarpment's eastern face. Anselm was ancient in the way of mountain trees—bent, weathered, obviously going to outlast everyone. He poured her tea from a dented kettle without asking whether she wanted any, and she drank it because refusing hospitality in this place felt like a tactical error.

"You are the archivist," he said. "Odile Marchetti. The one who reads ashes."

"You know my name."

"We know all the names now." He smiled without much humor. "Two days ago a man sat where you sit—a guide with a criminal's eyes and a girl who carries her mother's face like a loaded weapon. Before that, soldiers. Before that, years of nothing. Forty years of nothing. And now, in one season, everyone remembers we exist." He folded his hands. "When a country forgets a place for forty years and then sends four kinds of visitor in a month, the place learns to ask why. So. Why are you here, Odile Marchetti?"

She set down the cup. This was the moment where her training said to give him the official version—the recovery of stolen state property, the routine disposition of documents pertaining to a dissolved administrative territory. The words were ready. They were always ready. They were the smoothest thing about her.

Instead she heard herself say: "I'm here because I burned my own copy of the Vhalorian registry nine days ago, and I've been trying to understand why I did it ever since."

Anselm went very still.

"It was my working copy," she continued, the words coming faster now, as if they'd been waiting years for the door to open. "Duplicate records, unofficial, the kind every archivist keeps against the day the official ones are falsified. I've kept it for eleven years. I never opened it. And when the report came in about a salvage chest on the Greywater—when I understood what was in it—I went back to my office and I burned it. Not to destroy evidence. To destroy the temptation to file it away again and forget." She looked at her own hands. "I told myself it was procedure. Containing a security risk. I am very good at telling myself things. But I climbed your stair this morning, Father, and I still don't know whose errand I'm on."

The abbot studied her for a long time. Outside, the wind changed pitch, testing the shutters.

"Do you know what we did during the purge?" he said at last. "Not the dramatic part. The daily part. For three years, monks carried documents up this stair in flour sacks and medicine crates and hollowed-out Bibles. Every birth record. Every land deed. Every succession instrument of a country that officially no longer existed. Two hundred steps each trip, at night, in winter, past checkpoints where discovery meant the annexation authorities would burn this building with us inside it." He tapped the table once, softly. "We were not preserving history, Archivist. History is what you keep in museums. We were preserving *evidence*. We knew that someday someone would come asking questions, and we intended the answers to survive."

"And did they? Survive?"

"That depends," Anselm said, "on what you mean by *they*."

Something in his tone made her stomach drop before he said anything else. She had interrogated enough men to recognize the pause that precedes bad news delivered honestly.

"The archive was rifled," he said. "Yesterday night, or early this morning—they left before first light. Three of them, from the size of the prints, moving well, moving armed. They took the sealed dispatches from the eastern vault. The correspondence. The writs of passage from the final year." He watched her absorb it. "They were thorough. They were also hurried. And they left the ledgers."

"The ledgers."

"The purge ledgers. The annexation administration's own records—confiscated by us in the chaos, hidden here ever since. Lists of names. Collaborators, informants, the men who signed the transport orders." His voice did not change at all. "Your three thieves did not want those. Which tells you what they already knew, or whom they serve."

Marchetti felt the cold move up through her boots into her spine. "Show me."

* * *

The archive occupied a long vaulted chamber cut deep into the cliff, lit by oil lamps because the monks had never trusted electric light to be private. Shelves of vellum and linen-paper rose into the dark. And along the near wall, exactly as Anselm had said, the rifling showed: empty cradles where document cases had lain for forty years, dust disturbed in clean rectangles, a broken wax seal ground under somebody's heel in the corner. Professional work. Hurried at the end. She crouched and read the floor the way she read everything else—boot prints overlapping, a drag mark where a heavy case had been hauled, and, half-hidden in the shadow of a shelf, a brass cartridge casing stamped with a manufacturer's code she recognized because she had catalogued it in three separate mercenary incidents over the past decade.

Corvane's people. Or Corvane himself. The distinction was getting thinner.

"They took the succession papers," Anselm said behind her. "Everything touching Ilse Vane and the child."

"Then the claim is gone."

"Perhaps." The abbot's eyes were unreadable. "Or perhaps it is in the hands of the only people who might choose not to use it. You cannot know that from here. Neither can I." He gestured toward the far shelves. "But you can know what your employers' grandfather did. If you have the stomach. The ledgers are yours to read, Archivist. We kept them for exactly this kind of visitor."

She found the volume by its index tab—*Annexation Administration, Internal Security Directorate, Years One Through Four*—and carried it to the lamp table, and opened it, and the smell of forty-year-old ink rose up around her like something exhumed.

She had expected to skim. She had expected the dry machinery of occupation: requisitions, quotas, the banal accounting of a swallowed country. Instead the ledger opened directly onto the internal security rosters, and the names ran down the columns in a clerk's careful hand, each entry annotated with district, function, and payment record. Vhalorian names, most of them. Her own countrymen's names, bought one by one. She read page after page with the professional detachment that was supposed to be her armor, and the armor held, right up until the third section, the one headed *Special Liaisons—Continuing Engagements.*

And there it was. Entry 114. Dated in the fourth year of the annexation, renewed annually thereafter, the payments escalating in neat increments across a decade of columns.

**CORVANE, ALDRIC — Transport & Interdiction Liaison. Identified the Vane escort party, Kestrel approach. Facilitated interdiction. Compensation per schedule C. Status: active.**

Identified the Vane escort party.

She read it again. Then a third time, her finger under the line as if the words might rearrange themselves if unwatched. *Facilitated interdiction.* Four flat words containing a pregnant woman hunted across a mountainside, a guide named Nystrom caught between survival and honor, a family's whole future traded for schedule-C compensation and a continuing engagement that had run, year after year, like a pension for betrayal.

Aldric Corvane. Grandfather of Marcus Corvane—deputy minister, rising man, the patron whose quiet phone call had gotten her helicopter grounded and her escort withdrawn. The man whose mercenaries were at this moment carrying a dead nation's last testament down some other route of this same escarpment, under orders she now understood completely: not to recover the claim. To *destroy* it. Because the writ didn't just name an heir. It named the flight from Kestrel. It named the escort party. It named, somewhere in the correspondence her thieves had taken, the man who sold them.

The claim wasn't a threat to the republic. It was a threat to one family—one family that had spent forty years laundering a collaborator's blood money into ministries and respectability, and that had engineered the blacklisting of a guide's son for the simple crime of being his father's child.

Marchetti closed the ledger. Her hands were steady. She noted that with distant interest, the way she noted everything, and then she noticed something else underneath the steadiness, something with weight and heat to it, something she had not felt in her government career even once:

She was angry.

Not the cold procedural anger of an auditor finding irregularities. This was older and simpler. Eleven years she had kept a duplicate registry she couldn't explain. Nine days ago she had burned it and couldn't explain that either. Now, under a lamp in a hole in a cliff, the explanation assembled itself with terrible clarity: she had spent her whole career serving an archive whose founding collection was crime, and some part of her had always known it, and the chest on the Greywater had been the first crack in the wall, and Aldric Corvane's name in entry 114 was the light coming through it.

"Father," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her. Level. Decided. "I need paper and ink. And I need to know every route down from this mountain that doesn't pass a checkpoint."

Anselm regarded her across the lamplight, and something in his expression eased, very slightly, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried for forty years.

"You're changing sides," he said. It was not quite a question.

"I'm correcting a filing error," Odile Marchetti said. "Forty years overdue."

She wrote through the afternoon—statements, transcriptions, a certified copy of entry 114 in her own hand with her seal beside it, the seal she had a sudden fierce pleasure in using for this instead of for cover-ups. By the time the light failed in the arrow-slit window, she had a plan with the clean lines of all her best work: reach the valley garrison ahead of Corvane's mercenaries, put the ledger evidence in front of the one magistrate in the province who had never taken Corvane money, and let the machine she served devour the man who had corrupted it. It was elegant. It was deniable. It was, for the first time in her career, true.

The young monk brought her tea at dusk and news with it, breathless from the stair.

"A rider came up from the valley—an old friend of ours, a trader. Mercenaries on the north road, six of them, moving fast. And word of a fight in the high country two days ago. Shots heard near the narrows." He hesitated. "The guide and the girl were seen alive, heading for the high col. Toward the storm line."

Outside, as if summoned, the first flakes came down through the last light—fat, slow, deliberate, the opening move of a winter arriving weeks early.

Marchetti stood at the window and watched the snow begin to bury the passes, and understood with perfect clarity that her elegant plan had just become a race, and that the race was up a mountain, into a storm, after three people who had every reason to shoot her on sight.

"Get me the trader's map," she said. "And whatever rope you can spare."
