## 18. Doss Takes the High Col

The col was called the Shepherd's Tooth on the old survey maps, though no shepherd had grazed a flock up there in living memory. It cut through the spine of the Karst Escarpment at just under nine thousand feet, a notch of broken shale and old snowpack wedged between two faces of grey limestone that went up like cathedral walls and down like a judge's sentence. From the notch, a single trail descended northward into a defile the locals called the Narrows—a slot of rock barely wide enough for two mules abreast, walled on both sides, with scree slopes above it that shed stones in the afternoon heat like a slow, patient rain.

It was, Doss Ferrin thought, the finest killing ground he had ever been paid to stand in.

He crouched at the lip of the notch with his binoculars and watched the morning light crawl down the far wall of the defile, picking out the trail in increments, the way light always did in high country—honest about everything, eventually. Below, the Narrows ran half a mile before it opened onto the hanging meadows where the trail from the monastery would feed in. Anyone coming up from Saint Aldric's would have to pass through it. Anyone passing through it would be visible from the col for the entire length of the slot, exposed, funnelled, with no cover deeper than a man's shadow.

Four days ahead of the party. That had been the arithmetic. Cut over the Tooth while Nystrom took the low route around the shoulder of the massif with his client, arrive first, set the ground, and let the geography do the heavy lifting. Corvane's lieutenant had laid it out in a rented office in the valley town with the flat confidence of a man reading someone else's menu. *The narrows. Take them there. The chest intact. No witnesses.* And then, when Doss had asked the obvious question—the one he'd been turning over for two weeks like a stone with something underneath it—the lieutenant had smiled and said, *You don't need to know why. You need to know where.*

Doss had run crews for eleven years. He'd worked extraction in the delta provinces, security for mining interests that paid their taxes in blood, escort jobs on three borders where the borders themselves were theoretical. He had a personal rule set that fit on an index card: get paid, keep your people alive, don't ask questions that get answered with bullets. The card had kept him whole through jobs that had hollowed out better men. He'd never once regretted it.

But this job had been wrong from the first briefing, and the wrongness had a texture he couldn't stop touching.

He lowered the binoculars and looked back at his crew. Five of them, spread along the notch in the positions he'd assigned—Kell and the twins up on the eastern spur where they could enfilade the whole length of the defile, Rook and Maret down at the meadow mouth to close the back door. Good people, in the way that word applied to people like them: reliable, unsentimental, expensive. Rook was brewing coffee on a spirit stove tucked behind a boulder, the thin blue flame invisible from more than ten feet. Maret was checking the sight on her rifle with the bored tenderness of a woman cleaning her teeth.

And Doss was standing in the wind with a photograph in his breast pocket that he had looked at too many times.

He took it out again. He knew he was going to; he'd known all morning. It was a studio portrait, grainy, maybe fifteen years old—a young man with dark hair going grey early at the temples, a face all angles and stubbornness, eyes that looked at the camera the way a man looks at a creditor. Emrys Nystrom. The lieutenant had given it to him along with the route intelligence, and Doss had pinned it to the wall of his tent the first night and studied it the way he studied terrain, because faces were terrain too, and he'd learned long ago that the ones you underestimated were the ones that killed you.

*He's dangerous,* the lieutenant had said. Doss had almost laughed. The man in the photo was lean and hard-used, but he had the posture of someone who'd been kicked a lot and had stopped flinching—dangerous the way a trapped animal is dangerous, maybe, but the file said disgraced guide, blacklisted, broke. The file said a man who salvaged river wrecks for brass fittings. Dangerous was a generous word.

Except the lieutenant hadn't laughed when he said it. That was the thing Doss kept coming back to. He'd said it the way you say something you've been told to say, and underneath it there had been something else—a wariness, almost a respect, and beneath *that*, something Doss had needed a while to name. Fear. Not of the man. Of the man finding something out.

You didn't fear a washed-out guide. You feared what a washed-out guide might stumble into.

Doss turned the photograph over. On the back, in the lieutenant's neat administrative hand: *NYSTROM, E. — take alive if practicable.*

*If practicable.* He'd read that phrase a hundred times in eleven years and it always meant the same thing: dead is fine, alive is preferred, and the preference is not about mercy. Somebody wanted Nystrom alive. Somebody wanted to talk to him. And nobody wanted to tell Doss why.

He should have walked away from the job right there. He knew that. The vagueness was the tell; it was always the tell. Clean jobs came with clean answers—*this man stole from us, this woman saw our faces, this cargo is ours.* Dirty jobs came with *you don't need to know why,* and *you don't need to know why* meant the why was the whole job, and you were the part that could be discarded once the why was handled. He'd told himself the money was too good. He'd told himself a lot of things. Eleven years of index-card rules, and he'd signed on to a job where the rules had nothing to hold onto.

He put the photograph away and climbed down from the notch to the sheltered hollow where Rook sat with the coffee.

"Route check," Doss said, crouching. "Show me the low approach again."

Rook unfolded the map without complaint. He was the youngest of them, twenty-six, with a farm boy's patience and hands that could field-strip a rifle blindfolded. He traced the trail with a blunt finger: the monastery spur, the hanging meadows, the Narrows, the col. "They're four days behind us if the weather holds. Five if the client's slow. Marchetti's people are still down in the valley—the tavern keeper talked, we know that much."

"The client." Doss looked at the map without seeing it. "What do we know about her?"

"Sable Vane. Cartographer. Hired Nystrom for the crossing." Rook shrugged. "Corvane's people say she's a hired hand like him. Working for someone who wants the chest."

"And Corvane's people lie the way other men breathe." Doss traced the defile with his own finger. "A cartographer hires a blacklisted guide to cross the escarpment in autumn. That's not a job. That's a pilgrimage. Somebody's looking for something."

"Maybe she is the something."

Doss looked up. Rook said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light. The kid was sharp in a way the others weren't; he noticed the gaps in a briefing the way other men noticed tripwires. "Maybe she is," Doss said. "Keep that thought where I can find it."

* * *

They spent two days setting the ground, and Doss hated every hour of it, because the ground was perfect and the job was not.

He walked the Narrows end to end twice, memorizing the fall of the scree, the choke points, the one place where the trail pinched against the eastern wall and a falling man would drop forty feet onto talus. He placed Maret on the spur with a clear field and clean wind. He showed Kell the signal line—a single whistle note for movement in the defile, two for the order, and the order was what the lieutenant had made him repeat back twice, verbatim, like a catechism: *the chest intact, no witnesses.*

No witnesses. He'd run that phrase around his skull until the grooves wore smooth. In eleven years he had never once been given an order that naked. Escort jobs had rules of engagement. Extraction jobs had target packages with names and reasons. Even the mining work, ugly as it got, had come wrapped in paperwork and plausible deniability, layers of *we never said that* between the men with money and the men with guns. This job handed him a rifle and a slot of rock and two words, and expected him not to notice that the two words described a massacre.

Five people, maybe six. A guide, a client, possibly an archivist with a requisitioned helicopter and a warrant she wasn't supposed to have. *No witnesses.* The lieutenant hadn't said *eliminate threats.* He hadn't said *if they resist.* He'd said *no witnesses,* which meant the witnesses were the problem, which meant whatever was in that chest was worth more than six lives, which meant—

Which meant Doss had been hired to be the part of the machine that grinds.

He sat alone on the eastern spur the second evening, above Kell's position, watching the sun go down the far wall of the escarpment. The light did its slow honest work. The rock went from grey to gold to a red so deep it looked like the mountain was bleeding out, and then the cold came down all at once the way it does up high, and the shadows filled the Narrows like water filling a ditch.

He took out the photograph.

He didn't know why he did it, that time. Habit, maybe. Or maybe because the light had been right—the same slanting evening gold that had been in a different place, a different mountain, twenty years ago, when he'd been twenty-four years old and working rescue on the northern approaches with a crew out of Vellmark, before he'd learned that courage was a commodity and started selling his.

The Kestrel Pass disaster. Everyone in the guiding world knew the shape of it—a commercial party caught by an early storm, eleven dead, the lead guide blamed and broken. Doss had been on the recovery teams. Three days of hauling bodies down through weather that didn't care about grief. He hadn't known Nystrom then, hadn't known the name beyond the reports. But that wasn't the memory that was rising in him now, slow and cold as meltwater under a glacier.

It was older than Kestrel. It was a different pass entirely—the Serrat col, a rescue, summer, twenty years back. A guided party caught by a rockfall on the approach. Two dead, one trapped—a woman pinned under a slab, and her boy, ten years old maybe, eleven, who wouldn't leave her. The boy had gone under the slab's edge trying to lever it with a piton hammer, a child's arms on a rock the size of a truck, and Doss had crawled in after him because someone had to, and the boy had looked at him with eyes that—

Doss's breath stopped.

Eyes that looked at the camera the way the man in the photograph looked at the camera. Like a creditor. Like the world owed him something and he intended to collect or die trying.

He held the photo up against the last red light and his hands were not quite steady. The boy at Serrat had been all angles and stubbornness even at ten, a thin fierce child with his mother's blood drying on his sleeve, who had fought Doss when he tried to pull him clear—not because the boy didn't want saving, but because his mother was under the stone, and the boy's whole body was a single refusal: *not without her.* Doss had pried him off the slab and carried him down the col on his back, and the boy had not cried, not once, not the whole way. At the trailhead he had looked up at Doss with those eyes and said, in a voice like flint dragged over flint: *You should have been faster.*

And Doss had said, because it was true: *I know.*

The boy's name. He'd never known the boy's name. The rescue reports had listed the party by the guide's name—Nystrom, senior, a name that had meant nothing to Doss then and meant everything now—and the survivors as *one adult female, deceased; one male minor, uninjured.* One male minor. He'd carried that minor down a mountain on his back and never learned his name, and twenty years later the minor had grown into a disgraced guide with his father's face and his mother's stubbornness, and someone was paying Doss Ferrin a small fortune to put a bullet in him at a place called the Narrows.

Doss sat with that for a long time. The light died. The cold came up through the rock into his bones, and he let it.

*Take alive if practicable.*

He thought about the lieutenant's face. He thought about the phrase *no witnesses.* He thought about a ten-year-old boy under a slab, fighting to stay with his dead mother, and the man that boy had become—a man who salvaged wrecks, who took the only work a blacklist left him, who was now up this mountain with a stranger's client and a stranger's chest, running from the same machine that had eaten his father. And somewhere behind all of it, the thing Doss had been circling for two weeks finally stood still long enough to be looked at: *they were afraid of him finding something out.* Not afraid of Nystrom. Afraid of Nystrom *learning.* Which meant the blacklist, the disgrace, the river salvage, all of it—the whole ruined shape of the man's life—might be something that had been done to him. Arranged. Maintained. Kept broken, the way you keep a dog's fence mended.

And Doss had been hired to make sure the fence held.

"Boss." Kell's voice, low, from below the spur. Doss pocketed the photograph and stood. Kell climbed up to him, a squat shape against the last grey light, and handed him the field glasses without being asked. "Movement. Low meadows. Single figure, then a second. They're moving fast—night march."

Doss took the glasses and found them: two tiny figures on the far trail, moving with the economical pace of people who knew the mountain was against them. One tall, one shorter, roped together. Even at this distance, at this light, he knew which one was which. He'd have known the stride.

"Range?" Kell asked.

"Eight hundred. They'll make the Narrows by midmorning if they hold that pace."

"Maret's asking for the order."

Doss lowered the glasses. Below him the Narrows waited in the dark, a slot of stone with a perfect field of fire, and the order was two words long, and the man walking into it had once weighed nothing at all on his back and said *you should have been faster.*

"Tell her to hold," Doss said. His voice came out even, and he was grateful for that, because everything under it was moving. "Nobody fires until I give the word. Nobody. I want to see his face when he comes through."

Kell paused. "That's not the plan."

"No," Doss said. "It isn't."

He stood alone on the spur after Kell dropped back down, and the wind came off the col and carried the smell of old snow, and Doss Ferrin took out the photograph one last time and held it up to the stars, and made himself say the thing he'd been refusing to say since the briefing room:

*They sent me to kill the boy I carried off the mountain.*

And the question that had been a stone under his tongue for two weeks turned over at last and showed its underside, and it was worse than the first side: *why does a man like Corvane fear a salvager enough to burn six people?* Whatever Nystrom was carrying up this mountain, it wasn't cargo. It was a reckoning. And Doss had two days, maybe less, to decide which side of the gun he was standing on when it arrived.

Below him in the dark, the Narrows waited, and far down the trail, two small lights—hand lanterns, moving fast—climbed toward him through the night.
