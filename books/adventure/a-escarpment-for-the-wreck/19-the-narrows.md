## 19. The Narrows

The narrows announced themselves an hour before we reached them.

I'd been reading the escarpment the way my father taught me—by its silences, by what it withheld—and all morning it had been withholding too much. No choughs on the thermals. No marmot whistles from the talus fields where they usually argued like market women. The mountain had gone quiet in the particular way a house goes quiet when someone is standing behind a door.

"Emrys." Sable's voice was low, pitched under the wind. She'd learned to read me over the past weeks, or at least to read the set of my shoulders, and I supposed my shoulders were saying something now. "What is it?"

"The birds," I said. "There aren't any."

She looked up at the empty sky as if she could argue them back into it. Above us the trail narrowed into a slot between two walls of shattered limestone—the kind of ground every guide learns to hate and every soldier learns to love. A hundred meters of scree funnel, steep enough that a falling man wouldn't stop until the couloir below, tight enough that four people couldn't walk abreast. There was no way around it. The eastern flank dropped away in cliffs the color of old bone; the western side rose into slabs even I wouldn't lead a client across.

We could go back. Back meant three days' retreat to the monastery, and behind us somewhere was Doss Ferrin with his quiet eyes and his men who moved through high country like they'd been poured into it. Back also meant winter catching us in the open, and winter on the Karst did not negotiate.

"We could wait for dark," Sable said.

"They know we have to come through here. They'll be watching it day and night." I crouched at the mouth of the slot and picked up a handful of scree, letting it run through my fingers. Fine stuff, unstable, the whole slope held together by habit rather than anything solid. "They'll want us in the middle of it."

"So we don't go in the middle of it."

"We go in fast, spread out, and we don't stop." I looked at her—at the locket chain visible at her collar, at the graze of exhaustion under her eyes that no amount of mountain hardening had erased. "Sable. If I say run, you run where I point. Not where you think you should go. Where I point."

She held my gaze for a long moment. Somewhere above us, a stone ticked against another stone—a sound so small it might have been nothing, except that mountains this cold don't fidget.

"All right," she said.

* * *

I went first.

It wasn't bravery. It was arithmetic: if there was a rifleman up on the western slabs, he'd take the lead position first, and I would rather that position be mine than hers. I'd done the same calculation on a dozen ugly routes and never once told a client the truth about why I walked point.

The slot swallowed us. Wind funneled through it, keening off the rock, carrying grit that stung like thrown sand. My boots crunched and slid on the scree and I kept us moving in short bursts—ten steps, freeze, listen, ten steps—my ears straining against the wind for the sound that didn't belong.

It came from above and to the left. A flat crack, wrong-sounding, like a branch snapping in a world without trees.

"DOWN!"

I hit the scree and grabbed Sable's pack strap and hauled her down with me as the second shot cracked past where her head had been and spanged off the far wall in a shriek of rock splinters. The echo rolled down the gorge and came back doubled, tripled, so that it was impossible to say how many rifles there were—one voice shouting itself hoarse in the stone throat of the narrows.

Then a third shot, lower, deliberate, and a fourth, and the ambush stopped being a possibility and became a place we were standing in.

"Run!" I pointed—not along the trail but up-slope, east, toward a rib of more stable rock that jutted out of the scree like the spine of something buried. "There! Move!"

Sable ran. God help me, she ran well—low, fast, not wasting breath on screaming, her boots finding purchase on ground that wanted none. I came behind her, half-crouched, feeding the slope glances while the shots kept coming. They weren't hitting us. That wasn't luck. Whoever was shooting knew exactly what they were doing: walking their fire toward us in measured steps, herding, not killing. Doss Ferrin's orders, whatever they were, evidently didn't include leaving bodies in the narrows. Yet.

We gained the rib and got behind it, and the wind changed, and in the changed wind I heard voices. Two, maybe three, up-slope on our left, moving. They'd expected us to bolt down-trail, back the way we'd come, into the open ground below the slot where a second team—I'd have bet the compass case on it—was waiting. Instead we'd gone up, onto the one line they hadn't covered, because covering it would have meant standing on scree so steep that a misstep was a two-hundred-meter ride.

Which meant they had to come after us. On my ground.

"Sable." I was already pulling rope off my shoulder, hands working without consulting the rest of me. "How are your nerves?"

"Worse than my legs," she said. Her voice shook and her eyes didn't. "Why?"

"Because we're going to give them the slope instead of them giving it to us." I flaked the rope across a horn of limestone the size of a grave marker, tested it with everything I had, felt it hold like bedrock. "When they come onto that scree field, they'll be spread thin—nobody rushes loose rock in a group, they go one at a time, spaced apart, or they bring the whole hillside down on themselves. One at a time means one at a time."

"One at a time against what?"

I reached into the side pocket of my pack and took out the thing I'd carried since the foothills and never mentioned: a coil of det cord and a block of climbing explosive, bought in a border town from a man who hadn't asked questions because I'd paid him not to. Guides carry avalanche charges in some ranges. Nobody carries them into a country where permits matter, which is why I'd bought mine where permits didn't.

"You're joking," Sable said.

"I'm a disgraced guide with a felony's worth of salvage experience and a talent for making rock do things rock shouldn't do." I wedged the charge into a seam beneath a slab the size of a dining table—a slab I'd marked an hour ago, before the narrows, because I mark everything, because my father taught me that the mountain always shows you its levers if you're humble enough to look. "That scree field sits on a slick clay layer. Old lakebed. When this goes, the whole apron lets go, and anyone standing on it gets a free ride to the bottom."

"And if they're not standing on it?"

"Then we've made a very loud noise and lost our element of surprise." I ran the cord back along the rib, paying it out, keeping low. "But they will be standing on it. Because it's the only way to us, and men like that follow the obvious line. The obvious line is a trap. That's the first thing—" My voice caught, just once, on the edge of the sentence. "That's the first thing my father ever taught me."

Below and to the left, gravel whispered. Then more than whispered—a controlled descent, boots placed with care, someone coming up the scree in the careful spaced-out rhythm I'd predicted. Through a gap in the rib I saw him: dark gear, rifle slung for climbing, face wrapped against the grit. He moved beautifully. He moved like a man who had never once doubted the ground would hold him.

He was thirty meters onto the apron when I fired the charge.

* * *

The mountain answered.

It didn't explode so much as exhale—a deep structural groan, then the whole slope shrugged, and the shrug became a slide, and the slide became a river of stone. The slab I'd keyed lifted, tilted, and went, taking a thousand tons of its neighbors with it, and the scree field turned to liquid and poured itself down the couloir in a white-roaring torrent. The climber on the apron threw himself sideways and rode the edge of it, and I lost sight of him in the dust, and I would be lying if I said I spent much time looking.

The sound went on forever. Dust boiled up through the narrows and blotted out the sun, turning noon to a brown twilight, filling my mouth and eyes and lungs with the taste of powdered mountain. I pulled Sable's collar over her nose and mine over mine and we crouched behind the rib while the world avalanched around us, and when it finally stilled, the trail we'd been ambushed on no longer existed. The route through the narrows was buried under forty feet of fresh rubble.

"They can't follow," Sable coughed.

"Not today." I peered through the settling haze, counting shapes. Two figures had retreated to the western slabs, small against the sky, and one of them was gesturing—not at us, at the slope, at the ruin of their ambush. A third figure was picking his way down the far edge of the slide, checking the debris line, methodical as a coroner. Doss Ferrin. Even at this distance I knew the unhurried economy of how he moved. He looked up, directly at our rib, directly at me—or so it seemed through the dust—and stood there for a long moment, doing sums.

Then he raised one hand. Not a wave. Something more like a salute, or a warning, or a question I couldn't parse. And he turned and led his men back the way they'd come.

"He let us go," Sable said.

"He counted costs." I didn't believe it either, entirely. "Come on. We've bought hours, not days, and we've spent something to buy them."

"What?"

I stood up to answer her, and that was when I saw the blood.

* * *

It was on her sleeve first—a dark smear she must have taken for dust, because she'd been cradling her left arm against her ribs since the slide and hadn't said a word. Mountain people do that. They bank pain like fuel, saving it for later, and later always comes.

"Sit," I said, and she sat, and I peeled the sleeve back with the gentleness I save for frozen ropes and other things that break.

A furrow, high on the outer arm, just below the shoulder. A bullet graze—cleaned the skin off in a stripe three inches long, bled freely, hadn't touched bone. One of those early shots, the ones I'd thought were misses. They hadn't been misses. They'd been the tax, collected in advance.

"Why didn't you say something?" I was already cutting the sleeve away, already pulling the kit.

"It didn't seem important. Compared to the shooting." She watched me work with a clinical detachment that frightened me more than panic would have. "Is it bad?"

"It's ugly and it's shallow. You keep the arm." I flushed the wound with water we couldn't afford, packed it, bound it tight enough to hurt and not tight enough to kill circulation. My hands were steady. I noticed that from a strange distance, the way you notice weather. "You'll favor it for a week. Climbing will be misery."

"Climbing is already misery."

Despite everything, I almost laughed. I taped the dressing down, checked her fingers—warm, pink, responsive—and sat back on my heels in the brown dust with the adrenaline draining out of me and leaving behind that hollow ringing silence that follows every fight, win or lose.

Above us, the last of the haze was tearing away, and the sun came back, indifferent and clean. The couloir below the narrows wore its new scar openly, a raw tongue of displaced stone reaching down toward the valley. Somewhere under it lay our ambushers' route, and maybe a rifle, and maybe worse. I found I couldn't make myself care, and I noted that too, filed it away, the way you file the first symptom of a sickness you've seen in other men.

"Supplies," Sable said suddenly. "Emrys—our cache. We cached the resupply below the slot. Before the narrows. The spare rope, the food, the—"

I was already turning to look.

The cache site was gone. Not buried—worse than buried. The slide had caught the lower apron and swept it, and where our sealed barrel and bundled packs had sat tucked under an overhang, there was now only stripped rock and a debris fan spreading wide across the valley floor, a quarter mile below. Somewhere in that fan, scattered through tons of fresh rubble, were eleven days of food, our spare rope, the stove, the tent fly, and most of the medical kit I'd just used the last good dressings from.

"No," Sable said quietly. It wasn't a word aimed at me. It was aimed at the mountain, at the whole unjust arithmetic of it.

I got out the glasses and swept the fan. Once, twice. On the third pass I caught a flash of color—a scrap of the orange barrel liner, snagged on rock, impossibly far down. Nothing else. Nothing retrievable. Anything not smashed was scattered across a half-kilometer of unstable debris that would shift again with the next freeze-thaw.

I lowered the glasses and did the inventory in my head, because that was the discipline, that was the craft: what we carried versus what we needed versus what the route demanded. What we carried was thin. What the route ahead demanded—the high traverse, the col, the hanging valley beyond it—was not thin. The equation had just tipped, and both of us could feel it tipping.

"How long to the monastery if we turn back?" Sable asked. Her voice was level. Too level.

"Two days down. But the monastery won't feed us for the return, and Marchetti may have reached it by now, and Ferrin's men will expect us to retreat—they'll cover the low route." I folded the glasses away. "And winter is six days out at most. Maybe less. The wind's changed."

"Then forward."

"Forward means the father's route." I said it plainly, because she deserved plainness, and because saying it aloud made it real in a way thinking it hadn't. "The dangerous one. The one nobody guides. With your arm, and this kit, and no margin."

Sable looked down at her bandaged arm, then up at the escarpment wall rising above us—sheer, white-veined, indifferent, waiting. The wind came down off it carrying the first true bite of the season, a cold that had traveled a long way to find us.

"My mother crossed these mountains pregnant," she said. "In wartime. In winter, possibly. With nothing." She flexed her bandaged arm once, testing it, and grimaced, and set her jaw. "I can be miserable for a week, Emrys. Show me the route."

I shouldered the pack. It weighed what it weighed, which was now everything we had left, and I turned us toward the wall, toward the faint thread of my father's line stitched up the face of the world—and above us, at the very top of the ridge, a single chough finally appeared, wheeling once against the sun before dropping out of sight over the col.

Birds meant wind at the top. Birds meant the pass was open.

For now.
