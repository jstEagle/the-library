## 20. Alone Above the Treeline

The treeline ended the way all endings do on the escarpment—not with a line but with a thinning, a giving up, the pines squatting lower and lower until they were only krummholz, only wind-bent shrubs, and then only stone. Sable Vane watched it happen beneath her, the last green country falling away in folds, and understood that she was watching her own margin of safety recede with it.

Above the trees there was nothing to hide behind. Nothing to burn for warmth. Nothing soft anywhere.

She put her left hand against the rock and lifted her leg over the ledge, and the leg—her right one, the one the bullet had kissed two days ago at the narrows—sent its white signal up through her hip and into her teeth. She made no sound. She had gotten very good at making no sound. It was, she reflected grimly, the one inheritance she had never needed papers to prove.

"You're favoring it."

Emrys Nystrom stood six feet above her on the switchback, a coil of rope over one shoulder, looking down with that face of his that gave away nothing except, if you knew how to read it, everything. She was getting better at reading it. She hated that.

"I'm climbing," she said.

"You're favoring it. There's a difference, and the difference matters more up here than it did down there." He waited while she made the move, and when she passed him he didn't offer a hand, which she understood was its own kind of respect. He'd offered one at the rope bridge. She'd nearly drowned him for it. "How's the bleeding?"

"There's no bleeding."

"There's seeping. I can see it through the bandage when the wind pushes your trousers flat."

"Then you're looking at my leg too much, guide, and not enough at the route."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, the place where a smile had been before the world had taxed it out of him. He turned and climbed on. Sable followed, counting her breaths the way he'd taught her on the first day: four up, hold, four up. The count kept her honest. The count kept her from thinking about how high they already were, and how much higher they had to go, and how the sky above the col had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

* * *

They made camp at midafternoon, which was early, and Sable knew it was early because of her, and knowing it made her angry, and being angry made her tired, and being tired made her slower, and the whole ugly spiral of it turned in her like water down a drain. Emrys pitched the tent with the economy of a man who had done it ten thousand times, in wind, in dark, probably once upside down. He melted snow for water. He checked her dressing without asking permission, which she allowed because arguing cost more calories than she had.

The wound was not terrible. That was the cruelty of it. A graze, the field dressing called it—a furrow torn along the outside of her calf where a mercenary's round had skipped off stone first and taken most of its malice with it. Two days of climbing on it, though, of cold and load-bearing and the particular violence of scree, had turned a furrow into a problem. The edges were angry. The tissue around them had gone hot and tight. She was not a doctor, but she was a cartographer, and she knew what it meant when a line on the map started to swell beyond its surveyed bounds.

"You're limping worse than this morning," Emrys said. He sat across the vestibule, working a whetstone along his knife with long, patient strokes. He did not look at her while he said it. That was his way of being kind.

"I'm managing."

"You are. That's the problem. Managing is what you do when the alternative is stopping, and up here, managing has a shelf life." He sheathed the knife. "Tomorrow the route goes over the shoulder of the Black Tooth. Class three scrambling, exposed, two thousand vertical feet of it. After that, the high plateau, and after that—" he gestured at the west, where the escarpment's spine rose into clouds that had been sitting there all day like a verdict, "—the pass, if the weather lets us have it."

"Then we move fast."

"We move at the speed of the slowest person. That's not a philosophy, Sable, it's arithmetic. It's the only arithmetic on a mountain that never lies."

She looked at him then, really looked, and saw what he was building toward the way you see a contour line closing on itself and know a summit is coming. Her stomach dropped ahead of the words.

"Say it," she said.

He was quiet for a moment. The wind moved the tent fabric in small worried motions. "I'm saying we should think about it," he said. "The split. I go on, fast and light. I make the pass, I reach whatever's at the end of this, and I come back for you with a litter, or a rope team, or whatever the situation wants. There's a route down the east drainage from here—hard, but hard in the walking sense, not the climbing sense. You could sit tight at the burned village. There's shelter there. Water."

"The burned village is a day behind us."

"It's the last shelter."

"And you'd leave me there. Alone. With mercenaries somewhere on this mountain who have already shot me once."

"Doss's people went east after the narrows. I'd stake my ticket on it."

"You staked your ticket on the weather holding at the rope bridge too."

He had no answer for that, because there wasn't one. He looked out at the clouds instead, and Sable watched his profile, and felt the thing in her chest that wasn't the wound begin to ache.

"You want me to say it for you?" she said. "You want me to agree that I'm the ballast. That the mission would be cleaner, faster, likelier, without me. Fine. I've done the same math you have, Emrys. I've been doing it since the narrows, every step, every time this leg lights up like a struck match. I know exactly what I cost this expedition. I can price myself to the ounce." Her voice came out low and even and she was proud of that. "So let's not pretend it's arithmetic. If you wanted to leave me, you'd have made the case with numbers and I'd have lost to them honestly. What you're doing is dressing it up as mercy so neither of us has to look at it straight."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "None of this is fair. Fairness is a lowland concept. It doesn't survive the treeline."

* * *

The storm front came down off the western peaks an hour before dusk, not the storm itself but its outriders—high cirrus gone to mare's tails, then a cold that arrived like a change in management. They ate cold rations in the vestibule, knees almost touching, watching the light die over the plateau they'd have to cross. Sable flexed her leg under the blanket, testing it, hating it.

"Ask me," she said.

"Ask you what?"

"Ask me why it matters. Why I won't turn back. You've been circling it for two days. You think I'm stubborn, or proud, or that I'm chasing my mother's ghost and I've lost the judgment to know it. So ask."

He set down the ration tin. In the last grey light his face was all planes and shadow. "Why does it matter?"

"Because I spent my whole life drawing other people's borders." The words came out of her with a strange ease, as if they'd been waiting years for the door to open. "That's the work, right? That's what a cartographer does. Someone with an army decides where the line goes, and someone like me makes the line look inevitable. Clean. Natural. Like rivers decided it, like mountains decided it, like nobody decided it at all." She pulled the locket from under her collar, the little worn thing she'd carried since she was nine years old, half a name inside and half a life. "I redrew Vhaloria off the maps myself. Did you know that? Early in my career, before I knew what the name meant to me. There was a revision series, and the old borders were in the way of the new roads, and I erased a country with a pen. It took me an afternoon." Her hand closed around the locket. "It took the republic forty years and it took me four hours. I've been trying to walk that back ever since."

Emrys said nothing. He had the stillness of a man who understood that some things, once said, should not be interrupted.

"So when you ask me to turn around, you're asking me to be the pen again. To draw a line through my own country a second time, with my own feet this time. And I won't. I can't." She turned to face him fully, and the wind gusted, and the tent leaned and recovered. "But here's the part I need you to hear, because I think you've had it wrong from the beginning, and I've let you have it wrong because it was easier. It isn't my inheritance."

His brow moved. "The writ names—"

"The writ names the child of Ilse Vane. I know. I've had two weeks to read that sentence ten thousand ways." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "You know what I've actually inherited? A locket with no picture. A maiden name my mother never used. A school register in a burned village with a girl's name in it that might be hers and might be her cousin's. Rumors. Ash. A country that exists now only in the negative space of other countries' maps. That's not an inheritance, Emrys. That's a debt. And debts don't care about bloodline. They land on whoever's standing closest."

"Then why climb for it at all?"

"Because I'm the one standing closest." She said it simply, and found that it was true, and that the truth of it was heavier than any crown would have been. "And because if I don't, someone else will. Corvane will, or the republic will, or some historian fifty years from now will dig it up and turn my mother into a flag. That's what happens to dead nations when nobody stands up for them. They get used." She looked down at her leg, at the dressing gone grey at the edges. "I would rather carry it to the top and burn it myself than let it be carried by men who want it."

Emrys was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was different—lower, rougher, the guide's professional cadence cracked down the middle. "You think I don't understand debt? My father bought our lives with silence and then went back up Kestrel Pass to die reaching for that chest. Everything I've been since—the blacklist, the salvage line, the whole ruined ledger of my life—has been interest on a payment I never knew he made." He shook his head. "I've spent two weeks telling myself I'm guiding you for the money. Then for the clearing of my name. Then because I'm the only one who knows the route. Every reason I've given myself has been a smaller lie than the one underneath."

"Which is?"

"Which is that it's mine too." He said it like a man confessing to a crime. "Whatever's in that cache—my father bled for it. Maybe died for it. I have as much standing on this mountain as you do, and less proof, and I have hated you a little for that. For having the name. For having the locket. For being the one the papers point to while I'm the one they point through." He looked at her, and there was no guide's mask on him now, only the man, tired and honest and forty-some years of altitude in his eyes. "So no. I'm not leaving you behind, Sable. And it's not mercy, and it's not arithmetic, and you were right to call it that. It's that I'm not letting you walk into my father's mountains alone and come back without me. Whatever's up there belongs to both of us or neither of us, and I'm done pretending otherwise."

The wind rose. Somewhere far to the west, thunder spoke in a voice too deep for thunder, the sound of a storm deciding.

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard," Sable said, and her eyes were burning, and she didn't try to hide it. "You'll slow yourself down for a woman who can't walk. You'll die for a country that doesn't exist anymore, over papers that might not even be there."

"Yes," Emrys said. "Probably."

She reached out and took his hand. It was a climber's hand, scarred and cold, and it closed around hers with a care that undid her more than any gentleness would have.

"Then we go together," she said. "And when my leg finally quits—and it will quit, Emrys, I know my own body's paperwork better than anyone—you don't get to decide for me. We decide together. That's the term. That's the only term."

"That's a terrible term."

"It's the only one on offer. Take it or draw your own map."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Cartographers," he said, the way another man might say *children of the devil*, and squeezed her hand once, and let go.

* * *

She woke in the black hours to the sound of snow.

Not the whisper of it—the hiss, the long sustained hiss of wind-driven spindrift scouring the tent, and beneath it a silence so total it rang. She knew without looking that the storm had arrived early, that the safe pass was closing or closed, that the arithmetic they'd been arguing about had just been rewritten by a hand bigger than either of theirs.

Emrys was already awake. She could tell by his breathing, by the way the dark beside her had the quality of attention. Outside, the world had gone white and howling.

"The window," she said quietly.

"Gone. Or going." His voice was flat, which from him meant the fear was very large. "We'll know by first light."

Sable lay in the dark with her hot leg and her cold locket and listened to the mountain take away their options one by one. And it was then—between one gust and the next—that she heard it, or thought she heard it: a sound beneath the storm, rhythmic and wrong, like boots on scree, like someone climbing toward them out of the white.

She put her hand on Emrys's arm. He was already listening.
