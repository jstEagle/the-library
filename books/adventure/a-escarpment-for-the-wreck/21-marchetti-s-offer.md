## 21. Marchetti's Offer

Odile Marchetti had spent eleven years learning to read what fire left behind, and in all that time she had never once trusted her own legs to carry her as fast as they carried her now.

She had left the escort at the treeline. Not officially—officially she had told Sergeant Vellum that the horses could not manage the switchbacks above the old quarry and that the two of them would proceed on foot while the rest circled to the western approach. Then, at the first bend where the trail narrowed between granite shoulders, she had stopped to adjust a bootlace and let him walk on ahead of her, his broad back receding into the pines with the patient confidence of a man who believed his superior officer was fifty meters behind him. She had watched him go for exactly ten seconds. Then she had turned east, up the goat track, alone.

It was insubordination. It was possibly criminal. Her superiors wanted deniability, and she was handing them a gift-wrapped version of it: if this went wrong, Odile Marchetti had acted alone, without orders, an archivist overreaching. They would feed her to the inquiry boards with genuine regret and genuine relief.

She climbed anyway.

The altitude took its toll honestly. She was forty-six years old and had spent her career in basements, in the Republic Archive's sublevels where the air tasted of dust and cold iron and where the only weather was the slow corrosion of paper. Her lungs burned. Her thighs shook by the third hour. Twice she stopped and seriously considered turning back, and both times she thought about the registry—the one she had burned in her own office six weeks ago, feeding it page by page into the incinerator while her hands stayed perfectly steady, because they always did—and how burning a document did not burn what it described. Somewhere above her, in the white stone country where maps went thin, a woman walked who should not exist, carrying a name the republic had spent forty years and considerable blood making sure no one would say aloud.

Odile climbed.

* * *

She found them at dusk, in a hollow below a shelf of limestone where a stream ran shallow and loud over slate. The guide had a fire going—small, disciplined, screened by rocks so its light would not leak—and he was melting snow in a tin cup with the economy of a man who had done it ten thousand times. The woman sat against the pack, her left arm bound at the shoulder where the mercenary's bullet had grazed her at the narrows. Odile had read the reports from the ambush site. She had read the scree like a letter: three shooters above, a party of two below, and the terrain used against trained men by someone who knew it the way a blind man knows his own hallway.

Nystrom saw her before she meant him to. Of course he did. A man does not survive two blacklists and a mountain range by being surprised at his own fire.

He stood up slowly, and he did not reach for the ice axe leaning against the stone beside him, which was itself a kind of message. *I don't need it yet.*

"Archivist," he said. His voice was rougher than she remembered from the tavern keeper's account, scraped down to gravel by altitude and grief. "You're a long way from your filing cabinets."

"I left my escort at the treeline." She came down into the hollow openly, hands visible, moving the way you moved near wild animals or desperate men—which was to say, the same way. "I'm alone."

"That's either very brave or very stupid."

"It's usually both." She stopped at the edge of the firelight. Up close he looked worse than the photograph Corvane's people had circulated through her office—thinner, grayer, with a healing cut along his jaw and eyes that had the flat shine of a man running on something deeper than sleep. But his hands were steady. That was the thing she noted, professionally, the way she noted everything: the hands were steady, and the hands were the tell. "May I sit? I've been climbing since dawn and I'd rather negotiate sitting down."

"Negotiate." He almost smiled. It was not a warm expression. "The republic sends one unarmed woman to do what its mercenaries couldn't."

"The republic didn't send me." She lowered herself onto a boulder at the fire's margin, wincing as her knees filed their complaints. "That's rather the point. May I?" She nodded at the tin cup.

He hesitated, then poured the melted snow into a second cup—he had two, she noticed; they were traveling like people who expected to live—and handed it across. Their fingers did not touch. He made sure of it.

* * *

"You burned the registry," Emrys said.

It was not a question, and it landed in Odile's chest like a dropped stone. She kept her face still. Eleven years of reading ash taught you also how not to look like ash yourself.

"You know that," she said.

"Sable found the residue in your office records. Transfer logs. You requisitioned the Vhalorian civil registry in the spring, signed for it personally, and returned nothing but weight. Ash has weight, Marchetti. Even filtered through an incinerator, even mixed with the general waste stream. Someone who reads documents for a living should know that someone else can read their absence."

"And yet I'm here," Odile said, "and not in a cell."

"Yet," he agreed.

She drank the snow water. It tasted of tin and cold. Around them the mountain made its small night noises—rock settling, water insisting, wind combing the high grasses with fingers that were already carrying the first mineral suggestion of winter. The season was early. Everyone on this mountain could feel it, the way animals feel an earthquake coming through their feet.

"I'm going to tell you things," Odile said, "that I have never said aloud, and I need you to understand why before you decide whether to believe them. I am not here for the republic. I am not here for Corvane—I know about Corvane now, more than he'd like, and we'll come to that. I'm here because I have spent my entire career being the person who certifies that destroyed things stay destroyed, and six weeks ago I realized I had become very good at helping a country lie to itself, and I find—" her voice caught, just once, and she hated it, and continued, "—I find I can't do it anymore. Not this particular lie. Not this one."

Emrys said nothing. The fire ticked. Sable Vane had opened her eyes at some point during the speech and was watching Odile with an expression that contained, beneath the pain and the wariness, something that might have been recognition. Two women who dealt in records, in proof, in the terrible authority of paper.

"Tell me what you found in the monastery," Odile said quietly.

"Why?"

"Because I already know most of it, and I want to hear whether you'll lie to me. It will calibrate everything that follows."

Emrys looked at her for a long moment. Then, apparently deciding that honesty was cheaper than performance, he told her. Ilse Vane. The flight south in the final year. The guide named Nystrom—his father—who had carried her over the escarpment under the noses of the annexation patrols. The purge ledgers Marchetti herself had never been cleared to see, though she had long suspected their existence: the collaborator's signature, the family name that Corvane had spent three generations laundering into respectability. The succession writ. The twin document waiting in the hidden cache above, in the hanging valley, in the place his father had died trying to reach.

When he finished, Odile set down her cup with great care, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not permit it.

"My grandfather," she said, "was a customs clerk in the occupied valleys. Forty-one years old when the purges came. He processed exit papers—for a fee—for perhaps two hundred families. He kept copies. When the republic consolidated the archives, those copies went into sealed holdings, and when I was promoted to the restoration division, I found his handwriting in the finding aids and understood that my family's small decency was sitting in a vault alongside other men's crimes, all of it classified, all of it buried, all of it *managed*." She met Emrys's eyes. "I have known since I was thirty-five that the history I curate is a forgery. I burned the registry because I was ordered to, and I obeyed, and I have regretted it every day since with a specificity that keeps me awake. So when I tell you what I'm about to offer, understand that I am not offering it lightly, and I am not lying, and I will swear to it on whatever you ask me to swear on."

"Say it, then."

"Give me the writ."

* * *

The word hung in the smoke. Sable Vane had gone very still against the pack.

"In exchange," Odile continued, "I clear your name. Both countries. The Kestrel Pass findings overturned—not quietly, publicly. The blacklist dissolved. Your father's service record restored, posthumously decorated if you want it, though I suspect you don't. Compensation for eleven lost years. Permits, standing, the right to work any route on this range for the rest of your life." She paused. "And protection. Corvane's mercenaries are still on this mountain, Nystrom. Doss Ferrin's band lost two men at the narrows and they are angry and they are paid by the week. If you come down with me, under my authority, they cannot touch you without touching the republic itself. That is worth more than you currently grasp."

"And the writ goes where?"

"To the republic archive. Sealed. Classified at the highest tier." She said it plainly, because dressing it up would insult him. "It will never be published. Never be acted upon. Vhaloria stays dead on paper, officially and permanently, and the claim dies in a vault instead of in a civil war."

"And that's the deal." Emrys's voice was very quiet. "My life back. In exchange for burying her country forever."

"Yes."

Sable spoke for the first time. "You'd give us everything except the one thing that matters."

"I'd give you everything except the thing that gets people killed," Odile said, turning to her, and her voice gentled despite herself. "Mademoiselle Vane. Or should I say—" she stopped, because saying it felt like violence, and she was tired of being the instrument of violence. "I know who you are. I've known since the tavern. Do you understand what happens if that writ comes down off this mountain alive? Not what Corvane does—what *everyone* does. There are four million people living in those valleys whose grandparents were Vhalorian, whose deeds and graves and marriages are recorded in a language the schools no longer teach. There are bordering states that have waited forty years for a legal pretext. There are veterans of the annexation on both sides, old men with rifles in barns. A piece of paper naming an heir doesn't resurrect a nation. It lights a fuse in a room full of dry powder, and the people who burn will be shepherds and schoolteachers, not archivists and not claimants. I have read enough burned things to know what fire leaves behind. I am asking you not to make more of it."

"You're asking us to trust the same state that erased them," Emrys said.

"I'm asking you to trust *me*, knowing full well what I am. I burned your registry. I obeyed orders for eleven years. I am the machine, Nystrom—I am its pen and its stamp and its filing system. And I am telling you that the machine, this once, can be turned to mercy instead of erasure. Your name restored. Her existence acknowledged—" she nodded at Sable, "—quietly, privately, in ways that cost the republic nothing and mean everything to the two of you. A grave marker for your father with his true record on it. Access to the sealed holdings so the valleys' dead can at least be counted honestly. All of it real. All of it deliverable. I have the authority and I have the leverage and I am not bluffing, because bluffing is for people who expect to meet you again, and after tonight I intend to disappear into my basement for the rest of my career."

* * *

The silence stretched. Emrys fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch, and Odile recognized the gesture—it was what men did when they needed their hands busy while their hearts decided something.

"You're telling the truth," he said finally. It was not a compliment; it was a diagnosis. "That's the worst part. I've been lied to by experts for eleven years and I know what it sounds like, and you're not doing it. You'd actually do it. Name cleared, father honored, safe passage down. Clean hands all around."

"Yes."

"And Vhaloria goes in the ground. Again. This time with my help." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were terrible and tired. "Do you know what my father bought with his silence, Marchetti? Our lives. Mine and my mother's. He carried a woman and her unborn child over these mountains, got caught, and traded his silence for our survival, and then spent twenty years dying by inches under the weight of it until Kestrel Pass gave him permission to stop. Every bad thing that happened to me—the disgrace, the blacklist, the engineered disaster—they were all downstream of that one bargain. Silence in exchange for safety. Safety purchased with a buried truth." He shook his head slowly. "And you're offering me the identical bargain. Word for word. Just with better terms."

Odile felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs, in the place where she kept the things she did not file.

"It's not the same," she said, but even as she said it she heard how it sounded, and she stopped, and started again, more honestly. "It may be the same. I can't promise it isn't. I can only promise that I will spend the rest of my life making it less same than it has to be—that I will count the dead, and restore the names in the margins, and leave doors unlocked that my superiors want welded shut. It is not resurrection. It is memory, maintained. It is the difference between a murdered man and a forgotten one. I am offering you a murdered nation that gets to at least be mourned, instead of a murdered nation that gets to be *nothing*."

"And if she chooses otherwise?" He tilted his head toward Sable. "If the heir decides her mother's country deserves its day in court?"

"Then I will arrest her," Odile said, "or die failing to, and I will hate every minute of both. But I am asking you—both of you—to consider what Ilse Vane would have wanted. Because I've read her file. All of it, including the parts Corvane hasn't seen. And everything in it says a woman who chose disappearance over dynasty. Who raised her daughter under another name in another country and never once, in thirty years of letters intercepted by three different governments, wrote a single word of longing for the throne. She didn't run from Vhaloria, Nystrom. She ran from what claiming it would cost. Ask yourself why."

Sable made a sound—small, involuntary, the sound of a wound being pressed. Odile did not look at her. Some griefs required privacy even in company.

* * *

Emrys stood. He walked to the edge of the hollow, where the limestone shelf fell away into darkness and the valley lights of the lowlands glimmered far below, tiny and orderly, the republic's electric testimony to its own permanence. Odile let him stand there. She knew better than to hurry a decision that would define a life; she had spent her career watching people sign things, and the ones who signed fast regretted it longest.

When he turned around, his face was unreadable in the way of mountains themselves.

"I need until morning," he said. "There's something above us—in the hanging valley. My father died reaching for it, and I won't trade anything, not even my own name, until I've seen what he was reaching for. If the answer's there, I'll give you my answer tomorrow. On the descent. Whatever it is."

"That's not—"

"That's the offer," he said, and his voice had the finality of closing ice. "Take it or put me in irons, Archivist. But you climbed all day to talk, not to fight, and we both know you're too honest to pretend otherwise."

Odile Marchetti looked at him for a long moment—at the disgraced guide with his steady hands and his father's compass, at the wounded heir pretending to be a cartographer, at the small fire screened against a darkening world—and she felt, with terrible clarity, that she had just lost a negotiation she hadn't known she was having.

"Until morning," she said.

She wrapped herself in her coat at the fire's edge and did not sleep, and sometime past midnight, listening to the wind rise out of the north carrying the smell of snow that was still two ranges away but coming, she heard Emrys Nystrom whisper something to Sable Vane in the dark—too low to catch, too deliberate to be nothing—and understood that whatever the hanging valley held, the morning's answer had already begun to form without her.
