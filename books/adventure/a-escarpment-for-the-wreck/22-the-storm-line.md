## 22. The Storm Line

The snow came out of the west at midday, and it did not fall so much as arrive.

Emrys felt it before he saw it—a pressure change in his ears, a flatness in the light that turned the glacier from white to pewter, a smell like iron filings on the wind. He had spent twenty years learning the mountain's moods the way other men learned wives' tempers, and every instinct he owned stood up at once and said: *now*.

"Sable." He didn't shout. You never shouted on a glacier unless you wanted the answer to come back as an avalanche. "Camp. Now."

She was fifty meters ahead, picking her way along the medial moraine with the careful, economical steps he'd drilled into her over nine days of goat track and scree and one bad river crossing. She looked up, read his face, and turned without argument. That was the thing about her, he was learning. She argued with him about everything—pace, rations, the morality of maps—but when it mattered, she moved.

They dropped into the crevasse camp an hour later, the one Emrys had marked on their second day out: a deep blue fissure where the glacier had cracked itself open along an old stress line, its upper lip bridged by a slab of wind-packed snow thick enough to hold a team of oxen. He'd cut a entrance ramp with the shovel on the way past, half as superstition, half because a guide who survived Kestrel Pass did not leave shelters unmade behind him.

Now it would hold three people through whatever the sky intended.

Doss arrived last, as always, walking sweep with the easy rolling gait of a man who'd carried a rifle across enough ranges to stop thinking about his feet. He ducked under the snow bridge, stamped the rime off his boots, and looked at the gray light filtering down through the entrance slot.

"Early," he said.

"Three weeks early," Emrys said. "Maybe four."

"You said we'd beat it."

"I said we *might*. There's a difference, and you knew it when you signed on."

Doss grunted and began unpacking the stove without further comment. That was another thing about the mercenary: he complained exactly as much as was useful and not a syllable more. Emrys still didn't trust him—didn't trust the supply map Doss had produced from Corvane's cache, didn't trust the story of why he'd walked away from the men who'd shot at them in the narrows—but he trusted his hands, and for now hands were what they needed.

The storm hit full voice two hours after dark.

It came down the col like a freight train with no brakes, screaming through the crevasse mouth in gusts that made the snow bridge above them groan and shed fine powder that drifted down through the lamplight like ash. Sable sat against the wall with her wounded arm cradled against her chest—the graze from the narrows had closed ugly, hot to the touch, and Emrys didn't like the shine in her eyes that wasn't entirely pain—and watched the entrance slot flicker white-out, black, white-out.

"How long?" she asked.

Emrys listened. The storm had a voice, and this one had a long sentence to deliver. "Two days. Maybe three if it swings south and reloads."

"And then?"

He didn't answer immediately. He took out the map instead, spread it on the flattened ration crate between them, and weighted the corners with a carabiner, a tin cup, a stone, and the compass case he still hadn't opened.

Sable leaned in. The lamp threw their shadows huge against the blue ice wall, three giants hunched over a paper country.

"The safe pass is here." His finger found the western col, the broad saddle that every map since the annexation had marked with the confident dotted line of a maintained route. "Forty kilometers by the moraine, then the switchbacks. In good weather it's four days. We've done it in six with a client who smoked."

"But."

"But the pass closes when the cornices load. And they're loading now." He tapped the ridge line north of the col. "Every gram of snow this storm drops on that ridge hangs over the trail. Three days of this and the crossing window doesn't close—it ceases to exist until spring. We'd be sitting in this hole watching each other rot for five months."

Doss, feeding shavings to the stove, spoke without looking up. "There's another way. I've seen you looking at it all week like it owes you money."

Emrys's finger moved east, off the edge of the printed map, onto the blank margin where Sable's own hand had inked in the old names. The Karst Escarpment's eastern face dropped eight hundred meters in a series of ice chutes and rock bands that no government survey had ever bothered to draw, because no government surveyor had ever been fool enough to climb them.

His father's route.

"There," he said quietly.

Sable went very still. "That's not a route. That's a rumor."

"It's a route. It goes. My father—" The words caught, the way they always caught, like a boot in a crevasse lip. He pushed through. "My father took Ilse Vane over it in the last year of the war. Down, not up—they were fleeing the other direction. But a line is a line. What goes down can go up, if you're willing to pay the toll."

"What's the toll?" Doss asked.

"Everything, usually." Emrys traced the line with his fingertip, feeling the terrain rise under the paper the way he could feel it under his boots. "The chute system starts at the Hanging Valley—that's where the cache is, where the documents are. From there it's four pitches of ice at seventy degrees, maybe more, with a rock band in the middle that avalanches whenever the sun touches it. Then the traverse under the Serac Wall. Then the final couloir, which my father's notes call—" he almost smiled, and it hurt—"which the monks called the Throat. One way in, one way out, and the mountain decides which."

"And in winter?" Sable asked.

"In winter it's suicide." He met her eyes. "In late autumn, with fresh snow loading the slopes and no sun to consolidate it? It might be something slightly better than suicide. It might be a coin flip."

The stove hissed. Above them the bridge groaned, and a sift of powder came down through the lamplight and settled on the map like a small cold blessing.

"We have supplies for how long?" Sable said.

"Nine days. Ten if we're honest about hunger."

"And the safe pass needs three clear days we won't get. So it isn't really a choice between two routes." Her voice was steady, but he could hear the effort holding it level, the same effort she used to hold her injured arm still. "It's a choice between waiting here to be found, and going up your father's line while the mountain is still deciding whether to kill us."

"That's the arithmetic," Emrys said. "Yes."

Doss sat back on his heels. For a long moment the only sound was the storm's long scream and the tick of cooling metal. When the mercenary finally spoke, his voice had lost its professional flatness.

"I've held positions worse than this crevasse. Sat out sieges in ruins with less food and worse company." He nodded at the map. "But those were wars men started. This—" he tipped his head toward the roaring dark above, "—this doesn't negotiate. So I'll ask plain, Nystrom. Do you believe the line goes?"

Emrys looked at the map a long time. But he wasn't seeing the map. He was seeing the things he'd found over the past weeks: the cairn built in a style forty years dead, the chiseled arrow on the granite below the moraine, weathered almost back into the stone. The rusted piton driven into a crack at the head of the first chute, placed by hands that had known exactly what they were doing. His father had left a message across that face, mark by mark, and Emrys had been reading it for days now like a letter addressed to no one, or to everyone, or—to the thought arrived and settled like the snow—to *him*.

The old man had taken a pregnant woman across that face in wartime, hunted, in bad season, with gear that would embarrass a modern school group. And he'd died on Kestrel Pass trying to reach the chest that would have set them all free.

Emrys had spent his whole life believing he was nothing like his father. Sitting in the blue dark with the storm overhead, he understood at last that this was the one inheritance he couldn't refuse: the same stubbornness, the same willingness to look at an impossible line and ask not *whether* but *how*.

"I believe my father put marks on that face for someone to follow," he said. "And I believe I'm the someone."

Sable exhaled slowly. In the lamplight her face was hollowed with pain and exhaustion, and underneath both, something harder was setting like concrete. "Then we go," she said. "As soon as it breaks."

"Not the moment it breaks." Emrys held up a hand. "Fresh snow is the killer. We wait for the sun—one day of sun, minimum, to let the new load settle or slough. We watch the slopes. If the mountain sheds it clean, we climb. If it holds it—" he shrugged, "—then it's holding it for us, too, and we go anyway, carefully."

"And if it never breaks?" Doss asked. "If this storm sits on us for five days?"

Emrys looked at the dwindling stack of ration tins, at Sable's arm, at the entrance slot where the darkness pulsed white and black like a failing heart.

"Then the question answers itself," he said. "We starve comfortably or we freeze ambitiously. I've done neither. I'm told they're both educational."

Sable laughed—a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her—and then pressed her good hand over her eyes, and for a moment Emrys thought she was crying, and then he realized she was simply letting herself be tired, which on this mountain took more courage than either.

* * *

He took first watch, because he always did, because sleep came to him last anyway and left him first.

He climbed to the crevasse lip just below the bridge and wedged himself into the niche he'd cut, where he could see the entrance slot and hear the storm without wearing its full weight. The wind came through in pulses, and between pulses there were moments of terrible clarity—stars tearing open overhead for a heartbeat, the black mass of the escarpment's eastern face visible against them, vast and indifferent, before the cloud sealed shut again.

His father had looked at that face. Had studied it the way Emrys was studying it now, reading the chutes and ribs and rock bands, finding the thread of weakness that would let a human being pass through solid mountain. And then he'd gone and put his life on that thread, twice—once carrying another man's future down it, once dying short of the prize at its foot.

Emrys took out the compass case. He'd carried it for thirty years without opening it; it had been the one thing the salvage crews let him keep, the one thing his mother had pressed into his hands at fourteen with a look he'd spent decades failing to decode. The wax seal scarred into the leather matched the seals on the Vhalorian dispatches. He'd known that since the night he pried the chest open on the Greywater bank, and he had not opened the case, because some doors you only get to walk through once, and he wasn't sure what waited on the other side of this one.

Not yet, he told himself, as he had told himself every night for weeks. Not yet.

Below him, the lamp in the crevasse made a warm gold coin of light against all that blue. Sable's shadow moved across it—she wasn't sleeping either; he could tell by the angle—and Doss sat sharpening his knife with the patient rasp of a man keeping his hands busy so his mind wouldn't wander somewhere it shouldn't.

Three people in a crack in the ice, betting their lives on a dead man's route and a living woman's refusal.

The storm screamed. Emrys pulled his collar tighter and did the arithmetic again, though he'd already done it ten times, because arithmetic was a rope and right now he needed something to hold.

Supplies: nine days. Storm: two, maybe three. Settlement day: one. Climb: two days up the chute system to the Hanging Valley, one day for the cache, two days down the far side if the Throat allowed it. Total: eight days, best case, with zero margin and a wounded woman on seventy-degree ice.

Best case. On his father's route. In early winter.

Any rational guide would refuse. Any rational guide would have refused nine days ago, in the lowlands, with permits and money on the table. But Emrys Nystrom had stopped being rational somewhere on the banks of the Greywater, when a flood gave up a chest with a double-eagle stamp and his life stopped being his own.

He looked east, at the place where the escarpment's face would be if the clouds would only move, and spoke aloud, barely louder than breath, into the wind.

"Hold together," he told the mountain. "One more week. Then do whatever you want."

Between one gust and the next, the clouds tore open for three full seconds—an eternity, a gift—and the eastern face stood revealed in starlight, enormous, white-plastered, silent. Fresh snow lay heavy on every ledge and rib. But along the line his father had carved, the rock bands showed dark and clean, the chutes ran narrow and steep, and the whole route read like handwriting he suddenly, finally, knew how to read.

*It goes,* the face said. *If you're fool enough.*

The clouds closed. Emrys sat back against the ice, heart hammering, and permitted himself—for the first time since the river—not fear, but resolve, hard and bright as a piton driven home.

Behind him, down in the gold coin of lamplight, Doss's knife stopped rasping.

"Nystrom," the mercenary said quietly. "You'd better come look at this. There are lights on the western moraine."
