## 23. Corvane Revealed

The resupply point was a shepherd's hut below the Trevel Col, a stone beehive of a building with a sod roof and a door that had not closed properly since before the war. Doss Ferrin reached it an hour after dark, moving the last two miles with his lamp hooded because the valley below carried sound like a bell tower. Behind him his team was camped in the pines—Vasko on watch, the Renner twins asleep, Pell nursing the ankle he'd turned on the scree three days ago and pretending he hadn't. Ahead of him, in the hut, was a man named Ivo Kastellan who smelled of government issue boot polish no matter how far into the mountains he climbed.

Doss knocked twice, paused, knocked once. The door swung inward on a wedge of yellow light and woodsmoke, and Kastellan stood there in a wool coat too clean for the altitude, looking like a banker who'd wandered off a train platform.

"Ferrin."

"Kastellan." He stepped past the man without waiting to be invited. That was a small courtesy Kastellan resented and never mentioned, and Doss offered it every time for exactly that reason.

Inside, the hut was one room: a iron stove, a cot, crates stacked against the far wall with the Corvane shipping stencil burned into the slats. A kettle sat on the stove plate. Two cups. Kastellan had brought two cups, which meant he'd come to talk, and Doss had learned over six weeks of this contract that when Kastellan came to talk, someone above him wanted something said carefully enough that it couldn't be put in writing.

"Food first," Kastellan said, and kicked one of the crates loose from the stack. "Hard bread, sausage, the good chocolate. Medical kit's restocked. And—" he hesitated, just a fraction, "—ammunition. Another two hundred rounds."

Doss crouched at the crate and began checking the inventory by touch more than sight. Bread, hard as roof slate. Sausage in waxed casing. The chocolate wrapped in paper printed with a mountain range no one had ever climbed. The ammunition boxes were factory sealed, which told him nothing except that somebody in a warehouse had counted them.

"Two hundred rounds," he said. "For four men chasing three people through high country."

"You've had engagements."

"I've had one engagement. At the narrows." He kept his voice flat. "We lost position, we lost supplies down the couloir, and we lost the element of surprise. What we didn't do was expend two hundred rounds. We expended eleven."

Kastellan poured the kettle into both cups without asking whether Doss drank it black. He did. Everyone in this trade drank it black; it was practically a professional credential.

"The narrows," Kastellan said. "Tell me about it."

"Why?"

"Because I'm paid to ask and you're paid to answer, and neither of us enjoys it, so let's be efficient." He handed over a cup. "The girl was hit."

"Grazed. Upper arm. She'll carry a scar and nothing else. The guide moved them fast after that—north and up, onto terrain nobody sane would take with a wounded client. Which is its own problem, because it means they're on the old route now. The Vhalorian route. And that route goes where we can't follow quickly and can't flank at all."

"So you need the high col."

"I need the high col, or I need to know why we're still doing this." Doss set the cup down on the crate lid and looked at Kastellan directly. Six weeks he'd held the question behind his teeth. The photograph in his breast pocket had been there so long the crease had gone soft. "Because here's what I know, Kastellan, and I want you to listen to all of it before you give me the speech about scope and discretion.

"One: we were hired to retrieve a chest intact. The chest is gone—it went into the river, it came out of the river, and somewhere between the salvage line and the foothills it stopped mattering, because our orders changed to *the documents* without anyone telling me what documents. Two: we were told the rival claimant wants them destroyed. Fine. Rival claimants are a business I understand; there's always a cousin. Three: our orders say leave no witnesses, but every time we've had a clean chance at witnesses, the orders have bent around them. *Take the guide alive.* Take him alive, Kastellan. In my experience the only people you take alive are people someone wants to talk to—or people someone is afraid to kill."

Kastellan drank his tea. Outside, the wind worked at the sod roof with a low dry rasp, like something reading a long document aloud.

"And four?" Kastellan said at last.

"Four is the photograph." Doss took it out and laid it on the crate between them. Emrys Nystrom, younger by twenty years, standing in front of a rope coil with the half-smile of a man who has just been caught doing something he's good at. "You gave me this and told me he was dangerous. He isn't dangerous. I've fought beside dangerous men and I've buried them, and this one is competent, careful, and tired. So either your employer is afraid of the wrong man, or he's afraid of the right man for the wrong reason. Either way, I don't shoot at shadows for money. I shoot at things I can see."

Kastellan set his cup down. For a moment he looked older—not older the way men get, but older the way buildings get, settling under a weight that had been there longer than anyone admitted. He walked to the door, opened it, checked the dark, and closed it again. Then he pulled a second chair from the corner, which the hut did not own, which meant he'd carried it up the col himself, which meant this conversation had been planned down to the furniture.

"Sit," he said. "Or stand. You'll want to stand for part of it."

Doss stood.

"Forty years ago," Kastellan began, "when Vhaloria was annexed, the republic didn't conquer it. It bought it. There were men inside the Vhalorian court—officers, ministers, a handful of the old families—who sold the passes, the garrisons, and finally the royal family itself, in exchange for land grants and positions in the new administration. The purge wasn't an invasion. It was a delivery. The republic signed for goods its collaborators had already packed."

"I know the history."

"You know the version in the schoolbooks. Here's the version in the ledgers." Kastellan reached into his coat and produced a folded paper—not typed, handwritten, in a cramped clerk's hand. "One of those collaborators was a man named Aldous Veyra. Keeper of the Privy Seal. The man whose signature authorized the succession registry—the document that made the royal line legally disappear. When Ilse Vane fled north with her child, it was Veyra who informed on her. It was Veyra who named the guide who carried her over the escarpment. And it was Veyra who spent the rest of his life making sure the writ naming her heir never surfaced. He hunted it for thirty years. His son hunted it for ten more. Neither found it."

Doss felt the cold come into the room ahead of the words, the way it comes under a door before you hear footsteps.

"Aldous Veyra had a daughter," Kastellan said. "She married a man named Corvane. Their grandson runs the shipping concern whose stencil is on those crates you're inventorying."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls, a marmot or a mouse rearranged its winter.

"He's not a rival claimant," Doss said slowly.

"There is no rival claimant. There never was. The story was built for people like you—professionals who need a shape to their work. A claimant means a contest. A contest means money changing hands, and money changing hands explains everything without explaining anything." Kastellan tapped the handwritten page. "Corvane needs the writ destroyed because the writ names Ilse Vane's betrayer. Not as an accusation—as a fact, in a notarized hand, dated forty years ago, witnessed by monks who are mostly dead but not all dead. If that paper surfaces, it doesn't put a crown on anyone's head. It puts a crime on his family's name, and crimes like that have statutes of inheritance, Ferrin. Land grants. Estates. A shipping concern built on the proceeds. Half the republic's founding families would wake up wondering whose grandfather sold the passes—and Corvane's grandfather is the one who wrote the receipt."

Doss looked at the crates. Hard bread, sausage, chocolate, two hundred rounds. All of it stamped with the name of a man who wasn't buying back an inheritance. He was buying back a grave.

"The guide," Doss said. "Nystrom. Why alive?"

"Because his father was the guide who carried Ilse Vane." Kastellan said it plainly, the way you hand someone a heavy thing rather than let them fumble for it. "Aldous Veyra's informers caught the father on the return crossing. The man bought his family's lives with silence—gave up the route, gave up what he knew, and lived the rest of his days blacklisted and broken trying to reach the chest before Veyra's people did. He died on Kestrel Pass for it. The son doesn't know any of this yet. But if he reaches the monastery archive, he'll learn it, and then he won't be a washed-out guide with a grudge anymore. He'll be the last living link to the original documents. Corvane wants him found before that happens. Talked to. Turned. Or simply kept—somewhere quiet, indefinitely, until the paperwork problem resolves itself."

"*Kept.*"

"That's the word that came down."

Doss turned away and stood at the narrow window, where the glass had gone milky with age, and looked out at the dark shoulder of the escarpment rising against the stars. Twenty years ago he had pulled a boy off a cornice on Kestrel Pass—a guide's kid, all elbows and terror, clawing at the ice while the mountain ate the older man's rope team below him. Doss had carried the boy down on his back, and the boy had cried into his collar the whole way, and Doss had told him what you tell them: *you're alive, that's the whole job, you're alive.* He hadn't known the name. He knew it now. He'd known it since the narrows, when he'd studied the photograph by firelight and the creased face had finally given up its secret, and he had lain awake until dawn doing arithmetic he didn't like.

So the father had been silenced. The son had been ruined to keep him blind. And now the son was climbing his father's route toward the truth, with a mercenary band behind him under orders to make sure he never arrived—or arrived as property.

"And the girl?" Doss asked, without turning around. "Sable Vane. Is she the heir? Does Corvane know?"

"Corvane suspects. He's not certain, and certainty frightens him more than suspicion—if she's the heir, she's a symbol someone else could use; if she isn't, she's a witness who's seen too much. Either way his instruction is the same." Kastellan paused. "You know what it is."

*Leave no witnesses.* Doss had read it a dozen times, and each time the sentence had seemed to shrink, the way sentences do when you finally look straight at them. Four words. Two of them were lies. They weren't witnesses. Witnesses saw something. These were people carrying something—carrying it without knowing what it weighed.

He thought about Pell, twenty-three years old, who believed they were on a retrieval contract. About Vasko, who didn't ask questions because asking questions was how you ended up working alone. About the twins, who would do whatever the pay chit said because the pay chit had never lied to them before. Six weeks ago Doss would have said his job was to keep them alive and get them home. He still said that. The difference was that he now understood the two goals had come apart, quietly, somewhere back down the trail, and only one of them could survive the trip.

"If I refuse," he said, "what happens?"

"Nothing dramatic. Your contract terminates. Your final payment routes to an account that stops existing. And the next band gets the contract, with better intelligence than you ever had, and none of your hesitation." Kastellan came to stand beside him at the window. In the glass their reflections overlapped, two gray shapes against the black mass of the escarpment. "I'm not threatening you, Ferrin. I'm describing the machine. I've been a part in it for thirty years, and tonight I'm telling you what it's grinding toward, because—" he stopped, and Doss heard him choose the next word with visible care, "—because someone should say it out loud once. What happened to Vhaloria wasn't policy. It was theft with an army attached. And the man paying us is the grandson of the thief, spending stolen money to keep the receipt burned. I can live inside that machine. I always have. But I find I can't hand it to another man's conscience without telling him what it weighs."

Doss was silent for a long time. The wind had dropped, and in the stillness he could hear the river far below, working at the valley floor the way it had worked for ten thousand years, indifferent to every seal and signature and succession writ that men floated down it.

"The resupply," he said finally. "It stays here. My team eats from it—we're not martyrs—but the ammunition stays boxed. And you'll tell Corvane's office that I've picked up the party's trail at the high col and expect contact within three days. That buys me time and keeps the next band sitting on its hands."

Kastellan studied him. "And what will you actually do in three days?"

"What I should have done at the narrows." Doss took the photograph back, folded it along its soft crease, and put it away. "Decide which side of the gun I'm standing on. I've been paid to be on one side of it my whole career, and I've told myself the money made it simple. It turns out the money was just loud." He picked up his pack. "There's a supply map in your coat, Kastellan. Corvane's cache locations, the rotation schedule, the names of the second band waiting at the lowland garrison. I watched you check the pocket twice since you walked in. You didn't carry a chair up a col to have a conversation. You carried it to have a defection, and you needed it to be mine so yours would have somewhere to hide."

For the first time all night, Kastellan smiled—a thin, tired thing, like a crack in glaze. "The map's in the medical crate. Under the morphine. And Ferrin—the second band isn't four men. It's nine, with a radio, and they're already at the garrison. Whatever you're going to do, do it faster than you're planning to."

Doss opened the door. Cold poured in, clean and enormous, smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was already thinking about it. High on the escarpment, above the treeline, the stars were going out one by one behind a moving veil of cloud—the first storm of the season assembling itself in the dark, right on schedule, or early, the way everything in these mountains came early now, as if the year itself were hurrying toward some conclusion.

Three people were up there somewhere on his father's route—one of them bleeding, one of them lying, one of them climbing toward a truth that would break him open. Nine armed men were below them with a radio. And Doss Ferrin, who had spent twenty years believing the boy on the cornice owed him nothing and the mountain owed him less, slung his pack and started up into the dark to settle the account himself.

Behind him, in the hut, Kastellan banked the stove and waited for dawn, and the wind came back and went on reading its long document to no one at all.
