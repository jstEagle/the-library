## 24. The Turncoat

Doss Ferrin had spent twenty years learning the difference between a lie and a mistake, and the supply cache at Raven Hollow was neither.

It was a message.

He crouched over the aluminum crates in the gray light before dawn, his breath smoking, and counted what wasn't there. No rope. No high-calorie rations beyond three days' worth for four men. No spare batteries for the radios. Instead: a sealed envelope with his name on it in Corvane's precise, expensive hand, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, a map of the Karst Escarpment's southern approaches with two routes inked in red.

The western route—the safe one, the one any competent guide would take—was crossed out with a single decisive stroke.

The eastern route, the one that killed people, was circled.

*They expect him to go east,* Doss thought. *And they want us waiting there.*

He'd been chewing on the wrongness of this operation for days now, like gristle he couldn't spit out. The orders had never made sense. Retrieve the chest intact—that part was clean, professional, the kind of contract his band took twice a year without losing sleep. But *leave no witnesses* attached to a party of three, one of them a woman cartographer and one of them a blacklisted guide who hadn't held a client in years. You didn't pay Ferrin's rates to kill a schoolteacher and a washed-out mountain man. You paid those rates when you were afraid.

And then there was the photograph.

Doss had carried it since the briefing in Corvane's glass-walled office above the garrison town: Emrys Nystrom, younger than his file suggested, standing in front of a tarp-covered salvage skiff with the wary half-smile of a man who'd learned not to trust good luck. Doss had looked at it a hundred times, and every time, something at the edge of it itched—a shape just outside recognition, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

Last night, in the cold of the hollow, it had finally surfaced.

A boy on a ledge. Twenty-two years ago. A rescue on the Kestrel face that Doss himself had performed as a young climber working the valley rescue brigade, back before he'd learned that courage was a commodity and sold his. A boy with a broken wrist and frostbitten fingers, whose father stood at the base of the pitch screaming instructions up through the wind—not panicked screaming, but *precise* screaming, the voice of a man who knew every hold on that wall better than he knew his own children's names.

*Nystrom,* the man had shouted. *My son. His name is Emrys Nystrom.*

Doss had carried that boy down on his back, the kid's good arm locked around his neck, and at the bottom the father had gripped Doss's hand so hard the bones ached for a week and said nothing at all, because some men couldn't say thank you without their whole lives cracking open. Doss had thought about that family maybe five times in two decades. Now he couldn't stop thinking about them.

And Corvane wanted the boy taken alive.

Not the woman. Not the guide. The *boy*. Alive, specifically, while everyone else in the party was expendable. Doss turned the map over in his hands and understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that whatever Corvane wanted with Emrys Nystrom had nothing to do with a chest or an inheritance—and everything to do with why a disgraced guide's name appeared in orders written by one of the most powerful men in the republic.

He put the envelope in his jacket unopened. Some doors you didn't walk through unless you meant to burn the house behind you.

Then he went to brief his men on the route they'd take—which would not be the route he told Corvane they'd taken.

* * *

Vasko found him at first light, chewing the last of the hard sausage, his rifle slung across his back like an afterthought. Vasko was the best of them and the worst of them—best shot, worst conscience, a man who treated contracts like weather, something that happened to other people.

"Boss says the narrows," Vasko said, dropping onto a crate. "East approach. We're ahead of them by a day if we push."

"We're not taking the east approach."

Vasko stopped chewing. "Corvane's map says—"

"I know what Corvane's map says." Doss kept his voice level, bored even, the tone he used for men who needed managing. "Which is exactly why I don't trust it. Think about it. Corvane's people wrote that map for us, and Corvane's people have known about this operation from the start. If I were paying a fortune to bury a document, I'd make sure my hired guns were standing exactly where I expected them to stand—so that later, if anyone asked questions, the questions died with them on a scree slope." He let that sit. "We go west. High line above the Goat Track. We'll see them coming and choose our ground instead of having it chosen for us."

It was a good lie. It was built on a true foundation—distrust of employers was gospel in their trade—and Vasko nodded slowly, the suspicion rerouting itself toward Corvane where Doss wanted it.

"And the chest?"

"We take it when the opportunity's real. Not when a piece of paper tells us it should be."

Vasko grinned, all teeth. "That's why we follow you, boss."

Doss watched him go and felt the weight of what he was doing settle onto his shoulders like a pack full of stone. He'd fed his own men a false route. By nightfall, if he did the next thing he was considering, he'd be feeding Corvane's lieutenant one too. There was no version of this where he stayed clean. There was only the version where he decided, before the shooting started, which side of the gun he was standing on.

He still didn't know. That was the honest truth of it, and honesty was the one luxury he'd promised himself he could afford on this mountain. He only knew that he'd carried a boy down a cliff once, and the boy had lived, and somewhere between then and now Doss had forgotten what that felt like.

Maybe the mountain remembered it for him.

* * *

The lieutenant met him at the second cache, two days later, in a fold of country below the treeline where the pines grew thick enough to swallow voices. Rennick was Corvane's man through and through—soft-handed, soft-voiced, with the patient eyes of someone who collected information the way other men collected debts.

"You've deviated from the plan," Rennick said, without greeting.

"The plan was a trap."

Rennick smiled. "The plan was insurance. Mr. Corvane is very thorough."

"Mr. Corvane," Doss said, "circled the eastern route and crossed out the western one. Any guide worth his salt takes the west. So either Corvane doesn't know mountains, or he knows exactly which way Nystrom will run and wants us positioned to catch him—and wants us dead right after, so there's no one left who knows how much he paid and for what." He stepped closer. "I don't work contracts with coffins already measured for me, Rennick. So here's what happens. My band holds the high line west. When Nystrom comes through—and he'll come through the west, because his father's route knowledge runs that way—we take the chest and we call it done. And you tell Corvane whatever keeps him writing checks."

Something moved behind Rennick's eyes—calculation, quick and cold. "You've read the letter."

Doss hadn't. But he watched Rennick's face do the arithmetic and understood that the envelope in his jacket was worth more unopened than opened, that Rennick had just confirmed its existence and its weight in a single flicker of alarm.

"There are things in this operation," Rennick said softly, "that Mr. Corvane would prefer you didn't concern yourself with. The Nystrom boy, for instance. Your orders say alive. I'd suggest you focus on that and let the rest stay buried. Burial is rather the family specialty."

*The family specialty.* Doss turned the phrase over as Rennick signed for the resupply and melted back into the trees. Family. Not Corvane's business interests. Not the republic's archives. *Family.*

He walked back up toward the high line alone, and by the time he reached the ridge the decision had made itself, the way avalanches make themselves—one grain too many, and then the whole slope moving.

He was going to give Emrys Nystrom the supply map, the radio frequencies, and everything else he had. And then he was going to find out what kind of man you had to become to buy your way off a mountain you'd sold yourself on.

* * *

Catching the party alone was the easy part. Catching it without getting shot was the problem.

Doss shadowed them for half a day above the Goat Track, watching through his scope as the guide moved his two charges along the broken ground with a competence that made Doss's professional pride wince and his personal dread deepen. Nystrom read the mountain the way his father must have—choosing lines before the terrain forced them, placing his clients where the rock did the guarding. The woman moved well too, favoring her left side where the graze from the narrows fight was healing, but moving. They were slower than a fresh party and tougher than Doss had priced them for.

Near dusk, Nystrom sent the women into a shallow overhang camp and went out alone to check the anchor line for the morning's traverse. Alone. Deliberately alone, Doss realized—Nystrom had posted himself as sentry, and sentries who separate themselves from their charges are either fools or fishermen.

Doss chose to believe fisherman.

He came down through the rocks with his hands visible and empty, stopping ten meters out, close enough to talk and far enough that a knife wouldn't reach him before his pistol—though his pistol was stowed, and they both knew it.

"Don't," Nystrom said. He'd heard him, of course; the man had ears like his father. He stood with his back half-turned, a coil of rope over one shoulder, and his voice was flat as slate. "Whatever this is, I'm not in the mood."

"You were expecting me."

"I was expecting *someone*. Corvane's dogs have been circling since the narrows." Nystrom turned fully now, and up close his face was thinner than the photograph, carved down by the altitude and the weeks, with eyes that had gone from wary to flinty somewhere on the escarpment. "So. Which are you? The one who shoots, or the one who talks first?"

"The one who's about to hand you a map."

He took it out slowly—oilcloth bundle, the works: the annotated route map, the radio frequency card, the cache locations, the photograph of the supply schedule Rennick had signed. He laid them on a flat stone between them and stepped back.

Nystrom didn't move. "Why."

"Because your employer—" Doss began.

"She's not my employer anymore. She's—" Something crossed his face, too fast to name. "Doesn't matter. Why."

"Because the orders say take you alive and leave no witnesses, and I've spent six weeks trying to figure out why a blacklisted guide matters enough to spend what Corvane's spending. Because his lieutenant let slip that burial is a *family* specialty. And because twenty-two years ago I carried a boy with a broken wrist down the Kestrel face, and his father squeezed my hand hard enough to crack bones, and I've been wondering ever since what happened to that boy." Doss held the flinty gaze. "I'm looking at him. And I don't like what's been hunting him."

For a long moment there was only the wind moving through the scree, and the distant tick of cooling rock.

"That rescue," Nystrom said carefully. "Big man. Red beard going gray at the chin. Carried me down singing some filthy marching song to keep me conscious."

"Your father hated that song."

"He hummed it for years after. Thought I didn't know." Nystrom's jaw worked. "He's dead. Kestrel Pass. The disaster that got me blacklisted."

"I know. I've read the reports." Doss hesitated, then gave him the last of it, the thing that had been turning in his gut since Raven Hollow. "Nystrom. The eastern route on Corvane's map was marked for an ambush. I moved my men west and told them a story. But Corvane's people will figure out I've gone sideways, probably within a day. When they do, they won't send Rennick. They'll send everyone. You need to be off this mountain or through it before that happens, and you need to know that the man who hired me isn't after a chest. He's after *you*, alive, and I don't think it's mercy."

"Why alive?"

"If I open that letter, I stop being able to choose. Right now I'm a turncoat on instinct. Open it, and I'm a turncoat on evidence, and evidence gets people killed faster." He shrugged. "Superstition. Call it that."

Nystrom studied him the way he'd study a suspect snow bridge—probing for the hollow places. Then he moved, fast, and Doss almost had the knife out in time.

Almost.

The blade came to rest against Doss's throat, cold and steady, Nystrom's other hand locking his wrist. Up close his breath smelled of tea and woodsmoke, and his eyes held no anger at all, which was worse.

"Trust is negotiated at knifepoint on this mountain," Nystrom said quietly. "You'll forgive the terms."

"Forgiven," Doss said, and didn't swallow.

"Here's the negotiation. You walk into our camp tonight, you eat our food, you tell Sable Vane everything you just told me, and she decides whether you live to see morning. Not me. Her. Because she's the one your employer's mercenaries grazed at the narrows, and because I'm tired of being the only one carrying decisions that should be shared." The blade withdrew a centimeter. "If she says yes, you're with us until the far side. If she says no—"

"She won't say no. She needs every gun on this ridge and she knows it."

"She might say no anyway," Nystrom said. "She's stubborn that way. It's her best quality." He stepped back, knife vanishing into his belt, and jerked his chin toward the overhang where firelight was beginning to glow. "Come on, then, Ferrin. Let's go see what the lady says."

They picked their way down together through the blue dusk, two armed men walking side by side with nothing settled between them except the direction of travel. At the edge of the firelight, Sable Vane rose from her bedroll with a pistol already in her hand—she'd heard them coming too, Doss realized; the whole party was all ears and old wounds—and looked from Nystrom to the stranger with an expression that weighed lives the way a moneylender weighs coins.

"Well?" she said.

"He brought a map," Nystrom said. "And a confession. And he wants to defect."

Doss spread his empty hands. "Redemption's on the table," he said. "Nobody's accepted yet."

Sable looked at him for a long, cold moment. Then she turned to Nystrom, and Doss saw her glance flick—to the map on the stone, to the knife at the guide's belt, to the darkening eastern sky where, in a day or less, the rest of Doss's band would come over the col with orders none of them had read closely enough.

"Start talking," she said. "And Ferrin—if one word of it smells like bait, the mountain gets your body and Corvane gets your regrets."

Doss sat down by the fire, and began.
