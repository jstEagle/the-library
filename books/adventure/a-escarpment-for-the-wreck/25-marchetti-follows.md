## 25. Marchetti Follows

Odile Marchetti had spent thirty-one years in the republic's archives, and in all that time she had learned one truth that never made it into any training manual: the most dangerous thing a person could do was read carefully. Reading carefully led to questions. Questions led to ledgers. Ledgers led to names, and names, in her country, led to graves—usually other people's, occasionally one's own.

She had read carefully at the Monastery of Saint Aldric. She had found Corvane's grandfather in the purge ledgers, inked neat as a confession, and she had sat in the abbot's cold scriptorium for a long time afterward with her hands flat on the table, listening to the wind worry the shutters, understanding that her career had just ended and her conscience had just begun.

The abbot had watched her from the doorway, ancient and unimpressed.

"You could put it back," he said. "The ledger. Unread."

"I could."

"You won't."

"No," she agreed. "I won't."

* * *

She left the monastery at first light, alone.

Her escort—two men from the Interior Ministry who had been assigned to "assist" her, which in ministry dialect meant to report on her—she had left in the valley town with a forged requisition for vehicle maintenance and a note explaining that she was returning to the capital by rail. The note would buy her perhaps two days. Perhaps less. It didn't matter. Two days was enough to lose herself in the foothills, and after the foothills there were no roads to lose her on.

Her pack was wrong for the mountains and she knew it. She had bought what the valley town offered: a canvas rucksack with a broken strap she'd repaired with wire, boots two sizes generous, a coil of rope that the outfitter had assured her, with a straight face, was "probably rated for something." She had climbed in her youth—university alpine club, the Dolomiten annex, twenty years and forty pounds ago—and her body remembered the grammar if not the vocabulary. Her knees informed her of this within the first hour.

The route was not difficult to find. Nystrom's party had left the monastery two days ahead of her, and they were three people moving deliberately, one of them wounded. They left sign like a confession: a boot print in a seep, a wrapper tucked under a stone against the wind, a cairn rebuilt where the trail forked. Odile had spent her life reading documents. Tracks were documents. They said: we were here, we were careful, we were afraid.

She followed.

By the second day she had gained the treeline and lost the certainty that this was anything other than madness. The escarpment rose ahead of her like a verdict—the great limestone wall of the Karst, streaked with old snow, its upper reaches wrapped in cloud that moved with the slow menace of something deciding. The safe pass, the one any sensible party would take, lay to the east. The tracks did not go east.

The tracks went north, toward the chute.

She had read Nystrom's file. All of it, including the sealed annexes, which was how she'd known from the beginning that the blacklist was a fiction stitched by someone with ministry access. She had read his father's file too, or what survived of it: Aldric Nystrom, guide, deceased on Kestrel Pass, forty years ago this autumn, in the company of persons unknown. Persons unknown. In the archives, that phrase was a door. Behind it, always, was a name someone had paid to remove.

She camped that night in a hollow below the last pines, and the cold came down off the wall like water, and she lay in her inadequate bag listening to the mountain tick and settle, and she asked herself the question she had been refusing to ask since the scriptorium: *what exactly do you think you're going to do when you catch them?*

She had no answer. She had a pistol, a warrant that was no longer worth the paper, and the growing suspicion that the only thing she could offer Emrys Nystrom was the one thing he had every reason to refuse: her word.

In the morning she climbed on anyway.

* * *

She caught sight of them on the third day, and the sight nearly ended her expedition on the spot.

They were high on the shoulder of the chute approach, three dark figures on a white slope, moving with the careful, spaced-out rhythm of people who understood the terrain. Through the battered binoculars she'd requisitioned along with the helicopter she was forbidden to use, she picked them out one by one. Nystrom, in front, breaking trail, moving like the mountain was a language he'd been raised in. A man she didn't know—big, deliberate, carrying too much, which meant he was either a prisoner or a defector, and his position in the rope team suggested the latter. And the third—

The third was a woman, slighter than the others, favoring her left side, and Odile felt her stomach drop through the ice of the slope, because the woman was roped last, on the outside of the line, above the fall line.

*Amateurs,* Odile thought, with a fury that surprised her by its heat. *You put the wounded one in the middle. You put her in the middle or you put her down and you turn around.* And then, underneath the fury, colder: *they know. They know she's hurt, they know the route is killing them, and they're going on anyway, because whatever's up there is worth more to them than—*

Than what? She didn't finish the thought. She didn't have to. She had spent three days climbing toward people she had been sent to destroy, and somewhere on the second day the mission had quietly inverted, the way a document inverts when you hold it to the light and see the watermark. She wasn't following them to stop them. She was following them because they were the only people on this mountain who knew what the truth was, and she had decided—somewhere between the treeline and the last pines, without ceremony, without permission—that the truth was going to come off this mountain alive.

Even if she had to carry it herself.

* * *

The crevasse took Sable Vane at midafternoon.

Odile was perhaps four hundred meters below and to the east, traversing a bench of old snow, when she heard it—not the crack itself, which was swallowed by the glacier's own groaning, but the scream, short and human and cut off, and then Nystrom's voice bellowing a word she couldn't make out at that distance but whose meaning was universal in every language on every mountain: *hold.*

She watched through the binoculars with her heart going like a fist on a door. The rope team had stopped. The big man had thrown himself flat. Nystrom was at the lip, paying out rope, his whole body a single line of tension. The rope ran into a slot in the snowfield that had been invisible from above and below alike—a snow bridge, thinned by the warm autumn, gone.

They got her out. Of course they got her out; Odile watched the slow, agonizing mechanics of it, the big man as deadman anchor, Nystrom hauling hand over hand, and then a long terrible interval where nothing moved and Odile's breath fogged the binoculars and she made herself breathe again. Then movement: a figure rising out of the slot, hauled up the lip, and the three of them collapsed together on the snow like puppets with cut strings.

But she had seen what they hadn't. From her angle, lower on the glacier, she could see the whole architecture of the snowfield—the way the bridge had failed, and the way the failure had a twin. Another bridge, thirty meters on, on the exact line they would have to travel, sagging in exactly the same way, a trap set by the same warm week. They would walk into it within the hour. They would walk into it in the flat exhausted stupor of people who had just survived one fall and believed the mountain had taken its due.

Odile stood on her bench of snow and made the calculation that would define the rest of her life, and it took her perhaps four seconds, which she would later consider the fastest decision of her career and the first honest one.

Then she started up.

* * *

She reached them an hour later, and she made sure they saw her coming—no stalking, no advantage taken. She walked the last two hundred meters in the open, hands visible, and she watched Nystrom come up the slope toward her with the rope still coiled over his shoulder and an expression she had seen on a thousand faces in a thousand interrogation rooms: the look of a man deciding what you are before you can tell him.

"Archivist," he said. It was not a greeting.

"Marchetti." She stopped six feet away, close enough to talk, far enough to be polite about the pistol on his hip and the one on hers. Her breath came hard; the altitude had been collecting its interest all day. "You crossed the snowfield."

"We did."

"You crossed it on the line of the fall. You put your wounded client on the outside of the rope." She was too tired to soften it. "There's a second bridge. Thirty meters on, same sag, same warm-week rot. You'd have found it with her weight."

Something moved behind his eyes—not gratitude, not yet. Recalculation. "You followed us from the monastery."

"From before the monastery."

"Your ministry thinks you're on a train."

"My ministry," Odile said, "thinks a great many things." She opened her pack, slowly, and took out the flask of tea she'd been husbanding since the treeline, and held it out. It was a small gesture and she knew it was a small gesture and she made it anyway, because on a mountain, warmth is an argument no one can refute.

Nystrom looked at the flask. He looked at her. Behind him, the big man—the one she now placed, from the wanted circulars, as Doss Ferrin, which raised questions she shelved for later—had moved to flank her without appearing to move at all. And beyond them, sitting against a pack with her leg bound and her face gray with pain and adrenaline's aftermath, was Sable Vane, watching Odile with an expression that Odile had also seen in archives: the look of someone recognizing a document they'd hoped was lost.

"You read the ledgers," Sable said. Not a question.

"I read the ledgers."

"Then you know what's in them."

"I know what's in them," Odile said, "and I know what my superiors want done about what's in them, and I know what Corvane wants done about what's in them, and I have decided—" her voice caught on the altitude and she pushed through it, "—I have decided that I am no longer in that business."

The wind came off the wall above them and filled the silence, and in the silence Odile Marchetti, thirty-one years a servant of the republic, stood on a glacier in boots that didn't fit and waited to find out if she was a prisoner, a corpse, or—against every probability she could calculate—an ally.

* * *

They talked for an hour, and it was the hardest negotiation of her life, harder than any ministry budget fight, because there was nothing to trade and everything to trust.

Nystrom did not soften. She respected that; a man who softened on a glacier didn't live to be a man on a glacier. He laid out the terms like a route: she would rope in behind them, she would carry her share, she would take orders on the mountain the same as anyone, and the moment they were down, the moment there was flat ground and law, all bets resumed and she was the enemy again.

"Understood," she said.

"And this," he said, and his voice changed, dropped into something rawer, "whatever you think the writ is worth, whatever deal you were sent to make me—my answer is no. It was no in the valley and it's no up here. You can climb with us. You can't buy from us."

"I'm not here for the writ."

"Then what are you here for?"

Odile looked past him, up the glacier, to where the chute cut the wall like a scar—his father's route, she knew now, the one that killed Aldric Nystrom, the one the whole republic had been built on top of. She thought of the ledger in the scriptorium, the neat ink, the name. She thought of thirty-one years of filing truth in the dark.

"Proof," she said. "Not the writ. I don't care about the writ. Corvane can have the writ, the throne, the whole resurrected kingdom, and it will change nothing that matters, because a dead nation can't be convicted." She heard her own voice go quiet and precise, the voice she used for the reading room. "But a living crime can. Your grandfather's collaborator is alive in that family, Nystrom, and his grandson is on this mountain trying to burn the evidence, and I have spent my whole life in an archive where men like him decide what gets kept and what gets ash. I'm here because I'm done carrying ash."

The wind again. Sable Vane, against her pack, was watching her with something that had thawed several degrees. Doss Ferrin had not moved from her flank, but he had stopped looking at her like a target and started looking at her like a problem, which on a mountain was nearly the same as respect.

"And if we get down," Nystrom said. "If we get down with what we're after. What then? You file your report, the ministry buries it, and we're all back where we started, except colder."

"Then I do what I should have done thirty years ago," Odile said. "I take it out of the archive. I put it in the street. I'm an old woman with a pension and nothing left to lose, Mr. Nystrom, and I promise you—" and here, for the first time on the mountain, she smiled, and it was not a kind smile, "—I read very carefully, and I write very clearly, and I have been waiting my entire career for a document worth the trouble."

Nystrom studied her for a long moment. Then he took the flask of tea after all, drank, and handed it back.

"Rope's behind you," he said. "Ferrin, check her knots. We cross the second bridge on the east line, single file, and nobody—" he glanced back at Sable, and Odile saw the fear in him, plain and unarchived, "—nobody walks flat anywhere on this glacier. Ever again."

* * *

They crossed the second bridge at dusk, and Odile went third in line, and when the snow beneath her boots flexed—that unmistakable hollow drumbeat of a void wearing a disguise—her whole body understood, in one cold flash, what would have happened to the woman behind her. She crossed without stopping, because you cross without stopping, and on the far side she turned and watched Sable Vane come across last, favoring her leg, pale as the snowfield, alive.

Their eyes met over the rope.

"Thank you," Sable said. It was the first thing she'd said to Odile that wasn't a question.

"Don't thank me," Odile said. "I'm still the enemy. I'm just the enemy who's read the same books." She shouldered her pack and looked up the glacier, where the chute waited in the last violet light, a seam of ice going up the wall into the dark like a sentence that hadn't been finished.

Above them, at the head of the glacier, on a ridge against the sky, something glinted. Once. Twice. The sun off glass, or off a scope, there and gone.

Nystrom saw it too. She watched him see it—watched the whole line of him change, the tiredness burned off in a second, replaced by the flat bright alertness of a man who has been hunted before and knows the difference between wind and witness.

"Camp," he said quietly. "Now. Cold camp, no fire, and nobody stands on the skyline."

They moved into the shadow of the icefall as the light died, four strangers roped together on a glacier that had killed better parties than theirs, and Odile Marchetti, enemy and ally, archivist and apostate, took her place in the line and climbed.
