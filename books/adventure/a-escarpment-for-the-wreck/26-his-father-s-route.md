## 26. His Father's Route

The snow held off through the night, as if even the weather were holding its breath.

Emrys woke before dawn in the crevasse camp with his back against a wall of blue ice and Sable's breathing shallow beside him. Marchetti was already awake—she slept like a soldier now, in snatches, one hand always near her pack—and when he sat up she handed him a cup of melted snow without a word. The tea was terrible. He drank it anyway.

"Three days," she said. "You said the safe pass closes in three days."

"I said it *might* close in three days." He looked up at the sky, which was the color of dirty wool stretched over wire. "I'm not willing to bet her life on might."

"So it's the other route."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. Marchetti had been reading him for two weeks now, and she'd learned that Emrys Nystrom's silences were louder than his arguments.

Sable woke an hour later, gray-faced, her side bound where the mercenary's bullet had grazed her above the hip. The wound had stopped weeping; the cold was doing what a medic would have done, sealing everything down to a dull throb. She could walk. She'd proven that yesterday, on the argument of it, marching twenty paces past him and back just to end the discussion before it started.

"The deadly route," she said, when he laid it out. Not a question either.

"My father's route."

She was quiet for a moment. Wind moved across the lip of the crevasse and sifted fine snow down onto them like flour through a sieve. "Then let's go meet him," she said.

* * *

They left the crevasse camp at first light with everything they owned on their backs—which, after the Narrows, amounted to one rope, six ice screws, two stoves, a battered tent, Marchetti's pistol, four days of food, and the letters from the lowland archive wrapped in oilcloth against Emrys's spine. Doss's supply map rode in his breast pocket, folded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth.

The route began where the safe pass ended: at a wall of rock that no sane guide would look at twice. It rose out of the glacier's shoulder like the hull of a capsized ship, eight hundred meters of mixed ground—rock, ice, and the arguments between them—leading to a col that wasn't marked on any map printed in the last forty years.

But it had been marked once.

Emrys found the first cairn within ten minutes of leaving the glacier, and stopped so suddenly that Sable walked into his pack.

It was small, waist-high, built of flat slabs stacked with a mason's patience, and it had survived four decades of avalanches and thaw because whoever built it had set it in the lee of a granite fin, tucked into the one pocket of stillness on the whole slope. The stones were furred with lichen the color of old brass. Someone had wedged a shard of slate into the topmost gap, and though weather had eaten the surface, Emrys could still make out the chiseled stroke—a single arrow, pointing up and left.

His father's hand. He knew it the way you know a voice through a wall. The same strokes were on the lintel of the house in Greywater, carved the year Emrys was born: *Nystrom*, and beneath it, crooked, added later by a boy with a borrowed chisel, *and son*.

"He built cairns," Sable said softly. She'd come up beside him, breath steaming, and was looking at the marker with an expression Emrys couldn't read—or maybe could, and didn't want to. "On a route nobody was supposed to follow."

"That's the point of them." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "A cairn isn't a signpost. It's a promise. It says *someone came this way alive*. It says the next person can too."

"And you believe that?"

He looked at the arrow again. Up and left. Into the wall.

"I believe he wanted me to find them," he said. "Whether I believe the rest is a different question."

* * *

The first pitch nearly killed them before the sun cleared the ridge.

The rock was worse than it looked from below—which was saying something, because from below it looked like a graveyard. Forty years of freeze-thaw had chewed the holds to gravel. Emrys led it unprotected for the first sixty feet, placing nothing, moving the way his father must have moved: fast, light, committing to each hold fully or not at all, because hesitation on rotten stone was more dangerous than speed. Behind him Marchetti climbed like a woman who resented every meter and refused to be beaten by any of them. Sable came last, slow, favoring her right side, her jaw set so tight he could see the muscle jumping from fifteen feet away.

At the belay ledge he brought them up and found the second marker waiting.

Not a cairn this time. A piton.

It was driven into a horizontal crack at chest height, and it was the rust color of dried blood, flaked orange, its head deformed where someone had hammered it home hard enough to bend the steel. A piton placed in desperation or in haste—or both. Emrys touched it and the rust came off on his fingers like a wound reopened.

"You could still clip it," Marchetti said behind him. She was studying the crack line, professional despite herself. "Question is whether you'd want to."

"No." He said it without thinking, then heard how it sounded, and made himself say the rest. "Forty years in a freeze-thaw crack. That pin is a lie now. It looks solid. It would hold my weight until it didn't." He wiped the rust on his trousers. "My father trusted it. That's not good enough for me."

Marchetti looked at him for a long moment. "That's the first sensible thing anyone's said on this mountain," she said, and started racking screws.

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the mountain gave up its secrets the way the river had given up the chest—one piece at a time, each one heavier than the last.

The third marker was an arrow chiseled directly into the rock face itself, deep enough to outlast the stone around it, pointing across a traverse that Emrys took three full minutes to convince himself was possible. The fourth was a cairn again, larger, sheltering under an overhang—and inside it, wrapped in lead foil gone black with age, a stub of candle and a box of matches fused into a single brick of corrosion. A bivouac cache. His father had planned to sleep here. Had perhaps slept here, in this notch of wind-scoured granite, with the whole dark escarpment leaning over him.

Emrys sat down on the ledge beside it and, for a moment, could not move.

He had spent twenty years building a story about his father and Kestrel Pass. The story was simple, the way the stories you build to survive are simple: Aldric Nystrom had made a mistake. A bad judgment in bad weather, the kind of error that ends careers and sometimes people, and his son had inherited the shame of it along with the compass case and the scar. Emrys had carried that story like a stone in his chest, and he had hated his father a little for dying, and hated himself more for being the son of the man who got eleven people killed.

Now he sat on a ledge his father had cut a rest stop into, forty years before the disaster, on a route that existed on no map, marked with a craftsman's care for a traveler who hadn't yet been born—and the story came apart in his hands like wet paper.

This wasn't a mistake. This was a mission.

"He knew," Emrys said aloud. He hadn't meant to say it. Sable looked up from retying her boot; Marchetti went still over her stove. "He knew about the chest. He knew where it was going. He scouted this route—he *engineered* it—" Emrys pressed his palm flat against the chiseled arrow, against stone cold enough to burn. "And then they caught him. And whatever they did to him, whatever they threatened—it worked, because he never came back here. Until Kestrel Pass. Until the end."

Sable crossed the ledge and sat down beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his. She didn't say anything. Below them the glacier sprawled white and blind, and above them the col waited, and between those two facts grief moved through Emrys like weather through a valley—unavoidable, directional, passing through rather than staying.

"He left these for whoever followed," Sable said finally. "He couldn't have known it would be you."

"He left them for *somebody*." Emrys stood. His legs were steady. That surprised him. "Somebody who needed the way. That's the job. That was always the job." He shouldered his pack. "Come on. We're losing the light window."

* * *

The afternoon turned the route cruel.

Above the traverse the angle steepened and the character changed: ice over rock, rock under ice, and everywhere the mountain's long memory of violence—fractures filled with verglas, gullies polished smooth by avalanches that had run here since before either nation drew its lines. Emrys led the whole afternoon, placing screws with a miser's economy, and the party moved as a single organism roped together by necessity if nothing else. Marchetti seconded like a metronome. Sable climbed with a ferocity that had nothing to do with fitness and everything to do with will, and Emrys found himself adjusting his pace not to spare her but to match her, because slowing down would have insulted them both.

At the base of the final headwall, as the light went bronze and long, he found the fifth marker.

It stopped him the way the others hadn't.

It was a cairn—but built differently. The others had been functional, patient, a mason's work. This one was hurried. Stones jammed into a gap rather than stacked, some of them barely seated, as if the builder had been racing the dark or the weather or something worse. And on the largest slab, instead of an arrow, there were words.

Chiseled small, in a hurry, the letters shallow and uneven:

**N — THE COL GOES. TRUST THE ICE NOT THE ROCK. FORGIVE ME.**

Emrys read it four times. His breath fogged the stone and he wiped it clear with his sleeve and read it again.

*N.* Not a name. An initial. Or—

Or a son.

"Forgive me," Sable read aloud, very quietly. "Forgive you for what?"

"For turning back." Emrys heard his own voice as if it belonged to someone standing nearby. "Kestrel Pass was on the far side. Everyone said he died trying to force the pass in a storm. But he wasn't forcing the pass." He touched the last word of the carving, the deepest-cut letter, the one his father had spent the most time on. "He was coming *here*. To this route. To reach the cache ahead of them—or to finish what he'd started forty years ago. And the storm caught him on the wrong side of his own escape line, and he turned back, and the mountain—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "The mountain did what mountains do."

Marchetti said nothing. She had taken off her glove and was looking at the inscription with an expression Emrys had never seen on her—the archivist's mask entirely gone, and underneath it something older and sadder, the face of a woman who had spent her career cataloguing what fire leaves behind and recognized, in five chiseled words, exactly that kind of remnant.

"He was a guide," Marchetti said at last. "A real one."

"Yes."

"They'll want to know that. The families. When this is over—they'll want to know their fathers and daughters died behind a man who was worth following." She pulled her glove back on, and the mask came up with it, but not all the way. "Make sure they hear it."

Emrys nodded. Then he took out his knife.

* * *

He worked by headlamp while the others made camp in the lee of the cairn, and the work was simple: beneath his father's inscription, on the same slab, he cut a reply.

It took him half an hour. His hands were shaking—not from cold, or not only from cold—and the letters came out uneven, but he didn't care, because the message wasn't for the mountain and it wasn't for history. It was for one man, dead twenty years, on the chance that the dead can read stone.

**CAME THIS WAY. FOUND THE END OF IT. NOT YOUR FAULT. NEVER WAS. GO HOME.**

He sat back on his heels and looked at the two inscriptions together—father and son, forty years apart, the same chisel-stroke hand running in the family like a vein of ore—and felt something shift in his chest that he had no name for. Not peace. Peace was too small a word. It was closer to what he imagined a man felt setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying, the sudden strange lightness of a body unaccustomed to its own weight.

Grief didn't leave. He understood that now, on this ledge, in this wind. But it could change cargo. It could carry something else.

"Emrys." Sable's voice, low, from the edge of the camp. "You need to see this."

* * *

She was standing at the lip of the col, where the headwall spilled over into the hanging valley beyond, and her headlamp was aimed downslope into the blue dusk. Emrys crossed to her and followed the beam.

The hanging valley opened below them like a cupped hand: a bowl of snow and shattered rock walled in on three sides, invisible from every approach except this one. And in the middle distance, at the base of a rock rib that split the bowl like a keel, there was a structure. Small. Man-made. A cairn, but vast—a tower of stones taller than a man, ancient, its silhouette unmistakable even in failing light.

And beside it, half-buried in the drifts, something that caught the lamplight and threw it back in a thin, bright line.

Metal.

Sable's breath came fast and shallow. Marchetti had come up silently behind them, and even she made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"Nobody's been here," Sable whispered. "In forty years. Nobody's found it."

Emrys stood at the lip of his father's route, at the top of the climb that had killed him, and looked down at the place his father had died trying to reach. The wind came up out of the hanging valley and broke over the col and moved on, indifferent, carrying nothing.

"We camp here," he said. "First light. All three of us go down together."

Below them, in the last of the light, the metal glinted once more—and went dark.
