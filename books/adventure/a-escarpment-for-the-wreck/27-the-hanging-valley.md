## 27. The Hanging Valley

The route stopped being a route an hour after dawn.

Emrys stood at the lip of the couloir and studied it the way he'd studied everything for three days now—with his father's eyes, or what he imagined were his father's eyes, reading stone the way Odile Marchetti read ash. The chute above them was a seam of ice and shattered rock running up into cloud, steep enough that their breath came back at them off its face. At its top, barely visible through the weather haze, a shoulder of granite leaned out over nothing, and behind that shoulder, if the old markings told the truth, lay a valley no map had ever named.

Sable watched him work the terrain with her hands jammed in her armpits against the cold. Her ribs still ached where the graze had scabbed over, and every step since the narrows had been a negotiation between what she wanted to reach and what her body agreed to carry. But she'd said nothing about turning back, and neither had he, and somewhere in that shared silence a kind of contract had been signed that neither of them would have put into words.

"That's it?" she asked. "That's the way up?"

"That's the way." Emrys crouched and brushed snow from a boulder at the base of the chute. Beneath it, half-eroded by forty years of meltwater, a chiseled arrow pointed upward—crude, deep-cut, made by someone who'd wanted it found by the right person and missed by everyone else. "He cut these on the way down, I think. After he hid it all." His voice did something strange on *hid*, a small fracture she was learning to listen for. "He knew he'd come back for it. He just didn't know when."

"Or whether," Sable said quietly.

"Or whether." He straightened. "We rope up here. The ice is old—it'll take screws, but it'll be rotten in places. You stay three meters behind me, no closer. If I go, you don't catch me. You let me swing and you climb around."

"I've heard this speech before."

"You've survived it before. That's not the same as having listened."

She almost smiled. Almost. There wasn't much room left in her for smiling; the last week had burned most of it away, along with her certainty about nearly everything—her name, her mother's silences, the shape of the life she'd thought she was living. What remained was thinner and harder and, she suspected, more honest. She clipped into the rope and looked up at the white seam rising into the clouds.

"All right," she said. "Show me my mother's road."

* * *

The chute took four hours.

It took four hours and most of what Sable had left. Twice the ice under Emrys's boots calved away in plates the size of doors and went whispering down past her into the void below, and twice she pressed herself against the slope and waited for the rope to go taut with his weight, and both times it didn't—he'd read the failure coming, moved left, moved right, placed his tools in the sound spots the way a man reads a sentence ahead of where he's speaking. She began to understand, watching him, that this was what his father had been. Not a hero in any storybook sense. A craftsman. Someone who had learned the mountain's grammar so thoroughly that even grief couldn't make him illiterate in it.

Near the top the angle eased, and then the granite shoulder was above them, and Emrys traversed beneath it with his hand trailing the rock like a man greeting an old acquaintance. At a shallow corner he stopped.

"Here," he said.

The cairn was small—a dozen stones stacked with deliberate care, furred with frost. He dismantled it with his bare fingers, taking each rock off as if it might bruise, and underneath, wedged into a crack lined with lead sheeting gone white with oxidation, was a tin box the size of a bread loaf.

Nobody spoke. The wind spoke for all of them, worrying the rope, plucking at their clothing. Sable's heart was doing something she didn't have a name for—not pounding, exactly. Hovering.

Emrys lifted the box out and set it on a flat slab. His hands were steady. She noticed that specifically, because hers weren't, and because she could see the effort the steadiness cost him, the way you can see the effort in a bridge holding a weight it was never rated for.

"It should be you," he said.

She blinked. "What?"

"He carried it for your mother. Forty years ago, a man named Nystrom carried Ilse Vane's whole life over this escarpment in his arms, and it killed him trying to do it twice." He pushed the box an inch toward her across the stone. "I'm not going to be the one who opens it."

Sable looked at the tin. Frost had sealed the lid in a thin rime. Underneath the frost, stamped into the metal, was the double-eagle—and beside it, smaller, almost hidden, a second mark: a compass rose. The same rose that lived on the lid of Emrys's compass case. The same rose, she realized with a slow cold spreading through her chest, that had been branded into the wax of the succession writ they'd carried up half a country.

Two families' marks, side by side, on one box.

Her hands shook so badly it took her three tries to break the seal.

* * *

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were papers.

She unwrapped them on the slab with the wind tearing at the corners and Emrys kneeling close, anchoring the cloth with his knees, and together they read what Ilse Vane had carried out of a burning country while pregnant with the daughter who now knelt freezing above a hanging valley forty years later.

The first document was the original succession writ—the twin of the one in Emrys's pack, but older, earlier, executed in Vhaloria itself before the fall. It named her. Not by the name she used. By the name she'd never been given: *the child of Ilse Vane, born in exile, rightful heir to the valleys and the high seats.* Below the formal language, in her mother's handwriting—the handwriting Sable had seen on grocery lists and margin notes and birthday cards her whole life without ever once suspecting it—was a single line:

*I sign this so it exists. Not so it is used.*

Below that, letters. Dozens of them, bundled by year, tied with cord. Letters written in exile, in the lowland towns, in the river towns, in the last decades when Ilse Vane had been nobody's heir and everybody's neighbor—a seamstress, a schoolteacher, a woman who walked to market and argued about prices and raised a child alone. Sable read the first one standing and had to sit down halfway through.

*They will tell you blood is a debt,* her mother had written, in the careful script of a young woman deciding what to believe. *It is not. Blood is a fact. Debts are things we choose. I choose to owe the people who fed us, who lied for us, who buried our names in their mouths and kept them safe. I do not owe a throne. Thrones are how the powerful make the children pay for the grandparents' wars.*

Another letter, years later: *A man came today asking questions. I moved us again. I am sorry, little one, that your childhood is made of leaving. One day I will explain that staying was the braver thing, and I could not afford brave.*

And another, later still, the handwriting looser, older: *I saw the mountains today from the market square. The escarpment. Your mountains, though you don't know it yet. I stood there like a fool in front of the fishmonger's stall and cried. Not for what was taken. For what I chose. Remember the difference, if you ever learn it.*

Sable read until her tears froze on her cheeks, which did not take long, and which she did not notice until Emrys wordlessly handed her a cloth from his pocket and looked away to give her the mountain's version of privacy: his back, and the wide empty sky.

"She knew," he said finally, to the horizon. "All those years. She knew where it was. My father must have told her before—" He stopped. Started again. "He got her over the escarpment. Got her to the lowlands. Then he went back for the chest, the one I pulled out of the Greywater, the dispatches, the writ. And they caught him. And Corvane's grandfather—whatever he was, whatever he did—they made a trade. His silence for our lives. And then twenty years later he tried again. Kestrel Pass. He tried to reach this valley and the mountain—" Emrys's jaw worked. "The mountain did what mountains do."

"And they blamed you for it," Sable said. "So no Nystrom would ever come looking."

"So no Nystrom would ever come looking." He laughed, a short terrible sound with no humor in it. "Forty years of engineering. Two generations. All of it to keep a dead nation's paperwork out of the light. And the whole time the person it actually belonged to was drawing maps of other people's borders, carrying a locket, asking strangers about a name."

Sable folded the letters along their old creases, carefully, the way you handle something already broken once. "She chose this," she said. "You understand that, don't you? Nobody took it from her. She had this box within reach for forty years. She could have sent for it. She could have walked into some consulate and ended the republic with an afternoon's paperwork." She looked up at him, and the wind pulled her hair across her face and she didn't bother moving it. "She bought fish. She taught school. She let a whole country stay dead because bringing it back to life would have meant feeding it to men like Corvane."

"The writ names you."

"The writ is paper." She touched the oilcloth. "This is my mother. And my mother says no."

For a long moment Emrys said nothing. Then he reached into his own pack and drew out the copy of the writ they'd carried from the river—the one Marchetti wanted burned, the one Corvane's mercenaries had bled them for, the thing that had cost Doss Ferrin blood at the saddle and very nearly cost Sable everything on the rope bridge. He held it in both hands, weighing it, and she watched him arrive at the same place she'd arrived at somewhere in the middle of the third letter.

"Not yet," he said. "Not up here. If we burn it, we burn it down there, in front of witnesses, where burning it means something. Where it can't be replaced by a story." He glanced at her. "If that's what you want."

"It's what she wants." Sable stood. Her legs protested; she ignored them. "That's enough for me. It turns out it always was."

* * *

They cached the box where it had been—or rather, Emrys rebuilt the cairn around it stone by stone, refusing her help with a stubbornness she recognized as inherited, and only took the letters and the original writ, wrapped now against his own chest inside his jacket, next to the compass case. When she asked him why leave anything, he said, "Because someday someone else will need proof that a choice was possible. Better it stays in the mountain than in a museum."

They started down at noon.

The hanging valley opened beneath them as they descended past the shoulder of granite—a bowl of grey stone and old snow ringed by walls, nameless on every map Sable had ever drawn, and she found herself, absurdly, cataloguing it as she climbed down: aspect, elevation, the line of the drainage, the sheltered bench where a camp could be made. Forty years of training. Some part of her would always be a cartographer, even if every border she'd ever inked was a lie drawn over her mother's erased country. Maybe especially then.

Halfway down the chute, Emrys stopped at a chiseled mark she hadn't seen on the way up—a small arrow, pointing down this time, and beside it two letters cut deep into the rock: *E.N.*

His father's initials. Left on the descent, forty years ago, by a man walking away from a hidden valley and toward a betrayal that would eat his son's life.

Emrys laid his gloved hand flat against the letters for a moment. Said nothing. Climbed on.

But Sable had seen his face, and she understood that something had finished in him up there—not healed, nothing healed that fast, but *finished*, the way a sentence finishes, the way a debt closes. Whatever he'd been climbing toward besides the cache, he'd found it.

They reached the base of the chute as the light went copper on the western walls. And it was there, unclipping from the rope in the last warmth of the day, that Sable saw it: far below, at the mouth of the valley where the trail bent toward the lowlands, a point of light that did not belong. Steady. Deliberate. A signal lamp, blinking in short patient bursts.

Counting them out of habit—old field training—she felt the cold leave her body entirely.

Three flashes. Pause. Three flashes. Pause.

Someone down there knew exactly where they were. And they were waiting.
