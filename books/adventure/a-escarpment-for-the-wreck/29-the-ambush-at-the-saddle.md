## 29. The Ambush at the Saddle

The saddle announced itself the way all bad ground does: too easy.

Emrys had been reading terrain since he was nine years old, when his father first put a map in his hands and said, *the mountain lies in the valleys and tells the truth on the ridges*. The col ahead was a smooth notch between two granite shoulders, a gentle ramp of old snow leading from the hanging valley down toward the southern passes—the route Doss's false intelligence had promised was clear. It looked like mercy. That was the problem. Nothing above four thousand meters looked like mercy without wanting something for it.

He crouched at the lip of the moraine and studied it through the scratched lens of his father's binoculars. Wind combed spindrift off the eastern shoulder in long white banners. The snow on the saddle itself lay flat, unbroken, blue-shadowed in the late afternoon light. No tracks. No movement.

Too flat. Nobody had crossed it recently because nobody could—except that the wind should have carved sastrugi into it, ridges like corduroy, and there were none. The surface had been swept. Deliberately.

"They're here," he said.

Behind him, Sable sat propped against a boulder with her arm bound tight against her ribs, her face the color of old candle wax. The graze along her side had stopped bleeding two days ago, but the wound had a way of reopening its argument every time she moved fast, and they had been moving fast since dawn. Marchetti knelt beside her, checking the dressing with hands that had no business being as steady as they were for a woman who'd spent her life among paper. Doss stood apart, downwind, watching the saddle with an expression Emrys couldn't parse—something between guilt and arithmetic.

"You're sure?" Doss asked.

"The snow's been swept. They wanted us to see a clean crossing." He lowered the binoculars. "Your false route worked exactly well enough to bring them here instead of the north couloir. They've been waiting."

Doss said nothing for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was flat as slate. "Vasik doesn't wait. Vasik is a hammer. If they're set up on that saddle, someone made him wait, which means Corvane's lieutenant is up here himself, which means—" He stopped.

"Which means they're not leaving until we're dead or delivered," Marchetti finished. She rose, brushing snow from her knees, and looked at Emrys with those pale archivist's eyes that catalogued everything and surrendered nothing. "Options?"

"We go around." Sable's voice came out thin but level. "South ridge. It adds six hours."

"In this light, it adds a night in the open at five thousand meters with you bleeding," Emrys said. "And they'll have a man on the ridge by dark. We'd be giving them the same ambush with worse footing."

"Then what?"

Emrys looked at the saddle again. The swept snow. The banners of spindrift. Somewhere behind those granite shoulders, men with rifles were doing exactly what he would be doing: waiting for tired people to take the easy line.

"There's a third option," he said slowly. "We make them come to us."

* * *

They built the trap out of everything they had left, which wasn't much.

Their supplies had scattered down a couloir during the fight at the narrows; what remained fit in three packs and a coil of rope. But Emrys had spent twenty years learning how mountains lie, and a man who knows how terrain deceives can teach it to deceive on purpose.

They lit a fire in the open, low in the bowl below the saddle, feeding it green scrub so it smoked—a signal fire, sloppy, desperate-looking. Beside it they arranged their remaining food tins where firelight would catch the labels. Then Emrys took the rope and rigged a slanted traverse across the bowl's upper edge, staking it with pitons hammered into hidden cracks, and covered the anchor lines with fresh snow. To anyone glassing the bowl from above, it would look like a camp preparing to make a night crossing: fire, food, a fixed line strung toward the saddle. A target that thought it was being clever.

"It's thin," Doss said, studying the rigging. "Vasik's done this work longer than I have. He'll check the line before he commits."

"He'll check the line," Emrys agreed. "That's why the line is real. It'll hold a man's weight—for about four meters. Then the middle piton sits in a flake that's been working loose since the last thaw. One hard pull and it lets go." He met Doss's eyes. "I'm not trying to kill them. I'm trying to drop one of them into the bowl in front of his friends, loud, at dusk, when every shadow looks like a rifleman. Panic does the rest."

"And while they're shooting at shadows?"

"We cross high. The eastern shoulder. There's a ledge system—I used it coming up, before the storm erased our back-trail. It puts us on the saddle's flank, above their position if they've set up in the rocks on the western lip, which is where I'd set up."

Marchetti frowned. "You're gambling that they think like you."

"I'm gambling that Vasik thinks like Doss," Emrys said. "Because Doss trained half the men Corvane hires, and Doss just changed sides." He let that sit. "No offense."

"Some taken," Doss said dryly. "None wrong."

Sable struggled to her feet, refusing the hand Marchetti offered, and Emrys saw her jaw set against the pain with a stubbornness he was beginning to recognize as the family resemblance she didn't know she carried. "I'm coming," she said, before anyone could say otherwise. "Don't argue. I can walk a ledge with one arm. And if it goes wrong, you'll want every pair of eyes."

It went against every instinct he had. He'd already left enough people on mountains. But he looked at her—this woman who had carried a dead nation's name in a locket for thirty years and still walked toward the truth of it—and knew that ordering her to stay would cost more than it bought.

"One condition. You're roped to me. If you fall, I fall, and I'm heavier, so don't."

Something almost like a smile touched her mouth. "Deal."

* * *

They climbed the eastern shoulder in the last hour of light, moving the way his father had taught him to move on broken ground—three points of contact, weight never committed until the hold had answered. The storm had passed in the night, leaving the air scoured and brutally clear, and the cold came down off the icefields like a blade drawn slowly from its sheath. Emrys counted breaths. Counted steps. Kept Sable's rope taut without ever letting it tug.

Below and behind, the fire smoked in the bowl, a small orange heart beating in the blue dusk. He'd left Marchetti's coat draped over a rock near it, a silhouette-maker. She hadn't complained. Somewhere between the monastery and the narrows, the archivist had stopped being a chaser and become something else—harder to name, harder to trust, harder to leave behind.

At the shoulder's crest they bellied up onto a shelf of wind-scoured granite and looked down.

The saddle lay forty meters below and a hundred across. And they were there.

Six figures, maybe seven, positioned along the western lip exactly where Emrys would have put them—two riflemen prone behind rock, the rest tucked into a shallow depression with packs stacked, a glass glinting as someone swept the bowl below. Even at this distance Emrys could read the impatience in their posture, the way one man kept shifting, stamping his boots against the cold. Men who had waited too long on a cold saddle for a fire that wouldn't die.

Then he saw the seventh figure, standing apart, and understood why they'd waited.

Corvane's lieutenant was a story Doss had told in fragments: a man named Havel, who had learned his trade in the border wars and refined it in payrolls and disappearances. He was tall, silver-templed, dressed better than any of his men, and he stood at the lip of the saddle looking down into the bowl with the patience of a man reading a document he already suspected was forged.

"He's not buying it," Doss breathed beside him. Emrys hadn't heard him move up; the man moved like snowfall. "Look at him. He's counting. Fire, one silhouette, food for three—he knows the numbers are wrong."

As if he'd heard, Havel raised one hand, and the two prone riflemen shifted their arcs—not toward the bowl, but outward, sweeping the flanks. The eastern shoulder. Their shoulder.

"They'll glass this shelf inside a minute," Emrys whispered. "New plan. There is no plan. We go now, straight across the saddle while they're looking everywhere else, and we shoot at nothing and run."

"That's not a plan, that's a prayer."

"I've survived on worse theology."

He got Sable moving first, low and fast along the shelf's downward slope, then Doss, then himself, and dropped onto the saddle's snow as the last light drained out of the sky. The swept surface crunched under their boots, treacherously loud in the still air. Forty meters. Thirty. Behind them, on the shelf, the wind erased their traverse almost as fast as they made it.

Twenty meters. The riflemen on the western lip were still scanning the flanks. Ten—

A voice cracked across the saddle, amplified by the cold: "There! On the col!"

Everything happened at once and nothing happened quickly enough.

The riflemen swung. Muzzle flash stuttered white against the dusk, and the snow around Emrys's boots erupted in little geysers. He grabbed Sable's pack and hauled her flat behind the only cover the saddle offered—a rib of rock no higher than his knee, barely wide enough for two. Bullets shrieked off stone above their heads, spraying chips that stung his cheek.

"Doss!" he shouted over the din. "The line!"

But Doss wasn't running for the rope trick. Doss was running *at* them.

Later, Emrys would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. What he saw, in snapshots: Doss breaking cover, angling across the saddle in a low sprint, shouting something—*"Vasik! Stand down, it's Ferrin!"*—and the hesitation it bought, half a second where the riflemen's fire wavered because a familiar voice was a harder thing to shoot than a stranger's shape. What he saw: Havel turning, unhurried, drawing a pistol with the calm of a man closing a ledger. What he saw: one of the prone riflemen—young, nervous, muzzle tracking between Doss and the rock where Emrys and Sable lay—making his choice.

Emrys made his own choice in the same instant, though he wouldn't remember deciding it. He came up over the rock with Marchetti's borrowed pistol and fired twice at the young rifleman, deliberately wide, driving his head down—and in that half-second of distraction the second rifleman found his mark.

Not on Emrys.

On Doss.

Doss was ten meters from the enemy line when the round took him high in the chest, spinning him sideways, and Emrys watched—helpless, frozen behind a knee-high rock—as the man who had fed them a false route and a supply map and, somewhere on a glacier twenty years ago, a boy's life, folded into the snow.

The world went narrow and red-edged. Emrys was moving before thought caught up, dragging Sable by the rope, sprinting the last open stretch of the saddle while bullets chewed the drifts around him. The eastern descent gully opened at their feet—a steep chute of wind-packed snow dropping toward the glacier below—and he didn't hesitate, didn't calculate, just threw them both into it, riding the slope on his heels with Sable clutched against him, the gunfire fading above as the saddle's lip cut off the line of sight.

They slid two hundred meters before friction and a stand of rock took the speed out of them. Emrys came to rest against a boulder, ears ringing, lungs full of ice, Sable coughing beneath him.

"Doss," she gasped. "Where's Doss?"

Above them, the saddle was silent. The firing had stopped. In the deepening dark, Emrys could just make out small figures moving on the col—one of them lying very still, and others bending over it.

Then, faintly, carried down the wind: a single shot. The kind of shot that isn't aimed at anything.

Sable's hand found his arm. "Emrys—"

"I know." His voice came out wrecked. "I know."

He stared up at the black line of the saddle, at the place where a stranger's blood had soaked into a stranger's snow, paying a debt Emrys had been carrying since he was nine years old and a mercenary pulled a boy out of a crevasse on Kestrel Pass and never told anyone. The debt he owed strangers. Paid forward, at last, in someone else's blood.

He got up. There was nothing else the mountain would let him do.

"Marchetti's still up there," he said, and the words tasted like ash and iron. "Alone, with seven of them, and she saw everything." He checked the pistol—two rounds left—and looked south, where the glacier ran down toward the valley and the garrison and whatever waited for them at the bottom of the world. "We keep moving. We get to the valley. We find out if Doss's sacrifice bought anything at all."

Sable rose beside him, one-armed, unsteady, unyielding. Below them, far down the glacier, a light appeared—a lantern, then another, moving up the ice toward them.

They weren't rescuers. Rescuers traveled in daylight.

Emrys took Sable's good arm and turned them both downhill, into the dark, as the lights began to climb.
