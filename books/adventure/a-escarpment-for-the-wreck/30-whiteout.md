## 30. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive so much as it arrived *for* them.

Emrys felt it first in his teeth—a pressure behind his jaw like a toothache that wasn't one—and then in the wind, which had been moaning over the saddle all afternoon and now dropped an octave, the way it always did before the sky came apart. He had been reading weather for twenty years, and this was the oldest lesson of them all: when the wind goes quiet and low, it is not resting. It is loading.

"Get down," he said. "Get down now."

Sable was already moving—she'd learned that much about him by now, that when his voice went flat and short there was no discussion to be had. She crouched behind the boulder they'd chosen as their anchor point, her left arm cradled against her ribs where the graze had stiffened into something worse than a graze. Doss Ferrin knelt beside her, one hand pressed to the dressing on his shoulder where the bullet meant for Emrys had spent itself instead. He hadn't complained once. Emrys was beginning to understand that about him: the man treated pain like weather, something to be noted and worked around.

Marchetti was last to shelter, because Marchetti was always last to shelter, standing with her back to the coming storm as if she could interrogate it too.

"How long?" she shouted over the rising hiss.

Emrys looked west, where the escarpment's sheer face fell away into a whiteness that was already eating the horizon. "An hour until it's full dark up here. Then it's days."

He didn't say what he was thinking: *days, or forever.*

* * *

They dug in. There was nothing else to do. The crevasse camp was forty meters back along the ridge—a blue slot in the glacier's shoulder, wide enough at the lip to shelter four people if two of them were willing to be cold. Emrys had spotted it on the way up and marked it without knowing why, the way his father had taught him to mark things: *you don't choose your shelters, you notice them.* He'd carved the turn-off into a boulder with his axe, three strokes, and now those three strokes might be the only competent thing he'd done all day.

Getting Doss down into the slot took twenty minutes and cost them all something. The mercenary's face went grey-white during the lower, and he said, through his teeth, "Don't tell my men I needed carrying," and Emrys said, "Your men aren't here," and Doss said, "That's not the same thing," and let himself be lowered anyway.

By the time they had the tarp stretched across the crevasse mouth and weighted with snow blocks, the world above had ceased to exist. Not dimmed—*ceased*. The distinction mattered. Emrys had climbed in whiteouts before; you learned to function inside a ping-pong ball, navigating by rope and compass and the stubborn animal conviction that down was still down. This was different. This was the sky and the mountain agreeing to become the same substance, a white so total that when he held his gloved hand a foot from his face he couldn't swear he saw fingers.

The wind hit the tarp like something trying to get in.

Nobody spoke for a while. That was its own kind of conversation—the four of them breathing together in the blue light of the ice, listening to the storm try to unmake everything above them.

It was Sable who finally broke it. "Say it," she said. "Whatever you're calculating. Say it out loud. I can't stand watching you do arithmetic in your head."

Emrys almost smiled. She knew him too well already, and that frightened him more than the storm did.

"All right." He pulled out the map—Doss's supply map, the one the defector had traded them at knifepoint, and the irony of that still sat in Emrys's chest like a swallowed stone—and spread it flat between them, weighting the corners with ice screws. "Here's the truth. The safe route goes down the western flank. Switchbacks, fixed anchors from the old trade path, three days in good conditions. In these conditions—" he tapped the map, where the contour lines ran together like spilled ink— "it doesn't exist anymore. The switchback gullies will be loaded to avalanche by morning. Anyone on that flank tomorrow is buried by noon."

"So we wait it out," Marchetti said. "Four days. Five. We have food for—"

"Two days. Maybe three if we're honest about rationing, which we haven't been." He looked at each of them. "And the storm isn't five days. I've watched this front build since the saddle. It's a river of cold air coming off the high plateau, and rivers don't stop until they've found the sea. We'll be here a week. Ten days. And when it clears, the flank will be a graveyard of new slides, and we'll be too weak to move fast enough to avoid them."

Doss laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it. "So there's another way. There's always another way, and it's always worse."

Emrys was quiet for a moment. Then he turned the map over, to the blank side, and drew.

He drew the escarpment's face as he'd seen it from below a hundred times, in the years before the blacklist, when he'd stood in the valley town and looked up at the wall of ice and rock that divided Vhaloria-that-was from the republic that ate it. He drew the seracs, the rock bands, the couloirs. And then he drew a line—a thin, implausible line running straight down the middle of the face, through terrain no sane guide would look at twice.

"The chute," Sable said quietly. She'd heard of it. Everyone in the valleys had heard of it. It was the kind of thing guides told clients about around stoves, the way sailors told about the ship that sailed off the edge of the map. *The chute nobody descends. The chute that killed Nystrom.*

"My father tried it," Emrys said. "Forty years ago. Coming down, in a storm not unlike this one." He kept his voice level, and it cost him. "He never reached the bottom. They found him in spring, at the base of the second ice band. He'd placed eleven screws. Eleven. He was climbing down better than anyone alive, and the mountain killed him anyway."

"And you want to take us down it," Marchetti said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing—*cataloguing*, the way she catalogued everything, filing his words against some internal ledger of evidence.

"I want to be honest with you about our options. Option one, we sit here and starve and pray the flank stabilizes, and maybe it does. Option two—" he tapped the line— "we go down the chute, tonight, in the storm."

Silence. The wind screamed above the tarp like something being flayed.

"Why tonight?" Sable asked. Her voice was steady but her good hand had gone white-knuckled on her knee. "Why would the storm make it *safer*?"

"Because the chute is a wind funnel, and right now the wind is doing half our work. Look—" he traced the drawn walls— "the chute narrows to six meters at the top band and widens below. Storm spindrift pours down it constantly, which sounds like a reason to die, except it means the ice in the chute never gets a chance to form hollows. It's packed solid. Fresh every hour. And the snowfall fills the crevasses at the base—which are the real killers in clear weather, because you can see them and still misjudge them. Right now they're bridges. Bad ones, but bridges."

"You're saying the storm is covering our tracks," Doss said.

"I'm saying the storm is building our road." He met the man's eyes. "For about eighteen hours. After that the wind shifts north, the spindrift stops feeding the chute, and what's left freezes into something we can't trust. One window. Tonight or never."

* * *

They argued for two hours. Emrys let them argue, because he'd learned that fear needs to talk itself hoarse before it can listen.

Marchetti argued longest, and hardest, and most precisely—she laid out the fatality statistics of steep-ice descents in storm conditions with the fluency of a woman who had read every accident report filed in two countries' archives, and Emrys realized somewhere in the middle of it that she had probably read his father's death report, that she might know the number of screws better than he did, and that she was reciting it at him deliberately, testing whether he'd flinch.

He didn't flinch. "Eleven screws," he said, when she finished. "In 1983, with wooden-shafted axes and hawser-laid rope and no ice clips. My father descended nine hundred meters of the worst ground on the escarpment with equipment older than I am now, and he died at the second band, which means he survived the first eight hundred meters. With modern gear, with the route marked, with four people who have no better choice—" he paused— "I give us better odds than sitting here."

"That's not a number," Marchetti said.

"No," Emrys agreed. "It's a judgment. Numbers are what you hide behind when you can't make the judgment."

Something shifted in her face then—not agreement, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman who had spent her career inside institutions that hid behind numbers, and who had come up this mountain, alone, off the record, precisely because she was tired of it.

Doss voted with a shrug. "I've followed worse orders from better men," he said. "At least this one explains itself."

Which left Sable.

She hadn't spoken in twenty minutes. She sat with her wounded arm braced, looking at the line Emrys had drawn, and he understood suddenly that she wasn't looking at the route at all. She was looking at the name attached to it. The chute that killed Nystrom. The father whose route knowledge she had hired, whose son she had deceived, whose blood she carried in ways none of them had fully mapped yet.

"If we go down," she said finally, "and someone dies, it's because of your father's pride. Or yours. Or both."

"Yes," Emrys said. He didn't soften it. Softening it would have been a lie, and she deserved better than lies from him now—they'd had enough of those between them to last several lifetimes.

"But if we stay," she went on, "we die of caution instead. And my mother chose the mountains over the safe valley once, and it saved her life." She looked up. "Take us down your father's route, Emrys. Finish what he started."

* * *

Preparation took the rest of the light, such as it was—which was to say, preparation took the rest of the grey.

Emrys worked with a focus that bordered on prayer, though he'd have denied the word. Ropes flaked and coiled in order of use. Screws sorted by length into the pockets of his harness, each one tested with a thumb-flick, the dull ones set aside without sentiment. Anchors rehearsed aloud: *running belay through the upper narrows, double-rope rappels at both bands, screw placements every body-length below the second band where the ice steepens.* He made each of them repeat it back. Marchetti repeated it like a deposition. Doss repeated it like a soldier. Sable repeated it like a woman memorizing scripture, and when she stumbled on the third sequence, Emrys made her start over, and she did, without complaint, and something passed between them in that moment that neither of them named: the understanding that he would be hard with her because hardness was the only love he knew how to offer on this mountain.

Food: half rations, eaten mechanically, because digestion was fuel management and nothing else. Water: melted over the stove in the crevasse's throat, where the flame wouldn't show. Emrys drank until his stomach sloshed, then made everyone else do the same. Dehydration was the quiet killer at altitude; it thinned the blood and thickened the mistakes.

Then, in the last hour before they moved, he did the thing he had been avoiding.

He took out the compass case.

His father's compass case—brass corners, cracked leather, the scarred wax seal pressed into the lid that had matched the seal on the river chest, the seal that had started all of this. He hadn't opened it since the monastery. He wasn't opening it now, either. He just held it, turning it over in his mittened hands, feeling the weight of it, the weight of everything it contained and everything it didn't.

"He turned back," Emrys said quietly. He wasn't sure he was talking to any of them. "On Kestrel Pass, years before the end. He had a client in trouble and the weather closing and he turned back, and everyone lived, and they called it cowardice in some reports and wisdom in others. He told me once—he was drunk, the only time I ever saw him drunk—that the pass didn't care which one you were. Courage and cowardice were the same thing to the mountain. Only *craft* was different. Craft was the thing the mountain respected."

He looked up. Three faces in the blue ice-light, waiting.

"So we go down with craft," he said. "Not courage. Courage is for people who have a choice."

* * *

They moved at midnight, into the full throat of the storm.

The first hour was the worst hour of Emrys's life, and he had a competitive field to draw from. Above the chute's mouth, on the open shoulder, the wind found them like a pack of animals finding blood. It didn't blow at them so much as *through* them, tearing heat away faster than their bodies could make it, filling the world with a hissing white static in which the rope between them was the only true statement left. Emrys led. Behind him, at intervals, came Sable clipped to his rope-end, then Marchetti, then Doss, moving slow and deliberate, each of them reduced to the next foot placement and nothing beyond it.

He navigated by count and by feel. Two hundred paces bearing two-sixty. The drop of the shoulder under his boots changing texture—from windslab to the hollow boom of a snow bridge, which he tested twice with his axe before committing his weight, and gestured the others wide around it. Once, briefly, the storm tore a hole in itself and he saw the chute's mouth below them: a black vertical seam in the face, exhaling a plume of spindrift like breath in the cold.

It looked exactly like the drawing in his head. That terrified him more than anything else, that the mountain could match a picture drawn by the son of the man it had killed.

At the mouth, he built the anchor. Two screws, V-threaded, backed up by a buried axe pick, equalized. He weighted it with everything he had—hung his whole body plus the pack plus thirty pounds of gear—and it held with the deep, silent certainty of good ice. He clipped the ropes through and looked back up the slope at the three shapes crawling toward him out of the white.

"One at a time!" he roared into the wind. "Sable first!"

She came down to him hand over hand, and when she reached the anchor her face was a mask of rime, eyebrows frosted white, eyes the only living thing visible—and her eyes were calm. Terrified underneath, he could see that, terror banked like a coal under ash. But calm on top. She'd mastered it. Somewhere between the burned village and the narrows and the saddle where Doss had taken a bullet for a stranger, she had learned what he had learned at seventeen: that fear doesn't leave, it just learns its place.

"Ready?" he shouted.

She nodded. Below her boots the chute fell away into darkness and roaring white, four hundred meters of ice that had a reputation in two countries, that had a dead man's name on it in every guide's story, that everyone called unclimbable-down.

Everyone, Emrys thought, except the man who had gotten eight hundred meters down it with wooden axes. Everyone except his son, standing here now, with his father's compass case against his chest and his father's route under his boots and his father's stubbornness in his blood.

He fed the rope. Sable leaned back into the void, and the storm swallowed her, and Emrys paid out line into the dark, counting meters, feeling her weight live and alive against his brake hand—

—and then the rope stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped.* Hard, sudden, the dead-weight jolt every guide knows in his spine before his brain catches up.

He hauled. Nothing. He shouted her name into the wind and got back only the storm, and the rope hung slack-heavy in a way that meant she was swinging, or hanging, or—

Below him, faint through the roar, he heard ice fracture. A long, cracking exhalation, like the mountain clearing its throat.

The chute was talking. And Emrys Nystrom, alone at the lip of his father's grave, began to feed himself over the edge.
