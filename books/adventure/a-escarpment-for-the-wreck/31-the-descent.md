## 31. The Descent

The chute was a wound in the escarpment's face, a vertical seam of ice perhaps two hundred meters tall, fed by a hanging glacier that groaned above it like an old man turning in his sleep. In clear weather it would have been suicide. In the whiteout that had swallowed us since dawn, it was arithmetic: the storm would kill us on the saddle within a day, and the chute would only probably kill us now.

I had done the arithmetic three times. It kept coming out the same.

"Talk me through it," Sable said. She was sitting against the ice wall of our crevasse shelter, her wounded thigh wrapped in the last of the clean bandage, her face gray under the frost. "All of it. I want to know exactly how I'm going to die."

"You're not going to die."

"Then describe it anyway. I want to check your work."

So I did. Two hundred meters of ice at seventy degrees, broken in the middle by a rock band we'd have to traverse on the left. Six ice screws, four ropes, one anchor at the lip and one we'd have to place blind at the rock band if the ice there was sound—which I wouldn't know until I was hanging on it. Below the band the chute steepened again, then funneled into a runout of avalanche debris that would catch us or break us depending on how the snow had set. Wind out of the north at forty, gusting higher, visibility maybe ten meters. Temperature falling.

"V-threads for the anchors," I said. "We abseil the whole thing. Nobody climbs. Nobody fronts-points except where I tell them. Sable goes second, tied to me. Marchetti last."

"Why last?" Marchetti asked. She stood at the lip of the shelter with her back to the wind, and I noticed again how still she kept herself, how she'd learned to spend motion like a miser spends coin.

"Because if the anchor at the band fails, I want the failure to happen above you, not below. You're the lightest and the coldest. And because you're the only one of us the mountain hasn't been trying to kill for a week."

She almost smiled. "Flattery."

"Triage."

* * *

We rigged at first light, though light was a word that had stopped meaning anything. The world had contracted to a circle ten paces wide, gray-white, featureless, the wind screaming through it with a sound like tearing cloth. I placed the anchor at the chute's lip myself—two V-threads, tested with my full weight, then with Sable's, then with a boulder we rolled onto the sling. The rope went down into the white and vanished. When I dropped a snow block over the edge, I counted six seconds before I heard it hit. That was a long six seconds.

"Emrys." Sable's voice came flat through the wind. She had her hood up and her eyes down, the way she got before the hard things—the same look she'd worn in the burned village, kneeling over the school register. "My mother came over the escarpment. Forty years ago. Probably in weather like this."

"I know."

"She was pregnant with me."

"I know that too."

She nodded once, and I clipped her into the rope, and we stepped off the lip of the world together.

The first pitch was honest ice, blue and old, and my screws bit clean. I went down facing the wall, calling the placements back to Sable, counting her progress by the tension on the rope. The wind tore at us in gusts that lifted our feet off the wall and swung us out over nothing; I learned to climb down in the gaps between them, fast and ugly, driving my tools in where I could, hanging on the rope where I couldn't. Twice the ice under my tool shattered like window glass and I dropped a body-length before the next placement caught. Above me I heard Sable's breath, steady, deliberate—she was breathing the way I'd taught her on the goat track, four in, four out, and I thought: she's using it. She's actually using it.

The rock band announced itself as a shadow in the gray, then as a wall of black stone splitting the chute like a scar. I traversed left along its base, placing two screws in the ice seam where it met the rock, and found what I'd feared: the ice at the band was rotten, layered, sugar over stone. No anchor there would hold a fall.

So I built one in the rock instead. There was a crack—a vertical seam, thumb-width, choked with ice—and I cleared it with my knife and my fingers, losing feeling in both, and set a piton. Not one of my father's. Mine, from the cache, iron and honest. I hammered it home with the flat of the tool, hung from it, bounced. It held. I hung two more ropes from it and called Sable across.

She came to me across the traverse with her face pressed to the rock, moving the way I'd shown her, and when she reached the piton anchor she put her forehead against the stone and stayed there for three breaths. Then she looked up at me.

"How far down?"

"Another hundred meters. Then the runout."

"And then?"

"And then we walk out of this story."

She laughed—a short, cracked sound, half sob, and it was the first time in the whole descent I'd heard anything from her that wasn't discipline. I clipped her through and sent her down, and I stayed at the anchor until Marchetti arrived, because the piton was now the only thing in the world holding three lives, and I wanted to watch each of them pass through its keeping.

* * *

It happened on the last pitch.

The runout slope below the rock band steepened to eighty degrees for thirty meters, and the ice there was thin over rubble, and I was twenty meters above the debris field when I heard it—a sound I knew from the Greywater, from Kestrel Pass, from every bad place I'd ever been: a deep, structural *crack*, felt in the teeth before the ears.

The hanging glacier above the chute was letting go.

"ICE!" I screamed, and there was no time for anything else. The whole face above us turned white—a river of it, tons of it, coming down the chute like the mountain emptying a lung.

I was on the rope. I could go down fast and pray the debris field caught me. Sable was below me, in the line. Marchetti was at the anchor.

Sable didn't go down. She went sideways—drove both tools into the wall and traversed hard right, out of the chute's throat, into a shallow alcove behind a rib of rock, and I understood a half-second too late that she'd read the terrain faster than I had, that the rib would split the flow, and I went down the rope as fast as friction would let me, toward the rib, toward her, as the avalanche hit.

It hit like the end of the world. The air went solid. I remember the rope jerking, the world going white, the sound—not loud so much as *total*, a sound that left no room for any other sound, for thought, for self. I remember being buried to the chest and digging. I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like a stranger's voice in my head: *the anchor, check the anchor*, because if the piton had shifted under that load we were all dead and it wouldn't matter what I remembered.

The piton held. The rib had held. And Sable's alcove—

"Sable!" Nothing. Wind. My own heart.

I dug toward where she'd been, hands like blocks of wood, and hit fabric. Her rope. I followed it down through a meter of debris and found her boot, then her leg, then all of her, packed into the alcove like a fossil, one arm up over her face, eyes open.

"I'm here," she said. It came out slurred, dreamlike. "I'm here. I can't—my leg's pinned."

"Marchetti!" I bellowed upward, and the wind took my voice, but the rope at the anchor twitched, twitched again, and then Marchetti was descending through the settling cloud, fast and controlled, and I thought: *she didn't wait to be called. She saw it and she came.*

The debris had Sable's right leg wedged between two blocks the size of tables. We dug for an hour—Marchetti and I, alternating, because hands stopped working in that cold after ten minutes, and we traded places wordlessly like men at a pump. The blocks wouldn't move. Sable drifted in and out, and at the worst of it, when her eyes rolled back and her breathing went shallow, Marchetti took her face in both gloved hands and said, "Look at me. Sable. Vane. You look at me. Your mother walked off this mountain and you will too, do you hear me? You will *not* make her a liar."

Sable's eyes focused. "You knew," she whispered. "You knew who I was. Before the monastery. That's why you came."

"I came for a chest," Marchetti said. "I stayed for the truth. Now dig."

We got the second block moved with the piton hammer and a length of sling for purchase, and when Sable's leg came free she made a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life—low, bitten off, furious—and then she stood on it, testing, and it held, and she said, "Get me off this mountain," in a voice that meant she intended to walk down and argue with anyone who disagreed.

* * *

The last of the descent we made in the storm's deepest hour, when even the wind seemed to lose its way. I went first, down the runout, across the debris field, onto the glacier tongue below the chute, and the others came down the rope behind me hand over hand, and when Sable's boots finally touched flat snow she sat down in it and did not get up for a long time. Marchetti sat beside her. Neither of them spoke. I stood watch over them with my back to the wind and counted them—two, alive, breathing—and found that my hands were shaking so badly I had to put them in my armpits like a boy.

We had started the day with four ropes, six screws, one piton, and three people. We finished it with one rope, two screws, no piton—it had sheared off in the avalanche, and I grieved that little iron tooth more than I can explain—and three people. The arithmetic of the mountain is simple and it is never kind. Everything else we'd carried was somewhere in the debris above us, or in the chute's throat, or in the hanging glacier's next breath. The writ was gone—burned, weeks ago, by my own hand. The letters from the hanging valley were sewn into Sable's coat, and Sable's coat was on Sable, and Sable was alive. That was the entire ledger.

We walked out onto the valley floor at dusk, or what the gray light insisted was dusk. The storm was thinning behind us, the escarpment rising out of the murk like the wall of the world, and down here the snow fell soft and straight, almost gentle, the way it falls in the low country where it has nothing to prove. Pine trees. The smell of them. I hadn't known I'd missed the smell of living things until it made my eyes sting.

Sable stopped at the treeline and turned and looked up at the chute—a thin white seam, impossibly far above, already healing over with fresh snow. "My mother's route was the safe pass," she said. "Yours was this."

"His," I said. "Mine now, I suppose. That's how inheritance works. You don't get to pick what you're left."

She put her hand on my shoulder—brief, hard, gone—and we went down into the trees.

* * *

We made the garrison road by full dark, following the sound of a river we could not see, and it was Marchetti who heard the other thing first: engines. More than one. Boots on frozen mud. A voice giving orders in the clipped cadence of the republic's military, and under it, cutting through, a voice I did not know but understood at once, the way you understand a blade at your throat—smooth, entitled, used to being obeyed.

Lights ahead. A checkpoint strung across the road, lanterns and wire, soldiers in the republic's gray, and standing among them a man in a fine coat with no frost on it at all, as though the weather had been instructed not to touch him.

Marchetti's hand closed on my arm. "That's Corvane," she said quietly. "And Emrys—he's not supposed to be here. He's supposed to be waiting in the valley town. Which means he knew our route. Which means someone told him."

Behind us, the escarpment stood black against the last of the storm, holding everything we'd left on it. Ahead, the lanterns swung, and Corvane turned toward us, and smiled.
