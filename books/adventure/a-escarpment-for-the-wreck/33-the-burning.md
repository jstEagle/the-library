## 33. The Burning

They gave him the writ at dusk.

Not in the garrison's command office, where Corvane had spent the afternoon arranging himself behind a desk too large for the room, but out on the gravel parade ground between the barracks and the valley road, where Marchetti insisted the thing be done in the open. "Custody transfers require witnesses," she said, and her voice had that flat archival precision he'd learned to distrust and then, somewhere above the treeline, to rely on. "Four witnesses. No more."

The four of them stood in a rough half-circle: Marchetti with her document case held like a shield, Sable with her arm still bound from the crevasse fall, Doss Ferrin leaning on a crutch he refused to admit he needed, and Emrys himself, holding a leather folio heavier than any rope he'd ever carried.

Behind them, the escarpment filled half the sky. It had been there all day, gray and patient, the way it had been there for every hour of the last six weeks, and Emrys found he could not stop looking at it. From the valley floor it looked almost gentle—a long pale wall catching the last of the light, its couloirs softened by distance into something like brushwork. He knew better. His hands knew better. There was not a finger on either of them without a crack in the skin, and his left shoulder still sang where the ice screw had torn free on the third pitch of the chute. The mountain had let them down. That was all. It would not do it twice.

"You understand what you're holding," Marchetti said quietly.

"I understand what I'm *not* holding." He turned the folio over. The wax seal on the outer wrapper had cracked during the descent; a thread of red wax clung to the canvas like a scab. Inside were the original succession documents—Ilse Vane's own hand, notarized twice, witnessed by three monks who had died within a decade of signing—and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as leaf, the letters. Ilse Vane lived out her days under another name in a town that no longer appeared on maps. She never claimed anything. She asked, in her final letter, only one thing: *Do not resurrect me to be used.*

"Corvane will contest this," Marchetti said. "Legally, procedurally, endlessly. He'll say the transfer was coerced. He'll say you're a blacklisted guide with no standing—which, incidentally, you no longer are, as of an hour ago, though the paperwork hasn't reached anyone who matters." She paused. "He'll say a great many things. None of them will matter if there's nothing left to claim."

"And if I burn it," Emrys said, "you'll record it as destruction of state evidence. You could charge me. You'd be *right* to charge me."

Marchetti looked at him for a long moment. In the lowland light, without wind or altitude to account for, her face seemed older—the lines around her eyes carved rather than weathered. "I spent thirty years believing that what survives in an archive is what's true," she said. "Then I read a purge ledger in a monastery and found my own ministry's founder signing deportation orders with a flourish. Some things should not survive. That is also an archival judgment, Nystrom. One of the hardest ones." She stepped back. "I'm not charging you. I'm witnessing you."

Across the yard, near the garrison gate, Corvane stood with two soldiers and a lawyer whose briefcase probably contained four injunctions. He had not approached. He didn't need to; his presence did the work. A tall man made entirely of tailoring and patience, Corvane had watched the descent party come off the ice that morning with the expression of a man watching a delivery arrive late. When Marchetti had stepped between him and the writ an hour ago, presenting not the claim but the evidence—his grandfather's signature in the purge ledgers, page after page, in ink that had waited forty years for a reader—Corvane's face had done something remarkable. Nothing at all. But his hands had closed, briefly, like a book snapping shut.

Now he watched from distance, and Emrys watched him back, and understood that this was the last move available to either of them, and that Corvane knew it too.

Sable came to stand beside him. She smelled of woodsmoke and garrison soap, and her bound arm was cradled against her ribs in a way that meant it hurt more than she'd admit. Up close, in honest light, she looked less like a cartographer and less like an heir and mostly like someone who had walked very far on a very bad leg and was still standing, which was its own kind of nobility.

"You don't have to ask me," she said.

"I know."

"But you're going to anyway, because you're you."

He almost smiled. "It's your name on it. Your mother's hand. I found it, but I never owned it. Nobody owns it. That's rather the point."

Sable looked up at the escarpment, following his gaze. For a while neither of them spoke. Somewhere beyond the wall, the first snows were already falling on the high cols, sealing the passes they'd nearly died to cross, erasing their tracks, erasing the cairns, erasing in a season what his father had spent a lifetime carving. The mountain kept nothing. That had once seemed like cruelty to him. Standing here, it seemed like mercy.

"She chose this," Sable said finally. "Forty years ago, with a pen, in a kitchen somewhere, knowing exactly what she was refusing. Everyone else in this story got to want things. Corvane wants a trophy. The republic wants a relic. My whole life I wanted—" She stopped. Started again. "I wanted a name that fit. And she's the one person who had the right to want everything, and she wanted nothing, and she was *at peace* with it. I read the letter, Emrys. There's no grief in it. There's no bitterness. There's just a woman who decided that a life is not a throne." She turned to him. "Burn it. Burn it before I get sentimental and start hearing drums."

Emrys looked at Doss. The mercenary stood apart, weight off his wounded leg, arms folded, watching with the detached attention of a man cataloguing exits even now. Twenty years ago, on a glacier Emrys barely remembered, this man had pulled a boy out of a crevasse and carried him down a mountain and never told him. Two days ago he had taken a bullet meant for Emrys's spine. Between those two facts lay a debt so large that Emrys had given up trying to calculate it and simply said, "Doss," and Doss had said, "Don't," and that had been the whole settlement.

"What?" Doss said now, catching his look.

"Nothing. Witnessing."

"I've witnessed worse executions," Doss said. "Get on with it."

* * *

There was no ceremony. Marchetti had forbidden one, and besides, none of them were ceremonial people. They were a salvage diver, a mapmaker, an archivist, and a hired gun, standing on gravel in the blue hour, about to end a nation for the second time.

Emrys knelt. The gravel bit through his trousers, cold working up through both knees, and he thought absurdly of the ice chute, of kneeling on nevé at four thousand meters with the world howling, and how he had promised himself then, in the wordless grammar of terror, that if he ever stood on flat ground again he would never waste a single act of it. This was the act. This was the flat ground.

He opened the folio.

The documents inside were beautiful, which was the trap of archives everywhere: beauty argues for preservation. Heavy rag paper, iron-gall ink gone brown at the edges, the Vhalorian double-eagle stamped in gold along each margin—not the aggressive eagle of the republic's heraldry but something older, wings folded, watchful, almost tired. The succession writ itself ran seven pages. He had read them twice in the hanging valley, by candlelight, while Sable slept with her back against the cache chest, and he had memorized the sentence that mattered, the one he would carry whether or not the paper survived:

*I, Ilse Vane, being the last of my line and the last who remembers my country as it was, do hereby decline, renounce, and extinguish all claim, title, and inheritance whatsoever, for myself and for my descendants forever, so that no one may ever again kill in our name.*

So that no one may ever again kill in our name.

His father had died on Kestrel Pass carrying these papers toward safety, or toward destruction—Emrys would never know which, and had stopped needing to know somewhere on the descent, somewhere around the fourth rappel, when the storm erased the world and craft was all that remained. His father had bought silence with silence and died carrying other people's secrets, and the least Emrys could do was finish the errand in the direction the dead woman had asked.

He took out his knife, cut a twist of birch bark from his pocket—he'd carried it since the foothills, some salvager's instinct—and built a small nest of it on the gravel. Then he struck his lighter, the cheap brass one he'd bought in a tavern the week before everything began, and touched flame to bark, and waited until the fire stood on its own.

"Any last words?" Doss asked dryly. "People usually want last words."

"The woman wrote her own," Emrys said. "That's enough words."

He fed the writ to the fire one page at a time.

Paper does not burn the way people imagine. It doesn't vanish; it transforms, reluctantly, in stages. The first page curled at the corners and the gold eagles caught light and flared green, and then the ink darkened and the words swelled briefly—*decline, renounce, extinguish*—before the fire took the vowels first and then the bones of the consonants, and the page lifted as ash, whole for one impossible second, a ghost of itself riding the evening air, and then came apart into nothing.

Page two. Page three. The notary's seal went up in a bloom of blue flame that made Sable inhale sharply. Each page he held until the fire had it completely, because he had seen what happened to documents surrendered halfway—incomplete burns, recoverable edges, Marchetti had taught him that much in another life, another arrangement—and because this deserved thoroughness. This deserved craft. Everything he knew about careful work, all of it, went into the burning of seven pages of paper.

Somewhere around page five, he became aware that Corvane had crossed the yard.

He heard the footsteps first—measured, unhurried, the walk of a man who had never once run anywhere—and then the shadow fell across the little fire, long and cool, and Emrys looked up without straightening.

"You're making a mistake," Corvane said. His voice was reasonable, modulated, the voice of boardrooms and ministries. Up close he smelled of cologne and the garrison's coal smoke, an expensive man temporarily misplaced. "You think you're ending something. You're not. Claims like this don't die; they hibernate. In ten years, in twenty, someone finds a copy, a fragment, a rumor, and everything you suffered on that mountain happens again—to someone else, in someone else's blood. What I offered was containment. Permanence. I am the only man in this valley offering an *end*, Nystrom, and you're choosing a bonfire."

Emrys held page six to the flames and watched the double-eagle curl and blacken.

"You're right," he said. "It might happen again. Someone might find a copy. Someone might make a myth out of it. Can't help that." He looked up. "But here's the difference between us, Corvane. You needed these papers destroyed because of what they prove about your family. She needed them destroyed because of what they *ask*. Those aren't the same fire. Yours is fear. Hers is a wish. I'm honoring hers. You can file a complaint."

"My grandfather's name in that ledger proves nothing a court would—"

"It proved enough for Marchetti," Emrys said, nodding across the yard, where the archivist stood with her case open and her pen already moving. "Ask her how courts work. She reads ashes for a living."

For just a moment—one flicker, quickly mastered—something moved behind Corvane's composure, and Emrys recognized it, because he'd worn it himself for years in taverns when people mentioned Kestrel Pass: the look of a man whose story has been taken out of his hands. Whatever Corvane had planned to be—the restorer, the claimant, the man who ended the Vhalorian question—Marchetti's ledger had rewritten him in a single afternoon, and no amount of tailoring would press that flat again.

He left without another word. His footsteps receded across the gravel, measured to the last.

Emrys fed the final page to the fire.

* * *

Afterward there was only ash—a gray fan of it on the gravel, already breaking apart in the evening breeze, threads of it lifting toward the escarpment as if the mountain were reading it. Emrys stayed kneeling longer than he meant to. The fire had burned down to orange eyes among the birch bark, and he watched them gutter, and felt—searching for the word for it, testing possibilities the way he'd test holds on rotten rock—felt *lighter*. Not happy. Not cleansed; that was a word for people who hadn't carried anything. Just lighter, the way a pack feels on the last morning of a traverse, when most of the food is gone and the body has learned the weight it must carry and stopped arguing with it.

Sable crouched beside him. She picked up a thread of ash between finger and thumb, delicate as handling a moth, and let the wind take it.

"My mother's handwriting," she said. "I used to practice it when I was small. Found samples in her field notes and copied the letters over and over. I thought if I could write like her, I'd understand her." She brushed her hands clean. "Turns out I just had to burn her mail."

"Sable—"

"Don't." But she was smiling, tiredly, really smiling, the first one he'd seen since the narrows. "It's fine. It's better than fine. Do you know what I've been doing my entire career? Drawing borders. Lines on paper that decide where people's loyalty is supposed to live. And the whole time, the truest map I ever saw was a woman drawing a circle around herself and her kid and writing *enough* inside it." She stood, wincing at her ribs, and looked down at him. "Thank you for doing it properly. All seven pages. I was counting."

"I noticed."

"You notice everything. It's exhausting." She extended her good hand and pulled him up. Her grip was strong; the mountain had made sure of that, whatever else it had taken. "Come on. Marchetti's buying dinner from the garrison mess, which means it'll be terrible, which means we'll complain about it, which sounds like the best evening I've had in six weeks."

They walked toward the mess hall, Doss limping ahead with the crutch finally conceded and deployed, Marchetti closing her case with the finality of a woman filing the twentieth century. Behind them the ash finished its journey on the wind, up and up, thinning to nothing against the darkening wall of the escarpment.

At the door Sable paused. "One thing," she said. "Spring. Early, the cook says. There's a commission—mapping the high country, the old names, all of it. Marginalia, nothing official." She glanced sideways at him, casual in the way that isn't. "I'll need a guide."

Emrys looked past her, out at the range going blue and then black, snow already sealing the passes his father had crossed and he had crossed and neither of them would cross again the same way.

"A guide nobody hires," he said. "You'd be taking a risk."

"I hear he comes recommended," Sable said. "By a monk, a mercenary, and a state archivist. Terrible references, honestly. Practically criminal."

He laughed—really laughed, the sound surprising him, coming up from somewhere below the ribs the wound hadn't touched—and held the door open for her, and the warmth of the mess hall breathed out into the cold like a small country that had never needed claiming at all.
