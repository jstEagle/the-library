## 34. Debts

The valley smelled of woodsmoke and thaw, and Emrys Nystrom walked through it like a man carrying a ledger he'd finally been allowed to open.

Three days had passed since the burning. Three days of garrison food and garrison cots, of Marchetti's paperwork grinding forward with the slow inevitability of a glacier calving, of Sable sitting by the infirmary window with her arm in a sling, sketching the escarpment from memory as though she could not bear to let it out of her sight. Three days of Emrys sleeping in fits, dreaming of ice chutes and his father's hands on a rope, waking each time with the strange lightness still on him—the feeling that something he'd carried so long it had grown into his spine was finally set down.

But not everything. Not yet.

He counted out the money at the garrison paymaster's desk, coin by coin, the salvage wages Corvane's seized accounts had somehow been persuaded to release—Marchetti's doing, though she'd never admit it—and wrapped the roll in oilcloth the way his father had taught him to wrap anything that mattered against wet.

"You're leaving already?" the paymaster asked. "The colonel wants statements. There'll be a hearing about the mercenaries, the shooting at the saddle—"

"I'll give my statement when I'm back," Emrys said. "There's a stop I have to make first."

* * *

Greywater looked smaller than he remembered, which was the way of towns you left in shame. The flood had receded weeks ago and the river ran low and brown between its banks, docile now, the way rivers always were after they'd finished killing. The salvage line was gone. The tavern where he'd tried to sell the chest's brass fittings stood with its shutters open and its sign freshly painted, and through the window Emrys could see Hesk—the keeper who'd once guided with him, who'd recognized him with that flat look of pity Emrys had never managed to forget—drawing beer for two farmers.

He stood outside for a long moment. Long enough that Hesk saw him through the glass and went still, mug suspended mid-pour.

Then Hesk set the mug down, came around the bar, and pushed the door open himself.

"Well," Hesk said. "You look terrible."

"I've been up the escarpment."

"Ah." Hesk's eyes flicked to the mountains to the north, where the high country wore its first white like a warning. "That would do it." He stepped aside. "Come in before the whole street starts looking. You know how this town talks."

Emrys knew exactly how this town talked. It had talked for six years.

Inside, the farmers took one look at his face and found urgent business elsewhere. Hesk poured two whiskies without asking and set one down in front of Emrys at the corner table, the one farthest from the window, and sat across from him with the particular patience of a man who has waited a long time for something and intends to savor it.

"The chest," Hesk said. Not a question. The whole valley had heard the rumors by now—the archivist, the soldiers, the helicopter that had come down into the garrison field like a dragonfly with a warrant. "It was true, then. All of it."

"Some of it."

"And you were in the middle."

"I was the middle." Emrys turned the whisky glass but didn't drink. "Hesk. I owe you an apology and I owe you money, and I'm going to do both right now because if I sit here much longer I'll lose my nerve."

Hesk raised an eyebrow. "Apology first. Money's boring."

"The night I came through here trying to sell those fittings—I treated you like a mark. You offered me a bed and a meal and I lied to your face about who I was and what I was carrying, and then I slipped out before dawn without paying because I thought you might turn me over to whoever came asking." He made himself hold the other man's eyes. "You didn't deserve that. You were decent to me when nobody in this valley had been decent to me in six years, and I repaid it like a thief."

Hesk was quiet for a moment. Then: "You did slip out before dawn. I noticed."

"I noticed you noticing. That's why I ran."

That got a short laugh out of him, rough as gravel. "Six years ago," Hesk said, "when the Kestrel Pass report came through, half this town wanted to string you up. The Aldous family, the Merricks—they lost people up there. And you—" He stopped himself, which was its own kind of kindness.

"And me. I know what they think I did."

"What *did* you do?"

Emrys looked at the whisky. At the scarred knuckles of his own hand resting on the table. "I led them into weather I should have read better. That part's true, whatever else is. Four people died because I made the call to push on, and no document, no blacklist conspiracy, nothing anyone does to my name now un-dies them." He said it plainly, because he'd had forty years of escarpment to learn that the mountain doesn't negotiate and neither should the truth. "But I didn't take their money and run. I didn't falsify the route log. And I didn't abandon Tam Aldous on the col like the second report claimed. I stayed with him for nine hours. I was there when—" His voice caught, just once, and he let it. "I was there."

Hesk nodded slowly. "I believe you. For what that's worth—it's worth something in this town, my word. Always was."

"It's worth more than you know." Emrys slid the oilcloth roll across the table. "What I owed you for the room and the meal, with interest for six years of insult. And there's more." He named the sum. Hesk's eyebrows climbed. "It's for the families. The Aldouses. The Merricks. I'm not asking you to hand it over with my compliments—I'm asking you to give it to them however it helps most, and tell them it comes from Emrys Nystrom, and tell them whatever else you want to tell them. If they throw it back in my face, fine. But it goes to them either way."

"This is salvage money? From the chest business?"

"Blood money, near enough. Seems fitting."

Hesk picked up the roll, weighed it in his palm, and set it down again without opening it. "You know what I remember about you, Emrys? Before all of it. You carried the heaviest pack on every trip and complained the least. And when young Pieter Voss twisted his ankle on the Greywater trail, you carried him four miles down on your shoulders and wouldn't take a tip." He tapped the oilcloth. "Men don't change that much. They just get buried."

"Sometimes they stay buried."

"Sometimes somebody digs." Hesk finally opened the roll, counted nothing, just looked at it, and put it away beneath the bar. "I'll see it done. And Emrys—the apology's accepted. The rest, you'll have to square with the dead, same as the rest of us."

"That's the plan."

They drank the whiskies. Outside, the light was going long and gold, and Emrys felt the town's eyes on him through the windows—not with hatred now, or not only, but with the hungrier appetite of a place that smells a story. He paid for the drinks over Hesk's objection and walked out into it anyway, chin up, the way his father would have wanted.

* * *

The Kestrel Pass memorial stood on the shoulder below the tree line, a cairn of stacked stones with four names cut into a slate slab, weathered soft at the edges. Emrys had avoided it for six years. He'd taken the long road around it twice, once in rain, once in snow, telling himself the detour was about terrain.

It wasn't about terrain.

He climbed to it in the late afternoon with his pack empty except for the compass case, and he stood in front of the slate for a long time without speaking, because everything he'd rehearsed on the walk up sounded like either excuse or theater.

Four names. Tam Aldous, twenty-six. Greta Merrick, thirty-one. Jonas Merrick, her brother, twenty-nine. And Wren Oduya, nineteen, the apprentice who'd trusted him most and died fastest.

"I'm sorry." The words came out small in all that wind and space. He said them again, louder, to the mountain and the stone and whatever listened. "I'm sorry. I've spent six years saying it wasn't all my fault, and some of that was even true—but fault was never the point, was it. The point is you're dead and I'm alive, and I got to keep walking while you stopped, and there's no arithmetic that makes that fair."

He crouched and touched the slate. Cold, even in the sun. "I found out why I was blacklisted. It wasn't you. It wasn't even really the pass. Some man named Corvane needed me ruined before I could ever find what my father hid up there, and he used your deaths to do it. I want you to know that, because I think you'd want me to know it too—you'd want me angry instead of ashamed. And I am angry. But I'm not letting go of my share. My share is mine."

Wind moved through the pines below, the sound the escarpment made when it had nothing to add.

"So here's what I can do. I can guide again. Properly, carefully, the way you deserved the first time. Every client I bring home safe from now on is partly yours. Every turnback I make in bad weather—every one I make *early*—that's for Tam, who always wanted to push on. That's the debt. Not money. Not a name cleared. This." He straightened. "I'll carry you up every season for the rest of my life, and I'll bring everyone back down. That's the only payment you ever wanted, I think. You'd have done it for me."

He took off his hat. Stood with his head bare in the cold until his ears burned. Then he set a small stone on the cairn—one he'd carried up from the Greywater bank, river-smoothed, from the water that had given him the chest in the first place—and went down the trail without looking back, because looking back was how people died on mountains, and he intended to live a long time now.

* * *

He found Doss Ferrin at the crossroads inn two valleys over, sitting alone in the taproom with a plate of sausages and the posture of a man who had decided to be nowhere in particular.

The mercenary looked up when Emrys came in, and something complicated crossed his face—recognition, wariness, and underneath both, the thing he'd carried since the saddle. Since the bullet.

"Nystrom." He gestured at the opposite chair. "Sit. You're making the landlord nervous."

Emrys sat. A serving girl brought bread and cheese unasked; Doss waved at it. "On me. Consider it a retainer I'll never collect."

"You're not going back to Corvane's people."

It wasn't a question. Doss shrugged. "Corvane's people are currently being interviewed by a woman from the republic archives who has a memory like a ledger and no sense of humor. Whatever's left of the band after Marchetti gets through will be lucky to find work escorting wool carts." He tore a sausage in half. "Besides. I shot at you for three weeks and then took a bullet standing in front of you. A man can't go back to the old employer after that. Bad for the reputation. Worse for the soul, if I still have one. We're checking."

"Doss." Emrys laid it out plainly, because the man deserved plain. "You saved my life at the saddle. You fed us the false route that kept us ahead of the ambush. You gave us the supply map that got us down the chute alive. By any accounting I know, I owe you."

"You don't."

"I do. Name it. Money—I have some. Work—I know people now who aren't disgusted by me. A reference, a horse, a letter to anyone you like. Name it and it's yours."

Doss chewed for a while. Looked out the window at the darkening road. When he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness of a man handling something fragile.

"Twenty years ago, there was a rockfall on the Serpentine Trail. A party caught under it, and a boy pinned by the leg, maybe eleven years old, screaming for his father who was somewhere above him in the smoke. I happened to be coming down with a survey crew. I dug him out. Carried him down myself." He turned his grey eyes on Emrys. "The boy had a compass case hanging off his belt. Scarred wax seal on it. I remembered the seal because it was odd—nobody's family crest looks like a double eagle anymore."

Emrys's hand had gone, without his permission, to the compass case at his hip.

"After the Kestrel Pass business, when Corvane's lieutenant showed me your photograph, I knew the face. Older, harder, but I knew it. And I told myself it didn't matter—that a job's a job, and the boy I pulled out of the rockfall wasn't my concern, and the man in the photograph was just a name on a paper." Doss shook his head slowly. "But it mattered. Every day it mattered more. Why do you think I missed my shots so often, Nystrom? I'm not a bad marksman."

The taproom noise seemed very far away. Emrys thought of his father, of the purge, of the chain of debts running backward through forty years like a rope through a belay, each man holding the next.

"You dug me out," Emrys said quietly. "I don't remember it. I remember the weight. Someone lifting the weight off my leg. That's all."

"That's enough." Doss drained his cup and stood, pulling his coat from the back of the chair. "So here's the answer to your offer. You don't owe me, because we're already square—we've been square for twenty years, and the last month was just the interest getting paid in the wrong direction. You want to settle accounts?" He smiled, and for once it reached his eyes. "Stay alive. Stay honest. Bring people home off that mountain. That's payment enough for any man who's ever worked a rope."

He put coins on the table for the meal, over Emrys's protest, and shouldered his pack.

"Where will you go?" Emrys asked.

Doss paused at the door. "South, I think. Somewhere the passes don't close. A man gets tired of white." He looked back once. "For what it's worth, Nystrom—the world thinks you're a disgraced guide who got lucky. Let it. Luck makes better armor than reputation."

Then he was gone into the dusk, walking south with an unpaid debt and an easy stride, and Emrys understood that some men refuse payment because accepting it would mean the account was closed, and closed accounts mean the relationship is over—and Doss Ferrin, who had spent his life being hired and dismissed, preferred to leave one door permanently open.

Emrys raised his untouched cup to the empty doorway.

"To open accounts," he said softly, and drank.

* * *

He walked back toward the garrison valley in the blue hour, stars hardening over the escarpment's black silhouette, and at the last rise he stopped, because there was a light moving on the road below—a lantern, unhurried, coming toward him.

Sable. Her sling was gone; her arm moved freely. She'd walked out of the garrison against medical advice, obviously, and she held the lantern up as she drew level, and in its glow her face was tired and amused and entirely unsurprised to find him on this road at this hour.

"Hesk sent a rider ahead," she said. "Word travels faster than you do. He says you settled with the town." She tilted her head. "And the memorial cairn has a new stone on it. Greywater stone. Somebody walked a long way to put it there."

"Somebody did."

She fell into step beside him, and for a while they walked in silence, boots crunching frost, the lantern swinging its small warm circle against all that dark.

"My mother's letter said not to resurrect her to be used," Sable said at last. "I've been thinking about what that means for the living. What we're allowed to carry and what we have to put down." She glanced at him sidelong. "You look lighter. Did you put something down today?"

"A few things." Emrys looked north, where the escarpment stood against the stars, vast and indifferent and, for the first time in his life, feeling less like a debt collector and more like a home. "Not all of them. There's one left."

"Oh?"

He touched the compass case at his hip—the case he hadn't opened, not once, not even in the hanging valley, not even with the original documents in his hands. The case whose wax-sealed scar had started everything.

"There's a letter in here," he said. "My father's. I've known it was there since the monastery. I haven't been able to open it." He met her eyes. "Tomorrow. Will you be there tomorrow?"

Sable smiled, small and real, and lifted the lantern higher so the light fell on the path ahead of them both.

"I'll bring the coffee," she said. "You bring the courage."
