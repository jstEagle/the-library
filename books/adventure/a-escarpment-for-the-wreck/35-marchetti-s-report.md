## 35. Marchetti's Report

The garrison at Greywater Ford smelled of wet wool, gun oil, and the particular staleness of institutions that had been wrong for a long time and had no intention of admitting it.

Odile Marchetti sat at a borrowed desk in the records annex—a room that had once been a chapel, judging by the arched window, now fitted with a steel shutter that turned the afternoon light into a gray blade across her paperwork. She had been writing for six hours. The report was nearly done. It was, by any professional measure, excellent: precise, chronological, internally consistent, and almost entirely false.

She read the last paragraph again.

*The chest recovered from the Greywater flood-line on the fourteenth contained water-damaged administrative documents of no historical or political significance, consistent with Vhalorian provincial record-keeping of the final occupation period. The documents, being illegible and water-ruined, were destroyed on-site to prevent contamination of the national registry with unverified material. No items of value were recovered.*

No items of value were recovered.

She set down the pen and looked at her hands. There was still rope burn across the right palm, a week old now, healing into two pink stripes she would carry into every archive and interrogation room for the rest of her career. Her left wrist ached when it rained, which it was doing now, a thin cold drizzle against the steel shutter. She had hauled a bleeding cartographer out of a collapsing ice chute with those hands. She had stood at a saddle at four thousand meters while a man she'd been hunting took a bullet meant for her—no, not for her, for Nystrom, but she had been there, she had been part of the reason the guns were on the mountain at all—and she had watched Doss Ferrin fold up like a dropped pack and slide ten meters down the scree before anyone could reach him.

None of that would appear in the report.

She picked up the pen again.

*The guide Emrys Nystrom, encountered in the high country during the search, cooperated with this office and provided material assistance during the descent. His prior record—*

She stopped. His prior record. Two words that had ended a man's life for eleven years, that had turned him into a ghost who salvaged brass off drowned riverboats because no expedition office in two countries would put his name on a permit. She knew now what the record was. She had known since the monastery, since she'd stood in the abbot's cold scriptorium with a lantern and read the purge ledgers by the light of her own held breath, and seen the name *Corvane* written in a clerk's careful hand against a column of villages that no longer existed. Collaborator. Informant. The man who had sold the valleys, sold the Vane line, sold a pregnant woman's escape route to the men who came for her—and whose grandson now sat in the garrison commandant's office two floors above her, with legal authority, a soldier's escort, and the patient expression of a man who had already won.

*—his prior record having been reviewed and found to rest on unsubstantiated testimony, this office recommends its expungement.*

That was true, at least. She had filed the expungement petition that morning, before the report, so that if anyone checked the sequence—and someone would; that was what people like her were for—they would find the petition first, the report second, and conclude that the archivist had done her diligence in the correct order. Institutions believed in sequence. Sequence was how they slept at night.

She wrote the closing paragraph the way she had been taught to write closing paragraphs thirty years ago, as a junior clerk in the provincial registry, copying the style of a supervisor who had taught her that a report was not a record of what happened but a machine for making what happened stop mattering.

*This office considers the matter of the Vhalorian chest closed. No further action recommended.*

She signed it. Odile Marchetti, Senior Archivist, Second Bureau. Her signature had not changed since she was nineteen—same downward hook on the final *i*, same impatient slash through the crossbar. She had once believed a signature was a small thing, a formality. Then she had spent a career learning that signatures were the only part of the state that ever touched the truth, and that most of them touched it in order to smother it.

She blotted the page. She did not feel relief. She did not feel shame, either, which surprised her more. What she felt was something she had no filing category for: a kind of clean tiredness, like the ache after carrying a load the whole way down.

* * *

Corvane's man came for her at dusk.

Not Corvane himself—she had only met the man once, in the commandant's office, and once had been enough. It was the lieutenant, a neat young officer named Vassek with the particular smoothness of someone promoted past his competence by family connections, and he stood in the annex doorway with his cap in his hands and his eyes anywhere but on her face.

"Archivist. The commandant asks that you attend. The gentleman Corvane wishes to review your findings before they're transmitted."

"Of course he does," Marchetti said.

She gathered the report, the expungement petition, and the thin folder of supporting documents—photographs of the chest, water-stained and open, its interior visibly empty; a salvage manifest from the Greywater line; a certificate of destruction she had persuaded a garrison clerk to countersign that morning. Everything in the folder was a lie, and every lie in it was documented, witnessed, and archivally sound. She had spent her life reading what other people burned. She had learned, somewhere on the ice above the escarpment, that she was equally gifted at constructing what never happened.

The commandant's office was warm in the way that only occupied buildings were warm—heat drawn off for the officers, the rest of the garrison making do. Corvane sat by the window with a glass of something amber and a copy of her preliminary field summary, the one she had filed from the valley floor, and he did not look up until she had crossed the whole length of the carpet, which was a technique, and she let him have it.

"Archivist Marchetti." He set the summary down. "You've had a difficult month."

"I've had a thorough one."

"So I understand." He smiled. It was a good smile, maintained the way a bridge is maintained—regularly, and with an eye to load-bearing. "You went up the mountain. Alone, I'm told, after your escort was dismissed. That was irregular."

"It was efficient. My escort's mules were failing and my schedule was not."

"And you found them. Nystrom. The woman." He turned the glass a quarter-turn on the side table. "The Vane woman."

"The client of a guide," Marchetti said. "A cartographer. She had a fall in the ice chute. I assisted in her rescue."

"A cartographer." He said the word the way a man tests a floorboard he already knows is rotten. "And in the course of this—rescue—you saw no documents? No papers of any kind?"

"The papers in the chest were destroyed. You've read the certificate."

"I've read the certificate." He rose, went to the window, and looked out at the rain coming down over the garrison yard, the sentry walking his post with his collar up. "My grandfather came from these valleys, you know. Before they had a different name. He used to say the mountains kept everything—every body, every secret—and gave it back only when it could do the most damage." He turned. "You strike me as a woman who appreciates that the past is a dangerous material, Archivist. It wants handling. It wants—" he searched, and found, "—*curation*."

"I am a curator," Marchetti said. "That is, in fact, my profession."

"Then you'll understand that certain materials are best left in the ground." He came back toward the desk, and his voice stayed pleasant, which was the most frightening thing about it. "The chest is closed. The matter is closed. A nation that died forty years ago has no claim on the present, and a woman who draws maps has no claim on a throne. If some sentimental clerk in the Second Bureau were to develop—curiosities—about the Vhalorian registry, about succession documents, about names in old ledgers—"

"Then that clerk," Marchetti said, "would be filing fiction. And I don't file fiction, Mr. Corvane. I file what I find. And what I found was an empty chest."

He studied her for a long moment. She had been studied by better interrogators than this—by her own superiors, by a mercenary with a photograph, by a blizzard—and she had learned that the trick was not to be blank. Blank invited digging. The trick was to be exactly as interesting as your paperwork and no more.

"The expungement petition," Corvane said at last. "For the guide. That's an unusual use of Bureau resources."

"It's a correction of the record. The Kestrel Pass findings rested on a single witness statement, and that witness was paid by a firm your family had interests in. I can show you the ledger entries, if you'd like. I brought copies." She let that sit. "I file what I find, Mr. Corvane. All of it."

Something moved behind his eyes—the first honest thing she'd seen in him, and she recognized it, because she'd spent her whole life watching for it in the faces of men who had just realized the archive was not on their side. He picked up her report, read the final page standing, and signed the transmittal order without another word.

At the door, she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your grandfather was right about the mountains. They do give everything back. Eventually. To whoever's willing to go up and get it."

She left him with that, and the rain, and his drink.

* * *

The report went into the pneumatic tube at dawn with the garrison's regular dispatch—three canisters of routine correspondence, one of them containing the end of Vhaloria's second death, this one on good paper with a dry signature. Marchetti watched the clerk feed it in and thought about the first time she had ever burned something.

She was twenty-three. A provincial archive, a flooded basement, a registry of the annexation years going soft in the water, and a supervisor with kind eyes who had told her: *Some things survive better as ash, Odile. Paper can be read by anyone. Ash can only be read by us.* She had done it. She had stood in the yard and fed the registry page by page into a brazier and told herself she was preserving it—translating it into the only language the state respected. She had been good at it ever since. She could read a fire the way other people read handwriting: what was fed to it, in what order, in what fear. She had read the ashes of Vhaloria her whole career. She had just never before been the one holding the match on purpose, for mercy.

She walked out of the garrison into the gray morning and found Nystrom waiting by the gate.

He had his pack on, which told her he was leaving today, and the tavern keeper's debt paid, which the whole town had told her about by the second day, and he stood in the drizzle with his hands in his pockets looking like a man who had set something heavy down and was still learning how to stand without it.

"Archivist."

"Guide." She stopped in front of him. "You're cleared, you know. The petition will process in six weeks. Both registries. After that you can take a permit for anything from the Alpine Bureau to the Karst Survey."

"I heard."

"You'll get offers. Big ones. The kind you spent eleven years not getting." She studied his face. "You'll turn them down."

"Probably."

"Good." She meant it, and he heard that she meant it, and something passed between them that neither of them would ever put in a report. "The woman," Marchetti said. "Vane. She'll be in the valley a while. The leg needs time."

"I know. I'm not leaving yet."

"No," Marchetti said. "I didn't think you were."

She turned to go, then stopped, because there was one thing left, and she had learned on the ice that some things must be said at altitude or not at all.

"Forty years ago," she said, "a woman came down off that escarpment carrying nothing, and let the world believe she'd died, because she looked at what her name would cost and decided it wasn't worth it. I've spent my career filing the paperwork of people who made the opposite choice. Kings. Ministers. Collaborators. Men who fed whole registries into fires to protect a name." She looked up at the escarpment, invisible above the cloud, but present the way a debt is present. "Last week I filed a report saying a chest was empty. It's the first document I've ever falsified that I'd sign again. You understand that?"

"I understand it," Nystrom said.

"Because the difference isn't what's in the fire. It's who it's for." She adjusted the strap of her satchel—the satchel with the lantern, the magnifier, the ash-reading kit, all the tools of her old trade, which she suspected she would never quite look at the same way again. "Tell her—tell the cartographer—that the registry of the vanished has one entry fewer as of this morning. Ilse Vane, deceased, no surviving line. It's done. It's ash. No one will ever read her out of it again."

Nystrom nodded slowly. "She'll want to thank you."

"She'll want to do no such thing, and I'll want no such thanks, and that's why it will hold." Marchetti allowed herself the smallest smile she had produced in thirty years of service. "Good luck, guide. Stay off the deadly routes."

"Can't promise that."

"No," she said. "I suppose your father couldn't either."

She walked back through the gate, past the sentry, into the annex with its chapel window and its steel shutter, and she sat down at the borrowed desk and opened a fresh file. There was still work. There was always work. The Second Bureau did not run on truth; it ran on the tension between truth and paper, and she had just spent a month learning which side of that tension she could live on.

She began a private index—her own hand, her own cipher, kept in her own head and in a notebook that would never enter an official archive. *Corvane, grandfather: collaborator, purge ledgers, Monastery of Saint Aldric, verified. Ferrin, Doss: wounded at the saddle, discharged himself from the garrison infirmary against advice, whereabouts unknown, disposition—friendly. Nystrom, Emrys: record expunged, petition filed, character: verified. Vane, Sable: cartographer. No surviving line.*

She wrote the last entry, closed the notebook, and put it in the inside pocket where she kept the things that were actually hers.

Outside, the rain was stopping. Through the arched window, for the first time in a week, the escarpment showed itself—a white line above the cloud, clean and indifferent, holding everything it had ever been given.

Marchetti looked at it for a long moment, the way she had once looked at ashes.

Then she dipped her pen, and began the next report.
