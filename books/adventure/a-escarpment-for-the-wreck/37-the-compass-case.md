## 37. The Compass Case

The tavern keeper's money was gone by the third day—paid out in small envelopes to names Emrys had carried like stones in his pockets for eleven years. The Kestrel Pass families had not all written back. Two had. One letter said *thank you*. The other said nothing at all, just a photograph of a young man in climbing gear, squinting into sun that no longer existed anywhere except in the picture. Emrys kept the photograph on the table of his rented room and looked at it each morning until looking stopped feeling like punishment.

Doss Ferrin had refused payment with a shrug that managed to be both gracious and insulting. "You fed me," he'd said. "That squares us." Then he'd walked down the valley road toward whatever life waited for a man who had changed sides of a gun in the middle of a mountain, and Emrys had watched him go the way you watch a bridge you're not sure will hold.

Now there was only the compass case.

It sat on the table beside the photograph, where it had sat for weeks—through the burning, through the descent, through Marchetti's report and Sable's departure south with her maps and her unburdened name. A leather case gone stiff and dark with forty years of handling, brass corners worn to shine, the flap held shut by a buckle whose tongue had long since snapped off. His father's initials pressed into the leather: **E.N.** The same initials Emrys carried. His mother had named him for a dead man and never once explained why, and he had grown up inside that silence the way a tree grows around a fence wire.

He had opened the case a hundred times. That was the thing no one understood—not Sable, not Marchetti, not even himself, if he was honest. The case had been open since the day the solicitor handed it over with the rest of his father's effects, when Emrys was nineteen and too angry to look carefully at anything. Inside: a brass compass with a hairline crack across the crystal, a spare needle wrapped in oilcloth, a folding knife with one broken scale, and a flat leather wallet that had always been empty as far as Emrys knew—or empty as far as he had ever let himself know.

Because here was the truth he had avoided for forty years' worth of other people's forty years: he had never actually looked inside the wallet. Not properly. Not with his hands steady and the lamp lit and nowhere else to be. He had glanced, as a boy glances at a closed door in a dead parent's room. He had assumed. And assumption, he had learned on the escarpment, is how people die on mountains—assuming the slope holds because it held last season.

He made tea he didn't drink. He trimmed the lamp. Outside, the first hard frost of the season had silvered the window, and the valley below his room lay quiet under it, woodsmoke standing straight up from the chimneys in windless columns. Winter was coming for the passes. In three days, maybe four, the high country would close like a book.

Emrys sat down at the table and put his hands flat on either side of the compass case, the way he'd seen the abbot at Saint Aldric lay his hands on the registry before opening it—as if what slept inside deserved a moment of respect before being woken.

"All right," he said aloud, to the room, to the frost, to a man thirty years dead on a slope called Kestrel Pass. "I'm listening."

* * *

The leather of the wallet had bonded to itself over the decades. He worked it open with the folding knife—one of his father's tools, used gently—and felt something shift inside, a paper weight sliding against the lining. His heart did something childish and enormous, a single hard knock like ice settling in a serac.

Inside the wallet were two things.

The first was a photograph, creased into quarters so it would fit a breast pocket. A woman stood on a ridge with the wind pulling her dark hair sideways, laughing at whoever held the camera. Behind her, unmistakably, the Karst Escarpment—the hanging valley, the twin horns of the saddle. Ilse Vane. He knew her face now; he had studied it in the monastery archive until it felt less like a stranger's and more like a relative's. She was younger in the photograph than Sable was now. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting Emrys recognized from the cairn markings and chiseled arrows on his father's route—a hand he had been reading all autumn without knowing it—two words:

*She made it.*

Four syllables. Forty years. His father had carried this photograph against his chest through every season of disgrace, every winter of silence, every mile of the route that finally killed him, and the whole of what he'd allowed himself to say about it fit in four syllables.

Emrys set the photograph down with hands that were not quite steady and picked up the second thing.

A letter. Sealed with wax—the same deep green wax, the same double-eagle stamp that matched the scar on his own compass case, the seal that had started everything on the Greywater flood line. But this wax was unbroken. Whatever was inside had been written for him and had waited for him, patient as stone, through two blacklists and a purge and a war of paperwork fought over a nation that no longer existed.

His name was on the front. Not *E. Nystrom*. Not *the Nystrom boy*, the way Corvane's letters had named him, like an item on a manifest. Just: *For my son, whenever he is ready.* And beneath it, smaller: *If you are reading this, then you chose your own path. Good.*

Emrys laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, only relief arriving ahead of schedule. Even sealed in wax, the old man had found a way to take the decision out of Emrys's hands and give it back to him at the same time.

He broke the seal.

* * *

The letter was four pages. His father's handwriting, which Emrys had spent his childhood imitating on school slates and had not seen since he was nineteen, filled them edge to edge, the script tightening where the writer's thoughts tightened, loosening where he must have stopped to look out some window Emrys would never find.

*My son,*

*If you have this, someone has finally told you the truth, or you dug it out yourself, which would please me more. Either way, I am sorry it took so long. Sorry is a cheap word. I'll spend it anyway, because it's the only coin I have left.*

*You will want the story in order, so here it is. In the last year of Vhaloria, I guided a woman named Ilse Vane over the Karst Escarpment. She was pregnant and frightened and braver than any man I ever roped up with, including myself. I got her out. They caught me coming back. What I traded for your mother's life and yours was my silence and my name, and I paid it gladly, and I would pay it again, and I never once regretted it—though I regret every day what the paying cost you, growing up in the shadow of a man the valleys called a coward.*

*I was not a coward. I need you to know that, though I know words on paper are thin rope. I was a man who chose, and the choosing was invisible, and invisible choices are the hardest ones to live under.*

*When they burned the registries, I took the compass case because it was mine and because inside the wallet was the only proof that she lived—that any of it happened. I meant to give it to you when you were old enough. Then Kestrel Pass happened, and I learned that Corvane's people had arranged my ruin years earlier, quietly, the way rot works into timber, because they feared someday I might talk. So I decided to talk with my feet instead. I went back for the chest. The mountain decided otherwise.*

*Here is the part that matters, and then I'll stop, because a dead man should not lecture.*

*I do not know what you will do with any of this. Maybe you'll burn it. Maybe you'll carry it to the capital and turn the country upside down. Maybe you'll do neither and just keep living, which is its own kind of answer. I want you to hear this clearly, because I have thought about it every night for thirty years: whatever you choose is right. Not whichever choice I would make—I don't get to make it, and thank God, because my choices are what buried us. Yours. Whatever you choose, having chosen it freely, with your eyes open—that is the inheritance. Not the name. Not the blood. Not the writ or the seal or the throne that nobody sane should want. The choosing.*

*A guide's whole trade is judgment. You read the snow, the wind, the light, the person on the other end of the rope, and you commit, and you live with what the mountain does with your commitment. I taught you none of this directly, which is my other regret, but you learned it anyway, because you're mine and because the mountains teach it to anyone who listens. So listen now: I forgive you. Whatever path you chose. Whatever you did to survive my silence. Whatever you're about to do. It is already forgiven, the way weather is forgiven—it simply happened, and I love you, and the escarpment stands.*

*Your father,*
*Edvard Nystrom*
*Who made it, in the ways that counted.*

Emrys read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way he'd read route descriptions before a first ascent, memorizing not the words but the shape of the mind behind them.

Then he did something he had not done since he was nineteen years old and had sworn he never would. He put his face in his hands and wept—not the ragged grief of the funeral, which had been mostly rage wearing grief's coat, but something older and quieter, a grief that had finally been given somewhere to rest. It came up out of him like meltwater finding a channel it had carved long ago, and he let it, because he was alone, and because his father had just told him, in writing, across thirty years of silence, that there was nothing in him requiring forgiveness in the first place.

The lamp burned low. The frost thickened on the window. Somewhere below in the village a dog barked twice and gave up, and the valley went back to its enormous patience.

* * *

Near midnight, he took the compass out of the case and turned it under the lamp. The hairline crack across the crystal caught the light like a river on a map. The needle swung, settled, swung again—he tilted the housing slightly, an old habit, testing for stickiness—and pointed north with the stubborn fidelity of a thing built to do one job forever.

His father had carried this through the purge, through the disgrace, through the years of silence, up Kestrel Pass. And somewhere along the way—Emrys saw it now, saw it with the clarity that comes only after the storm has passed—the old man had hidden the letter inside the very object his son was most likely to inherit and least likely to examine. Not the house. Not the papers. The compass. Because Edvard Nystrom had known his son would become what he became. Had known the mountains would take him back no matter what the valleys said. Had known that sooner or later, on some cold night, Emrys would sit down with the tools of the trade and finally, truly look.

*Whenever he is ready.* The old man had trusted him to be ready eventually. That trust, Emrys realized, had been holding him up for forty years like a fixed line he hadn't known was there.

He cleaned the compass with a soft cloth. He oiled the hinge of the case. He slid the photograph of Ilse Vane back into the wallet—but kept the letter out, and after a long moment, folded it into the inner pocket of his jacket, over his heart, where a photograph had once ridden against another man's chest through every bad season of a ruined life.

Some things you carry. Some things carry you.

He blew out the lamp and stood a while in the dark, watching the frost flowers grow on the glass, each one a tiny map of a country that existed for a single night and would never be drawn again.

* * *

Morning came white and still. The first true snow had fallen in the high country overnight—Emrys could see it from his window, a clean line drawn across the escarpment at about nine thousand feet, the peaks above it wearing the year's first white like men putting on their formal clothes. Winter, arriving early, exactly as the old-timers had grumbled it would. The passes would close within the week. The Karst Escarpment would fold its arms and sleep.

He was boiling water for coffee when the knock came.

Two knocks, unhurried, the knock of someone with legitimate business. He opened the door on a young man in a garrison greatcoat, cheeks red with cold, holding a sealed envelope and a clipboard like a man holding a snake he'd been assured was harmless.

"Emrys Nystrom?" The courier peered at him. "Sign here."

The envelope bore the crest of the regional survey office. Emrys broke it open standing in his doorway, breath steaming, and read it twice while the courier stamped his boots and studied the frost on the railing with elaborate disinterest.

An official pardon—full restoration of guiding credentials in both countries, the blacklist struck, the Kestrel Pass findings formally reopened and revised. Marchetti's work; he could see her fingerprints in every clause, precise and merciless. And beneath the pardon, a second document: an offer. A prestigious commission. Lead guide for the government's new high-country survey, a salaried position, a team of six, maps with his name printed in the corner. The kind of offer that would have meant salvation five years ago. The kind of offer that, five years ago, he'd have crawled across broken scree to accept.

Emrys looked up from the page at the escarpment. The morning sun had reached the high snowfields and set them burning, and the hanging valley where his father had once walked a pregnant woman to safety lay bright and unreachable and utterly indifferent, the way it had always been, the way it would be long after everyone currently alive had stopped drawing maps of it.

The courier coughed. "They'll want an answer by week's end."

"They can have it by week's end," Emrys said, and folded the letter closed, and stood a moment longer in the doorway with the cold on his face and his father's words warm against his chest—*whatever you choose is right*—weighing, for the first time in his life, nothing at all.
