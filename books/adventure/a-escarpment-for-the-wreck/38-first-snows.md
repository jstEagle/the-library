## 38. First Snows

The letter came up the valley on a mule.

That was how everything came to Thornmere in winter—mail, flour, lamp oil, news—all of it swaying in panniers along the river road behind a man named Hobb who had driven the route for thirty years and had opinions about all of it. So when Hobb appeared at the door of the old guide house with a thick envelope stamped twice in red wax, Emrys knew before he broke the seal that it was trouble. Nobody sealed a letter twice to say anything small.

He read it standing in the doorway, letting the cold in behind him, because some part of him wanted the cold as a witness. The letterhead belonged to the Alpine Institute in Corvane's capital—a name that carried weight the way ballast carries weight, useful only for sinking things. They offered him a position. Not a commission. A *position*: senior instructor, expedition lead, his name on their letterhead, his photograph in their prospectus. The blacklist was formally dissolved; enclosed was the document proving it, both countries' seals affixed, twenty years of professional death reversed by bureaucratic fiat. There was talk of a lecture series. There was talk of Kestrel Pass. They wished, very delicately, to discuss "the rehabilitation of the Nystrom name."

Emrys set the letter on the table, weighted it under the iron kettle, and went out to split wood.

The axe work helped the way it always did—one motion, one log, one clean report of sound off the valley walls. The mountains around Thornmere had gone white at the tops and grey at the seams, and the passes were shut. He could feel it the way an old sailor feels weather in a healed bone: the Karst Escarpment had buttoned itself up for the season, its high cols buried under cornices, its goat tracks erased, its whole vast face turned inward like a sleeping animal. Nothing moved up there now but wind and the things wind made. For six months, the escarpment would keep its own counsel.

He found he liked that. He found, sitting on the chopping block afterward with his breath steaming and his hands burning from the cold, that he liked a great many small things this winter, and that liking them felt less like settling than like arriving.

The Institute sent a second letter. Then a man—an actual man, fur-collared, boots too clean for the mud of the river road—who sat in Emrys's kitchen drinking tea he didn't finish and talking about legacy and restoration and the remarkable story of the guide who crossed the escarpment in the first snows. The story had gotten out. Of course it had. Doss Ferrin had walked into a garrison clinic with a bullet through his shoulder and said nothing worth repeating, but Marchetti's report existed, and reports leak, and somewhere between the valley floor and the capital the tale had grown a spine: the disgraced guide, the lost heir, the impossible descent down the ice chute in whiteout. People loved it. People would pay for it. The man from the Institute said the word *brand* without embarrassment.

"They want me to stand on a stage," Emrys said, "and tell them about my father."

"They want you to tell them about yourself. Your father is—the emotional core, yes, certainly—"

"My father died on Kestrel Pass because men like the ones signing your letters spent forty years making sure no one looked for what he was looking for." He kept his voice level. It cost something. "And then they blacklisted his son so he'd never go looking either. That's the emotional core. Shall I put that in the prospectus?"

The man left on the afternoon mule. The offer stayed open, the letter said. Offers like that stayed open the way traps stay open.

* * *

What Emrys did instead was teach children to self-arrest.

It had started by accident, the way most true things do. In November, before the snows closed in completely, the miller's boy—Toma, fourteen, all elbows and appetite—had followed the high trail past where he should have turned back and come home white-faced, having stood at the lip of a slope and understood, suddenly and completely, that the mountain did not care whether he lived. His mother came to the guide house the next morning with her jaw set and her fear dressed up as anger, and asked what it would cost to make her son competent instead of lucky.

"Nothing," Emrys said. "Bring him Saturday."

By December there were five of them: Toma and the miller's two younger girls, the ferryman's daughter Brekka who was fearless in exactly the way that got people killed until someone taught her fear properly, and a quiet boy named Anselm whose father had died in the mines and who watched everything twice. They came Saturdays. He taught them ice-axe arrest on the low slopes above the village, rope work in the barn loft, how to read a snowpack with a probe, how to turn around—turning around being the first skill and the last one anyone learned. He paid for their rope himself, out of the money Sable had pressed on him at parting and he'd sworn not to touch, and told himself the interest on that debt was being paid in kind.

"You're wasting yourself," Hobb said, one evening at the tavern, watching the kids pile out of the guide house with their axes over their shoulders like a tiny, ridiculous army. "Institute's offering you the world."

"The world's heavy," Emrys said. "I've carried it."

Hobb grunted. Hobb's grunts contained multitudes. But later, when Emrys was pulling on his coat, the old driver added, not looking up from his glass: "Kestrel families got your letter. Marta Osten read hers out at the hearth-night. Cried, they said. Good crying." A pause. "Took twenty years, is all I'll say. Took twenty years and a war's worth of trouble, but you wrote it."

"I owed it."

"Aye." Hobb finally looked up. "Most men never pay what they owe. Most men don't even open the account book." He raised his glass half an inch, which from Hobb was a parade.

* * *

Winter deepened. The valley shrank to its stove-warm rooms and its lantern-lit lanes, and Emrys learned the shape of ordinary days again—or learned it for the first time, since he couldn't remember ever having had them. He fixed the guide house roof. He traded labor at the mill for flour. He wrote to Doss once, care of a poste restante in the lowlands, and got back a single line in a hand like wire: *Shoulder's fine. Stop writing letters. —D.* It was, he understood, the warmest letter Doss Ferrin had written anyone in years, and he kept it in the drawer with the compass case he still hadn't opened.

That was the thing he circled, night after night, the way a man circles a locked room in his own house. The compass case sat in the drawer beneath his spare shirts, and he had not opened it. Everyone else's secrets had been dragged into the light—the writ burned, the registry read, Ilse Vane's letters shared by lamplight in the hanging valley while the storm built outside—but his father's last words to him remained folded in oiled leather, waiting, and Emrys found he could not yet bear them. Not because he feared what they'd say. Because whatever they said would be final, and he wasn't finished needing his father to be unfinished.

Sable wrote from the lowlands. Her letters arrived irregularly, mule-borne, smelling faintly of ink and woodsmoke, and they were the best thing in the mail sack. She was working, she said, on a map of the escarpment—the real one, the honest one, the old Vhalorian names restored in careful marginalia where they belonged: not on the land, not torn out of the republic's atlases, but held alongside, the way you hold grief alongside living. She described the engraver's shop. She described the archive copies she was making for the monastery, so that Saint Aldric's shelves would never again be the only vessel of a people's memory. She did not describe herself, except once, in a postscript: *I sleep well now. I didn't know that was a thing I was allowed.* Emrys read that line more times than he would admit to anyone, and answered it with three pages about self-arrest technique, which he suspected she understood perfectly.

Marchetti he did not write to, and did not need to. News traveled. She had survived her institution the way a blade survives a forge—changed in composition, same in shape. There was talk of a reform commission. There was talk that she'd refused a promotion. Both rumors sounded exactly like her.

* * *

In late January the weather broke, and for four days the sky over the escarpment was the hard blue of polished steel, and everyone in Thornmere went slightly mad with sunlight. Emrys took the children up the valley as far as the treeline—not climbing, just walking, teaching them the winter mountain from below—and let them run on the frozen shallows of the Greywater while he stood with his hands in his pockets and looked up at the high country.

From here he could see the notch of the saddle where Doss had taken the bullet meant for him. He could see the shoulder of the col where the narrows began. Higher, hazed by distance, the line of his father's route stitched across the face like a scar that had finally stopped aching. He had gone up as far as the cairns in October, alone, and rebuilt the first three with his own hands, and spoken aloud to the wind like a madman, and come down lighter than he'd come up. That was enough. That was, he understood now, what his father had actually left him—not the route, not the secret, but the knowledge that a mountain gives back exactly what you bring to it, and that you get to choose what that is.

"Toma," he called. "Stop throwing rocks at the ice. It's not the rock's fault."

"It's nobody's fault," Toma called back, with the profound philosophy of fourteen, and threw another rock.

Brekka came and stood beside him, following his gaze upward. She was twelve and sharp as a burr. "Did you really climb down that?" She pointed at the ice chute, invisible at this distance but present in every story the valley now told.

"Yes."

"Were you scared?"

"Yes."

She considered this with great seriousness. "Good," she decided. "Anselm says if you're not scared you're not paying attention. Were you scared the whole time?"

"No." Emrys watched the light move on the high snowfields. "There was a stretch near the bottom where I wasn't scared at all. That was the worst part. Scared means you're still deciding. When it stops—" he shrugged— "that's when you're in trouble."

Brekka nodded slowly, filing it away next to everything else she filed away, and ran off to throw rocks at the ice with renewed moral authority.

* * *

The Institute man came back in February, or rather a different Institute man came back, which amounted to the same weather system. This one was subtler. He admired the guide house. He admired the children's program—"community roots, excellent optics"—and he laid it out gently over tea: the lecture circuit was optional, truly, but the Institute needed a name for its new high-mountain school, and Nystrom was the name, and there would be funding, facilities, a staff, a life in the capital with the mountains viewed from a window designed for viewing them.

"You'd be respected," the man said. "Fully. Publicly. Everything that was taken from you, returned with interest."

Emrys turned his teacup slowly on its saucer. Outside, the light was going blue over the snow, and somewhere down the lane a dog was barking at nothing, and Toma was presumably doing something ill-advised that would require mentioning tomorrow.

"When I was blacklisted," Emrys said, "I thought the worst thing was the shame. Twenty years I thought that. Then I got the name back and discovered the shame hadn't been the worst thing. The worst thing was what the name was *for*. Somebody's tool. Corvane's people used my disgrace. The Institute wants to use my redemption. Same hand, different glove." He set the cup down. "I guide people up mountains. That's the whole trade. When I do it here, I know why I'm doing it. Ask me to do it for a brand and I won't know anymore, and a guide who doesn't know why he's on the mountain gets people killed. You'd be making your school less safe by hiring me."

The man blinked. He had clearly prepared for gratitude, for negotiation, even for anger, but not for a refusal with reasons in it. He left before dark, polite and baffled, and the offer stayed open, and Emrys put another log on the fire and felt the strange, specific pleasure of a door closing from the inside.

Hobb heard about it within a day, because Hobb heard about everything within a day. "Turned down the Institute," he said at the tavern, not asking.

"Turned down the Institute."

"Marta Osten says you're a fool. Half the valley agrees with her."

"And the other half?"

"The other half says you're a fool but they'd trust you on a rope." Hobb drank. "Best review you'll ever get."

* * *

The passes would not open for months yet, but already the light was changing. By late February the afternoons stretched ten minutes longer each day, and the snow on the south-facing slopes developed that faint wet gleam that meant the mountain had begun, secretly, to plan for spring. Emrys noticed it the way he noticed everything now—not hungrily, not obsessively, but with the steady attention of a man who had learned what attention was for.

On the last evening of the month he sat at his kitchen table with the drawer open and the compass case in front of him, and his thumb moved over the worn leather, over the scar in the grain that had matched a wax seal half a lifetime ago, and he thought: soon. Not tonight. But soon. Some doors you had to walk to and stand before a while before you could open them, and that wasn't cowardice, it was craft. You didn't rush the last pitch. You waited for the light to be right.

He was reaching to close the drawer when he heard the mule.

Hooves on the frozen lane, slow, and then Hobb's voice outside, pitched strangely—too loud, too cheerful, the voice of a man delivering news he'd been carrying for thirty miles and enjoying every step. Emrys rose and opened the door onto the blue evening, breath steaming, and saw Hobb dismounting with exaggerated ceremony, and saw, climbing down stiffly behind him, travel-worn and wind-reddened and unmistakable, a woman with a cartographer's hands and her mother's eyes, a canvas map tube slung across her back like a rifle.

Sable Vane looked up at him across the snowy yard, and the smile she wore was small and certain and arrived like spring coming early.

"Hobb tells me," she said, "you're the best guide in the valley who doesn't know why he's on the mountain."
