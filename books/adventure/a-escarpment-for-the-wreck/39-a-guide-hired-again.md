## 39. A Guide Hired Again

The thaw came early that year, the way it sometimes does when a winter has been too proud of itself. By the second week of March the Greywater had shrugged off its ice in long grinding sheets, and the willows along the salvage line were already showing that faint green haze that isn't quite color yet—just the promise of one, held at a distance.

Emrys noticed it first from the porch of the cottage he'd rented below the village, the one with the sagging lintel and the view of the escarpment's southern shoulder. He had a mug of coffee going stale in his hands and no particular reason to be anywhere, which was still a strange sensation after two years of being chased by necessity. The mountains stood clear against a washed-blue sky, the high cols still white but the lower ridges already darkening to bare rock and larch.

He'd been offered work in February. Real work—the kind with a letterhead on it. A mountaineering institute in the lowlands wanted him for their summer program: glacier travel instruction, crevasse rescue seminars, his name printed in a prospectus beside men who'd never once been blacklisted. The director had said *rehabilitated* twice during the interview, as if the word were a bandage he was applying and Emrys were expected to sit still for it.

He hadn't answered yet. The letter sat on the table inside, weighted under the compass case he still hadn't opened past its first compartment. Some things you let rest until they tell you they're ready.

The knock came mid-morning. Three raps, even, unhurried—the knock of someone who knew he was home and wasn't pretending otherwise.

He opened the door and Sable Vane stood on his step with snowmelt on her boots and a leather map tube slung across her back like a rifle.

She looked different from the woman who'd intercepted him on the road last autumn. Thinner through the face, maybe, or just settled—a face that had stopped running. Her hair was shorter. There was a new scar above her left eyebrow, thin and pale, a souvenir of the ice chute or the saddle; she'd never said which, and he'd never asked, because some scars are invoices you don't itemize out loud.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You're early," he said. "Spring's early."

"Everything's early this year." She glanced past him into the cottage with the frank appraisal of someone who'd slept in worse places than his floor and wasn't shy about it. "Are you going to make me stand on your step like a bill collector?"

He stepped aside. She came in, set the map tube against the wall with the care other people gave to infants, and took the chair across from the stove without waiting to be offered it. That hadn't changed either.

"Coffee," he said, and poured her a cup without asking if she wanted it. She wrapped both hands around it the way she always did, as if the cup might try to leave.

For a while neither of them spoke. The stove ticked. Outside, meltwater ran loud in the ditch along the road, a sound like the whole valley clearing its throat.

"I heard about the institute," she said finally.

"News travels."

"Doss wrote to me." A small pause, the kind she made when she was deciding how much truth a sentence could carry. "He's working a ferry crossing on the Veld now. Says the water's easier than scree. He also says you turned down a job offer and haven't told anyone why."

"I haven't told anyone anything."

"That's what I told him." She drank. Set the cup down. And then, because Sable Vane had never once in her life approached a subject sideways when she could go through the middle of it: "I have a commission."

He waited.

"The Geographical Bureau. The real one this time—not the republic's cartography office I used to freelance for. They want the high country surveyed properly. The Karst Escarpment, the hanging valleys, the passes between the Greywater headwaters and the old frontier. New triangulation, new elevations, everything." She reached over and laid the map tube across her knees, not opening it yet, just resting her hands on it. "And here's the part that matters. They've agreed to something no bureau has agreed to in forty years. The maps get to carry the old names."

Emrys looked at her.

"As marginalia," she said. "Not restoration. Not resurrection. Just—" she made a small gesture, the same one she'd used in the monastery archive, turning pages with two fingers as though the paper might bruise, "—a note in the margin where a name used to be. *Vhalorian: [name]. Extinct.* So that anyone who reads the map knows the valleys had voices before ours did. Memory carried lightly. You remember."

"I remember."

"They need a guide who knows the routes. Not the safe routes." Her eyes came up and held his, steady and unblinking. "All of them. Including the ones that don't appear on any survey, including the ones a man carved into the rock forty years ago because he was trying to move quietly through a country that wanted him dead. I can hire any guide in three countries now. Half of them would take the job for the money alone. But there's exactly one person alive who's read those cairns the way you have, and I'm sitting in his kitchen drinking his bad coffee."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a crow argued with the morning.

"Sable," Emrys said slowly, "I turned down the institute."

"I know."

"Not because of the blacklist. That's done. Marchetti saw to it—both countries, clean papers, an apology so bureaucratic it barely counts as words." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "I turned it down because I spent a year learning what my name costs when other people spend it. Corvane spent it. The republic spent it. My father's silence got spent before I was born. I'm not interested in being a name in a prospectus. I'm interested in—" he gestured vaguely at the window, at the ridge, at the light coming off the snowfields, "—this. Small routes. Local people. Work I can hold in my hands."

"Good," Sable said.

He blinked. "Good?"

"Because I'm not offering you a prospectus." She unrolled the map tube at last, and spread the draft across his table, weighting the corners with the salt cellar, the coffee tin, the compass case, and her own cup. It was beautiful—he could see that immediately, even unfinished, even with half the high country still blank. Clean linework, careful hachures, the escarpment rendered with the affection of someone who had nearly died on it and loved it anyway. And along the margins, in a smaller, older-style hand, the ghost names. *Kestrel Pass — Vhalorian: Tsern Kael.* *The Hanging Valley — Vhalorian: Aldric's Cradle.*

"I'm offering you this," she said. "A blank third of a map, four months of season, and a partnership. Not employment. Partnership. Your name goes on the plate next to mine—*surveyed by*—and nobody spends it but us. We go up in April, before the melt makes the rivers stupid. We come down in September. In between we walk every route your father ever cut a mark into, and we put them on paper so the next fool who wants to cross doesn't have to die finding out where the ice lies."

Emrys looked at the map for a long time. At the blank spaces. At the marginalia, small and patient, like footnotes written by ghosts who only wanted to be mentioned, not mourned.

"There's a col above the hanging valley," he said at last. "Northeast aspect. It's not on any survey because the approach is a chute that everyone calls unclimbable-down, and they're mostly right. My father marked it. I've been meaning to go back and see whether the mark means *go* or *never*."

"Then we'll find out together," Sable said. "That's the job."

"And if it means never?"

"Then we write *never* on the map." She smiled—small, dry, entirely hers. "Cartography is mostly the honest recording of refusals."

He laughed. It surprised him, the sound of it, rusty as an old piton coming out of rock after a winter. He couldn't remember the last time laughing had cost nothing.

* * *

They spent the afternoon planning, which is to say they spent the afternoon arguing, which is to say they spent the afternoon the way they'd learned to spend all their time—two stubborn people negotiating terrain that neither would yield and both respected. She wanted to start from the north; he explained, with diagrams drawn in spilled coffee, why the north approaches were suicide before June. He wanted to cache supplies at the burned village; she went quiet for a moment, then nodded once and said, "We'll camp there anyway. I owe the place a visit." Neither of them said Ilse Vane's name. Neither of them needed to. The dead were folded into their plans the way the old names were folded into the margins—present, honored, not resurrected.

Toward evening she helped him re-ropes the gear in his shed, checking knots with the brisk competence of someone who'd learned rope work on a rope bridge in a storm and never forgotten the lesson. Her hands moved through the knots without looking. His did too. Once, briefly, their hands met over the same coil, and neither of them pulled away fast, and neither of them made anything of it, and the not-making-anything-of-it felt like the truest thing either of them had said all day.

"You know," she said, flaking the rope into neat loops, "when I hired you last autumn, Doss told me you were dangerous."

"He wasn't wrong."

"He meant it as a warning. I took it as a reference." She tied off the end. "Best hiring decision I ever made, and I've made very few."

"You hired me because I was the only guide who knew my father's route."

"I hired you," she said, "because you lied badly about Kestrel Pass and it was the most honest thing anyone had said to me in years." She hung the rope on its pegs and turned around. "Also the money. Don't forget the money. I was rich and terrified and you were broke and furious. It was a perfect match of deficiencies."

"We've upgraded our deficiencies since."

"Some of them." She looked at him across the dim shed, dust motes drifting in the last of the light through the door. "Some of them we kept. I find I'm attached to a few."

Outside, the light went amber and then grey, and the first stars came out over the escarpment while the high snowfields still held the day's pink. They ate bread and cheese and the last of a jar of pickled things the tavern keeper had sent down in autumn, with a note that said *Debts closed, friendship open*, which Emrys had framed by propping it against the wall behind the stove and never moving it.

"So," Sable said, when the plates were cleared. "April."

"April," he agreed. "We'll want two weeks of food cached at the goat track mouth before the melt takes the ford."

"I'll handle the permits. Both sides of the range—I still know people in the bureau who owe me."

"You always know people who owe you."

"It's my single marketable skill besides drawing." She gathered the map, rolled it with the tenderness she showed to nothing else, and slid it back into its tube. Then she paused, tube in hand, and looked at him with an expression he'd learned to read over a hard year: the expression she wore when she was about to say something plain and didn't want to make a ceremony of it.

"Emrys. Last year I asked you to guide me to an inheritance. This year I'm asking you to guide me away from one. Same mountains. Different reason." She shouldered the tube. "Is that a yes?"

He thought about the institute letter under the compass case. He thought about his father's marks on the northeast col, waiting forty years to be read. He thought about the word *rehabilitated*, and how it had sounded like a leash, and how *partnership* sounded like a rope between two climbers—useless unless both ends are held.

"Yes," he said.

Sable nodded, satisfied, and picked up her pack, and was halfway to the door before she stopped and turned around.

"One more thing." She dug in her coat pocket and tossed him something small. He caught it out of the air: a brass fitting, green with age, stamped with a double-eagle. A piece of the chest. He'd sold the fittings for passage money two years ago in a tavern that smelled of shame—and apparently the tavern keeper had bought one, kept it, and given it back to her to return to him, because debts closed didn't mean everything had to stay sold.

"For the shed wall," she said. "Next to the note. Every expedition needs a figurehead."

She stepped out into the blue evening. He heard her boots on the frost-stiff mud, unhurried, heading for the inn where she'd clearly already booked a room, because Sable Vane planned everything except the things she refused to plan.

Emrys stood in the doorway a while longer, the brass fitting warm in his fist, and looked up at the escarpment. The last light was leaving the high cols. Somewhere up there, under snow, a chiseled arrow pointed the way his father had gone, and in April he would follow it—not to claim anything, not to bury anything, just to read it, and write down what it said.

He went inside, moved the compass case off the institute letter, and put the letter in the stove.

It burned quickly. He watched it all the way down, then set the brass double-eagle on the shelf above, where the firelight caught it, and began, in a fresh notebook, on the first page, under the date:

*Party of two. Route: all of it.*
