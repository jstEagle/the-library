## 1. The Man Who Rode the Dead Lines

The handcar had a name, which was ridiculous, and Mirabel Sterling had given it to her anyway, which was worse. *Widow*, he called her, because she carried him along a line nobody else would mourn.

She was a decommissioned section car, painted the gray of old dishwater, with handles worn smooth by men long since pensioned or buried. He'd bought her off a scrap dealer in Kettle Falls for forty dollars and a lie about working for the county, and in three years she had never once betrayed him. She rolled when he pumped. She stopped when he braked. She asked nothing of him except oil and silence, which were the two things he had in surplus.

The line he rode that morning had been dead four years. The Copper Queen spur, they'd called it, before the mine played out and the company pulled its rolling stock east and left the rails to rust like veins in an abandoned arm. Forty miles of track climbing through the pass, crossing gorges on trestles that the snow and the wind and the indifferent years were slowly unpicking joint by joint.

That was why Mirabel rode it. Not because it went anywhere — it went nowhere beautifully — but because dead lines still needed keeping, and there were men downstream who would pay cash to keep them. Telegraph companies with wire strung along the right-of-way. Water districts with flumes that followed the grade. Ranchers who used the old bed as a road and cursed every rotted stringer between them and town.

He worked under names the way other men changed shirts. In Kettle Falls he had been Mr. Ames. Down at the flume camp, Mr. Corliss. The rancher whose fence he'd mended in September knew him only as the quiet fellow who did good work and asked no questions, and who had paid him in coin because Mirabel had said, mildly, that paper made his hands itch.

It wasn't entirely a lie. Paper made his hands itch the way a rope makes a neck itch.

* * *

He came down out of the pines at midmorning and saw the trouble from half a mile off: the third trestle over Miller's Fork, listing to starboard like a drunk leaning on a friend. He braked Widow on the grade above, set her brake iron under the wheel, and stood a while in the cold looking at it the way a doctor looks at a chest he already knows is bad.

The trestle was sixty feet of timber on four bents, spanning a creek that ran loud and white below. Two of the stringers had cracked clean through, and the whole span had sagged maybe eight inches, enough that if you didn't know bridges you might walk across it without noticing, and if you did know bridges you wouldn't walk across it at all.

Mirabel knew bridges. That was the whole of his misfortune.

He unloaded his tools — the adze, the auger, the froe, the box of spikes he kept wrapped in oilcloth against the damp — and spent the morning doing what his hands remembered better than his head wanted them to. There was a comfort in it, a mercy almost. A cracked stringer did not ask you questions. A bent cap either held or it didn't, and it held or didn't according to laws older and more honest than any man's word.

By noon he had jacked the span level and was fitting a new stringer he'd milled two weeks back from a lodgepole he'd felled himself, when he heard the bell.

Not a church bell. The dinner bell at the Hollis place, the ranch that sat in the elbow of the valley a mile downstream. Old man Hollis rang it at noon the way his father had, and the sound came up the canyon thin and clear, and Mirabel sat down on the new stringer with his legs hanging over the creek and ate bread and cheese and let the bell tell him what time it was, because he owned no watch. Watches could be pawned. Pawnshops kept ledgers.

He ate slowly. Below him the creek ran over stones the color of bruised plums. Above him the pass stood white to its shoulders, winter coming on early this year, and somewhere behind all that snow was the main line, and the towns, and the world where men wrote things down.

In his coat pocket, folded into eighths and worn soft as cloth, was a newspaper clipping. He took it out now, not because he needed to read it — he could have recited it in his sleep, and often did, in the bad hours — but because some part of him checked on it daily, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

**TWENTY-ONE DEAD IN TRESTLE COLLAPSE AT CAÑON VERDE**, the headline said. **Engineer's Inspection Signed Day Before Disaster; Company Vows Full Inquiry.**

There had been no full inquiry. There had been an inquiry, brief and quiet, and then there had been a settlement fund, and then there had been nothing at all except twenty-one names carved into a stone in Cañon Verde that Mirabel had visited exactly once, at night, and never again.

His signature was on the inspection report. *M. Sterling, Assistant Engineer.* He had signed it honestly, God help him — that was the joke of it, the black joke he carried everywhere. He had inspected that trestle with his own eyes and his own instruments and found it sound, because the load reports said it was sound, because the survey crew's figures said the timber was rated for twice what it carried, and he had been young enough then to believe that figures were facts.

They weren't facts. They were inventions. Somebody upstream had falsified the load ratings to save money on timber, and the trestle had held for eleven months under trains it was never built to carry, and then one gray October evening it had decided, with the terrible patience of timber, that it was finished.

Twenty-one men. He had learned all their names afterward, from the stone. He had made himself learn them, the way you make yourself look at a wound.

Afterward, when the inquiry began asking its soft questions, Mirabel had understood something with total clarity: that the men who falsified those reports were still employed, still respected, still signing things. And that if he stayed and told the truth, he would be a ruined man testifying against respectable ones, and the truth would drown in paperwork before it ever reached air. So he had run instead. Which was cowardice, probably. He thought of it as arithmetic. Either way, the result was the same: a man with no name who rode dead lines and fixed trestles for cash and flinched, every time, at the scratch of a pen.

He put the clipping away. The creek went on talking to itself below, indifferent and clean.

* * *

He finished the stringer by late afternoon and spiked it home, and then he did the thing that separated him from the ordinary itinerant carpenter, the thing that made the telegraph company pay him double and ask no receipts. He walked the whole span twice, testing each board with his weight, listening to the wood speak. Timber talked if you listened. It groaned before it cracked. It told you its age and its burdens and how close it stood to its own ending, and Mirabel had spent three years learning the dialects of dying wood because he could not bear to stand on anything that might fall.

When he was satisfied, he packed his tools, pumped Widow onto the span — slowly, testing, always testing — and rolled her across the creek and on down the grade toward the Hollis place, where he'd promised to look at their wagon bridge before the snow closed the road.

Old Hollis met him at the gate with the usual ceremony, which consisted of handing him coffee in a tin cup and saying nothing for a full minute, which both men understood to be a form of respect.

"Span's done," Mirabel said.

"Saw you at it from the field. You work like a man paying off a debt."

Mirabel looked at him. Hollis looked back, mild as milk, and drank his coffee.

"Bridge," Mirabel said. "You wanted me to look at your bridge."

"I did." Hollis led him around the barn to the little crossing over the irrigation ditch, and Mirabel knelt and put his ear to the planking like a horse trader, and pronounced it sound for another season, and took five dollars for saying so, and felt the familiar small shame of taking money for good news.

It was Hollis, at supper — because Hollis's wife would have it, and her suppers were not to be refused — who mentioned the stranger.

"Fella come through Tuesday," Hollis said, passing the potatoes. "Prospector, by the look of him. Half-starved. Asked after work on the upper river." He chewed a while. "Odd one. Kept his coat on indoors. Talked about a source of something up past Gray Fork. Wouldn't say what."

"Prospectors talk," Mirabel said.

"This one talked like a man with wolves behind him." Hollis's wife clucked at that, but Hollis went on, watching Mirabel with those mild dairy-farmer's eyes that missed less than they let on. "Heading for the way station at Dusk Hollow, I'd guess. Nobody else up that draw this time of year."

Mirabel said nothing. But that night, lying in the loft above the Hollis barn with the clipping against his chest like a coal, he found himself thinking about the way station at Dusk Hollow, which lay six miles down the dead line, and about the man with wolves behind him, and about how in three years of riding nowhere he had learned that the world had a way of finding men precisely when they believed themselves unfound.

He slept badly. Toward dawn he dreamed of the trestle at Cañon Verde, and in the dream it held, it held, it held, and then it didn't, and he woke with his hands aching from gripping the loft rail.

He was rolling south before sunup, telling himself he was only going to Dusk Hollow for lamp oil. Widow ran smooth under his hands. The rails sang their one dry note. And far ahead, down in the blue fold of the hills where the way station sat with its stovepipe smoking, something waited that would put an end to three years of perfect, careful, exhausting invisibility — though Mirabel Sterling, whose name was not yet Mirabel Sterling again, knew none of this, and pumped on into the morning, a man paying off a debt he could not name, toward a promise he could not yet imagine.
