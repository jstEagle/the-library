## 2. A Trade at Dusk

The way station at Miller's Bend had been dying for years, and like most dying things it had stopped pretending otherwise. The roof sagged in the middle like a tired back. The water tank leaned against its legs the way a drunk leans on a fence post. Mirabel Sterling—though no ledger within two hundred miles knew him by that name, and he intended to keep it that way—pulled his mule up short when he saw the door standing open.

Doors in empty country didn't stand open. Doors in empty country were shut against the wind, or shut against wolves, or shut because there was nobody left to open them. An open door was an invitation or a warning, and out here those were usually the same thing.

He should have ridden on. That was the sensible move, the move he'd have counseled any man to make: keep moving, keep your name off paper, keep your shadow short. He'd built a whole life on that counsel, such as it was—a life made of trestle repairs under borrowed names and handcar rides down lines the railroad had forgotten, always west or north or wherever the ledgers weren't.

But the horse tied outside wasn't empty-country horseflesh. It was a good animal, well kept, saddled with a prospector's pack rig, and it stood with the patient misery of a creature whose master had been inside too long.

Mirabel sighed at himself. He could feel the argument starting—the one between the man who wanted to survive and the man who occasionally insisted on being human—and he already knew which one would win, because the other one always did.

"Hello the house," he called, and got nothing but wind off the bend of the river.

* * *

Eben Crow lay behind the cold stove where somebody had dragged him, one hand pressed to his belly, the other curled around a canvas packet like a mother around a child. He was sixty-some years old, gray-bearded, built like a fence post that had weathered forty winters and refused to fall over on principle. His eyes found Mirabel in the doorway and sharpened with an effort that cost him visibly.

"You're not them," Crow said. His voice was gravel poured slow.

"No," Mirabel said. "Who's them?"

Crow laughed, and the laugh turned into something wetter, and he stopped laughing. "Three men. Rode through yesterday looking for me. I figured they'd come back tonight." He lifted the hand from his belly, and even in the dim light Mirabel could see the dark stain had soaked clear through to the boards beneath him. "Somebody else got here first."

Mirabel crouched. Old habit made him want to check the wound, do something useful with his hands. But he'd seen enough gut wounds to know what this arithmetic looked like, and so had Eben Crow, apparently, because the old man waved the offer off before Mirabel could make it.

"Don't waste your kindness," Crow said. "I've done my sums. What I need is a banker, not a doctor."

"I'm neither."

"You're a man who came through an open door when every sensible instinct said ride past. That'll do." Crow fumbled at the canvas packet, and Mirabel watched him work at the knot with fingers gone clumsy, and finally, hating himself a little, reached over and untied it.

Inside was oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth was a map.

Mirabel had spent half his life reading drawings by bad light. He knew survey work the way a preacher knows scripture—the line weights, the elevation marks, the little precise hieroglyphs of bearing and distance. This map was drawn in a professional hand, though it had been annotated over years in a rougher one, notes crammed into margins, corrections inked over corrections. It showed a river running up out of country Mirabel didn't know, toward a source marked with a small, careful symbol and a single word in the draftsman's neat capitals: HEADWATERS.

Something about that word went through him like a cold draft through a broken window. He set the map down carefully, the way you set down something that might be loaded.

"I don't want it," he said.

"Everybody wants it," Crow said. "That's the trouble with it." He shifted, and the pain moved across his face like weather. "You know what's up at that source? Everybody figures gold. Silver, maybe. Men have killed each other over less than a rumor of silver." His mouth twisted. "It ain't gold. But it's worth more, and Halloway knows it, and that's why there's three men riding my trail and why I'm leaking out on a way station floor."

"Halloway," Mirabel repeated. The name landed somewhere in him and sat there, heavy, familiar in a way he couldn't yet account for and didn't want to examine.

"Silas Halloway. Survey company. Water rights." Crow said the words like a man spitting out bad teeth. "There's a spring up in that high country feeds everything downstream. Every town, every ranch, every farm from here to the territory line drinks out of that river. Man who owns the source owns the valley. Owns the water, owns the people. Halloway means to dam it and sell it back to them by the cupful." He coughed, wet and long. "I gathered papers. Proof of what he's done, what he means to do. They're cached up there. The map's the key to all of it."

"And you want me to—"

"My daughter." For the first time the old man's voice cracked, and the crack went through Mirabel worse than the blood had. "Delia. Calder Junction. She works the telegraph office there." Crow's eyes fixed on Mirabel with a ferocity that had nothing left behind it but will. "She thinks I ran out on her. Twenty-two years, she's thought it. And maybe I did, in every way that counts. But I'm not going to her with empty hands and a story. I'm not going at all." He pressed the map toward Mirabel. "You take it to her. You tell her—" He stopped. Started again. "You tell her whatever you can tell her that lets her believe I thought about her every day I was out there. Because I did. Every day. That's the God's honest truth of it, and it's the only thing I've got left to give her besides paper."

Mirabel looked at the map. Then at the old man. Then at the door, and the dusk coming down blue beyond it, and the ridge line where, if he squinted, he could imagine riders.

"I don't take promises," he said. "Promises leave trails. A promise is a piece of paper with your name on it, and names get followed."

"Then don't sign anything," Crow said. "Just carry it. You've carried things before—I can see it in how you sit a saddle. Like a man hauling weight he won't talk about." He almost smiled. "We're alike that way, maybe. Two men full of cargo."

"I'm not alike anybody," Mirabel said, and heard how hollow it sounded, and hated that too.

* * *

They talked while the light died. Or rather, Crow talked, in the manner of dying men who know the talking is the last thing they own, and Mirabel listened, and told himself the whole time that he hadn't agreed to anything.

Crow told him about Delia learning the telegraph code at fourteen because her father taught her the alphabet on a slate before he left, and how she'd wired him once, years ago, a single word—WHY?—to a relay address he'd given her and never answered. Crow told him about the cache in the high country, a hollow beneath a split granite shelf marked on the map's margin in pencil. Papers, letters, a ledger of bribes. Enough, he said, to hang Silas Halloway twice.

"And if these riders catch up to me?" Mirabel asked. "If they find me carrying it?"

"Then you give it to them and live," Crow said flatly. "I'm not buying your death, mister. I'm asking a favor of a stranger, which is the oldest trade there is. But I'll tell you this." He gripped Mirabel's sleeve with the last of his strength. "Men who'd shoot an old prospector in the belly for a map—they're not going to stop at the map. Whatever Halloway's building up that river, it's big enough that he can't afford witnesses. You're already in it. You walked through the door."

Outside, the first stars were out. Somewhere up the darkening ridge, a pebble clicked loose and rolled.

Mirabel took the map.

"All right," he said. "Calder Junction. Your daughter. I'll say what I can." He hesitated, then added, because it seemed owed: "My name is—"

"Don't," Crow said, and now he did smile, a real one, crooked and tired. "Don't tell me your name. Names are for men who are staying. You just get there. That's enough."

So Mirabel held the dying man's hand through the last hour, and listened to him talk about a girl on a slate with chalk dust on her nose, and when the grip finally went slack near midnight, Mirabel sat a while in the cold with the map against his chest like something stolen.

* * *

He dug the grave by lantern light, up on the bench above the station where the ground was soft under the sagebrush. It was hard work alone, and honest work, and he was grateful for it in a grim way—grateful for anything that used the body and let the mind go quiet.

He wrapped Crow in his bedroll, laid him in, and was fitting the last stones along the head of the grave when he heard them.

Hooves. More than one horse, moving unhurried along the ridge trail, the way men move when they're looking at the country instead of the road.

Mirabel killed the lantern and went flat among the rocks. Three riders crossed the skyline against the stars, silhouettes with rifles slung, and paused at the crest. One of them dismounted. Even at that distance Mirabel could read the shape of what he was doing: crouching, studying the ground, reading sign the way Mirabel read drawings.

The rider picked something up. Held it to the starlight. A scrap of canvas—Mirabel's stomach dropped—he'd burned the wrapping, he was sure he'd burned it, but oilcloth leaves threads, threads leave traces—

The rider turned his head and spoke to the others, and though the words didn't carry, the gesture did: a long arm sweeping south, downriver. Toward Calder Junction. Toward the direction a courier would run.

Then they mounted and moved off along the ridge, unhurried still, men with time and a description and nowhere to be but everywhere ahead of him.

Mirabel lay in the cold a long moment, the fresh-turned earth smelling beside him, the map a flat weight against his ribs.

Well, he thought. So much for invisible.

He finished the cairn in the dark, said nothing over it because he'd never been any good at words for the dead, and led his mule down toward the river road while the stars wheeled overhead, indifferent and bright.

South, then. Toward Calder Junction. Toward a telegraph office and a woman who'd sent her father a single word twenty years ago and never gotten an answer.

Toward whatever Silas Halloway was building at the top of the water.

And somewhere behind him, faint on the night air, he could almost hear the click of a key beginning to tap out his name.
