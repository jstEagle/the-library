## 3. What the Map Wouldn't Say

The fire was small on purpose. A man who wanted to stay unrecorded learned to want small things: a fire the size of a cupped hand, a camp tucked behind a shelf of granite where the light wouldn't carry, coffee brewed weak enough to stretch two days into three. Mirabel had gotten good at wanting small things. It was the large ones that kept finding him.

He spread the map across his knees and let the firelight do its slow work on it.

Eben Crow had drawn it himself—that much was plain. The margins were crowded with a prospector's handwriting, all capitals and certainty: *GOOD WATER HERE. COLD EVEN IN AUGUST.* A sketch of a deadfall shaped like a bent elbow. A note reading *DO NOT TRUST THE MAN WITH THE GRAY COAT*, underlined twice, which Mirabel found less alarming than he might have a week ago, since he now owned a gray coat.

But the map itself—the lines of it—was another matter entirely.

The river ran down the center of the page in a blue so steady it looked ruled rather than drawn. Elevation marks stepped along its length like stair treads. Contour lines closed around the canyon walls in concentric whispers. And in the lower corner, where a draftsman signs his shame or his pride, there was a surveyor's notation block, neat as a hymnal:

*RECON. SURVEY — HEADWATERS DIVISION. GRADE TOLERANCE 0.4%. DATUM CORRECTED PER H.S.C. FIELD BULLETIN 9.*

Mirabel stopped breathing for a moment, which was foolish, because breathing was one of the few things still fully his.

H.S.C. Halloway Survey Company.

He knew that notation style the way a man knows his own signature forged on a check. The elevation figures carried their decimals stacked in threes. The datum correction was cited by bulletin number instead of date—a house habit, adopted because old Halloway himself had once dated a bulletin wrong and flooded a season's work. And there, along the river's upper reach, a tiny annotation in pencil over ink: *verify spring flow seasonal variance.*

*Verify.* Underlined. In that particular impatient hand, with the crossbar of the *f* slashing through the next letter like a man in a hurry to be somewhere better.

Mirabel had watched that hand make ten thousand marks across four years of his life. He had mixed the ink for it, once, when the crew ran short above Silver Bend and Tobias Finch sent him down the mountain with an empty bottle and a dollar fifty.

"Toby," Mirabel said aloud, to nobody, because saying it aloud made it either more real or less, and he'd take his chances on which.

Tobias Finch. Junior draftsman, Halloway Survey Company, second expedition. A thin, quick man who chewed his pencils to splinters and could calculate a grade in his head faster than most men could read one off paper. A man who used to say, laughing, that paper was the only honest thing in the territory—paper didn't drink, didn't gamble, didn't lie unless a man made it lie.

And then, one autumn, men made it lie.

* * *

The trestle at Corrie Narrows had been Mirabel's inspection. His signature, his stamp, his *certified sound for traffic* in ink that dried brown-black like old blood. He'd walked every span of it two days before the first ore train crossed. He remembered the walk. He remembered the wind coming up the gorge with a smell of snow in it, and he remembered thinking the timber sang oddly under his boots—not wrong, exactly, but tired, the way a rope goes tired before it parts.

What he hadn't known—what he had no way of knowing, though knowing had never once stopped him from blaming himself—was that the load reports were fiction.

Tobias Finch had drafted them. Mirabel understood that later, in the ugly arithmetic afterward, when the inquiry men laid the papers out on a table in a hotel parlor and asked him questions with no good answers. The reports certified ore cars at eleven tons gross. The actual loads, weighed at the mine head by a scale everyone agreed was honest, had run fourteen and fifteen. Somebody upstream had decided the difference was worth more than the bridge, and somebody downstream—in a parlor, in ink, in a fair hand—had made the numbers agree.

Twenty-one men. He counted them still, some nights, the way other men counted sheep. Brakemen mostly, and a fireman named Aldous Pike who owed Mirabel two dollars and had promised to pay it back Saturday. The trestle went at the third span, mid-afternoon, in weather so clear and ordinary that witnesses kept describing the sky as if the sky were somehow culpable too.

Mirabel had signed the inspection. That was true, and true things are the heaviest cargo there is. But he had signed it on the strength of reports that said the bridge would carry what the bridge was told it would carry, and the men who wrote those reports had gone on writing reports, promoted and pensioned, while Mirabel's name went into the ledgers as the man whose bridge fell down.

So he had done the only arithmetic left to him. He subtracted himself. New name, new territory, work paid in cash on lines no company bothered to keep records of anymore. Dead lines. Fitting employment for a dead man.

And now here was Toby Finch's handwriting, riding folded against Mirabel's chest, warm from his own body heat, as if the past had simply been waiting for him to sit still long enough to catch up.

* * *

He turned the map toward the fire and studied the notation block again, and this time he made himself look past the shock to what it meant.

Crow hadn't drawn this alone. Crow couldn't have—the contour work alone was beyond any prospector's patience. No: Crow had met someone in the high country, someone with a transit and a training, someone who surveyed the headwaters and then handed the results to an old man with a grudge and a gun. Or perhaps it went the other way. Perhaps Finch had come to Crow. Either way, the conclusion sat in Mirabel's stomach like a swallowed stone:

The Halloway Survey Company had already been to the source of that river. Had measured it. Had put numbers on it. And companies did not spend survey crews on water they didn't intend to own.

He thought about the way Crow had gripped his wrist at the end, the grip of a man spending his last coin. *They killed for this. They'll kill again.* At the time Mirabel had taken it for the fever talking, a dying man inflating his life's work into a treasure. Now the notation block looked back at him with different eyes. This wasn't a treasure map. It was evidence with a border drawn around it.

And Toby Finch had made it.

Which raised a question Mirabel turned over slowly, the way you turn over a timber to see what the rot has eaten. If Finch had surveyed the headwaters for Halloway, why pass the knowledge to Crow? Men didn't betray employers over nothing. Unless the something was big enough. Unless Finch had stood at that cold spring, pencil in his teeth, doing the sums in his head the way he did, and arrived at a number he couldn't live inside.

Or unless Finch was dead, and this was his ghost, walking around in ink.

Mirabel fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Somewhere upslope a branch cracked—wind, probably, or a deer, or a man with a gray coat of his own—and he held still until the night resolved back into its ordinary noises. His heart took its time coming down.

Here was the trouble, he thought, and it was the same trouble he'd been circling for six years without ever quite landing on it: he had built his whole life on the theory that a man could be subtracted. That if you kept your name off paper and your face off ledgers, the past would eventually lose your scent, the way a hound gives up a cold trail. Six years of small fires and false names and cash wages, and he had almost believed it.

But the past didn't lose your scent. It just took shortcuts you didn't know about. It waited inside the handwriting of an old friend. It folded itself into a dying stranger's coat pocket and rode a handcar forty miles to find you. Every road he'd taken to get away from Corrie Narrows had, without his noticing, been quietly bending back toward it.

He was not running alongside this story anymore. He was in it. He had been in it since the moment Crow pressed the oilcloth packet into his hands, maybe since the moment he'd first heard the name Halloway spoken in a rail yard years ago and felt something cold walk up his spine. Staying invisible wasn't a plan. It was a debt accruing interest, and the collector had finally found his address.

Which left the question of what a man did about it.

Mirabel looked at the map—at the blue line of the river climbing toward its source, at Toby's patient contours, at the prospector's block capitals promising cold water even in August. Tomorrow he would point himself upriver, because a promise was a promise and he'd given his word to a dead man, and also because—though he was not yet ready to say this part plainly, even to himself, even alone, even to a fire the size of a cupped hand—if Halloway's crew was drawing lines toward that spring, then somebody ought to be standing at the end of them who knew exactly what those men were capable of building, and what they were capable of letting fall.

He banked the fire to embers, folded the map along its old creases, and slid it inside his shirt, where it lay against his sternum like a second heartbeat.

Sleep came late and thin. And near dawn, half-dreaming, he heard again the tired song of timber under his boots, and woke with his hand already reaching for the map—to check, one more time, whether Toby Finch had signed his name anywhere on it.

He hadn't. But in the bottom margin, so faint it might have been an accident of the pen, someone had written a single word in that slashing, hurried hand:

*Calder.*

Calder Junction. Where a daughter was waiting who didn't know yet that her father had died holding the truth in his fists—and where, if the word meant what Mirabel feared it meant, someone else might already be looking.
