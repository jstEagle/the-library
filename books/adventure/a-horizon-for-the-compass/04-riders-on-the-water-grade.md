## 4. Riders on the Water Grade

The train to Calder Junction was called the *Mule*, which was honest of it, because it was stubborn, slow, and prone to standing in the middle of nowhere refusing to explain itself. Mirabel Sterling rode it under the name Aldous Finch, third from the back in a car that smelled of stove oil and wet wool, and he did not look up when the conductor called the stations in a voice like a man reading his own eulogy.

He had been *Aldous Finch* for eleven days. Before that he had been *Tobias Marsh*, a bridge carpenter out of Redfern, and before that a name he'd already forgotten, which was the general idea. Names, in Mirabel's experience, were like boots: you wore them until they wore out, and you never let yourself get attached.

The trouble with the *Mule* was that it stopped everywhere. It stopped at water towers and whistle-posts and once, memorably, for forty minutes because a cow had opinions about the right-of-way. A man trying to stay unrecorded does not love a train that stops everywhere, but a man with no money does not love walking, either, and so Mirabel had made his peace with the cow and settled in with his collar up and the map sewn flat against his ribs, inside his shirt, where it lay against his skin like a second heartbeat.

He'd studied it every night since the burial. By the light of the stove he had traced the river's whole argument — the blue thread of it climbing through the canyon country, past Gray Fork, past the Narrows, up to a small precise star at the head of the line where the draftsman had written, in that careful engineer's hand: *SOURCE. ELEV. 9,140. VERIFY.*

Verify. As if the source of a river were a load of gravel that might come up short on the invoice.

He was thinking about that hand — about the particular way the man had made his *f*s with a crossbar like a fence rail, a habit Mirabel had seen on a thousand survey sheets in another life — when the train began to slow at a water stop called Dunlow Siding, and three men came down the platform who did not look like they were waiting for anything.

They looked like they were *checking* for something.

Mirabel knew the type the way a deer knows a certain stillness in the grass. Two of them wore canvas dusters and carried themselves like men whose work was standing on things and looking at other things. The third was different. He was lean and gray at the temples, with a face like a filed-down tool, and he wore a surveyor's coat with the buttons polished, and he walked the platform the way an auditor walks into a room — already certain something was missing from the books.

The two dusters climbed into the forward cars. The lean man stood on the platform and spoke to the conductor, and the conductor pointed, and his finger traveled down the line of windows toward the back of the train.

Toward Mirabel.

There is a particular cold that starts in the sternum and works outward, and Mirabel had felt it before — on the morning the trestle at Corben's Mill came down, in the half-second when he understood the sound the timbers were making and understood, too, that understanding would not save anybody. He felt it now. He kept his face doing nothing at all, which was the one skill twenty years of rail work had perfected in him, and he slid his hand inside his coat and touched the map through the cloth.

*You're being foolish,* he told himself. *They're looking for a prospector's killer, not a bridge carpenter.*

But the conductor was coming up the aisle with the lean man behind him, and the lean man was looking at hats. That was the detail that turned the cold into ice: he was checking hats, and coats, and the set of men's shoulders, working his way down the car like a man ticking boxes on a form. Somewhere there was a description. Somewhere there was a paper with a man's height and his build and the fact that he favored his left leg on cold mornings, and the paper had been passed from Silas Halloway's office to this filed-down face, and the face was here to match it to a body.

Mirabel had buried Eben Crow four days ago with his own hands, in ground that was still soft, while riders watched from the ridge like crows who'd come to see fair play. He had told himself then what he told himself every night: *deliver the map, keep your head down, stay a ghost.* Ghosts did not get matched to descriptions. Ghosts did not have faces the lean man could put a finger on.

But the conductor had stopped three seats away and was saying, "This here's Mr. Voss, of the Halloway Survey Company. Company business. He'll just have a look at the passengers, no trouble to anyone."

No trouble to anyone. Mirabel had heard that sentence before every kind of trouble there was.

The man called Voss worked his way down the aisle without hurry. He did not grab hats off heads. He looked, and he let you feel him looking, and there was a small leather notebook in his gloved hand that he consulted the way a priest consults a breviary. Two seats away, a drummer selling patent liniment met his eye and got a polite nod and was passed over. One seat away, an old woman with a birdcage covered in flannel got a nod and a murmured apology.

Then Voss was standing in the aisle beside Mirabel, and the train, with the *Mule*'s gift for terrible timing, chose that moment to lurch and begin gathering steam.

"Morning," Voss said.

"Morning," said Aldous Finch, bridge carpenter, and was privately astonished at how steady it came out.

"Traveling far?"

"Far as the work is." He let a little weariness into it, the honest weariness of a man who'd slept sitting up. "There's trestle work on the upper line. Seasonal."

"Trestle work." Voss said it the way another man might say *gold in them hills*, and his eyes — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who had learned that people tell you everything if you give them silence to fill — went over Mirabel's coat, his boots, his hands. His hands. Mirabel watched him look at the hands and felt the ice go all the way through. A bridge carpenter's hands were wrecked in a particular way, but so were an engineer's, and Voss had a notebook, and somewhere in that notebook there might be a line about a man who knew rail work too well to be what he claimed.

"You'll be familiar with the river country, then," Voss said. "Upstream of Gray Fork."

"Nobody's familiar with that country. That's the point of it."

Something moved at the corner of Voss's mouth. Not quite a smile. A measurement. "There's a man we're looking for," he said. "Prospector. Older fellow. Gray beard, half-blind in one eye. He's taken something that belongs to the company, and the company wants a conversation with him. You see anybody like that?"

Mirabel looked out the window with the mild, unhelpful gaze of a man trying to remember. "Saw a fellow at Redfern fit half that. Old men and gray beards — this country's full of them. Half-blind too, half the time. Bad whiskey." He shrugged. "You'd do better asking at the way stations. Old men don't ride the *Mule*. Costs too much."

"No," Voss agreed softly. "They don't." He consulted his notebook, wrote nothing, closed it. "Well. Keep your eyes open, Mr.—"

"Finch. Aldous Finch."

"Mr. Finch." Voss touched his hat brim, and it was the most courteous thing Mirabel had ever felt a threat do. "If a gray-bearded man asks you to carry anything for him — a letter, a package, a *map* — the company would consider it a favor, and pay for the favor, if you brought it to us instead."

"I'll keep it in mind."

Voss moved on up the aisle. At the far door he paused, spoke a low word to one of the dusters, and the man got off at Dunlow Siding and stood on the platform with his hands in his pockets, watching the windows. Watching the back of the train.

* * *

Mirabel did the arithmetic while the *Mule* grumbled and gathered itself. Voss had boarded at Dunlow, which meant Voss had come *up* the line from the south — from Calder Junction way — which meant the net was strung across the country ahead of him, not behind. They knew Crow's daughter was at Calder. They were watching the roads in, watching the trains, watching for an old man or whoever carried his paper. Voss hadn't matched Mirabel to anything yet. Voss had *fished*. But a man who fished like that would fish again, and the next time there'd be a better description, and the *Mule* stopped everywhere, and every stop was a platform, and every platform had room on it for a man with a notebook.

There was also the matter of what Voss had said. *A map.* They knew it was a map. Which meant someone had seen it — seen it and talked, or died before they could talk, and either way the company had moved from hunting a thief to hunting a document, and documents could be carried by anybody. By a bridge carpenter, say. By a man with wrecked hands and a favoring leg.

The *Mule* began to slow at the far end of Dunlow Siding — one of its famous unexplained stops, a signal check, the cow of the rails — and Mirabel was moving before he had finished deciding to move. That was the thing about fear, he'd learned: used rightly, it was faster than thought. He came off the rear platform on the blind side, away from the duster, dropped the four feet with a grunt his knee protested, and was in the ditch scrub before the train had finished its complaint.

He crouched in the greasewood and watched the *Mule* pull out without him. It felt, absurdly, like being left by a slow-witted friend. He watched the back of the last car shrink up the line, watched the duster on the platform turn and confer with a second man who'd been out of sight behind the water tower — two of them, then, left at Dunlow, walking the train's route with their eyes — and then he turned his back on the railroad for the first time in twenty years and looked at the river country.

It did not look welcoming. It looked like God had started the mountains and lost interest halfway through the paperwork: canyon after canyon shouldering up against each other, the river invisible somewhere in all that stone, and above it all the gray suggestion of the high country where a small precise star said *SOURCE. ELEV. 9,140. VERIFY.*

He dug out the map, checked his bearings against the sun, and did the thing he was actually good at, the thing no false name could touch: he read the country like a drawing. There — the notch in the eastern ridge would be the river's cut. There — the talus fan meant a slide path, which meant game trail, which meant water. An engineer could not help it. Give him broken ground and he started seeing how it was held together, and how it might be crossed, and — the old, cursed, useful part of him — how it might come down on a man's head if he stood in the wrong place.

He thought about Voss's notebook. He thought about *the company would consider it a favor*. He thought about twenty-one men whose names he still knew, all of them, in the order they'd been lost, the way other men knew psalms.

"Finch," he said aloud, trying the name on one last time. It sat wrong, like a boot on the wrong foot. It had sat wrong for eleven days.

He folded the map away, set his face upriver, and started walking. Behind him, faint on the wind, he could hear the *Mule* whistle at the far crossing, going on to Calder Junction without him, carrying his name — *Aldous Finch, seasonal* — in the conductor's memory, the last place it would ever be written down.

Ahead of him, somewhere in the stone and the pine and the cold climbing water, there was a girl who believed her father had abandoned her, and a company that believed a map was property, and a river that believed, with the simple total conviction of water, in reaching the sea.

Mirabel meant to see that all three of them were set right — though of the three, he suspected the river would be the easiest to convince.

He walked. And behind him, at Dunlow Siding, a lean man with a polished-button coat stood on the platform a long while, looking at the place where the train had stopped and started again, and at the ditch scrub, and at nothing — and then took out his notebook and wrote something down.
