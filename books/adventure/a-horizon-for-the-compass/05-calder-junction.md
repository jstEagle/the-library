## 5. Calder Junction

The wire came in at eleven minutes past four on a Tuesday afternoon, which Delia Crow would remember later because it was the hour she usually allowed herself one cup of coffee and a private grudge against her father.

She had the grudge down to a fine art by now. Eleven years of practice. It had a shape and a schedule, like church. On Sundays it was a soft thing, almost fond — *he could have written, he knew where the office was.* On Tuesdays, when the freight schedule came through and she had to log shipments of ore she'd never see the other end of, it hardened into something with edges. *He taught me the alphabet of dots and dashes before I could read print, and then he used that knowledge to never once send me a word.*

Eleven years. Not one telegram. Not one letter. Eben Crow had walked out of Calder Junction with a mule and a promise to be back by the first snow, and the first snow had come and gone forty times since, and Delia had grown from a girl of fourteen into a woman who wore her hair pinned so tight it gave her a permanent look of mild disapproval, which she had decided suited her.

The key chattered under her hand and she took the message without thinking, her pencil moving in the shorthand of her trade. Then she stopped.

It wasn't addressed to the station. It wasn't addressed to anyone at Calder Junction at all.

TO: M. STERLING — CARE OF FREIGHT OFFICE CALDER JUNCTION — REPEAT CARE OF —

She sat back. The office was empty except for her and the stove, which ticked as it cooled, and outside the window the street did its Tuesday business: two dogs arguing over a bone, old Mrs. Tackett beating a rug with the violence of a woman who had buried three husbands and intended to outlive the fourth's memory too.

M. Sterling. She turned the flimsy over, as though the name might have a twin on the back. It didn't.

The message itself was worse than the name.

STERLING — HALLOWAY SURVEY PARTY LEFT DENVER NINTH — VOSS CARRYING COMMISSION PAPERS AND GUN PERMIT — THEY HAVE YOUR DESCRIPTION — DO NOT TAKE THE NORTH LINE — REPEAT DO NOT TAKE THE NORTH LINE — WIRE ACKNOWLEDGMENT THROUGH THIS OFFICE ONLY —

There was no signature. There was no acknowledgment to send, either, because there was no M. Sterling in Calder Junction, and there was no M. Sterling expected on any manifest she'd seen, and yet here was a stranger's warning arriving at her window like a letter to a ghost.

Delia read it four times. On the fourth reading, two things happened in her chest at once, and they did not get along.

The first was fear, plain and cold: Halloway. She knew the name the way you know the name of a storm season. Her father had said it exactly once, drunk on rotgut whiskey the winter before he left, sitting in this very office with his boots on her desk, saying, *If a man named Halloway ever comes asking after me, girl, you tell him I'm dead and mean it.* She'd been thirteen and thought it was just prospector talk, the same genre as his stories about fish that could spell.

The second thing was smaller and sharper and it went off in her like a struck match:

*Her father was alive.*

Not alive now — the message didn't say that, and something in the phrasing of it, in the way warnings are worded by people who've already given up on gentleness, suggested otherwise. But alive recently. Alive enough to matter to men with gun permits. Alive enough that somebody, somewhere, believed Eben Crow's business had crossed paths with a man named Sterling, and that the crossing was worth a wire.

Eleven years of silence, and the first news of her father arrived addressed to a stranger.

Delia put both hands flat on the desk, which was what she did instead of screaming, and looked out the window at Mrs. Tackett, who had moved on from the rug to the porch rail, apparently dissatisfied with the rail's moral character.

"Well," Delia said to the empty office. "That's just like him."

* * *

Here is what Delia Crow knew about her father, arranged in the order she usually deployed the facts when defending him to herself, which was a battle she fought daily and lost nightly:

One. He had taught her Morse code across a kitchen table, tapping it out on the wood with a spoon handle until she dreamed in dots. Two. He had been, by every account except hers, a decent man — the kind who fixed strangers' wagon axles for nothing and remembered the names of other people's children. Three. He had loved her mother, who died of fever when Delia was nine, with a devotion that even grief could not make embarrassing. Four. And this fact always arrived last, like a guest who knows he isn't welcome: none of those things explained why he had never sent so much as a postcard.

She had built theories over the years the way other people built barns, adding to them season by season. Theory the first: he was dead, and had died ashamed of some debt, and shame kept his hand off the key. Theory the second: he had another family somewhere, a warmer daughter with better handwriting, and cowardice kept him from choosing between them. Theory the third, the one she only admitted to at night: he wasn't dead and hadn't remarried but simply couldn't come back, because of something he'd found or something he'd done, and every year of silence made the return impossible, the way a lie gets heavier the longer you carry it.

Theory the third had always felt truest. Delia distrusted it accordingly.

Now there was a wire on her desk with a dead man's name in it — no. With a *maybe*-dead man's name in it. Halloway Survey. Voss. A description of someone they were hunting. And an instruction: *do not take the north line.*

The north line ran upriver. Toward the high country. Toward, if you followed it far enough and then left it and followed the water instead, the kind of country her father had vanished into.

Delia got up, stoked the stove, and made her coffee, because important decisions should never be made uncaffeinated — her mother's rule, the only inheritance that had ever been delivered on time.

* * *

By six o'clock she had assembled the facts, and the facts were these:

Whoever M. Sterling was, he was coming to Calder Junction or had already passed through it, and he was carrying something that connected to Eben Crow. The wire assumed Sterling would check the freight office for messages, which meant Sterling was careful, which meant Sterling was in trouble, which meant trouble was the family business now whether she liked it or not.

And Voss was behind him. Or ahead of him. With papers and a permit and, presumably, the disposition of a man whose name was Voss.

Delia considered, briefly and honestly, doing nothing. It was a real consideration. She had a job, a room above the dry goods store, a life measured out in schedules and the small satisfactions of a well-sent message. Men with gun permits were not her weather. She could burn the wire, tell no one, and go on being the woman who beat rugs on Tuesdays.

But the thought of doing nothing lasted about as long as Mrs. Tackett's patience with that porch rail.

Because here was the thing underneath everything, the thing she'd have denied under oath: she didn't want the map, if there was a map. She didn't want whatever her father had found, or his share of it, or his debts either. What she wanted — what she had wanted for eleven years, through every theory and every Sunday-morning softness of the grudge — was an answer. One answer, delivered face to face, from the mouth of someone who had actually seen him. Why. Just why. Even a bad why. Even a why that broke her heart clean through would be worth more than all the gold in the territory, because at least then the not-knowing would end, and a person can survive a broken heart but not an open question carried past midnight for a decade.

If this Sterling had known her father — and the wire said he had, said it in the language between the lines — then Sterling was the closest thing to an answer that had ever ridden toward her.

And if Voss wanted Sterling, then Sterling was also bait, and Voss would follow the bait straight to her door, and she would very much rather meet trouble on ground she chose than wait for it to knock.

"Answers," she told the stove, "are not the same as inheritances." The stove declined to argue.

* * *

She worked until eight, because quitting early would raise questions, and Delia Crow's entire strategy for the next several days depended on raising no questions whatsoever. At eight she locked the office, wrote FALSE — NO SUCH PERSON HERE — WILL ADVISE in response to the wire, which was technically true and would buy time, and went upstairs to pack.

Packing took twenty minutes, most of which she spent arguing with herself and losing gracefully. She took her father's old field coat from the trunk at the foot of her bed — it still smelled faintly of pipe smoke and pine pitch, and she put it on before she could reconsider — a revolver she had cleaned monthly for eleven years without once firing, a week of hard bread and jerky, and, folded into her breast pocket like a talisman, the one object of her father's she owned besides the coat: a brass compass with a hairline crack across its face, the needle still true. *A horizon for the compass,* he used to say, tapping it. *Long as you know where north is, girl, you're never lost. You're only somewhere you haven't been yet.*

She had thought, packing, that she might cry. She had budgeted for it. But what came instead was a strange steady anger, warm at the center like a banked fire, and she recognized it as fuel and stopped questioning it.

She left a note for the stationmaster — *family business, back inside two weeks, mind the Thursday freight* — which was two lies and a truth braided together, and she found she was better at braiding than she'd known.

The night agent at the north line told her the morning train was delayed; a rockslide below Gray Fork had the crews out with shovels. Passengers were walking the grade to the crossing and picking up transport beyond.

Gray Fork. Upriver. Exactly where a careful man avoiding the north line would — no. Exactly where a man who *hadn't gotten the warning* would be walking anyway, straight into the open, with a description of his own face traveling faster than he was.

Delia stood on the dark platform with her bag in her hand and her father's coat around her shoulders and felt the whole shape of it settle over her: somewhere on that river road was a stranger carrying her father's last secret, and somewhere behind him were men carrying guns and commission papers, and the only person in the world positioned to reach the stranger first was a telegraph operator with a cracked compass, a month of saved wages, and a grudge eleven years deep that had just, finally, been given somewhere to go.

"You'd laugh," she said to the compass, to the night, to the absent man. "You'd laugh and then you'd say don't do anything foolish. Well."

She stepped off the platform onto the river road, north, into the cold, walking at the pace of a woman who intended to arrive before her questions did.
