## 6. The Crossing at Gray Fork

Delia had been following the stranger for two days, and in that time she had learned three things about him.

First: he walked like a man who expected the ground to betray him, testing each step before committing his weight, which was either caution or a guilty conscience. Second: he was careful with fire—small flames, deadfalls for smoke, never twice in the same hollow—which meant he wasn't merely traveling. He was hiding. Third: he stopped at noon on the second day and stood a long while over something small and pale in his hand, and when he put it away his shoulders were different, set like a man carrying freight instead of luggage.

Her father's map. She'd have bet her telegraph key on it.

She should have felt triumphant. Instead she felt the particular misery of a woman crouched behind a granite boulder in the rain, eating cold beans out of a tin with her fingers, watching a man she intended to rob eat cold beans out of a tin with his fingers, forty yards off, doing it neater.

"Papa," she muttered, "you couldn't have left me a house. Or a horse. You left me a *quest*."

The river answered with a sound she'd been hearing all morning and pretending not to hear: a low, grinding roar from around the bend ahead, where the canyon pinched and the water went from brown to white.

Gray Fork. The crossing her father's letters had mentioned once, years ago, back when he still wrote letters—the only ford between here and the high country, and by the look of the spray rising past the pines, the spring melt had turned it into something less a crossing than an argument the river intended to win.

The man reached the bank first. She watched him study the water the way she imagined he studied everything: patiently, unhappily, thoroughly. Then he began to do something so foolish she nearly called out—to unbuckle his pack and lash it tighter, cinching straps, preparing to cross.

In that water? Alone?

Delia got up from behind her rock and started down the slope, telling herself she was being practical. She needed him alive. Dead men don't answer questions, and she had a decade of them banked up like snowmelt behind a dam.

* * *

Mirabel heard her before he saw her—boots on scree, no attempt at quiet, which told him she'd decided stealth was no longer required. He turned with his hands loose at his sides, the way you did when you didn't know what a person wanted and wanted them to know you weren't the one who'd decide how this went.

She was young. Twenties. Rain-dark hair escaping a braid, a canvas coat too big for her, and eyes that looked at him the way auditors looked at ledgers.

"You're going to try to cross," she said.

"I'm going to try," he agreed.

"It'll kill you."

"It might." He nodded at the water. "But there are men behind me who definitely will, so I find myself weighing my options."

Something flickered across her face—not sympathy exactly. Recognition, maybe. "How far back?"

"Half a day. Maybe less. They ride; I walk."

"Then we have a problem," she said, "because I've spent two days catching you, and I'll be damned if I let some survey crew drown you before I get what I came for."

"And what's that?"

"The map," Delia Crow said, and watched the name land. She'd rehearsed it on the trail, imagined his face going smooth and careful, a liar's face. Instead he went still in a different way—like a bridge hearing a load it hadn't been built for.

"Eben Crow," Mirabel said quietly. "You're his daughter."

"He told you about me?"

"He said—" Mirabel stopped. Started again. "He said your name was Delia. That you worked the wire. That he'd failed you worse than he'd failed anything else, and he'd failed plenty."

The words hit harder than she'd braced for. She'd prepared for evasion, for lies, for a man who'd say *what map?* She had not prepared for a stranger quoting her father's grief back to her on a riverbank in the rain.

"Where is he?" she asked, though the question came out already knowing.

* * *

Mirabel told her the truth because lying to her suddenly seemed obscene. He told her about the way station at dusk, the gut shot, the smell of blood and woodsmoke, the way Eben Crow had gripped his wrist with a strength that surprised them both and said, *Not Halloway. Anyone but Halloway.* He told her about the grave under the cottonwoods, marked with a stone, and the promise, and the map pressed into his hands like a coal someone was passing because they could no longer hold it.

Delia listened without moving. When he finished, she sat down on a boulder as if her knees had filed their resignation.

"He died keeping it from them," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"And he gave it to a stranger. Not to me. To a *stranger*."

"He was dying fast," Mirabel said. "I think he took the nearest honest pair of hands. I don't think it was a judgment on you."

"Everything he ever did was a judgment on me," she said, and then pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes, furious at herself, at the tears, at the whole wet ridiculous canyon. "Ten years. Ten years of nothing. One letter a year, then one every other year, then nothing, and I told myself he was dead, because dead was better than *chose not to come back*. And now it turns out he was up here guarding a piece of paper worth killing for, and he sent it home with the first man he tripped over."

"He didn't trip over me," Mirabel said. "I found him."

"That's worse somehow."

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry."

And the terrible thing, the thing she'd chew on later, was that he sounded like he meant it—not the polite sorrow of strangers, but the flat, worn sorrow of a man who had apologized for things far larger than this and never been forgiven.

* * *

They were still standing there, two liars circling each other's truths, when the river made its move.

It came as a sound first—a deep wooden groan upstream—and then the water rose, quick and filthy, the spring melt surging as something gave way above. The gravel bar where they stood began to soften, to shift, to become not-ground.

"Move!" Mirabel grabbed her arm, and they ran for the cutbank, but the river was faster where it mattered: the channel between them and the high bank filled in three heartbeats, gray water chest-deep and climbing, dragging ice-scoured logs through it like battering rams.

Delia went down first. The current took her feet and swung her sideways, and the world became noise and cold and a log bearing down on her with the patience of a train.

Then a hand closed on her coat collar. Mirabel had come in after her—he'd have denied it later, called it arithmetic, two swimmers better than one—but the truth was written in his grip: he held on like a man who had once lost people to water and ground alike and had sworn privately, in whatever dark ledger he kept, *never again.*

"Left! Toward the willows!" he shouted against her ear.

They fought for it together—her legs finding purchase on submerged stone, his arm hooked around a willow trunk thick as a mast, hauling them both into the tangle of branches on the far side, where they collapsed among the roots, gasping, soaked to the soul, alive.

For a long moment neither spoke. The river roared its opinion.

"Well," Delia managed finally, spitting out water. "That's one way to avoid a conversation."

"You were going to drown before you finished interrogating me," Mirabel said. "Seemed wasteful."

She laughed—a short, startled bark of a laugh, the kind that escapes before grief can catch it. He almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

They built a fire in the lee of a fallen spruce, both of them stripped to dry underlayers, their outer clothes steaming on sticks like scarecrows at a funeral. Delia wrung out her braid and studied the man across the flames. He'd saved her life without hesitation and now sat hunched like the fire owed him money.

"My turn," she said. "You know my name, my father, my grievance. I know nothing about you except that survey crews chase you and rivers can't kill you. Who are you?"

He fed a stick to the fire. Took his time.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters that you're carrying my father's last act in your pack. It matters that men with rifles want it. Yes. It matters."

Another stick. The fire crackled.

"When I was younger," he said at last, "I was a railroad engineer. A good one. And I signed an inspection report on a trestle—a load rating. I signed it because the survey company told me the figures were verified, and because I wanted to keep my position, and because checking would have taken a week and made powerful men inconvenient." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "A freight train crossed it eleven days later. Twenty-one men. I survived because I was in the caboose."

Delia sat very still.

"The reports were falsified," he went on. "Halloway Survey Company. Silas Halloway himself signed the verification above mine. But mine is the name on the inspection, and mine is the name that vanished afterward. I've been running under borrowed names for six years. The man you've been following doesn't exist. I'm Mirabel Sterling, and I am, officially, the reason twenty-one families have empty chairs."

The rain ticked on the spruce boughs. Somewhere upstream, the river kept chewing at the world.

"Halloway," Delia said slowly. "The same Halloway."

"The same."

"So my father spent ten years gathering proof against the man who ruined you. And handed it—" she gestured at his pack, drying by the fire, "—to the one man in the territory with a reason to see it delivered."

Mirabel stared into the flames. Something moved through his face, slow as weather. "I hadn't put that together until just now," he admitted. "I thought I was doing him a kindness."

"You thought you were paying a debt."

"I thought I was paying a debt," he agreed. "Turns out I may be collecting one."

Delia pulled her knees up and considered him. Here was the strange arithmetic of it: every word out of this man was a confession, and yet she trusted him more than she'd trusted anyone since her father's last letter. Liars, she thought, recognize each other. She had spent ten years telling the town of Calder Junction she didn't care where her father was. He had spent six years telling the world he was nobody at all. They were both walking archives of things unsaid.

"All right, Mirabel Sterling," she said, and watched him flinch at his own name like a man touching a burn. "Here's my offer. You deliver the map to me—that part's done, consider yourself paid in full. But I'm coming with you to the source. My father died for what's up there, and I intend to know what it was. And if Halloway's men are behind us—" she nodded downstream, toward the sound of distant hooves on the far grade, faint but unmistakable, "—then you'll want someone who can work a telegraph line, because I saw the wire strung along this canyon yesterday, and my father taught me that information travels faster than bullets."

Mirabel looked at her, then at the fire, then at the darkening mouth of the canyon ahead, where the river ran narrow and white toward whatever secret Eben Crow had guarded with his life.

"They're closer than half a day," he said. "An hour, maybe."

"Then I suggest," said Delia Crow, kicking dirt over the fire and reaching for her boots, "that we stop trading confessions and start moving. You can tell me the rest about the twenty-one men tonight—if we live to have a night."

She was already walking. After a moment, Mirabel Sterling—who had spent six years ensuring that no one anywhere would walk beside him—shouldered his pack and followed.
