## 7. Winter in the Narrows

The snow began as a rumor and became a verdict.

For two days it had been falling in fat, indifferent flakes while Mirabel and Delia followed the river up through the Narrows—a canyon so tight in places that the water seemed to be holding its breath. The walls rose gray and sheer on either side, streaked with ice like old candle wax, and the sky above was a ribbon of pewter no wider than a wagon road.

"We're being swallowed," Delia said. She said it without drama, the way she reported everything, in the flat cadence of a telegraph operator reading off a wire. *Canyon closing. Send help. Stop.*

"Swallowed's not so bad," Mirabel said. "It's the chewing I worry about."

She almost smiled. He'd learned to count her smiles the way he counted trestle spikes—rare, essential, each one placed exactly where it needed to bear weight.

They made camp that third night under an overhang where the rock leaned out like a patient father, and Mirabel built a fire small enough to hide from God and large enough to lie to themselves about warmth. They had half a sack of flour, a strip of salt pork gone the color of old rope, and a tin of coffee beans they were rationing at four beans a day, which Delia insisted on counting aloud.

"Four," she said, dropping them into the pan. "That's breakfast."

"That's a suggestion of breakfast."

"It's arithmetic, Mr. Sterling. Four beans times two people is eight beans, and eight beans is a meal if you chew slowly and hate yourself a little."

He laughed before he could stop himself—the first real laugh since Gray Fork—and it startled him, coming out of him like a bird flushed from dead brush. He hadn't known there were birds left in there.

* * *

The trouble announced itself at dawn, as trouble prefers to.

Mirabel climbed out first to check their backtrail and found the canyon behind them filled—not with men, but with the sound of men: axes on wood, the ring of iron shod on stone, a mule complaining somewhere around the bend with the moral indignation only mules can manage. Voss's crew. A day behind them, maybe less. The snow that should have covered their tracks had instead given the pursuers a roadbed, packed smooth by the storm, and now six or seven figures moved along it like beads on a wire.

"Delia." He crouched back under the overhang. "Company."

"How much?"

"Enough to share whether we want to or not."

She looked past him at the falling snow, then ahead, upcanyon, and her face did something complicated. "Then we go forward."

Forward lasted until noon.

The Narrows pinched shut at a place where the river itself had been forced through a gap barely wide enough for a barrel, and above the gap the cliff had let go sometime in the night—or perhaps the freeze and thaw of years had simply been waiting for an audience. Rock had come down across what passed for a trail: house-sized blocks tumbled together, snow sifting into the seams, a slope of shattered stone rising thirty feet to where the canyon wall resumed its climb. Beyond it, the river chattered on as though nothing had happened, which Mirabel supposed was the privilege of rivers.

He stood at the base of the slide for a long time, reading it the way he read any structure. His hands did the arithmetic without him. Too steep. Too loose. No anchor points worth trusting, and no tools but a hatchet and a length of rope. Even with a crew and dynamite it would take days, and every hour of hammering would ring down the canyon like a dinner bell for Voss.

Behind them, the axes kept talking.

"We can't go over," he said finally.

"I know. I watched you decide."

"And we can't go back."

"I know that too. I've been listening to those axes all morning, and one of them has a rhythm like a man who enjoys his work." She turned to look at him, snow gathering on her shoulders, her breath standing in the air between them. "So here we are, Mr. Sterling. Trapped in a hole in the ground with half a sack of flour and your four-bean philosophy."

"The beans are yours today. I ate yesterday's."

"You did not."

"I gave mine to the mule."

"There is no mule."

"Then I've been robbed," he said, and she laughed—a short, sharp thing, surprised out of her—but the laugh died quick, because there was nothing behind it to feed on, and they both knew it.

* * *

They went back to the overhang. There was nowhere else. The fire had burned to a red memory in its bed of ash, and Mirabel fed it splinters one at a time, husbanding heat like a miser, while Delia sat with her knees drawn up and her father's map spread across them, studying it by the light as though the paper might relent and show them a way out that wasn't there.

"He drew this in August," she said quietly. "I know because he wrote the date in the corner. August means he was alive in August. I spent August wondering if he was dead." Her thumb moved along the river's blue line, upstream, toward the source. "Do you understand how angry that makes me? Not that he died. That he was *alive*, and chose not to say so."

"Yes," Mirabel said. "I think I do."

And something in him—some door he had been leaning against for eleven years, shoulder set, boots braced—suddenly swung open, because he was tired, and cold, and the axes were coming, and there was no longer anywhere in the world left to lean.

"Delia." He fed another splinter to the fire. "You asked me once, at the crossing, why a man who builds bridges runs from them. I told you I didn't."

"You told me a great many things you didn't."

"I'm going to tell you one thing I did." He watched the flame take hold, small and stubborn. "There was a trestle outside Marlowe Bend. Eleven years ago. I inspected it. My signature is on the inspection report—it's probably still in some company file, yellowed and official, saying the structure was sound for service. Three weeks later it came down under a freight train with forty-one men aboard."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, snow slid off a ledge and hissed into the river.

"Twenty-one didn't come out," he said. "The water took some. The timber took others. I helped pull bodies out for two days, and on the third day I signed a statement, and on the fourth day I understood that the statement was a lie, and on the fifth day I was gone. New name. New line. New ledger with my name left off it." He turned his hands over, palms up, as though the evidence might still be written there. "Here's the part I never told anyone. I *knew*. Before the train ever came, I knew. The pilings were rotten below the waterline—I saw the soundings, I saw the numbers, and the numbers had been changed. Falsified. By men whose names I knew, because I'd eaten supper with them. And I signed anyway, because signing meant the run stayed open, and the run staying open meant my job stayed open, and I told myself the odds would hold. Twenty-one men, Delia. I bet their lives on the odds, and I collected my wages every Friday."

Delia hadn't moved. The map lay forgotten across her knees. When she finally spoke, her voice was very even, which he had learned was how she carried the heaviest freight.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because I've been running toward escape for eleven years, and escape keeps moving." He looked up at her. "And because those axes back there belong to the same kind of men who changed those numbers. Halloway's crew. Voss. They killed your father to keep a map quiet, same as the survey crew would have killed me to keep a report quiet, if I'd been the sort of man who couldn't be bought cheaper. And I am going to stop running from that fact and start walking toward it. Upstream. To the source. To whatever your father gathered there. And when we come out of these mountains, I'm going to say my name out loud—in front of witnesses, in front of a judge if it comes to that—and let the ledger write me down however it likes."

"That's a terrible plan," she said. "You'll hang."

"Probably not. But it's the first plan I've had in eleven years that doesn't require me to disappear, and I find I've gotten tired of practicing."

* * *

They ate the flour that night, boiled into a paste with river water and the last of the salt pork, and it was terrible, and they ate all of it. Delia counted the coffee beans one final time—three—and split them without discussion, which Mirabel understood to be a sacrament.

In the morning the axes had stopped.

He went out into the blue half-light and listened, and heard instead the crunch of boots, deliberate, unhurried, a quarter mile downcanyon. Lantern light swayed against the rock walls. Voss wasn't hurrying. Voss had done this arithmetic already: no way forward, no way back, two people and a winter. There was no need to spend bullets on a problem that cold would solve for free.

Mirabel stood in the falling snow and felt, instead of fear, something new arrive in his chest—something with weight and edges, like a tool pressed into his hand. He turned and looked up at the rockslide blocking the Narrows, at the tumbled tons of stone, and his mind, the old engineer's mind, went to work unasked: *load paths, leverage, the seam where the big block leans on the little one, the way frozen water pries at everything given time.*

A slide that came down could be brought further down. And a canyon full of Halloway's men, funneled onto a slope of loose rock—

"Delia," he called softly into the overhang. "How do you feel about engineering?"

Her head appeared, snow-dusted, skeptical. "Why?"

"Because we're not getting out of this canyon," Mirabel said, turning back toward the slide with his blood moving warm for the first time in days. "We're going to bring the canyon down on the men who followed us in—and then we're going to walk over the top of it, upstream, to the source. Your father left us testimony, and I intend to deliver it."

Above them, unseen in the gray, the river ran on toward the spring, patient as a promise.
