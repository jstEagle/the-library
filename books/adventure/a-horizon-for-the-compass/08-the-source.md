## 8. The Source

They smelled the water before they saw it.

Not the smell of a river—that green, patient smell of moving water they'd been breathing for two days—but something older and colder, like iron left out in snow. Mirabel stopped on the shelf of granite where the canyon finally opened its fist, and Delia walked into his back because she was reading her father's map upside down and not watching where the world ended.

"Careful," he said.

"I'm careful." She looked up, and whatever else she meant to say went out of her like breath from a struck bell.

The valley above them was a bowl of morning light, rimmed with peaks still wearing last winter like a collar they couldn't afford to lose. And at the center of it—impossibly, absurdly, in the middle of all that dry stone they had climbed through—the spring.

It didn't gush. That was the first thing that undid him. He had expected violence, some roaring upwelling worthy of the maps men killed over. Instead the water simply *arrived*, welling up through a floor of pale gravel in a dozen quiet places, so clear that for a moment the eye refused to believe it was there at all. It gathered itself into a pool the size of a church, then spilled over one lip of stone and became, in the space of twenty yards, the river they had followed upstream through canyon and cold and Voss's closing net.

"That's it," Delia said. Not a question. Her voice had gone strange and small. "That's the whole river. Right there. Like it's been sitting in somebody's cup this entire time."

Mirabel crouched at the edge of the gravel and put his hand in. The cold went through his palm and into his wrist and kept going, all the way to something behind his sternum he had thought frozen years ago. An engineer's arithmetic started running whether he wanted it or not: flow rate, gradient, the towns strung along that water like beads on a wire—Gray Fork with its flooded crossing, Calder Junction with its telegraph office and its one good diner, a dozen smaller places whose names he'd never learned but whose people drank from this every day without thinking about it any more than they thought about their own hearts.

One spring. One throat. And Halloway's hand reaching for it.

"He wouldn't dam the pool itself," Mirabel said, mostly to himself. "Too much silt. You'd choke your own intake inside five years." He stood, wiping his hand on his coat, and pointed downstream to a granite narrows below the outflow—a place where the walls pinched together like a man deciding to keep his mouth shut. "There. That's the dam site. Plug the narrows, raise a reservoir over this whole bowl, and sell the release downstream by the acre-foot." He heard his own voice go flat and professional and hated it a little, because flatness was how he talked when the numbers were too big to feel. "Every town on the river pays Halloway for what falls free out of this ground today."

Delia wasn't listening to the engineering. She was standing very still, looking at the spring with an expression Mirabel recognized from his own mirror—the look of a person doing sums they don't want finished.

"My father walked four hundred miles from here," she said. "Dying. Gut-shot, alone, carrying—" She stopped. Started again. "He could have sold this. To Halloway or anyone. One signature and he dies warm."

"Yes."

"And instead he gave it to a stranger on a dead rail line."

"Yes."

"Why?" The word cracked in the middle. "I've spent eleven years being angry at him for leaving, Mr.—for *Mirabel*. Eleven years I built him out of nothing but the leaving. And now it turns out he was up here building a case against the richest man in three counties, and he never once sent word, never once—" Her jaw set. "I want to know why. I want it more than I want anything he left me."

* * *

They found the answer forty minutes later, and it nearly killed them both.

It was Delia who spotted it—an unnatural regularity in the talus slope on the north side of the bowl, stones stacked with a mason's care rather than a mountain's indifference. A cairn, but too big, too deliberate. They climbed to it together, breath ragged in the thin air, and Mirabel got his fingers under the capstone and lifted, and the whole face of the slope swung inward on leather hinges Eben Crow had oiled within the last year.

A cave. Dry, floored with planks, and crowded full of paper.

Not gold. Of course not gold. Crow's hoard was boxes—cracker tins, ammunition crates, one fine cedar chest that must have cost him a season's wages—and every box was packed tight with documents wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. Mirabel knelt and unwrapped the first bundle with hands that were not quite steady, and the lamplight fell across a survey plat drawn in a draftsman's hand he knew better than his own.

Halloway's company seal. Halloway's letterhead. Dates going back nine years.

"Delia," he said quietly. "Come sit down."

She sat. For the next hour neither of them spoke much. They passed papers between them like survivors passing the last food, and the picture assembled itself piece by piece until it was too large to look away from.

There were internal surveys—three of them, each stamped confidential—showing Halloway's engineers had measured this spring as early as eight years ago and filed the findings under a fictitious section number so no county recorder would ever see them. There was a memorandum in Silas Halloway's own hand, all cold economy, instructing his land agent to acquire, through intermediaries, "every parcel fronting the narrows, quietly, and let the ranchers learn what they've sold when the gates close." There were letters from town councils downstream—Calder Junction among them—begging the company to confirm rumors of a diversion, and Halloway's replies, courteous as a knife laid on clean linen: *The Company has no interest whatsoever in the headwaters of the Gray.*

And there, at the bottom of the cedar chest, wrapped twice in oilcloth and tied with wire, a ledger. Eben Crow's own hand, cramped and relentless, page after page of dated entries: measurements of flow taken season after season; copies of deeds he'd traced through shell companies; names of witnesses—one of whom, Mirabel saw with a lurch, was the same draftsman whose notation he'd recognized on the map, a man named Aldous Wren who had signed Halloway's falsified plats and then, apparently, grown a conscience and fed Crow everything.

The map. Mirabel pulled it from his coat and spread it beside the ledger, and this time he read it not as a route but as a document. Every elevation, every bearing, annotated in Wren's precise hand—and threaded through the engineering figures, in Crow's rougher script, dates and file numbers cross-referencing the evidence in this cave. It wasn't a treasure map. It had never been a treasure map.

"It's an index," Mirabel said. His voice came out rough. "A key. He drew the location of the spring onto a working copy of Halloway's own survey so that anyone who found it would also find—" he tapped the annotations, "—this. Proof that Halloway knew. Proof he lied. It's testimony, Delia. The whole thing. Your father didn't find a fortune. He found a witness stand, and he spent eleven years gathering exhibits."

Delia had gone white around the mouth. She held a letter in both hands—one of Halloway's courteous denials to Calder Junction, dated the year she turned fourteen, the year her father's letters stopped coming.

"He knew," she said. "He knew what Halloway was going to do to my town. To *my* town—he used to carry me past the water tower on his shoulders and tell me the whole valley ran on that river, that we were rich and didn't know it. And he chose this. He chose to be a deadbeat and a ghost so that someday someone could stand in a courtroom and make it stick." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Eleven years I thought he couldn't be bothered to write."

"He wrote," Mirabel said. "He just wrote it here."

She looked at him then, and something passed between them that had no comfortable name in either of their vocabularies—recognition, maybe, of two people who had both mistaken silence for cowardice in the ones they loved, and were only now learning to count the cost of it properly.

Because that was the thing settling over Mirabel like the cold off the spring: silence had a price, and it was never paid by the silent man alone. Eben Crow had paid eleven years of his daughter's grief. Aldous Wren had paid whatever conscience cost a man who falsified plats by daylight. And somewhere south of here, twenty-one families had paid for a signed inspection that Mirabel had known—had *known*, in his wrists, in the singing of the trusses under the test train—was wrong, and signed anyway because Halloway's crew held his references and his name and his whole careful invisible life in their filing cabinets.

He had told himself for seven years that invisibility was survival. Standing in Crow's cave, knee-deep in the paper proof of what silence buys, he understood at last that it was only survival's invoice, deferred. Sooner or later the collector came. Crow had met his on a way-station floor with a stranger's hands pressing his wound. Mirabel's was still out there, riding the water grade, wearing Voss's face.

"We take all of it," Delia said. She was already repacking the oilcloth, movements quick and hard, grief converted directly into purpose the way her father's maps converted terrain into lines. "Downstream. To the federal marshal at Calder Junction, to the newspapers, to every town council Halloway ever lied to. All of it."

"There's a problem with all of it." Mirabel nodded toward the cave mouth, toward the long descent they'd have to make with crates on their backs while a crew of armed men combed the canyon behind them. "Weight is the problem."

"Then we solve it like engineers," Delia said. "Prioritize. What hangs him fastest?"

Mirabel almost smiled. Almost. Because she said *we* now, easily, as if it had always been true, and because the answer to her question was already in his hands: Halloway's own memorandum, the one in his private hand, the one no intermediary could be bribed to swallow.

But before he could say so, Delia went still, her head cocked toward the cave entrance the way she'd cocked it at the telegraph sounder a hundred mornings running.

Below them, faint but unmistakable, drifting up the bowl of the valley on the cold morning air: the ring of iron on stone. Many men. Driving pins.

Voss hadn't followed them to the source.

He'd brought the survey crew. The dam was already being staked.
