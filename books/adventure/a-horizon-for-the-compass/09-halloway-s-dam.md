## 9. Halloway's Dam

The spring came up out of the mountain the way a secret comes up out of a man—suddenly, and all at once, and impossible to push back down.

Mirabel stood at its edge with his hat in his hands, feeling foolish and reverent both. The water rose through a bowl of blue-green stone no wider than a wagon bed, clear as window glass, cold enough to make his teeth ache just looking at it. It pooled, gathered itself, and spilled over a lip of granite to begin the long journey down—the same water, he understood now, that turned wheels in Calder Junction, that filled stock tanks at Gray Fork, that every town in the valley drank and washed in and never once thought about.

"Hello," Delia said softly, to the spring itself. She crouched and touched it and pulled her hand back like she'd touched a stove. "It's *cold*."

"It's the whole valley," Mirabel said. "In one cup."

Around the spring's rim, Eben Crow had built his testimony. A dry-stone cache, mortared with mud and hidden under slabs of bark, held everything: survey notes in a draftsman's precise hand, letters on Halloway Survey Company letterhead, a ledger of payments to county men whose names Mirabel recognized from timetables. Crow had spent years assembling it, alone, in country that would kill a careless man twice before lunch.

"He knew," Delia said. She was holding one of the letters, her jaw tight. "He knew what Halloway meant to do, and he wrote it all down, and he never sent it because—" Her voice caught. "Because he didn't trust the mail. He trusted a stranger on a dead rail line instead."

"I'm flattered," Mirabel said, "and I wish he'd had lower standards."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile since the Narrows, and he treasured it the way you treasure dry matches.

Then Voss's voice came down the canyon like a rockslide with manners.

"Gentlemen and lady! You are trespassing on surveyed land!"

* * *

There were six of them, coming up the trail in single file, rifles carried with the casual insolence of men who'd never been told no by anything smaller than a grizzly. Voss led them, his coat brushed, his boots clean—a man who dressed for the photograph he expected to be taken afterward.

"You're a long way from a telegraph office, Miss Crow," he said pleasantly.

"That's the general idea," Delia said.

Voss looked at the cache, at the papers in her hands, and something flickered behind his eyes—not anger. Arithmetic. "Mr. Halloway is twenty minutes behind me. He'd very much like to talk. I'd strongly suggest you let him."

"We've been strongly suggested at before," Mirabel said. "We survived it."

Voss smiled thinly and gestured, and two of his men moved to flank the spring while the others spread along the rim. Mirabel did what engineers do when cornered: he stopped looking at the guns and started looking at the ground.

The camp was pitched thirty yards below the spring, on a bench above the creek. Tents, crates, a portable theodolite gleaming under an oilcloth, and—his heart gave a small professional lurch—a stack of record boxes stenciled HALLOWAY SURVEY CO., FIELD BOOKS 1–14, sitting in the open air beside a canvas document tent. Fourteen field books. Every falsified reading, every bribed signature, every note about damming the source and letting Gray Fork go dry, all of it in paper and ink, all of it downhill of forty feet of snowmelt-fed creek running through a cut bank.

And the cut bank, Mirabel saw, was gravel. Loose, saturated, poorly compacted gravel, laid down by some hurried crew who'd wanted a level bench more than they'd wanted it to still exist next spring.

He had signed inspections on better banks than that. He had watched better banks than that come down.

*Halloway's own men built it,* he thought. *Halloway's own men will bury Halloway.*

"Hold my hat," he murmured to Delia, and handed it to her.

"What are you doing?"

"Something I'm good at," he said. "God help me."

* * *

Silas Halloway arrived the way weather arrives—slowly, then all at once, and changing everything. He came up the trail in a mackinaw worth more than the handcar Mirabel had ridden for three years, and he took off his gloves finger by finger, and he looked at the spring with the expression of a man appraising a horse he intended to work to death.

"Well," Halloway said. "Eben Crow's little errand boy. And his daughter." He smiled without warmth. "I'll be plain, because I respect plainness. That water is worth four hundred thousand dollars a year to whoever holds the rights. You're standing on my fortune. Name a figure."

"A figure," Mirabel said.

"Any figure."

"Twenty-one," Mirabel said. "That's my figure. Twenty-one men on the Ashcombe trestle, nine years ago. Your survey crew filed load reports saying that span could carry a hundred and forty tons. They knew it couldn't. I signed the inspection anyway, because your company's man told me the reports were sound and my name was on the line either way." His voice didn't shake, which surprised him. "You bought the numbers. I supplied the signature. And twenty-one families got a telegram instead of a husband."

The clearing went very quiet. Even the creek seemed to listen.

Halloway studied him for a long moment. "Do I know you?"

"No," Mirabel said. "But you're about to. My name is Isandro Joubert. Engineer. Third District. I inspected the Ashcombe span eleven days before it fell, and I have spent nine years being nobody so I wouldn't have to say that sentence out loud." He looked around—at Voss, at the riflemen, at Delia, at the spring. "So now I've said it. In front of witnesses. Write it down. I understand your company is fond of records."

Delia made a small sound—not quite a laugh, not quite a sob—and pressed her father's letters to her chest.

Halloway's face did something complicated. "Joubert," he said slowly. "Ashcombe. We settled that. There was an inquiry. It found—"

"It found what your lawyers let it find," Mirabel said. "Yes. I read it. Several hundred times. In several states."

"And now you want money to stay quiet about—what? Old grief?" Halloway spread his hands, magnanimous, a man buying a round for the house. "Name your figure, Joubert. Everyone has one."

"You're right," Mirabel said. "Everyone does. Mine was invisibility, and I paid it for nine years, and it cost more than your four hundred thousand." He glanced uphill, at the cut bank above the camp, and let himself smile—an engineer's smile, the kind that precedes arithmetic. "But I find I'm done paying."

* * *

Later, Delia would swear she saw him check the grade with his eye, estimate the flow rate off the snowpack, and only then start walking—but at the time it looked like madness. He simply strolled past Voss, tipped an imaginary hat, and climbed the cut bank above the camp like a man going to inspect fence posts.

"Stop him," Halloway said, puzzled rather than alarmed, which was the last mistake of his morning.

Voss started up the bank. Mirabel, at the top, knelt, drew his knife, and cut loose the one thing holding the bench together: a seep channel the camp crew had dug to drain their cookwater, routed carelessly along the toe of the gravel slope. Nine years of studying failed earthworks told him exactly what that seep was doing—it was carrying fines, silt grain by grain, hollowing the bank from inside like a moth in a coat. All it needed was volume.

He widened the channel. Then he cut the snow berm above it, where six feet of winter lay packed against the slope, and kicked the plug loose.

The mountain did the rest. Water hit the gravel, the gravel sighed and began to move, and the whole cut bank let go with a sound like the world clearing its throat—a brown, roaring slide that took the path of every lesson Mirabel had ever learned and poured itself directly into the creek, and the creek, delighted, stood up and walked into camp.

It wasn't a flood. It was a *delivery*. The water went through the document tent like a debt collector, took the fourteen field books off their shelf, bloated them, scattered them, inked them into gray ghosts across the gravel. The theodolite went over with a splash of remarkable dignity. Voss's men scrambled for high ground, dropping rifles, dropping pretense, one of them hollering about his kit bag as though the kit bag mattered.

"My records!" Halloway shouted, from his rock. "Those are *company property*—"

"They're evidence," Mirabel called back, waist-deep in his own engineering, hauling Delia up beside him onto the granite lip of the spring. "And now they're *thoroughly examined*."

* * *

While the camp drowned, Delia ran.

She'd found the wire two days earlier, tucked in her father's cache: a spliced line, Eben Crow's own private telegraph, run down the canyon on insulators nailed to pines, tapping into the main trunk where no tariff clerk would ever see it. Her father had been a prospector, but he'd been a railroad man's son first, and he had wired this line himself, years ago, and kept it oiled and dry against exactly this day.

She sat down at the little brass key on its pine shelf, hands shaking, and keyed the message she'd composed in the Narrows when there was nothing to do but compose messages:

**TO FEDERAL LAND OFFICE DENVER — FROM D. CROW, RELAY CALDER JUNCTION. SILAS HALLOWAY, HALLOWAY SURVEY CO., CONSPIRING TO DAM HEADWATERS OF FORK RIVER AND SELL WATER RIGHTS BY FRAUD. POSSESS AFFIDAVITS, LEDGER, CORRESPONDENCE OF EBEN CROW DECEASED. ALSO PRESENT ISANDRO JOUBERT, ENGINEER, ASHCOMBE INQUIRY, SUBMITTING HIMSELF. SEND MARSHALS. ACKNOWLEDGE.**

Her fist was steady on the dash. She thought of her father's hands teaching hers the code when she was nine, *dit-dah, dit-dah*, patient as rain. *You didn't abandon me,* she thought, keying. *You were building this. All these years, you were building this.* The acknowledgment came back in under a minute, crisp and official: **MARSHALS EN ROUTE. HOLD POSITION.**

"Hold position," she said aloud, laughing wetly at the canyon full of drowning paperwork. "Sir, we intend to."

* * *

They stood together on the granite above the spring—Mirabel soaked to the ribs, Delia with the key's brass still warm in her memory, the wrecked camp steaming below them—as the marshals' horses came up the valley trail, and Silas Halloway, mud-spattered and ruined, turned to look at the man who had undone him.

"You," Halloway said. "Who *are* you?"

Mirabel took a breath, and found—to his genuine astonishment—that it came easy.

"Isandro Joubert," he said again, louder this time, for the marshals, for the riflemen, for the canyon, for the twenty-one. "Engineer. And I'll be riding down to Denver with you, gentlemen, because I have an inquiry to answer."

Beside him, Delia slipped her arm through his, and the spring sang on beneath them, unowned, on its way to every town that had ever needed it.

The marshal nearest him squinted, then frowned. "Joubert? The Ashcombe Joubert? Son, there's a warrant with your name on it older than my badge."

"Yes," Mirabel said, and felt lighter than he had in nine years. "I believe I'm here about that."
