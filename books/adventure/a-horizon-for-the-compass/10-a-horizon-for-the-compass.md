## 10. A Horizon for the Compass

The marshal's men came up the valley on the morning train, and Mirabel watched them from the ridge with the peculiar calm of a man who has already decided everything difficult in his life.

There were six of them, federal badges catching the early sun, and they fanned out through Halloway's camp like water finding its level. Silas Halloway stood at the center of it all in a coat too fine for the mud, arguing jurisdiction, then procedure, then the weather, as though the law were a stubborn mule that might be talked around given patience. It was not a stubborn mule. It took him away in irons before noon.

Mirabel did not go down to watch. He had seen enough of men in irons to last several lifetimes, and besides, he had a letter to finish.

It sat on his knee on a flat rock above the spring, written in the careful hand of a man who had spent years avoiding exactly this kind of document. *To the Board of Inquiry, Denver Division,* it began. *My name is Isandro Joubert.* Four words he had not put on paper in eleven years. They looked smaller than he remembered.

*On the fourteenth of October, 18—, I signed the final inspection report for the Cascade Fork trestle. I signed it because I believed the load figures supplied by the Halloway Survey Company. Twenty-one men died when it fell. I have run from that signature ever since. I am done running.*

He read it twice, folded it, and put it inside his coat, over his heart, where a man keeps the things he means to use.

Delia found him there. She came up the trail with her father's oilskin packet under one arm and two tin cups in her hands, and she sat down beside him without asking permission, which by then was her way.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been told I clean up well."

"By whom? The coyotes?" She handed him a cup. The coffee was terrible, boiled from snow and grounds that had been ground twice, but it was hot, and he drank it like a sacrament. "The marshals are asking about you. Voss gave them your description."

"My description," Mirabel said, "is 'a tired man on a handcar.' It has served me poorly all year."

"They also have your name now. Your real one." She said it gently, watching his face. "Delia Crow wired it downstream with everything else. I hope that was mine to give."

He considered it. A year ago—no, a lifetime ago—the thought would have stopped his heart. Now it felt like setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying. "It was yours to give," he said. "I gave it to Halloway first, if you'll recall. Loudly. In front of witnesses. I'm told I was quite dramatic."

"You were," Delia said. "I nearly applauded."

They sat and drank their bad coffee while below them the federal men catalogued Halloway's camp: the survey instruments, the dam schematics, the ledgers in which eleven years of bought silence had been entered in a tidy clerk's hand. Eben Crow's papers lay among them now, testimony wearing an oilskin coat, and every town down the whole length of the river would drink easier for it.

"I'm surrendering tomorrow," Mirabel said. "Riding down with the marshals on the afternoon train."

Delia nodded slowly. "I know. I heard you telling the tall one with the mustache."

"The tall one with the mustache is Marshal Okafor, and he seems like a reasonable man. For a man with a mustache like that."

"He's going to arrest my father's killer, so I'll forgive the mustache." She turned the cup in her hands. "And after the inquiry?"

"After the inquiry," Mirabel said, "I expect they'll either hang me or clear me, and I've made my peace with both. Though I confess I'd prefer clearing. The hanging would interfere with some plans I have."

"What plans?"

He smiled and looked west, where the dead lines ran—hundreds of miles of rusted rail and rotted trestle stitching the mountains together, abandoned by companies that had lost their nerve. He had ridden them like a ghost for years. He knew every mile of them the way other men knew their own kitchens.

"There are lines out there," he said, "that could carry freight again. That *should*. Towns starving for a railhead, timber rotting where it fell, mines flooding for want of a spur. Somebody has to survey them. Somebody has to walk every trestle and say *this one holds, this one doesn't, this one we rebuild.*" He paused. "Somebody who checks the load figures himself. Twice."

Delia looked at him for a long moment. "You want to go back to the railroads."

"I never left them. I just went underground about it." He drained the cup. "There's a difference between running and traveling, Delia. I intend to learn it properly."

* * *

The inquiry lasted nine days.

Mirabel testified in a Denver courtroom that smelled of lamp oil and old varnish, before five men whose faces he studied the way he'd once studied bridge drawings—for stress points, for what would bear weight. He told it all: the falsified load reports, the pressure from Halloway's engineers, his own signature, his own flight. He did not soften it. There was no version of the story in which twenty-one men came back, and he refused to pretend otherwise.

The hardest moment came on the sixth day, when they brought out the original Cascade Fork survey documents—recovered from Halloway's Denver offices—and the clerk read aloud the true load figures, the ones Eben Crow had copied into his field notes years before he ever drew a map. The numbers matched Crow's handwriting exactly. The numbers did not match the reports Mirabel had signed.

"So you were deceived," said the chairman, not unkindly.

"I was deceived," Mirabel agreed. "But I signed. And then I ran. Both of those are mine."

The chairman wrote something down. Later, Delia told him it was probably *honest*, and that honesty counted for more in courtrooms than most men expected.

On the ninth day they cleared him. Negligence, yes—but negligence practiced upon him, not by him; the criminal fault lay with the men who had forged the figures, and those men were already in custody or beyond reach. The board's foreman shook his hand afterward, which felt strange, and said, "We could use men like you, Joubert. Men who admit what they don't know."

"That's the entire job," Mirabel said. "Everything else is arithmetic."

Outside the courthouse, the afternoon light came down gold and ordinary, and Delia was waiting on the steps with her hat slightly crooked and her arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who has been right about something for a long time and intends to enjoy it.

"Well?" she said.

"Well," Mirabel said, "it appears I'm free to travel."

"Free," she repeated, tasting the word. "How does it sit?"

"Lighter than I expected." He looked down the street toward the depot, where the evening train was taking on water, steam rising soft against the cold. "Though I'll tell you a secret. All those years I rode the dead lines, I told myself it was freedom. It wasn't. Freedom you can leave. What I had, I couldn't put down."

"And now?"

"Now I can." He offered her his arm, formal as a dance, and she took it with a snort that he had learned to translate as *affection*. "Which brings me to a business proposition."

* * *

The handcar was older than the one he'd arrived on, and uglier, and he loved it immediately.

They pumped it west out of Calder Junction on a bright morning in late spring, the two of them falling into an easy rhythm—he'd taught her the count on the first day, *one-two-and-lift*, and she'd learned it faster than any crewman he'd ever trained, then informed him that this proved she was the superior Crow. He had not argued. Arguing with Delia was like arguing with a river; you could make all the noise you wanted, but the current went where it intended.

Behind them, the rebuilt section of the Gray Fork line hummed faintly under its new traffic. Ahead, the rails ran silver and empty into the mountains, and at every milepost Delia called out the numbers and he wrote them in a ledger—a real ledger, with his real name on the flyleaf, *Isandro Joubert, Consulting Engineer*, in ink that would dry permanent by nightfall.

At the third trestle of the day he stopped the car and walked it end to end, tapping the timbers, reading the grain of the wood like handwriting. Sound, mostly. One stringer soft at the center, needing replacement before winter. He marked it in red.

"You're smiling," Delia observed from the car.

"I'm working."

"You're smiling *while* working. It's very suspicious. Should I be worried?"

"Deeply," he said. "This is what peace looks like on a man like me. Terribly unsettling."

She laughed, and the sound went off down the canyon and came back changed, the way sounds do in high country, and Mirabel stood on the old trestle in the middle of it and let the wind move through him.

Below, the river ran clear and unhurried toward towns that would never know how close it had come to being sold out from under them. Above, the horizon opened the way horizons do when you finally stop looking over your shoulder—all of it available, none of it owed.

He climbed back onto the car. Delia set the count.

"One-two-and-lift."

They moved. Not fleeing, not hiding, not drifting—just moving, the way a line moves when it's going somewhere worth building to, and the compass in his chest, silent so long, swung easy and settled on due west.

There were four hundred miles of dead line ahead, and for the first time in eleven years, Isandro Joubert was in no hurry to outrun a single one of them.

But at the next water stop, the station agent handed Delia a telegram, and her eyebrows went up as she read it, and she passed it to him without a word:

*HEARD YOU'RE SURVEYING AGAIN. HAVE A CANYON FULL OF TROUBLE AND NO ENGINEER. INTERESTED? — OKAFOR*

Mirabel read it twice, and grinned.

"Well," he said. "It appears the dead lines aren't the only thing coming back to life."
