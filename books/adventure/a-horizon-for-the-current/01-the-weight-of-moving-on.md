## 1. The Weight of Moving On

The mule went lame an hour before dawn, and Zephyrine Pemberton knew before she touched the hoof that it would cost them the high pass.

She crouched in the blue half-light above the tree line, her fingers reading the heat in the animal's near forefoot the way other people read letters. A stone bruise, deep, working toward an abscess. Behind her, muffled in wool and self-satisfaction, her client shifted his weight and asked, in the tone of a man who had never once been told no by the world, whether they could simply continue.

"Not on this mule."

"Then we walk."

"We walk," she agreed, "and we make the Col de Sarras two hours after the patrol changes, instead of two hours before. Which is a different thing entirely."

Lord Edmun Vasse—no, she corrected herself, *Mr. Corbet*, as the forged papers said, though he kept forgetting—drew himself up against the wind. He was fifty, soft in the way of men whose hardships had all been purchased at retail, and he had paid her an obscene amount of money to walk him across the Verlaine passes without his name touching a single ledger. Fleeing creditors, she'd guessed, or a wife, or a scandal with a face too pretty to survive daylight. It didn't matter. What mattered was that he'd paid half in advance and would pay the rest at the coast, and that between here and the coast lay four days of ground she knew better than the empire's own surveyors, because she had mapped half of it herself, illegally, in years when her name was worth less than it was now.

"Two hours," Corbet said. "Is that so terrible?"

"Two hours is the difference between a border patrol that's finishing its tea and one that's decided to be thorough." She rose, shouldered her pack, and began unloading the mule. "We'll leave him with the shepherd at the stone hut below. He'll bring him down to the valley in a week and sell him honestly, which is more than either of us can say."

The wind came off the glaciers with the smell of iron and old snow, combing the coarse grass flat in long silver strokes. Zephyrine stood a moment with her face into it, letting it scour her, the way she did every morning and never admitted to anyone, because it was the closest thing to a prayer she still owned. Then she turned and got to work, because work was the other thing she had instead of a soul's ease.

They climbed.

The Verlaine passes were not the hardest country she'd ever guided—that honor belonged to the northern ranges, before, in the life with the other name in it—but they were the most *watched*. The empire had spent forty years stitching the old mountain kingdoms into its map with customs houses and patrol roads and garrisons that looked, from a distance, like a rash of gray lichen on the stone. Every col had a hut with a stove in it and a man with a list in it, and every list had a column for names and a column for rewards. Zephyrine's name had been on one of those lists for eleven years now. Not the Verlaine list—she was careful about that—but the lists traveled, the way rot travels through timber, quietly and along the grain. Two countries had formally blacklisted her. The paperwork said *gross negligence resulting in death*, which was the empire's way of saying *we needed someone to blame and you were convenient*.

She had stopped arguing with paperwork a long time ago. Paperwork, like weather, was something you navigated around.

"Tell me about the patrols," Corbet said, an hour later, when the sun had finally cleared the eastern ridge and laid a blade of gold across the snowfield they were traversing. He said it lightly, the way rich men ask questions they believe they already know the answers to.

"There's a pair at Sarras who change at midmorning. The older one is honest and slow. The younger one is dishonest and fast, which is worse, so we avoid them both." She set a boot precisely into a crack in the rock and pulled herself up a shelf of granite, then reached back without looking, because she'd learned that clients were like children on a ladder—you caught them before they fell, not after. "Below the col there's a goat track that isn't on any chart. It adds an hour and subtracts a great deal of conversation."

"You've done this often."

"I've done this exactly as often as it pays."

He laughed at that, a short bark, pleased. He was pleased by her, she knew. Wealthy men fleeing their lives always were, at first. They liked the austerity of her, the weathered hands and the flat gray eyes and the way she could read a slope the way a priest reads scripture. They mistook competence for character and solitude for mystery, and for three days, or four, or however long the crossing took, they basked in the company of a woman who asked nothing of them but obedience on the mountain. Then they reached the coast, and paid her, and forgot her, and she went on to the next one, and the loneliness of it was a thing she had packed away so long ago and so deep that she sometimes genuinely believed she'd left it on some summit years back, along with a broken ice axe and a man's glove she had never been able to throw away.

Three days. That was her rule. Never stay anywhere past three days. Never let a landlady learn her face, never let a market woman learn her habits, never let a client's gratitude ripen into curiosity. Three days was long enough to work, to eat, to sleep in a bed that wasn't moving, and short enough that by the time anyone thought to look twice, she was a rumor on a road going the other way.

She had kept the rule for eleven years. She intended to keep it until she died, or until the lists forgot her, whichever came first, and she no longer believed in either.

* * *

They came down off the snowfield as the light went long, and the mountain changed its mood the way it always did in late afternoon, the wind dropping, the rock giving back the day's heat, the shadows pooling blue in the couloirs like water finding its level. Corbet was winded and sweating and had stopped asking questions, which she counted as a professional triumph. Below them, threaded through the scree, the goat track unspooled toward a saddle of dark stone where two cairns stood leaning against the wind like tired sentries.

She stopped them in the lee of a boulder and ate. Bread, hard cheese, a strip of dried fish she'd bought in the last town from a woman who had looked at her exactly one second too long. That look was why they were on the goat track instead of the road. Zephyrine trusted faces the way sailors trusted barometers: not as prophecy, but as warning.

"May I ask you something professional?" Corbet said, around the cheese.

"You may ask."

"The wrecking grounds. On the coast. The Sable Current—is it true what they say, that the tide there can lift a ship's boat off a beach and take it out to sea?"

She chewed, considering him. Clients asked about the coast the way they asked about the passes: as scenery, as story, as something to dine out on when they were safely home again. "It's true. The Sable runs between the Verlaine headlands and the drowned shelf where old Vessany used to be. Forty years ago there was a kingdom out there with harbors and bells and forty thousand people. Now there's a sandbank with the tops of its church towers just under the water, and the current runs over it twice a day like a hand smoothing a bedsheet." She brushed crumbs from her knee. "It takes things. Boats. Nets. Once, a customs cutter that was chasing a smuggler who knew the water better than the cutter's pilot did. The smuggler came home. The cutter didn't."

"And you know that water."

"I've worked it. Salvage, mostly. Nets and lines and whatever the Current decides it's finished with." She said it flatly, without pride, because there was no pride in it. There was no pride in any of it anymore—guiding rich fugitives over passes, dragging the riverbed for other people's losses. There was only the work, and the work was good, and good was enough. It had to be.

Corbet looked out over the drop, at the far blue smudge where the land began its long descent to the sea he couldn't see yet, and something in his face went briefly, genuinely human. "I had a life once too," he said. "Before all this." He gestured at himself, the forged papers, the whole ridiculous enterprise of being Mr. Corbet. "You understand that."

"I understand that everyone on this mountain is running from something," Zephyrine said, "and the mountain doesn't care what."

He was quiet for a while after that. She let him be. Pity was a currency she didn't carry; sympathy, the honest kind, cost too much and bought nothing back.

* * *

They made the goat track's saddle as the sun went down behind the western peaks and the cold came up out of the ground like something rising from a grave. Below, in the last flat light, she could see the Sarras road winding along the valley floor, and on it, small as ants, a patrol: four men, a mule, the dull gleam of a lantern not yet lit. Heading up-valley. Toward the col.

"Off the track," she said quietly. "Now."

They scrambled into a fold of rock above the path and lay flat among the stones while the patrol came on. Zephyrine watched them through a gap between two boulders, her breathing slow, her body doing what eleven years had taught it to do, which was nothing at all. Beside her, Corbet's breath came fast and loud through his nose, and she put one hand flat on his shoulder blade, not gently, and held it there until his breathing slowed to match the pressure of her palm.

The patrol passed below them close enough that she could hear the creak of the mule's harness and one soldier complaining about his boots. Then the sound faded down the valley, and the dusk closed over the road like water closing over a stone.

"They were going the wrong way," Corbet whispered.

"They were going exactly the right way. Someone in the valley sold a description. A woman, tall, gray pack, guiding a man who doesn't look like he's ever carried anything." She rolled onto her back and studied the first stars. "Which means by tomorrow there'll be a second patrol on the high route, and by the day after, someone will be asking the shepherd at the stone hut about his mule."

"Then we go fast."

"We go exactly the way I say, at exactly the speed I say." She stood, brushed off her knees, and looked down at him where he lay in the scree, disheveled and frightened and suddenly much less pleased with her. This was the part of the crossing she disliked, the moment the romance drained out of it and the client saw what he'd actually bought: not adventure, but a woman who knew the exact weight of every risk on the mountain and had already spent them all in her head. "Fast is how people die up here. We move tonight, we sleep in a place I know, and tomorrow we're over the col and down into the next valley before the second patrol finishes its breakfast. And then, Mr. Corbet, you pay me the rest, and I put you on a boat at Salt Harbor, and you never see me again."

He looked at her for a long moment. "You make it sound like nothing," he said. "Like it's all just—weather to you."

"It is weather," she said. "To me."

But walking on in the dark, with the stars wheeling and the cold biting through her wool and the client's ragged breathing behind her, she found the lie in her own mouth and turned it over, the way she turned over stones in a riverbed, looking for what lived underneath. Because it wasn't weather. It had never been weather. Every pass she crossed alone, every border she slipped in the dark, every three-day town she left before anyone learned to say her name—it all weighed something, and she carried the weight, and there was no summit high enough to set it down on.

She had a map in her head of every place she could no longer go. It was a large map. It grew every year. And the loneliest thing about it, the thing she would have said to no one, was that the map was not of countries. It was of faces. Everyone she'd stopped writing to. Everyone she'd stopped being able to greet. A horizon ringed with closed doors, and she walking the rim of it, competent, tireless, and alone as the last light on a snowfield.

* * *

They crossed the Col de Sarras at three in the morning, under a moon three days from full, and the pass was empty and silver and utterly silent, and for one moment—she allowed herself the moment, she always did—Zephyrine stood at the top of her country and simply looked.

North: the ranges she'd been blacklisted out of, the glaciers where the other name was buried. East: the empire, its garrison lights strung along the valleys like a necklace of small dull fires. West and below, past the shoulders of the foothills, the land fell away toward the coast she could not see but could smell, faintly, on the wind—that mineral, living stink of tide flats and salt marsh and the great gray breathing of the Sable Current.

Forty years ago there had been a kingdom out there. Everyone said it like a story for children: Vessany, the drowned nation, the bells under the water. The empire had erased it so thoroughly that its name was illegal on official documents, its survivors scattered and renamed, its harbors marked on no chart. And yet the sea remembered. The sea kept everything, in its slow cold way. Things went down into the Current and the Current turned them over, year after year, decade after decade, and sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—it gave them back.

She'd pulled strange things out of that water. Anchors. Coins. A ship's bell once, green with age, that she'd sold to a dealer in Salt Harbor for enough to eat through a winter. The Current gave things back, and she took them, and that was the whole of her religion.

"Madam Pemberton." Corbet's voice, hushed with cold and awe, behind her. "It's beautiful."

"It's a road," she said. "Come on. We're losing the dark."

They went down into the next valley while the stars were still out, and by midmorning they were below the patrol lines, in green country, with the sea two days off. She counted the hours the way she counted everything now: in threes. Three days to the coast. Three days, and then she'd put him on a boat, take her money, and vanish up the river country until the next client, the next season, the next set of mountains that didn't know her face.

She did not know—could not have known, standing in that green valley with her client wheezing behind her and her rule intact—that the Current was already turning over something in its bed, something heavy and iron-banded, something with a drowned nation's seal stamped on its lid, and that it had decided, in its slow cold way, that after forty years it was finished holding on.

The tide would bring it to her. And three days would not be nearly enough.
