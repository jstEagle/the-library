## 2. What the River Keeps

The client's carriage had been gone an hour before Zephyrine stopped listening for it.

She stood on the shingle below the last house of Saltmere, watching the dust of the Verlaine road settle back into itself, and felt the particular emptiness that always followed a departure—the sudden surplus of silence, the way her own name came back to her like a coat returned from storage. For eleven days she had been *the guide*, a function, a voice that said *mind the scree* and *we camp above the snowline* and *no higher, not with that sky*. Now she was nobody again, which was safer, and lonelier, which was the price of safe.

Baron Hessler had tipped well. He had also, on the second night at the fire, asked her where she'd trained, in the pleasant tone of a man making conversation who was actually taking inventory. She had given him the answer she gave everyone: *here and there, along the coast, wherever the work was.* He had nodded and let it go, but she'd seen the small pause behind his eyes, the flicker of a man sorting through gossip he half-remembered. *Pemberton. Wasn't there a Pemberton in the Aldenmark thing?*

It didn't matter. In three days she would be gone from Saltmere anyway. That was the rule, and the rule had kept her alive through six years of blacklists and border patrols: never sleep twice in the same bed past the third dawn, never let a face learn yours too well, never give any town time to decide what you were. Three days of grace, then the road, or the water, or whatever lay between.

But the tide was right, and the river was up, and she had one day yet before the rule bit down.

* * *

The Sable Current ran brown and muscular that morning, swollen three days out from the mountain rains, carrying whole trees in its grip like a man carrying furniture. Where it met the sea off the Verlaine coast it slowed, spread, and dropped everything it had stolen—silt, timber, the occasional drowned sheep, and once, memorably, a piano. The locals called the stretch below Saltmere the Widow's Purse, because it took from the mountains and paid out to whoever was patient enough to wait on the shore.

Zephyrine was patient the way a knife is patient.

Her salvage net was a thing she'd built herself over two winters: weighted lead line, cork floats, a mouth of knotted hemp wide enough to swallow a rowboat, dragged on a long bridle behind her patched skiff at the exact depth where the Current's brown water met the salt beneath and shed its load. Most days it brought up mud, weed, broken crockery, the sad detritus of the high country's floods. Some days it brought up copper, or a bale of wool gone to rot, or a ship's bell she'd sold in Saltmere for enough to eat for a month. The river kept things. Her job was to be there when it changed its mind.

She worked the flood tide alone, as she worked everything alone, standing in the stern with the bridle lines wrapped twice around her hips, reading the water the way other people read letters. There was a language to it. A boil on the surface meant a snag below. A long smooth lane meant the Current had found its channel and was running honest. And where the brown freshet slid over the colder salt in great slow sheets, the river went quiet and began, gently, to give things back.

The first haul was weeds and a dead gull. The second was a door—just a door, oak and iron-hinged, torn from some farmhouse upstream, and she looked at it a moment too long, thinking of houses, thinking of doors that stayed shut against weather you knew. She shook it off. Sentiment on the water got people drowned; she'd written that in her own hand on the first page of her logbook, six years ago, in a different life.

The third haul was heavy.

Not snag-heavy—she knew the difference in her wrists by now, the difference between something caught and something carried. This was carried weight, rolling slow in the net's belly, turning over with a kind of deliberation. She hauled it up hand over hand, the skiff listing, the Current pulling at them both, and when the net's mouth broke the surface she saw iron.

A chest. Not big—a strongbox, really, the size of a sea chest built for a man with few possessions—but iron, real iron, riveted plates gone furred with forty years of rust and encrusted shell, so heavy with the river's gifts that it took her three tries and most of her strength to swing it over the gunwale. It landed in the bilge with a boom she felt through her boots.

Zephyrine sat down across from it, breathing hard, and looked at it, and did not touch it.

There were rules about this too. Rivers gave up bodies sometimes, and bodies came with grief attached, and grief came with questions, and questions came with magistrates, and magistrates came with names, and names were the one thing she could not afford. Salvage law along the Verlaine coast was a tangle of imperial writ and local custom, and anything stamped with an owner's mark belonged, technically, to somebody—and *somebodies* sent inquiries, and inquiries traveled, and somewhere down that road a clerk would cross-reference her name against a list she was on in two countries.

So the rule was: finders keepers only when nothing marked the finding. Unmarked copper, unmarked bells, unmarked wool. Anything with a crest went back over the side, no matter what it weighed, no matter what it might hold. She had thrown a silver candlestick into ten fathoms once rather than risk the Halloway arms engraved on its base. It had hurt. Rules that don't cost anything aren't rules.

She leaned forward and wiped the crusted shell from the chest's lid with her palm, working at it with her thumbnail, and the river's armor flaked away in gray-green scales, and underneath, cast into the iron itself, raised and clean as the day some foundryman had struck it, was a seal.

A tower, crowned, rising from three waves.

Zephyrine went very still. Even the skiff seemed to stop rocking, though of course it didn't; even the Current seemed to hold its breath, though of course it didn't. Gulls screamed somewhere behind her, indifferent, and their screaming made the silence around the boat louder.

She knew the seal. Everyone on this coast knew the seal, the way everyone knows the shape of a scar on a friend's face—it was on the old boundary stones half-swallowed by dunes, on the lintels of houses whose families had scattered, on the coins that turned up in fishermen's nets and were supposed to be surrendered and never were. Vessany. The drowned nation. Forty years gone, taken by the sea in a single terrible autumn—or so the story went, the official story, the story printed in every imperial schoolbook from here to the capital: *Vessany fell to storm and misfortune, and the Empire received her grieving people with open arms.*

Open arms. Zephyrine had grown up on stories about open arms. She knew what they usually held.

And she knew something else, something the schoolbooks didn't print, because she'd spent years running the coast roads and the coast waters and listening to the people the Empire preferred unheard: that the evacuation fleet had gone down in fair weather. That the survivors' grandchildren still left lamps in windows facing the sea on the anniversary. That the word *scuttled* existed in whispers all along this shore, passed mouth to ear like contraband, and no one ever wrote it down.

The chest sat in her bilge, dripping, wearing a dead nation's face.

*Put it back,* said the part of her that had kept her alive. *Seal means owner. Owner means inquiry. Inquiry means your name on paper, moving through offices, toward lists.* Six years of discipline spoke in a flat, reasonable voice, and it was right, and she knew it was right, and her hands were already reaching for the net to do exactly that—

—and stopped.

Because under the seal, half-hidden by a knot of barnacle, there was a second mark. Smaller. Scratched into the iron after casting, not by any foundry—by a hand, hurriedly, with a blade or a nail. She scraped the shells away with her knife, careful as a surgeon, and the scratch resolved into letters. Old Vessani script, the angular kind she'd seen on boundary stones, but she could read enough of the common characters to make it out:

*IF THE SEA GIVES IT BACK, IT WAS MEANT TO BE GIVEN.*

Zephyrine sat back on her heels.

Someone had put that message on this chest knowing it might sink. Knowing it might sit in the dark for years, decades, forever. Someone had believed—not hoped, *believed*—that the sea was a courier and not a grave, that someday, somehow, the Current would carry their words up out of the deep and into someone's hands. They had addressed it to whoever found it. To anyone. To her.

"That's a dirty trick," she said aloud, to the chest, because there was no one else. Her voice sounded strange after days of professional cheerfulness, rough and private. "Putting words on iron where a sensible woman can't ignore them."

The chest said nothing. Chests are good at that.

She told herself she would think about it overnight. She told herself the rule allowed consideration; the rule was about names and papers, not about thinking. She rowed the skiff in on the slackening tide with the strongbox riding low in the stern, heavier than iron had any right to be, and she did not look back at the river, because looking back is how rivers get hooks into people.

* * *

The boathouse she rented stood on stilts at the marsh edge, a mile downshore from Saltmere, owned by a half-deaf widow named Tamsin who asked no questions because asking questions required hearing answers. Zephyrine paid for three nights in advance, which was already a violation of her own rule and which she declined to examine too closely. The Cormorant, her ketch, rode at the rotten private dock out back, sails furled, ready to run—that was the one comfort. Whatever happened in the next three days, the sea door stayed open.

She got the chest inside by dragging it on a hatch cover, sweating, cursing, grateful for the widow's deafness. Then she sat on an upturned crate in the failing light, ate hard bread and smoked fish, and studied the thing while the marsh birds settled into their evening arguments outside.

Up close, in good light, it was worse. Or better. She hadn't decided.

The iron was naval work—she knew metalwork the way she knew rope, from rigging and repair and years of making do—and this was not merchant plate. The rivets were military-pattern. The seal was struck deep, meant to survive exactly what it had survived. And the lock was not a padlock hasp but a built-in mechanism, a keyhole shaped like nothing she'd seen, a slot with three internal channels. A cipher-lock. She'd heard of such things the way you hear of dragons: imperial diplomatic pouches supposedly used them, and the palace at Carreth, and nowhere else, and certainly not on the strongboxes of nations that no longer existed.

Which meant the chest mattered. Which meant whoever threw it from a sinking ship had known it mattered, had scratched their desperate little message into it in the dark while the water came in, and had trusted the sea with the one cargo they couldn't save.

Zephyrine set down her food. She became aware that her heart was going faster than bread and fish warranted.

Curiosity, in her experience, was the most dangerous animal on the coast. More dangerous than the Current, more dangerous than patrols. Curiosity made guides cross cornices to look at the far side. Curiosity made salvage divers follow a glint past their air. Curiosity had gotten better people than her killed, and she had watched it happen, and she had sworn—hadn't she?—that hers would stay on a short leash, fed on unmarked copper and released daily for exercise.

But the leash was already slipping. She could feel it going through her hands.

She circled the chest once, slowly. Ran her fingers over the scratched message until she had it by heart. Looked at the cipher-lock until her eyes ached. Tried the obvious: a pry bar at the lid seam, applied with care. The iron didn't so much as groan. Whatever was inside had been packed to survive the sea, and it was going to take either the key, the knowledge, or considerable violence to reach it, and violence might destroy what patience was meant to preserve.

Outside, the light went from gold to gray to blue. The marsh went quiet by degrees. Somewhere down the coast, invisible in the dusk, the Sable Current kept running, carrying its endless freight of stolen mountains toward the sea, giving up a piece here and there to whoever waited.

Zephyrine banked her small stove, lay down on her pallet with her boots on, as always, and stared at the rafters.

Here was what she knew, she told herself, arranging facts like stones across a stream. One: the chest was Vessani, and Vessany was legally erased—no owners, no heirs recognized, no claims admissible. Technically, beautifully, the salvage was *unmarked*. Two: the person who scratched that message had expected the chest to surface someday, and had wanted it found, which meant its contents were meant for eyes—not imperial eyes, surely, or it would have gone to an embassy, not overboard. Three: she was leaving Saltmere in two days, per the rule, and a chest weighing as much as a drowned man was a difficult thing to run with.

And four—the fact she kept circling and avoiding, the stone too big to step on—four: if the evacuation fleet had truly gone down in fair weather, then the bottom of the Sable Current wasn't just holding a strongbox. It was holding a graveyard, and possibly a crime, and crimes that old still had teeth. Empires did not forgive witnesses. Empires especially did not forgive evidence.

She should sell it unread. There were collectors—there were always collectors—for relics of drowned Vessany, men in port cities who paid absurd sums for a coin, a spoon, a scrap of flag. An intact sealed strongbox, provenance guaranteed by the seal itself? She could name her price. She could buy a new name, new papers, a life in some free port where no list had ever heard of Pemberton. No prying, no decoding, no danger. Just iron, sold blind, and the sea keeping its secret a while longer.

That was the smart play. That was the play the old Zephyrine—the one from before the avalanche, the one who'd had a partner and a reputation and a future—would have called cautious, and the current Zephyrine called survival.

She lay in the dark and listened to the tide turn in the marsh channels, a long soft exhale of water letting go of the land, and she thought about the hand that had scratched those letters. About how long that hand had believed the sea would deliver. About whether faith like that deserved, simply as a matter of decency, to be answered.

"Damn it," she said quietly, to the rafters, to the rule, to herself.

In the morning, she decided, she would ask Tamsin if the widow's late husband had left behind any tools finer than pry bars. In the morning she would start asking, carefully, obliquely, among the tide-market people who traded in older things than fish, whether anyone still read old Vessani script—because the message on the lid was only the beginning, she was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of weather coming; the chest would want a reader, and readers of dead languages were rare, and rare things drew attention, and attention was the one current she had spent six years learning not to swim in.

But that was morning's problem. Tonight there was only the sound of the river, and the strongbox sitting in the corner like a guest who intended to stay, and the unfamiliar, unwelcome, thoroughly dangerous warmth in her chest that she recognized, finally, and named.

Not greed. Greed she knew; greed was simple and cold and manageable.

This was curiosity overriding caution, and it felt like standing on a cornice in changing snow, knowing the slope, knowing the odds, stepping anyway.

She slept badly, and dreamed of a tower crowned, rising out of three waves, and woke before dawn with the certainty already settled in her bones: the river had not finished giving.

Two days later, nailed to the harbor post at Saltmere where every captain and every smuggler would see it, a printed notice went up in imperial black and gold:

*BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY: AN IMPERIAL SALVAGE FLEET WILL ARRIVE AT THESE WATERS WITHIN SIX TIDES, THERE TO SURVEY AND RECOVER ARTIFACTS OF HISTORICAL INTEREST. ALL PRIVATE SALVAGE OPERATIONS ARE HEREBY SUSPENDED. ALL RECOVERED OBJECTS OF VESSANI ORIGIN MUST BE SURRENDERED FOR INSPECTION.*

Zephyrine read it twice, in the crowd, saying nothing, her face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman who couldn't read much and cared less.

Then she walked back to the boathouse, fast, and started packing.
