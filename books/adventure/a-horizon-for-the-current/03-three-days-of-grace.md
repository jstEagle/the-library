## 3. Three Days of Grace

The boathouse at Graywater Staithe cost her three silver and a lie.

*Salvage diver, working the flood line,* she told the harbor-warden, a stooped man with gull-white eyebrows who looked at her hands rather than her face, which suited her fine. Hands like hers—scarred knuckles, rope-callus in the wrong places for a fishwife, the pale band where a ring had once been—told their own story, and it was not one anyone wanted to hear twice. He took the silver. She took the key to the end slip, the one with the warped door and the roof that leaked in exactly one place, directly over the spot where no sensible person would ever store anything.

Zephyrine Pemberton had been storing things there since before dawn.

She had carried the chest from the Cormorant in three trips, wrapped in oilcloth, hunched under it like a woman bearing a sick child through weather. The Sable Current was still running high with the flood tide, brown-green and muscular, slapping against the pilings below the boathouse floor as if reminding everyone whose water this was. She liked that sound. It meant the river was awake, and an awake river was an honest one. It did not pretend. It took what it took and gave back what it gave, and what it had given her two nights ago was currently sitting on a tarpaulin in the corner, dripping the last of forty years out through its seams.

The chest was iron, banded with bronze gone green as pond weed, and stamped into its lid—she had scrubbed the silt away with a horsehair brush until her wrist ached—was a seal: a tower above three waves, crowned by a star.

Vessany.

She had sat with that knowledge for most of one night, turning it over the way she'd turn a stone on the foreshore, looking for what lived underneath. Vessany was not a country anymore. That was the whole point of Vessany, as far as the empire was concerned—it had been drowned, literally and legally, four decades ago, its name struck from charts and treaties and even from the gravestones of its own dead, replaced by *the Verlaine Protectorate*, as though a nation could be protected straight down into the mud. The evacuation fleet had gone down in a storm off the Sable Current with some ten thousand souls aboard, or so the official account ran. Storms happened. Empires shrugged. The world moved on, because moving on was cheaper than digging.

And here was a piece of the world that hadn't moved on, sitting in a leaking boathouse, waiting for her crowbar.

She should have put it back in the river.

That thought arrived with her morning tea, uninvited, wearing the voice of every careful decision she had ever made. *Put it back. Let the Current keep its secrets. You have a rule, Zeph. Three days, never more, and nothing heavier than you can carry at a run.* The rule had kept her alive through two blacklists and a decade of harbors that closed like fists when her name came up. The rule was the only family she had left, and here she was, about to commit burglary against it.

She drank her tea anyway, and then she went to find her crowbar.

* * *

Iron rusts; bronze does not; and whoever had sealed this chest had known both facts intimately. The lock was a Vessani pattern she didn't recognize—a mechanism, not a padlock, something that wanted a key shaped like a folded bird—and after twenty minutes of coaxing it with picks and profanity, Zephyrine decided that patience was for people with more time than she suspected she had, and set the flat of the crowbar against the hasp instead.

The crack of the breaking seal went through the boathouse like a shot. She froze, listening. Gulls. Water. A cart on the staithe road, unhurried. Nobody came.

Inside, packed in wool that had gone hard and gray as felt, lay paper.

Not coin. Not plate. Not the treasury of a drowned nation, which was what part of her—the part that kept a mental ledger of debts owed to Marguerite Marchetti's landlord, to the chandler at Verlaine Quay, to the whole grinding apparatus of staying fed while being legally nobody—had half hoped for. Paper. Dozens of documents, oil-wrapped in bundles, each bundle tied with cord and stamped with the same tower-and-star seal. The wool had done its work. The ink, when she eased the first sheet free and held it to the light through the leaky roof, was faded but legible, a formal hand with the tall ascenders and clipped descenders of imperial chancery script.

She read it standing up, because sitting down would have meant admitting her knees had opinions.

It was a manifest. *Evacuation Fleet Third Convoy, Vessany Royal Navy, manifest of souls and stores.* Names. Hundreds of names, columns of them, ages and trades and berths assigned. A child of six listed as *passenger, third class, deck accommodation*. A midwife. A bell-founder. Whole families stacked in neat rows like cordwood, each row a household, each household a small universe of birthdays and grudges and recipes that ended forty years ago in the same cold water.

Her eyes found the bottom of the page, where manifests keep their verdicts, and stopped.

There was a second hand on the document. Darker ink, later date, a signature she did not know beneath a title she did: *By order of the Office of the Verlaine Protectorate, convoy clearance is refused. All vessels to be held at anchor pending inspection.*

Held at anchor. In the open roads off the Sable Current. In the season of storms.

She pulled the next document from the bundle. Then the next. She worked through them the way she'd work a salvage line, hand over hand, refusing to look at anything except the next grip, because if she looked up—if she let herself see the whole shape of it—she would have to stand inside that shape, and she wasn't sure the boathouse was big enough.

But the papers kept insisting. A requisition order for chain and shot ballast, drawn by a naval yard, dated nine days before the fleet sailed—and marked *delivered*. A letter, private, from a Protectorate commissioner to someone in the capital, complaining in the peevish tone of a bureaucrat asked to do arithmetic he disliked: *You ask me to reconcile the loss of the convoy with the survey report. I cannot. The storm was real, but the ships were scuttled at their moorings before it broke; the divers' affidavits are enclosed and are, frankly, damning. Burn this.* Someone had not burned it. Someone had bundled it with the rest, sealed the chest, and sent it—or tried to send it—to wherever chests go when nations die. And the Current had caught it, held it forty years in the caverns below the wreck, and given it up to a woman with a net and no country willing to hold her.

The last document in the first bundle was the smallest. A single page, a copy of a copy, the ink nearly ghosted:

*Order to the Third Squadron: the convoy is not to reach neutral waters. See it done quietly. The sea keeps better records than we do.*

Zephyrine set it down very carefully on the tarpaulin, the way you set down something that might go off.

They hadn't drowned. Not the way the world believed—not swept away by weather, not lost to the indifferent arithmetic of wind and wave. They had been *done quietly*. Ten thousand names in a manifest, and somewhere ashore a man had signed a sentence, and other men had opened the seacocks at anchor, and the storm had been allowed to take the credit.

She became aware, distantly, that she was shaking. Not fear, exactly. Something adjacent to fear that had teeth.

* * *

She made herself eat. Bread, hard cheese, an apple going soft, all of it chewed and swallowed without tasting, because the body was a boat too and you maintained it whether or not you liked the voyage. Outside, the light shifted through the day's middle hours, and the tide turned, and the water under the floorboards changed its voice from pushing to pulling. Twice she heard boots on the staithe and stood motionless by the door with the crowbar in her hand until they passed.

In the afternoon she rewrapped everything exactly as she'd found it, bundle by bundle, cord by cord, and memorized the order. This was an old discipline from her guiding years: when the mountain takes your map, you keep the picture of it in your head, because heads don't blow away. If the chest were taken from her tonight, she would still know what it held. She recited the sequence to herself—manifest, refusal, requisition, letter, order—until the words sat behind her eyes like charted rocks.

Then she went out to buy candles and heard the crier.

He was a boy, mostly, standing on the fish-stones with a printed notice and a voice cracking on the long words, reading to a thin crowd of stevedores and wives. *By proclamation of the Admiralty of the Empire, an Imperial Salvage Fleet is dispatched to these waters, arriving within six tides of this notice, to conduct recovery operations upon the site of the historical maritime casualty known as the Vessany Convoy Loss. All private vessels are instructed to give the operation a wide berth. All salvage rights within the designated zone are hereby revoked and reserved to the Crown.*

Six tides.

The crowd murmured the way crowds do about fleets—prices, press-gangs, the price of herring when naval men were buying—and dispersed, and the boy rolled his notice and went to read it again at the far end of the staithe, and Zephyrine stood among the fish-stones with a candle in each hand and felt the ground tilt under her in a way that had nothing to do with tides.

A salvage fleet. Nine ships, if rumor traveled true—dredges, diving bells, marines. Sent now. Forty years after the fact, within weeks—days—of a chest surfacing that proved the empire murdered a nation's exiles and called it weather.

Coincidence was a luxury she'd stopped affording herself around the time of the second blacklist. Somebody knew. Somebody in the Admiralty or above it had learned that the Current had given something up, and had moved with the speed institutions reserve for burying things, and the fleet was coming to finish the job the squadron had started—to dredge the wreck, break the caverns, and take or destroy whatever else the sea had been keeping as evidence.

And the notice said *all private vessels give wide berth*, which in the empire's grammar meant *anyone found near the site will be presumed guilty of something*, and Zephyrine Pemberton was already guilty of several things in two countries and could not afford a third.

She walked back along the quay slowly, letting the plan assemble itself the way she let a route assemble itself on a mountain face—by feel first, then by logic, then by the cold arithmetic of what was actually climbable.

Option one: run. Take the chest, take the Cormorant, disappear up the coast and bury the bundles somewhere only she would ever find. Safe. Also useless. The truth would stay buried, the fleet would dredge the rest, and she would spend the rest of her life as a courier who'd delivered nothing, jumping at every navy hull on the horizon.

Option two: sell. There were buyers for scandal—there always were, in the free ports, among the exile presses and the foreign ministries that enjoyed watching the empire bleed ink. But selling meant showing, and showing meant telling someone where she'd found it, and the moment a buyer knew the caverns produced chests, she was worth more dead than bought. She'd seen how empires treated loose ends. She was, professionally speaking, a loose end.

Option three—

She stopped at the head of the slip, the key warm in her fist, and looked at the water. The Sable Current ran past Graywater Staithe on its way to the wreck site, brown-green and patient, carrying everything toward the sea. Six tides. Twelve, counting both ways. Three days of grace, and grace was not a thing she'd ever been able to afford for long.

Option three was to go to the wreck herself, ahead of the fleet, through the caverns that every chart marked with the cartographer's equivalent of *here be death*—the flooded galleries under the reef that she alone had mapped, over four seasons of illegal diving, with a slate and a length of knotted string, because mapping forbidden places was the only kind of prayer she'd ever trusted. To get there before the dredges. To bring back whatever else the strongroom held. To make the proof so complete, so heavy, that no fleet could sit on it.

And then what? She couldn't read half the cipher-work on the sealed bundles—Vessani official script, the old double-hand, a language the empire had spent forty years unpublishing. She couldn't sell it safely. She couldn't publish it at all; no press in imperial waters would touch it, and she had no name any court would believe. A blacklist cut both ways: it kept her invisible, and it kept her incredible.

She needed someone who could read ashes. Someone who spoke the drowned language. Someone expendable enough to be deniable and stubborn enough to be useful.

She had no idea yet who that would be. But she knew the shape of the problem, and knowing the shape of a problem was halfway to knowing its face.

Back in the boathouse, she lit one of the new candles and set it beside the chest, and by its light she did the thing she'd been avoiding all day: she read the manifest again, properly, name by name, running her finger down the columns of the drowned. *Passenger, third class, deck accommodation.* The child of six had a name. Amalie Voss. Zephyrine said it aloud once, quietly, the way you speak over graves, and then closed the ledger.

"Three days," she told the chest, and the dark water under the floorboards, and the girl named Amalie Voss, and herself. "Then we move."

Outside, the sixth tide began to fall, and somewhere beyond the headland, already, she imagined she could hear the fleet weighing anchor.
