## 4. The Man Who Read Ashes

The archive at Salt Harbor had been a customs house once, and before that a counting hall for the Vessani salt trade, and the empire had changed nothing about it except the name on the lintel and the locks on the doors. Ivo Renner passed beneath the old carved lintel every morning—the stone still bore a wave motif worn smooth by two centuries of hands—and let himself in with the key they gave him because he was useful, and being useful was the only shelter left to a man with his surname.

Forty years since Vessany drowned. Long enough that the children of the survivors spoke imperial Verlaine in the streets and knew their grandparents' tongue only as a lullaby they couldn't translate. Long enough that the word *Vessani* appeared in official documents only inside quotation marks, as one quotes a dead language or a discredited theory. The nation had not merely sunk; it had been *administratively dissolved*, its charters struck, its harbors reassigned, its archives—every scrap of paper that survived the water—confiscated by decree and hauled here, to this gray granite hall above the tide line, to be catalogued by men like Ivo and then, eventually, pulped.

That was the schedule. He had read it himself in the third year of his appointment: *non-strategic materials of dissolved provenance to be reduced after full inventory*. Ashes, all of it. His whole country was waiting in numbered boxes to become ash, and his job was to count it first.

He was good at his job. That was the bitterest part.

Ivo hung his coat on the peg by the stove, lit the lamp on his desk—a real desk, oak, salvaged from some imperial office, too grand for him—and unlocked the day's crate with the small brass key chained to his belt. Crate 4471. He wrote the number in the ledger in his careful, upright hand, then lifted the lid.

Inside: water-stained folios, a child's arithmetic book swollen into a brick, bundles of harbor manifests tied with rotted cord, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth that someone had folded with desperate neatness, a sheaf of letters bound in blue ribbon. Vessani blue. The blue of the old merchant ensign, the blue that used to fly over the Sable Current before the Current belonged to everyone and no one.

He put on his white gloves. He took up his cataloguing pen. And he began, as he did every morning, to steal his country back one page at a time.

Not with his hands. His hands were watched; there were seals to break and forms to sign and a supervisor, Herr Doktor Lasswitz, who drifted through the stacks twice a day smelling of coffee and suspicion. No—Ivo stole with his eyes. He read each document twice: once to catalogue it, once to keep it. He had trained himself until the keeping was nearly effortless, a second sight laid over the first. A manifest of the evacuation fleet, listing tonnage and passenger counts for the ships that went down in the storm the empire said was an act of God—he could hold four hundred names in his head overnight if he read them in cadence, three beats to a breath, the way his mother had taught him to memorize psalms before the psalms were forbidden too. A letter from a harbormaster's wife to her sister, describing the naval vessels seen laying buoys off the reef two days before the fleet sailed. A requisition order, half-burned, for eleven hundred feet of chain and a quantity of iron plate, signed by a name Ivo had made himself forget how to hate: Corvane.

Every evening he walked home along the seawall and recited what he'd taken, silently, into the wind. Every morning some of it had settled into permanent storage, the way silt settles, the way grief settles. His memory was the last free port of Vessany, and it was always open, and nothing in it could be seized.

* * *

At midday, Lasswitz came down the aisle between the shelving with his hands clasped behind his back, which meant he wanted something, and stopped at Ivo's table without quite looking at the documents spread across it.

"Renner. You're from the coast families."

"I'm from Vessany," Ivo said, "insofar as that's anywhere." It was the answer he always gave, flat enough to sound like resignation rather than defiance, and Lasswitz always accepted it, because Lasswitz needed Ivo to be resigned. A man who had accepted the death of his nation was safe. A man who hadn't would have to be watched properly, and watching cost money.

"The fishing villages south of the Verlaine line." Lasswitz picked up a folio, glanced at it, set it down without reading. "You'd know the salvage customs there. What comes up in nets. Whether things get reported."

"Sometimes," Ivo said. "Mostly things get sold. Nets don't have loyalties."

"Hm." Lasswitz turned a page he wasn't reading. "There's been a report out of the Sable Current. A chest. Iron, sealed, bearing—" he paused, and Ivo watched him decide how much contempt the sentence could carry, "—bearing the old seal. The Provisional Authority wants it assessed before the salvage fleet arrives. If it surfaces through unofficial channels first, we need to know what it is and who's holding it."

Ivo kept his pen moving on the ledger, a small notation, nothing. Inside his chest his heart had gone quiet and hard, like a stone dropped into deep water.

"The old seal," he repeated. "From the wreck sites?"

"From wherever it came from. Six tides until the fleet anchors. If some fisherman's pulled up state papers of a dissolved polity, that's a security matter, Renner, not a curiosity. Papers can be dangerous. Words outlive their usefulness." Lasswitz smiled the smile of a man who believed he was being generous with information. "You'll want to be diligent. Anything you find in the collection relating to sealed dispatches, evacuation-era correspondence—flag it for me directly. Not through the queue."

"Of course," Ivo said.

Lasswitz drifted on toward the next aisle, and Ivo sat very still with his gloved hand resting on the blue-ribboned letters, feeling the shape of the morning change around him like weather.

A chest. Iron, sealed, the old seal. There had been chests aboard the evacuation fleet—he knew this the way he knew his own heartbeat, from the manifests he'd memorized and burned nothing of: strongroom inventories, diplomatic pouches, the captain's dispatch case of the *Meridian's Grace*, flagship of the last convoy. The empire had spent forty years pretending those papers went down as innocent ballast. The empire was sending nine ships and a dredge to make certain of it now.

Which meant someone else already suspected what was in the water. Which meant the race had started six tides ago, and Ivo was sitting in a room full of ashes reading other people's mail while the living proof sat in some net-hauler's boathouse waiting to be bought, buried, or burned.

He finished the catalogue entry for crate 4471 in his neat upright hand. Then, because diligence was his armor and had been for forty years, he opened the next folder, and read, and kept.

* * *

The archive closed at dusk. Ivo stayed past closing, as he was permitted to, finishing entries, and when the last clerk had gone and the lamps in the public rooms were doused, he walked the stacks with a hand lamp, pulling folders he had flagged over seven years: everything touching the evacuation convoy, the reef surveys, the naval requisitions of the final winter. He carried them to his desk and read them the way other men pray—not asking anything, just putting himself in the presence of what was lost.

It was in the third folder that he found what he had been circling for years without knowing it. A diver's report, filed eleven years after the sinking, by an imperial survey team assessing the wreck for scrap value. Dry bureaucratic prose, depth readings, structural notes. And one paragraph, initialed but never followed up:

*Strongroom of M.G. located. Door intact. Seal unbroken. Recommend no further attempt; access requires knowledge of internal currents; two divers lost on approach. Site noted as low priority.*

Low priority. The empire had known where the captain's dispatch case lay for twenty-nine years and had left it lying in the dark because going after it meant trusting the currents, and no imperial diver trusted the currents under the Sable Current. But the fishermen did. The net-haulers did. The smugglers and wreckers and river-guides did—the people the empire called flotsam and employed whenever it needed something done in water it didn't understand.

Someone had gone where the divers wouldn't. Someone had brought up the chest.

Ivo sat back in the lamplight and pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw constellations. He thought of his father, who had signed the occupation registries to keep the family fed, and died anyway, unforgiven by everyone including himself. He thought of the psalms in the forbidden tongue. He thought of the schedule in the third-year directive—*to be reduced after full inventory*—and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water closing over the head, that the inventory was nearly complete. The pulping orders had been drafted in spring. His country had perhaps a year left as paper, and then it would exist only in the skull of one middle-aged archivist whom nobody loved and everybody underestimated.

Unless the chest surfaced. Unless the words got out ahead of the ash.

He thought: *six tides*. He thought: *the Sable Current, the net towns, the boathouses*. He thought: whoever holds that chest is holding the last unburned body of Vessany, and does not know it, and will sell it to the highest bidder unless someone reaches them first.

Ivo blew out the lamp. In the dark of the archive, among forty years of confiscated breath, he allowed himself thirty seconds of weeping—silent, efficient, the way he did everything—grief for the letters in the blue ribbon, for the child's arithmetic book, for the two divers lost on the approach, for his father whose grave was in a country that officially had never existed.

Then he wiped his face, locked the folders away, and began, methodically, to plan a theft.

* * *

He went first to the harbor, because the harbor talked. Salt Harbor's tide-market ran all night in the season, lanterns strung between mastheads, and a man with coins in his pocket and a coast accent could buy anything from smoked eel to forged papers to the honest opinion of strangers. Ivo bought mulled wine he didn't drink and listened.

By midnight he had the shape of it. A chest had come up in a salvage net below the Verlaine cliffs, iron, seal intact, pulled by a woman who worked the Current alone—a guide, they said, a mountain woman who'd come down to the water after some trouble in the passes, blacklisted out of two countries, kept a patched ketch called the *Cormorant*. Name of Pemberton. Zephyrine Pemberton. She'd rented a boathouse at Grayhaven and hadn't been seen opening the chest yet, or hadn't been seen letting anyone watch her do it, which in the tide-market amounted to the same virtue.

And there was more, because there was always more. A broker had come through two nights ago asking quiet questions, spending loud money, wanting to know which boathouse, which ketch, which woman. Nobody knew who he fronted for. Everybody knew what that meant: whoever sent him already knew about the chest, and had decided it should change hands, and had not planned to ask permission of the woman who pulled it up.

Ivo stood at the market's edge with the cold coming off the water and did the arithmetic he'd been doing all evening, and the sum frightened him and freed him in equal measure.

If the broker reached her first, the chest would vanish into whatever vaults the empire kept for inconvenient truths, and Vessany would die its third death, and this one would be final. If the salvage fleet reached it, same ending with more ceremony. If Zephyrine Pemberton sold it—and why wouldn't she? a blacklisted woman, a fortune offered sight unseen—then the truth would belong to whichever government paid, and governments did not publish their crimes; they archived them. He knew. He worked in one.

No. There was exactly one path on which the chest became what it was: testimony. Read aloud. Witnessed. Loose in the world beyond repossession. And the only man alive who could decode the captain's cipher—the old diplomatic hand, the one the occupiers had never cracked, the one Ivo had learned at his grandmother's knee along with the psalms—was standing at the edge of a night market holding wine he didn't want.

She wouldn't trust him. She had no reason to; his family name was a curse in the survivor settlements, collaboration written in the registers his father had signed. She was, by every account, a woman who trusted nothing longer than three days.

But she needed the cipher. And he needed the sea, and the wreck, and the chance—maybe the last chance in the history of a drowned nation—to stand where the words were and carry them out.

Ivo tipped the untouched wine into the harbor, where it bloomed briefly red against the black water, and turned toward the tide-market's southern gate, where the boat brokers kept their lists and a passage south to Grayhaven could be bought before dawn.

Six tides. Five and a half now.

Behind him the lanterns swung, the market roared, the Current ran dark and patient toward the reefs where his country lay waiting with its seal unbroken—and somewhere ahead of him, in a rented boathouse, a woman he had never met was about to learn what she was carrying, and what it would cost, and who was already coming to take it from her.
