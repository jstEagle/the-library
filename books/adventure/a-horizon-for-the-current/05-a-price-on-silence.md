## 5. A Price on Silence

The broker came at dusk, which told Zephyrine everything before he opened his mouth.

Men who wanted nothing came in daylight. Men who wanted to buy fish or rope or a woman's labor came whenever it suited them. But men who arrived with the last of the light, when the harbor lamps were being lit and the tide was turning and every honest eye was elsewhere—those men came carrying something they didn't want seen.

She watched him cross the shingle from the window of her rented room above the chandlery, a lean figure in an oilskin coat too good for Saltmere, walking the way men walk when they've been told exactly where to go. Not wandering. Not browsing the stalls of the evening market. Straight as a sounding line toward her door.

Zephyrine set down the cup of tea she hadn't drunk and moved away from the window, into the shadow beside the frame, and did what she had done a thousand times on mountains when the snowpack started to whisper: she counted.

One: he had come alone, or appeared alone.

Two: her name was not written anywhere in Saltmere. She had paid for the boathouse in coin, registered under no name at all, and the harbormaster's clerk had a memory like a sieve for anything that wasn't a fee.

Three: therefore, whoever sent this man had not followed a paper trail. They had followed *her*.

That was the thought that turned her blood from cool water to something slower and heavier. She had been careful. Careful was the whole architecture of her life—the three-day rule, the false ports, the face that gave nothing back. And yet here was a stranger crossing the shingle at dusk with the certainty of a man who already knew the answer to every question he intended to ask.

A knock. Two raps, polite, unhurried.

She let him stand there long enough to feel it, then opened the door on the chain.

He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the soft hands of a man who had never hauled a line in his life and the patient eyes of one who had spent decades watching other people do the hauling. He smiled like a man greeting an old acquaintance.

"Madam Pemberton," he said.

"No such person here."

"Of course." He said it the way a priest says *amen*. "Then I'll speak to whoever is behind this door. I represent parties interested in acquiring certain salvage recovered recently from the Sable Current. Iron chest, Vessani seal, approximately so—" he held his hands apart, a precise eighteen inches, "—with a lock of Vessani naval pattern. My principals offer four hundred crowns. Sight unseen."

The number landed in the room like a dropped anchor. Four hundred crowns was more than Zephyrine earned in two years of guiding rich men over passes they were too proud to admit frightened them. It was a new ketch. It was a decade of freedom.

It was also, she understood instantly, a lie dressed as generosity.

"Sight unseen," she repeated. "You haven't seen it."

"I don't need to see it. My principals know precisely what it is."

*There it is.* The words settled over her like cold fog off a glacier lake. Not *we believe you have it.* Not rumor, not speculation. *Know.*

"You're mistaken about what I have," she said. "I pull up river-iron and sell it by weight. Last week it was a stove door and forty pounds of chain."

"Last week," the broker agreed pleasantly, "you pulled up a stove door and forty pounds of chain. Three nights ago, at the flood tide below the old quarry reach, you brought up something else. You hid it in the boathouse on Rope Lane. You checked the lock twice yesterday and once this morning, and each time you took a different route through the fish market first." He tilted his head. "You are a very careful woman, madam. That's why my principals are prepared to be generous. Careful people understand value."

Every hair on Zephyrine's arms stood up. Not fear—not exactly. It was the feeling she'd learned on the mountain, the day the avalanche she had predicted finally spoke: the moment when all your careful reading of the slope turns out to have been read just as carefully by the thing that's about to kill you.

Someone had watched her. Watched her work the net, watched her haul the chest, watched her carry it through the dark, counted her trips past the fish stalls. Which meant the watcher had been close enough, long enough, and had reported fast enough for this man to arrive within three days.

Which meant the empire—or whatever wore the empire's face in Saltmere—had eyes here. Good ones.

Or someone else entirely.

"Four hundred crowns," she said, "for a stove door."

"For what's inside it." He produced a folded paper from his coat, held it out through the gap in the door. "Half now, half on delivery. No questions, no records, no names. By morning you could be out of Verlaine waters entirely, with enough money never to guide another rich man anywhere."

She didn't take the paper. "And if I say no?"

The smile thinned by a degree. Only a degree—that was the frightening part. A man who showed anger was a man you could read. This one adjusted his cuffs instead.

"Then I'm instructed to tell you that salvage law in these waters is a matter of some interpretation. Unregistered recovery of sealed state property is, technically, theft. The penalty varies." He looked past her shoulder, into the dim room, as if cataloguing its contents. "But my principals would much rather pay than prosecute. Prosecution attracts attention. Attention is bad for everyone—including, I should say, for you. Your name has surfaced once before, madam, in connection with a mountain. Names that surface tend to keep surfacing."

The threat was wrapped so neatly she almost admired it. *We know who you are. We know what you did—or what they say you did. We can unmake the small quiet life you've built out of not existing.*

"Tell your principals," Zephyrine said, "that the stove door isn't for sale."

"A pity." He tucked the paper away without offense, as though she'd declined a second helping at dinner. "I'll be at the Gull and Anchor until tomorrow's noon tide, should you reconsider. After that—" a small shrug, "—my instructions change."

"And what are your instructions after that?"

"That, madam, is the kind of question careful women don't ask." He touched his hat brim. "Good evening."

She closed the door and stood with her forehead against the wood, listening to his footsteps recross the shingle—unhurried still, unhurried as a man with all the time in the world—and the sound of them did more to clarify her situation than any number of threats.

He wasn't afraid of her saying no. He wasn't even particularly invested in buying the chest. He was a message, sent to see how she'd flinch.

*My instructions change.*

She had until noon.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat at the table with a single candle and made herself do the thing she was best at, the thing that had kept her alive on mountains and rivers and in the gray spaces between countries: she stopped feeling and started measuring.

Fact: they knew about the chest within three days. Therefore they had eyes on the Sable Current, close enough to watch a night salvage. Fishermen worked those reaches—but fishermen would have sold the story for drink money in a tavern, loudly, and the whole town would have known by now. This had been quiet. Professional.

Fact: the broker knew her routes, her checks, her habits of three days' standing. So the watcher had been watching since before the chest. Watching *her*, specifically. Which meant the chest might have been an accident of their attention—or the reason for it.

That last thought stopped her candle-steady hand.

*What if the flood tide didn't deliver the chest to me by chance?*

She turned the idea over slowly, the way she'd turn a suspect snowpack core in her hands, looking for the weak layer. Forty years the wreck had lain in the Current. Forty years of storms and tides, and the chest surfaces now, in the same season an imperial salvage fleet is announced in the harbor notices? Coincidence happened on mountains—she'd survived on coincidence often enough. But coincidence this convenient deserved suspicion.

Possibility one: the chest escaped the wreck naturally, and the watchers were simply imperial eyes that happened to catch it. Bad luck, ordinary danger.

Possibility two: someone put the chest where she would find it. Someone who knew her habits, her stretch of river, her hunger. Someone who wanted the documents moving but couldn't move them themselves without dying for it.

Either way, the conclusion was identical, and it arrived with the clean, cold click of a carabiner locking home: *it doesn't matter. Either way, you are now the person holding it, and either way, they know.*

Paranoia, she thought, was just clarity arriving ahead of schedule.

She got out her charts.

Not the harbor chart—the real ones, rolled in oilcloth beneath her bunk, the ones she'd drawn herself over six years of working these waters: soundings no survey ship had bothered with, the shifting bars of the Sable Current, the drowned quarry channels, the reef mazes south of the cape. And beneath those, folded separately, the one chart she had never shown another living soul. Her father had left her half of it and she had completed the other half herself, breath by breath, in the black water under the point where the Current ran through the earth instead of over it.

The caverns. Everyone who sailed the Verlaine coast knew the stories—the sea caves beneath the headland where the current sucked boats down like a drain, where the water breathed in and out with the tide through passages no chart dared mark. Divers called it the death trap. Salvage fleets called it impassable and meant it.

Zephyrine called it the only road.

She spread the chart flat, weighted the corners with the candlestick and two iron shackles she used as ballast weights, and began to think in tides.

Six tides until the fleet arrived. Six tides until every dredge and dive-bell in the empire settled over the wreck site like gulls on a corpse. If she ran inland, overland, they would find her—she'd seen how far their eyes reached tonight. If she tried to outrun them along the coast, their ships were faster and their patience was longer.

But if she went *down*—

Her finger traced the line of deep water from the quarry reach, along the drowned channel, under the headland. Two hours of slack at neap. A boat as light and shallow as hers could do it, if the helmsman was mad enough and precise enough. Both of which she was.

They expected her to run. Running was what careful women did. Nobody—no broker, no admiral, no watcher in the fish market—would believe she'd take a patched ketch *into* the death trap while the fleet was sailing *toward* it.

Unless they knew the caverns too. Unless whoever had placed the chest—if anyone had placed the chest—had chosen her for exactly this.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark, and let herself feel, for exactly one minute, the full weight of what she was considering. Not fear of drowning; she'd made her peace with the water years ago, the way you make peace with any employer. Fear of the other thing: that if she ran into the dark under the headland, she would stop being invisible forever. Whatever was in that chest, whoever had scuttled a fleet full of drowning people, whoever sent brokers with soft hands—they would not forget her name again. Invisibility was her whole fortune. It was the only currency she'd had since the mountain took everything else.

And four hundred crowns was sitting at the Gull and Anchor, waiting to buy it all back for her.

She laughed once, short and without humor, at the arithmetic of it. Take the money, lose the chest, stay a ghost. Refuse the money, keep the chest, become a target. There was a third option, of course—the one the broker hadn't offered because he assumed a salvager would never think of it. Open the chest. Read what was inside. Find out why it was worth four hundred crowns sight unseen, worth a fleet of nine ships, worth forty years at the bottom of the Current.

Careful women didn't ask questions, the broker had said.

Careful women also didn't survive avalanches, blacklists, and two countries' worth of enemies by doing as they were told.

* * *

She went to the boathouse at three in the morning, by the back ways, along the drainage channel where the mud swallowed footsteps, and she stood a long moment in the dark before entering, reading the scene the way she'd read a snow slope.

The lock: untouched, her wax seal unbroken, her thread across the jamb exactly where she'd left it.

The mud outside: gull prints, her own boot marks from yesterday, and nothing else. Whoever had watched her haul the chest up had watched from the water, then. From a boat. At night.

Inside, the chest waited under a tarpaulin, squat and patient as a sleeping animal. She knelt beside it and put her palm flat on the cold iron, on the raised seal of Vessany—a sunburst split by a wave, the crest of a country that existed now only in confiscated ledgers and the memories of scattered old people—and she felt the strangest sensation, as if the metal were warm.

"Who sent me to you?" she asked it quietly. "Or did I just happen to be standing where you fell?"

The chest answered nothing, which was the honest answer.

She should open it. Every instinct said open it, know what you're carrying before you carry it into hell. But opening it here—in a rented boathouse on Rope Lane, in a town where the broker sat drinking at the Gull and Anchor and God knew who else had eyes—was to let the secret out of the iron. Once she read those papers, she would know things, and knowing things changed how a person walked, talked, hesitated. Watchers read hesitation the way she read snowpack.

No. First the boat. First the route. First the escape.

She repacked the tarpaulin, checked the thread on the jamb, and slipped out along the drainage channel as the sky behind the eastern hills went the color of dirty pewter. At the top of the lane she paused once, looking back at the harbor: gray water, gray sky, the masts of fishing boats stirring in the pre-dawn wind, and far out past the point—she squinted—yes, a smudge of sail too large and too square-rigged for any local hull. A patrol cutter, maybe. Or the fleet's advance eyes, arriving early.

Six tides, the notice had said. Perhaps fewer now.

Back in her room she packed in fifteen minutes, the way she always packed: no ceremony, no sentiment. Charts, tools, the wax-sealed tin of coins that constituted her entire fortune, a change of clothes, her father's diving knife. She left the room's rent under the candlestick and the key in the door, because leaving debts behind was how names got remembered.

At the harbor stairs, the Cormorant lay at her mooring, low and dark and ugly and beloved. Zephyrine dropped down into her, stowed the pack, and cast off with the last of the ebb, letting the current take her stern-first out past the breakwater while the town still slept.

As the point slid by, she looked back one final time at Saltmere—at the Gull and Anchor, where a soft-handed man was presumably sleeping off his patience, waiting for a noon tide that would find her gone.

Let him wait. Let them all wait, and wonder, and send their cutters sweeping the coast roads for a woman running overland. By the time they understood she hadn't run *from* the wreck but *toward* it, she'd be under the headland, in the dark water, on the only road in the world that belonged to her alone.

The Current took the Cormorant's bow and swung her south, and Zephyrine Pemberton stood at the tiller with the wind rising and her heart beating slow and hard, clearer than it had been in six years.

She didn't know yet what was in the chest. She didn't know who had watched her, or who had sent the broker, or whether the flood tide had been luck or design.

But she knew one thing with the certainty of a woman who had once stood on a mountainside and watched disaster come exactly as predicted: the slope was moving now, and there was no more choosing whether to be on it.

There was only choosing which way to ride it down.

Ahead, the headland lifted black against the graying east, and beneath it, unseen, the caverns breathed with the turning tide—in, and out, in, and out—like something vast and patient, waiting to see what she would dare.
