## 6. Six Tides

Zephyrine spread the charts across the boathouse floor and weighted the corners with anything heavy she owned: a coil of lead line, the iron pry-bar, two stones from the hearth, and finally the chest itself, because it was the heaviest thing in the room and because there was a certain justice in making the problem hold down its own map.

The lamp burned low. Outside, the Sable Current ran loud against the pilings, swollen with the flood tide, muttering like an old woman who knew something and wasn't going to say it cheaply.

She knelt at the edge of the paper with a pencil stub and began to do arithmetic.

Not the arithmetic of fear. She had done that kind for years—the counting of coins against weeks, of days against suspicion, of exits against entrances—and it had kept her alive but it had never once made her free. This was a different sum. This was the cold kind, the surveyor's kind, the kind you did when you had stopped asking whether you would survive and started asking only what the water would allow.

The harbor notice lay where she'd dropped it, one corner still damp. *By order of the Imperial Office of Recovery: salvage fleet expected Verlaine roads within six tides.* Six tides. Twelve turnings of the water, if you counted them the way sailors did—flood and ebb each a tide, the day divided into breaths. The empire counted differently, of course. The empire counted in paperwork. But the fleet would come on the water, and the water kept her accounts now.

"Six," she said aloud, to hear how small it was.

She found the wreck's position first, though she didn't need the chart for that. Forty years of storms had not moved the *Vessany's Grace* from where she'd settled on the shelf below the Sable Current's outer bar; the fishermen still gave that stretch of water a wide berth and called it the Widow's Floor, and they thought they were being superstitious. They were being accurate. The current ran over that shelf like a whip over a sleeping dog, and the dog was made of drowned stone.

Zephyrine marked it with a small cross. Then she sat back on her heels and looked at the cross for a long time, because marking it made the whole thing real in a way the chest had not yet managed. A chest could be a mistake. A cross on a chart was a decision.

*You could put it back,* said the voice that sounded like three days of good sense. *Drop it in the deep channel off Kestrel Point. Let the Current keep its own secrets.*

But the Current didn't keep secrets. That was the thing people never understood about rivers and tides—they were the worst gossips alive. Everything the water took, the water eventually gave back, usually at the worst possible moment, usually to the wrong person. The chest had come up in her net because the flood tide had decided it should. Somewhere in the empire's ledgers, someone had already written her name next to a question mark, and question marks, left alone, hardened into warrants.

Staying invisible had been her religion for eleven years. Three days in any port, no debts, no names, no patterns a patrol could learn. It had worked because she had never carried anything worth finding.

Now she carried proof that the empire had scuttled its own evacuation fleet—had drowned a nation's last ships in cold blood and called it weather—and every instinct that had kept her alive whispered that this made her the most visible woman on the coast.

So be visible fast, then. Visibility was only fatal when it was slow.

She bent over the chart again and began to plot the fleet.

* * *

The imperial salvage fleets ran out of Corvane Roads, four days' steam south, and Zephyrine had watched enough of them crawl up the Verlaine coast to know their habits. They traveled like a funeral procession because they were one—nine hulls, dredges slung at their davits like coffins waiting to be filled, a tender and a pump-barge and always, always a flagship too proud to sail less than a straight line.

She drew their likely track in pencil: out of Corvane Roads, north with the coastal stream, standing well offshore past the Halden shoals because no imperial captain risked his hull on uncharted ground. Then the long straight leg up the Verlaine roads, and arrival above the Widow's Floor at slack water, because dredging required slack water, and the empire scheduled even its crimes around convenient tides.

Six tides. If the fleet steamed on the flood after next, they'd drop anchors over the wreck before the fourth dawn.

She drew a second line: her own route, if she went by sea, direct, honest, dead. Straight into their teeth. She crossed it out without finishing it.

A third line, then, and here her pencil slowed, because the third line ran through the one place on the coast that existed on no imperial chart, no fisherman's memory, no map sold in any port from Verlaine to the Salt Straits. The caverns.

Everyone who sailed the Sable Current knew the reef had caves under it. The fishermen told stories—swallowed boats, whistling holes, a diver who went down through the Widow's Floor and came up two miles inland, or didn't come up at all. What none of them knew, what no chart recorded, was that the caves connected. That there was a way in through the drowned quarry mouth on the river side, a labyrinth beneath the shelf, and an exit—a bad one, a desperate one—that opened under the outer bar itself, almost beneath the wreck's shadow.

Zephyrine knew it because she had spent two winters mapping it alone, in the years right after the blacklist, when she had nothing but time and a grudge against dry land. She had gone in with lamps and knotted line and a slate, and she had come out each time with more of the labyrinth drawn in chalk on her memory, and she had told no one, because a secret passage is worth exactly as much as the number of people who don't know about it.

One person. That had been the whole value. Her.

Now she traced the route with her fingertip: upriver past the tide-mills, into the quarry cut at high water, through the first cathedral hall where the ceiling came down to within a boat's width of the flood, the squeeze at the Narrows where the Cormorant would lose her mast-step clearance by less than an arm's length, the long black gallery where the current reversed twice with the tide and would eat an engine whole, and out—*if* the exit hadn't silted, *if* the surge hadn't shifted the fallen slab she'd wedged herself past six years ago, *if* the timing held to within half a tide.

And at the end of it, the wreck. And at the end of the wreck, whatever the chest's documents pointed to. And somewhere behind all of it, closing like a hand, nine ships full of dredges and divers and men whose job was to make sure the sea gave up nothing inconvenient.

She sat back and looked at the whole shape of it and felt the old familiar cold settle over her thinking, the cold she trusted more than courage. Courage was for people with options.

The arithmetic went like this:

Run now, alone, with the chest, and sell it far away: survival, maybe, for a year, until whoever had sent the broker traced the sale. And the truth stayed buried under nine shiploads of imperial dredges, and Vessany died a second time, quietly, officially, forever.

Stay and fight the fleet head-on: death, certain, theatrical, useless.

Go through the caverns, reach the wreck first, take what needed taking, and be gone before the dredges bit: possible. Barely. On a margin so thin it was less a plan than a dare.

She got up, fetched the brandy she kept for celebrating other people's good fortune, and drank a finger of it standing, looking at the chart.

"Half a tide," she told the lamp. "I need half a tide of grace."

The lamp said nothing. Lamps were honest that way.

* * *

She worked through the ebb and most of the flood, and the boathouse slowly filled with the debris of decision: spare line flaked into coils, the diving hood and breastplate dragged out of their oilcloth and inspected rivet by rivet, the Cormorant's spare tiller, two lanterns, a slate and soapstone for notes underwater, hard bread and salt pork packed in oilskin. She checked the charges in her salvage kit—black powder, seven sticks, meant for breaking snagged wrecks loose, and she caught herself weighing them in her hand longer than inspection required, and put them down, and did not let herself think about why.

Near midnight she climbed to the boathouse loft and unnailed the loose board where she kept the only possession she'd never pawned: her father's almanac of tides, forty years old, its margins crowded with his handwriting. He had been a river pilot on the Sable before the empire standardized the channels, and he had taught her that the Current was not one current but a parliament of them—deep ones and surface ones, some that ran with the moon and some that ran with their own opinions—and that a pilot who learned to count votes could go anywhere a bigger, better-funded fool could not.

She found the page for the coming week and read his marginal notes by lamplight. *Spring tides, high and ugly. The Current will lie about its slack. Trust the birds, not the tables.*

Trust the birds, not the tables. She smiled despite everything. Her father had been dead fourteen years and was still the only advisor she'd ever fully trusted.

Below her, the chest sat on the charts like a patient animal. She had relocked it. There was no lock anymore, really—the hasp was sprung—but the gesture mattered. Inside it lay the papers she had read three times now, and each reading had peeled away another layer of the comfortable story the coast told itself: that Vessany had drowned in a storm, poor doomed nation, terrible weather that year. The papers said otherwise. The papers said *orders*. The papers said a fleet sent to carry refugees to safety had been sunk with intent, by ships flying the same flag that now, forty years later, sent dredges to make sure no one ever found the receipts.

And the empire was coming to finish the job. Not to salvage—to erase. She understood that now. Dredges didn't recover history; they chewed it.

Which meant the race was not for treasure. It was for the last copy of the truth, and the truth was currently sitting in a rented boathouse under a coil of lead line, and in five tides' time the most powerful salvage operation on the coast would anchor directly above the only other place the truth existed.

She had to get there first. Not to save the wreck—the wreck was beyond saving, had been dying for forty years and would not notice one more indignity. She had to get there first because the strongroom, if the documents were honest—and they had the ring of honesty, that flat bureaucratic honesty worse than any confession—held the master ledger, the signed orders, the name of the man who had given them. Papers could be denied. A signature in a captain's own hand, pulled from his own strongroom, could not.

Take that out from under their noses, and the chest became more than evidence. It became leverage. Leverage bought pardons. Pardons bought a life.

That was the sum she kept arriving at, and she noticed—as she had noticed each time, and each time looked away—that the sum had grown a second column lately, one she hadn't entered herself. Because the papers weren't only hers. Whatever Vessany had been, whoever those drowned refugees had been, there were people somewhere—old ones, scattered ones, children of children—who had lost more than a nation. They'd lost the *accounting of it*. And she knew what it was to have your disaster written up wrong, your blame assigned by other hands, your name entered in a ledger you never agreed to.

She shut that thought in a drawer and sat on it. Sentiment was ballast. Useful in a storm, fatal in a sprint.

* * *

Toward dawn she went out onto the boathouse deck and counted the tide herself, the way her father had taught her, by watching the weed on the pilings and listening to the change in the Current's voice.

Five tides left. Maybe four and a half, if the fleet's captains were eager men, and men promoted past their competence were always eager.

The river ran dark and enormous under a sky going grey at the edges. Somewhere upstream the tide-mills would be turning; somewhere downstream, past the bar, past the Widow's Floor, the water was already getting ready to receive nine ships and all their certainty.

She thought about the broker's face, the smooth offer of a fortune sight unseen, and understood at last what had frightened her most about it—not the money, not the threat underneath it, but the speed. Someone had known about the chest before she'd pried it open. Which meant word traveled faster than she'd assumed, which meant her margin was thinner than the harbor notice admitted, which meant—

Which meant she couldn't do this alone.

The admission arrived the way a capsize arrives: all at once, and obvious in hindsight. She needed someone who could read what was in the strongroom faster than she could carry it out—Vessani script, Vessani ciphers, a dead language locked in a dead bureaucracy. She had the way in. She did not have the key to the meaning. And there was exactly one kind of person on this coast who spent his nights learning forbidden languages from confiscated paper.

She'd seen him once, months ago, at the provincial archive in Verlaine town—a thin man with ink-stained fingers cataloguing seized Vessani documents, moving each page with a tenderness that had struck her even then as either madness or mourning. The clerks called him the ash-reader. She'd forgotten him until tonight. Now his face surfaced in her memory with the clarity of things the mind saves for emergencies.

Finding him meant contact. Contact meant exposure. Exposure meant everything she'd spent eleven years avoiding.

Zephyrine stood at the rail and let the cold arithmetic run its final column.

Invisible and useless, or visible and possibly sufficient. Those were the choices now. The water had already voted.

Above the eastern hills, the sky went the color of old pewter, and the flood tide turned below her, and the Sable Current began, patiently, to count down.

Four and a half tides. She went inside to pack.
