## 7. The Archivist's Bargain

Ivo had learned to read a crowd the way other men read weather, and the tide-market at Saltmere was a squall waiting to happen.

It spread along the harbor's mudflats like something the sea had coughed up: trestle tables on stilts, awnings of patched sailcloth, fishwives shouting prices that changed with every turn of the tide. Everything here was temporary by design. When the water came back in four hours, the market would be gone—stalls struck, coins pocketed, grievances dissolved into the gray churn. Saltmere's people had built their commerce around impermanence because permanence, on this coast, was a thing the empire taxed or burned.

He moved through it with his collar up and his satchel heavy against his hip. In the satchel were forty-one pages he had copied over three years in the coastal archive at Verlaine-Haut, pages he was forbidden by statute to remove, memorize, or mourn. The copying had been the easy part. The mourning was ongoing.

She was here. He'd known it since dawn, when a boy on the breakwater had described a woman with a salvage net who'd pulled an iron chest out of the Sable Current two nights past—a chest stamped with a seal the boy could only describe as "a sun with waves for rays," which was close enough to make Ivo's hands shake as he'd paid him.

The Vessani seal. Forty years drowned, and it had surfaced into the hands of a smuggler-guide with a blacklist in two countries.

He found her at the far end of the market, where the stalls gave way to mud and mooring stakes, negotiating with a boatwright over the price of hull nails. She stood the way he imagined soldiers stood—not tall so much as *settled*, weight distributed, eyes never resting on any one thing longer than a breath. Zephyrine Pemberton. He knew her file better than she would have liked. Smuggled antiquities out of Verlaine passes; salvaged wrecks along the contested coast; blacklisted from the empire after the Corvel Pass avalanche and from the northern kingdoms after an incident involving, according to the report, "an unauthorized extraction of cultural materials." The reports called her a hazard. The reports always called people like her hazards.

What the reports did not say was why a woman like this hadn't already sold the chest to the highest bidder. Two days. Two full days, and no broker in Saltmere had been approached—or if one had, no word of sale had reached the archive. That meant either she didn't know what she had, or she knew exactly what she had and was deciding what it was worth beyond coin.

Ivo preferred the second possibility. He was about to bet his life on it.

* * *

She saw him before he was ready to be seen.

He was still twenty feet off, threading between a rope-seller's cart and a barrel of brined eels, when her gaze flicked over the crowd and stopped on him with the flat finality of a door closing. Not recognition—he was nobody, a gray man in a gray coat—but assessment. He watched her take his measure in the space of a heartbeat: his boots (archive-issue, city-made), his hands (ink-stained, uncalloused), his satchel (heavy, guarded). He watched her decide he was trouble, and watch herself decide to hear him out anyway, because trouble was information and information was currency.

"You're staring," she said when he reached her. Her voice was low, unhurried, pitched under the market noise without effort. "Either buy nails or move along."

"Zephyrine Pemberton." He kept his own voice equally quiet. "Salvager. Guide. Formerly of the Corvel high routes."

Her expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a shutter drawing back half an inch, revealing darkness. "Formerly. That's a generous way to put it."

"I'm Ivo Renard." He let the name sit there. He watched for it—the flicker, the recalculation. It came. Everyone from the old country knew the name Renard; it was stitched through the last generation of Vessani civil service like a thread through cloth, and everyone knew what the thread had been sewn with.

"Renard," she repeated. No inflection at all, which took more control than anger would have. Around them the market roared on, oblivious: eels, nails, rope, herring, the small desperate music of survival. "The archivists' family."

"The archivists' family," he agreed. "My father catalogued the confiscations. My grandfather certified them. And I—" He paused, and found, to his surprise, that the sentence came out steady. "I catalogue them now. The same papers. The same shelves. Forty years later, still dusting the evidence of what was done to us."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you have a chest with the Vessani seal on your deck, and you haven't sold it, and I need to know why."

For a long moment she said nothing. A gull screamed somewhere overhead; the tide muttered against the mudflats, patient, inevitable. Then she turned back to the boatwright, counted out coins with deliberate slowness, and said, "Walk with me. If anyone asks, you're buying my opinion on hull nails."

They walked the market's ragged edge, past stalls selling everything the sea gave up and everything the land couldn't keep. She didn't look at him. He understood that too—it was easier to talk sideways, to people whose faces you weren't wearing.

"Two nights ago I pulled a chest out of the Sable Current," she said. "Iron, lead-lined, sealed. I opened it in a rented boathouse with a pry bar and a prayer, and inside were documents in a cipher I can't read, on paper older than I am, stamped with a nation that officially never existed. The harbor notice says an imperial salvage fleet arrives in six tides. So before you ask me anything else, ask yourself what you think I am, Renard, and whether the answer changes what you're willing to say next."

"What do you think you are?"

That got the first real look she'd given him—quick, sharp, almost amused. "A woman deciding between running and drowning. Same as everyone on this coast."

"No," Ivo said. "You're not running. If you were running, you'd have burned the documents and vanished north. You've had two days. Instead you're buying hull nails and talking to strangers at a tide-market. You're not running, Pemberton. You're *deciding*. And I'm here to give you one more thing to decide with."

* * *

He led her to the seawall at the market's end, where the mud dropped away into deeper water and the noise fell behind them. There he opened his satchel and took out a single page—not one of the forbidden copies, but a facsimile he'd made himself, laboriously, in the archive's margins during slow afternoons. On it, in careful ink, was a fragment of Vessani administrative cipher: the routing code used by the Ministry of Marine in the last year of the war.

She studied it without touching it. Good instincts. Paper carried fingerprints, and fingerprints carried hangings.

"This is gibberish to me," she said.

"It's not gibberish. It's a shipping manifest header. Dated eleven days before the evacuation fleet sailed from Vessany City." He watched her face as the arithmetic landed. "Your documents are written in this cipher, or one of its descendants. Without a reader, they're scrap iron wrapped around expensive paper. With a reader—"

"They're proof."

"They're whatever they are. I don't know yet. Neither do you. That's the point." He folded the page away. "Here is my offer. Take me to the wreck. Give me time with those documents. I will read them—all of them—and I will tell you the truth of what's in them, even if the truth costs us both our necks. In exchange, you get a decoder you don't have to pay, and I get—"

"What? Redemption? Revenge?"

"A chance to do my job properly," he said. "Forty years ago, my family's job was to preserve Vessany's records. Instead we preserved the empire's version of them. Every document I've catalogued has passed through imperial hands first, trimmed and translated until the truth bled out of it. This chest came up out of the water untouched. Untrimmed. For the first time in my life, I could hold the original." His voice caught, just slightly, and he hated it, and let it happen anyway. "You asked what I think I am. I'm a man reading ashes, Pemberton. I'd like to read something living for once."

She looked out at the water. The tide was turning; he could see it in the way the mooring buoys swung, all together, like a congregation shifting in its pews.

"The fleet arrives in six tides," she said. "Nine ships, dredges, divers, and whatever admiral they've given command of this particular erasure. Anyone approaching that wreck gets seen, boarded, and disappeared—you know how the empire handles witnesses on the contested coast. And you want me to sail you straight into its teeth in a patched ketch because you're homesick for paperwork."

"Yes."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll decode nothing, sell nothing, prove nothing, and spend the rest of your life wondering what you pulled out of the Current." He shrugged. "Or you'll find another reader. There are perhaps six people alive who can work Vessani marine cipher fluently. Three are dead. One is in an imperial prison. One is me. The sixth is ninety years old and lives in the cliff colonies, and good luck finding her without getting every survivor village raided in the process."

"You've thought about this."

"Every day for three years."

* * *

She walked to the edge of the seawall and crouched there, forearms on her knees, watching the water come back over the flats. He waited. He had learned patience in archives, among things that took centuries to explain themselves, and he could wait out a tide if he had to.

"You should know what you're signing onto," she said finally, without turning. "I don't trust you. Your family name bought cooperation from the occupation for a generation—there are people alive today who remember what that cost. If this goes wrong, if you're a plant, a watcher, a leash the empire put on its own loose end, I will put you over the side and I won't lose sleep. Are those terms acceptable?"

"Perfectly."

"And you should know the rest. Whatever's in that chest, I found it. Fair salvage, open water, my net, my risk. I'm not handing it to you, or to your ghost-nation, or to anyone else, sight unseen. Whatever we do with it, I decide. You read. I steer. Those are the terms."

"Agreed."

"One more thing." She stood and faced him fully for the first time, and up close her eyes were the color of deep water over rock—green going gray going dark, impossible to sound. "Why you? Really. Not the speech about originals and truth. Why does a man with a safe desk in a safe archive throw it over for a suicide run with a blacklisted smuggler?"

Because his father had died at a catalogue desk with ink on his fingers and nothing true ever written beneath them. Because his mother had hummed Vessani lullabies in a whisper, thirty years after the singing stopped being legal. Because last winter he had catalogued a box of children's schoolbooks confiscated from a survivor family, and found, penciled inside one cover in a child's hand, the words *remember the harbor*, and had sat in the archive stacks until midnight unable to breathe.

But he said none of that. He said, "Because someone has to be able to look at what's down there and know what it means. And right now, on this entire coast, that's only me."

Something shifted in her face—not softening, exactly. Recalculation. The look of a gambler who has counted the deck and found one card worth the risk.

"All right, Renard," she said. "Passage to the wreck, in exchange for your eyes. We leave at slack water tonight, ahead of the fleet, ahead of whoever else comes asking about that chest—because someone will. My ketch is the *Cormorant*, third row of the outer anchorage, blue tarp over the cockpit. Be aboard within the hour, bring nothing you can't swim with, and tell no one. Not your archive, not your friends, not your ghosts."

"I understand."

"And Ivo." She was already walking away, back toward the market noise, and she spoke over her shoulder without slowing. "If those documents say what I think they might say—that the empire murdered a nation and called it weather—then there'll be no middle ground left for either of us. No selling out, no pardons, no going back to desks. Make sure you can live with that before you step aboard."

He watched her go, a straight-backed figure dissolving into the temporary city of the tide-market, and felt the strange lightness that comes after a decision that cannot be unmade.

Six tides. One chance. He turned toward the outer anchorage and began, quickly, to walk.

Behind him, unnoticed by either of them, a man in a broker's gray coat closed the ledger he had been pretending to read, and followed.
