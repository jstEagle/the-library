## 8. Departure at Slack Water

The tide turned an hour before dawn, and Zephyrine was already awake to watch it go.

She stood in the doorway of the boathouse with a mug of chicory going cold in her hand and counted the lights across Saltmere Harbor. Fishing boats first—those were nothing, their lanterns swinging at anchor like patient dogs. Then the harbor office lamp, still burning, which meant the night clerk was still awake, which meant someone had paid him to be. And out past the breakwater, where no honest vessel would loiter at slack water, a dark shape without lights riding too high in the water for its beam.

A sloop. Fast. The kind of fast that cost money, and money was the one thing the broker had never pretended to lack.

"Zephyrine." Ivo's voice came low from inside, from where he'd spent the night cross-legged among her charts with the chest's documents spread around him like a man kneeling at an altar he wasn't sure would forgive him. "I heard oars. Twenty minutes ago. Two boats, hugging the pilings on the north side."

"I heard them too." She drained the chicory and set the mug down with the care of a woman who could not afford to break anything she might need later. "They're waiting for light. Nobody boards a strange ketch in the dark—they want to see what they're taking before they take it."

"That's a comfort."

"It's not a comfort. It's a schedule." She turned back into the boathouse, where the Cormorant lay in her slings like a patient mid-molt, patched hull gleaming with fresh pitch, sails bent on but furled tight. Three days of work compressed into two nights, and even now the starboard garboard seam wept a thin amber bead of pitch that would need watching once they hit open water. "We leave now, in the dark, on the last of the ebb, or we don't leave at all."

Ivo rose stiffly, knees cracking like green kindling. He was forty years her senior if he was a day, though there was something in his face that made age hard to reckon—a smoothness, almost, as if grief had planed him down to the grain. He gathered the documents with hands that trembled only when he thought she wasn't looking.

"You said slack water," he said. "This isn't slack. The ebb is still running."

"The ebb is still running *here*." She pointed at the harbor mouth, where the black water between the moles showed the first gray teeth of current. "Out there it turns early—the Sable doesn't wait for the almanac. We have perhaps forty minutes of quiet water at the mouth, and then the flood comes up from the south like a debt collector. If we clear the mole before it turns, it carries us. If we miss it—" she shrugged, "—we row. Against six men in a boat built for eight oars."

"And your boat is built for how many?"

"One," she said. "Occasionally one and a half, if the half doesn't touch anything."

He looked at her for a long moment in the lamplight, this archivist of a drowned country, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something in his own. "Then let us not touch anything," he said.

* * *

Getting the Cormorant out of her slings was a quarter hour of held breath and whispered profanity. Zephyrine worked the windlass herself—Ivo's hands were scholar's hands, clever with paper, useless with rope—and eased the keel down the ways until the black water took her weight with a sound like a long exhale. The ketch sat low and fat in the ebb, patched and scarred, her name painted over twice by previous owners until the letters had gone soft as old bruises. Zephyrine had repainted it anyway. A boat should know what she was called.

Ivo handed the document case down and came over the rail after it with all the grace of a falling wardrobe. The Cormorant rolled, complained, and steadied.

"If you're sick," Zephyrine said, pushing off from the ways with a boathook, "do it to leeward."

"I have been to sea before."

"On imperial ferries, I'd wager, eating custard."

A pause. "There was a custard," he admitted.

She shipped the boathook and took the tiller. The ebb did most of the work; she only suggested. The boathouse slid away behind them, dark and rented under a name that wasn't hers, another three days used up and left behind like a shed skin. That was the rule. Never stay anywhere past three days. She had kept it for eleven years, through nine ports and two countries that wanted her name for different reasons, and she felt its familiar comfort close around her now like an old coat—even as some stubborn part of her noted that the rule had never before required her to carry a nation's evidence out of a harbor with hired knives at her back.

The moles rose up on either side, wet stone breathing. Between them the water ran smooth as poured lead, the last stillness before the turn. Zephyrine steered for the gap with the tiller against her hip and her eyes doing the work her mouth didn't: the north molehead, where a shape crouched that might have been a bollard and might not; the fishing fleet at anchor, whose watchmen sold information to whoever paid in small coin; the harbor office lamp, still burning, still burning.

"They've seen us," Ivo murmured. He had gone very still amidships, one hand flat on the document case as if it were a sleeping animal.

"No."

"There's a boat putting out from the north side. I can hear—"

"I said no." Her voice stayed level, which surprised her. "They put out because they think we'll panic and run for the south channel, and the south channel has a bar across it at low water that would open our bottom like a letter. They want us in the shallows where they can wade out and take us." She watched the gap widen ahead, the gray line of the sea beyond it just beginning to show. "So we won't give them shallows. We'll give them the tide."

The boat behind them was pulling hard now, oars muffled, which told her everything—honest watermen didn't muffle their oars at dawn in their own harbor. Four strokes to her two, gaining, maybe two hundred meters back and closing. Ahead, the sea. Behind, the men. And beneath the keel, invisible, the Sable Current gathering itself for the turn like a fist slowly closing.

"Zephyrine." Ivo's voice had gone thin. "They're going to reach us before the mouth."

"Probably."

"*Probably?*"

"You wanted certainty, you should have stayed in the archive." She felt rather than heard the change come: a shiver along the hull, a soft dragging at the rudder, the water's whole temper shifting. The flood was early. Of course it was. The Sable never waited for anyone. "Sit down. Hold the case. Whatever happens, hold the case."

"What happens?"

She didn't answer, because the answer arrived. The tide met them at the harbor mouth like a hand meeting a shoulder—not violent, not yet, but absolute, swinging the Cormorant's head seaward with a firmness that made the tiller hum in her grip. The ketch surged. The patched seam forward of the mast gave a single warning groan and held. And the pursuing boat, caught mid-stroke in the same turning water, slewed sideways, its crew hauling on oars that suddenly pulled against a river running the wrong way.

Zephyrine raised the mainsail as they shot the gap—no time for ceremony, the canvas went up ugly and half-furled and caught the offshore dawn wind with a crack like a whip—and the Cormorant leaned, gathered, and ran.

Behind them, tiny with distance, a voice shouted something furious across the water. It might have been her name. It might have been a price. Out here it was all the same language.

* * *

An hour out, with Saltmere reduced to a smudge and the Sable Current running deep blue-green beside them, Zephyrine finally let herself breathe. She set the self-tending sheet, wedged the tiller, and dug bread and dried fish from the locker. Ivo sat amidships looking like a man who had been shown the machinery of his own survival and found it terrifyingly simple.

"You knew," he said. "About the tide. You knew it would turn early and you knew what it would do to them."

"I knew the water. The rest was arithmetic."

"That is not what most people mean by arithmetic."

"Most people haven't been thrown off enough water to learn." She tore the loaf and handed him half. He looked at it a moment too long before taking it, and she understood—he was rationing, out of habit, the way people do who learned hunger young or learned it late and thoroughly. "Eat it. There's more. There's always more on my boat; I got blacklisted, not poor."

"Not poor," he repeated, and something flickered across his face that was almost a smile. "No. You are something else entirely."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

He considered the question honestly, which she hadn't expected. Most people answered quickly or not at all. "You move through the world," he said slowly, "as though you expect it to take things from you, and you have arranged your entire life so that it never gets the chance. The three-day rule. The rented rooms under false names. The boat with two names painted out." He nodded at the rail, where the ghost-letters of the Cormorant's previous lives still showed through the paint. "You have made yourself very difficult to lose."

"Difficult to keep, too."

"Yes," he said quietly. "That occurred to me."

The wind filled and shifted and she trimmed the sheet without thinking, and for a while neither of them spoke. The coast unrolled to starboard—gray cliffs, white farms, the occasional thread of smoke—and beyond it, somewhere inland, the empire went about its morning, filing papers, paying clerks, forgetting.

"My father was a harbor pilot," Ivo said at last, seemingly to the water. "In Vessany City. Before."

Zephyrine kept her eyes on the sails. She knew what that *before* weighed. Everyone on this coast knew what it weighed, even the ones who pretended the sea had simply swallowed a country the way it swallows ships—by accident, impersonally, without anyone's signature on it.

"He taught me the tides here," Ivo went on. "Not these tides—the Vessani ones, the eastern channels. He used to say a pilot's knowledge dies with him unless he gives it away, so he gave it to me whether I wanted it or not. Every sandbar, every eddy, every place the current bites. I hated the lessons. I wanted to read. I wanted to sit in the archive with the dry paper and the dust." His hand tightened on the document case. "And then the empire closed the archives to Vessani readers, and the channels filled with imperial dredges, and I understood that my father had been trying to put something in my hands that couldn't be confiscated. And I had spent my childhood resenting it."

"What happened to him?"

"He drowned." Flat, immediate, the way grief goes when it has been carried so long it has worn smooth. "Not in the Scouring. After. He went out in a storm to guide an evacuation boat he wasn't assigned to, because the assigned pilots refused—they'd been ordered to stand down, you understand, ordered, weeks before the storm ever came, though no one understood why until much later. So he went out himself." Ivo looked at her directly for the first time since the harbor. "The order to stand down is in this case, Zephyrine. Page fourteen. I checked it four times last night. My father died obeying the instinct those pages prove was betrayed."

The Cormorant ran on. Somewhere below them, far down in the cold, the wreck of the evacuation fleet lay in its caverns with its dead, and Zephyrine felt the whole weight of it shift in her chest—from salvage to something else, something with a pulse.

"Why weren't you on those boats?" she asked. It was blunt and she knew it, but she had learned that exiles respected bluntness; courtesy was for people with futures.

Ivo didn't flinch. "Because I was nineteen and I had done something unforgivable. I had taken a position cataloguing confiscated Vessani records for the imperial administration. Paying work. Warm rooms. Access to books I'd been forbidden." His voice didn't shake, but it went very careful, the way a man walks on ice. "My neighbors spat at me. My mother wouldn't speak my name for a year. And when the evacuation came, when the lists were drawn for who would sail, my name was struck from every list. Collaborators' families sailed last, they said. Only there was no last. The fleet sailed full of the treasury and the favored, and the rest of us were told to wait for the second crossing that never came." He turned the case over in his hands. "So yes. I survived because I cooperated. Forty years I have told myself I survived to remember. Some nights I believe it."

Zephyrine found, to her surprise, that she had no contempt for him. She had expected some—she carried a ready supply, honed on clients and officials and the particular breed of coastal man who explained boats to her. But sitting across the heeling deck from this dry, precise, ruined man, she felt instead a grim kinship, unwelcome as a draft.

"I predicted an avalanche once," she said.

He looked up.

"High Verlaine, seven years ago. I was guiding a patron—a rich one, the kind who buys mountains the way other men buy hats. I read the snowpack and told him we waited a day. He'd paid for a summit on a schedule, so he fired me and hired the weather he wanted." She heard her own voice go flat and distant, reporting rather than remembering. "Three of his party came down with the slide. One of them was my friend. And afterward, when the inquiry came, the patron's family bought the testimony and mine was the name on the report, because I was the one who'd argued, and arguing looks like instability, and instability looks like guilt. I could have named the man who pressured me. He was young. First season. He'd have been finished forever, and he had a mother and no trade but the mountain." She shrugged. "So I took it. Blacklisted from Verlaine, then from the empire's passes entirely, and I came down to the water where nobody asks a salvage-woman for references."

Ivo was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—warmer by one degree, which from him was a heat wave. "You protected him."

"I protected a fool. There's a difference, and I've had seven years to stop pretending otherwise."

"No," he said. "You protected a person from becoming what I became. Ruined by a choice made under pressure. You paid so he wouldn't have to." He shook his head slowly. "That is either the noblest thing I have ever heard or the stupidest."

"Both," Zephyrine said. "Usually both."

And then, because the moment had grown heavier than a deck needed, she jerked her chin at the document case. "Page fourteen. The stand-down order. How long to decode the rest?"

His expression sharpened, the archivist surfacing through the mourner like a fish rising. "The cipher is Vessani naval, third revision—it changed twice during the war, and the second revision is keyed to a phrase. I know the phrase. What I don't know yet is whether the key holds for the final documents, the ones sealed with the captain's personal mark." He hesitated. "Those may require something from the wreck itself. From the strongroom."

"Then we'll hope the first thirteen pages are worth the trip."

"They are worth ten trips." He said it with such sudden force that the gulls scattered off the water nearby. "Every page is a name, Zephyrine. Names of people the empire drowned and then erased. As long as the cipher holds them, they are only lost. Decoded, they are *murdered*, and murder can be answered."

She studied him—this man who read ashes, who had crossed a war's worth of silence to sit on her filthy patched ketch and argue with the sea—and felt the reluctant respect settle into place like ballast. She didn't trust him. Trust was a cargo she'd stopped carrying years ago. But she believed him, and belief would do for now.

"All right, archivist," she said, and put her hand back on the tiller. "You read. I steer. And when we make the anchorage at Greywater Point by nightfall, you're going to tell me exactly what we need from that strongroom—because I've seen the charts of those caverns, and I'm the only person alive who knows them, and I already don't like what I remember."

Ivo opened the case. The wind freshened from the southeast, fair for the run down-coast, and the Cormorant leaned into it like a dog finally given work.

But far astern, at the edge of sight where the sea met the morning haze, a sail had appeared—white, tall, moving faster than any fishing boat had a right to move—and it was holding their wake.
