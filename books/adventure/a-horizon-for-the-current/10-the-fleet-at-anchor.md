## 10. The Fleet at Anchor

They came over the headland in the grey hour before dawn, when the Sable Current ran the color of tarnished pewter and the wind had not yet decided which way to blow. Zephyrine went first, as she always did on broken ground, testing each stone with the toe of her boot before committing her weight, and Ivo followed a half-step behind, learning her rhythm the way he seemed to learn everything—quickly, silently, and with an attention that made her feel more observed than she liked.

"Down here," she said, and dropped into the hollow beneath a shelf of granite furred with salt-crusted lichen. From there the whole sweep of Wrenwater Bay opened below them: the long crescent of shingle, the black teeth of the reef offshore, and beyond the reef, where the water deepened from green to slate to a blue so dark it looked like a hole cut in the world—the wreck site.

She had known they would be there. She had counted the tides herself, done the arithmetic in her head a dozen times since the tide-market, and every sum came out the same. But knowing a thing and seeing it were different animals entirely, and when she crested the lip of the hollow and looked down at what lay anchored in the bay, her breath caught in her throat despite herself.

Nine ships.

Not fishing boats, not coastal patrol cutters, not any of the scruffy armed traffic she'd spent her life dodging along this coast. These were imperial hulls—sleek, black-below-the-waterline, flying the double-tailed pennant of the Salvage Office from every masthead. Three of them were dredge-ships, their great gantry arms folded against the morning like sleeping insects, chains hanging from their jaws into the sea in slow, rust-red curves. Two were diver tenders, ringed with moored pontoons, air hoses snaking down into the water in pale braids. The rest were escorts, gunports closed but unmistakably gunports, riding at anchor in a formation so precise it looked drawn with a rule.

Above the wreck site. Directly above it.

"They found it fast," Ivo said quietly. He had gone very still beside her, the way he did when something mattered, his hands flat on the stone as if steadying himself against the sight. "Four days. I thought we'd have six."

"The harbor notice said six tides for the fleet," Zephyrine said. "It didn't say six tides before the survey ships arrived. Those came ahead of the fleet. Someone wanted the ground marked before anyone else could ask questions about why we're salvaging a nation that legally never existed."

Ivo didn't answer. His eyes were moving across the anchorage with the same methodical care he gave to confiscated documents, cataloguing, counting, and she realized he was reading the fleet the way she read water—for what it concealed rather than what it showed.

"Dredges forward," he murmured. "Tenders aft. The escorts are positioned in a screen facing seaward." He paused. "They're not afraid of us."

"No one's afraid of us," Zephyrine said. "That's the problem."

* * *

She made herself look longer than she wanted to. That was discipline, the same discipline that had kept her alive through white water and border patrols and three-day rooms in towns where her face was worth money. Fear made people look away; looking away got them killed. So she looked.

The light was strengthening now, coming up gold behind the eastern headland, and it caught the dredge-chains as they began, slowly, to move. Even at this distance she could hear it—a sound felt more than heard, a deep industrial groaning rolling across the water, iron on stone, the sound of a reef being eaten. The nearest dredge swung its gantry arm in a slow arc, lowered its chain-basket, and brought up a mouthful of rock and coral that it dumped onto its deck with a crash like a door slamming in a tomb.

They were dismantling the reef. Not working around it, not mapping it carefully the way a legitimate salvage operation would—they were eating it, bite by bite, tearing open the graveyard to get at whatever lay inside.

"They'll collapse the caverns," she said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew how angry she was. "All that dynamite and drag-chain, on a limestone shelf that's been hollowing out for ten thousand years. They don't care. They don't even know what's under there. To them it's just overburden."

"Overburden," Ivo repeated. The word sat strangely in his mouth, in his careful Vessani-accented Verlaine trade-speech. "That's what my father called the archive boxes. When the occupation authority ordered the municipal records burned, he was made to carry them to the yard himself. He told me they were overburden." His jaw tightened. "He carried them anyway. All night, box by box, down to a cellar, before he reported the job done. That's how I have anything to read at all."

Zephyrine glanced at him. In four days of shared deck and shared watch he had given her fragments like this—never a whole story, always a shard, offered sideways as though he were ashamed of its weight. She was beginning to understand that shame was simply the shape his grief came in.

Below them, a launch put off from the largest escort, cutting a white seam toward the shore party camped on the shingle beach. She counted figures through the glass she'd bought in Salt Harbor with money she couldn't afford: forty men on the beach, maybe, tents and a signal mast and a stack of crates she'd have bet her ketch held explosives. Forty men ashore, and by the look of the tenders, another sixty or more aboard, divers and tenders and dredge-crews. Against two. Against one patched ketch and an archivist who read ashes.

"We can't go over the reef," she said, thinking aloud because Ivo had earned thinking-aloud. "Even at slack, even at night, they've got picket boats strung across that gap—I can see the lantern masts. And the moment we showed ourselves above the wreck, we'd be boarded, searched, and either hanged as spies or robbed and sunk, depending on the mood of whoever's commanding."

"So the caverns."

"So the caverns." She let out a slow breath. "Every pilot on this coast will tell you the same thing: the Sable Caverns are a death trap. The charts mark them with a skull and the words *do not attempt*. Half the entrances silt shut or shift with every winter storm. The inner galleries flood and drain with the tide through channels nobody has ever properly surveyed, because everyone who tried is dead. There's a story about a smuggler named Corvane—" she stopped, hearing the name, and filed it away without knowing yet why it mattered "—who took a brig full of contraband through the outer caverns thirty years ago. They found the brig a month later, wedged in the second gallery, mast snapped, crew of nine gone. Just gone."

"And you know the way."

It wasn't quite a question, but she treated it as one, because the honest answer deserved honesty. "I know *a* way. The way I mapped myself, diving alone, over three winters, because I'm the kind of fool who finds death traps interesting. And I know it well enough to get a boat through the first two galleries at slack water if the surge isn't running hard. Past that—" she spread her hands "—past that, nobody knows. Including me. The inner caverns connect to the wreck site through channels that show on no chart, because the only people who ever mapped them were Vessani, and the empire drowned every chart along with everything else."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. Below, another basket of reef came up in the chains, and the crash of it rolled across the water like distant artillery.

"My grandmother," he said finally, "kept a phrase she wouldn't explain. She'd say it when the tide turned—*the stone remembers the water*. I thought it was just old-woman talk. Something to say instead of crying." He looked at Zephyrine, and in the growing light she saw that his eyes were wet, though his voice stayed level. "If your caverns were Vessani refuge routes—if people hid in them during the sinking, during the occupation—then somewhere in those walls there may be markings. Directions. My grandmother's generation carved things into stone the way other people breathe. If we go in blind, we won't be going in blind."

Zephyrine weighed that. Hope was a currency she'd learned to distrust—it inflated fast and spent badly—but this wasn't hope exactly. It was information, or the possibility of it, and information she could work with.

"Then we go in with your grandmother's stones and my three winters," she said. "And we go in soon. Because look at the pace they're setting." She nodded at the dredges. "At that rate they'll have chewed through the outer shelf inside two tides. After that, the surge patterns in the first gallery change—more water moving faster through fewer openings—and my route stops being difficult and starts being impossible. We don't have six tides anymore, Ivo. We might not have three."

* * *

They watched another hour while the sun climbed and the fleet woke fully into its work. It was terrible to watch and impossible to stop watching: the coordinated, patient, industrial destruction of a place that had been, forty years ago, a nation's last road home. Divers went down in relays from the tenders, pairs of them, walking the reef with lines and charges. A signal flag climbed the flagship's mast—she picked out the flagship now easily, the biggest hull, anchored dead center over the deep channel like a spider sitting in the middle of its web—and the dredges shifted their pattern in answer, concentrating on the eastern shelf.

"They're opening a channel," she said slowly. "A big one. Wide enough to bring the wreck's cargo up in bulk, not piecework. Which means whatever they think is down there, they want all of it, and they want it fast, and they don't much care what gets broken getting to it." She lowered the glass. "Which also means they haven't found the strongroom yet. If they had, they'd be working precise, not hungry."

"You're certain there is a strongroom."

"I'm certain the chest I pulled out of the Current came from somewhere, and it didn't swim. Iron, sealed, Vessani naval stamping—that's captain's stores or better. Somewhere under that reef is a compartment built to keep paper dry for forty years, and the chest was part of it, and the rest of it is still there." She tapped the headland stone beneath her, as if the wreck were directly below, though it lay half a mile out. "And somewhere near it is the proof of what happened. The orders. The signatures. Whatever your burned archives would have said if your father hadn't carried them into a cellar."

Ivo looked at the fleet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, harder, with an edge she hadn't heard in him before. It suited him, she thought, and then thought again, because noticing such things was becoming a habit she hadn't planned.

"Forty years ago they drowned a nation and called it weather," he said. "Now they've come back with nine ships to make sure the paperwork drowns too. And when it's done, some clerk in the capital will write *site cleared, nothing of significance recovered*, and it will be true, because they'll have made it true." He turned to her. "Zephyrine. I have spent my whole life being careful with what's left of Vessany. Cataloguing scraps. Memorizing what I'm forbidden to keep. Being *grateful* for crumbs. I am done being grateful for crumbs."

"I noticed," she said. "You threw a man into a fish barrel at the tide-market."

"He was reaching for your bag."

"He was reaching for *my* bag, and you threw him into a fish barrel. There's a difference between protecting me and protecting the documents, and I haven't decided which you were doing."

"Neither have I," he said, and the honesty of it startled a short laugh out of her, there on the cold granite with an empire chewing the reef below.

But the laugh died quickly, because the arithmetic underneath it hadn't changed. She made herself say the rest of it plainly, the way she'd say it to a client paying for her judgment and not her comfort.

"Here's what it costs. The cavern approach means no escort can follow us—no boat bigger than a skiff, and most of those die on the outer teeth. It also means no rescue if we're wrong. No patrol to spot our distress signals, no other traffic, nothing. If the Cormorant takes damage in the second gallery, we drown in the dark, and nobody finds us until the dredges do, and by then we'll be overburden." She met his eyes. "I've taken clients into bad water my whole career. I've never taken anyone somewhere I might not be able to bring them back from. I need you to understand that's what this is."

"I understand," Ivo said. "My whole nation is what this is. Forty years of drowned people are what this is." A pause, and then, quieter, with something that was almost gentleness: "And you are what this is. You could still take the broker's price. Sell what we've decoded, buy your pardon, vanish into some country that's never heard of Verlaine. Every hour we sit here, that door stays open. I want you to know I see it. I see that you're choosing past it."

Zephyrine looked away, out at the fleet, because it was easier than looking at him. The morning sun had cleared the headland now and lay full on the anchorage, gilding the black hulls, turning the dredge-chains to lines of fire. It should have been beautiful. Nine ships at anchor in a golden bay, canvas drying, flags lifting in the morning breeze—any painter in the empire would have wept at the composition. And underneath the beauty, the groan and crash of a graveyard being opened by force, and somewhere in the deep water below it all, the drowned, keeping their counsel, waiting.

Awe and terror braided together, she thought. That was the whole coast in one image. That had been her whole life.

"The pardon was never real," she said at last. "I understood that at the tide-market. Whoever sent the broker doesn't sell pardons. They rent them, and they collect. The moment I took their money I'd belong to them worse than the blacklist ever made me belong to anyone." She stood, brushing grit from her knees, and looked east along the coast to where a low dark line of cliffs marked the cavern country, three hours' sail with the morning flood. "We go tonight. Slack water at moonrise. I'll take us through the first gallery on memory and the second on nerve, and after that we'll trust your grandmother's stones."

"And if the stones aren't there?"

"Then we'll be the first ghosts those caverns have collected in thirty years," Zephyrine said, "and honestly, Ivo, at this point I'd rather be a ghost with the truth than a living woman with a rented pardon."

Below them, the flagship's signal mast broke out a new hoist, and across the anchorage the dredges swung their arms in unison, patient as clockwork, hungry as tide.

Three tides, maybe less. She turned from the edge and started down the back of the headland toward the hidden cove where the Cormorant waited, and Ivo fell in behind her, matching her stride, learning her rhythm still.
