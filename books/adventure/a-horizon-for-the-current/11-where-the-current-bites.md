## 11. Where the Current Bites

The entrance to the outer caverns did not look like an entrance. It looked like a mistake the sea had made and never corrected—a seam of darker water where the Sable Current bent around a drowned headland and slid beneath a shelf of black rock furred with kelp. From the deck of the *Cormorant*, at this distance, it was nothing. A shadow. A place your eye skipped over on its way to the horizon.

That was the whole point.

Zephyrine stood at the tiller with her weight low and her knees soft, the way she'd learned to stand in avalanche country, and studied the water for the hundredth time since dawn. The tide was three hours off slack—too early, by any sane reckoning—but the sky had gone the color of wet slate in the west, and she'd rather fight the current with light than wait for slack in the dark.

"You're going in there." Ivo's voice came from behind her, flat with disbelief. He stood amidships with his arms crossed over the coat she'd lent him, which hung on him like a borrowed opinion. "Through that."

"Under that," she said. "The rock shelf runs out maybe forty feet past the lip. After that it opens up."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we'll have a very short conversation about my navigational skills."

He didn't laugh. He hadn't laughed much since the tide-market, and she'd stopped expecting it. Instead he came forward carefully, hand over hand along the jackstay, and crouched at the rail beside her, looking down into the seam of dark water as if he could read it. Maybe he could. Archivists read dead languages; perhaps dead water spoke the same tongue.

"The charts call this the Gullet," she said. "Fishermen call it the Devil's Throat. Nobody calls it anything nice, because nobody who goes in comes back to name it better."

"Comforting."

"I'm not here to comfort you. I'm here to get us through." She checked the leads coiled at her feet, the spare line, the knife on her belt. "Listen, because I'll only say this once. The current through the Gullet runs four knots at flood, better at springs. We go in on the last of the ebb, riding it, not fighting it. The trick isn't power. It's timing. You do nothing until I tell you, and when I tell you, you do it exactly, immediately, without asking why. Can you manage that?"

"I spent thirty years following instructions I didn't understand," Ivo said. "It's practically a religious discipline."

"Good. Then you're overqualified." She put the helm over. "*Cormorant* turns now. Hold on to something that loves you."

* * *

The sea changed before they reached it. She felt it first through the tiller—a stutter, a hesitation, like a horse scenting a cliff edge—and then she saw it: the smooth water ahead beginning to pucker, to shiver, to break into long diagonal ridges that marched against the wind. Where two waters met and argued. The ebb pouring out of the caverns below met the flood tide curling around the headland, and between them the sea stood up in confusion.

"Hold the main sheet," she said. "Not fast. Just hold it. If I let go of everything, you let go of everything."

"Why would you let go of everything?"

"Because sometimes the boat knows better than I do." She said it lightly, but she meant it. That was the first lesson of white water, learned in rivers before she ever brought it to salt: the moment you insist, you lose. The current is stronger than your hull, faster than your rudder, older than your pride. You don't beat it. You negotiate.

They crossed the seam.

The world went loud. Not gradually—all at once, as though someone had opened a door onto a waterfall. Water stood in ridges on either side of the bow, green-grey walls taller than her head, and the *Cormorant* dropped into the trough between them like a stone into a well. Spray came aboard in sheets. Zephyrine heard Ivo swear in Vessani—a short, bitten-off phrase—and then he was silent, both hands locked on the sheet, eyes wide and fixed forward.

"Ride it," she said, more to herself than to him. "Ride it, girl."

The ketch surfed. For six or seven heartbeats they were not sailing but falling, stern up, bow down, the black mouth of the Gullet rising toward them with terrible patience, and then the bow entered the shadow of the rock shelf and the light went out like a snuffed lamp.

Inside, it was not dark so much as dim—a cathedral gloom, green-gold, lit from below by the luminous churn of the current itself. The channel was wider than she remembered, or memory had shrunk it. Walls of wet stone rose on either side, streaming with weed, their surfaces carved into flutes and hollows by forty years of tide. Overhead the rock shelf arched close enough that she could see the barnacles on it, each one distinct, each one a small white eye watching them pass.

"Breathe," she told Ivo, and realized she was giving herself the same order.

The current carried them. This was the part she loved, the part she'd never been able to explain to anyone who hadn't felt it—the surrender of it, the boat and the water agreeing for once, the tiller gone almost weightless in her hands. They ran the first reach in silence, fifty yards of green twilight, the sound of the sea outside fading behind them until all she could hear was the hollow voice of the cavern breathing.

Then the channel bent left, and she remembered what waited at the bend, and her stomach tightened.

"The surge pool," she said. "Past this point the channel widens, then narrows again. When the ebb hits the narrows it backs up into the pool and comes out in pulses. Every few minutes. Like a heart beating."

"Pulses of what?"

"Of everything the sea has. All at once."

She steered them into the widening, killed what little way the sails had, and let the ketch drift while she listened. That was the discipline no one taught and everyone who survived learned: in a cavern, your ears were worth more than your eyes. The water talked. It said *soon* in a hundred small ways—in the pitch of the echo, in the suck along the hull, in the way the foam on the walls crept upward instead of down.

Ivo watched her listen. She could feel him watching, hungry for the method, cataloguing it the way he must catalogue everything. Let him. If they lived through this, he could write it down.

"Now," she said, and put the helm hard over.

The *Cormorant* swung for the narrows just as the pool exhaled.

* * *

Later—much later, in the safe green silence of the inner chamber—she would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory broke them into shards, each one complete and terrible:

The pulse hitting them abeam. Not a wave—a wall, a moving hill of displaced water, eight feet of it, arriving without warning because the sound of its coming had been swallowed by the rock.

The tiller kicking in her hands like something alive and furious, wrenching itself sideways with a force that lifted her off her feet. Her grip holding. Her shoulder not. Something in the joint going hot and wrong, and then the tiller spinning free, useless, the *Cormorant* caught broadside in the throat of the narrows with the current pouring through her like water through a sieve.

The rail going under. Cold climbing her legs, her waist, her chest. The cavern ceiling swinging overhead, closer, closer—

And her own voice, very calm, very far away, saying: *this is how it happens. This is exactly how it happens.*

She went over the leeward rail without deciding to. One moment she was fighting the helm; the next the sea simply took her, peeled her off the deck like a label, and folded her into the green dark.

Cold first. Then noise—a vast, grinding, omnidirectional roar, water hammering her ears from every side, tumbling her end over end until up and down were opinions. Her line—the safety line she'd clipped to her belt before the narrows, the one habit she'd never broken, not in twenty years of bad water—snapped taut and hauled her up short, jerking her arm half out of its socket, and she hung there in the boil, tethered to a boat that was itself being dragged toward the rocks, and understood with perfect clarity that the line would save her from drowning only long enough for the rock to finish the job.

Her boots found stone. She pushed. The current took the push and made a joke of it. She was being drawn along the wall now, feet dragging, toward the narrows' throat where the water funneled through a gap she could have spanned with her arms, and she thought, absurdly, of chapter headings, of how she'd once read that drowning men see their lives in order, and hers wasn't doing that—it was just repeating one image, over and over: the black seam of water seen from offshore, the shadow her eye had always skipped over, waiting all these years for her to come.

Then the line went slack.

No—not slack. It went *alive*. It hauled, twice, three times, in short deliberate pulls that no current makes, and she looked up through the churning water and saw the hull of the *Cormorant* heeled above her, and a shape at the rail, and hands—hands hauling her line hand over hand with a rhythm she recognized even through the roar, the steady mechanical pull of someone who had decided not to panic and was making that decision new every second.

*Ivo.*

He couldn't haul her against the current—he was no stronger than she was, and lighter. But he didn't try to. He paid the line out and hauled it in, out and in, working her across the boil in increments, letting the current's own pulses do the lifting, timing each pull to the beat of the surge pool the way she'd timed the helm. He'd listened. Thirty years of forbidden papers and confiscated archives, and what he'd taken from today was her lesson: *do nothing until I tell you.* Except she hadn't told him anything. There had been no time. He'd simply watched her hands and her water and done what she would have done.

Her head broke surface in the lee of the hull, in the pocket of calm where the heeled rail made a windbreak, and she got one arm over the gunwale and hung there, coughing, while the cavern roared around them.

"Line," she rasped. "Get the line around—"

"I know." He was already moving, clumsy but quick, looping the bitter end around the base of the mast and cinching it. His face was grey. His hands shook. But his hands kept working anyway, and she thought: *there it is. That's the thing underneath the archivist. That's what forty years of surviving occupation builds.* A person who shakes and does not stop.

The *Cormorant* was pinned against the starboard wall, heeled hard, the current tearing at her keel. But the pulse had spent itself. In the lull between beats, the water in the narrows dropped, and the ketch rolled upright with a groan of relieved timber.

Zephyrine dragged herself aboard, collapsed on the deck plates, and lay there dripping, one arm cradled against her chest, staring up at the distant glimmer of the cavern's high air.

"You," she said, when she had breath for it, "were supposed to hold the sheet."

"You let go of everything," Ivo said. His voice cracked in the middle of the sentence. "You said. So I did."

"Not the sheet. Never the sheet—" She stopped. Started again. "Never mind. Come here."

He came. He sat down beside her with less grace than shock, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because the *Cormorant* was drifting slowly, gently, miraculously, into the wide still water beyond the narrows, and the cavern had closed its fist and opened its hand, and they were alive inside it.

"You pulled me out," she said finally. "Nobody's ever pulled me out. I've always been the one who pulls."

"I know." He was looking at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "I don't know how I knew what to do. I just—watched you. All morning. The way you listened. The way you counted." He looked up. "You count when you're afraid. Did you know that? Your lips move. You were counting the pulses."

She hadn't known that. Twenty years alone on bad water, and a disgraced archivist had known her better after one morning than anyone had in a decade.

"Well," she said. "Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

* * *

The inner chamber opened around them like held breath.

She'd described it to him as a room; the word failed it. The cavern vaulted up sixty feet and more, its ceiling lost in a haze of drip and shadow, its walls running with channels of pale mineral bloom. And the air—dry, cold, tasting of stone and old salt—moved faintly against her face, which meant another opening somewhere, which meant they were not sealed in a tomb but passing through a lung.

Ivo stood at the rail and turned in a slow circle, and she watched something move across his face that she had no name for. Not wonder. Wonder was lighter than this. This was the look of a man walking into a room he'd been told was demolished.

"Vessani came through here," he said quietly. "During the evacuation. Before the fleet sailed. They sheltered in places like this—everyone knew the caverns, every fisher family, every pilot. My grandmother—" He stopped. Swallowed. "My grandmother used to sing a song about the singing caves. I thought it was a children's thing. A myth."

"It's not a myth. Listen."

She shipped the oars and let the ketch drift, and the cavern did the rest. Wind moved somewhere deep in the rock, across some hidden throat of stone, and the chamber answered—a low harmonic, rising and falling, almost like a choir heard through a wall.

Ivo's eyes were bright and wet in the lamplight. He didn't wipe them. He just stood there and let the cave sing to him in his grandmother's language, whatever the words had been.

Zephyrine looked away, giving him the privacy of it, and busied herself checking the damage: the tiller fitting cracked but holding, the port rail sprung, her shoulder a hot wire of complaint she filed away for later. The anchor went down on a ledge she remembered, in ten feet of clear green water, and the *Cormorant* settled at rest like a tired animal.

"We're through the outer caverns," she said. "One more passage tomorrow, at slack, and we come up inside the reef, under the fleet's nose. Tonight we rest."

"Tonight we rest," Ivo repeated, as if committing it to record.

But as she doused the lamp and the dark came down, friendly and enormous, she saw that he had turned toward the far wall of the chamber, where the lamplight had caught something she'd missed on her last visit, years ago—something pale and regular, too regular for stone.

Lines. Rows of lines. Cut into the rock by a patient hand.

"Ivo," she said. "What is that?"

He was already reaching for the lamp, already moving toward the wall, and the look on his face made her forget her shoulder entirely.

"That," he said, "is writing."
