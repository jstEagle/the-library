## 13. A Traitor's Ledger

The lamp burned low because Zephyrine rationed everything—the oil, the water, the words between them—and so Ivo read by a light the color of old honey, hunched over the chart table in the Cormorant's cramped cabin while the ketch rocked at anchor in a cove whose name did not appear on any imperial map. She had called it a "smuggler's pocket" and refused to say more, and he had understood: some knowledge was currency, and she did not spend it in front of strangers.

He was not quite a stranger anymore. Not quite anything else yet, either.

The documents lay before him in their careful order, each page flattened between two panes of glass salvaged from a broken porthole, because Zephyrine handled paper the way other people handled explosives—with respect and a healthy fear of what happened when you didn't. Forty years of salt had gotten into the chest anyway. The edges of the topmost sheets had gone soft as moth wings, the ink bloomed brown at the margins, but the Vessani cipher held. It always held. That was the point of it. A nation that knew it was dying built its locks to outlive the door.

He had been decoding since dawn. His eyes ached. His hands ached worse, from the small repetitive violence of the stylus against wax, transcribing each broken line into plain script before he trusted himself to read it whole.

The first pages had been manifests. He'd expected that. Evacuation manifests, the last fleet of Vessany, the ships that went down in the Sable Current with—so the empire's histories said—three thousand souls who had chosen to stay too long and paid for it in cold water. He had memorized those histories as a boy. He had wept over them as a young man in an archive where weeping was forbidden.

But these were not the empire's histories. These were the fleet's own papers, sealed in iron, carried down with the ships they described.

And the manifests were wrong.

Not false—wrong in a different direction entirely. Where the imperial record listed refugee families, these pages listed crates. Where the empire counted souls, Vessany's own clerks had counted weight. *Crate 114, bronze, 240 lbs. Crate 115, bronze, 238 lbs. Crate 116—* on and on, ship after ship, hold after hold, until the numbers stopped being numbers and became a shape he recognized from his training in the archive, from the confiscated ledgers he had been forbidden to keep and had kept anyway, in his skull, line by line, for thirty years.

Treasury manifests. The reserve gold of the Bank of Vessany. The plate of the Cathedral of Saint Elmo of the Tides. The state seals. The regalia. The entire portable wealth of a drowning nation, loaded onto its evacuation fleet under cover of night, disguised in the paperwork as human beings.

Ivo set down the stylus very carefully, because if he did not set it down carefully he would have thrown it.

"They weren't carrying people," he said aloud, to the empty cabin, because saying it made it real and he needed it to be real the way a man needs a wound to bleed before he can believe the knife went in. "They were carrying the treasury."

* * *

Zephyrine came below an hour past midnight, smelling of tar and cold spray, and found him exactly where she had left him, in the same posture, which she seemed to find either admirable or alarming—he could never tell which.

"You haven't moved," she said.

"I've moved." He turned a glass pane toward her. "I've moved forty years."

She wrung out her hair with one efficient twist and sat across the table from him, close enough that the lamp threw their shadows together on the bulkhead. She had a way of sitting that suggested she could leave at any moment and had merely decided not to yet. He had learned to treasure the decision without asking how long it would last.

"Read it to me," she said. "You keep going quiet and making faces. I'd rather have the faces explained."

So he read. The manifests first, dry as driftwood, and he watched her eyes glaze and then sharpen as the pattern emerged, because she was a woman who read water for a living and patterns were her mother tongue. Then the covering letters, in the hand of the fleet's paymaster—a man named Aurel Sennet, whose signature Ivo knew from a dozen confiscated documents, whose fate the empire had recorded as *lost with his ship* and whose fate these papers recorded as something else.

"Listen to this," Ivo said. "*By order of the Regency Council, the embarkation lists are to show families. The true cargo is not to be entered in any manifest shown to harbor officials, escort captains, or crew below the rank of master. The nation's wealth must reach the exile government at Corvay, whatever the cost in*—" He stopped.

"In what?" Zephyrine asked.

"*In tonnage.*" His voice came out flat. "They budgeted for it. They calculated how much gold a ship could carry and how many people it would displace, and they chose the gold. There are marginal notes. Sennet wrote in the margins. Here—" his finger trembled slightly, and he hated it, and pressed harder— "*Ranier protests that we are sending coffers where children should sleep. Overruled. The Council says a nation is its treasury, not its tears.*"

Silence, except the water slapping the hull and the wind worrying the rigging high above them.

Zephyrine reached across the table and turned the next pane herself. "And the empire?"

"That's the second thing." He pulled the deepest sheet from the stack, the one written in the second cipher, the diplomatic hand, the one that had taken him three days and a borrowed key-phrase from a funeral song to crack. "This isn't Vessani. This is imperial. It was in the chest because someone in the Vessani fleet captured it—or bought it, or stole it—in the last week before the sailing. Look at the date."

She looked. He watched her do the arithmetic, watched her arrive.

"Four days before the fleet went down," she said.

"Four days before the fleet went down, the Imperial Admiralty issued this order to the Verlaine squadron." He read it aloud slowly, because some things deserved to be heard at the speed of grief. "*Intelligence confirms the rebel fleet carries the Vessani treasury rather than the civilian population reported. The treasury must not reach Corvay. You are authorized and directed to prevent its transit by all means necessary. No salvage. No survivors' depositions regarding cargo. The loss of the fleet in the Current will be attributed to weather and navigational error. See to it the attribution holds.*"

He finished and discovered his hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as if the papers might otherwise fly apart like startled birds.

"They knew," Zephyrine said. Not a question.

"The empire knew the fleet carried gold instead of refugees, and they sank it anyway, and then they spent forty years telling the world the fleet was full of mothers and children—that Vessany abandoned its own people out of greed or panic—and using that story to finish what the torpedoes started. Every Vessani survivor has spent four decades being told their nation died cowardly. Drowned its own. And the truth is the exact inverse." He heard his own voice rise and did nothing to stop it. "The truth is that Vessany tried to save its future and the empire murdered the future and kept the corpse as a lesson."

Zephyrine said nothing for a long moment. Then, quietly: "Your archive. The one you worked in. They had the manifests?"

"The empire had everything. Confiscated records, captured correspondence, the survivors' interrogations." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "I catalogued it, Zephyrine. Shelf by shelf. I held these truths in my hands and I was not permitted to read them, and I obeyed, because I was permitted to live, and I had decided that living was the thing I would do instead of being brave."

* * *

He had never told anyone the whole of it. Not because it was secret—his cooperation was a matter of imperial record, stamped and filed—but because it was shameful, and shame is the one cargo a man will drown before he jettisons, right up until the moment the water closes over the hatch.

But the cabin was small and the lamp was low and the woman across from him had already seen him weep once, in the drowned cathedral cave, with his forehead against a wall painted by dead hands, and she had not looked away then. So he told her.

"I was nineteen when Vessany fell. My father was Undersecretary of the Harbor Registry—you understand what that means? Not a soldier. Not a hero. A man with stamps. When the occupation came, the new authorities needed clerks who knew where everything was filed, and my father raised his hand before the ink on the surrender was dry. He called it *preserving continuity*. He called it *keeping the records safe*. What he did was hand them the keys to every Vessani life: property rolls, debt registers, the census that told them exactly which families had sons of fighting age." Ivo turned the stylus over and over between his fingers. "And when my father died, I took his desk. Because the alternative was a labor battalion in the northern mines, and because—I tell myself this still, some nights, and some nights I even believe it—because someone had to stay inside the archive and remember what the papers really said."

"You memorized forbidden documents," Zephyrine said. "For thirty years."

"I collaborated for thirty years and committed theft in my head. Both are true. I have never once managed to make them balance." He looked at the manifests, at the neat clerk's hand of Aurel Sennet, dead in the Current, and felt the kinship like a rope around his neck. "Sennet protested. Sennet wrote *coffers where children should sleep* in the margin of an order he then obeyed. That's my whole biography, written forty years early by a better man than me."

"It's not," Zephyrine said.

"You don't know me."

"I know you cracked a diplomatic cipher with a funeral song and no reference texts. I know you saved my line in white water without being asked. And I know—" she tapped the imperial order with one blunt finger— "that the men who wrote this are the ones who get to define what collaboration means. They've been doing it for forty years. They called a fleet full of gold a fleet full of cowards. Why would you take the accounting of your own soul from men like that?"

Because it was easier. Because guilt, at least, was a burden he had learned to carry, and innocence would be a strange country requiring papers he didn't have. He said none of this. He said, "My family name is on the occupation rolls. Marchetti-Vessani branch, registered collaborators, three generations. When the survivors see my name, they don't see the man who memorized their archives. They see the stamp on the door of the office that destroyed them."

"Then we change what the name means," Zephyrine said, with the terrifying simplicity she brought to reefs and to moral questions alike. "You can't un-sign your father's name. But this—" she spread her hand over the decoded pages, the manifests, the order, the whole drowned truth of a nation— "this is a signature too. First honest document your family's been attached to in forty years. Make it yours."

Something in his chest that had been clenched since he was nineteen years old shifted—not loosened, nothing so kind, but *shifted*, the way ballast shifts in a rough sea, changing what the vessel could bear.

Fury, he discovered, was lighter than shame. It had somewhere to go.

* * *

He worked until the lamp guttered and Zephyrine refilled it without comment, and then he worked past that, and when grey light began to seep through the cabin's single porthole he had the ledger complete: every crate, every ship, every name in the margins, and one final entry that stopped his breath.

The last page of Sennet's hand was different. Shakier. Written, Ivo judged, in the final hours, maybe in the final minutes, with the fleet already under way and the imperial squadron already sighted.

"*If we do not make Corvay,*" he translated aloud, his voice roughening, "*let it be known the treasury of Vessany lies in the third cavern beneath the Sable reef, where the current runs backward twice a day. We could not outrun them weighted as we are. Better the sea keeps our gold than the empire spends it. Let whoever finds this find also our names, and say them.*"

Then the list. Not the crew roster—the empire had published that, sanitized, alphabetical, three thousand entries reduced to statistics. This was different. This was Sennet's own tally of the men who had volunteered to stay aboard and scuttle the ships properly, in deep water, so the wreck could never be casually dredged. Two hundred and eleven names. Men who had kissed their families goodbye on the quay at Vessany City and sailed out to die among the crates, on purpose, so that the lie would hold and the gold would wait.

Ivo read the names aloud. All of them. It took twenty minutes and Zephyrine sat unmoving through every one, and when he finished, neither of them spoke for a long time.

"The third cavern," she finally said. Her voice had changed—flatter, faster, the voice he'd begun to recognize as her working voice, the one that meant arithmetic had replaced feeling. "Backward-running current. Twice a day. That's the inner gallery system. The one the charts mark with a skull and the word *unsurveyable*."

"You know it."

"I know enough of it to hate it." She stood, bracing against the roll of the ketch, and went to the porthole. Grey morning, grey water, and far off along the eastern horizon—faint, but unmistakable to eyes trained on horizons—a smudge of smoke that was too straight and too steady to be weather. Funnel smoke. Many funnels.

Ivo joined her at the glass. "Is that—"

"Salvage fleet. Or its advance screen." She was already moving, pulling on her oilskins, coiling decisions the way she coiled lines. "They're ahead of where I wanted them. Which means the tide schedule I gave you yesterday is dead, and the new one is worse."

"Zephyrine." He caught her arm at the companionway. "Two hundred and eleven men stayed behind to sink their own ships, and the empire has spent forty years calling them deserters and cowards. Their names are in this chest. Their gold is in that cavern. And the fleet that murdered them is coming back to finish the job." He heard the fury in his own voice now, clean and hot and finally useful, and did not apologize for it. "We are not leaving here with copies. We're leaving with everything, or we're leaving with nothing, and I no longer care which frightens me more."

She looked at him for a long moment, sea-grey eyes reading him the way she read water—for depth, for undertow, for what lay beneath the surface.

"Good," she said. "Because the storm front coming down off the Verlaine hills says we've got maybe two tides before this anchorage becomes a death trap, and I need you angry. Angry men row hard."

Above them, the wind swung abruptly north and began to sing in the rigging, and the first cold spit of rain struck the deck like a warning.
