## 14. Tide Against Tide

The storm came out of the northwest like something with a grudge.

Zephyrine felt it in her teeth before she saw it—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste of iron on the wind that had been blowing soft and warm off the water all morning. She had grown up reading weather the way other people read letters: fluently, hungrily, with an ear for what was being said between the lines. And the sky over the Sable Current was saying nothing good.

"Barometer's falling," Ivo said from the doorway of the Cormorant's cabin. He held the instrument up as if she might not believe him. The needle had dropped half an inch since noon and was still falling, steady as a man walking downhill.

"I can smell it," she said.

"You can *smell* it."

"Ozone. Kelp torn off the bottom. Old fish rot stirred up from the mudflats." She nodded at the western horizon, where a bruise-colored wall of cloud was assembling itself out of clear air. "That's not rain coming. That's the whole sky falling down at once."

Ivo looked where she pointed, and she watched his face do the arithmetic hers had already finished. Two days of hard sailing behind them, the decoded documents sealed in oilcloth beneath the floorboards, the wreck of the *Vessany Star* lying somewhere under that darkening water with its strongroom still locked. Two tides remained before the window into the inner caverns—two tides, and then slack water for exactly long enough to dive or die.

And now this.

"How bad?" he asked.

She took her time answering, because the honest answer was ugly and she wanted to deliver it without flinching. "Bad enough that we lose both tides. We won't be able to hold position above the wreck, let alone work the cavern mouths. If we try, we'll be driven onto the reef and broken open like a mussel shell."

"So we run."

"We hide." She was already moving, coiling lines, lashing the dinghy down, doing with her hands the things her mind had decided while her mouth was still talking. "There's a cut behind Gullwrack Island—deep enough for the Cormorant, narrow enough that no fleet ship will fit through it. We tie down, we wait it out, and we lose two tides."

"Two tides." He said it flatly, tasting the loss. "That leaves us one window."

"One window," she agreed. "And if we miss it, we wait another full lunar cycle for the tides to come back around. By which time—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. By which time the imperial salvage fleet would have stripped the wreck to its bones, and everything they had bled for would be sitting in an admiral's cabin, catalogued and confiscated and erased.

Ivo went below without another word. Through the deck she heard him moving among their things, and then the particular silence of a man sitting very still with his grief. She let him have it. There were kinds of mourning you couldn't hurry, and she'd learned long ago that standing next to someone while they grieved was worth more than any words you could offer.

Then she turned the Cormorant's bow toward Gullwrack, and the storm swallowed them whole.

* * *

It lasted eighteen hours.

They rode it out in the cut with four anchors down and the sea coming over the island's spine in white sheets, and Zephyrine did not sleep because sleeping in weather like that was how boats died. She sat in the cockpit with the tiller lashed and her hands hooked through the lifelines, watching the anchors' strain by lightning-flash, and somewhere in the black hours Ivo climbed up beside her and wedged himself against the leeward rail and stayed there without speaking until dawn.

He didn't complain. That was the thing about him she kept having to revise her opinion of. She'd taken him for a paper man when they met—an archivist, ink-stained, built for dry rooms and older dust. But he'd hauled lines through the storm without being told, learned the knots fast, and when a wave broke clean over the cockpit and nearly swept him overboard, he'd come up coughing and grabbed the line she threw him and *held*, no panic in it, just grim animal stubbornness.

Now, gray light leaking through the last rags of cloud, he said: "The documents are dry."

"Good."

"And I've been thinking about the cipher key."

"Later," she said. Her whole body ached. Her hands were cracked from salt. "First we look at what the storm left us."

What the storm left them was visible within the hour, once they worked back out of the cut and stood away toward the wreck site under shortened sail—and it stopped Zephyrine's breath in her throat.

The fleet had ridden out the blow too, anchored heavy in the lee of the outer shoals. Nine ships. But now, in the washed-clean morning light, she could see what they were doing, and understanding arrived cold, the way cold water arrives—first at the feet, then everywhere.

Small boats swarmed between the hulls. From them, men were lowering something on long lines to the reef itself. Even at three miles she knew the shape of the crates, the way the handling crews stood so carefully apart from them.

"Dynamite," Ivo said quietly. He had the spyglass, and his knuckles were white around it. "They're setting charges on the reef."

"They're not salvaging," Zephyrine said. The words came out of her slow and terrible. "They're demolishing. They're going to break the reef open and dredge the wreck out of the rubble."

"The caverns—"

"Will collapse." She took the spyglass from him because she needed to see it clearly, needed to make her eyes confirm what her mind already knew. Through the lens: the flagship at anchor, black-hulled, gun ports closed but present; the dredge barges; the charge boats working methodically along the reef line, laying their cargo into the coral like men planting a field. "Every passage I know. Every air pocket. Every route in and out of that drowned cathedral. When those charges go, the whole system comes down. The reef is the roof."

Ivo lowered his head for a moment. When he looked up, his face had gone very still—the stillness she'd learned to read as the surface of something enormous moving underneath. "Then the window doesn't matter anymore. It's not about catching the tide. It's about getting in before they fire the first charge."

"Yes."

"How long?"

She studied the reef through the glass again, forcing herself to be precise, because precision was all she had left. "They'll want to survey the placement first. Check the tide tables. A big demolition doesn't happen fast—it happens *correctly*. My guess is two days. Maybe three if the storm damaged their gear." She handed the spyglass back. "Which means our one tide window just became our only chance, and it has to happen before they're ready to fire."

"Two days to do what should have taken six."

"Welcome to my entire life," Zephyrine said, and turned the Cormorant toward the sheltered anchorage where they could plan.

* * *

They planned in the cabin with the chart spread across the table and weighted at the corners with a lamp, a hammer, and the iron chest's empty lockplate, which Ivo refused to throw away.

"Walk me through it again," he said. "From the top. As if I'm stupid."

"You're not stupid. You're an archivist who's about to dive into flooded caves. Different problem, same courage required." She put her finger on the chart, on the small cluster of markings she'd added herself in pencil—markings that existed on no official chart anywhere, because she'd drawn them from memory, from dives she'd made alone over years, from currents she'd mapped with her own body. "Outer approach here. Everyone knows it—that's why the fleet's anchored over it. The main channel into the wreck runs through open water above the reef shelf. Their divers will use it. Their charges will target it."

"But?"

"But the reef isn't solid. Underneath the shelf there's a system of galleries and tunnels, carved by the current over ten thousand years, and forty years ago the Vessani refugees used them. Shelter, storage, escape routes. The frescoes we found—they didn't paint those walls for decoration. They painted them because people *lived* down there, waiting for ships that never came." Her finger traced a line through the penciled passages. "This gallery connects to the main channel through a throat about four meters wide. At slack water, the current through it drops to almost nothing. That's our door."

"And the timing?"

"Slack water tomorrow evening. Nineteen minutes of usable calm, maybe twenty-two if the swell's kind. In that window we go through the throat, cross the second gallery, reach the strongroom shaft, get the key ledger, and come back." She looked up at him. "Nineteen minutes, Ivo. Down, across, down, up, across, out. With lamps. With limited air. In water cold enough to stop your heart if you panic."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "You've done this before. Alone."

"Some of it."

"Some of it," he repeated. "Which parts haven't you done?"

"The strongroom shaft. Nobody's mapped it. It's below the flood line of the old galleries—I've seen the entrance, never been through." She met his eyes steadily. "So I'll tell you honestly: I don't know what's down there. I know the way in, and I know the way out, and I know the water between them. Everything else is new country."

"And if the charges go early?"

"Then none of it matters, because we'll be inside a mountain when it falls on us." She said it plainly, because he deserved plainness, and because saying it aloud made it real in a way that planning around it never could. "That's the risk. Not drowning, not the cold, not the dark. The risk is that men with dynamite and orders don't keep our schedule."

Ivo looked down at the chart for a long time. His finger came to rest—not on the strongroom, not on the galleries, but on the small penciled X she'd marked years ago where the third cavern lay, the one even she had never entered, the one the old woman's warning had circled like a moat around a secret.

"If we get the ledger," he said slowly, "and the final page needs decoding, and the key is in the strongroom—we come out with everything. The order. The names. The proof of what they did, signed by the father of the man currently trying to bury it." He raised his eyes to hers. "Or we come out with nothing, because we're dead."

"That's the shape of it."

"Then I want to say something before we go in, because if we die down there I don't want my last thought to be unfinished business." He straightened, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not grief, not guilt, but a kind of settled ferocity, banked and burning low. "Forty years ago my family helped the empire catalogue what it stole from Vessany. I've spent my life among their papers, memorizing what I was forbidden to keep, telling myself that remembering was enough. It wasn't. Remembering is what you do *after*. This—" he touched the chart, the throat, the penciled door into the dark— "this is doing something *before*. While it can still matter."

Zephyrine said nothing for a moment. Outside, the water slapped against the hull with the patient rhythm of a thing that had all the time in the world, and she hated it a little for that.

"Then we make it count," she said finally. "We go in at slack water tomorrow. Before that, we prepare everything—lamps, lines, air counts, backup routes. Nothing improvised. Improvisation is how people drown."

"And after?"

"After," she said, "we come out through the throat, we take the Cormorant out past the fleet's pickets in the dark, and we disappear with the truth in our hands before Corvane knows what's happened." She began rolling the chart, and her hands were steady, and she made sure he saw that they were steady, because right now steadiness was a gift she could give him. "One tide. One window. One chance."

"It'll have to be enough."

"It always is," Zephyrine said, "until it isn't. So we make sure it is."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day preparing, and the preparation had the quality of ritual—each task performed twice, checked, performed again. Lamps filled and wicks trimmed. Lines measured and coiled in flaking loops that would pay out without kinking. The oilcloth-wrapped documents transferred to a waterproof satchel that could be strapped to a body. Air counts written on slate: breaths per minute at rest, at work, at fear.

Ivo practiced breathing from the air-pocket technique she showed him until his lips stopped going blue. Zephyrine swam the cut twice in the cold green water, reacquainting herself with the weight of her own body submerged, the way sound moved differently underwater, the way panic felt in the chest and how to breathe it down before it became decision.

Toward evening she surfaced from the second swim and found him sitting on the rocks above the tideline, watching the horizon where the fleet lay at anchor. The sunset was viciously beautiful—gold and blood-orange, the masts of nine ships printed black against it like the bars of a cage.

"They've started the survey boats," he said as she climbed out. "Little lights, moving along the reef line. Marking positions."

"Then we're ahead of schedule. Barely." She wrung the seawater from her hair and sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. The stone was still warm from the day. "Get some sleep tonight. Both of us. Tomorrow we'll need everything we have."

He didn't move. "Zephyrine."

"What?"

"When we're down there—if something goes wrong, and one of us can make it out and the other can't—" He stopped. Started again. "Don't stay for me. Whatever you feel you owe me, whatever you think I'd want—the mission matters more than either of us. The documents matter more. Forty years of dead people matter more."

She looked at him, this man who carried a nation's ashes in his head, who had offered his guilt to her like a wound and waited to see what she'd do with it. The tide was turning below them, the water beginning its long pull back out toward the deep.

"No," she said.

His jaw tightened. "Zephyrine—"

"I've left people behind before. I've made the cold calculation, saved myself, told myself survival was the priority." Her voice was quiet and absolutely level. "I did it once to protect someone I loved, and it cost me eleven years of exile, and I'd do it again, because that choice was *right*. But I am not leaving you in a collapsing cave to save a piece of paper, Ivo. The dead don't need us to become them. If we go down there, we come back together, or we don't come back at all. That's the deal. Take it or swim home."

For a long moment he said nothing. The last light drained out of the sky, and the first stars came out over the Sable Current, cold and indifferent and impossibly far away.

"Together," he said finally. "All right."

"All right."

They sat on the warm rock as the darkness thickened, neither moving, the fleet's distant lanterns strung along the horizon like a string of low orange beads. Somewhere out there, men were measuring reef and stacking dynamite and consulting tide tables, preparing to erase the last physical evidence of a nation's murder.

Tomorrow at slack water, Zephyrine thought, she and Ivo would slip through a door in the world that only she could find, into the dark beneath the sea, to steal the truth back from the empire that had drowned it.

One tide. One window. One chance.

And then, out on the black water, a light flickered—brief, sharp, answered almost immediately by another farther along the reef line. Signal lamps. The fleet talking to itself in the dark.

Zephyrine went very still, counting the flashes, translating the rhythm she'd learned in another life on another coast.

*Ivo,* she said softly. "*They've moved the demolition up.*"
