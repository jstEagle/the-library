## 15. The Broker's Shadow

They anchored in the lee of Gullspit Island as the light went the color of old pewter, and Zephyrine told herself the choice was practical, not superstitious. The islet sat like a broken tooth off the shoulder of the Verlaine coast, ringed by shoals that no chart drew honestly—sandbars that walked with every storm, channels that opened and closed like mouths. She had run these waters since she was sixteen, hauling salvage nets by lantern-light, learning the Sable Current's moods the way other people learned prayers.

Gullspit was a place where nothing followed you. That was what she'd always believed.

She cooked salt cod and hard bread over a brazier on deck while Ivo sat cross-legged against the mast, decoding by lamplight, his fingers moving across the water-stained pages with a tenderness that made her chest ache in ways she refused to examine. He'd been at it for hours. Ink from his improvised pen—cut quill, squid ink thinned with brine—stained his knuckles to the second joint.

"The third page," he said, without looking up. "It lists the holds. Not passengers' names—*cargo manifests*. They weighted the evacuation fleet's manifest to look like refugees. Grain, timber, household goods." His voice had gone flat, the way voices go when feeling would otherwise drown the speaker. "But the tonnage doesn't balance. Forty tons unaccounted for in the *Meridian's* hold."

"Forty tons of what?"

"That's what I intend to find out."

Zephyrine scraped the last of the cod onto his plate and stood, stretching her back until it popped. The evening was settling into that particular coastal hush she loved best—the tide turning, the wind dropping to a whisper, the water going glass-smooth between swells. From the masthead she could see the dark line of the mainland, the faint smudge of the imperial fleet's anchorage fires far to the north, and nothing else. No sails. No smoke.

That should have comforted her. It didn't.

"You keep looking north," Ivo said.

"I keep looking everywhere. That's how I'm still alive."

"And yet." He set down the page. "You've checked the northern horizon four times in an hour."

She didn't answer. Instead she went forward and began checking the anchor rode, running her palm along the wet rope out of habit more than need. Three fathoms down, the hook was buried in good holding ground, sand over clay. The *Cormorant* swung gently to the flood, her patched sails furled, her hull riding light because they'd burned through most of their fresh water and hadn't dared put in anywhere to refill.

Six tides. They'd already spent two. Four remained before the fleet's dredges would begin tearing apart the reef above the wreck—and with it, collapsing the cavern approach that was their only way in. Every hour at anchor was an hour stolen from the race. But running the outer shoals at night without rest was how guides died, and Zephyrine Pemberton had not survived two blacklists by being reckless when recklessness wasn't required.

Rest, then. A few hours. Then the ebb would carry them south toward the wreck site, and they'd make the caverns before dawn.

She was halfway through rigging the storm canvas as a precaution—it was a superstition of hers, preparing for weather that never came, so that the one time weather did come, she wouldn't be caught flat—when she heard it.

Not a sound. An absence.

The gulls.

Gullspit Island earned its name from the ten thousand terns and gulls that nested on its windward scarp, and at dusk they settled into a raucous, quarrelsome bedlam that could be heard half a mile out to sea. Zephyrine had been listening to it all evening without hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Now it stopped. All at once, like a door closing.

She went still, one hand on the halyard. Ivo looked up from his papers. "What?"

"Listen."

He listened. To a landsman, there would have been nothing—the lap of water, the creak of the *Cormorant*'s timbers, the sigh of wind through the island's grasses. But Zephyrine had spent thirty years reading coastlines, and the silence coming off Gullspit was wrong in a way that raised every hair on her forearms. Birds didn't go quiet for weather. Birds went quiet for predators.

Then she saw it: a shape against the last grey band of western sky, standing in past the island's northern point under working sail, no lights showing. A sloop. Fast lines, raked masts, the low silhouette of a vessel built for exactly one purpose.

"They found us," Ivo said quietly.

"Not us. Me." Zephyrine was already moving, dousing the brazier, kicking sand over the coals. "Nobody knows your face, archivist. Everybody who matters knows mine."

"How—"

"The broker. He didn't come to my boathouse blind. Someone sold him my position, or he's had eyes on the harbors since I refused him, waiting for me to stick my head up." She hauled the anchor with quick, brutal efficiency, muscles remembering work her mind barely needed to supervise. "Which means he's not guessing anymore. He knows the *Cormorant*, knows her silhouette, probably knows her draft. And he's got a sloop that can outrun anything I could buy with five years of salvage money."

Ivo scrambled to coil the anchor rope as the *Cormorant* gathered way. "Can we outrun him?"

"No." She said it without drama, the way she'd report a rotten plank or a fouled propeller. "We can't outrun him. So we won't try."

The sloop was standing in past the northern point now, and Zephyrine saw the moment its lookout spotted them—a slight alteration of course, the barest straightening of sheets, a predator adjusting its line of pursuit. In the dying light she made out perhaps six figures on deck. Six men on a fast hull against two on a tired ketch. If it came to boarding, they'd lose. If it came to a straight flight, they'd lose slower.

So it would be neither.

"Ivo," she said, and something in her voice made him go very still. "Do you trust me?"

A pause. Then, with the ghost of a smile: "Less than I should. More than I can explain."

"Good enough. Take the tiller. Keep her pointed at that gap between the island and the southern spit—you see it? Where the water looks smoothest?"

He squinted. "The calm channel?"

"*Especially* the calm channel. Steer for it and don't deviate no matter what I say about anything else. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Then steer."

She went forward and began unfurling sail—not the working canvas but the big drifter, the pale expanse of cloth she kept for light airs, and behind it, after a moment's thought, she rigged something else: a storm lantern hung from the forestay, shuttered so it threw its beam dead astern, painting the *Cormorant*'s wake in a long smear of gold.

"What are you doing?" Ivo called.

"Giving him something to aim at."

The sloop was a mile back and closing, eating the distance with the arrogant ease of superior speed. Zephyrine watched it come and felt, rising through the fear, something else—something hot and bright and entirely unwelcome in a woman who'd spent decades cultivating caution.

Joy.

Because this—this was the thing she knew that no broker, no admiral, no blacklist could take from her. The Sable Current's shoals were not marked on any honest chart, because the men who drew charts worked from surveys done in fair weather from big ships, and the shoals moved anyway, walking their slow migration across the estuary floor like animals grazing. But Zephyrine had been salvaging these waters alone for eleven years. She knew where the bars lay tonight, this tide, this season, the way she knew the scars on her own hands. Somewhere out in that darkness was a sandbar called the Old Wife, because it waited patiently for husbands to bring it ships. There was a channel called the Deceiver, which ran clean for a hundred yards and then turned to mud without warning. And there was a bar off Gullspit's southern spit that no living soul but her had ever crossed twice.

The sloop's captain was almost certainly a professional. Good seaman, fast boat, probably local pilotage bought at some price. But bought pilotage was yesterday's knowledge, and the banks here shifted with every spring tide.

Zephyrine intended to make that difference fatal.

"Harder to starboard," she called. "Take us wide around the spit—give him the inside line."

Ivo obeyed, and the *Cormorant* swept out in a long curve that offered the pursuing sloop exactly what every pursuing captain wants: the shorter course, the direct intercept, the chance to cut the corner and close the distance fast. Behind them, the shuttered lantern painted their wake like a lit fuse leading straight to their transom.

"He'll take it," she murmured. "He has to. It's the only water that looks safe."

The sloop took it.

She heard the change before she saw it—the drumming increase of its bow-wave, the crew's voices carrying flat across the water—and then the pursuer came driving down the inside passage with its rail down and its bow-wave creaming white, gaining forty yards with every swell. Close enough now that she could see the broker himself standing at the stern rail, unmistakable even at this distance: the silver-topped cane, the immaculate coat, the posture of a man watching money come within reach.

For one suspended moment their eyes met across the darkening water. Zephyrine touched two fingers to her brow in mock salute.

Then she said, "Now, Ivo. Hard to port. All of it. Don't think—*turn*."

Ivo spun the tiller and the *Cormorant* heeled, sweeping away to the south just as the sloop, committed to its intercept, drove on into the water she had abandoned.

Into the Old Wife.

The sound was extraordinary. Not a crash—nothing so simple—but a deep, grinding *shudder* that came up through the water itself, as though the sea floor had cleared its throat. The sloop's bow rose, hung, and slammed down onto the bar with a force that Zephyrine felt through the *Cormorant*'s deck a quarter mile away. Her topmast whipped. Men shouted. The beautiful hull, built for speed, began to pound itself against the sand with each retreating swell, and the sound carried across the flats like a bell tolling.

"Anchor watch," Zephyrine breathed, mostly to herself. "He grounded on a falling tide. He'll be hard aground within minutes, and he'll sit there until tomorrow's ebb—no, longer. He'll need a full tide to float off, if he doesn't hole himself first." She allowed herself one long exhale. "And every minute he spends digging out is a minute we don't spend being chased."

On the stranded sloop, figures moved in furious confusion. She could hear the broker's voice now, high and cracking, cutting across his crew's shouts. The cane gestured. Somebody began sounding with a boat-hook. The ship pounded again, and something in the sound changed pitch—deeper, uglier.

"She's holed," Zephyrine said with clinical satisfaction. "Or close to it. He's not going anywhere tonight."

Ivo stood at the tiller, staring back at the wrecked pursuit, and when he turned to her his face held an expression she couldn't immediately name. Wonder, maybe. Or the beginning of something warmer.

"You knew," he said. "You knew that bar was there. You steered him onto it deliberately."

"I steered him onto the only sensible course, and the sea did the rest." She took the tiller back from him, their hands overlapping briefly on the worn wood. "That's the whole art of it, archivist. You don't beat the water. You let the water beat whoever's chasing you."

* * *

They ran south through the dark with the flood tide under them, the *Cormorant* moving sweeter than she had any right to, and Zephyrine permitted herself the luxury of laughing—actually laughing, head back, the sound torn away by the wind—once the stranded sloop dropped below the horizon. Ivo watched her laugh the way a man watches a fire in a cold room.

"You enjoy that," he said. "Being hunted."

"I enjoy *winning*." She trimmed the sheet a hand's width. "There's a difference. Enjoying being hunted gets you killed. Enjoying winning keeps you sharp."

"And the broker?"

"He'll get off. Probably. Professional crews carry spare spars, and the Old Wife isn't deep enough to drown a determined man." She glanced north, where the first stars were pricking through. "But he's lost his fast sloop's schedule, and possibly his fast sloop's bottom, and either way he's a day behind us now. Which means when he reports our position to Corvane's fleet, the report will be stale."

"So we've bought time."

"We've bought *hours*. Hours are better than time. Time you can waste. Hours you spend." She nodded south, toward the black line where the wreck site waited beneath its reef, its caverns, its drowned secrets. "Four tides left, minus the ones we just spent. We make the outer caverns on the next slack, or we don't make them at all."

Ivo was quiet for a while. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—softer, careful, the voice of a man approaching dangerous ground.

"When you saluted him. On the stern rail. You weren't afraid at all, were you? Not in that moment."

Zephyrine considered lying. It was her habit, her armor, the reflex of a woman whose survival had depended on giving away nothing. But the night was dark, the water was kind, and the man beside her had already seen more of her than anyone alive.

"I was terrified," she said. "Right up until the moment I turned him. Then—" She searched for the word. "Then it was like coming home. Isn't that a mad thing? My whole life, the only place I've ever been certain of myself is somewhere I could die in the next ten seconds."

"It's not mad." Ivo looked south, toward the drowned nation that had made him what he was. "I feel the same way in the archives. Surrounded by everything that destroyed us, and it's the only place I'm not lost."

The *Cormorant* ran on through the dark, her wake dissolving behind them, and ahead the southern horizon stayed stubbornly, mercifully black.

Too black, Zephyrine thought, some hours later, as she stood the middle watch alone while Ivo slept below. Too black, and too quiet, and with a smudge on the southern horizon that might have been cloud—or might have been the dredge-fires of nine ships anchored over a grave, burning through the night as they prepared to tear it open.

She counted tides on her fingers. Counted hours. Counted the things she was willing to lose and found the list shorter than she'd expected, which frightened her more than any sloop ever could.

Behind them, far to the north, a single flare went up from the stranded sloop—a red star climbing, bursting, fading. Calling for help. Or calling for the fleet.

Either way, someone would come.

Zephyrine trimmed the lamp wick, set her course by a star she'd trusted since girlhood, and ran south into the dark with the tide.
