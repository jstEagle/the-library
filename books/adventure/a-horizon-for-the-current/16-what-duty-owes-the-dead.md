## 16. What Duty Owes the Dead

The quarrel began, as the worst ones do, over something small.

Ivo had spread the decoded pages across the Cormorant's chart table and weighted their corners with a brass cup, a coil of twine, a lump of beeswax he'd found in a drawer. The ketch lay at anchor in a cove behind a shingle spit, hidden from the sea-lane, and the evening light came through the stern windows in long amber bars that made the ink look freshly spilled—as if the men who had written these orders had signed them only yesterday, and were still waiting to be answered for it.

Zephyrine stood at the tiller-side rail, coiling line. She had been coiling line for an hour. She was very good at looking busy when she wanted to avoid a conversation, and Ivo had learned to read her silences the way she read water: by what moved beneath them.

"You've stopped decoding," she said at last, without turning around.

"I haven't stopped. I'm deciding what to do with what I have."

"That sounds like a conversation."

"It is one." He set down his pen. "I'd like to have it with you instead of at you."

She laughed—short, unamused—and hung the coiled line on its peg with more force than the line deserved. Then she turned, leaned back against the rail, and crossed her arms, and he saw that she had already fought this battle in her head and come armed.

"Say it, then."

"The documents go to the Vessani diaspora." He kept his voice level. "All of them. The originals and every copy."

* * *

He had rehearsed the argument for two days, through the storm and after it, while she slept in the leeward berth and he sat up with the lamp turned low, translating page after page of a language the empire had tried to bury along with the nation that spoke it. He knew every counter she could raise. He had answers ready for all of them. What he had not prepared for was how much it would cost to say the words aloud in front of her, because saying them made real the thing he had been circling since the tide-market: that she might choose otherwise, and that he would still—

He would still what? Stay? Leave? He didn't know. That was the wound under all of it, and he kept his hand pressed flat over it as he talked.

"They are not mine," he said. "They never were. They're not yours either, Zephyrine, however they came into your net. They belong to people who are still alive. There are Vessani in Salt Harbor, in the free ports, in the cliff villages—children of children of the drowned—who have spent forty years being told their country sank itself. That their fleet was lost to weather. That there is no one to blame because there is no crime."

"And these papers change that."

"These papers *are* that. Look at them." He touched the top sheet, the one he'd finished before the storm: the manifest of the evacuation fleet, cross-checked against the imperial naval ledger in a cipher hand only three living archivists could read, himself now the third. "Fourteen ships cleared Vessany's outer roads carrying eleven thousand souls. The ledger records nine ships arriving. Two lines further down, sealed orders, countersigned. *Sink at position, permit no survivors to land.* It's not ambiguity. It's arithmetic. Eleven thousand minus nine ships' worth. That's what the empire wrote down in its own hand and then locked in a chest and dropped into the dark, and the dark kept it, and the dark gave it to us."

"To me," she said. "The dark gave it to my net."

"Yes. And I'm asking you to give it onward."

She pushed off the rail and walked to the chart table, and for a moment he thought—hoped—she was going to look at the pages again, really look, let them do their work on her the way they'd done their work on him. Instead she put her palm flat on the table beside the papers, deliberately not touching them, and looked up at him with eyes the color of slate in rain.

"Sell them," she said.

The word landed like a hook in his chest. "No."

"Hear the number first." Her voice was steady, almost gentle, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "I had a message passed to me at the last harbor. Before you say brokers can't be trusted—and they can't, I know, I've been dodging them since the chest came up—the number wasn't invented by one man. It's what the market will bear, and the market has been set by people who want this quiet. Two hundred thousand crowns. Pardon papers for both of us, full and public, my name struck from two blacklists, your cooperation statute dissolved. Plus passage, plus silence guaranteed, plus enough left over to buy a ship better than this one and never salvage another drowned man's boot for wages."

"You'd take it."

"I'd *consider* it, which is more than you're letting yourself do." She straightened. "Ivo. I have spent nine years being no one. I sleep in boats and rented rooms and I leave anywhere I stay past three days because staying is how they find you. I have watched patrol cutters burn a guide's boat at anchor for the crime of knowing a pass too well. You think I love this life? I'm *good* at it. That's not the same thing."

"I know it isn't."

"Do you?" Something flared in her face. "Because from where I stand, you're asking me to stay exactly what I am—nameless, hunted, worth nothing on paper—for the sake of a country that died before either of us could walk. A country that, I'll remind you, had a government, and fleets, and archives, and every one of them failed to save itself. Where were Vessany's protections when its own evacuation fleet needed them? Dead nations don't get justice, Ivo. They get footnotes. And the living—" she gestured at herself, at him, at the small wet world of the ketch around them— "the living get to decide whether a footnote is worth drowning for."

* * *

He should have answered calmly. He had composed calm answers during the storm, whole paragraphs of them, reasonable and patient. What came out instead was the truth, unarmored, and it came out loud.

"My father's name is in those pages!"

The cabin seemed to rock with it, though the anchorage was glass-calm. Zephyrine went still.

"Page eleven," he said, quieter now, because the worst of it was out and the rest could only be said softly. "The cipher key was held by the Office of Records. My family held the Office of Records for six generations. The last Registrar of Vessany—my grandfather—trained the clerks who encoded these orders. Some of them were his students. One of them was my mother's brother." He looked down at his hands. They were steady; he made them be steady. "I told you I survived because I cooperated. I didn't tell you the shape of it. When the empire annexed the coast, they needed someone who could read the old registries—property rolls, shipping manifests, the census of the drowned. My family provided that service. We called it preservation. We told ourselves we were keeping the nation alive in ink while its body was buried. And all the while, in the deepest vault, under seals my grandfather personally applied, sat the proof that the nation hadn't simply died. It had been *executed*, with paperwork, by the same empire whose stamps we were so carefully preserving."

Zephyrine said nothing. She had the gift of silence when it mattered; it was one of the first things he had loved in her, though he had not yet admitted to loving anything, even inside his own skull.

"So when you ask where Vessany's protections were," he went on, "I can tell you exactly where. They were in my family's vault, forty years deep, and my family kept them locked. Not out of malice. Out of fear, and habit, and the slow comfortable cowardice of people who have a salary and a title and a story about why those things are virtuous. I catalogued confiscated papers for nineteen years, Zephyrine. Do you know what that means? It means I alphabetized my country's autopsy. And every year I told myself: next year, something will change, some door will open. And every year I memorized a little more of what I was forbidden to keep, because memory was the only smuggling they hadn't outlawed." He lifted his eyes to hers. "These pages are the door. It finally opened. And you're proposing to sell the key back to the jailer."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing about this is fair. But fairness isn't the question. Duty is."

"Duty." She said the word like she was tasting something spoiled. "Duty to whom? To the dead?"

"To the living who remember them."

"The living who remember them are scattered across four coastlines and mostly over sixty years old. The young ones have new names and new flags and they don't speak the tongue except for curses and lullabies. You want to hand eleven thousand deaths to a diaspora that can't feed a delegation to a hearing, let alone force an empire to account. What happens then, Ivo? The papers get read at a memorial. People weep. Someone writes a ballad. And the empire issues a statement expressing regret for the tragedies of a difficult era, and the dredges keep working the wreck site until there's nothing left but sand, and the only surviving proof of the crime is a certified copy in an imperial evidence locker where it will be misfiled within a decade." She shook her head. "I've watched institutions eat truths before. Bigger teeth than yours."

"Then what does selling them buy? A pardon?" His voice cracked on the word and he hated it. "You'd be purchasing your good name with their bad graves. Every time someone congratulates you on being cleared, on being *restored*, you'll know what the restoration cost. You'll know it every morning for the rest of your life. I know that arithmetic too. I did it for nineteen years. It doesn't balance. It never balances. You just get very practiced at not looking at the remainder."

* * *

The lamp guttered and he trimmed the wick, grateful for the small task, the few seconds of not facing her. Through the stern windows the cove had gone violet-dark; the first star stood over the shingle spit like a fixed point on a chart neither of them had drawn.

When he turned back she had sat down across the table from him, elbows on the wood, chin on her fists, and the anger had gone out of her posture and left something rawer underneath.

"Tell me what you think a pardon is worth," she said. "Actually tell me. Because I don't think you know what it's like to not have one. You had a life. A desk, a title, a city, a function. You lost it, yes—but you lost it to history, to a flood everyone drowned in together. I lost mine to a single day on a single mountain, and I've carried the blame alone for nine years. There are harbors I can't enter. There are families who spit when my old name comes up. If I could buy that back—not the past, just the *permission to exist somewhere*—don't tell me you wouldn't at least weigh it."

"I would weigh it," he said. "I have weighed it. That's why I know which side of the scale it lands on."

"Easy for you. Your name isn't on any blacklist. Your name is on nothing. You're a ghost with a library card."

That hurt precisely because it was true, and he let the hurt sit between them for a moment rather than flinching from it. "Yes," he said. "I am a ghost. And ghosts are exactly the right people to carry messages for the dead, because we've got nothing left to lose and everything left to owe." He turned one of the pages toward her, gently, the way he'd turn a wounded bird. "Read this name. Just this one."

She looked. He watched her lips move slightly, sounding out the Vessani syllables she'd been picking up from him without knowing it.

"Maren Ostrevik," she read. "Quartermaster, third class."

"A quartermaster, third class, aged twenty-three, from a fishing town you couldn't find on any current chart because the empire redrew the coast after the sinking. She had a mother. The mother lived nineteen more years in Salt Harbor and died asking one question: did Maren reach a ship? Did anyone see her in the water? Was she quick?" His throat tightened. "There are ten thousand mothers like her, Zephyrine, and most of them are dead now too, and their grandchildren are asking the same question with nobody left to answer it. These pages answer it. *No. No ship reached them. No survivor was permitted. It was ordered, signed, and sunk.* That answer belongs to them. Selling it would be the second scuttling. I won't help commit it, and I can't stand aside while—"

"While what?" she said sharply. "Say it. While I do it? Is that what you think I am?"

"I think you're tired," he said, "and frightened, and you have earned the right to both, and I think fear makes excellent arguments. Mine certainly did, for nineteen years."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The tide turned outside; he felt it in the way the ketch's hull shifted against its anchor, a small patient adjustment, the sea reminding them it kept its own schedule regardless of human quarrels.

Zephyrine rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. When she lowered them she looked older and younger at once, and terribly honest.

"Here's what frightens me," she said. "Not the patrols. Not Corvane's fleet, not even really the broker. What frightens me is that you're right." She laughed, hollow. "I ran the sale through my head a dozen times since the storm. Every time, I got to the moment of handing over the chest, and in my head the buyer's clerk opens it, and I see Maren Ostrevik's name go under someone's thumb, filed, stamped, closed. And I feel—" she searched— "like I'm holding her under the water myself. So no. I'm not going to sell them. Don't celebrate. I still think you're a fool, and I still think the diaspora will fail, and I still think we'll end with nothing but the truth and no roof over us. But I can't sell them. Apparently I have a line, and it turns out it runs exactly there."

Relief went through him like warm water through cold, and with it something larger and less nameable that he refused, for the moment, to name. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Arguing with you is like arguing with a tide chart." She stood, briskly, armor going back on piece by piece. "But understand what you're asking. If we give the originals to the survivors' council, we hold copies, and copies can be disputed, seized, forged. The originals in imperial hands are leverage. The originals in ours are a target painted on the hull. Either way, from tonight forward, everyone hunting this chest hunts *us*, and they hunt us harder because we've stopped being sellers and become witnesses. Witnesses don't get pardons, Ivo. Witnesses get disappearances."

"Then we make sure we disappear loudly, in daylight, in front of crowds, in places they can't bury the story along with us."

"That," she said, reaching for the lamp, "is the single most reckless plan I've ever heard, and I once guided a client who insisted on crossing the Verlaine passes in thaw season." She paused with her hand on the lamp's brass stem, and something in her face shifted—the planner surfacing through the arguer. "Which means it needs a route. And a destination. And I know exactly one place on this coast where a crowd of Vessani descendants gathers safe from imperial reach, and it's not Salt Harbor."

He went very still. "The cliff village."

"The cliff village." She blew out the lamp, and the cabin filled with the blue dusk-light off the water. "Two days' sail, if the wind cooperates and the fleet doesn't. Which means we sail on the morning tide, and tonight you teach me every name in those pages, because if we're carrying eleven thousand dead to their answer, I intend to arrive knowing at least a few of them by heart."

In the dark, Ivo gathered the pages and squared their edges with care, and found that his hands were not entirely steady after all.

"One more thing," Zephyrine said from the companionway, her voice half shadow. "The old woman who keeps the village—Maren's kin, if the genealogies hold. When we hand these over, you'll be giving her the answer she's asked the sky for forty years. Think hard tonight about whether that answer is a gift or a wound. Because I've delivered both kinds, and I promise you, they weigh the same in the hand."

She went up on deck to check the anchor watch, and Ivo sat alone in the dark with the nation's last testament spread before him, listening to her footsteps overhead, and understood that whatever this was between them—allyship, trust, the other thing neither had spoken—it had just been tested by fire and had held, and that the holding frightened him more than the burning would have.

Above him, her footsteps stopped. Then her voice came down through the deck boards, tight and quick:

"Ivo. Get up here. Lights—three of them—coming round the spit. And they're not flying any colors I know."
