## 17. The Survivor Colony

**Zephyrine**

The village did not exist, and that was precisely how she found it.

Ivo had described the approach three times over two days of sailing, each recitation identical, as though he were reciting scripture rather than directions: *Past the split rock with the white guano stripe, hold the Cormorant's bow on the notch in the headland until the water goes from green to slate, then turn hard to starboard before you lose your nerve.* She had asked him what happened if you lost your nerve anyway. He had said, without any expression at all, "You find out why they call it the Gullet."

The water went from green to slate exactly where he said it would. Zephyrine turned hard to starboard, and the cliffs opened like a book.

Not opened — that implied welcome. They *parted*, a seam in the rock barely twice the Cormorant's beam, and for thirty heartbeats she steered through a corridor of stone so narrow the ketch's yardarm whispered against the walls, tide sucking at the hull from both sides like hands arguing over a prize. Then the passage spat them into a lagoon no chart would ever admit to: a bowl of grey-green water ringed by cliffs two hundred feet high, terraced with stone houses the color of the stone itself, built into the rock so thoroughly that from the sea — if the sea could ever see this place — it would read as nothing but shadow and striation.

People stood along those terraces. Perhaps forty of them, old mostly, some very old, watching the intruder vessel with the stillness of herons.

"They know we're coming," Ivo said quietly beside her. He had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with seasickness. "They always know. There are watchfires on the outer headland. Smoke signals older than the empire."

"You didn't mention watchfires."

"Because I wasn't sure they'd let us in." He looked at his hands. "I'm still not sure they have."

Zephyrine shipped the oars and let the Cormorant drift toward the one wooden jetty in the whole amphitheater of rock, gray and salt-bleached and looking like it had been rebuilt a hundred times by people who expected to rebuild it again. She threw a line around a bollard worn smooth as bone.

An old man came down the cliff stairs to meet them. He walked slowly, leaning on a staff of black driftwood, and he stopped ten feet short of the jetty's end and simply looked at Ivo for a long time.

Then he said something in a language Zephyrine didn't know — liquid, formal, with a cadence like a sentence being closed — and spat into the water between the jetty and the shore.

Ivo received it standing straight. "Yes," he answered, in the same tongue. Then, in Verlaine trade-speech, for her benefit: "He knows my family name. Marchetti-Vessane. My grandfather signed the occupation registry."

"And that means?"

"It means," Ivo said, "that here, I am the grandson of a collaborator, and the name is enough. It doesn't matter what I've done since. Names don't drown here. They just get handed down." He swallowed. "They'll let us land because you carry the chest. Not because of me."

The old man spoke again, gesturing up the stairs with the staff. Zephyrine caught only one word, repeated twice, and even she understood it: *Vessany*. The dead nation's name, spoken the way you'd speak at a graveside.

"He says the Council will hear us at dusk," Ivo translated. "Until then we're guests of nobody. We sleep on the boat."

* * *

**Ivo**

He had forgotten how much silence a Vessani village could hold.

Not emptiness — this place was full of sound, of gulls and looms and the knock of mallets on stone, of children, a handful of them, dark-haired and quick, staring at the strangers from behind their grandmothers' skirts. But underneath all of it ran the other silence, the one he remembered from his grandmother's kitchen when certain names were spoken: the silence of things decided long ago and never reopened.

He spent the afternoon being not-spoken-to. Women passed him on the stairways with their eyes fixed on the middle distance. A boy of perhaps eight threw a pebble that struck his boot, deliberately, and was not scolded. An old fisherman mending nets on the jetty worked an arm's length away from him for an hour without once acknowledging his existence, which took a discipline Ivo could only admire.

Zephyrine, by contrast, was treated with rough courtesy. She was the stranger; she was unburdened. She sat with the net-mender within the first half hour and asked questions about tides and channels in her blunt, competent way, and the old man answered her, grudgingly at first and then, Ivo noticed with something like grief, with real interest — because she knew the Sable Current's moods the way these people once had, and knowledge was the one currency that had never been confiscated.

By late afternoon she came back to him at the boat, where he'd been re-stowing the document chest against damp, and sat down on the jetty beside him with the frankness of someone who has decided a thing and moved on.

"They hate you," she said. No question in it.

"They remember. It's different." He tied off the last knot. "My grandfather kept the registry office in Vessany-port after the annexation. He decided who got exit papers. Who got rations. Who got summoned." He heard his own voice go flat and clinical and made himself stop. "He told himself he was saving lives. Some of the families here may be alive because of him. All of them lost someone because of him. Both things are true, and only one of them is forgivable, and it isn't mine to say which."

Zephyrine watched the water. "I blacklisted myself once," she said, "to keep someone else's name clean. Different crime. Same arithmetic." She didn't elaborate, and he had learned not to push her toward doors she hadn't opened. But she stayed sitting next to him, shoulder almost touching his, until the light went bronze and a bell somewhere up the terraces rang dusk.

"That's the Council," Ivo said, standing. His legs felt like someone else's. "Time to be judged."

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The council chamber had been a chapel once — she could tell by the arched window, now filled with fitted stone, and the niche where a saint or a sea-god had stood, now holding a lamp and a folded flag so old its colors had gone the color of weak tea. Twelve elders sat in a crescent on stone benches. The old man with the driftwood staff sat at the center. Behind them, on the wall, hung a carved wooden plaque bearing words in the Vessani script, and beneath the script, added later in smaller characters, a translation into imperial Verlaine that someone had clearly chiseled in as an act of defiance rather than compliance:

*WE WERE NOT SUNK. WE WERE SENT.*

She stood where she was told to stand, with the chest at her feet, and listened to Ivo translate the proceedings in a low voice at her shoulder.

The elders questioned him first. Their voices were dry and unhurried, and though she couldn't follow the words, she could follow the shape of it: they were taking testimony, the way you'd take testimony about a shipwreck. Where had he been. What had he done. Whose archive did he serve. When he admitted — plainly, without flinching, and she gave him credit for that — that he had spent twenty years cataloguing the empire's confiscated Vessani papers, memorizing what he was forbidden to keep, a murmur went through the crescent like wind through wheat, and not a kind one.

Then the center elder — his name, she'd learned, was Danel — turned to her, and Ivo translated her answers back and forth, and the questions were simpler than she'd expected. Where had she raised the chest. What condition was it in. Had anyone else seen it. Had she been offered anything for it.

"Yes," she said. "A fortune. Sight unseen. I refused."

That produced the first reaction aimed at her that wasn't neutral: several of the elders leaned forward. One woman, small and iron-backed, with hands knotted like tree roots, spoke a full minute without stopping, and her voice climbed at the end, and Ivo's translation came slower than usual, as though the words cost him.

"She asks," he said finally, "if you understand what you're carrying. Not what it proves — everyone here knows what the empire did, we've known for forty years, our parents died knowing it. She means: do you understand that proof is a weapon, and weapons choose their wielders. She asks who you intend to fire it at."

Zephyrine looked at the iron-backed woman directly. "At whoever pays least and deserves most," she said. "Which so far looks like you."

The translation went across the room. Silence. Then the iron-backed woman laughed — one short bark, entirely without warmth but not entirely without something — and said something brief.

"She says," Ivo murmured, "that honesty is cheaper than flattery and lasts longer."

Danel raised his staff and struck the floor twice, and the council rose, and that was apparently the verdict: undecided, adjourned, pending. As they turned to go, the iron-backed woman spoke once more, and this time she addressed Zephyrine alone, and pointed one knotted finger at the chest.

"She says the documents will want blood before they're done," Ivo translated quietly. "And that we should decide whose, before the documents decide for us."

* * *

**Ivo**

Afterward, in the blue hour, the old woman came to the jetty.

Ivo saw her coming down the cliff path with a lantern and braced himself for another round of contempt, but she walked past him as though he were furniture and stopped in front of Zephyrine, who was coiling line in the last light with the economy of motion he'd come to love watching.

The woman held out her hand. In it lay a charm: a disc of black coral, drilled and threaded on braided hair — human hair, Ivo realized, and very old — carved with the Vessani sign for *breath*.

"For the diver," the woman said, in accented but fluent trade-speech, making it clear she'd understood everything all along and had simply preferred not to spend her own language on the Council's theater. "Your man there translates, but the Current doesn't need translating. It takes who it takes." She pressed the charm into Zephyrine's palm and closed the diver's fingers over it. "It belonged to my daughter. She dove the inner caverns in 'sixty-one, hunting the treasury before the empire's divers found the wreck. She found the way in. She did not find the way out."

Zephyrine stood very still. "Why give it to me?"

"Because you're going in anyway. I can see it in both of you — you've already decided, you're just waiting for the tide to agree." The old woman's face in the lantern light was a map of every year since the drowning. "So take the charm and take the warning, and don't confuse the two. The inner caverns breathe. Everyone thinks the danger is the water. The danger is the air. There are pockets in there, chambers that hold breath from the old world, sealed forty years — and they lie. They sit calm as a mirror and then the pressure shifts and they inhale. My daughter surfaced into one such pocket thinking she'd found sanctuary. The pocket took her breath to feed itself. What came back up was not my daughter."

Ivo felt the hair rise on his arms. "Is there a way through? Truly?"

"There is a third way out of the inner dark. Not the way you dive in, not the way the maps pretend exists. The flooded gallery. The treasury gallery." She said the word *treasury* the way other people said the names of their dead. "If the Current loves you, and your breath holds, and the charm does its work, you will find it. If any of those three fail—" She shrugged, a small terrible gesture. "Then the caverns will have another story to keep."

She turned to go, then paused, and for the first time looked directly at Ivo. Her eyes were pale, nearly colorless, like sea-worn glass.

"Marchetti-Vessane," she said. "Your grandfather wrote my son's name on a list. The list sent him to the mines. He lived. Barely. He is upstairs in that village right now, older than God and angrier than the sea." She lifted the lantern higher, studying Ivo's face as though comparing it to some older photograph. "But he also told me, once, drunk on festival wine forty years ago, that there was a clerk's son who used to smuggle bread to the listed families. A boy who risked the registry's own gallows. He never knew the boy's name."

Ivo could not speak.

"My son remembers the boy's hands," the old woman said. "Small hands. Quick. You have his hands." She held his gaze a moment longer. "Names are handed down here. So are debts. Go and make sure yours comes down lighter than it arrived."

She walked back up the cliff path, and the lantern shrank to a spark, and vanished.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

They sailed on the morning tide — the Council had given grudging leave and a barrel of water and not one word of blessing — but the night before, Zephyrine sat on the jetty turning the black coral charm over in her fingers while Ivo sat beside her, unusually silent even for him.

"The boy with the hands," she said eventually. "Was that you?"

"I was nine years old," Ivo said. "My grandfather ran the registry. I carried the bread because children weren't searched. I didn't understand what the lists meant. I thought I was playing at being brave." He looked out at the black water. "Forty years later it turns out it was the only honest work anyone in my family ever did, and I can't even claim it, because I don't remember most of the houses I delivered to. She remembers more than I do. That's what survival does — it makes the ones who stayed the keepers of everything, and the ones who left the owners of nothing."

Zephyrine closed her hand around the charm. It was warm from her skin, or maybe from forty years of someone else's.

"My grandmother had a saying about debts," she said. "*Pay forward what you can't pay back.*" She tossed the charm lightly, caught it, and looped its cord around her neck, under her shirt, against her sternum where a heartbeat could reach it. "We'll come back here after. Whatever we find down there — the truth goes to these people first. Before any court, before any buyer, before anything."

Ivo turned to look at her, and in the dark she could feel the weight of it, the thing neither of them had said yet, gathering like water behind a dam.

"You mean that," he said. Not a question either.

"I've sold a lot of things to survive," Zephyrine said. "I'm tired of surviving being the whole job."

Far above them, on the outer headland, a watchfire kindled — one flame, then a second, then a third, answering fires along the coast beyond the cliffs, messages traveling down the shoreline faster than any ship could sail. Ivo watched them and went rigid.

"That's not for us," he said. "That's a warning chain. Someone's sighted something on the water." He was already on his feet, shading his eyes toward the seaward gap in the cliffs, where far out past the Gullet's mouth — impossibly far, and yet unmistakable — lights rode at anchor in a pattern Zephyrine knew from bitter experience: nine ships, ranked, dredge-booms silhouetted against the last of the moon.

The fleet had not waited at the wreck site.

It had come looking for them.
