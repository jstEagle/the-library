## 18. Half a Pardon

The tide was wrong for the inner caverns, and the fog had come down off the headland like a lid, and so there was nothing to do but wait—which was, Zephyrine thought, the one discipline she had never mastered. She sat in the Cormorant's stern with her back against the mast step, a coil of line across her knees she wasn't really mending, and Ivo sat opposite her with the decoded pages spread on an oilcloth between them, and the silence between them had gone soft in a way that frightened her more than any surge current ever had.

He looked up. "You said once you'd been blacklisted from two countries."

"I did."

"You've never said why."

She worked the fid into the rope's throat, pushed it through, pulled it tight. The knot came out clean. She started another.

"Zephyrine."

"It's a long story."

"We have a tide and a half," he said. "I've read forty years of confiscated testimony. I know what long stories look like when they're being avoided."

* * *

The mountain was called the Weißgrat, and it stood at the head of the Val d'Ombra like a judge who had never once acquitted anyone. Zephyrine had been twenty-six, four years licensed, three seasons guiding for the Pemberton name that wasn't hers by blood but by apprenticeship—old Halvard Pemberton had found her half-starved in a border town when she was sixteen and taught her rope and ice and the arithmetic of weather until she could read a snowpack the way Ivo read his ashes: fluently, and with grief.

The client's name was Lord Aldery Voss, and he was the kind of patron who bought mountains the way other men bought horses—to be seen riding them. He wanted the north face of the Weißgrat in late March, out of season, because no one else had done it that year and because his rivals had done it the year before. He paid triple rates. He brought a party of six: himself, two friends of similar appetite, a photographer, and two porters hired out of the valley.

And he brought Sera.

Sera Quill was the reason Zephyrine took the contract, though she told herself otherwise at the time. They had come up together under Pemberton, two orphans of the trade, and where Zephyrine was careful Sera was brilliant, and where Zephyrine counted risks Sera simply declined to see them, and somehow between them they'd made a whole guide—the one who watched the sky and the one who danced on the edge of it. Sera had signed on as second guide for Voss's expedition because the money was enough to buy her mother a house out of the flood plain, and because Sera had never met a slope she believed could kill her.

Zephyrine should have refused the contract outright. She knew that now. She had known it then, too, which was the worst part—not ignorance, not inexperience, but knowledge, clear and cold as meltwater, and the decision to set it aside because the fee would buy her a boat and because Sera would be there and because saying no to Sera had never once worked in eleven years.

Here is what she saw, and here is what she said.

On the third morning, at the hut below the face, she dug a pit in the wind-loaded slope above their route and cut the columns back, and the layers failed exactly where she expected them to fail—a hoar crust buried under nine days of new snow, weak as rotten bread, loaded past anything she'd want beneath her boots even in January. She did the test twice. She wrote the numbers in her book. And at breakfast she laid the book open in front of Lord Aldery Voss and told him, in words she still remembered because she had chosen each one carefully, that the face was primed to slide, that the warming trend would peak in two days, that if they climbed they must climb the couloir before ten in the morning and be off the upper slopes by noon, and that even then she would rate the odds as worse than even.

Voss looked at the pit data the way a man looks at an itemized bill. He smiled. He said, "My dear Miss Marchetti, I have paid for the summit, not for your anxieties." He said the guides in this country were famously timid. He said he had climbed in the Oberland without incident. He said, and this was the sentence that lodged in her like a splinter working toward the heart: "If you will not lead, I will find someone who will."

And Sera, standing behind him with her crampons over her shoulder and the sunrise catching her hair, said: "I'll lead."

Not defiance. That was the thing Zephyrine had spent years refusing to understand. Not recklessness, not contempt for the warning. Sera had looked at the numbers too—she'd helped dig the pit—and she had made her own calculation, the way she always did, weighing the odds against the house her mother needed and the season they both needed and the simple animal fact that Sera Quill had never yet lost an argument with a mountain. She said, quietly, only to Zephyrine, while Voss's party stamped and blew steam: "We go early, we go fast, we're down by noon. You taught me that. It'll hold."

"It might not," Zephyrine said.

"Then stay in the hut," Sera said, and smiled, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible confidence of someone who has never been wrong about the things she loves. "But you won't. You never do."

She was right. Zephyrine went.

They left at four in the morning, ahead of the sun, and for five hours the plan held. The couloir was firm, the party moved well, Voss complained but kept pace, and Zephyrine allowed herself the small fatal luxury of believing that competence was a form of control—that her skill could stand between the mountain and the people on it like a wall, that reading danger was the same as disarming it.

At half past nine they crossed the upper bowl beneath the serac line, four hundred meters of wind slab above them, and the sun came over the eastern ridge and touched the snow.

She felt it before she heard it—a change in the light, a softening, the whole slope exhaling. Then somewhere above, deep in the pack, the mountain cleared its throat.

"Rope's off!" she screamed, because a roped team dies together and a free team might scatter survivors, and she shoved the nearest porter downslope and turned to shout the order again—

—and the world came down.

She would tell people afterward that she remembered nothing of the next minute, and that was a lie she maintained for their comfort. She remembered everything. She remembered the crack running across the bowl like lightning finding ground. She remembered Sera, thirty meters up and left, turning—not running, turning, planting her axes, facing it, because Sera faced everything. She remembered the white wall taking her the way the sea takes a swimmer, the tumbling black-and-white chaos, the ice packing into her mouth, her ears, the pressure like the whole mountain's hand closing around her body. She remembered thinking, with absurd clarity, *so this is the arithmetic resolving itself*, and then nothing for a long time.

She woke buried to the chin, one arm free, in a silence so total it rang. The debris field ran eight hundred meters down the bowl. Of the seven others, they found five. Voss was dead. Both porters were dead. One of Voss's friends was dead. The photographer survived with a crushed leg. And Sera—

They found Sera last, on the fourth day, when the searchers had given up and Zephyrine had not, digging alone with her hands through the nights, frostbitten, half-mad, following some thread only she could feel. Sera was under two meters of set snow at the runout, in the pocket of air her raised arms had made, and she was alive, barely, and she lived—but her legs had been broken in the tumble and pinned so long in the cold that the surgeons took the left one below the knee, and the right one she kept but never trusted again.

A guide who cannot trust her legs. A dancer whose partner is a crutch. Zephyrine had done the arithmetic a thousand times since, in a thousand rented rooms, and it always came out the same: the mountain had taken its toll from everyone on the slope except the one person who had paid for the climb.

* * *

"The inquiry was a farce," she said to Ivo. The fog pressed against the deck; somewhere out in the estuary a buoy clanked its slow idiot bell. "That much was almost honest. Everyone knew it was a farce going in. Voss's family wanted a villain, the guild wanted its license fees protected, and the empire's magistrate—who turned out later to be Voss's wife's cousin, though that only surfaced after the verdict—wanted the whole thing quiet. I was the obvious candidate. Foreign-born, unlicensed in that canton, the surviving guide, the one who'd written the warning in a book that conveniently proved I *knew*. They didn't even have to twist it. The evidence convicted me all by itself: here is a woman who predicted the avalanche and led clients onto the slope anyway. What kinder word is there for that than criminal?"

"But you didn't lead them onto it," Ivo said. "You tried to stop it."

"Sera led. I followed. Which under guild law makes me responsible for every boot on that mountain, because a first guide who defers to a second guide has abdicated, and abdication is negligence." She laughed, short and ugly. "They had me on procedure. Isn't that always how it goes? Not the crime you actually committed—there wasn't one—but the paperwork of the crime they needed."

"So you appealed."

"There was no appeal worth the name. There was a hearing where I could speak and be ignored, and then there was the alternative." She stopped mending the line. Her hands had gone still, and she let them. "There was a second version of events. The true one, or truer. The pit tests were done jointly—I dug, Sera read the layers beside me. Her signature was on the assessment page. Her recommendation to proceed was verbal, private, and mine to report or withhold. If I put it in front of the magistrate—if I said *she overrode me, she chose to lead, the decision was hers*—it wouldn't have saved me. Guild law would still have hung it around my neck. But it would have hung it around hers too. Second-guide culpability, contributing negligence, license revoked, possibly charges—she'd broken the same procedures I had, and she'd done it while knowing more than any of them understood about what she was risking."

"She'd already lost her legs."

"She'd already lost her legs." Zephyrine heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used on bad ledges. "She was twenty-eight years old, lying in a charity ward in a country that wasn't hers, with one leg and a mother in a flood plain and nothing in the world but a reputation. And I could keep my reputation intact by spending hers. That was the trade on offer. My name or hers. The magistrate practically laid it out for me—he asked me, very gently, whether I wished to amend my testimony regarding the division of responsibility on the climb. He gave me the door and waited to see if I'd walk through it."

"And you didn't."

"And I didn't." She looked at her hands, at the rope coiled in her lap, at anything but Ivo's face. "I took the whole weight. I said the decision was mine, the error was mine, the leadership failure was mine. All technically true. All a lie in the way that matters. They struck me from the rolls, published my name in the guild bulletin, and recommended a five-year ban, which the magistrate extended indefinitely because Voss's family had influence in two countries and grudges in three. Two blacklists. One signature each. Cheapest ink I never spent."

The buoy clanked. Ivo said nothing for a long moment, and when he spoke his voice was low and roughened. "Does she know? Sera. Does she know what you did?"

"She knows I didn't testify against her. She's never known the rest—that the magistrate offered me the door, that walking through it would have cost her nothing more than she'd already lost, that I stood in that room and chose her over myself and have resented the choice in my worst hours ever since." Zephyrine finally looked up. "That's the part I don't tell people. Everyone loves a martyr. Nobody knows what to do with a martyr who keeps a ledger of what her sacrifice cost, and pays interest on the resentment, and hates herself for the interest."

"That's not resentment," Ivo said. "That's just grief with nowhere to go."

"Don't flatter me, archivist."

"I'm cataloguing," he said, and there was the ghost of his own terrible smile. "It's what I do with unbearable documents. I file them somewhere they can't hurt anyone until someone's ready to read them." He gathered the decoded pages, squared their edges, and did not look at her when he said: "Forty years ago I translated confiscation orders for the men who burned my country's archives. I read every document I destroyed—or filed, they'd say, filed beyond recovery—and I memorized what I could and told myself memory was resistance. Some nights I believe that. Most nights I know I cooperated, and that cooperation kept me alive and comfortable while my nation drowned, and that I have spent four decades building a shrine out of the excuse."

The fog thickened. Out in the dark the tide had begun its long turn, and Zephyrine felt it in the hull before she heard it—the faint shiver along the waterline, the world's slow machinery engaging. She rose, stiff, and began stowing the oilcloth and the pages, and her hands were steady now, steadier than they'd been all day.

"Why tell me this?" Ivo asked. "Tonight, of all nights. Before the dive."

"Because tomorrow we go into a cave full of drowned people to pull a truth out of a strongroom," she said, "and I won't take you somewhere like that carrying a secret. Secrets are ballast. You jettison them before deep water or they roll the boat." She paused at the companionway, one hand on the frame, and the lantern light caught the scar tissue along her jaw where the ice had scoured her on the Weißgrat—the mark she'd stopped explaining to strangers years ago. "Besides. You gave me your worst day. Fair trade. I don't sail with unpaid debts either."

"And the pardon?" he asked quietly. "If we surface with these documents, and the empire offers you everything they took away—"

"Then I'll finally learn," Zephyrine said, "whether I ever wanted it."

She went forward to check the anchor rode, and the fog swallowed her footsteps, and out in the black water the sixth tide was gathering itself to run.
