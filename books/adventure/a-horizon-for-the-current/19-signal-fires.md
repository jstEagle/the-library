## 19. Signal Fires

Ivo smelled the smoke before he saw it.

He had been below decks in the *Cormorant*'s cramped cabin, hunched over the decoded pages with a stub of pencil, transcribing names into his own hand because memory alone had begun to feel like a sieve with a hole worn in it by forty years of grief. The names ran down the page like a litany: Vessani ministers, harbor captains, the treasurer's own sister. People the empire had put aboard the evacuation fleet knowing exactly where that fleet would end up.

Then the smell reached him—woodsmoke, but wrong for the hour. Zephyrine did not cook at midday. Zephyrine barely cooked at all.

He came up the companionway ladder two rungs at a time and stopped dead on deck.

She was standing at the little iron stove bolted near the stern, feeding it green boughs from a bundle she must have cut at their last anchorage. Wet leaves hissed and spat, and the smoke that rolled off it was thick and white and utterly unmistakable—a column climbing straight up out of the cove where they'd sheltered overnight, visible, he judged, for five miles in every direction.

"Zephyrine." His voice came out flat with alarm. "What are you doing?"

"Reading the water." She didn't turn. Her eyes were fixed on the mouth of the cove, where the Sable Current ran silver-green between black headlands. "There's a cutter standing off the point. Been there since dawn, hull-down, sails just showing. It's been holding station against the tide, which no fishing boat does, and it's flying no colors, which no honest ship does." She fed another wet branch to the stove. "They've seen our smoke from last night's fire. I'm making sure they see plenty more."

"That's—" He groped for the word. "That's madness. You're calling them to us."

"I'm telling them a story." Now she turned, and there was something in her face he had learned to read over the past days: not fear, not bravado, but the cold bright pleasure of a woman doing the one thing she was unquestionably great at. "A story about two fools who panicked when they realized they'd been seen. Fools who are going to bolt up the coast in broad daylight, exactly where a fast cutter can run them down in open water."

"And what will we actually do?"

Her smile was thin as a knife's edge. "The opposite of everything they can see."

* * *

They spent an hour preparing the deception, and Ivo discovered that watching Zephyrine Pemberton work a deception was like watching a Vessani calligrapher lay ink—the economy of it, the certainty, not one motion wasted or repeated.

She rigged the anchor buoy with a slow-burning oil lamp under a scrap of tarred canvas, so that come dusk the cutter's glass would find a light bobbing where the *Cormorant* ought to be. She soaked a spare sail in lamp oil and folded it into the bilge beneath a layer of fish-netting, positioned so that if anyone boarded and rummaged carelessly, they'd find evidence of a crew planning to burn something—or flee burning. She even scattered ash from the stove across the deck boards in a pattern Ivo didn't understand until she explained it, almost gently, as though to a child.

"Boarding parties look at galleys first. A galley that's been used hard and hurried says a crew left in a panic. A clean galley says a crew left in control. We want them reading panic."

"You've done this before," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Twice." She said it without pride. "Once off the Verlaine coast, once in the mountain passes, which is a stranger trick than you'd think. Fire lies well in fog country. Fog forgives all liars." She glanced at the sky, where a gray ceiling was lowering itself over the headlands with the unhurried confidence of something that owned the place. "And we're about to have more fog than either of us knows what to do with. That's our luck. Don't thank it yet."

Ivo stood at the rail while she worked, and let himself feel the thing he had been refusing to feel since the tide-market: that he trusted her. Not completely—he was too old and too damaged for completeness—but in the specific way that mattered. He trusted her hands. He trusted her arithmetic of survival. When she said *the opposite of everything they can see*, some part of him that had been clenched since childhood simply unclenched and believed her.

It frightened him more than the cutter did.

* * *

The fog came down an hour before dusk, rolling off the Current in banks so dense that the far side of the cove dissolved into nothing. Zephyrine waited until the last usable light, then took the *Cormorant* out on the ebb—not north up the coast, where the cutter would expect flight, but south, back toward the fleet's anchorage, close-hauled along a line of broken water that made Ivo's stomach climb into his throat.

"There's a shoal there," he said, staring at the white streaks in the darkening water.

"There are three shoals there. We're threading between the second and third." She had the tiller lashed to her knee and both hands free, working the jib sheet by feel. "The cutter will have to stand off to the east to catch anyone running north. By the time they realize we went south, we'll be inside the estuary."

"The estuary." He knew the name from her charts—the Miren Estuary, a drowned river valley that opened onto the Current through a maze of sandbars and tidal flats. "You told me yourself no charts of it are accurate. That it shifts with every storm."

"It does." Her teeth showed white in the gloom. "That's the entire point, Ivo. Their charts show three channels. There are seven. Two of them exist only at half-flood, and one of them exists only because a gravel bar migrated in the winter four years ago and nobody has lived long enough and foolish enough to re-survey it." She looked over at him, and in the dimness her eyes held the same light he'd seen in the cavern approach, in the white water, every time the sea offered her something impossible. "I am that foolish, and I have lived long enough."

The cutter found them anyway.

Of course it did. Ivo understood later that it had never been about the smoke at all—that Corvane's patrol boats would have been sweeping the coast regardless, and that a small ketch beating south toward the fleet's anchorage was precisely the silhouette their officers had been ordered to hunt. The first warning was a lantern winking at the edge of the fog bank, twice, then twice again: a signal passed from some picket they'd never seen. The second warning was the sound—a low, resonant boom rolling across the water, a signal gun, and then, faintly, a bell.

Zephyrine went very still at the tiller.

"They'll send the cutter in after us," Ivo said. "The estuary's mouth is wide enough for—"

"For them, yes. If they know where the channels are." She was already moving, dropping the main to a scrap of storm canvas, slacking the jib until the *Cormorant* slowed to a crawl that would read, from a distance, like a vessel blundering blind. "But they don't. And they're about to make the mistake every navy makes with an estuary. They'll assume the chart is the territory."

"How long do we have?"

"A quarter hour, perhaps less. They'll come in fast because they think we're slow." She pointed the tiller toward a wall of fog that looked identical to every other wall of fog. "Hold this course exactly. Not a degree off. What you're about to see is going to look like a mistake. It isn't."

* * *

What he saw, over the next hour, rearranged something in his understanding of her permanently.

The *Cormorant* slid into the fog and the world contracted to a sphere perhaps thirty feet across: the bow, the jib's pale ghost, the black water sliding past, and Zephyrine—always Zephyrine, motionless at the tiller except for those small corrections that seemed to anticipate the boat's needs before the boat itself knew them. Ivo stood forward as lookout, though lookout was a generous word; he could see nothing, and reported nothing, and felt useless in a way that gnawed at him.

She navigated by ear. That was the first impossible thing. He heard only fog and water and, once, the distant clang of the cutter's bell somewhere behind them. But she tilted her head at intervals like a bird, listening to the way the current spoke against unseen banks, and adjusted course by fractions.

"Gravel bar to starboard," she murmured at one point, so quietly he almost missed it. "Hear it? The water stutters. Old channel's to port, but it silts out in fifty yards. Trust the stutter."

He learned to listen. That was the second thing. By the third mile he could hear it himself—the faint broken rhythm where current dragged across shallows, the deeper hush of the true channel—and when he called a change of sound before she acknowledged it, she glanced at him with an expression he decided not to name, because naming it would have required them both to stop working.

Behind them, the cutter blundered.

They heard it happen rather than saw it: a sudden increase in engine strain—she had a steam auxiliary, then, which meant speed they could never have outrun in open water—then a grinding, tearing shudder that carried across the flats like the groan of a dying animal, followed by shouting, many voices, and the frantic ringing of the bell.

"Sanded on the Middle Bar," Zephyrine said, without satisfaction or surprise. "Just where I wanted them. They'll float on the next flood, but they'll float with a bent keel and a captain who's lost his nerve." She allowed herself one breath of something that might have been laughter. "Now comes the part I actually enjoy."

* * *

The decoy fires were her idea, and Ivo's hands built them.

She anchored the *Cormorant* in the lee of a mudflat island—anchored properly, with two hooks set and a tripping line, because she anchored the way she did everything, redundantly—and they rowed the tender into the fog with a bundle of supplies: oil, tarred canvas, dry kindling wrapped in waxcloth, and two tin lanterns whose glass she painted with mud.

The first fire they set on the northernmost sandbar, a low mound of drying spartina grass that burned hot and orange and threw a wavering glow through the fog like a ship's lights beyond the shallows. The second went on a stake-driven platform in the eastern channel, rigged to flare when the rising tide lifted a cork float and pulled a friction match-cord taut. The third—

"The third is the lie that matters," she said, crouched in the tender, her face smeared with soot and lit orange by the burning grass behind them. "Two fires say *we split up*. Three fires say *there are more of us than you thought*. A navy that thinks it's hunting smugglers will always assume a gang. They'll spread their boats thin looking for a crew that doesn't exist." She struck the match herself, touched it to the cord, and watched the flame take with the tenderness other people reserved for children. "By morning they'll have reports of fires on six different bars. Some of those reports will be real. None of them will be us."

Rowing back through the fog, Ivo found himself laughing—quietly, helplessly, a sound he hadn't made in years and barely recognized. She looked at him across the thwarts with genuine curiosity, oar blades dripping.

"What?"

"My father," he said. "Before Vessany fell. He kept a shop that sold surveying instruments, and once a year he'd take me out on the bay and teach me to read the channel markers. He used to say—" The laugh caught in his throat and turned into something else, something older. "He used to say the sea keeps honest books. That no one cheats the water for long." He shook his head. "You cheat it constantly. You've made a life of cheating it. And it carries you anyway."

For a moment she said nothing, and the fog pressed close around the tender, and the only sounds were the oar-locks and the distant confusion of the grounded cutter.

"Your father wasn't wrong," she said finally. "The sea keeps honest books. I don't cheat it. I *read* it—faster and more honestly than the people chasing me do. There's a difference." She shipped the oars as the *Cormorant*'s dark shape loomed out of the murk ahead. "The empire writes its books in ink and assumes ink is permanent. The water rewrites its books every tide. You have to stay humble enough to keep rereading." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "That's the whole secret. That's the only secret there is."

* * *

They cleared the estuary on the midnight flood, ghosting out through the seventh channel—the one that existed only because of a migrated gravel bar—while behind them the fog glowed with decoy fires and the cutter sat stranded on the Middle Bar like a beetle on its back, its boats scattered across the flats chasing lights that had already gone out.

By dawn they were ten miles south, running easy under a gray morning sky with the Current's swell lifting the hull in long, patient swells. Zephyrine slept in snatches at the tiller, waking fully each time the wind shifted, and Ivo sat in the bow with the decoded pages wrapped in oilskin against his chest, watching the coast slide past.

He should have felt triumphant. Instead he felt the weight of the unfinished page pressing through the oilskin like a hand.

Because the documents still had their gap. Nine pages decoded, and the ninth ended mid-sentence—instructions referencing a final order, a complete accounting, sealed aboard the evacuation fleet's flagship and stored, per the fragmentary text, *"in the captain's strongroom, keyed to the admiral's private cipher."* Everything else was corroboration. That page, if it existed, was the confession itself: the signature, the treasury, the deliberate drowning of a nation's government and gold in a single ordered act.

And it lay forty feet down in the wreck they were sailing toward, reachable—if Zephyrine's grim charts of the inner caverns were to be believed—only through passages that had killed every diver who'd attempted them, and only at slack tide, and only within a window measured in minutes.

Zephyrine woke as the sun cleared the haze, read his face in one glance, and read the oilskin packet in the next.

"You're thinking about the strongroom," she said.

"I'm thinking that yesterday I watched you navigate a drowned river by ear, burn a navy's patience to ash with three tin lanterns, and steal a night from Admiral Corvane that he will spend the rest of his career trying to buy back." He met her eyes. "And I'm thinking that none of it matters unless we go into the inner dark and come back out with that page."

She was quiet for a long moment, watching the water. Then she reached into her coat and produced a folded scrap of paper—old, soft with handling, marked with a diver's charm pressed flat as a coin—and held it out to him.

"The old woman at the colony gave me this," she said. "And a warning about the inner caverns that I haven't told you yet, because I was hoping I wouldn't need to." Her jaw tightened. "Slack tide at the wreck is in two days. We'll have one window. One." She turned the *Cormorant*'s bow south, toward the fleet, toward the reef, toward the drowned cathedral and whatever waited beneath it. "So eat something, Ivo. And sleep if you can. Because tomorrow I'm going to tell you why everyone who's ever dived the inner dark has died—and then we're going to dive it anyway."
