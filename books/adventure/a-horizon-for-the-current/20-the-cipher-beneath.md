## 20. The Cipher Beneath

The lamp guttered twice before Ivo admitted it was dying.

He had been hunched over the chart table in the Cormorant's cramped cabin for six hours, long enough that his back had learned the shape of the bench and his eyes had learned to read by the amber smear of whale oil. Around him the ketch creaked at her moorings in the fog-bound estuary where Zephyrine had lost the patrol cutter two nights past—a channel she navigated by memory of a sandbar's shadow, she'd said, as if that were a normal thing for a person to say. Outside, the tide was turning. He could hear it in the way the hull's complaints changed pitch, the water sliding from ebb to flood with a sound like a great animal shifting its weight in sleep.

On the table lay the last document.

It was the smallest of them all—a single leaf, water-stained amber at its edges, recovered from the iron chest that had started everything. The other pages had yielded to him over the weeks of their flight: the scuttling order, the manifest of the drowned, the treasury ledger with its damning column of figures. Each had been written in the Vessani naval cipher, the one called *kellim-var*, the deep-speech, and each had surrendered because the cipher was built on a rotating substitution keyed to a phrase—and Ivo had known the phrase. Every archivist of the Vessani diaspora knew it. It was the first line of the Lamentation, the poem mothers still whispered to children who had never seen the homeland: *The sea keeps what the king could not.*

But this last page refused him.

He had tried the Lamentation. He had tried the naval hymn, the coronation anthem, the names of the nine tides as Vessany counted them. He had tried his own name, in grief or vanity, and the name of the woman asleep in the bow compartment whose breathing he could hear through the bulkhead when the wind dropped. Nothing. The characters came apart under every key and reassembled into nonsense, patient and impenetrable, like a door with no lock—only a shape that wanted a very particular hand.

He rubbed his eyes and looked at the page again, really looked, the way he had taught himself to look at confiscated documents in the imperial archive at Grayharbor, where he had spent eleven years cataloguing the paper corpse of his nation while clerks with clean fingernails decided which memories were worth preserving and which were fuel for the winter stoves.

There. In the lower margin, so faint he might have imagined it: a second layer of ink beneath the first. Not a correction. A notation. The old Vessani shorthand used by ships' captains for private addenda—the same shorthand his grandfather had used on the backs of receipts, the shorthand Ivo had learned at nine years old sitting on a dock in exile while an old man pointed at fish crates and made him write their Vessani names until his fingers cramped.

His heart began to work harder. He tilted the lamp, caught the margin at an angle, and read.

*Key held aboard. Strongroom. Captain's hand only. If this reaches shore without me, ask the Current.*

Ivo sat back so fast the bench scraped the deck above him like a scream, and he froze, listening, but Zephyrine's breathing did not change.

*Key held aboard.* Of course. Of course it was. He almost laughed. The captain of the evacuation fleet—Admiral-Captain Sarel Voss, whose name appeared on the manifest in the third position, third position because the man had gone down with his ship rather than abandon the treasury he now knew the empire meant to steal—the captain had done what any Vessani officer of the old school would have done. He had not trusted the cipher to survive capture. He had kept the true key physically separate, locked in the strongroom of his own vessel, guarded by forty feet of black water and forty years of silence.

Which meant the last document—the one that named, in full, the officer who signed the scuttling order, the one Ivo needed more than he needed breath—was a locked box, and the key lay in a dead man's safe at the bottom of the Sable Current.

He heard the hatch before he saw her. Zephyrine moved quietly for a woman of her size; it was one of the first things he'd noticed about her, the way she occupied space without announcing it, a quality he recognized because he had spent his whole life doing the opposite. She came down the companionway with her hair still damp from the evening watch and a look on her face that meant she'd already read something in the weather or the water or his posture—he never knew which, and she never told him.

"You found something," she said. Not a question.

"The last page is keyed differently." He turned the leaf toward her. "The captain kept the real key in his strongroom. On the wreck."

She stood over the table, arms folded, reading the marginal note twice. Her face did what it always did with bad news: nothing. It was maddening, and he had come to understand it was also armor, and that understanding it did not make it less maddening.

"So we're back to the beginning," she said. "The chest wasn't the prize. It was the invitation."

"It was the invitation," Ivo agreed. "And the strongroom is the answer."

"And the strongroom is under the fleet."

"Under the fleet." He spread the chart he'd been annotating all evening—the Sable Current roadstead, with the nine ships of Admiral Corvane's salvage flotilla marked in red ink like a rash across the anchorage. "Through the inner caverns. At slack tide."

Zephyrine was quiet for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her voice had the flatness she used for arithmetic. "You've been listening to me talk about the inner caverns for two weeks. Tell me what you think you heard."

"That everyone believes they're a death trap."

"They are a death trap. That's not superstition, Ivo, that's hydrology. The outer caverns are bad enough—we nearly died in them once already, and you were there, you can stop pretending you weren't frightened." She tapped the chart where the reef line curled around the drowned fleet's resting place like a closed fist. "The inner galleries run beneath the wreck itself. Flooded, most of them. The ones that aren't flooded fill and empty with the tide through channels no survey has ever mapped, because the surveyors who tried didn't come back. My grandmother fished these waters. She drew me three of those channels from memory, once, on the back of a flour sack, and she made me swear never to dive them. Slack tide gives you maybe twenty minutes where the water stops trying to kill you. Twenty minutes, in the dark, under a reef that Corvane is currently blowing apart piece by piece."

"Then we have a problem of arithmetic," Ivo said, "not of possibility."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the expression she wore when he surprised her. "Go on, archivist. Do your sums."

He pulled the tide tables toward him, the ones he'd copied out in his precise archive hand during the storm days when there was nothing else to do but watch rain hammer the deck and feel the clock of the fleet grinding down. "Corvane's dredges have been working the outer reef since the storm. You said yourself he'll breach the cavern system within days whether he means to or not. When he does, the currents change. Whatever channels exist today won't exist next week. So the window isn't just slack tide." He looked up at her. "It's the next slack tide. Or the one after. Two windows, and then the geography itself closes."

"Two tides," Zephyrine said.

"One day. Perhaps thirty hours, if the weather holds, which it won't—it's building in the southwest, I can smell it, and so can you, don't pretend otherwise."

"I wasn't going to pretend otherwise." She pulled out the opposite bench and sat, heavily, the way she did when she'd stopped performing competence and started calculating. This, too, he had learned to read: the difference between Zephyrine-the-guide, who was all certainty and swagger, and Zephyrine-at-work, who was silent and cold and honest. He preferred the second one, though he'd have died before telling her so. "Twenty minutes of slack. Dive time through the inner galleries to reach the strongroom—call it fifteen if the visibility is anything better than soup, forty if it's not. Time to find the strongroom, open it, get the key. Time to get out before the flood starts pulling everything back into the dark." She exhaled through her nose. "It's tight. It's the kind of tight that kills people who hesitate."

"Then we won't hesitate."

"You say that like it's a decision instead of a hope."

"It's both. Most important things are."

She looked at him then, really looked, and Ivo felt the familiar dangerous warmth in his chest that he had been carefully not naming for three weeks. There was seawater drying in the lines around her eyes and a bruise on her jaw from the night chase through the shoals, and she was the most competent person he had ever met, and she was about to risk her life in a flooded cave for documents belonging to a country that no longer existed, and he could not decide whether that made him responsible for her death or witness to something holy.

"Why?" she asked suddenly. "You've never actually said it straight out. The empire will fall eventually, empires always do. The survivors are scattered and aging. You could take your copies, settle somewhere the imperial writ doesn't run, live to be old. Why does this page matter enough to die in the dark for?"

Because my grandfather helped them do it, Ivo thought. Because cooperation has a price that compounds like interest across generations. Because I have spent my entire adult life handling the ashes of my nation and calling it duty, and I am tired of ashes.

What he said was: "Because the dead were lied about. And a lie that goes unchallenged long enough becomes the truth, and then it becomes policy, and then it becomes the reason the next fleet gets scuttled. I'm an archivist. Challenging lies is the whole of my profession. Everything else is filing."

Zephyrine held his gaze for a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then she nodded, once, and turned to the chart, and the moment passed the way their moments always passed—unspoken, banked, stored somewhere against a colder night.

"All right," she said. "Here's how it works, if we do this. We go in on the next slack, not the one after—I want a margin for error, and the storm will eat whatever margin we don't spend. We approach from the north channel, the one my grandmother called the Throat, because it narrows to boat-width and runs fast even at slack. We anchor the Cormorant inside the first gallery, in the air pocket, and we dive from there. Lamp each. Counted breaths—you breathe when I tell you to breathe, and you surface when I tell you to surface, and if I signal up and you're not beside me in ten counts, you go up anyway and you don't come back down. Are those terms acceptable?"

"They're acceptable."

"Say them back."

He said them back. She made him say the last one twice.

* * *

They spent the next day preparing, and Ivo discovered that watching Zephyrine prepare for danger was its own education.

She worked in silence mostly, checking and rechecking with the economy of someone who had learned preparation as a survival trait rather than a virtue. She stripped the Cormorant to essentials—water, the document case wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas, the salvaged diving gear she'd been repairing since the storm, two lamps with fresh wicks, a spare knife lashed inside each of their jackets in case of fouled lines. She coiled rope the way other people prayed. Once, finding him watching her, she tossed him a length of line without looking. "Tie me a bowline that'll hold a drowning man."

He tied it. She inspected it, grunted—which he had learned was high praise—and threw it back. "Again. Eyes shut."

By the fourth repetition his fingers knew the knot without his mind's help, and he understood the lesson: in the dark, under pressure, with lungs burning, there would be no room for thinking. Only for hands that remembered.

In the afternoon he climbed to the headland alone, ostensibly to check the weather, actually because he needed to see the fleet.

From the ridge above the estuary, through the thinning haze, he could see them: nine ships at anchor over the grave of Vessany's evacuation fleet, their dredge booms swinging like slow pendulums, the thud of their work carrying across the water as a vibration felt in the chest more than heard. Smoke rose from the flagship's galley funnel, domestic and obscene. Somewhere on that quarterdeck was Admiral Corvane, and Ivo had spent enough evenings with the decoded pages to know what Corvane wanted and why: not treasure, though the treasury would be welcome. Erasure. The last physical proof that his father's generation had murdered a nation in cold water, sitting forty feet below his keel, waiting for a dredge or a diver or a collapsing reef to set it free.

They're not salvaging, Ivo thought. They're excavating a crime scene to destroy the evidence.

He stayed on the headland until the light went copper, memorizing the fleet's disposition the way he memorized everything—because memory was the one archive no clerk could confiscate. Then he went back down to the boat, where Zephyrine had laid out the charts of the inner caverns: her grandmother's flour-sack drawings, copied fair, annotated in two hands now, hers and his.

"We should rehearse the signals again," she said, without looking up.

"We rehearsed them this morning."

"Again."

They rehearsed them again. Rope-tug once: advance. Twice: hold. Three sharp: surface, immediately, no discussion. A long steady pull: follow my line out, trust the line, not your sense of direction, because in a flooded cave your sense of direction is a liar with a pleasant voice. He had asked her, early on, how she knew so much about diving caves when her reputation was built on mountains, and she had given him one of her sidelong looks and said, "Water runs downhill, Ivo. Mountains are just where it waits." He suspected there was a story underneath that answer, the way there was a story under most of her answers, sealed like the strongroom itself, waiting for a tide to open it.

Evening came down gray and smelling of the coming storm. They ate salt pork and hard bread at the chart table, the last document between them under its oilcloth, and Ivo found himself unable to keep his hands off it, lifting the corner, looking at the stubborn cipher, putting it down again.

"It'll keep," Zephyrine said.

"It's kept forty years."

"Then it'll keep one more day." But she reached over and put her hand flat on the oilcloth, over the hidden words, as if steadying them—or being steadied by them. Her hand was close to his. Neither of them moved away. Outside, the first spit of the storm ticked against the cabin roof like fingertips.

"I keep thinking about the captain," Ivo said quietly. "Voss. Third name on the manifest. He must have known, at the end, what the order meant. He locked the key in his strongroom knowing the ship was going down, knowing that someday—" He stopped. Started again. "Knowing that someday someone would come asking. He left a message in the margin. *Ask the Current.* Forty years ago, a drowning man wrote instructions for a stranger he'd never meet, and trusted the sea to deliver them."

Zephyrine was silent a moment. "That's either faith or arrogance."

"In Vessany they were the same thing."

That earned him the ghost-smile again, brief as a fish rising. She took her hand off the oilcloth and stood, stretching, joints cracking. "Sleep. Both of us. Slack comes at midday tomorrow, and I want us in the Throat with hours to spare, because if the storm speeds up we may be diving in worse than slack, and I'd rather be early and bored than late and dead."

She went aft to her bunk. Ivo sat a while longer with the lamp turned low, listening to the storm arrive in earnest, the wind finding the estuary mouth and moaning down it like something searching.

Two tides, he thought. One window. A dead captain's key at the bottom of the world, and a living admiral determined that no one should ever turn it.

He thought of the wall inscriptions in the drowned cathedral, the refugees' last testament he had read aloud while Zephyrine held the lamp: *We were here. Remember us rightly.* Not *remember us*—*remember us rightly*. As if they had known, even then, that remembrance could be falsified, that memory itself had enemies with dredges and admirals' commissions.

Tomorrow, at slack water, he would go down into the dark and try to keep that promise.

He slept badly, and woke before dawn to Zephyrine shaking his shoulder, and her face in the lamplight told him the news before she said it.

"The storm's turned," she said. "It's coming faster than it should. Which means the fleet will speed up their blasting to finish before it hits—and our slack tide just got shorter." She was already moving, throwing back the hatch cover, letting in the smell of rain and iodine and urgency. "Get up, archivist. The window's opening early. We sail now."
