## 21. Confession at Low Water

The tide went out and kept going out, as if the sea had decided to take everything with it and think better of returning.

Zephyrine sat on the shingle above the exposed flats of the estuary, her back against the Cormorant's hull where the ketch lay canted on the mud like a beached bird, and watched the water retreat past the second sandbar, past the third, past the black ribs of a wreck so old no one remembered its name. The channel they needed would not refill for eleven hours. The window into the inner caverns opened at slack water after that—two tides away, as she'd told Ivo, though telling him had been easier than living inside the waiting.

Waiting was the worst country. She had crossed every kind of terrain the Verlaine coast could offer, and none of it was as hostile as an empty afternoon with nothing to do but think.

Ivo came down from the headland with two gulls wheeling over him like escorts he hadn't asked for. He carried driftwood in his arms and a question in his face; she'd learned to read both by now. He stacked the wood beside the small iron stove they'd rigged on the stones, knelt, and struck flint until the kindling caught.

"You've been quiet since dawn," he said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always *occupied*. There's a difference." He settled onto the shingle beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, not quite. "When you're occupied you move like water finding its level. Today you keep looking at the flats like they owe you money."

A laugh escaped her before she could stop it—short, surprised, real. The fire cracked and spat. Somewhere inland a curlew called its thin descending note, and the light was turning the color of old brass, and she understood, with a sensation like a knot slipping loose, that she was going to tell him. Not because he'd earned it, exactly. Because the waiting had made a room around them, and rooms were dangerous things, and this one had a fire in it.

"You asked me once," she said, "why I never stay anywhere past three days."

"I didn't ask. I noticed."

"You noticed. That's worse." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "It's not a rule because I'm clever. It's a rule because I'm a coward."

He said nothing. He was good at that—she'd learned that too. He let silence do the work of questions.

"Eight years ago," she began, "I guided in the Serrezine Alps. High passes, glacier routes, the kind of clients who pay in gold and complain about the wine." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for reading currents. "My best client was a man named Aldous Ferrant. Mining money. He'd climbed with me three seasons. He trusted me the way rich men trust things they've bought—which is to say completely, and only as far as it suited him.

"The fourth season, we were taking the Col de Vaire route in late spring. I read the snowpack the way I read water now. It's the same skill, if you want the truth of it. Layers under load, a weak stratum, warm days and cold nights building stress. I told him we should wait four days. Four days for the pack to settle or the weather to break the layers apart. I told him plainly. I drew him the diagram in the snow."

"And he didn't listen."

"He'd summited twice before with lesser guides," Zephyrine said. "In his mind, that made him the expert and me the staff. And there was—" She stopped. The curlew called again, farther off. "There was someone else on that expedition. My friend. My—" She chose the word the way you choose a foothold. "Her name was Sera. We'd come up through the guide schools together, shared tents, shared wages, shared everything except the one thing that mattered, which is that I loved her and she knew it and neither of us ever said it aloud because saying it would have required deciding something, and we were both very good at not deciding.

"Sera wanted the crossing done. Her father was ill back in Verlaine City; she needed the fee, and she needed to be home, and she looked at me across that morning camp and said, *Zeph, you're being careful for no reason. You're always careful.*" Zephyrine's jaw tightened. "And I—"

She had never said the next part aloud. Not to the inquiry board, not to the priest who'd offered absolution at the border chapel, not to herself in the dark hours when the memory came with its teeth out.

"I let her voice outweigh my own reading. One morning. One vote. I told myself the margin was wider than it was. I told myself the snow would hold because Sera believed it would, because wanting her to be right felt safer than insisting I was." She stared at the fire. "We crossed at midday. The release came at the serac band, two hours later. It took Ferrant and two porters and it missed Sera by thirty meters, and everyone lived, and that's the part people don't understand. Nobody died. That's what saved me and damned me at once.

"Ferrant was furious. Humiliated. A man like that can't have been wrong, so the guide must have been negligent—or worse, mercenary, rushing the route to pocket an extra fee. His family had reach. The inquiry was going to strip my certification and Sera's both, because she'd countersigned my route assessment. Her signature was on the same page as mine. Her father was dying. If she lost her license, she lost everything—the work, the income, the ability to get home to him with anything to show for eight years."

"So you signed the fault alone."

"I wrote a statement saying I'd misjudged the pack, that I'd overridden caution for speed, that the decision was mine entire. Sera fought me on it for two days. She cried, actually, which I'd never seen her do. And then her father worsened, and she stopped fighting, and I let her stop." Zephyrine turned her hands over, palms up, as if the evidence might still be written there. "They blacklisted me in Verlaine. The neighboring republic followed their lead—the guilds share their grudges the way harbors share water. Two countries. Eight years. And here's the thing I've never told anyone, Ivo, the thing that keeps the three-day rule alive: I didn't do it only for her. I did it because if I'd insisted on my own judgment—if I'd stood there and said *no, we wait*—then the disaster that didn't happen would have been mine to own too. Taking the blame for the one that did was easier than facing what it meant that I'd traded my certainty for her approval. The lie I told the board was kinder than the truth. To them *and* to me."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Ivo was watching her with an expression she couldn't name—not pity, thank God, she'd have walked into the estuary rather than wear pity—but something more like recognition, the look of a man seeing his own house in a foreign country.

"You predicted it," he said quietly. "You were right. And you've spent eight years paying for being right in the wrong way."

"Paying for being a coward about being right." She shook her head. "Don't dress it up. I've had eight years of dressing it up and it never fits."

* * *

The tide reached its lowest ebb as full dark came down. The stars came out hard and cold over the flats, and the exposed seabed gleamed faintly, ribbed and alien, a landscape that existed for only a few hours in any day and belonged to no one. Ivo fed the fire again and then, instead of sitting back down, stood looking at it for a long moment with his hands in his pockets.

"You gave me your worst day," he said. "It seems discourteous not to match it."

"You don't have to."

"No," he agreed. "But I'm going to, because you'll sleep better, and so will I, and tomorrow we dive into caves that kill people, and I find I'd rather do it without carrying this." He sat down slowly. "You know I cooperated with the occupation. I told you that much, the first night aboard. What I didn't tell you is what cooperation *meant*, in practice, in the year after the sinking."

Zephyrine waited.

"I was nineteen. My father was the deputy keeper of the Vessani royal archive—you know this. When the empire annexed the coast, the archive wasn't burned. Burning is wasteful. They *confiscated* it, all of it, forty thousand documents, and they requisitioned the archivists to catalog what they'd stolen. My father refused. They took him away in a cart on a Tuesday morning, and on Wednesday a man in an imperial uniform put a ledger in front of me and said, *Your father's position is open.*"

The fire popped. Ivo's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse.

"I sat down at my father's desk," he said, "in my father's chair, and I catalogued my nation's papers for the men who had drowned it. For eleven months. I told myself I was protecting the documents—keeping them ordered, keeping them dry, keeping track of what was taken where, so that someday, someday, they could be recovered. And some of that was even true. But here is my worst day, Zephyrine. Here is the day I mean when I say worst.

"A woman came to the archive. A widow from the fishing quarter whose husband had been a harbor master—Vessani naval records, proof of his rank, proof of his pension rights, proof he'd existed. Under occupation law, imperial clerks could simply declare unregistered persons nonexistent, and then their property reverted to the crown. Her husband's house was already gone. She needed the record of his service to contest the seizure of his boat, the last thing she owned. She'd found out I was Vessani. Everyone knew what I was; that was partly the point of giving me the desk. She knelt—she *knelt*, in the reading room—and begged me to pull one file. One file, from a shelf I could touch with my hand.

"And I looked at her, and I calculated." His voice finally broke its level line, just slightly, like a keel touching bottom. "Because helping her meant marking myself. Because the section chief watched me. Because I had a system, a long plan, a someday, and one widow's boat was not worth the someday. I told her the file was lost in the transfer. I watched her understand that I was lying, and watch her understand *why* I was lying, and I watched her decide I wasn't even worth hating. She bowed to me. She thanked me for my time. And she left, and I went back to my cataloguing, and I have never found out what happened to her, and I have looked, Zephyrine. In every port, in every colony list, in every register the diaspora keeps. Forty years of looking for one woman whose name I never even learned properly, because learning it would have made refusing her harder, and I knew that, and I didn't learn it anyway."

The estuary lay silent around them. Far out on the flats, water was beginning to whisper back into the lowest channels—the sea reconsidering, patient as arithmetic.

"They broke you the way they break everyone," Zephyrine said at last. "Nineteen years old, your father in a cart, a uniform across the desk. That's not a choice, Ivo. That's a trap with the door painted open."

"It was both." He shook his head. "That's what I need you to understand, because you'll want to forgive me and forgiveness is another way of not looking. It was a trap, *and* I chose. Both things are true, and I spent forty years hiding inside the first truth so I wouldn't have to carry the second. Same as you." He glanced at her, and there was no accusation in it, only the terrible gentleness of a man handling his own wound in front of witnesses. "You hid inside the blame you took. I hid inside the blame I escaped. We're both very well hidden, aren't we? Two people who've made careers out of not being seen."

Zephyrine laughed—a raw sound, half grief. "Three days," she said. "Never longer than three days anywhere. Do you know why three? Because that's how long it takes people to start asking questions. Where are you from. Why did you leave. What happened to your hands, your accent, your name. Three days and then I'm gone, before anyone gets close enough to see whatever it is they'd see." She looked at him across the fire. "I've been running from a morning on a mountain for eight years, and you've been running from a reading room for forty, and we sailed out of that harbor together in under an hour of knowing each other. I've been trying to work out why."

"Have you?"

"I think it's because you looked at my charts the way you look at those documents. Like the truth in them mattered more than the danger of holding it." She fed a stick of driftwood to the fire and watched the salt flare green. "Everyone else looks at my charts and sees a tool. You looked at them and saw a testament."

* * *

The tide turned fully in the smallest hours, and they sat and watched the sea come home—channel by channel, bar by bar, the flats drowning in reverse until the Cormorant lifted on her mooring lines and sighed and remembered she was a ship. Neither of them slept. Neither of them suggested sleeping. Something had been built between them tonight, quietly, out of confessed ruin, and it seemed important not to leave it unattended.

"I keep thinking about Sera," Zephyrine said eventually. "Whether she knows. Whether she's seen my name on the lists all these years and said nothing. Whether she's dead. I don't even know if she's dead." She wrapped her arms tighter around her knees. "When this is over—when the documents are wherever they're going—I think I'll find out. Whatever the answer is. I think eight years of not knowing costs more than any answer could."

"That's the first time you've said *when this is over* instead of *if*," Ivo observed.

She hadn't noticed. She turned it over in her mind, testing it, the way she'd test new ice. He was right. Somewhere between the caverns and the fire and the confessions, the mission had stopped being a gamble she might survive and become a thing she intended to finish.

"Slack water's in nine hours," she said. "Inner caverns. Strongroom. One window, and Corvane's dredges eating the outer reef every hour he can." She nodded toward the dark water, where the current was running strong now, tugging at the ketch's lines with insistent fingers. "Get some rest, archivist. Tomorrow I need your eyes and your cipher-head, and I need that breath of yours slow, because the inner dark doesn't forgive fast hearts."

Ivo rose, paused, and looked down at her with the firelight moving on his face. "Zephyrine. The widow from the archive. If I ever find her name, I'm going to say it out loud in a crowded place. That's not penance. Penance is private. It's going to be a correction of the record." He smiled faintly. "I learned that from you. Records matter. So do the people in them."

He went aft to his bunk beneath the stern sheets. Zephyrine stayed by the dying fire, watching the channel fill, watching the moon climb, and it was only when she finally closed her eyes that she saw it—a smudge of orange low on the southern horizon, beyond the headland, where no fisherman burned lamps and no village stood.

Not stars. Not dawn. Dawn was hours away and would come from the east.

She stood up slowly, every muscle in her back suddenly awake, and counted the lights. Three. Four. Moving in formation along the coast road, steady as a convoy.

Signal fires. Someone was burning the night open behind them—and by their bearing, they marked the headland directly above the wreck site.

Corvane had found the smoke of their passage, or the broker had sold their course, or both. Either way, the window she'd been counting on had just begun to close early.
