## 23. Into the Inner Dark

The sea went still at the turn of the tide, and in that stillness there was no mercy—only a door that would not stay open long.

Zephyrine stood at the rail of the Cormorant and watched the water below the cliff mouth go glass-flat, the way it did for perhaps forty minutes twice a day, when the Sable Current forgot for a moment which way it was going. Above them, hidden behind the headland's shoulder, the salvage fleet's dredges thudded against the outer reef like a slow heartbeat. Dust rose from the water in pale plumes. Every hour, the reef grew thinner. Every hour, the caverns beneath it grew closer to becoming tombs with the door bricked shut.

"Slack water," she said. "We go now or we don't go."

Ivo was already dressed for it. He had spent the last two hours wrapping himself in the old woman's borrowed diving gear with the methodical patience of a man cataloguing his own funeral. The canvas suit hung loose on his frame; he'd padded the wrists with strips of linen so the lamp harness wouldn't chafe. On his chest, over the heart, he'd tucked a scrap of paper bearing three names in Vessani script—the names from the decoded ledger, the ones the empire had drowned rather than ransom.

"You don't need to bring those," Zephyrine said.

"Yes," Ivo said, "I do." He said it the way he read ashes: quietly, without argument, as though the matter had been settled by someone with more authority than either of them. "If I drown down there, I want to be carrying something worth drowning with."

She didn't answer that. There was nothing honest she could say that wasn't cruel, and nothing kind that wasn't false. Instead she checked his air line twice, ran her thumb along the valve fittings, tested the lamp, and made him recite the breathing count back to her.

"One hundred twenty strokes of the current," he recited. "At ninety, we turn whether we've found the strongroom or not. At one hundred ten, we drop everything but each other."

"And if the line goes slack?"

"I hold still and wait. If it goes slack twice, I follow the wall left-hand until I find air or you."

"And if the surge comes early?"

He looked at her then, and in the grey pre-dawn light his face was very plain and very steady. "Then I trust your memory of water no chart has ever recorded, because it is the only map in the world that matters."

It was the closest thing to a prayer either of them owned. She turned away before he could see what it did to her face.

* * *

They went down through the throat of the cliff together, hand over hand along the weighted guide line she'd rigged at the last slack tide, when she'd come alone to set the markers. The light died above them in layers—grey, then green, then a brown-black that swallowed the lamp beams whole. Below thirty feet the Sable Current's famous clarity ended, and the water became something older than water: a cold pressing dark full of silt ghosts that swirled up from their fins and hung around them like the breath of the drowned.

Zephyrine counted. She had learned to count underwater the winter she was nineteen, working wreck salvage off the Verlaine coast with a partner who breathed too fast and panicked too slow, and counting was the discipline that kept terror in its cage. Four strokes, check the line. Eight strokes, check your man. Twelve strokes, check the ceiling. The numbers were a rope thrown across the black, and she climbed it stroke by stroke, and she made herself believe the rope would hold.

Ivo swam beside her, slightly behind and to her right where her lamp could reach him without blinding him. He was not a natural diver. His kicks were too deliberate, a scholar's kicks, each one placed like a footnote. But he did not waste breath on fear, and he kept the count, and when she glanced back at the interval marks his eyes found hers through the mask and held steady. That was all she needed. A man who could keep a count in the dark was a man who could be brought home.

The guide line ended at the first marker—a brass ring she'd wedged into a crack of rock, polished bright by forty years of current. Beyond it lay the part of the caverns no line could follow, because the current here did not run in one direction. It ran in every direction at once, slowly, like something turning over in its sleep.

Here the charts failed. Here even her own memory had to take over.

She had found these caverns seven years ago, running from a patrol cutter in weather bad enough to make lying about it pointless. She'd been swept through a gap in the reef she never should have survived, spat into an air pocket, and spent nine hours mapping the inner passages by lamp and instinct while the storm outside decided whether to kill her. She had never told anyone they existed. She had never written them down. A chart could be confiscated; a memory could only be killed, and hers had proven difficult to kill.

Now she led Ivo into that memory.

* * *

The first passage was the easy one, if anything down here could be called easy. It ran narrow and smooth-walled, the stone scoured to a dull gloss by centuries of tidal breathing, and the current in it moved with the slack tide's lazy indecision. Zephyrine swum it by feel, one glove trailing the wall on her left, counting strokes between the landmarks she'd fixed in her mind years ago: the shelf where a school of blind shrimp clustered white as teeth, the split in the rock shaped like a wishbone, the place where the ceiling dipped low enough that she had to flatten herself and pull through on her fingertips while the suit canvas whispered against stone overhead.

Behind her, Ivo's lamp swung and steadied, swung and steadied. He had learned already not to look at the darkness beyond his beam. Looking at the darkness was how men drowned—not because the darkness took them, but because they turned toward it and stopped swimming.

At the wishbone split, the passage forked, and the fork was where the caverns began keeping their dead.

The left branch was theirs. The right branch, she knew from her nine hours of exile seven years ago, opened after eighty feet into a low gallery where the current deposited whatever the sea was finished with. She had gone eighty feet into it once, out of the same discipline that made her count, because a diver who doesn't know what lies behind her is a diver who can be surprised, and surprise underground is death.

She had seen the bones.

Not scattered bones—arranged ones. Laid in rows along the gallery floor, sorted by size, the small ones separated from the large with a care that was worse than chaos. Someone had organized this. Someone in the last hours of a drowning nation had crawled into the dark and done the only work left to do, which was to make the death legible. And beside the bones, furred with silt, lay the things the dead had carried: a child's shoe. A lacquered box gone soft with rot. A bundle of letters tied with wire, fused into a single brick of pulp.

She had not gone further. She had backed out, marked the fork in her memory, and sealed that gallery away in the part of herself where she kept the things she could not afford to feel yet.

Now she felt it coming again, ahead of her in the dark, and she put her hand on Ivo's arm and steered him left.

* * *

The second passage was where the lifeboat was.

It came into view gradually, the way wrecks do—one shadow resolving into a shape, the shape into a keel, the keel into a boat. It was a ship's gig, maybe twenty-six feet, Vessani-built; Ivo would know the lines of it better than she did. It had been driven into the passage by some ancient flood and shattered against the rock, and the rock had kept it. The hull lay on its side, stove in along the starboard strakes, half-buried in silt, its oars fanned out around it like the legs of a dead insect. The nameboard had broken free and stood propped against the wall, and Zephyrine's lamp caught the gilt lettering through the grime:

*VSR Meridian — Station 4*

Station four. One of the evacuation boats. Not the fleet that sailed and was scuttled—this was from the harbor itself, launched in the last hours, when the water was already coming into the lower city and the order had gone out to save whoever could be saved.

There were belt buckles in the silt around it. Brass, green with age, still buckled. That was the detail her mind kept returning to as she swam past, counting, counting: the buckles were still fastened. Whatever had happened here had happened to people wearing their belts, holding their belongings, following orders. They had come into this passage the way she was coming into it now, by lamp and counted breaths, believing there was air on the other side.

For some of them, maybe there had been. The air pocket in the great chamber ahead—the Drowned Cathedral, Ivo called it, and the name had stuck—had sheltered survivors once. The frescoes proved it. Maybe the gig's people had reached it. Maybe some had lived days, weeks, in that stone room with the painted walls, listening to the tide breathe through the rock, waiting for a rescue that the empire had already decided not to send.

She didn't look at Ivo. She couldn't have borne looking at Ivo just then, and she suspected he couldn't have borne being looked at. So she swam, and counted, and let the discipline carry them both past the dead.

But at the far end of the passage, where the rock narrowed before opening into the cathedral chamber, she felt his glove touch her wrist—two taps, the signal they'd agreed on—and when she turned, he pointed his lamp back at the lifeboat, and then pressed his fist briefly against his chest, over the paper with the three names.

*I will remember this,* the gesture said. *Someone will remember this.*

She tapped his wrist twice in return. Then she pulled them both through the narrows, into the breathing dark of the cathedral.

* * *

The air pocket hit them like a country.

One moment they were swimming in pressed black silence; the next, her head broke the surface of a still inland sea, and there was space above her—vast, vaulted, utterly dark except for the twin lamps—and the sound of their own breathing came back to them off stone walls fifty feet high. Water dripped somewhere with the regularity of a clock. The air tasted of salt and stone and something faintly sweet, like old varnish, like paint.

"The frescoes," Ivo said, and his voice in that chamber sounded the way voices sound in churches, and she understood he was not speaking to her exactly. "Marguerite—they're still here."

They were. Her lamp found them along the eastern wall: figures painted in mineral pigments by hands that had worked by lamplight in the last days of a dying nation. Processions of people walking inland, carrying children, carrying books. A harbor with ships at anchor, rendered lovingly, every rigging line precise. And above them all, painted across the highest curve of the vault where the water had never reached, a single line of Vessani script in ochre and white, tall as a man.

Ivo read it aloud. His voice cracked on the third word and he started again.

"'We were a nation of sailors,'" he translated, "'and the sea has taken our house. But the sea cannot take what we carried. Let these walls say what the courts will not: we did not abandon our dead, and we did not surrender our living. Whoever finds this room—remember us as we built, not as we sank.'"

Below the inscription, smaller, in a different hand, dozens of signatures climbed the wall like ivy. Names. Just names, painted by people who might have died within sight of them, who had used their final pigment not to plead or accuse but simply to exist—to write *I was here* on the inside of the world that swallowed them.

Zephyrine floated in the black water and let the silence hold her. Terror was still there—it lived in her chest always now, a cold coal—but the discipline kept it banked. Count the drips. Check the lamps. Ninety strokes at the turn; they had time; they had just enough time. Grief could have its share later. Right now there was only the next passage, and the next count, and the strongroom at the end of them.

"We're at sixty," she said. "Thirty strokes of rest, then the deep channel."

Ivo wiped his face with a wet glove. When he spoke again his voice was level, and she heard in it the same thing she relied on in herself: the choice, made fresh each moment, to keep functioning.

"Lead on," he said. "I'm counting with you."

* * *

The deep channel was the reason no imperial chart would ever show a route to the strongroom, and the reason the fleet's divers would die trying to find one.

It began where the cathedral's far wall met the water in a seam barely wide enough for a body, and it dropped. Not gradually—dropped, a vertical cleft plunging past the reach of her lamp, filled with a current that even at slack tide moved with a slow, muscular purpose, like a throat swallowing. Seven years ago she had descended it on a single breath-hold she still didn't fully believe she'd survived, following the feel of the water rather than any sight: the cold layer at twelve feet, the warmer tongue hugging the east wall at twenty, the dead-still column at the bottom where the channel leveled and ran horizontal toward the captain's strongroom.

No line could be rigged down it; the current shredded anything left overnight. No marker could be trusted; the silt erased them in hours. It could only be swum from memory, by someone who had done it once and lived, and there was exactly one such person in the world, and she was looking at the bottom of a hole in the earth with a lamp that suddenly seemed very small.

"Breathe down," she told Ivo. "Slow everything. We go on my count, and we do not stop in the cleft. Stopping in the cleft is dying in the cleft."

"And if the warm tongue isn't where you remember it?"

"Then it's moved four feet and we find it anyway. Currents shift. They don't vanish." She said it with more certainty than she felt, and saw him accept it, and hated herself a little for the ease of the lie and loved him a little for needing it.

They hyperventilated slow, purged, breathed down until their hearts knocked like fists on a door. Then Zephyrine took the seam, and the dark took them both.

The descent was forty seconds that lasted a season. The cold layer found her shoulders at twelve feet, exactly where memory said. The warm tongue brushed her cheek at twenty, hugging the east wall, and she hooked it with her body and rode it down through pressure that squeezed the lamp housing and popped her ears and pressed the whole ocean's weight gently, patiently, against the cage of her ribs. Behind her—Ivo's fin-beats, steady, steady, a scholar's rhythm, footnotes in the dark. At the bottom the current died, the channel leveled, and her gloves touched smooth-cut stone.

Dressed stone. Quarried, faced, fitted. The Vessani had cut this passage themselves, in the years before the end, and the knowledge steadied her more than any count: human hands had made this dark, and human hands had made a door in it, and doors opened.

Her lamp swept forward and found the passage running straight and true, walls of fitted granite furred white with mineral bloom, and at its end—forty strokes, maybe fifty—a shape that resolved as she kicked toward it into an arch, and within the arch, an iron-bound door hanging open on hinges that forty years of salt had not defeated.

Beyond it, darkness deeper than the water's. The strongroom.

And beside the door, mounted on the wall at a drowning man's eye height, a bronze plate caught her beam and threw it back, and the letters cut into it were crisp as the day they were cast. Ivo drifted up beside her, and she saw his gloved hand rise toward the plate, and heard him read it aloud through the regulator's hiss, his voice fraying at the edges:

"'Captain's strongroom. Key rests with the log. The log does not leave this room.'"

Zephyrine checked her count. Sixty-eight strokes since the cleft. Twenty-two to the turn.

Twenty-two strokes to learn what the empire had killed a nation to bury.

She kicked for the door.
