## 24. The Captain's Strongroom

The lamp burned low in its brass housing, and Ivo counted the oil by habit, the way he counted everything now—breaths, strokes, minutes of slack water bleeding away beneath a drowned nation. Half a tank. Perhaps forty minutes of light, and less than that of air in their lungs' worth of patience.

"Here." Zephyrine's voice came thin through the water, distorted by the mouthpiece, but he knew its shape even muffled. Her gloved hand appeared in the lamplight, pointing down a corridor of stone that no chart had ever recorded.

They had been swimming through the inner dark for what felt like half his life. Past the bones—small ones, delicate, a child's ribcage furred with white coral that made it look almost like lacework, like something beautiful, until you understood what you were looking at. Past the shattered lifeboat wedged sideways in a fissure, its planks split along the grain as if the sea had simply pried it open to see what was inside. Past the niches where Vessani hands had carved marks into the rock: tallies, names, a single repeated phrase in old script that Ivo had translated without meaning to, the way you translate your own heartbeat.

*We waited for the tide.*

He had not told Zephyrine what the phrase said. Some mercies were still possible.

Now the corridor narrowed, then opened, and the lamp caught something that stopped Ivo's heart for one full beat before discipline restarted it.

A door.

Not stone. Iron, green with forty years of Patience—the sea's slow patience, which was only another word for corrosion—but intact, set into a frame of black granite that had been cut and dressed by masons who had never imagined their work would end below the world. And upon the door, crusted but legible, a seal: the double wave and rising sun of Vessany, struck deep enough into the metal that the sea had not been able to erase it.

"The strongroom," Ivo breathed, forgetting the mouthpiece, and swallowed seawater, and coughed, and Zephyrine's hand closed hard on his shoulder—not comfort, exactly. Correction. *Breathe properly or die properly.* He nodded, cleared his regulator, and tried again. "It's real. It's actually real."

She looked at him through her mask, and even distorted by glass and lamplight he could read her expression: *Of course it's real. Now work.*

Work. Yes. That was the antidote to awe, and he had learned it young. He swam to the door and ran his fingers along its edge, feeling for the mechanism. The lock was a Vessani naval pattern—he had catalogued three hundred confiscated documents describing such locks, had held the drawings in gloved hands while an imperial clerk watched him with bored eyes, and had memorized every measurement because memory was the only archive they could not burn.

"Tumbler sequence," he signed against the door, then remembered she couldn't see him well enough. He turned, pulled his slate from his belt, and wrote: *Four tumblers. Naval pattern 9. I know the sequence. Watch the corridor.*

She took up position without argument, lamp angled back the way they'd come, body a silhouette against the black water. Trusting him. The thought landed strangely—Ivo Rellanev, trusted. His family name had been spat at by an old woman on a cliff not ten days ago, and here was this woman who owed him nothing, guarding his back in a cathedral of drowning because he could read dead men's locks.

He worked the tumblers blind, by feel, counting the clicks through his gloves. One. Two. The third resisted, and his heart stuttered—corrosion, salt-welded, forty years of the sea doing what empires do, sealing things shut—

Then it gave, all at once, so suddenly he nearly lost his grip, and the fourth tumbler turned under his finger like a key turning in his chest.

The door swung inward with a groan of escaping air bubbles, and the lamp light poured into the Captain's strongroom.

* * *

It was small. That was the first thing. History, Ivo had learned, happened in small rooms.

A desk bolted to the floor, its drawers swollen shut. A chair, tipped over, still lying where some man had knocked it in his last minutes. A shelf of logbooks fused into a single brick by rot and pressure. And on the desk itself, centered with the precision of a man who kept order even facing death, a ledger bound in grey leather, weighted down by a bar of lead stamped with the Vessani assay mark.

Ivo drifted over the threshold and felt the room close around him like a hand.

This was where Captain Elzavar Maeth had spent his final hour. Ivo knew the name the way he knew his own; it was in the third document of the cache Zephyrine had pulled from the Sable Current, in a supply requisition countersigned in a cramped, disciplined hand. Maeth had been given an impossible order and had obeyed it, and obedience had brought him here, to a small iron room at the bottom of the sea, where he had apparently decided that if he must drown, he would drown leaving evidence.

Zephyrine entered behind him and put her hand on the ledger. She looked at him across the desk, and tilted her head: *Take it?*

He shook his head. *Read here.* If the paper had survived forty years in this airless room, moving it might kill it. And besides—and he admitted this only to himself—some part of him needed to read it here, in the room where it was written, with the author's chair overturned beside him. You did not carry a man's testament out of his house like luggage. You read it where he left it.

He eased the lead bar aside. The leather cover came away soft as skin. The first pages were routine—course corrections, depth soundings, the mundane litany of a ship doing its duty. Then, dated the night of the scuttling, the handwriting changed. Larger. Pressed harder. The pen had torn the paper twice.

Ivo began to translate, writing on his slate in compressed shorthand because there was no time for elegance:

*"By order transmitted under imperial cipher, we are instructed to admit water to the lower holds during evacuation loading. Passengers aboard: 1,140 souls, of whom 412 are children. The fleet carries also the treasury of the Republic, per sealed manifest, which I am ordered to secure in the after gallery and deny to all parties."*

Zephyrine had moved to stand beside him, reading his slate upside down with the quick comprehension of someone who navigated by inference. Her hand found his arm and gripped.

He kept writing. *"I have refused the order once and been threatened with the arrest of my officers' families. I will not be the man who drowns children. But I am not free. So I do what remains: I record. Let whoever finds this room know that the order came signed, and I name the signature."*

And then the last page. The page the whole journey had been for. The page whose cipher key had been hidden in this very room, tucked inside the ledger's spine—a strip of vellum Ivo drew out with fingers that would not quite stay steady, matching character to character in the old Vessani naval cipher, the one the empire had never broken because the empire had never believed a drowned language worth breaking.

The final page decoded itself under his pencil line by line, and each line was a stone laid on his chest:

*Order No. 114-Fall. Issued by the Office of Maritime Security, Verlaine Command. Authorization for the scuttling of the Vessani evacuation fleet, all vessels, passengers and cargo, to prevent recovery of state assets by hostile powers. Signed: Rear Admiral Edvard Corvane.*

Ivo read the name four times. The letters did not change. The sea did not rush in to wash them away. The dead captain's pen strokes sat on the page in their torn, desperate honesty, and the name sat beneath them in its official flourish, and both were true, and both had been true for forty years while the empire called the sinking a storm, called the loss a tragedy of war, called the survivors collaborators and beggars and ghosts.

Corvane. The father of the man who now commanded nine ships above their heads, dredging the reef, dynamiting the outer rocks, hunting two divers and a chest of papers with the whole weight of an imperial fleet.

"It was never lost," Ivo wrote on the slate, because his voice would not survive the mouthpiece and his hands needed to do something. "The treasury. Read the manifest reference—it says *after gallery*. It was never recovered. They sank it with the fleet and then spent forty years pretending they didn't know where it was, because admitting they knew meant admitting they'd sunk it on purpose."

Zephyrine went very still. He watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he had: the third cavern. The flooded gallery the old woman's warning had circled around. The treasury of Vessany, sitting in the dark a few meters of stone away, guarded by the skeletons of the men ordered to carry it home.

Vindication should have felt like fire. It felt like stone. Heavy, cold, settled permanently against the ribs. He had spent his life among ashes, cataloguing the paperwork of a murdered country, telling himself that if he could just preserve enough—if he could memorize enough, smuggle enough, remember enough—then someday the record would speak. He had imagined that day as a kind of sunrise.

It was not a sunrise. It was this: a small iron room, a dead man's torn handwriting, and a name that explained everything and changed nothing about how much it cost.

* * *

Zephyrine touched his shoulder and pointed upward, then at her gauge. Time. Always time. The slack window was closing; the current that threaded these caverns would wake within the quarter hour and begin to chew.

But Ivo held up one finger. *One more thing.*

He went to the desk drawers. The first three were fused solid; the fourth opened with a shriek of rust that sounded, in the enclosed space, like a scream. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work faithfully for four decades: a pistol, corroded useless. A portrait miniature of a woman and two children, faces gone to shadow but the love in the composition still legible. And a second document—shorter, unciphered, written plainly because it was meant to be found by anyone, friend or enemy, if only they came looking.

*"To whoever reads this: the truth is in the ledger and the ledger is in God's keeping now. My wife is Anka Maeth, of the Salt Harbor parish. If any of my crew's families survive, tell them we refused as long as refusal was possible. Tell them the order was signed. Do not let them bury us as a storm."*

Ivo copied it onto his slate in full, letter by letter, though his vision had blurred past usefulness and he was working partly from memory of what his heart had already seized. Then he wrapped the original back in its oilcloth, replaced the lead bar, and closed the drawer gently, the way you close a coffin.

Zephyrine was watching him. When he turned, she did something he had not seen her do: she removed her glove underwater and pressed her bare palm flat against the strongroom door, against the crusted seal of Vessany, and held it there for three full seconds. A salute. An apology. An oath—he couldn't say which, and suspected she couldn't either.

Then she pointed up, urgent now, and he saw why: the water at the corridor's mouth had begun to move. Threads of sediment unspooling from the dark, the current flexing its shoulders, waking.

They kicked for the exit together, Ivo clutching the vellum cipher key inside his suit, against his skin, where no fleet and no flood and no admiral could reach it without going through him.

Behind them the strongroom door stood open at last, and Captain Maeth's testimony lay in the dark like a lamp left burning for someone who had finally come.

Above them, faint through forty fathoms of water and stone, came a sound Ivo felt more than heard: a dull, rhythmic concussion. The fleet's charges, eating the reef.

They were running out of world.
