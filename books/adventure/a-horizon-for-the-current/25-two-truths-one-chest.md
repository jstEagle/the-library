## 25. Two Truths, One Chest

The light came first.

It came the way it always did in the deep places of the Sable Current—not all at once but in layers, grey to pearl to a green-gold shimmer that trembled on the surface overhead like a curtain drawn back by patient hands. Zephyrine rose through it slowly, counting her ascent as she had counted everything for the past hour: breaths, kicks, fathoms, heartbeats. Discipline was the only rope that held in the dark, and she had climbed it rung by rung until the water above her turned from black to silver and the world remembered it had a sky.

Ivo came up beside her, his lamp still burning, its glow swallowed whole by the daylight. He broke the surface a moment after she did, gasping, and she heard him laugh—a raw, disbelieving sound, half sob, torn out of him before he could stop it.

"We found it," he said. "Zephyrine. We found it."

She spat salt and pushed wet hair from her eyes. The Cormorant rode at anchor thirty yards off, small and patched and beautiful against the pale morning, her sail furled, her deck empty in the flat light. Beyond her, through the mouth of the lagoon, Zephyrine could see the salvage fleet at their moorings—nine hulls, dredge boars swinging idle in the calm, smoke rising thin and straight from the flagship's galley stack. Corvane's ships slept like dogs at midday. Two hours of slack water left, maybe less. Then the Current would wake, and the window would close behind them like a door.

"Strongroom's real," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "Ledger's real. You read it yourself."

"I read it." Ivo trod water beside her, and even now, even exhausted and blue-lipped, there was something lit in his face that she had never seen there before—the look of a man who had spent forty years reading ashes and had finally been handed fire. "My grandfather's name is in the margin. In his own hand. He countersigned the order to abandon the third cavern." His laugh again, broken. "He knew they were down there. He sealed them in and *countersigned* it."

"Ivo."

"He knew." 

"*Ivo.*" She caught his shoulder. "Later. Grieve later. Right now we swim, we climb aboard, we get those documents dry, and we get out of this lagoon before that man's dredges start singing again. Later you can hate your grandfather properly, with a drink in your hand and solid ground under your feet."

He looked at her then, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod. They swam.

She noticed it before he did. She always noticed things first—it was what had kept her alive through two blacklists and a hundred bad crossings—and so it was Zephyrine who stopped, three strokes from the Cormorant's stern, treading water with the cold suddenly arriving in her gut ahead of any reason for it.

The dinghy was gone.

They'd left it tied astern, tucked under the overhang where no patrol glass would find it. It wasn't there. And the anchor line—the line she had set herself, with the specific tension she always set, the little private signature of a woman who trusted nothing—rode wrong. Too short. Adjusted.

"Ivo," she said quietly. "Don't stop swimming. Don't look up yet. Someone's aboard."

His stroke faltered, recovered. She saw his eyes flick toward the deck, saw him understand, saw the fire in his face bank itself down to coals. Forty years of surviving occupiers had taught him how to be still.

"Options?" he breathed.

"One." She filled her lungs. "We go up anyway. Whoever it is has been sitting on our deck for an hour with every reason to cut our throats and hasn't. That means he wants something. Men who want something talk first." She began swimming again, unhurried, arms cutting the water in long easy strokes, a guide bringing her client home across a mountain lake. "So we let him talk."

"And if talking's over when we arrive?"

"Then we arrive angry," she said, "and wet, and armed." The knife was still strapped to her calf beneath the dive suit. She'd felt its weight through every fathom of the inner dark, and she felt it now like a promise.

They reached the boarding ladder. She went up first.

* * *

He sat in the cockpit under the boom tent like a man waiting out rain, legs crossed, gloved hands folded on one knee, a flask of something amber open beside him and two cups poured. Grey-haired now, broader than she remembered, but she would have known that silhouette anywhere—in a market crowd, in a dream, in the worst hour of any night. He'd taught her to read tide tables when she was nineteen. He'd taught her that every man's price could be found if you looked long enough, and he'd never once pretended otherwise about himself.

"Sarel Voss," she said.

"Marguerite." He used the name nobody used, the name from before, and smiled the smile that had opened a thousand doors and closed a few permanently. "You look well. Diving agrees with you."

"You're a long way from your counting house."

"The Current moves things," he said mildly. "Chests. People. Old debts." He gestured at the second cup. "Sit. Dry off. We have perhaps ninety minutes before your tide turns, by my reckoning, and I've waited an hour already. A few more minutes of courtesy won't kill either of us."

Ivo came over the rail behind her, dripping, and stopped dead when he saw the broker's face. "You," he said. "The archive. You were at the archive. Years ago—you signed the confiscation registers."

"I sign many registers." Voss's eyes moved to Ivo without interest and back to Zephyrine with a great deal of it. "Your archivist has a good memory. Useful in his trade. Less useful in ours." He nudged the second cup an inch toward her. "Brandy. Saline solution for what ails you both. Drink or don't."

Zephyrine didn't sit. She stood at the top of the companionway with seawater running off her in ropes, and she took inventory the way she'd been trained to take inventory of weather: fast, total, without flinching.

Voss alone. No visible escort. But the dinghy gone meant a larger boat somewhere beyond the point, and Voss never traveled without muscle within shouting distance. On the deck between the mast and the windlass lay a small oilskin packet, weighted with a stone, and she knew—Ivo's copies, the decoded pages he'd transcribed night after night in the cabin, wrapped against damp exactly that way. Voss had found them. Voss had read them, or enough of them. Which meant—

"You know what we have," she said.

"I know what you *had*." Voss lifted his own cup, tasted, set it down. "A strongroom ledger. A captain's cipher key. And a final page bearing a signature that certain people have spent forty years and considerable money ensuring stayed underwater." He said it the way other men discussed cargo tonnage. "I also know that Admiral Corvane's dredges resume work at the next tide, and that when they do, the outer reef comes down, and the caverns with it, and anything still inside becomes geology. So. Arithmetic, Marguerite. You were always better at arithmetic than anyone gave you credit for."

"Say it plainly."

He smiled again, and this time the warmth in it was real, and that was the obscenity of it—that after everything, the warmth was real. "The originals, intact, delivered to me before the sixth tide. In exchange: full pardon, both nations, sealed and notarized. Your blacklist burned. Your name clean. Passage papers, letters of credit, a new ketch if you want one—" his glance took in the Cormorant's scarred rails with polite regret "—and enough left over that you never have to pull another drowned stranger's chest out of a river for coin as long as you live." He spread his hands. "Everything you've wanted since the mountain. Everything you lost. Restored. For one chest of old paper."

The wind moved through the rigging, a low note like a struck wire. Below them the lagoon lay green and quiet, and somewhere under that quiet floor the treasury gallery sat sealed behind Ivo's careful masonry, holding its skeletons and its gold and its terrible arithmetic of its own.

"You've had this offer ready a while," Zephyrine said.

"Since the river," Voss said. "You think I learned about the chest from a harbor rumor? Marguerite. I put the notice in the harbor office myself. Six tides, imperial fleet, salvage rights claimed—all of it printed to flush whoever pulled that chest upstream. It worked. It worked beautifully. The only thing I miscalculated was that you'd dive the inner caverns instead of running." He shook his head, almost fond. "You always did go through the mountain instead of around it."

"You sent the broker to my boathouse in Verlaine."

"I *was* the broker in Verlaine. You refused me a fortune for goods unseen. Very principled. Also very expensive, as principles go." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the broker's polish dropped away and she saw the man underneath, the one who had once carried her, fevered, down off the Verlaine passes on his own back when she was twenty-two and stupid and the weather turned. "Listen to me. Not as a buyer. As the last person alive who watched you give up everything for someone else's mistake. Corvane is not going to negotiate. He is going to bury this lagoon with everyone in it, and he will sleep fine afterward, because his father's signature dies with the paper. Take the pardon. Take the money. Let the empire eat its own dead in peace, and let yourself live. You've paid enough. God knows you've paid enough."

And there it was. Not the words—she'd heard versions of the words from better liars—but the voice. The voice that had talked her down off a ridge in a whiteout. The voice that had said *you can't save everyone, girl, only yourself, and that's not cowardice, that's seamanship.*

Temptation wearing a familiar face. Wearing, God help her, the face of the only mentor she'd ever had.

She became aware of Ivo beside her, very still, watching her the way men watch a fuse.

"My copies," Ivo said quietly. "You'll return them regardless?"

Voss didn't look at him. "The copies are insurance, archivist. They come back when the originals do. Not before."

"They're worthless without the cipher key," Ivo said. "Which exists only in my head and in the strongroom below us. Burn them if you like."

"They're worth plenty," Voss said, "to the people who want to know exactly how much the empire has to answer for. Knowledge of the shape of a weapon is nearly as valuable as the weapon." He finally glanced at Ivo, and there was nothing warm in it at all. "Your family understood that principle intimately."

Ivo went white. Zephyrine stepped between them before the archivist could spend words he couldn't afford.

"Ninety minutes," she said to Voss. "You said the tide gives us ninety minutes. Here's mine." She crouched, slow, deliberate, and picked up the brandy cup, and drank it down, and set it on the deck between them—a courtesy returned, a line drawn. "You'll have my answer before the water starts moving. Until then, you'll wait in your dinghy—which I assume you stowed rather than lost—or on whatever boat brought you here, and you'll leave this deck."

"Marguerite—"

"That's not a negotiation. That's hospitality expiring."

For a long moment he studied her, and she let him, and she kept her face the way she kept it in whiteouts: blank as snowfall, giving nothing, promising nothing. Then Voss sighed, gathered his flask and his gloves, and rose with the unhurried grace of a man who believed the game was already won.

"At the turn of the tide, then," he said, moving to the rail. He paused there, half over the water, and looked back at her, and his voice gentled into something almost kind. "For what it's worth—I hope you say yes. I've watched you carry that mountain for fifteen years, girl. Nobody deserves to put it down more than you."

He went down the ladder. The dinghy, she saw now, was tethered under the counter where theirs had been. He rowed away toward the point without looking back, small and grey against the green water, and the sound of his oarlocks faded into the wash against the hull.

* * *

Ivo spoke first, when the point had swallowed the dinghy. "You're considering it."

She didn't answer. She walked to the oilskin packet, weighed it in her hand, and set it down unopened. Her hands were shaking. She looked at them with distant irritation, as at tools failing on a job.

"Fifteen years," she said. "Do you know what a pardon actually is? It's a piece of paper that says the last fifteen years didn't happen. That the passes I walked, the rivers I worked, the nights I slept in ditches two miles from border posts because a bed meant a register and a register meant a noose—it says none of that needed to happen. Except it did happen. It happened to me. A pardon doesn't give me back the years. It just forgives the empire for taking them." She laughed, short and bitter. "And he knows that. He's known me since I was nineteen. He knew exactly which wound to press."

"Yes," Ivo said. "That's why he's dangerous. Not the money. The mercy."

She turned to look at him. He stood by the mainmast with the wind pulling at his soaked clothes, and his face had gone quiet in a way she'd learned to distrust and, worse, to rely on.

"There are names in that ledger," he said. "Three hundred and eleven names. I counted them while you were checking our air. Three hundred and eleven people the empire drowned in the dark rather than ransom, rather than rescue, rather than admit they existed. My grandfather sealed the door on them and wrote his initials beside the order like a man signing for a delivery." His voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "If you sell the originals, the copies might someday convince someone. Might. After lawyers. After decades. After everyone who remembers Vessany is dead of old age. Or the copies vanish, because Voss keeps his word only as long as keeping it costs nothing. Either way the truth stays a rumor. Rumors don't raise the drowned. Rumors don't make admirals drown in their own flagships."

"Is that what you want?" she said sharply. "Corvane dead? Is that justice, or is it just your fury wearing justice's coat?"

"It's both," Ivo said simply. "I've stopped pretending they're different things. Have you?"

The question landed harder than he could have known. She looked away, out across the lagoon, to where the flagship lay at anchor with its lights beginning to show as the afternoon thickened. Somewhere on that deck a son was finishing his father's work. Somewhere below her keel, behind Ivo's wall of stone, a nation's treasury sat in the dark with its carriers' bones, waiting for a tide that would decide whether it stayed buried forever or came up into the light to burn everything it touched.

The current would turn soon. She could feel it already in the way the anchor line had begun to breathe, that faint change in tension, the sea clearing its throat.

"If I refuse him," she said slowly, working it through aloud because saying it made it real, "he takes the copies to Corvane. Tells him exactly what we hold and exactly where we are. Corvane seals the exits and lets the reef do his killing, and he never has to explain a sunken flagship to anyone." She met Ivo's eyes. "If I accept, we walk away rich and pardoned and owned. Every port we ever enter, we enter as the empire's bought witnesses. There's no version of that life where I ever sleep without listening for oarlocks."

"So it's the third thing," Ivo said.

"There's always a third thing. There's a third channel through every mountain; you just have to be willing to drown finding it." She crouched and pulled the knife from her calf, laid it on the deck, and began, methodically, to strip the dive lamp for parts—battery, filament, the little charge of flare compound she kept in the housing against emergencies. Her hands had stopped shaking. "Voss expects an answer at slack water's end. He'll get one. But not the one he's priced."

Ivo watched her work, and understanding came into his face slowly, the way dawn comes into a valley. "Zephyrine. What are you going to do?"

She looked up at him, and for the first time since the surfacing she smiled—a thin, cold, entirely awake expression that had once made a Verlaine harbormaster hand back her fees unprompted.

"I'm going to bargain," she said. "Right up until the moment I don't."

Out past the point, faint on the wind, came the first far-off thud of the dredges waking early.
