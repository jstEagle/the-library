## 26. The Turn

The broker was sitting on the Cormorant's stern bench when they surfaced, as comfortable as a man waiting for a ferry.

Zephyrine saw him first—of course she did; her eyes went to the boat before they ever went to the sky—and for one long breath she hung in the green water with the regulator hissing and let herself feel the whole shape of it: the trap closing not with violence but with patience, the way all the worst traps worked. He had boarded while they were down. He had had the deck to himself for the better part of an hour. Anything he wanted to take, he had taken. Anything he wanted to read, he had read.

She kicked upward slowly. No point in surfacing fast. No point in surfacing at all, some cold voice said at the back of her skull—but Ivo was beside her, and his air would be running shorter than hers, and there was nowhere else to go that wasn't the deep channel or the fleet.

They broke the surface thirty feet off their own stern.

"Masterful diving," the broker called down, pleasantly. He was a narrow man, grey as driftwood, in a coat too fine for a working deck, and he had Ivo's oilskin packet open across his knees like a man reading letters from home. "I watched your bubbles come up along the channel edge. Textbook. You could have taught courses, Mistress Pemberton, if your name hadn't become what it became."

Ivo surfaced beside her, tore out his mouthpiece, and started to shout. She put a hand flat on his chest under the water. *Wait.* He looked at her—water streaming off his face, fury and fear fighting for the same ground—and waited.

"You're standing on my boat," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "That's trespass in three jurisdictions."

"It's salvage in four." The broker smiled. He held up the packet, letting the wind flutter the pages. "Your archivist has been busy. Beautiful work, this cipher. My employers will want the originals, of course, but these copies are quite persuasive on their own. Which is why you're going to listen to what I've brought."

He reached into his coat and produced, of all things, a document case of oiled leather, imperial make, the kind that carried commissions and pardons and death warrants with equal indifference. He set it on the stern bench beside him, deliberately, where they could both see the seal.

"Full pardon," he said. "Both names. Yours, Mistress Pemberton—the alpine matter expunged, the Verlaine blacklist lifted, right of passage restored in every port the empire controls. And his." A small nod toward Ivo, who had gone very still in the water. "Restoration of archive rank. His family's confiscations returned. All of it, signed, witnessed, sealed. In exchange: the original documents from the strongroom, delivered intact, and these copies burned in front of me. That's the whole bargain. It doesn't get better, and in about two hours it stops being offered, because Admiral Corvane's dredges are going to close this reef like a fist, and dead women can't be pardoned."

*Dead women can't be pardoned.* She turned the sentence over. It was true, and it was meant to be heard as true, and it was also the sound of a man reading from a script someone else had written.

"How did you find us?" she asked. Stall. Always stall. Every minute the tide ran, the suck-current at the cavern mouth grew teeth.

"The same way I always find people. Someone sold you." He said it without malice, which made it worse. "A harbor clerk at Salt Harbor remembered a patched ketch with a repaired rudder gudgeon buying storm anchors. People remember unusual purchases. That's all treachery ever is, mostly—memory, and a price on it."

Beside her, Ivo had stopped treading water. She felt it more than saw it—the change in his breathing, the way his head came up. When she glanced over, he was looking past the broker, past the Cormorant, at the sloop.

It lay anchored forty yards off, riding the slack with its mainsail furled, a fast hull, black-bottomed, crewed by three men she could count at the rail. And it was anchored badly. Anchored for convenience, not for current—bow to the breeze, no second hook, no scope worth the name. Its master had expected this to be a parley, not a fight. Men who expect parleys anchor like guests.

Men who anchor like guests die like guests, in a place like this.

Zephyrine had grown up on rivers before she ever saw a mountain, and she had spent eleven years learning the Sable Current the way other people learned scripture. She knew what happened at the cavern mouth when the tide began to turn. Everyone knew something happened there—that was why the charts said *avoid*, why the wreck above had never been stripped until Corvane brought dredges and dynamite and the arrogance of nine ships. But knowing *that* it killed and knowing *how* it killed were different knowings, and she had the second kind.

The flood tide would begin in a little under an hour. The water would pile against the reef wall and find the cavern mouths, and the mouths would drink, and everything loose within two hundred yards would be pulled toward them in slow, patient, irresistible ribbons. A well-anchored sloop would groan and hold. A guest-anchored sloop would swing, drag, and go around like a leaf toward a drain.

And between here and there lay the deep channel—the one she'd dived past an hour ago, the one whose floor dropped away into cold black nothing, the one where anything that sank was sunk forever.

She looked at the stern bench. At the document case with its fat imperial seal. At the oilskin packet in the broker's lap, holding three weeks of Ivo's nights, every name, every order, every drowned official rendered legible at last.

Then she looked at Ivo.

He was watching her. He knew her face well enough now—God help them both—to see the arithmetic moving behind it, and whatever he saw there made him go white. His hand found her wrist under the water and gripped hard. *No,* the grip said. *Not like this. Not again.*

*Ivo,* she thought, *you cooperated once to survive and it has been eating you for forty years. I am not going to hand you a second helping.*

Because that was the truth of the bargain, laid bare. Take the pardon, and the originals went into an imperial evidence vault to be lost in procedure until every witness was dead. Take the pardon, and the Reading at Salt Harbor—no, there'd be no reading at all, just sealed testimony and quiet settlements and old women in cliff villages dying with the truth still classified. Take the pardon, and she'd be exactly what the broker's employers wanted: bought. Not forgiven. Bought. There was a difference, and she had spent eleven years learning that difference the hard way, in border towns, in rented rooms, in the three-day rule she kept because staying anywhere meant belonging somewhere and belonging could be used against her.

Her whole life since the mountain had been one long exercise in keeping things—keeping moving, keeping silent, keeping herself. Keeping had kept her alive. It had also kept her alone.

The chest was heavy in the flooded strongroom below, lashed where they'd left it for the second dive, and even if it weren't—even if it sat on this deck right now—she understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like the moment a wave lifts you and you stop swimming and simply ride, that she had already decided. Somewhere between the broker's smile and the seal on the case, between *pardon* and *both names*, she had decided. This was just the part where her body caught up.

"I want to read the pardon," she said aloud. "Both pages. Before anything moves."

"Of course." The broker beckoned. "Come aboard. Bring your friend. I'm not a monster, Mistress Pemberton—I'm a professional, and professionals honor terms."

*Professionals honor terms.* She swam for the ladder.

* * *

The Cormorant's deck tilted gently under her boots as she came up the side, and she let herself drip, unhurried, hands visible, the picture of a woman weighing paper against freedom. Ivo came up behind her, breathing hard, and she positioned him without seeming to—one glance, one tilt of her chin—so that he stood between the broker and the tiller, and she stood between the broker and the sea.

The broker handed her the case. She opened it. The pardon was real; she could tell real imperial vellum from forgery the way she could tell real rope from rot, and this was real, both names inked in a chancellor's hand, seals hanging heavy and red. Her own name spelled correctly, which somehow hurt worse than a misspelling would have. Someone had taken care with her. Someone had decided she was worth the ink.

"It's generous," she said.

"It's final," the broker said. "The originals, please. Then the copies, and we burn them together—you'll want to see the smoke yourself, I imagine. Trust, but verify. I do."

"Eleven years," Zephyrine said, still looking at the page. "Do you know what it's like? Being a ghost with a body. Walking into harbors sideways. Counting three days everywhere because on the fourth day someone starts asking questions." She looked up and gave him her best smile, the client-winning one, the one that had gotten rich fools over passes they had no business crossing. "You're offering me my life back."

"I'm offering you better than your life back. Your life *forward*."

"Yes." She folded the pardon closed, carefully, reverently, and set it on the stern bench. "Here's my counter."

The broker's eyes narrowed a fraction. Professionals hate counters.

"The originals stay where they are until I'm standing on solid ground in a free port with this signed and registered. Not before. You keep the copies as surety—burn them when I say, not when you say. And your sloop stands off to fifty yards, because frankly, Captain, your anchoring is going to kill someone when the tide turns, and I'd rather it wasn't me."

Something flickered across the broker's face—not anger. Calculation. He glanced at the water, at the flat grey calm of the slack, and she watched him decide that the tide was an hour away and a woman alone on a ketch with one disgraced archivist was not a threat but a formality.

"The sloop stays where it is," he said. "Everything else is acceptable. Shall we shake on it?"

"We're not done." She turned toward the cabin. "There's a manifest below—inventory of the strongroom contents, in my hand. If your employers want originals, they'll want to know what they're getting. I'll fetch it."

"That's not necessary—"

"It is if you want me cooperative instead of compliant." She was already at the cabin door. "Compliance breaks, broker. Cooperation sells. Ask anyone who's bought a guide."

She went below.

The cabin was dark and smelled of tar and wet wool, and her heart was going so hard she could hear it in her ears, but her hands were steady—her hands were always steady; that was the one inheritance from the mountain she'd never regretted. The oilskin packet's twin lay in the chart drawer, because she had copied everything twice from the beginning; Ivo thought that was paranoia. Paranoia was just foresight with bad manners. She took the decoy packet—blank pages under a real cover, weighted to sink—and tucked it inside her coat.

Then she knelt at the sea-cock.

Every working boat on this coast had one: a valve to flood the bilge in emergency, or to flush it, depending on the century of the fitting. Hers was stiff with salt but it turned. She opened it a quarter-turn, no more—a slow seep, invisible, the kind of leak a careless owner wouldn't notice for twenty minutes. By then the bilge would be ankle-deep and the pump would be busy and everyone's attention would be exactly where she wanted it: on the water rising under their feet, and not on the tide.

She closed the cabin door behind her and came up smiling.

* * *

The next half hour was theater, and she played it like a woman who believed in the ending. She showed the broker the manifest. She answered questions about the strongroom's location with a precision just vague enough to require follow-up dives. She let Ivo argue—genuinely argue, poor man, because she hadn't told him either—for a clause protecting the survivor colony from reprisal, and watched the broker concede it with the weary magnanimity of a man who intended to honor nothing.

All the while, in the soles of her feet, she felt the sea change.

Slack water lies like a held breath. Then the breath goes out of it, and everything begins to move, and only someone who has lived on this water knows how early the movement starts—fifteen minutes before any tide table admits it, a long low shudder in the current, a change in the way the ketch swung at her hook. Zephyrine felt it at 4:12 by her father's watch. The Cormorant's bow swung six degrees east, then held, then swung again.

The sloop swung too. Farther. Faster. No second hook. No scope.

"Broker," she said pleasantly, "how much do you know about the Sable Current?"

"Enough to employ people who know more."

"Mm." She walked to the rail, unhurried, and looked at his sloop. Forty yards had become sixty. The black hull was lying beam-on now, its anchor line drawn bar-tight toward the reef. "Then let me tell you what happens next. In about ten minutes, the cavern mouths under this reef start drinking the flood tide. Everything loose gets pulled toward them in ribbons. Your sloop is anchored like a dinner guest. In about fifteen minutes, her anchor starts to drag. In about twenty, she goes around, and either she fetches up on the reef and breaks, or—" she pointed, gently, at the dark seam of deeper water beyond the ketch, "—she goes into the deep channel, and nothing that goes into the deep channel comes back. Not hulls. Not chests. Not men, if they're foolish enough to stay aboard."

The broker followed her finger. For the first time, doubt moved through his face like weather.

"You're bluffing."

"I never bluff about water. It's the one thing I don't lie about." She turned to face him fully. "So here is your last professional decision. You can take your crew off that sloop onto my deck—we'll be crowded and miserable and nobody drowns—or you can stand there holding my archivist's homework while your ship teaches you the difference between slack water and safety."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—she thought he might actually do it. He was a professional, and professionals survive by cutting losses. His mouth opened to give the order.

And that was when Ivo moved.

She hadn't told him. She realized that later, turning it over in the dark hours afterward, and it never stopped astonishing her: she hadn't told him, and he had read the whole design anyway—from the sea-cock maybe, or the decoy packet's weight in her coat, or simply from eleven years of her face—and he chose his moment to match hers. While the broker's eyes were on his sinking ship, Ivo stepped forward and took the oilskin packet off the stern bench, smooth as a pickpocket, and the broker spun back with a shout—

—and the shout died, because the sloop's anchor let go.

It didn't drag so much as *leave*, the fluke pulling free of the sand with a jerk visible even at sixty yards, and the black hull swung its stern toward the cavern mouth like a compass needle finding south. One of the crewmen screamed something. The other two were already dropping the tender over the side, oars scrabbling, and the sloop—emptying, lightening, unloved—began to travel.

"No," the broker said. It was almost a whisper. He looked at the packet in Ivo's hands, at the pardon on the bench, at the water carrying his fortune away, and Zephyrine watched a man weigh his employer's mission against his own skin and arrive, at last, at the correct answer. He went over the rail without another word, coat and all, and swam for the tender like a man half his age.

Which left the deck of the Cormorant to her, to Ivo, to the pardon, and to the packet.

"Burn it or keep it?" Ivo shouted, holding the copies up, spray whipping off the pages.

"Keep it! The copies live!" She grabbed the pardon off the bench—she wasn't a fool; leverage was leverage, even unsigned, even despised—and then, on impulse she would examine for the rest of her life, she hurled it after the broker. It caught the wind, planed once across the grey water like a wounded bird, and settled in the wake of the departing tender. Let him explain to his employers why the pardon came back unsigned and soaked. Let the paperwork bury itself.

The sloop crossed the seam of the deep channel.

She went gracefully, in the end—no drama, no broach, just the black hull settling by the stern as the channel took her, the water folding over her rail with a sound like a sigh, and then the fold, and then the foam, and then nothing but a spreading slick of oil and one floating fender turning slowly in the suck. The Current drank her and asked for more.

"The chest," Ivo said suddenly, white-faced, gripping the rail. "Zeph—the chest is still in the strongroom. We left it lashed for the second dive—"

"I know."

"If the caverns flood—if the pull reaches into the inner galleries—"

"I know." She was already at the winch, hauling the dive lines, her voice coming out flat and strange and calm. "It's gone, Ivo. Or it will be within the hour. The deep channel runs under the third cavern—everything drains there eventually. We could dive for it, maybe, if we had a week and a death wish." She got the line coiled, turned, and found him staring at her. "We're not going to dive for it."

"You're giving it up." Not accusation. Bewilderment. "The originals. The proof. Everything we—"

"I'm giving up the *paper*." She crossed the deck, took his face in her salt-stiff hands, and made him look at her. "Listen to me. The paper was never the proof. *You* are the proof. Every name in that cipher is in your head—I've watched you recite them in your sleep. The colony knows. Salt Harbor knows. The old woman with the diver's charm knows. You cannot drown a thing that lives in forty thousand memories, Ivo, and as of tonight there are going to be a great many more than forty thousand." She let him go and stepped back. "Corvane wants the chest because Corvane thinks truth is an object. Objects sink. I'm done defending objects."

The Cormorant swung hard at her hook, bow swinging toward the cavern mouth, and the sound of the current came up out of the reef like a long intake of breath. Behind them, far off across the darkening water, the fleet's dredges boomed against the outer reef—muffled, rhythmic, patient. Corvane was still closing his fist. He just didn't know yet that it was closing on nothing.

Ivo looked at the slick of oil where the sloop had been. At the floating fender. At her.

"You planned this," he said. "Before he ever boarded. You planned it and you didn't tell me."

"I was going to tell you."

"When?"

"When it was over." She managed a crooked smile. "Old habit. Guides don't scare their clients on the pitch."

"I'm not your client."

"No," she agreed, and the word landed somewhere in her chest and stayed there, warm, inconvenient, true. "You're not."

The anchor came up foul and reluctant, the Current already worrying at the ketch's keel, wanting to draw her toward the mouths too. Zephyrine took the tiller. There was one channel out of this anchorage that ran against the pull, a thread of counter-current along the northern shelf that existed on no chart and lived only in her memory and her muscles, and it was narrow, and it was fast, and it was theirs.

"Hold on to something," she said, "and whatever you see, don't pray out loud. It puts me off."

The Cormorant leaned into the stream and ran.

Behind them the deep channel swallowed the last of the oil slick, and somewhere beneath it, in the cold and the dark, the chest of Vessany settled into the silence it had kept for forty years—unopened by empire, unread by admirals, its secrets already loose in the world on paper that floated and in mouths that would not be shut. Ahead, the reef boomed and boomed, each detonation a little closer, a little harder, shaking grit from the cabin beams.

And far out at the fleet's anchorage, a flagship showed a sudden scatter of lights, as if a man there had just received news he did not believe.
