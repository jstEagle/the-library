## 27. Corvane Closes the Net

The dredge *Iron Meridian* struck the reef at dawn, and Admiral Lucen Corvane felt the blow through the deck of his flagship like a heartbeat that was not his own.

He stood at the stern rail of the *Vigilant Crown*, coffee untouched on the binnacle beside him, and watched the outer reef of the Sable Current come apart in stages. The first charge had gone off before light—a muffled cough beneath the water, a white bloom of spray, then a long rolling shudder that traveled up through the hull plates and into the soles of his boots. Now the dredges were working in earnest: three great iron-hulled machines anchored in a line across the shoal, their crane arms dipping and rising with the patience of animals feeding, tearing coral and limestone from the spine of the drowned nation and letting it fall back as rubble.

Forty years of patience, he thought. And now four days to undo what the sea had spent forty years perfecting.

"Admiral." Lieutenant Haskel came up the ladder at the quick, careful pace of a man who had learned not to run on this ship. "Dredge Two reports the channel mouth is down another fathom. The divers' survey confirms—the cavern system runs under the whole eastern shelf. If we keep cutting the outer face, the interior galleries will start shedding."

"They'll start shedding anyway," Corvane said. "That is rather the point."

Haskel hesitated. He was young enough that hesitation still showed on him, a flicker at the corner of the mouth, a weight shifting from one foot to the other. Corvane found he could no longer remember being that young—that particular honesty of the body, before a career taught it stillness.

"The dive teams, sir," Haskel said. "We have six men in the water on the western approach. If the interior begins to collapse—"

"Then recall them." Corvane did not turn from the rail. "Recall them now, in fact. Signal all boats. No further dives today or any day. The site is closed."

"Closed, sir?"

Corvane turned then, and let the lieutenant see his face fully, because there were moments when clarity was kinder than gentleness. "The wreck of the *Vessani Star* is hereby sealed as an imperial grave site under Article Nine of the Maritime Succession Act. No salvage. No entry. No recovery of remains. The dead will be left to the Current, which has kept them well enough until now." He paused. "You may find the wording in the fleet orders I signed last night. They are already distributed."

Haskel's eyes went briefly to the horizon—to the east, where the headlands ran ragged against the morning haze—and Corvane knew exactly what the boy was thinking, because every man on this fleet was thinking it. The ketch. The two fugitives. The chest that had surfaced like a body refusing to stay buried.

"You believe they're already inside," Haskel said quietly.

"I believe," Corvane said, "that if they are inside, they are exactly where the empire can account for them. And if they are not inside—" he lifted his chin toward the dredges, the falling rock, the white scars spreading along the reef face, "—then we are simply doing our duty ahead of schedule."

* * *

His father had stood on a deck once too. That was the inheritance no portrait quite captured.

In the great cabin, behind a locked drawer, Corvane kept the painting: a small thing, oil on board, a young officer in the grey-and-gold of the old Verlaine Navy standing at the rail of a ship whose name had been painted out of the registry and out of history. Rear Admiral Edmun Corvane, commanding officer of the evacuation interdiction squadron, autumn of the sinking year. In the portrait he looked tired and resolute, a man carrying something heavy and setting his jaw against its weight. Lucen had been nine years old when the order was signed. He had been eleven when his father came home thinner, quieter, with a commendation sealed in a drawer and a silence sealed around it, and he had been twenty-two when his father finally spoke of it—one night, one glass of brandy, one sentence that Lucen had carried ever since like a splinter working toward the heart:

*"Someone had to make sure nothing floated up."*

Nothing floating up. That had been the mission, dressed in whatever language the ministries preferred—security, stability, the peaceful resolution of a succession crisis. Vessany had been drowning by treaty; the treaty required that its ending be clean. But the *Vessani Star* had gone down carrying more than refugees, and a clean ending could not afford witnesses, and so his father had made sure the witnesses went down with the proof, and had spent the rest of his life making certain the sea kept its mouth shut.

And the sea had. For forty years. Until a flood tide, a salvager's net, and one blacklisted woman with a boat and no sense of her own insignificance had dragged a piece of the past up into the light.

Corvane poured himself the brandy his father would have drunk and did not drink it. He had learned that trick early: hold the glass, smell it, set it down. His father had never mastered it. He had died at sixty-one with his liver ruined and his conscience apparently intact, whispering on the last night that he had saved ten thousand lives by burying five hundred, and that arithmetic was the whole of morality, and Lucen should never let anyone make him ashamed of a sum done correctly.

Lucen had built his entire career on that sum. Every posting, every promotion, every quiet word passed along by men who understood what his name meant—he had climbed the ladder of his father's secret rung by rung, and now, at fifty-three, he commanded the very fleet sent to finish the work. There were those who would call it irony. He called it filial duty.

The trouble was the documents. Not the treasure—treasure was negotiable, deniable, dissolvable into ledgers and private hands. Documents were different. A signed order in a dead admiral's hand, naming names, describing the scuttling charges placed against the *Vessani Star*'s hull while her passengers slept—an order bearing the countersignature of three ministry officials, one of whose grandson now sat in the Regent's cabinet—if that surfaced, the sum itself would be questioned. And when the sum was questioned, everything built upon it settled. Careers. Fortunes. The comfortable fiction that the empire's expansion along the Verlaine coast had been a rescue and not a drowning.

So the papers had to go into the deep again. And anything that had touched them had to go with them.

He had told himself, in the first days, that it need not come to that. That the fugitives would be taken alive, the papers recovered intact, the matter handled with the discretion the service was famous for. He had even drafted the offer—a pardon for the woman, a pension for the archivist, exile somewhere soft and far away—in the honest belief that reasonable people prefer reasonable outcomes.

But the ketch had slipped the harbor at slack water with the broker's men twenty minutes behind it. It had grounded the broker's sloop on the Tail sandbar with a seamanship that bordered on insolence. It had vanished into fog, into shoals, into the maze of channels that appeared on no chart the fleet possessed—and Corvane had understood, watching the pursuit reports accumulate like snow, that he was dealing with someone who knew these waters the way his father had known the corridors of the Admiralty: intimately, illegally, and better than anyone officially responsible for them.

Reasonable people, it turned out, were not always available.

* * *

At midmorning he convened his officers in the flag cabin, and the map lay open between them: the Sable Current, the drowned coast, the reef like a broken necklace strung along the shelf edge, and beneath it, in dotted lines borrowed from a survey forty years old and never meant for public eyes, the cavern system.

"Here." Corvane's finger rested on the eastern shelf. "Three primary exits. The outer mouth, here at the reef face—which my dredges are presently reducing to gravel. The northern fissure, too narrow for a ketch but passable to swimmers. And the southern gallery, which the old survey marks as flooded and unstable." He straightened. "By nightfall, the outer mouth will be impassable. By tomorrow noon, I want the northern fissure sealed as well—concrete, rock, whatever the engineers recommend. The southern gallery we leave. It does the work for us."

Silence around the table. Captain Orelli of the *Iron Meridian*, a broad weathered man who had dredged harbors from one end of the empire to the other, studied the map with the expression of a craftsman being asked to do ugly work.

"You're entombing the site," Orelli said. "Permanently."

"I am closing a grave."

"With anyone inside it."

Corvane met his eyes. "With anyone who chose to break into it. Captain, let us be precise about what has happened here. Two individuals—one of them a convicted smuggler barred from two nations, the other a former imperial employee who stole state property—have entered a restricted maritime site containing classified material of the highest sensitivity. Their presence endangers the security of the realm. My responsibility is the security of the realm." He laid each clause down like a stone in a wall. "If they die in the attempt they chose to make, the law calls that consequence, not murder. I have checked. I had the advocates check it twice."

"And your conscience?" Orelli asked. It was not insubordination; it was something worse, something almost gentle, a veteran's question put plainly because he might not get another chance to put it.

Corvane thought of his father's voice. *Someone had to make sure nothing floated up.*

"My conscience," he said, "is a professional instrument, Captain, and it is calibrated. You have your orders."

After the others filed out, Orelli lingered at the door. "My son is stationed on the patrol cutter *Kestrel*," he said. "Off the estuary. If your sealed site and my dredging operations happen to shift the currents along that stretch of coast—" he did not finish it.

"Then the *Kestrel* will keep a respectful distance," Corvane said, "and you will speak to no one about currents. Dismissed."

Alone, he went to the locked drawer and took out the portrait and set it on the chart table, propped against the compass binnacle, facing the map. His father gazed out over the drowned coast with that tired, resolute look, and Corvane imagined showing him this: the fleet, the plan, the net drawing closed. *It ends here, Father. Nothing floats up. Nothing ever floats up.*

He almost believed it. That was the mercy of belief—it arrived most easily at the moments when doubt would be unbearable.

* * *

The sealing began at dusk.

From the quarterdeck Corvane watched the work boats swarm the outer reef in the last red light, lowering cut stone and quick-set concrete into the wound his dredges had opened. The sound carried across the water: the groan of winches, the splash and grind of rock finding rock, and underneath it all the deep irregular thunder of the sea testing its new obstruction and finding it, for now, sufficient.

The tide turned at half past seven. He felt it in the ship—the slow swing at anchor, the current's voice changing pitch against the hull—and he thought: somewhere below that turning water, in the dark, in the cold, there are either two corpses or two fools, and by morning the difference will no longer matter.

A signal lamp blinked from the reef line. Haskel read it aloud: *"Outer mouth closed. Divers confirm no passage. Northern fissure operation commencing at first light."*

"Very good."

"Admiral—" Haskel lowered the lamp. His face in the lamplight was pale, and younger than ever. "The watch sighted smoke earlier. West of the headland. A single fire, brief, then doused. Could have been fishermen."

Could have been. Corvane looked west, where the dark had already swallowed the headlands, and felt the first cold thread of doubt work through the certainty he had woven so carefully all day. Fishermen doused their fires carelessly. Fugitives doused them carefully. And a fire lit only to be seen, only to be counted—

"Signal the *Kestrel*," he said. "Patrol the estuary approaches. And double the picket boats off the southern gallery." He heard his own voice go flat and hard, the voice his father must have used on the last night, giving the last order. "If anything surfaces—anything at all—I am to be woken. Do you understand me, Lieutenant? Anything."

"Aye, Admiral."

Haskel went. Corvane remained at the rail with the brandy he still had not drunk, and below his keel the Current ran on through its sealed and darkening halls, patient as arithmetic, carrying whatever it carried toward the morning.

Far out past the reef, where no picket boat thought to look, the sea gave a small, deep shudder—as if something far below had begun, very quietly, to move.
