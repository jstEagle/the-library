## 28. Trapped Below the World

**IVO**

The dark came down on them like a lid.

Ivo heard it before he understood it—a sound like the world's largest door closing, stone grinding against stone somewhere above and behind them, and then the lamp flame in Zephyrine's hand guttered sideways as the air pressure shifted. Dust sifted through the light in a slow golden rain. Somewhere in the black beyond the lamp's reach, water was finding new paths, filling old ones, rearranging the architecture of a drowned nation around two living bodies.

"Zephyrine." His voice sounded small. "The east passage."

"I know." She was already moving toward it, lamp held high, and he followed her shadow along the gallery wall—past the treasury alcove they had sealed behind them with their own hands, past the skeletons of the men who had carried Vessany's gold home to die beside it. He had not wanted to leave them. Some part of him had wanted to lie down among them and be done with forty years of carrying what could not be put down.

Then the ceiling spoke again, deeper this time, a groan that traveled up through the soles of his boots, and the east passage exhaled a breath of cold salt air that smelled of dynamite.

"No," Zephyrine said quietly. It was not a question. She raised the lamp and the light found what her nose already knew: the passage mouth half-choked with fresh rubble, and beyond it, through the cracks, the flicker of disturbed water. The fleet's charges had done their work. The tremors Corvane had been sending into the reef for two days had finally reached this deep, and the caverns were settling like an old house in an earthquake, one room at a time.

"The west galleries, then." Ivo turned. "The route we came in by—"

The sound that answered him was not a groan but a crack, sharp as a rifle shot, directly overhead.

He did not remember deciding to move. He remembered Zephyrine's hand hitting his chest, shoving him sideways, and then the world came apart above them. Stone the size of a man's torso sheared from the roof of the gallery and came down through the lamplight, and he threw his arm up—not to shield himself, some animal arithmetic told him there was no shielding against that weight, but blindly, uselessly—and the edge of it caught him across the shoulder and flung him into the wall.

The pain arrived late, the way pain does when the body has more urgent business. When it did arrive it came as a white heat from his left shoulder down to his elbow, and he realized he was on his knees in six inches of freezing water with the lamp rolling away from him, its flame drowning, and Zephyrine was saying his name in a voice stripped of everything but function.

"Ivo. Ivo. Look at me."

He looked at her. Her face floated in the sudden near-darkness, lit only by the second lamp she'd snatched up, and behind her the fallen rock sat where it had landed, missing him by the width of a prayer.

"My arm," he managed. "I can't—" He tried to lift it and the shoulder answered with a nausea so complete his vision went grey at the edges. Not broken, he thought distantly, cataloguing like the archivist he had been all his life. Torn. Dislocated or torn. The arm would answer to him eventually, but not quickly, and not without cost.

She got her hands under him and hauled him upright, and for a moment they stood pressed together in the dark while the cavern ticked and settled around them, and Ivo thought: *this is how Vessany died.* Not in fire, not in battle. In the dark, in the water, listening to the weight of the world rearrange itself overhead, waiting for a door that never opened.

"The exits," he said. "Both of them."

"Gone or going." Her voice was flat, which he had learned meant she was thinking at full speed. "The east is buried. The west will follow within the hour—the whole reef's shifting. And even if it didn't, the lagoon above the west mouth is full of dredges. We'd surface into their nets."

"Then we're sealed in."

She didn't answer. He felt her turn, felt the lamplight swing across the gallery, across the water, across the frescoed walls where refugee painters had made a cathedral of a sea cave four decades ago—saints with fish tails, a sunken city rendered in blues that still burned after forty years of brine. He had read those walls aloud to her two nights ago. *We were here. Remember us.* 

Now the remembering was going to be a tomb.

His legs wanted to fold. He let himself slide down until he was sitting in the shallows, cradling the arm, and the despair that came over him was not dramatic; it was administrative, a clerk's despair, the quiet recognition that the ledger balanced against them and there was nothing left to enter. The air pocket above them was large—he could hear it, the hollow resonance when the water moved—but pockets breathed, pockets shrank, and the tide was coming back in. Six hours, maybe eight, if the rock held. It would not hold.

"Zephyrine." He said it because someone should say something, because silence in a tomb felt like agreement. "If we can't get out—take the copies. The copies are sewn into your coat. If you can find any way, any crack, any—"

"Stop."

"—then at least the truth—"

"*Stop.*" She crouched in front of him and took his face in both hands, and her palms were cold and wet and absolutely steady. "I have spent eleven years being the woman who survives things other people don't, and I am telling you that we are not dead yet, and I do not say that to comfort you. I say it because I need your mind working, because yours is the mind that reads ashes, and mine is the mind that reads water, and between us we are going to find a door that Corvane doesn't know exists. Do you understand me?"

He looked at her—at the salt drying white in her hair, at the fury banked down behind her eyes like coal—and something in his chest unclenched. Not hope, exactly. Something harder than hope. The thing hope becomes when it stops asking permission.

"Tell me about the old woman," he said.

* * *

**ZEPHYRINE**

The old woman's name had been Maren, though she hadn't offered it; Zephyrine had learned it later, whispered by the colony's children. What Maren had offered, pressing the diver's charm of carved whalebone into Zephyrine's palm, was this:

*"You'll be tempted to trust the ways men made. Doorways, channels, the routes drawn on charts. Don't. The Current makes its own doors, girl, and it keeps them secret out of spite. When the rock closes, look for where the water wants to go. Water always wants to go home."*

At the time it had sounded like the kind of blessing old women give divers: true enough to be useless. Now, sitting in a shrinking pocket of air beneath a collapsing reef with a wounded archivist and two lamps, Zephyrine turned the words over like a coin she'd finally earned the right to spend.

Water always wants to go home.

She stood, waded to the gallery's far wall, and held the lamp low over the waterline. "Ivo. The tide. Which way is it running?"

He blinked at her, pain-drunk, then the archivist surfaced through the man. "Flood. It turned—" he checked some internal clock she'd never been able to catch him consulting, "—an hour ago. Why?"

"Because the flood comes in through the outer reef, through every crack and channel in this system, and it's coming in *now*, under pressure, looking for volume." She moved the lamp along the wall, watching the water. "And this gallery used to connect to the third cavern. The treasury gallery. The captain's route. That means somewhere along this wall there's a channel the flood tide knows and we don't."

"The charts show nothing there."

"The charts show nothing anywhere. That's rather the point of me." But her eyes stayed on the water, and slowly, slowly, she began to see it. Where the flood pressed in against the gallery's dead end, the surface didn't lie flat. Along the base of the wall, just below the lamp's reach, there was a seam in the disturbance—a line where the chop ran backward against itself, the way current runs when it meets current, when water coming in finds water already moving out.

Or already moving *through*.

"There." She knelt, plunged her good arm into the black water, and felt along the submerged rock. Cold, then colder, then a thread of movement—faint, insistent, directional. A draft, underwater. The cavern breathing through a keyhole. "There's a channel behind this wall. Small. Flood-fed. It goes *somewhere*."

"Somewhere could be a crack wide enough for a finger."

"Somewhere could be the third way." She pulled her arm out, flexing feeling back into her fingers. "Maren warned me about the inner caverns. She also said—" Zephyrine closed her eyes, hauling the memory up whole. "*When the rock closes, look for where the water wants to go.* She knew these caves. Her people sheltered here during the evacuation. If anyone alive remembers a route the charts lost, it's her people, and she gave me the route in the only language she trusted—an old woman's riddle."

Ivo had gone very still. "The flooded gallery," he said slowly. "The treasury gallery. The third cavern connects to the sea through the old harbor channel—it must, the evacuation fleet loaded the treasury *by boat*—"

"And the fleet never sailed. So the channel was never blasted shut, never charted as a hazard, never worth an imperial engineer's attention." Zephyrine felt the plan assembling itself in her chest with the cold clarity of a knot tying. "Corvane sealed the doors he knew about. He sealed the doors his father's maps knew about. But the treasury channel isn't on imperial maps. It's on *Vessani* maps. Maps that exist now only in the heads of survivors and in the documents rotting in my coat."

"It will be flooded. Fully flooded, if it runs below the old harbor level."

"Yes."

"And narrow."

"Probably."

"And you're proposing we dive it. Blind. With one working arm between us and lamps that last an hour."

Zephyrine looked at him—at the bloodless set of his mouth, the arm bound in strips of his own shirt, the absurd, unbowed straightness of his spine—and she felt the despair that had been circling her all morning finally break and burn off, converted in a single hot instant into something with teeth.

"I'm proposing," she said, "that we drown in the attempt instead of the waiting. I've done the arithmetic of both, and I prefer ours."

* * *

**IVO**

They prepared the way scholars prepare for war: methodically, obsessively, with whatever lay at hand.

Ivo sat against the frescoed wall and directed operations with his right hand while his left hung in its sling of shirt-linen, throbbing in time with his heartbeat. Zephyrine worked like a woman possessed—not frantic, never frantic, but continuous, a machine that had been switched on and would not be switched off until it reached its purpose. She waterproofed the document copies in oilskin and bound them flat against her ribs. She lashed the lamps to cork floats salvaged from the Cormorant's emergency kit, so that light could travel ahead of them through the channel like lantern-fish. She cut a length of salvage line and knotted it around both their waists with the particular tension of someone who had once watched a partner drift out of reach in white water and had never allowed it since.

"Talk to me," she said, testing each knot. "Keep your mind busy. Tell me what happens if we live."

Ivo watched her work and considered the question seriously, because she deserved seriousness. "If we live, we surface in the old harbor basin. Inside the lagoon. Behind the fleet."

"That occurred to me."

"Corvane's flagship sits in the middle of it."

"That also occurred to me." She looked up, and in the lamplight her smile was not comforting in the least. "Ivo, I spent three days charting that anchorage from the headland while you decoded. I know where every hull lies, where the dredge lines run, where the flagship's boats are moored. If we come up inside the lagoon, we don't come up empty-handed. We come up behind the man who buried us, holding the proof he drowned a nation to destroy, standing in the one place he cannot bombard without sinking his own fleet."

Ivo stared at her. Then, despite the shoulder, despite the tomb, despite everything, he laughed—a short, disbelieving bark that echoed strangely off the painted saints. "You intend to steal his own anchorage out from under him."

"I intend," Zephyrine said, rising and pulling him gently to his feet, "to make him regret ever learning his father's name. Now. The channel."

They stood together at the seam in the wall where the water breathed. Zephyrine took three long, deliberate breaths—the rhythm she'd taught him in the inner dark, counted breaths, discipline against terror—and pressed the lamp-float into his good hand.

"When the pocket narrows, we go single file. You first—you're lighter, and if you wedge, I can pull you through. I follow. The line keeps us honest. We surface or we don't, but we do it *together*, and we do not stop moving. Stopping is the killer. Stopping is how the cave keeps you." She met his eyes. "Say it back."

"We go. We don't stop. Together."

"Together." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under a boat—something that had nothing to do with caves or currents—and she leaned forward and kissed him, brief and fierce, tasting of salt and iron and decision. "For luck," she said. "Vessani custom. I read it on the wall."

"That is not what the wall says."

"It is now. Dive."

She went under, and the line snapped taut, and Ivo filled his lungs with the last of the dying air and followed her into the throat of the mountain.

The channel was exactly as bad as they'd feared and exactly as passable as they'd prayed. It narrowed until the rock pressed his shoulders, until the lamp-float ahead showed him only Zephyrine's heels kicking above him and the pale worm of her wake. Once the passage pinched so tight he had to exhale and turn his head sideways to grind through, the stone scraping his injured arm so that sparks of white pain burst behind his eyes and he screamed into the water, a useless bloom of bubbles, and kept moving, because stopping was the killer. The line sang taut behind him. She was there. He kept moving.

And then the pinch released, and the channel opened, and the water around him changed—warmer by a degree, moving with a new current, and above him the blackness lifted into a vast grey-green gloom shot through with drifting motes, and Ivo rose through it with his lungs burning and his heart hammering a Vessani word he hadn't spoken aloud in thirty years—

—and broke the surface into air.

Air, real air, moving air, in a space so large his first breath barely echoed. He trod water, coughing, blinking salt and darkness from his eyes, and found the lamp-float bobbing beside him and Zephyrine surfacing next to it, hair plastered black to her skull, grinning at him like a wolf.

Above them, faint and far, filtered down through fissures in the rock: the sound of dredges. The fleet. They had come up inside the ring.

But it was not the fleet that made Ivo forget his ruined arm, forget the drowning, forget everything. It was what the lamplight touched when Zephyrine lifted it high.

Shelves. Racks. Crates stacked in the careful geometry of a cargo hold, tarred and corded, stretching back into the dark farther than the light could reach. And glinting along the nearest rack, where forty years of water had stripped the wrapping from a corner: gold. Coin-gold, ingot-gold, the dull patient gleam of a nation's ransom, sitting in the dark where no one had ever come for it.

"The treasury," Ivo whispered. His voice cracked on the second word. "It was here. It was *here* all along—they never moved it, they couldn't, the fleet sank before—"

"Later." Zephyrine's grin faded into focus. She was scanning the edges of the chamber, reading the waterline, reading the rock, and Ivo saw her expression change. "Ivo. Look at the water. Look at how fast it's climbing."

He looked. The tide mark on the shelving was higher than it should have been. The flood was filling this chamber too, faster than it should, fed by the same pressure that had sealed them in—and somewhere in this vast dark hall there would be an exit, or there wouldn't, and either way the fleet's next charge was already falling somewhere overhead, and the whole reef was coming down around the treasure it had guarded for forty years.

Zephyrine turned to him, and in the lamplight her eyes held no despair at all—only that ferocity, banked and blazing, the fire of a woman who had decided the world would not get to bury her twice.

"Find me a boat," she said. "There were skeletons in the gallery below. There were men loading this treasure when the order came. Which means somewhere in this room, Ivo, there are oars."
