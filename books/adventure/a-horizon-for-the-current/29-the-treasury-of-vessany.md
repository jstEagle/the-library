## 29. The Treasury of Vessany

The water in the gallery was still.

That was the first thing Ivo understood, and the last thing he would ever stop understanding: the water here did not move. In the outer caverns the Sable Current came through like a living thing, shoving and worrying at everything it touched, but past the third seal—past the throat Zephyrine had swum them through with her lamp held in her teeth—the sea simply stood. It stood as if it were waiting. Forty years of tides had come and gone above them, and down here the water had kept its vigil without once stirring the dust of what lay beneath.

His lamp beam crossed it and the dust rose in slow galaxies, gold in the light, and then settled again, unhurried, back onto the things it had covered for four decades.

"Steady," Zephyrine said behind him. Her voice came strange through the air pocket they'd found—a low vaulted space where the ceiling dipped to meet the waterline, leaving a wedge of breathable dark no taller than a man kneeling. "Breathe through your nose. Don't splash. The gallery runs another forty paces east, if my grandmother's chart was honest."

"It was honest," Ivo said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, flat and certain, the way certain things do when they have been waiting too long to be said. "I've read the surveyor's marginalia. The third gallery was called *the Long Room*. They kept the state records there during the last winter. And after—" He stopped. His throat closed on the rest.

After, they had brought the treasury.

Zephyrine's hand found his shoulder in the dark, brief and hard, and then she was moving, swimming low and quiet with the economy that never ceased to frighten him a little—the way she moved through drowned places like something that had been granted a standing exemption from drowning. Ivo followed, kicking gently, one hand trailing along the wall because he could not help himself. The stone under his fingers was dressed masonry, Vessani work, the blocks fitted so finely he could not find the joints. His people had built this. His people had cut these stones and known, even then, that they were building for the water. There was a word for that kind of foresight in old Vessani—*salvëth*, shelter-against-the-flood—and his grandfather had used to say it was the only prayer their nation had ever really written.

The gallery opened.

He felt it before he saw it: the pressure of the walls falling away, the echo of his own breathing suddenly given room. Then Zephyrine's lamp swept left, and Ivo forgot how to breathe.

Gold.

Not the gold of coins scattered loose, not the gold of a pirate's fantasy. This was a nation's gold, arranged, accounted, stacked with the terrible tidiness of people who believed someone would come back to count it. Chests in ranks, iron-banded, their lids still seated. Sacks of minted coin slumped against them like sleeping animals. Candlesticks from the Basilica of the Tides, wrapped in oilcloth gone to lace. The great processional chain of the Lord Warden of Harbors, coiled in its case like a serpent of suns. Plate from a hundred houses. Signet rings in velvet trays. The reserve bullion of the Bank of Vessany, stamped with the wave-and-lamp seal he had spent forty years forbidden to draw, and which he now drew, unconsciously, in the water with one finger, over and over, like a man checking his own pulse.

"It's all here," Zephyrine said softly. Her voice had changed. He knew that voice; she used it for wrecks, for things that deserved reverence whether or not anyone living remembered why. "Every ledger we decoded said the treasury went aboard the evacuation fleet. Corvane's father signed the order to sink it. Everyone assumed the salvage crews took it up afterward." She turned her lamp slowly across the ranks of chests. "They never came. Or they came and couldn't find this."

"They never came," Ivo said. "I'd know. I've read every salvage manifest the empire filed for this coast. There's nothing. A blank. I always thought the blank was a lie covering theft." He heard his own voice go strange. "It wasn't a lie. It was an omission. They wrote nothing because there was nothing to write. They sank a nation's whole wealth and then they simply—" He made himself say it. "—left it."

*Abandonment.* The word arrived in him whole, the way a bone arrives when it finally breaks.

Because that was the second death. He had spent his life inside the first one—the scuttling, the order, the names in the captain's ledger, the officials drowned rather than ransomed. That death had a signature on it, an author, a crime you could point to. The empire had killed Vessany deliberately, and the empire could be hated cleanly for it. But this—this gold sitting patient in the dark for forty years while the survivors scattered to cliff villages and foreign quarters and graves in other people's soil—this death had no author. It had only an absence. The nation had died twice: once by order, and once by the simple, unforgivable fact that nobody had ever come back for it.

And he knew why nobody had come back. He was why, in part. His family's name was a byword for cooperation in the colony at Ravenreach. The old woman had spat it. *Vessari. The ones who helped them count what was left.* His grandfather had catalogued confiscated papers for the imperial archive, and his father had done the same, and Ivo had done the same, each generation telling itself it was preserving what could not be saved, keeping embers in ash, and all the while the true treasure of everything they claimed to mourn had lain forty years beneath their feet, waiting for a blacklisted guide from Verlaine and a disgraced archivist to blunder into it through a passage no chart recorded.

"Ivo." Zephyrine's voice, closer now. "You're shaking."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. Look at me."

He looked at her. Her face was half lamp-shadow, salt-streaked, exhausted, lit from below by the water's reflected gold until she seemed to stand in the middle of a small burning. She had swum him through a collapsing throat of rock with a broken rib strapped and three fingers of air left in his tank, and now she was looking at him as though his grief were cargo she intended personally to see delivered.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is. Say it out loud. It doesn't keep well in the dark."

So he said it. "They didn't take it. They didn't even try. My whole life I thought the empire's cruelty was at least thorough—that they destroyed us completely, efficiently, that our suffering had a shape. But this—" his lamp hand trembled and the gold rippled across the walls like water, "—this means they didn't think we were worth finishing. They sank the fleet and walked away. The treasury wasn't hidden. It was *discarded*. We weren't even worth robbing properly." His voice cracked on the last words and he let it. "A nation dies twice, Zephyrine. Once when it's killed. Once when everyone agrees to stop looking for the body. And I—" 

"And you just found the body," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"Then you're the first mourner in forty years." She held out her hand. "Do it properly, then. Not for me. For them."

* * *

They went deeper along the gallery, because the gold was not the thing. Ivo understood that within the first hundred paces. The gold was the wound's dressing; the wound was further in.

The Long Room narrowed and then widened again into a final chamber, and here the masonry gave way to rougher work—cut stone reused, salvaged, fitted by hands working fast and badly in the last days. Refugee work. And here the lamp found them.

The skeletons sat in rows.

Not thrown. Not piled. *Seated*, along both walls of the chamber, perhaps thirty of them, arranged with their backs to the stone as though resting between tasks. Some still wore the rotted remnants of uniform—the dark blue of the Treasury Guard, the braid long since dissolved to thread. Others wore civilian cloth, coats and dresses gone to shadow. Between them, along the floor, ran the loading gear: hand-carts on rails, slings, tally-slates still hanging from hooks. They had been carrying the treasury out. Loading it, chest by chest, onto boats that were meant to run the blockade and carry it to whatever fragment of the nation survived in exile.

The boats were still here. One lay keel-up against the far wall, holed. Another was simply gone—no planks, no wreckage, nothing, as if it had been taken or had never existed at all except as a promise.

Ivo drifted down the row of them, and his lamp shook, and he did not try to steady it.

Near the entrance to the chamber, apart from the others, sat a single skeleton smaller than the rest, and beside it, where a child's hand might have set it down, a wooden toy boat with a painted sail, the paint still faintly red after forty years in the dark.

He heard Zephyrine make a sound behind him. Small. Quickly silenced. When he turned, she had her fist pressed to her mouth, and her eyes were bright and furious, and she was looking at the toy boat the way she had once told him she looked at avalanche slopes—counting, measuring, refusing to look away because looking away was how people died.

"They waited," Ivo said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Look at the tallies. Look—they're *organized*. Someone was keeping count of the chests as they loaded. They expected relief. Another boat, another tide. They sat down to wait, and the water came up, and they—" He could not finish it. He made himself finish it. "They kept order. Even at the end. Even drowning in the dark, they kept the count. Because that's what a nation *is*, isn't it? Not the gold. The count. The agreement that the numbers matter, that someone will come to check them."

"No one came," Zephyrine said.

"No one came." He knelt in the shallows at the row's end, water to his waist, and looked at the nearest skeleton's face—or the place where a face had been—and spoke to it in Vessani, the old formal register, the language of ledgers and last testaments, because it was the only language he had that was equal to the moment. *We are here. We are late. We are few. Forgive us.*

The bones said nothing. The water said nothing. But the gallery held the words the way it had held everything else—patiently, completely, without letting any of it drift away—and Ivo understood that this, too, was an answer.

* * *

They worked after that, because grief that cannot become work becomes drowning, and Zephyrine had taught him that without ever saying it. They could not take the treasury—Ivo knew it before she said it, saw the arithmetic already moving behind her eyes: two divers, one damaged ketch, six hours of slack, a fleet sealing the exits overhead. The gold would stay. The gold had stayed forty years; it could stay forever, if staying was what protected it.

But the documents—the strongroom ledger, the decoded pages, the final cipher key they had risked everything to reach—those were small, and light, and they fit inside a waterproof satchel against Ivo's chest, and those were the nation's true remaining wealth. Ink outlasted gold. Ink was the only thing the empire had never figured out how to sink.

Still, before they left, Ivo did one more thing.

He found the tally-slates. Thirty-one of them hung on their hooks, chalk marks ghosted but legible—chest counts, weights, initials of the guards who had kept the record to the end. He copied every mark into his notebook by lamplight, every initial, every number, his handwriting steady because he forced it to be. Names. He had spent his whole career among confiscated papers, cataloguing the empire's evidence against his own people, memorizing what he was forbidden to keep. Now, at last, he was keeping the right things. These initials were the closest thing Vessany had left to a roll call, and a roll call, he had learned in the free-port reading halls of his childhood, is the seed of every memorial ever built.

When he finished, he unhooked the toy boat from beside the small skeleton, weighed it in his palm, and—after a moment—set it back down exactly where it lay.

"Some things are grave goods," Zephyrine said. She had watched him decide.

"Yes." His throat ached. "Some things stay with their dead. That's the whole difference between a museum and a tomb. The empire never learned it." He looked up at her, and the gold light moved on her face, and he loved her with a clarity that hurt more than the cold water did. "We'll come back. When it's safe. With the colony's elders, with priests if any are left, with proper lights. We'll bring them up and put them in Vessani ground and read every name. I give you my oath on the archive."

"I don't need your oath," Zephyrine said. "But I'll hold you to it anyway." She glanced upward, toward the stone and the stone beyond it, where somewhere above the fleet's dredges were chewing the reef to gravel, and her jaw tightened. "Provided we live long enough to keep it. Tide's turning, Ivo. I can feel it in my ears. We have maybe an hour of slack left, and then this gallery starts breathing again—and when it breathes, it breathes *in*."

They began the swim back toward the throat, lamps dimmed, moving single file through the ranked gold that gleamed and guttered around them like a city seen from very far away. At the mouth of the Long Room Ivo paused, one last time, and looked back down the gallery—at the chests, at the seated guard, at the small red sail in the dark.

*Nation of mine,* he thought, in the tongue that was dying and was not dead. *Twice you died. Twice you were counted. I am taking the count out of the dark, and I will make them hear it.*

Then the current stirred, faint as a first breath, and Zephyrine touched his arm, and they swam up together into the narrowing stone—toward the sealed lagoon, the fleet, and whatever the sixth tide intended to do with them.

Above them, muffled through forty feet of rock and water, came a sound neither of them had hoped to hear so soon: the deep, rhythmic concussion of dynamite, and the reef beginning, at last, to come down.
