## 30. Sixth Tide

The water in the gallery had risen to their knees.

Zephyrine felt it first as a coldness climbing her shins, then as a sound—a soft, patient lapping against the treasury's marble steps, the way a tide tests a door it has been promised. Above them, through the crack Corvane's charges had opened in the outer reef, the sixth tide was gathering itself in the dark like a fist.

She stood at the edge of the flooded gallery with the decoded pages pressed flat against her chest inside an oilskin wrap, and she did the arithmetic one last time, because arithmetic was the only prayer she trusted.

The treasury of Vessany lay behind her: forty years of a drowned nation's gold and plate and coin, stacked in the dark exactly where its carriers had died beside it. Enough to buy a fleet. Enough to buy a pardon twice over, in two countries, with change left for a life. Enough to outrun nothing at all, because gold weighs what it weighs, and the Cormorant could carry perhaps a tenth of it before her patched seams gave out under the strain—and a tenth of a treasure is not wealth, it is a target with a receipt attached.

The documents weighed nothing. The documents could outrun everything.

"You're doing it again," Ivo said.

He sat on the third step above the waterline, his broken arm bound tight against his ribs, his face gray in the lamplight but his eyes clear—the particular clarity of a man who has already grieved for himself and found nothing there worth keeping. "Counting," he said. "You count when you've already decided."

"I haven't decided."

"You decided on the second tide, when you told me the Cormorant draws too much water to load even a tenth of this." He nodded toward the stacks of coin glinting dully where the lamp reached them, each piece furred with four decades of the sea. "You're counting now because you want me to hear the numbers and agree. So let me save us both the hour." He rose, carefully, favoring the arm. "Take the papers. Seal the rest."

"Seal it." She turned to look at him fully. "Ivo. That's Vessany down there. Not the idea of it. The actual thing. Gold struck with your seal, your kings, your—"

"My kings are bones on that floor." His voice didn't waver, but she saw his throat work once. "I counted them while you were checking the air pocket. Eleven. They were meant to carry it home, Zephyrine. Every one of them came back instead, and they've been guarding it ever since, and no one asked them to." He looked past her, into the gallery, where the water now lapped at the lowest step of coin-stacks, touching the nation it had been meant to save. "If we take even a chest of it, we make it salvage. We make it *cargo*. And everything it means dies in the carrying. If we seal it—if we give it back to the dark with the dead who carried it—then it stays what it is. A grave. A grave can testify. Cargo can only be sold."

"And the truth?" She held up the oilskin bundle. "This testifies fine without the gold."

"The truth needs witnesses who aren't bought." He almost smiled. "You taught me that. Or you will have, by the time we surface."

The tide made its argument beneath them. She felt it in the stone—a deep pressure shift, the cavern system breathing in, the whole drowned architecture of the Sable Current tightening around them like a hand closing. Somewhere far above, through rock and reef and forty fathoms of green water, the imperial dredges would be working the outer reef even now, and every hour brought the caverns closer to collapse. The old woman's warning came back to her: *the inner dark keeps what it takes.* They had come in by the third way, the flooded gallery passage, and the third way out was closing with the tide.

"We have maybe two hours of window," Zephyrine said. "Less if the reef goes early."

"Then stop arguing with a man who agrees with you."

* * *

They worked fast, because grief works fastest when it has a deadline.

The documents were already secured: the captain's order-book, the cipher key from the strongroom, Ivo's decoded copies wrapped in waxed linen inside the oilskin, and the original scuttle order with Corvane's father's signature—ink forty years old and still legible, still damning, still heavier than any gold in the room though it weighed less than a bird. Zephyrine strapped the bundle high across her back, under her arms, where swimming wouldn't shift it.

Then they turned to the treasury, and Zephyrine understood that what Ivo had called sealing was really a kind of funeral rite, and that he intended to perform it properly.

"There's powder left," she said. "From the charges. Half a keg."

"No." He said it gently, which somehow made it more absolute. "Blasting it shut tells the world there's something worth blasting toward. Let the sea do it. The sea keeps secrets better than rubble does." He crouched at the water's edge, awkward with the splinted arm, and began moving stones—flat slabs of the gallery's own facing, tumbled loose when the ceiling had shed rock onto him. "Help me build the wall. Low. Just enough that a diver with a lamp sees a collapsed gallery and turns back, and a surveyor's sonar reads it as fill. We're not hiding it forever. We're hiding it until it doesn't matter anymore—until everyone who'd strip it is dead, and all that's left is people who'd mourn it."

So they built the wall together, knee-deep in rising seawater, passing stones hand to hand in the yellow circle of the lamp. It was strange, holy work. Zephyrine had spent her life taking things out of the water—wrecks, cargo, the possessions of the drowned, sold honestly or otherwise—and here she was putting the greatest prize of her career back into the deep, stone by stone, and feeling not loss but something adjacent to it, something cleaner. Renunciation, she thought. Her mother had used the word once, about a saint. She'd never expected to learn its meaning up to her thighs in the Sable Current.

Halfway through, Ivo stopped, holding a slab, and looked at the nearest skeleton—the one lying closest to the coin, arms arranged by decay rather than design, reaching not for the treasure but outward, toward the passage home.

"He was the paymaster's clerk," Ivo said quietly. "I know his name. It's in the manifest—Aldric Vey, age thirty-one. He signed for every chest." He set the slab down atop the wall, aligned it true. "When I read the ashes in the archive, Zephyrine—when I memorized what they forbade me to keep—I thought my duty was to the paper. To the record. But it was never the paper. It was this. Making sure the men who were abandoned get to stay *someone's*, and not become inventory."

"Aldric Vey," Zephyrine said, testing the weight of it. A name was a small thing to carry out of a drowned country, but names were how nations survived being erased. "We'll say it at Salt Harbor. Him and the others. All eleven."

"All eleven." Ivo placed the last stone. "And the ones on the evacuation fleet, and the ones in the camps after, and—" He stopped himself. "Later. There'll be time for the whole list later, if we live."

The wall stood waist-high across the gallery mouth, rough and honest, reading to any future eye as simple collapse. Beyond it the treasure dimmed as the water climbed, coin by coin going dark, the nation's wealth returning to the nation's grave. The last thing visible was the topmost stack of plate, a chased silver ewer catching the lamplight one final time, and then the sea took that too, and the gallery was only a tomb again, and had been all along.

Ivo stood before it with his good hand pressed flat against the wet stone, the way a man touches a door he means to knock on again someday, in another life.

"It stays," he said. Not to her. To the dark, or to the dead, or to himself. "It stays, and it's enough."

* * *

The swim out was the worst of Zephyrine's life, and she had a long ledger of worsts to measure against.

The flooded gallery passage ran forty yards through the heart of the reef, rising in three slow terraces toward the air pocket where the Cormorant lay moored in the shrinking cavern lake. Going in, they'd had slack water and full lamps and unbroken bodies. Coming out, they had a flooding tide pushing against them through a narrowing throat, one lamp between them, and Ivo swimming one-armed with his legs cramping from cold and his breath coming short.

She went first, towing the line, counting strokes aloud so he could hold her rhythm in his ears when the dark closed in. At the first terrace the current grabbed them sideways and slammed her shoulder into rock hard enough to white out her vision for three strokes; she kept counting. At the second terrace the passage narrowed and the water shallowed and they had to go through on their backs, faces inches under the ceiling, breathing from the thin lens of trapped air along the stone, and Zephyrine heard Ivo behind her making a sound she'd never heard from him—a low, continuous note, half song and half prayer, some Vessani drowning-hymn his grandmother must have taught him—and she matched her strokes to it instead of her count, because a hymn holds a soul together better than numbers do.

At the third terrace the current simply refused them. They hung in the throat of it, kicking, gaining a body-length and losing one, the lamp beam strobing off the churning water, and Zephyrine felt the old familiar arithmetic begin—*air, distance, strength, margin*—and watched every column turn red.

Then Ivo's good hand closed on her ankle, and he pulled himself up the line hand over hand until they were level, and shouted over the roar, close to her ear:

"Together or not at all! On three—we go with the surge, not against it!"

She understood. The tide came in pulses; she'd known that since she was nine years old and the Sable Current nearly took her off the shallows at Verlaine. Between pulses there was a heartbeat of slack, even here, even in the throat of the world. You couldn't beat the sea. You could only wait for it to blink.

They hung in the black water, lungs burning, waiting for the sea to blink.

It blinked. They went.

Three strokes, five, ten—the terrace lip scraped her back, the oilskin bundle caught and tore free on one side and she jammed her elbow through the strap and held it against her body with her arm like a mother holding a child—and then the current released them all at once, spat them up the final slope, and they broke surface gasping into the air pocket, into lamplight, into the impossible ordinary sight of the Cormorant riding at her moorings with water slapping her hull like applause.

Zephyrine got an arm over the gunwale and hung there, coughing brine, laughing at nothing, at everything, at being alive specifically enough to hurt everywhere. Ivo surfaced beside her and gripped the hull with his one hand and rested his forehead against the wet planking, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, because the sea had just listened to them refuse it the richest prize on the coast, and they were both waiting to see if it minded.

It didn't mind. It never had. That was the whole lesson of the Sable Current, and she'd finally learned it: the sea doesn't punish renunciation. It punishes greed, slowly, and pride, quickly, and it lets go of anyone humble enough to travel light.

They climbed aboard.

* * *

The Cormorant came down the cavern lake under mizzen alone, silent, her seams weeping but holding, and Zephyrine steered by memory and the sound of the swell beyond the exit arch. The air pocket's ceiling was lower than on their arrival—Corvane's blasting had settled things, and fresh cracks feathered overhead, weeping threads of water. Behind them, faint through the rock, came the rhythmic concussion of the dredges working the outer reef. The empire was dismantling the roof of the world above their heads, patiently, on schedule, certain that anything buried in the collapse would stay buried.

Let it collapse, then. Let it fall on an empty grave.

At the mouth of the exit passage, Zephyrine shipped the oars and paused, one hand on the tiller, and looked back one last time into the inner dark.

Somewhere back there, behind a wall of honest stone, lay the treasury of Vessany, guarded by eleven loyal skeletons and the patience of the sea. Somewhere back there, Aldric Vey kept his books. She had taken from the drowned country only what it had come to give: the truth, the names, the signature of the man who had murdered it. Everything else it could keep, and keep well.

"You're smiling," Ivo said. He sat amidships, cradling the splinted arm, the oilskin originals transferred now to a waterproof satchel buckled to his chest—he'd insisted, and she'd let him, because a nation's papers belonged in a nation's keeper's hands, whatever else happened.

"Am I."

"You're smiling like you did when you grounded the broker's sloop. Like a woman who's just won something by giving it away." He shook his head slowly. "I don't understand you yet, Zephyrine Pemberton. But I'm beginning to trust the parts I don't understand."

"Hold that thought." She put the tiller over. "Because in about an hour you're either going to understand me completely, or we're both going to be a story other people tell."

"What's in an hour?"

She nodded forward, toward the exit arch, beyond which—she could hear it now, feel it in the hull, a deep irregular trembling in the water itself—the imperial fleet lay anchored across the lagoon, nine ships and a dredge barge, sealing the cavern mouths, hunting two divers who had gone into the inner dark and were presumed by now to be dead.

"Sixth tide," she said. "Our last window out. And the only road home runs straight through Admiral Corvane's anchorage."

She bent to the salvaged powder keg stowed under the thwart, and began, methodically, to count what was left of it.
