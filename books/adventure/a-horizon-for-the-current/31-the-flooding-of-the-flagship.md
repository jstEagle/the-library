## 31. The Flooding of the Flagship

The lagoon had become a grave with the lid nailed down.

Zephyrine stood at the Cormorant's rail and looked up at the rock that sealed them in. Corvane's engineers had done their work well—three days of blasting, methodical as a surgeon closing a wound, until the outer reef passages they'd slipped through were choked with rubble taller than her mast. The cavern mouths on the seaward side still breathed, but barely: narrow throats of water where the tide fought through in white fury, impassable to anything larger than a seal. She had watched a patrol cutter try one that morning. It had come out the far end as kindling.

Behind her, Ivo sat against the cabin bulkhead with his arm bound tight to his ribs, the rockfall having caught him in the treasury gallery. He was gray around the mouth but his eyes were clear, and he was watching her do arithmetic.

"You're going to say it," he said.

"I'm going to say it."

"Then say it plainly, because if you're wrong we die in here, and if you're right we probably die out there."

She almost smiled. "That *is* the plain version."

* * *

The plan had assembled itself in her mind over the last day, piece by piece, the way salvage comes up in a net—one thing at a time until suddenly there's a whole shape in your hands. The charges from the strongroom gallery: Vessani naval demolition stores, wax-sealed, dry after forty years behind the pressure wall, because Vessany had built things to last and the sea had honored the contract. Eleven sticks. Enough, if placed correctly, to bring down the thin wall of rock that separated the third cavern—the treasury cavern—from the flooded channel that ran beneath the fleet's anchorage.

She had felt that channel with her own body, years ago, diving the Sable Current before she knew what lay above it. A drowned riverbed, deep and wide, running under the anchorage like a cellar under a house. The imperial charts showed nothing there. Her memory showed everything.

Breach the wall, and the sea would take the shortest path between two pressures. The lagoon would drain—or rather, the sea would pour *in*, down through the old riverbed, and the anchorage above would shudder and sink its ships by the bow, or split them open on the rising turbulence, or simply swallow whatever moorings weren't built for a current that could move boulders. Nine ships. Dredges. The flagship, *Imperator's Mercy*, riding fat and arrogant at anchor directly over the deepest part of the channel.

"You understand," Ivo said quietly, "that when that wall goes, this whole cavern system becomes part of the ocean again. There will be no air pockets. No third way out. Whatever happens next, we ride it out on the deck of this boat or not at all."

"I know."

"And the flagship—there are men on those ships. Sailors. Boys, some of them, probably."

Zephyrine closed her eyes. She had done this accounting already, more than once, lying awake in the dark while the tide ticked against the hull. "Corvane sealed us in here to drown," she said. "He'll have posted picket boats outside the rubble. But the anchorage itself—the main fleet—he won't expect anything to come *up* out of the ground beneath him. And a flooding ship launches its boats. That's not mercy, Ivo. That's physics. The sea does the rescuing whether Corvane likes it or not." She opened her eyes. "I'm not trying to kill sailors. I'm trying to kill a lie. The flagship is just what's standing on top of it."

Ivo was silent a long moment. Then he said, "My grandfather used to say the Vessani never lost a battle they chose themselves."

"He sounds like he'd have liked you better than you deserve."

"He did." Something moved across his face—grief, humor, love, all braided too tight to separate. "All right, Zephyrine. Show me where you want the charges."

* * *

They worked through the ebb, when the water in the third cavern fell low enough to expose the base of the pressure wall—a shelf of layered limestone no thicker than her forearm, veined with cracks forty years old. She drilled the charge holes with a hand auger while Ivo held the lamp, his bound arm braced against a boulder, and read her the depths off a knotted line by lamplight because his hands shook too badly to trust with the auger.

Eleven holes. Eleven charges, wired in series with salvaged fuse-cord and a striker mechanism she'd built from a ship's bell hammer and a length of spring steel. It was ugly work. It would hold together exactly once.

"The old woman's charm," Ivo said, nodding at the diver's charm hanging at her throat—the bit of carved whalebone the survivor in the cliff village had pressed into her hand. "You didn't wear it before."

"It seemed like tempting fate."

"And now?"

"Now I've decided fate can be tempted." She set the last charge, tamped the clay around it, and wiped her hands on her trousers. They came away black. Everything about her came away black these days; she'd stopped noticing. "How's the rib?"

"Present."

"That bad?"

"Worse than present. Better than absent." He levered himself up carefully. "Zephyrine. If this works—if the wall goes and the channel floods—we have maybe four minutes before the surge reaches us in here. The Cormorant has to be positioned in the outflow current the moment it starts, or we get rolled against the ceiling and that's the end of the story."

"I know the spot. Under the high dome, where the old riverbed drops away. The water will pull toward the breach like a throat swallowing. We ride the swallow."

"*Ride* the—" He stared at her. "You want to sail a ketch down a collapsing whirlpool into an active flood surge, under an anchored war fleet, in the dark."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"How else would I say it?"

"With respect." She grinned at him, and was surprised to find the grin was real—not bravado, not armor, but something closer to joy. This was the thing about her nobody had ever understood, not the mountain patrons, not the border patrols, not the brokers with their soft offers: that at the very edge of death, her mind went quiet and clean, and what lived in that quiet was not fear. It was appetite. For years she'd been ashamed of it. Standing in the dripping dark with eleven charges of dead men's explosives wired to a ship's bell, she let the shame go. "Get aboard, archivist. Tie down everything you love."

"That list is short," he said, moving toward the boat, "and currently climbing a ladder ahead of me."

* * *

They took the Cormorant back through the second cavern in the dark, Zephyrine steering by memory and by the feel of the current against the rudder, counting strokes of the sweep the way she'd once counted breaths on dives. The dome of the third cavern rose above them, black stone sweating seawater, streaked pale where the refugee frescoes had run with damp—faces, hands, a horizon painted by people who knew they were painting a testament. Ivo kept his eyes on it until the dark took it.

At the position she'd chosen, under the high dome where the riverbed dropped away, she dropped a grapnel onto a ledge she'd scouted at slack water and paid out line until the ketch hung balanced above the abyss like a fly on a windowpane. Then she rigged the striker cord to run aft to the helm, tested it twice, and took the wheel.

"Four minutes," Ivo said. He'd wedged himself at the mast with his good arm wrapped around it, and he had put the decoded documents—the copies, the truth, the whole reason any of this was happening—in an oilskin satchel strapped to his chest. "You counted the swim out of the inner caverns at three. Add a minute for argument."

"There's no time for argument."

"There's always time for argument. It's how I show affection."

She pulled the striker cord.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The dark held its breath. Then the sound arrived—not a bang but a *shove*, a vast subterranean exhalation, as if the world's chest had been struck from inside. Dust and spray came off the dome in a single sheet. The lamp died. And below them, through the soles of her boots, through the hull, through the bones of the boat, Zephyrine felt the wall go.

The sea came in like a verdict.

It did not roar—that was the terrible thing, the thing she would remember all her life. It made almost no sound at all. The water level in the cavern simply *rose*, fast and smooth and total, a black floor ascending, and the air above it compressed into a shrinking pocket that pushed past them in a warm wind smelling of salt and old stone. The grapnel line snapped taut, sang, parted. The Cormorant dropped, swung, and then the current had them—not sideways, not rolling, but *down and forward*, sucked into the throat of the old riverbed exactly as she'd promised, exactly as she'd prayed, the ketch surfing a flood-tide that existed nowhere on any chart because she had just invented it.

"Steer!" Ivo shouted, which was absurd, because there was nothing to steer by, no light, no reference, only the pull of the water and the screaming in the rigging and the feel of the rudder telling her truths her eyes couldn't. She steered anyway. She steered by the seat of her hands, the way she'd steered the Sable Current in flood, the way she'd read avalanches by the pitch of the snowpack's silence. Left. Hard left—the riverbed bent here, she remembered, she *knew*—and the hull groaned as the current slammed them around the bend and the ceiling opened above and—

Light.

Gray, churning, rain-hammered light. The riverbed's roof ended and they burst up into the open lagoon, into the anchorage, into hell.

* * *

The fleet was dying.

The *Imperator's Mercy* was down by the bow already, her great hull canted at an angle that no ship survives, her decks sliding green with seawater as the flood surge beneath her found every seam her builders had ever caulked. Around her, chaos propagated outward like cracks in ice: a dredge had dragged its anchor and was grinding against a frigate's flank with a shriek of timber that carried across the water; boats were lowering everywhere, badly, from davits that swung wild; and over it all came the sound Zephyrine had heard only once before, on a mountain, and had hoped never to hear again—the sound of many human voices discovering at the same instant that the solid world is not solid.

Rain lashed her face. The Cormorant planed across the surface of the ruined anchorage, half-swamped, shipping water faster than the pump could argue, and Zephyrine drove her straight for the gap in the reef where the outer barrier had been breached by Corvane's own blasting weeks ago—the one passage wide enough for a ketch, too narrow for a frigate, and completely unguarded now, because every guard the admiral owned was busy swimming.

A shot cracked somewhere behind them. Then another. She didn't look. Looking cost speed, and speed was the only currency they had left.

"They're firing," Ivo reported unnecessarily, hunched over the satchel.

"They're firing at a legend," she called back. "By tomorrow we'll be bigger."

The gap came up fast—a black slot in the rain-lashed reef, white water streaming through it, maybe eight feet wider than the Cormorant and no wider than that. She lined up the approach with the cold, perfect calm she trusted more than any prayer, felt Ivo's hand close briefly, hard, on her shoulder, and took them through. Rock scraped the hull once, a long terrible kiss along the starboard strakes, and then they were through, into the open sea, into the storm, into the night.

Behind them, the *Imperator's Mercy* settled by degrees onto the drowned riverbed that had eaten her, her stern rearing once against the lightning like a monument being erected to somebody else's sins, and went down. On her quarterdeck, small even at that distance, a figure in an admiral's coat stood motionless among the abandoning crew, refusing the boats.

Zephyrine saw him for one flash of lightning and understood everything: that Corvane would rather drown than explain, that power's last corruption is the belief that it cannot end, that the sea was taking from him exactly what his father had taken from Vessany—and that she felt no triumph at all, only a cold, enormous pity, and underneath the pity, steady as ballast, relief.

They were alive. The documents were alive. The truth was loose in the world, strapped to a drowning man's chest in an oilskin satchel, sailing out of the storm in a patched ketch with a blacklisted pilot at the wheel.

"We did it," Ivo said. He sounded astonished, as though he'd believed in the plan the way one believes in fairy tales—necessary, beautiful, impossible.

"We're not done," Zephyrine said. "Look astern."

Through the rain, out of the chaos of the sinking anchorage, a single longboat had cleared the wreckage—manned, oars out, driving hard through the swells on an intercept course. Even at this distance, even in the dark, she could read the cut of it: not a rescue boat fleeing a sinking ship, but a pursuit boat, lean and determined, full of men who had nothing left to lose except orders.

And standing in its bow, gripping the forestay against the storm, a figure she recognized even before the lightning confirmed it—the broker, soaked and furious, pointing directly at them.

"Hold on to your papers, archivist," Zephyrine said, and put the wheel over. "This story isn't finished with us yet."
