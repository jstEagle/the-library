## 32. Admiral Down

The water came for Admiral Edric Corvane the way his father had always promised it would: patiently, from below, without malice and without mercy.

He was on the flag deck of the *Imperator's Justice* when the first shudder ran through her keel—a deep, subterranean groan, like a giant turning over in its sleep. He had felt that sound once before, as a boy of nine, standing on the seawall at Port Vessany while the demolition charges of the scuttling fleet went off in the harbor roads. His father had held his hand and told him it was thunder. His father had lied to him about many things, but that lie had been the kindest.

Now the thunder was beneath his own ship, and no one was holding his hand.

"Report." His voice came out steady. He had trained it to. Forty years in the imperial navy, and the first lesson his father ever taught him was that the voice was the uniform beneath the uniform. A man who commanded his voice could command anything. A man whose voice broke was already dead.

The signals officer stumbled up the ladder from the chart room, salt spray whipping off his oilskin. "Compressor room's flooding, sir. The breach is—sir, the breach is the cavern mouth itself. The pressure wall blew. The whole anchorage is—" He stopped, because the word for it was too large to say.

The whole anchorage was dying.

Corvane walked to the rail. He did not run; admirals did not run on their own decks. Below him, the lagoon that had been a fortress was becoming a maw. The surge from the breached caverns came out in a brown-white wall, carrying with it the wreckage of the reef his dredges had spent three days hammering apart—the reef he had ordered destroyed to seal the divers in, the reef that had apparently been the only thing holding the sea's patience. The *Kestrel* was already over. He could see her masts going down in a slow, obscene tilt, her crew strung along the yards like beads of black water. The dredge barges had simply ceased to exist, swallowed by the whirlpool forming where the cavern mouth had been.

And the *Imperator's Justice*—nine hundred tons of imperial oak and iron, his father's last command before retirement, the ship Corvane had requested by name when he was given the fleet—was being dragged stern-first toward that whirlpool by an anchor chain no boatswain could cut in time.

"Cut the cable," he said.

"Sir, the capstan's—"

"Then put axes in men's hands and tell them to chop as if their mothers are watching. Go."

The boy went. Corvane stayed at the rail.

In the chart room behind him, his father's portrait hung where it had hung for the entire voyage—bolted to the bulkhead, as tradition demanded, the commanding officer's lineage sailing with him. Rear Admiral Halveth Corvane, painted at the height of his career, in full dress uniform, the Vessani campaign medal at his breast. The artist had given him calm grey eyes and a mouth that might have been smiling or might have been about to speak an order. Corvane had looked at that mouth every morning of this voyage and heard it speak.

*Finish it, Edric. The old men are dying out. The papers are ash or lies. But if even one page surfaces, everything we were becomes a crime instead of a victory. Finish it.*

He had come out here to finish it. He had dynamited a reef to bury two divers and a chest of lies. He had sealed the cavern exits with his own hands on the chart, drawing the closure lines in red ink while his officers watched with the particular blankness of men who suspected but did not ask. He had done it because his father had done it, because his father's order—signed in his father's own hand, in the strongroom of a drowned ship, in ink that had outlasted the nation it destroyed—was down there in the dark, waiting to be read aloud by archivists and survivors and children not yet born.

The deck heeled. He caught the rail. Somewhere below, the axes were ringing against the anchor chain, and the sound had the rhythm of a heartbeat running out.

* * *

The water reached the flag deck at the twentieth minute.

It did not rush in, at first. It came quietly, through the scuppers and the ladder well, a black glass spreading across the planking, and Corvane stood in it ankle-deep and gave the order to abandon ship with the same flat clarity he had used for every order of his career. The boats were mostly gone already—two swamped, one capsized, one pulling away overloaded toward the headland. He watched his officers go. He did not blame them. He had made them, and men are made to survive.

"Admiral." The signals officer again, soaked to the skin, holding a line. "There's a boat. The cutter *Vigilant*, she's coming back for you. They're holding off the suction. You have to come now, sir. Sir—"

"I have not finished my inspection," Corvane said.

The boy stared at him. Water was at their knees now, and rising, and the *Imperator's Justice* was groaning like something being flayed.

"Admiral, please—"

"Take the fleet's muster book. Take the code books and burn them if you cannot carry them. Take my report to the Ministry—" He stopped. His report to the Ministry. Four pages of careful language explaining that the loss of the reef was an act of God, that the caverns had been structurally unsound, that no documents of any significance had been located. He had written it two days ago, in anticipation. He had always been able to write the ending before the story happened. His father's gift, passed down like the medal, like the name, like the lie.

"Take nothing," he said. "Go."

The boy went, weeping, which Corvane noted with distant disapproval and, beneath the disapproval, a colder and older feeling: envy. The boy could weep. The boy had not yet been promoted past weeping.

When the deck was empty, Corvane went into the chart room.

The water was waist-deep on the lower ladder, and he descended into it like a man descending into his own history. The portrait hung above the chart table, and the sea had reached it—spray spotting the varnish, running down the painted glass in long tears, so that his father appeared to be weeping, or dissolving, or being born out of the water that had made him.

Halveth Corvane had been a hero. That was the word on the plaque, the word in the histories, the word Edric had grown up inside like a suit of armor that fit worse every year. *Hero of the Vessani pacification. The officer who spared the fleet a bloody landing by resolving the evacuation question decisively.* Decisively. Corvane had learned, at nine, at the seawall, that *decisively* meant charges in the hulls of crowded ships at night. He had learned it and said nothing, because his father had looked down at him afterward—both of them watching the harbor burn with fuel oil and the sea take the lights one by one—and his father had said, *One day you will understand that mercy is a luxury of the victorious. We were not yet victorious. Now we are, because of me, and you will inherit it.*

He had inherited it. He had spent his whole life paying the inheritance's interest: the promotions that came too easily, the postings that kept him near the Ministry's secrets, the quiet understanding that he would be the one sent if the Vessani question ever stirred. He had been sent. He had dynamited a reef over two divers because his father's signature was down there, and a signature, his father had told him once, drunk, in a voice Edric had never heard before or since, *is the only part of a man the sea can't digest.*

The sea was digesting everything now.

The water took the portrait's frame off the bulkhead with a soft crack, and Corvane caught it before it sank. Heavy—oak and gilt and the weight of the man inside it. He held it against his chest, and the cold rose around him, and he found that he was not afraid. That was the strangest part. Forty years of commanding men into water, and now that the water had him, it felt almost like an appointment long deferred, a meeting with a colleague who had always been patient with him.

* * *

The ship rolled. He went under and came up, and the chart room was gone—the ceiling, the table, the red-inked charts, all of it stripped away into the brown violence, and he was in the open sea inside the dying lagoon, one arm locked around his father's portrait, his dress coat dragging him down until he tore it open button by button, an act of undressing he had not performed since his wedding.

The whirlpool had him by the boots.

He could see the sky—a hard grey circle, turning. He could see the headland. And on the headland, impossibly, absurdly, he could see smoke: the thin black smear of a signal fire, the one his patrol had reported two days ago, the one that had led him to seal the caverns. The divers. The guide and the archivist. The two small lives he had decided were worth less than a signature.

They were still alive, then. Or they had been. The caverns were flooding; he could feel it in the pull, in the way the whole sea was being drawn into the mountain like breath into a lung. If the divers lived, they would come out of this water the way he was going into it—spat up, ruined, carrying pages.

*Finish it,* said the mouth in the portrait, and the water was in his mouth now too, and the two voices were the same voice, had always been the same voice, his father's voice and the sea's, the empire's, the inheritance speaking in its native tongue.

A boat was near. He could hear oars, shouting—a cutter, the *Vigilant* coming back against every sane instinct, men leaning into the strokes toward the whirlpool's rim because an admiral was in the water and the navy did not leave its admirals, not even this one. A rope snaked past him, close enough to touch.

Corvane looked at the rope.

He looked at the portrait.

He understood, in the last clear second the cold allowed him, that he could take the rope and live, and that living would mean the inquiry, and the inquiry would mean the strongroom ledger, and the ledger would mean his father's signature read aloud in a free-port hall to weeping descendants, and his name—his name, the only thing the inheritance had actually given him—becoming the son's half of a single crime. *Halveth Corvane scuttled the fleet. Edric Corvane drowned the truth.* One entry. One family. The circle closed by his own surviving hand.

Or he could hold the portrait, and go down with the proof of what his father was, and the sea would keep the secret the way it had kept it for forty years. The empire would call it a tragic loss in a freak surge. There would be a plaque. There might even be the word *hero*.

His hand closed tighter on the frame.

The rope drifted past, out of reach, and the men in the cutter would say forever after that they had thrown it in time, that the Admiral had seen it, that something in his face had—chosen. They would be right, and they would never know how right.

The water took him the way it had taken Vessany: patiently, from below, without malice and without mercy.

He went down with his father in his arms, and the last thing he saw, through the churn and the dying light, was the grey circle of sky turning above him—and across it, small and swift and impossibly alive, the patched sail of a ketch riding the surge out of the mountain, half-swamped, flying, escaping with the truth in her hold.

He tried to hate it. He found, as the Current closed over him, that he could only admire it—and that this, too, was his father's inheritance: to recognize, always a moment too late, the courage he had spent his life commanding men to have and never learned himself.

The sea kept the portrait. The sea did not keep the secret.

* * *

Far above, on the headland, the smoke still rose.
