## 33. Surface

The pressure wall broke with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Zephyrine felt it before she heard it—a shudder through the water itself, a deep tectonic groan that traveled up through the Cormorant's keel and into her spine where she crouched at the tiller. Then the roar came, rolling down the flooded gallery behind them like an avalanche given voice, and the sea that had been pressing them down for two days suddenly turned and *pushed*.

"Hold on!" she screamed, though Ivo was already flat against the deck planks, one arm hooked through the base of the mast, the other pressed over the oilskin bundle lashed to his chest. The documents. The truth of Vessany, wrapped in sailcloth and tarred string, worth more than every coin still sleeping in the treasury gallery they had sealed behind them.

The Cormorant went from dead drift to hurtling in the space of three heartbeats.

Water filled the cavern channel ahead of them—not rising so much as *arriving*, a brown-green wall of displaced sea shoved forward by the collapse of the pressure wall Zephyrine had breached with salvaged blasting powder and a prayer she hadn't believed in since childhood. The boat surfed it. There was no other word. The Cormorant rode the surge the way a leaf rides a gutter in flood rain, spinning, shipping water over both rails at once, her patched sails useless and flogging, her hull screaming along stone on both sides close enough to touch.

Zephyrine steered by memory and by the lamp strapped to the bow, its beam swinging wild across walls that were no longer walls but moving shadows—rockfall, cascading silt, the pale flash of bones stirred from their forty-year rest as the Current reclaimed everything at once.

"Port!" Ivo's voice, raw and cracked. "PORT—"

She hauled the tiller. Something vast scraped past the starboard side with a sound like a giant dragging its fingernails—the shattered lifeboat, or what remained of it, tumbling end over end in the flood. She caught one glimpse of its nameboard, letters she'd learned to read only this week: *Mercy of Vessany*. Then it was gone, swallowed astern, and the cavern mouth opened before them like a birth.

They shot out of the inner dark into the outer channel, and the outer channel was dying.

Even half-blind, half-drowned, Zephyrine could read it. The dynamiting Corvane had ordered over the past two days had weakened the reef's bones, and now the flood surge was finishing the work. Chunks of the outer reef calved away in slow, terrible silence above the waterline and fast, splintering violence below it. The ceiling of the world was coming down. Dust and silt bloomed through the water in great gray clouds, turning the lamplight into something solid, something you could almost grab.

And behind all of it, deeper in, the wreck of the *Vessani Crown*—the flagship of a drowned nation, the tomb of its captain and his strongroom and its sealed treasury—folded inward with a groan that Zephyrine would hear for the rest of her life.

It closed forever. She knew it the way you know a door has locked behind you: not by seeing, but by the sudden, absolute quality of the silence that followed, even inside the roaring.

"There," Ivo said. Not a shout. A statement, quiet as a man pointing out a star. Ahead and above, a ragged seam of gray light where the cavern roof had already fallen in some earlier age—the gap between the Sable Current's teeth, the third way out, the old woman's warning made into a doorway.

Zephyrine put the Cormorant's nose at it and did not look back.

* * *

The last hundred yards took a lifetime.

The surge carried them up the throat of the collapsed chimney, but the chimney narrowed, and the boat was too wide, and twice the hull struck rock with impacts that Zephyrine felt in her teeth. The first blow stove in a plank somewhere below the waterline; she heard the new sound join the old ones, the thin urgent whistle of the sea coming aboard. The second blow spun them sideways, and for one suspended moment the Cormorant hung in the falling water like a moth pinned to a board, lamp beam wheeling, Ivo's face white and streaming, the documents clutched against him like a child.

Then the sea decided.

It lifted them the final distance the way a hand lifts a thing it means to keep, and the Cormorant burst through the surface of the open Current into gray morning light and driving rain, and Zephyrine discovered she had not been breathing.

She dragged air in so hard it doubled her over the tiller. Around her the world was chaos—rain, spray, the towering headland of the Verlaine coast off to port, and ahead—

Ahead, the fleet burned.

Corvane's anchorage, the snug lagoon where nine ships had ridden at their moorings with their dredges and their divers and their certainty, was a scene out of the oldest stories, the ones mothers told children about the wrath of the Current. The breach Zephyrine had blasted in the cavern's pressure wall had funneled the whole fury of the displaced sea directly into the lagoon, and the lagoon had no room for it. Ships lay against ships. The dredge barges had gone over like toys kicked from a table. And the flagship—the *Imperial Resolve*, Corvane's floating fortress, the pride of the salvage fleet—was down by the bow, black smoke pouring from her midsection where she'd been driven onto the reef shelf, fire climbing her rigging in bright hungry ropes.

Men dotted the water around her. Even at this distance Zephyrine could see the small desperate movements, boats pulling away, lines thrown, the whole grand machine of the empire reduced to men swimming.

Ivo pulled himself upright beside her, one hand pressed to his ribs where the falling rock had caught him in the dark. His face was gray under the salt. He looked at the burning fleet for a long moment, and then he looked at her.

"You did that," he said. It wasn't accusation. It wasn't quite wonder either. It was the voice of a man cataloguing a fact he intended to carry for the rest of his life, filed alongside the names of the drowned.

"The Current did that," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out ruined, scraped raw by spray and screaming. "I just opened the door."

"She's sinking," Ivo said. "We're sinking too."

He was right. The Cormorant sat low in the water, lower than she had any right to sit, and the whistle of the incoming sea had become a gurgle. Zephyrine tore herself from the spectacle of the fleet and looked down at her own deck, ankle-deep and rising, and began to laugh—a wild, ugly sound that had nothing to do with humor and everything to do with being alive.

"Bailer," she managed. "There's a bailer under the thwart. Bail like your nation depends on it."

"It does," Ivo said, and dropped to his knees, and bailed.

* * *

They fought the Cormorant eastward through the morning like two people fighting a house fire with buckets.

Zephyrine got the storm jib up alone—Ivo couldn't climb, not with his ribs—and ran them off before the wind with the tiller lashed and both of them bailing, taking turns, ten strokes each while the other rested, because neither of them could have lasted an hour alone and together they might last three. The rain helped and hindered: it cooled the burn in her hands where the blasting fuse had spat sparks, but it filled the boat as fast as they emptied it.

Somewhere in the second hour the leak slowed. Either the swelling planks closed around the damage or the sea simply grew bored of them. Zephyrine chose to believe the first. She had earned one miracle today and saw no reason not to claim a second.

By midday the rain thinned to a drifting haze, and the Cormorant wallowed on, half-swamped but stubborn, her patched sails sagging, her crew barely better. Zephyrine sat slumped against the tiller post, watching Ivo sleep sitting up with the document bundle still strapped to his chest even unconscious, his fingers curled around the tarred string like a man holding a rope over a long drop.

Behind them, when she turned, the coast was a smudge. The smoke of the fleet rose above it in a single vast column, bending east with the wind, visible, she guessed, from thirty miles out. From every port on the Verlaine coast. By nightfall everyone from Salt Harbor to the imperial stations would know that Admiral Corvane's salvage fleet had met disaster above the Vessany wreck, and no one would know why, and the not-knowing would grow stories, and the stories would grow legs.

Let them run, she thought. Stories were the one cargo she'd never been able to salvage, and the one cargo that never sank.

Her hands shook. She let them. There was no one to see, and shaking was honest work—it meant the body still expected to live. She counted the shakes like she'd counted breaths in the inner dark, and somewhere past two hundred they slowed, and somewhere past four hundred she noticed she was crying, silently, without any decision to, salt water running into salt water, and she let that happen too.

Forty years ago the empire had drowned a nation and called it weather. Today the weather had answered. She didn't believe the sea kept accounts—she was too good a sailor for that superstition—but standing at the tiller of a sinking boat with a dead nation's truth in her hold, she understood why people needed to believe it. Some things were too large to be called accident. You had to give them a name with teeth.

Ivo woke with a gasp, the way she'd learned he always woke, arriving in the world like a man surfacing from deep water. His eyes found hers immediately.

"The wreck," he said.

"Gone." She said it plainly, because he deserved plainness. "The whole chamber came down. The strongroom, the gallery, everything. No fleet will ever strip it. No one will ever—" She stopped. "No one will ever disturb them again."

Ivo was silent a long time. Rain dripped from the boom. Somewhere behind them a gull cried, absurdly ordinary, the first living thing they'd seen since the dark.

"I spent forty years keeping ashes," he said finally. "Cataloguing them. Memorizing what they let me touch and mourning what they didn't. I thought the nation was the papers. I thought if I saved enough paper, someday it would add up to a country again." His hand moved over the bundle at his chest. "But it wasn't the papers. It was the people who read them. It was—" He gestured weakly at the boat, at her, at the gray water running east. "It was whoever was left to carry them."

"You carried them," Zephyrine said.

"We carried them," he said, and there was something in his voice she'd never heard there before, and she was too tired to name it and too honest to pretend she hadn't noticed.

* * *

Toward evening the wind backed and freshened, and the Cormorant, waterlogged as a drowned bird, began to make honest way. Zephyrine rigged a line from the tiller and sat beside Ivo on the thwart, shoulder to shoulder, sharing the last of the ship's biscuit from a tin that had spent two days underwater and tasted of it. They ate in silence. It was a good silence. She had known silences that were wars and silences that were tombs, and this was neither. This was the silence of two people who had seen the same darkness and come out of it facing the same direction.

The burning column of the fleet's smoke fell slowly astern. Ahead, the Verlaine coast unrolled in the failing light, and beyond it, somewhere south, the cliff village of the survivors waited with its terraced gardens and its old woman and its contempt for the name Marchetti's family had carried. Ivo would want to go there. She found she wanted it too, which frightened her more than the inner dark had.

"Zephyrine." He said her whole name. He almost never did.

"Mm."

"When we make land—there will be ships. Imperial ships. Rescue parties looking for fleet survivors. They'll find us first, probably. Two half-dead castaways in a swamped ketch are easier to find than anyone wants to be." He turned to look at her, and in the last light his eyes were very clear. "They'll offer us things. Blankets, brandy, passage. And then they'll ask about the smoke. And then they'll ask what we were doing in the caverns. And then someone clever will ask to see what's in the bundle."

"I know."

"They'll offer amnesty," he said. "Pardon. A clean slate for the guide with the blacklisted name. They'll dress it up as mercy, and it will smell like mercy, and it will be a cage with a ribbon on it."

"I know," Zephyrine said again.

He studied her. "Do you? Because I've watched you want that pardon the way drowning men want air. Six tides ago you'd have traded this bundle for it without blinking."

"Six tides ago I hadn't stood in the treasury of Vessany," she said. "Six tides ago I hadn't read the order your admiral's father signed. Six tides ago I thought a pardon was the same thing as being free." She looked east, where the first star was pricking through the haze above a horizon she knew better than any chart. "I've been invisible my whole life, Ivo. Invisible is safe. Invisible is also just another word for drowned, if you stay under long enough." She reached over and laid her hand on the bundle between them, on the tarred string, on the truth. "This comes out. All of it. To whoever will listen. That's the only trade I'm making."

Ivo put his hand over hers. Neither of them spoke. The Cormorant ran on through the darkening water, and the Current, which had tried to kill them and failed, carried them gently now, like something that had finished testing them and found them, if not worthy, at least unwilling to sink.

Near midnight, Zephyrine saw the lights.

Two of them, low on the southern horizon, moving—running lights, white over green, closing on a converging course. A ship, and a fast one, sweeping the coast in the wake of the disaster, plucking survivors from the sea. Within the hour it would find them. Within the hour there would be warm blankets and hot brandy and polite questions and a ribboned cage, exactly as Ivo had said.

Ivo saw them too. He straightened despite his ribs, wincing, and looked from the lights to her face.

"Well," he said quietly. "Here it comes."

Zephyrine watched the lights grow, and touched the tiller, and held her course—straight at them, straight into whatever came next, with the truth riding dry in its oilskin and the wreck of a nation sleeping forever at her back.

"Then let's go tell them what happened here," she said. "Every word of it. Whether they want to hear it or not."
