## 34. Deliverance Refused

Ivo Vessani had spent forty years learning what it meant to be handed something and told to be grateful.

He had been handed bread by the occupation garrison when he was a boy, and told to be grateful. He had been handed a clerk's desk in a confiscated archive, and told to be grateful. He had been handed his life, once, on the condition that he never speak of what he had seen, and he had said thank you in three languages and meant none of them.

So when the rescue ship's boat came grinding alongside the half-drowned Cormorant, and a lieutenant in imperial blue extended a hand and said, *You are being delivered*, Ivo recognized the sentence for what it was. He had heard its ancestors all his life.

But that was an hour away yet. First there was the water, and the work, and Zephyrine's blood in the bilge.

* * *

The morning came up gray and gentle, which felt like an obscenity.

Behind them the Sable Current steamed where the caverns had collapsed, and the smoke of the fleet's burning rose in a black column that bent eastward on the wind. The flagship—Corvane's flagship, whatever her name had been, *Sovereign's Mercy* or some irony of that kind—was a glow beneath the smoke now, settled on the reef like a lamp going out. Ivo could not stop looking at it. He kept turning at the tiller to check that it was real, the way a man checks a wound to confirm it still hurts and therefore still happened.

Zephyrine sat slumped against the mast with a sailcloth over her shoulders, shivering in the way of someone whose body had spent everything it had. Her hands, wrapped in strips of his shirt, were ruined—torn by line and rock and the pressure wall's edge when she had set the charges. She had not let him look at them closely. She had not let him do much of anything except steer, bail, and keep the Cormorant's nose into the leftover swell while the sea sorted itself out around them.

"You're bleeding again," he said.

"I'm aware."

"The wrapping's soaked through."

"Then it's working," she said. "Pressure stops bleeding. Everyone knows that."

He wanted to laugh. He wanted, more strangely, to weep, and he did neither, because the Cormorant was riding two inches above the waterline and both of them knew it. She had come out of the collapsing caverns half-swamped, riding the surge Zephyrine had loosed with her own two ruined hands, and she had not sunk, and that was the whole of the miracle. The bilge pump worked in the way that things Zephyrine Pemberton owned worked—badly, ingeniously, continuously. Ivo pumped. Zephyrine steered when she could and slept against the mast when she couldn't, and between them they kept the little ketch moving north-northwest, away from the smoke, toward nothing in particular.

They had not spoken of the treasury gallery. They had not spoken of the chest lost to the deep channel, or the copies sewn into the oilskin wallet inside Ivo's coat, or of Corvane going down with his father's portrait. There would be time. There was, for the first time in six tides, time.

It was near noon when Ivo saw the sail.

* * *

She came out of the haze to the east under working canvas, a two-masted imperial dispatch vessel, clean-lined and quick, flying the blue pennant of the Salvage Directorate. Ivo saw her first and said nothing for a long moment—just watched, with his heart going flat and cold, the way it had learned to go in the archive when an inspector's footsteps sounded on the stair.

"Zephyrine."

She came up out of her half-sleep like a gull off water. One look at the sail, one read of its cut and pennant, and she said, "Dispatch boat. Not a warship. They'll have picked up the fleet's distress traffic." She was already moving, assessing, her exhaustion folding away into that cold bright competence he had watched her wear like armor since the tide-market. "They'll want survivors. Witnesses. Us."

"Then we run."

"On what?" She gestured at the Cormorant's deck, at the water sloshing ankle-deep despite the pump, at the mainsail patched with three different fabrics. "We've been running on fumes and stubbornness for a day. They'll have us inside the hour if they want us, and they want us." Her eyes narrowed. "The question is what they want us *for*."

The dispatch boat closed. Ivo counted guns—two, small, ceremonial—and men at the rail, perhaps thirty. Not a boarding party's worth of menace. A rescue's worth of ceremony. That was worse. He had learned in the archive that the empire did its most thorough work with ceremony.

They hailed from a hundred yards, a speaking-trumpet voice cracking across the water: *Vessel ahoy. You are in imperial waters. Heave to and prepare to receive assistance.*

Zephyrine looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at Zephyrine. Between them passed one of those conversations that had grown, over six tides, to need no words—her eyebrow asking, his chin answering, both of them arriving at the same arithmetic at the same moment. They could not run. They could not fight. They could only be delivered.

"Heave to," she said, and her voice did not shake, and Ivo loved her for it and hated that he noticed.

* * *

The boarding was gentle, which Ivo distrusted completely.

The lieutenant who came across in the second boat was young, pink-cheeked from the wind, with the careful diction of a man who had rehearsed his lines. He took in the Cormorant—the swamped bilge, the scorched patch where a burning spar had landed, the two figures at her rail who looked like drowned things given temporary shape—and his rehearsed lines visibly wobbled.

"You came out of the Sable Current," he said. "Out of the caverns."

"We came out of the sea," Zephyrine said. "The sea is large."

"Madam, the *entire anchorage*—nine ships—there was a breach in the reef, a pressure wave, the *Mercy* went down with—there were men in the water for *miles*—" He stopped himself, visibly reassembled his composure, and started over. "You are survivors of the disaster at the Vessany wreck site. I am Lieutenant Hallow, of the dispatch vessel *Prompt*. You are hereby taken into imperial protection."

"Protection," Zephyrine repeated.

"Rescue," the lieutenant amended, and Ivo watched him choose the word with the care of a man stepping between stones. "And passage. And—" Here the rehearsed lines returned, and the lieutenant delivered them with the relief of a man reaching solid ground. "In recognition of your ordeal, and at the direction of the Admiralty, you are offered full passage to Verlaine City, medical care, and—should you be able to provide testimony regarding the events of the past days—*amnesty*. Full and unconditional amnesty. For any and all prior offenses. Both of you."

The word *amnesty* sat on the morning air like a coin on a counter.

Ivo felt Zephyrine go very still beside him. He had learned her stillnesses by now. There was the stillness of the hunt, when she read water; there was the stillness of the liar, when she built a bargain out of nothing; and there was this stillness, the rarest one, the stillness of a woman listening to a trap being described in detail so she could admire its workmanship.

"Amnesty," she said. "For me. A blacklisted guide with two countries' worth of paper on her. And for him." She tipped her chin at Ivo. "An archivist who's been dead on the rolls for forty years."

"The Admiralty is prepared to be generous," Lieutenant Hallow said.

"Is it." Zephyrine smiled, and Ivo saw the lieutenant take an involuntary half-step back, because it was not a comforting smile. "And what does the Admiralty want in exchange for its generosity? I assume it's not our gratitude. The Admiralty doesn't take payment in gratitude."

"The Directoriate requests," Hallow said, and here he faltered, just slightly, "all documents, papers, charts, and written materials recovered from the wreck site. For preservation. For the imperial courts. The matter of the Vessany evacuation is to be examined by a tribunal, and the tribunal will require—"

"The tribunal will require the evidence to disappear," Ivo said.

Everyone looked at him. He had not spoken since the hailing. His voice came out rusted, and he found he did not care.

Lieutenant Hallow recovered beautifully. "Sir, I assure you the tribunal is convened under imperial charter, with imperial judges, under imperial—"

"Under imperial everything," Ivo said. "Yes. I spent forty years in your archives, Lieutenant. I have read your charters. I know exactly how imperial justice handles evidence that embarrasses the empire. It handles it the way the Current handles a hull. Slowly. Thoroughly. Until nothing is left."

* * *

They were brought aboard the *Prompt*—there was no refusing, not with the Cormorant sinking by inches and Zephyrine's hands the color of old meat—and given a cabin, hot food, dry clothes, and a guard at the door who was polite enough to pretend he was a steward. The Cormorant was taken under tow. Zephyrine watched her go with an expression Ivo could not read and did not try to; some griefs are private even between people who have drowned together.

The cabin had a table. On the table, when they were left alone, Lieutenant Hallow placed a document: two pages, formal, stamped, the amnesty laid out in beautiful legal prose. All prior offenses forgiven. Names restored. Passage home. Freedom.

All it required was their signatures, and the surrender of "all recovered materials" into imperial custody, pending tribunal.

Zephyrine read it twice. Then she set it down and looked at Ivo, and what passed between them in that look was the whole argument they had already had, weeks ago, in another life, on the deck of the Cormorant under different stars—the quarrel about duty, about pardons, about what the dead were owed. She had argued then for selling the documents. For buying their lives back with them. He had argued for the diaspora, for the truth, for the scattered survivors who had waited forty years to learn why their nation had died.

And then she had flooded an anchorage and lost the original chest to the deep channel rather than trade it, and he had understood that she had answered her own argument somewhere in the dark water, and had not told him.

"Your hands need a surgeon," he said quietly.

"My hands need a surgeon," she agreed. "That's not what this is about."

"No," Ivo said. "It isn't."

He picked up the amnesty. He read it a third time, slowly, the way he had read ten thousand confiscated documents—looking not at what it said but at what it was built to prevent. And he thought about the reading they were going to give the truth, the reading it had never had, the reading that was owed to it. He thought about a hall full of people who had grown up being told their nation had drowned itself through its own weakness. He thought about the old woman in the cliff village, and the diver's charm she had pressed into Zephyrine's palm, and the skeletons in the treasury gallery, sitting at their posts beside the gold no one had ever come to carry home.

The empire was offering him his life back.

The empire was offering him his life back *in exchange for the only thing that had ever made his life mean anything.*

"Ivo," Zephyrine said. It was not a question. It was barely a word. It was, he realized, the sound of a woman who had decided for both of them and was giving him one last chance to fight her, and who would not blame him if he did—because she remembered the quarrel, and knew what she was asking him to give up. His pardon. His name. His restoration to the world of paper and record, the world he had lived in for forty years, the world of catalogues and stamps and the small dignities of the archived.

He set the document down.

"When I was twelve," Ivo said, "an imperial officer handed my mother a form. It renounced her citizenship—retroactively, so that legally she had never been Vessani, so that I had never been born Vessani. She signed it because I was hungry. And the officer thanked her, and said, *Now you are protected.*" He looked at Lieutenant Hallow's carefully blank face. "I have been protected ever since, Lieutenant. It has been the great project of my life. I am very good at being protected. I am an expert. And I am telling you—" his voice did not rise at all, which somehow made it worse; he saw the young man's throat move— "that you may take this document, and this offer, and this ship, and you may tell whoever sent you that the archive they thought they burned is in a hundred heads by now, and that the copies they want are already beyond their water. You may tell them that the truth does not sign amnesties. The truth *is* the amnesty. It forgives no one, and it belongs to everyone."

The silence in the cabin was very long.

Zephyrine did not cheer. She did not grin. She did something worse, something that undid him more thoroughly than any celebration could have: she simply looked at him, and nodded once, the way one sailor acknowledges another's seamanship in dangerous water. *Well read,* the nod said. *Well read, and well spoken, and we are lost together, and I have never in my life been less afraid.*

"Madam," Lieutenant Hallow said, turning to her with the desperation of a man whose career was drowning in front of him. "The offer stands. The amnesty is genuine. You would be a free woman. You could guide again. Any pass, any country under the imperial—"

"Lieutenant." Zephyrine picked up the two pages, folded them with neat, deliberate care, the way she folded everything, and held them out. "I have spent my whole life invisible. Do you know what invisibility costs? It costs everything, and pays nothing, and the bill always comes due in the end. I paid it for fifteen years." She smiled again, and this time it was almost kind. "I'm done being protected."

Hallow took the folded amnesty as though it were something that might go off. "You understand that without it, the charges—smuggling, salvage piracy, the destruction of His Majesty's vessels—"

"The destruction of His Majesty's vessels," Zephyrine said, "was the most honest work I've ever done. I'll answer for it in a court that isn't yours."

"There is no other court."

"There will be," Ivo said. "We're going to build one. It's called a hall, with people in it, and a reader. We've done it before. Nations have started smaller."

* * *

They were confined to the cabin—politely, with the steward-guard and the locked door and the excellent food that Ivo distrusted on principle. Zephyrine slept, finally, truly, her bandaged hands curled against her chest like something rescued. Ivo sat at the little table by the porthole and watched the coast slide by, and took stock.

The originals were gone. The chest, the strongroom ledger, the captain's final page—all of it at the bottom of the deep channel, sealed in the dark where no dredge would ever reach it, because the channel was Zephyrine's and the channel did not give back what it was given. What remained was the copies—his copies, his hand, his forty years of memorizing what he was forbidden to keep—sewn into the oilskin wallet against his ribs. Decoded. Complete. Enough.

Enough to burn an admiral's name. Enough to burn an empire's lie. Enough, if they could reach a free port, if they could reach a hall and a crowd and a reader—

He heard boots in the passage. Not the steward's soft tread; heavier, several men, moving with purpose. Zephyrine's eyes opened without her moving anything else, the way they did, already awake behind the sleep.

The lock did not turn. The boots stopped outside, and a voice—Hallow's, pitched low, urgent—spoke to someone, and another voice answered, and Ivo could not make out the words but he could make out the *shape* of them, the shape of a man receiving new orders he did not like.

Then the boots moved on, toward the bow, toward the wheel, and the *Prompt* came slowly, deliberately, about, her heading swinging away from Verlaine City and toward the open south.

Zephyrine sat up. She looked at Ivo. She looked at the porthole, at the gray water, at the coast falling away behind them.

"They're not taking us to Verlaine," she said softly.

"No," Ivo said. His hand found the oilskin wallet through his coat, and beneath the fear—because he was afraid, properly, in his bones—he felt something else kindle, something that felt almost like the old woman's charm against his palm. "They're buying time. Somewhere quiet. Somewhere with no halls and no crowds."

"Then someone had better tell them," Zephyrine said, swinging her legs off the bunk, wincing at her hands, already calculating—masts, guards, water, distance—"that we know every quiet place on this coast better than they do. And that we've never once needed a door to leave a room."

She was already at the porthole, reading the water, reading the light, reading the hour. Outside, the Sable Current's smoke still stood on the horizon behind them like a lit fuse, and somewhere south, in a hall they had not yet found, the truth was waiting for its reader.

Ivo stood up, and went to the door, and put his shoulder against it beside her.
