## 40. A Horizon for the Current

The schoolroom had been a smokehouse once, and it still smelled faintly of salt and old oak smoke, which Ivo had decided was fitting. A nation that lived by the sea should be taught in a room that remembered it.

He stood at the slate board he'd built himself from the hull planking of a Vessani fishing skiff—salvaged, like everything here, from forty years of forgetting—and wrote the word *saelith* in careful strokes. The chalk was cheap and crumbled at the edges. The children watched him the way children watch anything new: half hunger, half suspicion.

"Say it," he said. "Slowly."

"*Sah-eh-leeth,*" they answered, a ragged chorus of nine voices, ages six to fourteen, perched on benches made from overturned crab crates.

"Again."

"*Saelith.*"

"What does it mean?"

A small girl in the front row—Maren, seven years old, with her grandmother's dark eyes and her grandfather's stubborn chin—raised her hand before anyone else could. "It means *the water that carries you home.*"

"It means more than that," Ivo said. "It means the water that carries you home *even when you didn't know you were going there.*" He tapped the board. "Your great-grandparents named the Sable Current this. Not because it was gentle. Because it always brought them back. Even when they were lost. Even when they thought they never wanted to come back." He let that sit a moment. "Vessani is not a dead language. It's a sleeping one. You are all of you its alarm clock."

That got a laugh, as it did every morning, because he said it every morning, and Maren grinned at him with a gap where her front tooth had been.

He turned back to the board and wrote the next word, and while his back was to them he felt the familiar ache settle into his chest—not grief exactly, or not only grief. It was the ache of a man doing the one thing he was made for, after decades of being forbidden to do it. In the imperial archive he had memorized what he could not keep. Here, he gave it away freely, syllable by syllable, to children who would carry it further than any archive could.

Old Sefra sat in the corner as she always did, ostensibly mending nets, actually listening. She had given Zephyrine the diver's charm eleven months ago, and she still pretended she hadn't. When Ivo caught her eye she looked down at her knots and muttered something about tourists.

The lesson moved on. He taught them the counting song fishermen used to sing when hauling lines, the one with the rhythm of the pull built into it, so that the words themselves taught your arms when to work. The children sang it badly and loudly, and through the single window behind him Ivo could see the sea, flat and silver under a high autumn sun, and beyond the point where the cliff village ended, the long line of the Verlaine coast running north toward waters that no longer belonged entirely to the empire's maps.

Three months ago, the first survey ship flying a neutral flag had anchored off Salt Harbor. Two months ago, the empire had formally acknowledged—in language so carefully hedged it took Ivo an evening to parse it—that "irregularities attended the loss of the evacuation fleet of Vessany." Last month, the survivor colony had received its charter of sanctuary, signed, sealed, and grudging. And next week, if the tides held, the first memorial expedition would sail for the wreck site, carrying descendants of the drowned to lay flowers over water that had finally been allowed to tell the truth.

Ivo had written the petition for that expedition himself. Four drafts. He'd burned three of them in the stove rather than let the imperfect versions linger.

"Saelith," Maren said suddenly, pointing past him out the window. "Look."

He turned. Far out past the headland, a sail had appeared—a patched ketch with a white scar along her port rail where a sloop had once tried to board her and lost instead. She was standing in toward the village on the turning tide, and even at this distance Ivo knew her the way you know a voice in a dark room.

The Cormorant.

His heart did the thing it always did, the thing he had stopped pretending was surprise. He looked at the children, who were all craning toward the window now, the lesson abandoned.

"Well," he said, closing the slate book. "Class dismissed. Go see who's come home."

They poured out the door like water finding its level, and Ivo followed more slowly, pausing at the threshold to look back at the room—the slate, the benches, the smell of salt and smoke. A year ago he had been cataloguing ashes in a basement for men who wanted the fire forgotten. Now he was teaching children to sing hauling songs in a tongue the empire had tried to bury.

Arrival, he thought. It doesn't feel like stopping. It feels like the moment the current takes hold of the keel and turns you toward shore—but a shore you chose.

He went down the cliff path to meet her.

* * *

Zephyrine saw the children first.

They came scrambling down the switchback trail above the landing beach like a spill of bright stones, shouting in a language she didn't speak but had learned to love the sound of, and she stood at the Cormorant's tiller with the tide lifting her gently toward the shingle and let herself smile without guarding it. That was new, too. Eleven months of practice, and the smiling still felt like unclenching a fist she hadn't known she'd made.

"Passengers," she called to the deck behind her, "this is your captain speaking. We are approaching a hostile shoreline defended exclusively by children. Surrender immediately."

Her passengers laughed. There were four of them this run: a cartographer from the free ports who wanted to chart the cavern approaches honestly, for the first time; a widow whose father's name was on the decoded list, coming to see the coast he'd died trying to reach; two young divers out of Salt Harbor who had heard about the third way out of the inner caves and wanted to learn it from the woman who'd found it. They lined the rail the way her passengers always did now, leaning into the wind, hungry for a coastline most maps still drew wrong.

She brought the Cormorant in on the last of the flood, kissing the shingle with barely a bump—the kind of landing that had taken her twenty years to make look easy—and the children swarmed the shallows before the anchor was even set, grabbing the mooring lines, hauling with the fierce seriousness of small people given real work. One of them shouted an order in Vessani and the others obeyed it, and Zephyrine felt the words go through her like a bell struck underwater.

Forty years the empire had said this language was gone. Here it was, bossing around a bucket line of seven-year-olds.

Ivo came down the path behind them, and she watched him come the way she always did—as if confirming something. Greyer at the temples than when she'd fished him out of a tide-market argument. Standing straighter. He reached the waterline and stopped, hands in his pockets, and just looked at the boat, at her, with that expression he had that was neither question nor answer but somehow both.

"You're late," he said.

"Tide turned against us off the Verlaine shoals. Had to beat north and come down the outside." She stepped onto the shingle, boots in the wet sand, and the ground did its usual trick of feeling simultaneously foreign and inevitable. "Also we stopped to rescue a fishing crew off Cape Merren. Their mast step had rotted through. They'll be telling the story in every harbor bar between here and the capital by week's end, and in their version I'll have fought off pirates."

"Were there pirates?"

"There was a very aggressive seal. But yes, probably pirates in the retelling."

He laughed—that low, unhurried laugh she'd learned meant he was happy and would admit it only sideways—and then he grew quieter, looking past her at the ketch, at the strangers leaning on her rail. "Four this time?"

"Five, counting me. The widow's staying a fortnight. Her father was *Kestrel's* second officer." She said the ship's name carefully. Everyone here did. "She wants to walk the cliff path at dawn. Says he used to describe it in letters. Said the gulls sounded like arguments."

"They do," Ivo said. "Sefra will want to meet her. Half this village lost someone on the *Kestrel*."

"I know. That's why I brought her."

He nodded, and for a moment they stood in the noise of the children and the surf and the creak of the anchored ketch, and neither of them filled the silence, because they had long since stopped needing to.

"The schoolroom's warm," he said finally. "There's fish stew on. Sefra made it, which means it's mostly bones and opinions, but it's hot."

"Sold."

They walked up the beach together, and the widow fell into step beside Ivo and began, hesitantly, to ask him questions, and he answered each one with the patience Zephyrine had watched him spend so freely on strangers ever since the reading at Salt Harbor—the patience of a man who understood that grief shared is grief halved, and memory shared is memory doubled. The divers peppered Zephyrine with questions about the inner caverns, and she gave them answers in the shape she always gave them: enough to respect the water, never enough to die of confidence. Some knowledge you sell. Some you teach. The difference, she'd learned, was whether you'd be able to sleep after.

At the top of the beach she stopped once and turned around.

The Cormorant rode her anchor in the gold light, patched and scarred and floating, and beyond her the Sable Current ran south in long silver seams, the same current that had carried a sealed chest to her net two years ago, the same current that had nearly drowned her twice and saved her three times, the same current the drowned nation had named *saelith*—the water that brings you home even when you didn't know you were going there.

She had spent her whole life believing the horizon was a thing you kept. That safety meant staying unseen, unnamed, three days gone before anyone learned your face. She had traded that belief away piece by piece—in a flooded anchorage, in a refusal signed with silence, in a sanctuary charter argued into existence across a magistrate's desk—and what she'd gotten back for it was this: a beach full of children speaking a resurrected tongue, a man walking beside her who knew her worst day and stayed anyway, a boat with a waiting list.

Visibility cost. Every day it cost. And invisibility had cost more.

"You're doing the thing," Ivo said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the horizon like it owes you money."

"It paid up," she said. "Finally. Took long enough."

They climbed the cliff path as the tide turned below them, the current swinging around the headland in its ancient patient arc, and ahead of them the village lights were coming on one window at a time, and behind them the sea ran on toward every port that would listen, and Zephyrine Pemberton—blacklisted, pardoned-in-one-country, exiled-by-choice, guide, salvager, smuggler of truths—walked into the smoke-smelling warmth of a room where her name was known and welcome, and began, as she would tomorrow, and the day after, and all the days the current carried her, to tell them how to read dangerous water.

The current runs on. So do they.
