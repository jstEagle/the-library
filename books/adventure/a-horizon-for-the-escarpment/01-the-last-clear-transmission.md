## 1. The Last Clear Transmission

The radio room at Vell's Landing smelled of kerosene and wet wool, and Isandro Montrose had come to find the smell comforting in the way that a person finds the walls of a prison comforting—because they are hers, because she chose them, because no one can take them away without her noticing.

She had the midnight-to-dawn watch, which nobody else wanted. The upper river went quiet after dark in the weeks before calving season. The ice above the gorge groaned and settled like an old man turning over in bed, and the freight traffic that usually chattered across the network all day—the coal barges out of Kettering, the fish runners, the government survey pings that came through at odd hours like birdsong from another country—all of it thinned to nothing by ten o'clock. What remained was static, and the slow tick of the dial lamp, and Isandro's own handwriting filling the log book in her small, careful engineer's script: *Conditions clear. No traffic. Barometer steady.*

She had been very good at small, careful scripts for three years now. It was one of the things hiding required.

The port of Vell's Landing sat where the river widened and forgot its ambitions—a scatter of warehouses with roofs the color of old zinc, a customs house, four taverns of varying honesty, and a long timber quay where the water lay brown and patient against the pilings. Upstream, past the last bend you could see from the harbor mouth, the river narrowed into the gorge, and beyond the gorge rose the escarpment: a wall of gray limestone two hundred meters high, seamed with ice in winter, holding back the high country and the glacier that fed everything. Her father had surveyed that escarpment. Her father had closed it. And then he had died, and left her the paper, and she had spent three years pretending the paper meant nothing.

The compass sat on the shelf above the set, where it always sat during her watches. Brass-cased, lid dented where it had once taken a fall on rock—he'd told her the story a hundred times, the day the transit tripod slipped at Kilometer Forty—and engraved inside the lid with a line she had stopped reading years ago because reading it hurt: *For Isandro, who will finish what I started.* She kept it there not as a memento but as ballast. A thing to weigh down the part of her that still wanted to be somebody's daughter instead of nobody's engineer.

At 0341, the Halvard called in.

Isandro knew the voice before the callsign finished—Expedition barge *Halvard*, working the upper gorge approach under contract to the Northern Cartographic Institute, eleven souls aboard including the cook, who had once bought her a drink at the Anchorline and told her, laughing, that the expedition's radio operator was a drunkard and a sentimentalist and that both conditions were necessary for the work. The signal came in weak and ragged, riding a carrier wave that stuttered like a man running uphill.

*"...Halvard to any station, Halvard to any station... we have lost the marked channel at—"*

Static swallowed a full second. Isandro's hand was already moving, pencil finding the log, time stamp automatic. 0341. Signal strength two, fading.

*"—repeat, we have lost the marked channel. Ice movement on the north wall. We are taking a bearing off the old survey marks, the ones on the—"*

Another wash of noise, longer this time. She leaned into the set, one earpiece pressed hard, and turned the gain up until the static became a physical thing, a wind blowing through the room.

*"—bearing zero-seven-one, confirm, zero-seven-one, following the escarpment line, the closed line, do you copy, the CLOSED—"*

Then the transmission broke apart entirely, syllables shredded into carrier hiss, and what came through the wreckage was thin and strange and made the hair rise along Isandro's forearms. Not words. A tone. Someone keying the transmitter in long, deliberate pulses—three short, three long, three short—and then nothing at all, not even static where the Halvard's frequency had been, just the clean empty silence of a channel that no longer had anyone standing in it.

She sat with her pencil hovering over the log for a long time.

Zero-seven-one. She didn't need the folio to check it. She had grown up inside those numbers the way other children grew up inside their family names. Bearing zero-seven-one true from the gorge mouth ran straight along the base of the escarpment, through the narrows, past Kilometer Forty, into the high channel that Aldous Montrose had surveyed over six seasons and then sealed with the full weight of the River Authority behind him—closed to commercial traffic, closed to salvage, closed even to the fishing boats that had used it for generations, and everyone in Vell's Landing had a theory about why and none of them were kind. The common one was that the old man had found a fault, or hadn't found one, or had wanted the river to himself out of pride, or had been paid to close it by someone who wanted it closed. The kindest one—the one Isandro herself had tried to believe—was simply that the surveys had failed, that the line was unsafe, that her father had sealed it to keep people alive.

The transmission said otherwise. The transmission said the Halvard had lost their own marked channel—the new one, the certified one, the one the Institute had opened two years ago with great ceremony—and in the fog and falling ice they had turned, deliberately, toward the dead man's line. Toward the only bearings on that stretch of river that had ever been trusted completely. The crew knew. Some engineer aboard that barge, standing in the dark with spray freezing on the rails, had looked at the charts and understood what every riverman secretly understood: that the closed line was the good line, and whatever reason it had been closed, safety wasn't it.

And then they had keyed SOS, and then the channel had gone quiet, and the calving season was nine days out, which meant the gorge would open for perhaps six tides of broken ice and moving water and then seal until spring.

Isandro looked at the log book. The regulations were simple and she had recited them to herself often enough to know them by heart: *All distress traffic received shall be logged in full and reported to the harbormaster within one hour of receipt.* One hour. It was 0352. She had fifty minutes to walk forty meters across the frozen yard to the harbormaster's quarters and wake Odile Ferran's problem before it became her own.

She did not move.

Here is what she told herself, sitting in the kerosene smell with her hands flat on the desk: *You don't know anything yet. A garbled transmission is not a wreck. Barges lose contact in the gorge approach every season; half the time they're alongside again by noon, complaining about their aerials. If you report a phantom disaster and the Halvard comes steaming home tomorrow, you'll have shut the river for nothing, cost the port a week of traffic, and put your name—the name you've spent three years burying—onto an incident report that goes to the Authority, to the insurers, to anyone who cares to read the fine print of Isandro Montrose's signature. Prudence says wait. Prudence says wait for confirmation. Prudence says this is not your river anymore.*

It was a good argument. It was even mostly true. She built it carefully, plank by plank, the way her father had taught her to build a case for closing a channel—test each span, load it, see if it holds. And it held, mostly, right up until she noticed that her hands were shaking, and that prudence had never once in her life made her hands shake.

Because underneath the argument, in the hold below the waterline, was the simpler truth: if she filed the report, someone would ask her questions. Where is bearing zero-seven-one, Miss Montrose? Why does that bearing sound familiar? Isn't that your father's line? And then the name would be loose in the world again, off the paper and into mouths, and the small careful life she had constructed—radio watch, rented room above the chandlery, drinks alone at the Anchorline, debts paid down a few crowns at a time under a name she'd shortened and a history she'd sanded smooth—that life would take water fast. The Montrose debts were not abstract. There were men in Kettering who held notes signed by her father's estate, and estates pass liabilities down along with compasses, and Isandro had learned that a woman with a famous surname and an insolvent inheritance is a woman who gets found. Hiding was not cowardice. Hiding was arithmetic.

So she did the arithmetic. And the arithmetic said: wait for morning. Wait for the scheduled check-in at 0800. If the Halvard answers, log a routine contact and sleep well. If they don't—

If they didn't, she would file it then, and tell herself the delay had cost nothing, and spend the rest of her life checking whether that was true.

She wrote in the log: *0341 — fragmentary transmission, station identifying as Halvard, signal fading. Content unintelligible. No action.* The lie sat on the page in her small careful script, neat as a weld, and she looked at it and understood that she had just signed something. Not with her name—with something worse, something that would follow the name around forever. Three years of clean paper, and she had dirtied it in ninety seconds, for the oldest reason there was: to stay invisible.

The dawn came up gray over the quay. She logged the 0800 window—no contact from the Halvard, which was itself an anomaly she recorded as *no scheduled traffic*—and walked home through the frozen mud with her collar up, past the customs house where the flag hung stiff as sheet iron, past the quay where a fleet she didn't recognize yet was beginning to make fast: three black-hulled salvage tugs flying a house flag she'd never seen, taking on coal and cordwood with the brisk unhungry efficiency of vessels that expected to go somewhere soon.

In her room above the chandlery, she did not sleep. She took the compass down from where it lived in her sea bag—it traveled with her, always, though she told herself it was habit rather than meaning—and opened the dented lid, and read the engraving for the first time in years. *Who will finish what I started.*

"I'm not finishing anything," she said aloud to the empty room, to the compass, to the man who had died on the escarpment with his survey folio under his arm and his debts in her name. "I'm keeping my head down. That's different."

The compass, being brass, said nothing.

But at some point in the gray middle of the morning, sleep finally took her, and she dreamed of the gorge—not the way she'd seen it as a girl from the survey camps, but the way it must look now, sealed in ice, with a barge somewhere inside it holding eleven people who had trusted the wrong chart, and a strongbox, and a story. In the dream, the ice was already calving. It sounded like artillery. It sounded like a door closing.

She woke at dusk to knocking on the stair door—Teo from the chandlery, breathless, wanting to know if she'd heard, wanting to know if it was true, the whole quay talking about it: the *Halvard*, overdue, silent since the night watch, and strangers in black hulls at the coal wharf asking questions about the upper river.

"Miss," the boy said, stamping cold out of his boots, "there's a broker lady down at the Anchorline asking after anyone who knows the old escarpment routes. By name, near enough." He grinned, oblivious, a boy delivering gossip like bread. "Says she'll pay for local knowledge. Real money."

Isandro stood in the doorway with the compass in her pocket and the weight of an unfiled report in her chest, accruing interest.

"Did she give a name?" she asked.

"Ferran," the boy said. "Odile Ferran."
