## 2. A Map of Closed Lines

The broker arrived on the morning tide, which was how Isandro knew she was not a tourist. Tourists came at noon, when the fog burned off and Vell's Landing showed its one good face—the painted gables, the fish market steaming in the cold, the cranes swinging like patient herons over the water. Nobody with serious business came at dawn. Serious business wanted to catch you before your coffee, before your defenses, before the town had time to gather around you the way small towns do even for people they barely trust.

Isandro saw her from the radio shack window: a woman in a gray wool coat too fine for the mud-slicked boardwalk, walking the length of the pier with the unhurried stride of someone who had never once been kept waiting. Two men trailed her at a respectful distance, carrying nothing, which meant they were the kind of men who carried everything that mattered under their coats.

The knock came three minutes later. Isandro counted it out of habit—she counted everything now, tides, transmissions, debts—and opened the door.

"Isandro Montrose." The woman said it not as a question but as a confirmation, the way a surveyor confirms a benchmark. She was perhaps fifty, silver shot through dark hair cut short as a man's, eyes the flat pale color of river ice. "Odile Ferran. I represent the Corvane Salvage Consortium."

"I know who you represent." Everyone in Vell's Landing knew by now. Three ships had come upriver in two days flying the consortium's black-and-copper pennant, and the harbormaster had spent the whole of yesterday being polite to men who asked questions about draft depths and gorge conditions. "You're here about the Halvard."

"I'm here about cargo." Ferran stepped inside without invitation, the way people did when they'd already decided the world belonged to them. Her gaze swept the shack—the cot, the kettle, the wall of logbooks going back eleven years, each spine labeled in Isandro's careful hand. It lingered nowhere long enough to seem interested, which was itself a kind of interest. "The Halvard Expedition carried a sealed manifest. Survey instruments, core samples, a company strongbox. My clients have an interest in recovering company property."

"Company property." Isandro leaned against her desk. "Nine people went up that river twelve days ago. Their last transmission was garbage and static. And you're standing in my shack asking about a strongbox."

"Nine people," Ferran agreed mildly, "who signed contracts waiving rescue obligations beyond the insurance minimum. I don't expect you to approve of my industry, Miss Montrose. I only need your competence." She produced a folded paper from her coat—not a chart, Isandro noted, but something typed, official-looking. "You logged their final transmission. The harbormaster confirmed it this morning. A bearing, garbled but recoverable. You're the last person in this port who heard them clearly."

"The transmission was static."

"It was a bearing." Ferran's voice didn't rise; it lowered, if anything, going soft and precise as a depth gauge. "And the bearing crosses the escarpment line. The closed line. The one surveyed by Edouard Montrose and sealed by order of the Northern Rail & River Commission fourteen years ago." She let that sit in the room like cold air. "Your father's line."

Isandro said nothing. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"My clients believe the Halvard attempted the escarpment passage—either deliberately or driven off course by ice. If so, they are stranded in a stretch of water that appears on no current chart, no pilot guide, no salvage registry. Except—" Ferran's pale eyes came up and fixed on her, "—except possibly in the private folio of the engineer who mapped it. A folio that passed, on his death, to his daughter."

"I wouldn't know anything about that."

"No," Ferran said pleasantly. "I imagine you wouldn't." She set a card on the desk, heavy cream stock, embossed. Then, beside it, a second object: a bank draft, folded once, thick enough that its weight made a sound against the wood. "That's for whatever you remember. Not the folio—not yet. Just what you heard in that transmission, and what you know about the approaches to the second narrows. There will be more where that came from. Considerably more."

Isandro looked at the draft without touching it. She thought about the number behind her on the ledger in the back room—her number, the family's number, the debt that had followed her out of the capital like a dog that would not stop following. She thought about how many years of radio watches it would take to pay down, watching the tide clock, eating herring, growing old in a town that called her *the Montrose girl* in tones that managed to be both pitying and accusatory.

She thought about her father's hands on a compass, and what he'd died protecting.

"I logged static," she said. "I filed it as static. That's all there is."

Ferran studied her for a long moment, and Isandro had the uncomfortable sense of being measured—not as a person but as a structure, load-bearing capacity assessed, weak points catalogued. "The fleet runs the gorge within six days," Ferran said finally. "With or without your help. Without it, we'll be navigating blind through water your father considered dangerous enough to seal. People may die, Miss Montrose. Possibly people who could have lived." She retrieved nothing—left the draft and the card where they lay. "Keep those. Offers improve with reconsideration."

At the door she paused, half-turned, coat catching the gray light. "Your father refused money too, near the end. Did you know that? He was offered a great deal to reopen testimony about that line. He chose silence instead." A thin smile, almost kind. "Silence is expensive, Miss Montrose. I do hope you can afford it better than he did."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the boardwalk, and Isandro stood in her shack breathing carefully until the gulls took up their noise again and the world resumed its ordinary shape.

* * *

She didn't touch the draft. That mattered to her, and she noticed that it mattered, and hated herself a little for noticing. Instead she locked the shack, walked the long way home along the seawall where the wind could scour her face clean of expression, and climbed the stairs to the rented room above the chandlery where she had lived for six years under a name that was only half a lie.

The folio lived under the floorboard beneath the bed, wrapped in oilcloth, exactly where it had lived since the night she'd carried it out of the capital in a satchel along with a compass and a debt. She had not opened it in four years. Opening it felt like exhumation.

But she knelt, and lifted the board, and unwrapped it anyway.

The folio was leather gone soft as cloth at the corners, stamped with the old commission seal—Northern Rail & River, crossed pick and paddle—and tied with a cord her father had knotted in a particular way, a surveyor's trick, so that any tampering would show. The knot was intact. Her own knot from four years ago. Her hands shook slightly as she untied it.

Inside: forty-one sheets of drafting linen, each one a masterpiece. Isandro had grown up believing her father was a meticulous man; she had not understood, until she was old enough to read drawings herself, that he was something closer to a fanatic. Every sounding recorded. Every current arrow drawn with its velocity figure. Every rock, shoal, and eddy along ninety kilometers of escarpment gorge rendered in ink fine as hair. This was the map that did not exist. The line the commission had struck from every public record after his death, declaring it unsurveyable, unnavigable, closed.

She spread the sheets across the floor by lamplight, weighting the corners with books, and began at the beginning the way he had taught her: *read the water first, the notes second.*

The water told her things she hadn't expected. There were corrections—dozens of them—in a different ink, a darker iron-gall brown, layered over the original fair copy. Her father's hand, but older, harder, pressed deep into the linen as though he'd been angry or afraid or both. Dates in the margins spanning three years after the supposed final survey. He had gone back. Again and again, he had gone back into the gorge after the line was already sealed, re-sounding channels, revising bearings, annotating.

*Second narrows — false channel east of the pillar. Reads safe at low water. It is not. Marked on commission charts 1908–1911. Who drew that chart?*

The marginal note stopped Isandro's breath. She read it three times. The commission's published charts—the ones Ferran's fleet would be carrying—still carried the 1911 bathymetry. Still showed that false channel as the preferred route through the narrows. Her father had proven it wrong, documented it wrong, and the documentation had vanished into the same seal that swallowed the whole line.

She turned sheet after sheet, and the annotations grew stranger, more urgent:

*Depth figures on file do not match soundings taken. Discrepancy is not error. Someone wants this line to fail.*

*Met with R—— today. He says the commission will not amend. Asked me to withdraw my objections. Offered to buy the folio. Declined.*

*If anything happens to me, the line must stay closed until the false charts are corrected. Better a dead passage than a killing one.*

The lamp guttered. Isandro sat back on her heels among the scattered sheets, her father's handwriting swimming in the yellow light, and felt the ground shift under assumptions she'd carried for fourteen years. She had believed the story everyone believed: Edouard Montrose, brilliant and difficult, had surveyed a passage the commission judged unrunnable, and the shame of it had broken him, and the river—or the debt, the stories varied—had finished the job. She had inherited the disgrace and the paperwork and the compass, and she had hidden from all three.

But this wasn't a failed survey. This was a war conducted in ink. Her father hadn't sealed the line because it couldn't be run. He'd fought to keep it closed because the charts everyone else relied on had been falsified—and someone, somewhere upstream of him, had wanted it that way. Wanted a route through the escarpment that killed the unwary. Or wanted the true route known to no one but themselves.

Her eyes fell on the last annotated sheet, the ninetieth kilometer, where the gorge narrowed to the throat her father had called *the Escarpment Bearing*. In the margin, in that hard late hand, a single line she had somehow never registered, or never understood:

*Bearing holds true. The passage exists. God forgive whoever needs it before the truth comes out.*

Whoever needs it.

The Halvard expedition was up there. Nine signatures on a waiver, a garbled bearing crossing her father's closed line, and a salvage fleet assembling at the docks with charts that were wrong in exactly the way her father had spent his last years documenting.

Isandro looked at the bank draft's memory sitting heavy in her coat pocket, and at the folio spread around her like an open grave, and understood at last what she had actually inherited. Not a disgrace. Not a burden alone.

Bait. She was bait, and always had been—the daughter of the only man who knew the truth of the escarpment, waiting fourteen years in a river port for someone to come asking the right questions. Ferran had come asking.

Outside, far upriver, the first ice of the calving season cracked somewhere in the dark—a sound like a rifle shot, like a knuckle rapped twice on a door.

Six tides, the harbormaster would say tomorrow. Six tides before the gorge opened, and six before it sealed again.

Isandro began, carefully, to fold the maps.
