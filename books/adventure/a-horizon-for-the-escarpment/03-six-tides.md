## 3. Six Tides

The harbormaster's office smelled of tar, cold coffee, and the particular staleness of paper that had been handled by too many wet hands. Isandro stood at the counter while Dree Halloway—harbormaster of Vell's Landing for nineteen years, a woman built like a mooring bollard and about as given to sentiment—spread the season charts across the scarred wood.

"You want to know about the gorge." It wasn't a question. Dree didn't waste breath on questions she could answer with statements.

"The calving window," Isandro said. "When does it open this year?"

Dree's eyes came up from the chart, and there was something in them that Isandro recognized and disliked: the look of a woman reassessing someone she'd thought she had catalogued. "Most people ask when it closes."

"Most people aren't asking because they're thinking of going through it."

"No," Dree agreed. "Most people have sense." She turned the chart so it faced Isandro and tapped one blunt finger against the upper river, where the gorge cut its black line between the escarpment walls. "Ice bridge on the upper tongue started moving nine days ago. Calving season runs early this year—the meltwater's been eating at the seracs since midwinter, and the whole face is rotten above the waterline. When it lets go, it'll open the channel for—" she calculated, lips moving slightly, "—six tides. Maybe seven if we're lucky. Then the ice comes down like a dropped ceiling and nothing larger than a canoe goes through until next spring."

"Six tides." Isandro did the arithmetic without wanting to. The Halvard expedition had gone silent eleven days ago. Whatever was left of them—if anything was left—had been sitting in that gorge country through the freeze-thaw, through the nights that dropped to killing temperatures even in the open season.

"And here's the other thing you already know," Dree said, leaning back and crossing her arms. "The Halvard's last bearing put them inside closed water. Your father's line. Which means whatever I say next, half this port will decide I said it wrong." She lowered her voice, not from secrecy but from weariness. "Ferran's fleet filed transit papers yesterday. Three vessels. They're running the gorge whether the harbormaster's office likes it or not, and my office doesn't much like it, but their bonds are in order and the river doesn't belong to me."

"When do they sail?"

"Second tide tomorrow morning. The *Corvane* leads. Ferran herself aboard." Dree gathered her charts with the brisk finality of a woman closing a door. "Now get out of my office, Montrose, and think very carefully about what you're doing standing there looking at my gorge chart like your father used to look at it."

* * *

Isandro walked the waterfront until dark, which took longer than it should have, because Vell's Landing's waterfront was only four hundred meters of rotting pilings, fish sheds, and moored barges going soft at the waterline. She walked it twice anyway. Three times.

The problem was simple, and simplicity made it worse. There were exactly two kinds of people who ran the upper gorge: fools and navigators. The fools died in the first narrows. The navigators were dead, or retired, or named Montrose—and the last of those had spent four years making herself into no one at all, a radio-watch woman with steady hands and an unremarkable face who logged transmissions and filed reports and owed money to nobody who mattered, except that she owed money to everybody, inherited down to the last copper from a father whose estate had been eaten alive by creditors before the escarpment lines were even sealed.

She could stay. That was the sane choice, the choice she'd been making every day for four years. Stay, keep the watch, let Ferran's fleet go up the river and come back—or not—with whatever they found of the Halvard crew. Sign nothing, say nothing, know nothing. The debt of silence accruing interest, compounding nightly, and her lying awake with her father's compass on her sternum like a stone she couldn't digest.

But she had heard the transmission. She alone had heard it whole—the garble stripped away by four years of listening to dying radios, the way you learned to hear the signal underneath the noise. *Bearing two-seven-one, holding at the shelf ice, taking on—* and then the sound that wasn't static, that was steel giving way, and then nothing. Two-seven-one. Her father's line. And somewhere behind that bearing, men and women who had trusted their survey charts were drowning or freezing or waiting, and the only person in Vell's Landing who understood what those waters did between the second narrows and the escarpment shelf was currently walking the waterfront in circles trying to talk herself out of caring.

That was the thing about hiding, she thought, stopping at the end of the north pier. You got good at it. You got so good that eventually you hid from yourself, and then one night a bearing came across the wire that matched a number you'd known since childhood, and all the hiding turned out to be a kind of debt too—one that couldn't be paid in coin.

The lights of Ferran's fleet rode at anchor off the breakwater. Three vessels, running lamps doubled, deck cranes silhouetted against the last grey light. Salvage ships didn't carry that many cranes for rescue work. She'd noticed that days ago and told herself it meant nothing.

* * *

Odile Ferran received her aboard the *Corvane* at eight bells, in a cabin that managed to be both luxurious and temporary—good brandy in a decanter, charts rolled and strapped like cargo, everything ready to be gone within the hour. Ferran herself was small and precise, silver at the temples, with the unhurried eyes of someone who had never once needed to raise her voice.

"Montrose." She pronounced the name carefully, tasting it. "The harbormaster's daughter says you've been asking after the calving window. And the radio office confirms you logged the Halvard's last transmission personally." She poured two glasses without being asked. "You'll forgive me for having done my reading. A navigator who won't navigate is still a navigator."

"I'm not offering to navigate."

"No?" Ferran set a glass in front of her anyway. "Then why are you aboard my ship at night, uninvited, three days after refusing to sell me so much as a rumor?"

Isandro looked at the decanter, at the charts, at the woman. Every instinct she had honed in four years of invisibility screamed at her to stand up, thank her politely, and walk back down the gangway into her small safe life. Instead she heard herself say: "Your transit papers list a pilot of record. Corven Brast. He ran the lower rapids twice, ten years ago, before the channel shifted. He doesn't know the second narrows has migrated forty meters east since the flood year. He doesn't know the shelf ice above kilometer thirty calves without warning in a warm wind. If he takes your lead vessel into the gorge on old water, you lose the *Corvane*, and probably the two astern of her, and every soul aboard."

Ferran's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a door opening onto a room Isandro couldn't see. "And you do know."

"I grew up on that water. My father surveyed every meter of it twice."

"Your father," Ferran said softly, "whose line is sealed, whose folio is sealed, whose daughter has spent years pretending the name means nothing to her." She lifted her glass but didn't drink. "I'll be direct, because I suspect you respect directness. I need a navigator. Not a pilot—a navigator. Someone who can read that gorge the way its maker read it. The pay is obscene. The bonus, if we recover what we're sent to recover, would clear every debt your family left rotting under your name. And in exchange, you sign the articles, you take my orders, and you don't ask what the fleet is salvaging until it's on deck."

"That last part isn't negotiable for me."

"It's entirely non-negotiable for me." Ferran smiled then, and it was almost warm, which made it worse. "Six tides, Montrose. The river opens for six tides and then it closes for a year. Whatever the Halvard left in that gorge stays there, or comes out in my holds. You can stand on your pier and listen to the ice groan from a safe distance, or you can come aboard and find out what your father was protecting. But you can't do both, and you have until second tide to choose."

Isandro stood. The brandy sat untouched between them, catching the lamplight like something amber and preserved—like the past itself, sealed in glass.

"Second tide," Isandro said. "I'll be here."

* * *

She went back to her rooms above the chandlery and packed the way she'd learned to pack in the bad years: fast, light, assuming she might never return. Spare clothes. Charts of her own—hand-copied, unregistered, the ones she'd sworn she'd never use. Her father's compass, which went into her breast pocket where it lay against her heart like a second pulse.

Last, the folio. She took it out of the floor cavity and held it a long moment, the oilcloth stiff under her hands, the survey office seal still unbroken along the spine. She didn't open it. Opening it felt like a step she couldn't take back, and some stubborn part of her wanted to board the *Corvane* still able to tell herself she was going as a hired hand, nothing more, a pair of eyes for hire with no inheritance attached.

She knew it was a lie even as she wrapped the folio in canvas and slung it over her shoulder. Ferran hadn't hired a pair of eyes. Ferran had hired a name, and both of them knew it.

At the window, the fleet's riding lights burned steady off the breakwater, and beyond them, far upriver, the glacier sat in the dark like a held breath. She thought of the transmission again—*bearing two-seven-one, taking on—*and the steel-voice scream beneath it, and the silence after, eleven days of silence, six tides left to answer it.

Her father had chosen the line over his life, in the end, though the order of events depended on whom you asked. She had chosen hiding over everything else for four years, and hiding had kept her breathing and cost her everything else.

Invisibility ended at second tide. She signed the articles in the grey pre-dawn light at the *Corvane*'s rail, her signature—the first she'd written in four years that wasn't a lie of omission—going onto the page in ink that immediately smeared in the river damp. Ferran countersigned without ceremony. A bosun with a face like a closed fist showed her to a narrow cabin between the chain locker and the engine room, and by the time the mooring lines came off, the whole crew knew what she was: the navigator. The Montrose girl. The one job no one else could do.

Vell's Landing fell away behind them in the mist, small and grey and safe, and Isandro stood at the bow with her father's compass open in her palm, watching the needle swing toward the gorge and hold there, trembling, like something that had finally been pointed home.

Ahead, faint through the morning fog, the upper river was already beginning to speak—the deep, grinding complaint of a glacier preparing to let go of a hundred years of ice.

They had six tides.
