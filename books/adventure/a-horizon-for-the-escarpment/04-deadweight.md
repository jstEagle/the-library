## 4. Deadweight

The Corvane smelled of diesel, rust, and old rope—the honest stink of a working boat, which Isandro had expected, and beneath it something sweeter and less honest: fresh paint over patched plating, the smell of a vessel dressed up for a job it wasn't built for. She noticed it her first hour aboard and filed it away with everything else she noticed and said nothing about.

She'd been given a bunk in the forward crew compartment, narrow as a coffin shelf, and a chart table in the wheelhouse that someone had wiped down but not cleaned. Coffee rings ghosted the varnish. Someone else's tobacco shavings still lived in the seam of the drawer. The wheelhouse itself was cramped and high-windowed, and from it she could see the whole gray expanse of Vell's Landing falling away astern as the tug took the tide out—cranes, fish sheds, the harbormaster's tower with its lazy semaphore arms—and then nothing but river.

The lower rapids began at Kilometer Nine.

Isandro stood at the table with her father's compass open beside her elbow like a small black witness, and ran the water in her head before she ran it on paper. That was how he had taught her, back when teaching was still something he did with patience instead of desperation: *Read it twice, Isandro. Once for what the map says, once for what the river wants. They are never the same conversation.*

The Halvard's last transmission had been a bearing. She had not told Ferran that. She had told Ferran only what a disgraced navigator would tell a broker who owned her contract for six tides: that she knew the gorge approaches, that she could read water, that she was cheap and desperate and had no one to write to if she drowned. All true. None of it the whole truth. The debt of silence accrued another day's interest, compounding quietly, and she let it.

"Navigator." The helmsman was a broad woman called Sefa with burn-scarred forearms and a voice like gravel poured slow. "Ferran wants speed through the lowers. Says we lose the light if we lollygag."

"Ferran can want," Isandro said, and heard the wheelhouse go quiet around her. Two deckhands by the door stopped pretending to check the lashings. "The lower rapids at this stage run four meters above the marked channel on the east side and there's a sweepwood snag below the second bend that isn't on anyone's chart because it wasn't there last season. We take it at eight knots or we take it apart."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment—not hostile, exactly. Appraising. The way you look at a tool you've been handed without instructions. "You've run this?"

"I grew up on it."

Another silence, shorter. Then Sefa turned to the engine-order telegraph and rang down *slow ahead*, and whatever the crew had decided about the woman Ferran had hired off the docks, it shifted by a degree. Not warmth. Recalibration.

The lower rapids took them two hours and cost them nothing, which was the point. Isandro called the channel from the table—*starboard ten, midships, starboard twenty, hold*—and the river came up to meet them white-lipped and brown with snowmelt, and every time the hull shuddered over a boil she felt the crew's attention on her back like sun. By the third bend they were answering her orders before Sefa relayed them. By the fifth, when she called *hard aport* and swung wide around the sweepwood—a black arm of spruce waving at them from the current like a drowned man's greeting—one of the deckhands whistled low under his breath.

Wary respect. She recognized it because she'd spent three years cultivating its opposite: the studied invisibility of a woman nobody bothered to look at twice. Invisibility had been a shelter. It was also, she understood now, a kind of debt too—you paid for it daily in small surrenders, and the interest never stopped.

They made the anchorage below the narrows at dusk. Ahead, the river narrowed between walls of gray basalt where the escarpment shouldered up out of the earth like the spine of something buried alive, and the water between went dark and quick. Somewhere beyond those walls, upriver, an expedition had gone silent. Somewhere beyond them, her father's sealed line threaded through country no certified chart admitted existed.

Ferran came to the wheelhouse after supper. She moved like a woman who had never once entered a room uncertain of her welcome—gray suit, gray eyes, a ledger of a face. She set a folio on the chart table without ceremony.

"The fleet's approach charts," she said. "Standard issue. I want your corrections before we run the narrows tomorrow. Mark anything that disagrees."

Isandro opened the folio and the cold walked up her spine before her mind caught up with why.

The chartwork was good. Better than good—it was recent, surveyed within the last two seasons by the look of the soundings, and beautifully drafted. And near the second narrows, at Kilometer Forty where the channel doglegged hard to port between two shoals, the fairway buoy was drawn forty meters east of where it belonged.

Not misprinted. Misplaced. A misprint scatters; this was surgical. The error sat precisely where a navigator trusting the chart instead of her eyes would cut the corner early and put her bow onto the eastern shoal at full flood. It was the kind of mistake that got made on purpose by someone who understood charts the way a locksmith understands locks.

Her father had taught her that too, though he'd never used these words: *A chart is a weapon pointed both ways.*

"Problem?" Ferran asked.

Isandro kept her face doing nothing at all. Three years of practice. "Soundings look thin here." She tapped a spot well away from the falsified buoy. "I'll want leadsman verification at the dogleg."

"You'll have it." Ferran collected the folio, unhurried. At the door she paused. "You come recommended as thorough, Montrose. Thorough is worth money to me. Curious is not." Then she was gone, and the wheelhouse lamp buzzed, and Isandro stood looking at the dark shape of the narrows through the glass and understanding, with terrible clarity, that the Corvane was meant to survive this voyage and some other vessel in the fleet was not—or perhaps none of them were, if the cargo mattered more than the crew.

She thought of the Halvard's garbled final words. *Bearing true—line holds—tell them the line—* and then static, and then nothing, for eleven days now.

* * *

She waited until the middle watch, when the boat breathed and ticked around her and only the anchor watch dozed in the deckhouse. She spread her own copy of the fleet chart on the table and weighted the corners with the compass tin, a spool of wire, her cup. From her seabag she drew her father's folio—not the sealed survey itself, which stayed wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the bag, but her own tracings of his annotations, made by lamplight over three sleepless nights in Vell's Landing. His hand marched across the tracing in pencil gone silver with age: soundings, eddy lines, a note at the second narrows reading *buoy station false by design—verify by transit, not by chart*, dated four years before the line was closed.

He had known. He had found the same falsification, or its ancestor, and written it down, and then the line had been sealed and he had died protecting the seal, and she had inherited the knowledge on paper alone like a deed to a house burned down long ago.

She laid the tracing over the fleet chart and aligned the meridians, and there it was: the same error, in the same place, grown more precise with time. Whoever had drafted Ferran's charts had inherited the lie the way she had inherited the truth.

"You're going to get us all killed with that, or save us. Which?"

Isandro didn't startle—she'd trained herself out of it—but her hand did move, flat across the tracing, covering her father's handwriting the way you'd cover a wound.

The deckhand from the lashings stood in the companionway. Young, maybe twenty-two, dark-haired, with the permanent salt-cracked lips of a man who'd grown up on decks. Teo, the muster list said. He'd been watching her since the lower rapids with an attention she'd mistaken for suspicion and now suspected was something closer to hunger—the hunger of a crewman trying to work out whether the person steering him through white water was a professional or a problem.

"That's fleet property," he said, nodding at the chart. "Copying it's theft."

"It's wrong," Isandro said. "Deliberately. The fairway at the second narrows is drawn forty meters east of true. Anybody who steers it puts us on the shoal at flood tide."

Teo came into the wheelhouse slowly. He looked at the chart, then at her hand still flattened on the tracing, then at her face. "Wrong how? Print error?"

"Print errors don't sit exactly where a loaded tug cutting the corner would strike." She lifted her hand. Let him see the pencil lines, the old silver annotations, the transits her father had noted—*align chimney ruin with notch peak, buoy true lies on that line*. "This is drafting. Somebody wanted a wreck at Kilometer Forty."

Teo was quiet a while. Outside, the river talked against the hull, patient and enormous. "Why are you telling me?"

Because you're the only one aboard who looks at the water instead of the wages, she almost said. Because I've been invisible for three years and I'm tired of carrying this alone. Because if I'm wrong about you I'd rather be wrong quickly.

"Because you bent the lashing on the number three cradle this morning and re-rigged it without being told, and because you watched me call the lowers instead of watching Ferran watch me," she said. "And because tomorrow somebody runs that dogleg first, and I'd rather it be a boat whose deck crew knows the buoy is a lie."

He studied her the way Sefa had, appraising, recalibrating. Then he pulled the wire spool off the corner of the chart, sat down across the table uninvited, and picked up a pencil.

"Show me the transit," he said. "If we're going to commit mutiny against a piece of paper, I want to know it by heart."

They worked until the sky went pewter behind the escarpment. He copied the corrected bearings onto a scrap of chart he folded into his shirt pocket—"paper burns, memory doesn't," he said, and she wondered what kind of life taught a deckhand that lesson—and he asked questions that were better than most junior engineers' questions, and somewhere in the second hour she realized she had spoken more true sentences tonight than in the past three years combined.

At the companionway he paused. "Ferran doesn't hire thorough," he said. "She hires useful and disposable. You're the first navigator she's signed who reads water like that. So either you're lying about who you are, or somebody told her you weren't worth checking on." He shrugged. "Either way. Watch your bunkmate's boots. Yours'll be gone before the narrows if she decides you're deadweight."

Then he was gone, and Isandro sat alone with her father's compass and the lamp buzzing, and thought about boots, and wrecks, and the fact that Teo had said *somebody told her*—which meant the fleet had records, which meant Ferran had known who Isandro Montrose was before she ever set foot on the dock at Vell's Landing, and had hired her anyway.

The anchor watch changed. The river ran dark toward the narrows. And in the morning, when the lead line came up singing its depths at Kilometer Forty, Isandro stood at the chart table with both charts open before her and made, quietly, the first decision of the voyage that was entirely her own:

Whether to correct the lie out loud—in front of Ferran, in front of the fleet, burning her invisibility forever—or to steer the truth silently and let everyone believe the chart had simply been lucky.

The leadsman sang the shoal. The dogleg waited. And Isandro reached for the pencil.
