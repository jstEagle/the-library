## 5. What the River Keeps

Teo Vask had been running cargo on the upper river since he was fourteen, and in all those years he had learned one rule above the others: a crew that doesn't ask questions stays fed. He'd kept to it through three owners of the *Corvane*, through two wrecks and one near-mutiny, through winters when the pay came late and summers when it didn't come at all. Questions were ballast you carried for nothing. He had never once regretted throwing them overboard.

Until the night he found the manifest.

It was his own fault, or his own diligence—on the *Corvane* the two were hard to tell apart. Ferran's fleet ran lean, every hand doubling at two jobs, and Teo's second job was stores. That meant the hold, after evening mess, counting crates by lamplight while the river muttered against the hull below him. The *Corvane* rode heavy even for a salvage tug; she sat low in the water like something carrying more than her lines allowed, and Teo had told himself it was chain, spare plate, the usual deadweight of a vessel built to recover other vessels.

The crate was stenciled HALVARD EXPEDITION — PROPERTY OF THE ROYAL HYDROGRAPHIC SURVEY, and it should not have been aboard.

He stood with his lamp raised for a long moment, listening to the deck above him creak as the watch changed. Then he set the lamp down and worked the lid loose, because there was diligence and there was foolishness, and he had never learned which one he practiced.

Inside: survey instruments wrapped in oilcloth, still bearing the Halvard expedition's inventory tags. A brass theodolite with a cracked spirit level. Field notebooks bound in waxed canvas, their spines stamped with dates from the spring—the spring, months before the expedition had ever sent its last transmission downriver. And beneath them, wrapped separately, a document case sealed with the Survey's lead stamp, unbroken.

Teo read the tags twice. The expedition's gear had come upriver on the *Corvane*. Not gone out to meet it. Come up before it.

He closed the crate and sat down on it, and the questions he had spent twenty years refusing to carry settled into his chest like water taken over the gunwale.

* * *

He did not sleep. At some hour past midnight he climbed to the aft deck, where the river ran silver under a half-made moon, and found Isandro Montrose already there, leaning on the rail with her father's compass open in her palm.

She glanced at him without surprise. She had that way—of looking at people as though they were features on a chart she'd memorized years ago.

"Can't sleep either," she said. It wasn't a question.

"The hold," Teo said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "There's Halvard equipment in it. Expedition issue. Inventory tags say it shipped from Vell's Landing in March."

The compass snapped shut. In the dark he couldn't read her face, but he heard the change in her breathing, quick and shallow, the sound of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want finished.

"Before they went missing," she said.

"Months before." He came to stand beside her at the rail. Below them the wake hissed against the plates, patient as a confession. "Montrose. I've hauled contraband, I've hauled bodies, I've hauled things I wasn't paid enough to describe. But this—" He shook his head. "This is different. This is a rescue contract with rescue equipment loaded before anyone needed rescuing."

"Then what are we?"

Teo looked at her. She was watching the water, but her whole body had gone still, the stillness of someone standing at the edge of a bearing they've been avoiding.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But I know who does."

* * *

They waited two days, because caution was the only currency Teo had ever trusted, and then the river gave them the answer without being asked.

It came at the second narrows anchorage, where the gorge pinched the current to a white roar and the fleet tied off to wait for the light. Ferran summoned her officers to the wheelhouse—Teo among them, because he was senior stores—and left the door's transom open a hand's width, which was either carelessness or a test, and Teo had long ago stopped believing in Ferran's carelessness.

"We're not the first asset on this river," Odile Ferran said. Her voice was level, unhurried, the voice of a woman reading a freight schedule aloud. "The Halvard expedition found something in the escarpment reach. Something the Survey wants kept quiet and my principals want recovered. The strongbox comes back with us intact. Everything else—" a small pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table "—is secondary. If there are survivors, we render what aid the contract allows. We do not log them. We do not report them. Names go nowhere. Understood?"

One of the mates shifted. "And if they talk anyway?"

"They won't be in a position to." Ferran turned to the chart table. "We make the narrows at first light. Dismissed."

Teo walked back along the deck with his heart going like a paddlewheel. Behind him the wheelhouse lamp burned steady, and ahead of him Isandro Montrose stood at the bow rail in the blue dark, and he understood suddenly that whatever she had suspected since the first garbled transmission, she had hoped he would prove her wrong.

He didn't have to say anything. She read it off his face the way she read water.

* * *

She went to Ferran that same night, and Teo went with her, because he had decided—somewhere between the crate in the hold and the transom of the wheelhouse door—that the rule about not asking questions had a twin rule nobody told you: once you ask, you'd better stand somewhere solid.

Ferran received them in her cabin, which smelled of coal oil and expensive paper. She was sealing envelopes when they entered, and she finished the one in her hands before she looked up, giving them both the full weight of her patience.

"Montrose," she said. "Vask. Sit."

Isandro didn't sit. "The survivors," she said. "You're ordered to leave them unreported."

"I'm *instructed*," Ferran corrected mildly, "to prioritize cargo recovery. Language matters on a contract, Montrose. You of all people should appreciate that."

"People are dying up that river."

"People die on rivers constantly. It's why the pay is good." Ferran set down her pen and folded her hands, and Teo saw the shift come over her—the broker's warmth draining away until only the ledger remained. "But let's not pretend this is about the Halvard crew. Let's talk about what's actually on the table."

She opened a drawer. What she laid out was not a weapon but a folio of papers, squared neatly, and Teo watched Isandro go rigid as a sounding line pulled taut.

"Creditor filings," Ferran said. "Three houses in Vell's Landing, one in Corso Bay. Your late father's debts, transferred to you on probate—which you accepted, and then fled. Interest accruing for four years. Do you know the total? I do. I made inquiries before I ever offered you work." She slid the folio across the desk. "Eleven thousand marks. Give or take the season's accrual."

"You had no right—"

"I had every right. I'm a broker. Rights are just information with paperwork." Ferran leaned back. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it once. Your father's complete survey folio—the escarpment lines, the annotations, everything you inherited on paper alone. You give it to me, and I buy your debts outright tonight. Every mark. You walk off this boat at the gorge mouth a free woman, richer than you've been since you were disgraced, and no one on this river ever hears the name Montrose again unless you say it yourself."

The cabin was very quiet. Teo could hear the river working at the hull, and he could hear Isandro breathing, slow and deliberate, the breath of someone holding something shut.

"And the survivors?" Isandro said.

"Are outside the terms of our agreement."

"Then there's no agreement."

"There's always an agreement," Ferran said gently. "The only question is whether you sign it now, on decent terms, or later, on mine." She tapped the creditor folio once. "Think about what you're protecting, Montrose. A dead man's ink. Lines on paper he sealed himself. You've spent four years hiding from that inheritance—now you're prepared to drown for it? Sentiment is expensive. I'm offering to cover the cost."

Isandro picked up the folio. For one terrible moment Teo thought she might actually weigh it—and then he saw her face, and understood she was weighing nothing of the kind. She was memorizing it.

"My father closed those lines," she said quietly, "because opening them killed men. Whatever's in that strongbox, whatever your principals think they're owed—it crossed his line to get there. And his line says *no*." She set the folio down, squared exactly as Ferran had squared it, and pushed it back across the desk. "Keep my debts. I've carried heavier."

She turned and went out. Teo lingered half a heartbeat, long enough to see Ferran's expression—not anger, nothing so wasteful. Recalculation. The look of a woman adjusting a figure in a column she'd thought was closed.

"Vask," Ferran said, without looking up. "Your loyalty is noted."

"It's not loyalty," Teo said. "It's ballast. I know what happens to boats that throw it overboard."

He followed Isandro out into the cold, where the gorge waited ahead of them, black and roaring, and the fifth tide was coming whether any of them signed anything or not.
