## 6. The Escarpment Bearing

The second narrows announced themselves a full hour before they arrived—not by sight but by sound. The river's murmur deepened into a growl, and the growl into something like argument, as if the water were quarreling with the stone about who would pass. Isandro stood at the Corvane's wheelhouse window with her father's compass open in her palm, the needle trembling against its glass like a small trapped bird.

Ferran had posted herself at the chart table since dawn, which was itself unusual. Most mornings she took her coffee on the aft deck and let the river be someone else's problem until it wasn't. Today she watched Isandro the way a moneylender watches a debtor count coins.

"Two hours to the narrows," Isandro said, not turning.

"I know the schedule."

"Then you know we'll want the pilot's bench clear before then."

Ferran smiled without warmth. "I wanted to ask you something first, Montrose. Off the record." She tapped one lacquered nail on the chart—the fleet's chart, the one Isandro had been studying in stolen minutes for three days now. "Your father ran this line eleven times. Eleven successful runs through the escarpment gorge, back when everyone said it couldn't be done. What do you suppose he'd think of you running it for a salvage outfit?"

"He'd think I was desperate," Isandro said. "He'd be right."

"Mm." Ferran rolled the chart closed with two fingers, unhurried. "Desperation is honest, at least. Rarer than you'd think out here."

She left. Isandro waited until the wheelhouse door had thumped shut behind her, then unrolled the chart again and looked at what she'd been looking at for three days: the second narrows, drawn in Ferran's surveyor's hand with a confidence that should have been reassuring and instead made her stomach tighten.

The channel was marked running wide of the eastern bluff—a clean line, a generous margin, the kind of routing a cautious pilot would thank the cartographer for. It was also wrong. She knew it the way she knew her own hands. Her father's folio, the pages she'd studied by lamplight until the annotations lived behind her eyelids, described the second narrows in his cramped engineer's script: *East channel lies to the eye. Bluff undercuts below waterline; shelf extends forty meters at low stage. Run west of the midstream marker or don't run at all.*

Someone had drawn the safe-looking route precisely where the drowned shelf waited. And not carelessly—carefully, deliberately, with soundings faked to half-meter tolerances that no honest surveyor would bother to fake. This was not an error. This was a trap laid in ink.

* * *

She told Teo that evening, in the engine room where the noise swallowed voices, standing close enough that their shoulders touched beside the throbbing housing of the number-two pump.

"The chart's wrong at the second narrows."

Teo didn't look up from the pressure gauge he was pretending to read. "Wrong how?"

"Wrong on purpose. East channel's got a shelf under it. Any vessel drawing more than a meter and a half goes up on it at speed, and the Corvane draws two-point-one."

Now he looked at her. In the red wash of the engine room his face seemed older than his years, and very tired. "You're sure."

"My father surveyed this river when Ferran's company was still a rowboat and a ledger book. I've had his bearings memorized since I was fourteen." She heard how it sounded—how much it gave away—and made herself keep going. "If we run the east channel, we wreck. We lose the boats, the cargo, probably half the crew to the cold. And we're stranded forty kilometers above Vell's Landing with winter coming down the gorge behind us."

Teo wiped his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands. "So we tell Ferran."

"And she says 'thank you, navigator,' and quietly moves me off the boat at the next landing. Or worse—she asks how I knew, and I have to explain why a disgraced port clerk knows her father's escarpment soundings better than her paid surveyors." Isandro shook her head. "No. If I say anything, I say it once, at speed, when there's no time to argue. A pilot correcting course reads as instinct. A pilot filing objections reads as mutiny."

"That's not a plan, that's a gamble."

"It's navigation," she said. "It's the only kind I've ever been any good at."

* * *

They came to the second narrows just after noon on the fourth day, under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. The gorge walls rose sheer on either side, banded with lichen and old high-water stains, and the river between them had narrowed to a third of its width and doubled its temper. Standing waves marched downstream in ragged ranks. Spray hung in the air like breath.

Isandro had the helm. Ferran had insisted on that much—her navigator would earn her pay—but Ferran stood at her shoulder, and the mate stood at the engine telegraph, and six crewmen lined the rail with boat hooks as if hooks would matter if the hull opened. Teo was below, hands on the throttles, waiting for her signal.

"East channel," the mate read off the chart. "Wide water past the bluff. Survey says four meters at this stage."

"Survey's wrong," Isandro said.

Ferran turned her head slowly. "Say that again."

"There's a shelf under the east channel. Forty meters of it, extending off the bluff at depth. Your chart shows four meters of clearance; there's less than one." She kept her voice flat, professional, the voice of a woman reading numbers rather than accusing anyone of anything. "We run west of the midstream marker. There's a chute there—narrow, fast, but deep all the way through. My father called it the Escarpment Bearing."

For three seconds nobody moved except the river. Then Ferran said, softly, "And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong, and we find out together."

Something passed across Ferran's face—calculation, Isandro thought, rapid and cold, the same arithmetic she'd watched her make over cargo manifests and insurance premiums. The arithmetic of whether a wager was worth placing. Whether a navigator who could spot a sabotaged chart was worth more than a navigator who could be discarded.

"West channel," Ferran said at last. "Ring down one-third."

The telegraph clanged. Below, Teo answered without hesitation—he'd been listening, of course he'd been listening—and the Corvane's engines dropped to a sullen mutter. Isandro brought the bow around toward the midstream marker, a leaning wooden post scarred white by ice, and felt the current take hold of the hull like a hand closing.

The narrows did not forgive hesitation. The moment they committed, the river shoved them sideways, and Isandro worked the wheel in small firm corrections, reading the water ahead the way other people read sentences: the dark seams where deep water ran, the pale boil where rock came near the surface, the V of smooth green water above the chute that her father had taught her meant *here, here is the door.* The bluff slid past on their right, close enough that she could see individual stones in its face, and beneath them—though nothing showed—she could feel the drowned shelf waiting like a held breath.

"Hold your line," Ferran said, though Isandro hadn't wavered.

"Hold your tongue," Isandro said, and heard several crewmen along the rail go very still.

Then the chute took them.

It was ninety seconds of white noise and certainty. The Corvane plunged, climbed, plunged again; spray came over the foredeck in sheets; somewhere aft a crate broke loose and thundered against the bulwark. Isandro's world contracted to the compass card, the seam of dark water, and the next ten meters of river. Her father's voice lived in her hands now—*small corrections, never big ones; the river respects a light touch and punishes a fight*—and she gave it exactly that, a light touch, again and again, until suddenly the walls fell back, the water smoothed to a long gray glide, and they were through.

Nobody cheered. That was how she knew how close it had been. Crews cheer when they've been scared for show; when they've been scared for real, they go quiet and look at their hands.

* * *

Ferran found her an hour later on the foredeck, where Isandro was re-rolling her father's folio with the particular care she reserved for the few things she owned that mattered.

"You saw it," Ferran said. No preamble. The wind pulled at her coat collar and she ignored it. "Three days you've been aboard, and you saw what my surveyor hid in plain sight. Soundings faked to the decimeter. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means someone wanted this boat on that shelf."

"It means someone in my own fleet sold my route to whoever paid best, and I didn't see it, and you did." Ferran's eyes were narrow and bright. "Which raises a question I should have asked in Vell's Landing. Who are you, Isandro Montrose? Port clerks don't read water like that. Disgraced engineers don't carry dead men's folios with proprietary soundings in the margins." She stepped closer. "That's your father's survey. The original. The one that was supposed to have been surrendered to the river commission when the line closed."

Isandro met her gaze and did not flinch, though every instinct honed by seven years of hiding screamed at her to lower her eyes, mumble, become small. *Invisibility ends,* she thought. *It ended the moment you rang for the west channel.*

"My father drew those lines," she said. "They're mine. And whatever game your surveyor was playing, it nearly killed twenty people today. So before you ask me another question, maybe ask yourself who else has a copy of that chart—and whether they want the Corvane at the bottom of the second narrows badly enough to try again."

For a long moment Ferran said nothing. The river talked for both of them, sliding past the hull with its new, gentler voice.

"You could have let us hit," Ferran said finally. "Wrecked boat, dead witnesses, and you adrift in a skiff with everything you know. Why didn't you?"

Because I'm not you, Isandro almost said. Because twenty people. Because my father ran this river to open it, not to close it. But what she said was: "Ask me again at the gorge mouth."

Ferran's mouth curved—not quite a smile, more the acknowledgment of a debt entered into a ledger. She turned and walked aft, and Isandro stood alone at the bow with the folio against her chest, understanding that something irreversible had happened. She had spent her anonymity the way her father had spent his life: on a bearing through bad water. There was no taking it back, no returning to the quiet clerk's room above the chandlery, no being nobody anymore.

Ahead, the gorge walls leaned closer together, and far upriver, faint as a headache, she thought she heard the first groan of the calving ice beginning to shift.

Five tides left. Maybe fewer.

And somewhere above the fifth rapids, if the Halvard survivors still breathed, they were waiting for someone who knew the way.
