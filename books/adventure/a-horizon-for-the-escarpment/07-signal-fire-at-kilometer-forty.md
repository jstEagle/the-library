## 7. Signal Fire at Kilometer Forty

The side channel opened before dawn like a seam in the river's coat, narrow enough that the willows on either bank dragged their fingers across the skiff's gunwales. Isandro ran the engine low, a mutter rather than a roar, and steered by the pale suggestion of current rather than anything she could properly see. Behind them, somewhere south and out of sight, the Corvane's running lights would be crawling up the main channel with the rest of Ferran's fleet, believing their navigator still slept in the fo'c'sle.

Beside her, Teo held the compass open in both hands like something that might bolt.

"Kilometer forty," he said. "You're sure."

"The transmission said forty. My father's folio says there's a gravel bench above the flood line at forty-one. High ground, fresh water, sightline down the gorge." She kept her eyes on the water. "If you were starving and hiding from your own rescue party, that's where you'd be."

"And if they're not there?"

"Then we've stolen a boat for nothing and I'll write Ferran an apology."

Teo almost smiled. Almost. He'd been different since the night he'd shown her the fleet's true contract—not braver exactly, but settled, the way a man is settled once he knows which way the floor is going to tilt. He'd come to her bunk two hours ago with a stolen skiff key and no explanation beyond *now or never*, and she had not asked for one.

The channel bent west, away from the main gorge, and the mountains closed around them. Frost smoked off the water. Somewhere above the cloud line a glacier ticked and groaned, a sound Isandro had grown up hearing in her father's stories and had told herself for eleven years she'd forgotten.

She hadn't forgotten anything. That was the problem with her. She never forgot anything; she only filed it.

* * *

They found the signal fire's remains first—or what had been a signal fire, built to be seen and then smothered to be missed. A ring of stones on the gravel bench, blackened driftwood inside it, and laid across the top when the fire was killed: green boughs, deliberately placed. Someone had lit a beacon and then changed their mind about being found.

"Or someone else made them change it," Teo said quietly.

Isandro grounded the skiff and stepped onto the bench with her father's compass in her pocket and every muscle in her body listening. The camp was thirty meters up-slope, tucked against a rock overhang—two shelters of torn expedition tarpaulin, a cook fire burned down to ash, and the particular devastated tidiness of people who had stopped expecting to leave.

A man rose out of the nearest shelter. He moved like a man made of wire and stubbornness, one arm cradled against his chest, and he held a surveyor's ranging pole leveled at them like a spear, which was the most Halvard-expedition thing Isandro had ever seen. Behind him, in the second shelter, a woman's voice said something hoarse and urgent, and the man did not lower the pole.

"We're not Ferran's," Isandro said. She spread her hands, empty. "We came ahead of her fleet. Alone."

"Ferran." The word came out of him like a stone dropped down a well. The pole dipped an inch. "You know that name."

"I work for her. For now. Against my better judgment and increasingly against my interest." She took one step forward, then another, letting him look at her—mud-caked, underfed, unarmed. "I logged your last transmission. The garbled one, eleven days ago. I should have done something then. I'm doing it now."

The man's eyes went from her face to Teo to the skiff and back. Something in him performed arithmetic and arrived at a number he could live with. The pole came down and became, again, a tool.

"Halvard expedition," he said. "Soren Vask, second engineer." He jerked his chin toward the shelter. "Mira Oduya, our hydrographer. She took ice through the shoulder in the wreck. It's bad, but she's alive, and she has been keeping—" his mouth twisted, "—*records*."

*Records.* Isandro felt the word land in her chest like a struck bell.

* * *

Mira Oduya was propped against an expedition crate with her left arm bound in a sling improvised from a chart tube's strap, her color the gray of old ice, her eyes ferociously alive. Around her, arranged with a librarian's precision despite everything: the expedition's sealed logbook, a bundle of survey flimsies, a waterproof document case, and a small tin box that she guarded with her good hand even as she talked, because talking was clearly the thing keeping her alive.

"We ran the gorge on the twelfth," she said. "The survey said the channel was clear. The survey was wrong. There was a shoal where the chart showed nine meters of water—a new one, calved debris, but the falsified soundings didn't just miss it, they *erased* it. We hit it doing eight knots. Lost the barge, lost four crew, lost everything that wasn't lashed down." Her voice did not shake. It flattened instead, the way water flattens before a drop. "Vask broke his arm getting us ashore. And we found this in the barge's strongbox wreckage, washed up on the same beach as us. As if the river wanted us to have it."

She nodded at the tin box. Vask brought it over and set it in Isandro's hands.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: correspondence. Originals, signed, dated. Letters between the Halvard expedition's chief engineer—a man named Aldous Renk—and a brokerage firm whose letterhead Isandro knew the way you know a scar. Ferran Salvage & Recovery. Terms for payment. A schedule of falsified soundings, in Renk's own hand, with the corrections marked for deletion. And at the bottom of the last letter, a line that made the gorge go very quiet around her:

*The escarpment line stays closed. Closed lines don't get surveyed twice.*

"They paid him to fail," Teo said. "And to keep the whole upper river dark."

"They paid him to make sure nobody ever checked whether the old charts were right," Mira said. She looked at Isandro then, really looked, and her gaze snagged on the compass case visible at Isandro's pocket, on the worn brass corner of it. "That's a Ravensworth instrument."

The name fell into the space between them and rang there.

"My father's," Isandro said.

Vask sat down slowly on a crate, as if his legs had filed their resignation. "Ravensworth. The escarpment engineer. The one who closed the line." He laughed, short and disbelieving and not amused at all. "Eleven years everyone in the trade said Ravensworth's surveys failed. That his bearings were bad, that the gorge shifted and he couldn't admit it. That's why the line got sealed. That's the story *Renk* told at every port society dinner on the network."

"It wasn't true," Isandro said. It came out flat, and she was grateful for that, because underneath the flatness was a pressure she did not trust herself to measure.

"No," Mira said gently. "It wasn't. Look at the flimsies."

Isandro looked. Survey sheets, original field copies, in a handwriting she knew better than her own—the quick decisive numerals, the little hooked fives, the marginal notes in her father's cramped shorthand. His soundings of the escarpment channel, taken the season before he died. And beside them, in different ink, Renk's "corrected" versions, shallower by meters, useless by design. Her father had measured the truth. Renk had buried it. And the world had spent eleven years burying her father along with it.

"He tried to protest it," Mira said. "There's a letter in the case—his, unsent, found in Renk's files. Dated three weeks before he died on the escarpment road."

Isandro's hands were steady. She noted that distantly, the way you note weather. She unfolded the last sheet, and her father's voice came up off the page at her after eleven years of silence:

*I have been asked to certify soundings I did not take and cannot stand behind. I am asked further to close the escarpment line on the basis of these figures. I will do neither. Let the record show the line is sound. Let whoever comes after me measure it themselves.*

He had died on that road six weeks later. Rockfall, the report said. Bad luck, the report said. She had been twenty-three and drowning in his debts and she had believed the report because believing it was easier than believing the alternative—that the line he died protecting had been murdered into closure, and that the men who did it had gone on eating dinners and signing contracts while she hid in a river port under a false calm, ashamed of a legacy that had never deserved her shame.

The grief came anyway. It came up through the soles of her feet off the cold gravel, through her father's compass warm in her pocket, through the hooked fives on the page. Beside her, Teo said nothing, which was the correct thing, and put a hand briefly on her shoulder, which was also the correct thing.

When she could speak again, her voice was level and it belonged to someone she recognized—someone who had been waiting eleven years to be needed.

"They're coming up the main channel behind us. Six vessels. Their contract isn't rescue—it's recovery. This box, the logbook, and any witnesses, unreported." She looked at Vask, at Mira. "Ferran can't have any of it. Not the letters, not the testimony, not the two of you."

"So we run," Vask said.

"We run," Isandro agreed. "But running isn't enough. Evidence in a strongbox can sink. Witnesses can drown. Testimony has to be *everywhere at once* to survive Ferran." She stood, and the plan assembled itself in her the way her father's bearings used to assemble—clean, inevitable, hers. "The calving starts tonight. When the glacier lets go, every radio operator on the network will be listening to the emergency band, because they always do. And I am going to give them something worth hearing."

"You'd burn yourself with it," Teo said. "Your name, your debts, all of it. Open channel. You'd never hide again."

Isandro looked down at the unsent letter in her hands, at the last honest sentence her father ever wrote.

"That was always the price," she said. "I'm done paying it to the wrong people."

Above the gorge, deep in the white silence, the glacier gave its first long groan—and this time, it did not stop.
