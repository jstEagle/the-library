## 8. Fifth Tide

The glacier began to speak at dawn.

Isandro heard it first as a sound she couldn't name—a long, grinding exhalation from upriver, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Then the water changed. The Halvard's skiff, loaded past any sane displacement with two hollowed survivors and a strongbox lashed under oilcloth, lifted on a swell that had no wind behind it and dropped again with a slap that soaked them all to the waist.

"That's the calving," said Maren Halvard, the expedition's second officer, her voice a ruin of frostbite and three weeks of starvation. She was wrapped in every layer they'd stripped from their own backs, her hands too damaged to hold the tiller, so Isandro held it. "The face is going. We heard it start two days before the radio died."

"Then we run ahead of it," Isandro said. "Kilometer forty to the gorge mouth is eleven miles of current doing most of our work for us. We let it."

Teo crouched amidships, one hand on the strongbox, the other on the shoulder of the second survivor—Vasquez, the expedition's cook, who hadn't spoken since they'd carried him down from the camp and whose eyes tracked the canyon walls as if expecting them to fall. Behind them all, above the rimrock, the glacier hung in the notch between peaks like a held breath.

The river took them. It was brown with rock flour and moving faster than anything Isandro had steered since her disgrace, faster than the Corvane's trial runs, faster than the survey launches of her girlhood when her father used to stand behind her saying *feel the water before you fight it*. She felt it now. The gorge narrowed, widened, split around mid-channel reefs that her father's folio had named in his small, ruthless handwriting: *the Teeth. The Widow's Step. Do not cut inside on the bend below the step; the eddy will take your stern.*

She'd spent twenty years pretending those annotations meant nothing to her. Now they were the only thing keeping five people alive.

* * *

They made the Widow's Step by midmorning, and that was where the radio crackled.

Teo had rigged the expedition's salvaged set to the skiff's battery, jury-rigged with wire stripped from the gunwale, and he'd been sweeping channels out of habit more than hope. Now he looked up sharply. "Fleet traffic. Ferran's calling the Corvane." He listened, jaw tightening. "She's through the lower narrows. All four vessels. She's asking for a position report on 'the cargo.'"

"The cargo," Maren repeated flatly. "That's us."

"That's you," Teo agreed. He glanced at Isandro. "And the box."

Isandro kept her eyes on the water. The bend was coming—the long left-hand sweep below the step where the current piled against the outside wall and the inside went slack and treacherous. She counted her father's markers in her head: the pale seam of quartz in the south bluff, the leaning spruce, the submerged shelf that showed as a faint green shadow even in silt-heavy water. *Cut wide. Trust the seam, not your eyes.*

"Ferran can't be through the narrows already," she said. "Not on those charts. Unless—" And then she understood, and the understanding sat in her stomach like swallowed stone. Unless someone had corrected them. Unless Ferran had found her penciled amendments aboard the Corvane and understood exactly what they meant: that Isandro Montrose knew this river the way a daughter knows a mother's voice.

"She'll close the gorge mouth," Maren said. "It's the only place she can. One exit, and we have to come out of it."

The skiff swung through the bend, stern skating the edge of the eddy exactly as the folio promised, and came out straight and fast into the long straightaway. Ahead, the canyon walls fell away by degrees toward the gorge mouth, and beyond it—visible now as a gray shimmer, a haze of spray hanging in the cold air—the lower falls thundered over the escarpment lip. Eleven years ago her father had closed this line with a signature and a locked gate. Today it was the only door in the world, and Odile Ferran would be standing in front of it.

* * *

They ran the last miles in a silence broken only by the river and, beneath it, growing louder by the hour, the glacier. The sound had become language. Short cracks like rifle shots. Long groans that started below hearing and climbed until the rock itself seemed to shiver. Once, a report so deep that Vasquez finally spoke, one word: "*Dios.*"

At the mouth of the gorge the current slowed, pooled, spread wide—and there was the fleet.

Four vessels rafted across the outflow in a picket line, anchors down, engines idling against the current. The Corvane led, her crane arm swung out over the water like an accusation. On her foredeck, small with distance but unmistakable in her charcoal coat, stood Odile Ferran, a megaphone raised to her mouth.

Isandro brought the skiff alongside the cliff-shadowed bank, into the slack water under the north wall, and killed the engine. The current here was gentle, holding them in an eddy like a cupped hand. Across the open water, Ferran's voice rolled, amplified and patient.

"*Skiff at the north bank. This is Odile Ferran, Corvane Salvage Company, operating under contract 4471. You are carrying company property. Come alongside peacefully and no one needs to get wet.*"

Maren laughed—a dry, cracked sound with nothing in it. "Company property. That's what we are."

"You're witnesses," Isandro said. "You're the thing she can't buy back." She looked at the strongbox under its oilcloth, at the documents inside it that Vasquez had guarded with a starving man's ferocity: the falsified gorge survey, the letters bearing Ferran's company seal, the engineer's confession signed in a shaking hand three days before the expedition's barge met its arranged wreck. "And you're testimony."

"They'll take it off us," Teo said quietly. "At Vell's Landing, with her money and her lawyers? Testimony drowns easier than people do."

Isandro looked at the radio.

It was a cheap set, expedition-grade, its range modest—but range wasn't the point. Open channel was the point. Every port on the network kept a watch on the common bands: Vell's Landing, Graywater, the fish camps, the timber stations, the little river towns where radios were lit like hearths all day because news was the only cargo that moved for free. Her father had taught her that too, in another life: *a broadcast can't be un-said, Isandro. That's its whole power and its whole danger.*

She had spent six years being nobody. Six years of filing other people's logs under other people's names, of paying down debts that weren't hers in coin that never touched her own account, of keeping her father's folio locked in a drawer because owning it openly would mean admitting she existed. Secrecy had been her house. She had built it board by board, and it had kept the rain off.

But houses could also be coffins.

"Teo," she said. "How much battery?"

"Enough. Twenty minutes of transmit if I don't push it."

"Then we spend it all."

* * *

They put Maren at the microphone because it had to be her voice—Halvard expedition, second officer, survivor of record—and because Isandro's name would come soon enough and she wanted the truth in the air first, naked and undeniable, before anyone could attach a price to it.

Teo keyed the transmit. "All stations, all stations, this is Maren Halvard, Halvard Expedition, second officer. We are alive. Four survivors of the Halvard expedition are alive at the gorge mouth, and we are broadcasting testimony regarding the loss of our barge. All stations record. All ports record."

Across the water, the Corvane's deck erupted into motion. Ferran lowered the megaphone. Even at two hundred yards Isandro could see the stillness come over her—the particular motionlessness of a woman recalculating everything.

Maren talked. Her ruined voice ground through the names: the barge *Halvard*, lost at kilometer sixty-two not to ice but to a hull opened by charges placed along the waterline; the engineer Dorn, bribed to certify the upper gorge navigable for salvage traffic and then silenced; the sealed strongbox recovered from the wreck by survivors who should not have survived; the company seal on the letters; the dates; the amounts. She read the engineer's confession aloud, word for word, while Vasquez held the pages steady and the glacier groaned overhead like a witness taking an oath.

Isandro watched the far shore. At the fish camp opposite the mouth, a figure stepped out of a hut and stood listening. Further down, a timber skiff stopped mid-river, its occupant letting it drift. The network was waking. Word was already running downriver faster than any vessel—toward Graywater, toward Vell's Landing, toward harbormasters and magistrates and the wives of men who owned shipping shares, all of them hearing a dead expedition speak.

Then Ferran's launch shoved off from the Corvane.

"Time to go," Teo said.

"Not yet." Isandro took the microphone from Maren's shaking hands. For a moment she held it without speaking, feeling the weight of it, feeling six years of silence press against the back of her teeth.

"This is Isandro Montrose," she said. "Daughter of Edric Montrose, surveyor of the escarpment line. My father closed this passage because he found what Ferran's company wanted buried—bad rock, false soundings, a route that would kill crews so long as profits depended on pretending otherwise. They hounded him for it. They're still trying to bury it. The documents my father sealed are real, and they are about to be public, and Odile Ferran knows it." She paused, and heard her own voice steadying, becoming something she recognized from a mirror twenty years old. "I am opening the escarpment line. Not for her fleet. For everyone. All stations, log this position and this name."

She keyed off. The silence afterward felt enormous, like the pause after a calving.

Ferran's launch hung in the middle of the outflow, oars backing water, her pilot visibly unwilling to close the distance with the whole listening network watching. Through the megaphone, one last time, Ferran's voice came across the water—no longer patient, no longer polite, stripped down to its load-bearing frame:

"*You've just signed away every debt you ever owed me, Montrose. And every protection.*"

"I know," Isandro said, to herself, to the water, to the microphone she'd already released. "That was the payment."

* * *

The glacier answered before anyone else could.

The sound came down the gorge like a train falling off the sky—a crack, then a roar, then a white wall of displaced air that flattened the river into ripples and sent every bird on the cliffs up at once. High in the notch, a section of the ice face the size of a cathedral leaned, hesitated, and let go. The splash reached them thirty seconds later as a swell that lifted the skiff and slammed it down, and after it came the flood—not water, at first, but the river's whole body shuddering, rising, accelerating, the pool at the gorge mouth beginning to boil as the calving wave funneled down the narrow water.

"Downriver!" Teo was shouting. "Now, now, NOW—"

Isandro's hand found the starter cord. Around them the fleet was scattering—anchors tearing free, the Corvane's horn bellowing, Ferran's launch turning hard for the bank. The river that had been holding them gently in its eddy was gone; it was a single muscle flexing now, and it pointed one way only, down the old escarpment line, through the gate her father had closed, toward the falls and the low country and whatever waited at Vell's Landing.

Maren gripped the gunwale. Vasquez closed his eyes. Teo braced the strongbox with his whole body.

Isandro opened the throttle and steered into the current her father had mapped and the mountain had unleashed, and behind them the gorge filled, from wall to wall, with the sound of everything hidden coming loose at once.
