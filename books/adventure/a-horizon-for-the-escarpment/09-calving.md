## 9. Calving

The glacier spoke first.

It was not a sound Isandro heard so much as one she felt—a long, grinding exhalation from the head of the gorge, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep and finding the bed too small. The ice above Kilometer Forty was failing. She had known it would; the calving season did not negotiate, and the six tides the harbormaster had promised were nearly spent. But knowing a thing and hearing it come down the river toward you were different animals entirely, and the second one had teeth.

"Corvane's engines are hot," Teo said at her shoulder, his voice pitched low the way men speak in churches and on rivers about to kill them. "Ferran's other two boats are holding back at the mouth of the side channel. They're waiting."

"They're waiting for us to find the way through," Isandro said, "and then they'll follow our wake and take everything we're carrying." She looked down at her father's folio, open on the chart table, its pages soft as cloth from forty years of hands. His annotations ran up the margins in pencil gone silver with age—bearings, soundings, corrections, and in one corner, pressed hard enough to dent three sheets beneath it, a single line in his blunt engineer's hand: *The river is honest. The charts are not.*

She had spent eleven years trying to be nobody. Nobody's daughter, nobody's debtor, nobody whose name meant anything past the door of the radio shack where she took her watch. And now here she stood at the wheelhouse of a salvage tug with two half-starved survivors wrapped in blankets below, a strongbox full of evidence lashed to the deck, and a fleet behind her that wanted all of it sunk to the bottom of the gorge with her name attached.

"Teo," she said. "Get below. Strap yourself in. Tell the survivors the same."

"And you?"

"I'm going to drive."

* * *

The Corvane was never built for what Isandro asked of her. She was a working tug, beamy and stubborn, with a draft like a barge and an engine room that smelled of diesel and old sweat. The true escarpment line—the passage her father had surveyed and sealed—was a knife-cut through the gorge: narrow, fast, threaded with submerged rock that the false charts showed as open water, and open water that the false charts showed as rock. Ferran's company had bought their falsified survey from a bribed engineer, and every vessel in the salvage fleet carried those lies laminated and official.

Isandro carried something else.

She stood at the wheel with the compass her father had left her open on the binnacle shelf beside the tug's own instruments, and she read the river the way he had taught her to read it before she was old enough to understand what she was learning—not by the charts, but by the water itself. A standing wave meant rock beneath. A smooth tongue of current meant depth. A seam of green water sliding against brown meant two bodies of flow arguing over territory, and the argument always happened over something solid.

"Third bearing," she said aloud, because saying it made it real, because her father's voice lived somewhere inside the numbers. "Mark: three-one-seven true. Hold until the cliff face shows its scar."

The left wall of the gorge rose sheer and black, seamed with white scars where ice had ground it for ten thousand years. Her father's annotation marked a scar shaped like a broken arrow—the *flecha rota*, he'd called it, and she'd laughed at the name when she was nine years old. It wasn't funny now. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, that pale wound in the stone, because it meant she was exactly where he had been, following exactly the line he had died protecting, and the river ahead of her was honest after all.

The Corvane came around the mark and the gorge narrowed to less than two hundred meters, and the current took hold of the tug like a hand closing.

* * *

Behind them, the first of Ferran's vessels committed to the false channel.

Isandro heard it before anyone reported it—a sound like a door slamming underwater, transmitted through the hull, through the water, through the stone itself. Then the radio crackled and a voice she recognized from the fleet's own channels was shouting coordinates that meant nothing, bearings off the lying charts, panic dressed in procedure. The *Meridian Star*, Ferran's fastest boat, had driven into the submerged sill at the narrows where the falsified survey showed twelve feet of clear water and there were, in truth, four.

"Hold your station," Isandro said into the transmitter, and her voice surprised her—it was steady, almost gentle. "You're on the stern of the sill. Back off slow, bow upstream, and you'll float free. If you put weight forward you'll tear your bottom out."

A pause. Static. Then, raggedly: "Who is this?"

"Isandro Montrose. I'm running the Corvane ahead of you. Follow my marks or don't follow at all—but do not trust your charts. They were sold to you by people who wanted this gorge to eat you."

She could hear the crew of the Meridian Star deciding whether to believe her. She understood the arithmetic—they had no reason to trust a stranger's voice over a laminated government survey. But the river was making the argument for her. The Star groaned, scraped, and then, blessedly, backed water, her bow swinging upstream, and the voice on the radio said, "Marks received. Following your marks, Corvane."

Isandro set the microphone down and found her hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the strange, vertiginous discovery that she had just done the thing her father had done his whole life—stood between a river and the people on it, and told the truth.

* * *

The glacier chose that moment to let go.

It began as a crack, high above and far behind—a rifle shot echoing down the gorge—and then became a roar that swallowed every other sound in the world. In her mirrors Isandro saw the face of the ice let loose, a wall of it, blue-white and city-sized, folding outward and downward into the upper river, and the displaced water came downstream as a brown wall taller than the Corvane's mast.

There was no outrunning it. There was only threading it—finding the deep channel, the one her father had sounded and re-sounded and written in the margins in his stubborn pencil, the one line through the gorge where the flood would pass over rather than through.

"Full ahead," she said. "Both engines. Now."

The Corvane surged. The gorge walls blurred. Isandro flew the tug down her father's bearings—three-one-seven to the flecha rota, zero-four-zero to the twin boulders, two-six-eight through the throat where the water compressed and screamed—and behind her the flood came on, and around her the river held its shape exactly as he had drawn it, because he had drawn it honestly, because he had never once lied to the water, and the water remembered.

The wave caught them at the gorge mouth.

It lifted the Corvane like a leaf, drove her sideways toward the cliff, and Isandro countered with everything the rudder had, wheel hard over, engines opposed, wrestling four hundred tons of tug against a mountain of moving water. Spray came over the wheelhouse in a white cataract. Something on deck tore loose and went overboard—the skiff, she thought, let it go, let all of it go—and then the wave passed beneath them, spent, and the Corvane settled into the wide brown water of the lower river as gently as a boat coming into harbor.

Behind them, the gorge filled with thunder and mist. When the spray cleared, the passage they had come through was gone—sealed under new ice, new rubble, a new geography that no chart would ever describe again except hers.

Teo appeared in the wheelhouse doorway, soaked, grinning, alive. Below, faintly, one of the Halvard survivors was laughing—a cracked, disbelieving sound that went on longer than laughter should.

"You drove his line," Teo said. "All the way through."

"I drove my line," Isandro said, and heard, for the first time, that she meant it.

* * *

Vell's Landing came up out of the evening haze with its lamps lit and its whole population apparently standing on the docks, because of course they were—she had broadcast the testimony on open channels, and every radio in town had been listening, and now here they all were, watching the Corvane come downriver flying a blanket from her signal halyard because someone aboard had decided survivors deserved a flag.

Two constables' boats waited at the fuel dock. Odile Ferran stood on the lead boat with her hands cuffed in front of her, which was wrong, Isandro thought—Ferran should have been given the dignity of arriving under her own power, if only so everyone could see exactly how she carried herself. She carried herself well. That was the obscenity of it. Even taken, even finished, she looked like a woman who had merely suffered a scheduling difficulty.

Her eyes found Isandro across the water as the Corvane slid alongside.

"You could have taken the money," Ferran called. "Your debts are still sitting in a ledger somewhere, Montrose. Custody doesn't discharge them."

"No," Isandro agreed. "But I will."

They brought Ferran up the gangway past the survivors, past the strongbox, past Teo, who watched her go with an expression Isandro couldn't read and didn't try to. A constable asked Isandro for her statement, and her name, and she gave both clearly, spelling the surname twice, watching it go into the record in ink.

Then the harbormaster pushed through the crowd—a heavy woman with a beard of gray stubble and a voice like a foghorn—and pressed a piece of paper into Isandro's hands. An official notice, freshly typed, still warm from the office machine.

*Notice of Claim: Salvage award pending adjudication. Escarpment passage — survey documentation requested from holder of record. Holder of record: I. MONTROSE.*

The crowd had gone quiet around her. Someone—Teo, probably—had told them whose line the Corvane had come through. Everyone in Vell's Landing knew the story of old Montrose and his sealed escarpment, the way river towns know all their dead.

"The gorge is closed," the harbormaster said, not unkindly. "But gorges reopen. Ice moves. And somebody's going to have to survey the new channel when it does." She nodded at the paper in Isandro's hands. "Holder of record. That's you now. On paper and everywhere else."

Isandro looked down at the notice, at her own name sitting there in plain type, belonging to her, claimed by her, in front of everyone. Above the dock lights the first stars were coming out, and behind them, miles upriver, the glacier groaned once more and fell silent, having said everything it came to say.

She folded the paper carefully and put it inside her coat, over her heart, next to nothing, because for the first time in eleven years there was nothing there she needed to hide.

"Get me a chart table," she said to the harbormaster. "I'll need to start tonight."
