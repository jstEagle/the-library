## 10. Open Passage

The certificate arrived on a morning so clear the escarpment itself seemed to lean in to read it.

Isandro Montrose stood at the counter of the Vell's Landing harbormaster's office, the document unrolled before her, its vellum stiff with official wax and three colors of ribbon. The letterhead of the Riverways Certification Board ran across the top in engraved capitals. Below it, in language that had taken eleven months and four hearings to settle, was the sentence she had read perhaps two hundred times since the draft first circulated:

*The Escarpment Line, Kilometer Zero through Kilometer Sixty-One, is hereby certified as an open public passage for commercial and civilian traffic, subject to seasonal calving closures, under the engineering authority of I. Montrose, P.E.*

Under the engineering authority of I. Montrose.

There was a space at the bottom for her signature. It had been waiting there for six weeks, because the Board's clerk had insisted the signing be done in person, in the town where the line began, with witnesses — and Isandro had kept finding reasons to delay. The ink had to be archival grade; she'd ordered it special. The pen had to be her father's; she'd spent a fortnight hunting it through the boxes of his effects she had never, until this winter, been able to open. And then, when everything was ready, she had simply stood at this same counter one gray afternoon with the pen uncapped and found that her hand would not move.

Signing things had ruined her once. She knew precisely how it worked: how a name flowed out of a pen looking like confidence, like competence, like a promise, and how the promise could be hollow all the way down. Seven years ago she had signed survey certifications for the Meridian Southern line with the same easy flourish, and every one of those signatures had been a small beautiful lie — numbers she hadn't checked, tolerances she'd waved through, a bridge abutment on the Kessel cut that she had approved because the schedule demanded it and the men above her smiled and the work was good enough, probably, almost certainly. The inquiry had used the word *negligent*. The newspapers had preferred *disgraced*. Her family had not needed a word at all; they had simply stopped writing.

So she understood signatures the way sailors understand weather that has already sunk them.

"You're going to wear a groove in my floor," said Harbormaster Ovid Senn from behind his desk. He was an old man built like a mooring bollard, with a voice like rope over a gunwale, and he had watched her stand there for the better part of ten minutes without offering to hurry her. That was his gift, and everyone in Vell's Landing knew it: he let people take the time they actually needed instead of the time they pretended to.

"I'm reading it," Isandro said.

"You've read it."

"I'm checking it."

"Against what? You wrote half the language yourself." He came around the desk, joints cracking like river ice, and set two cups of chicory coffee down beside the certificate. "My father helped close this line, you know. Forty years ago. He told me it was because the surveys were bad." Senn looked out the window toward the water, where the morning light lay flat and silver on the harbor. "He believed that his whole life. It bothered him. Bad surveys happen; it's no shame. But he never could make the closure figures add up, and it sat wrong with him until the day he died." He turned back to her. "It wasn't bad surveys. It was a bought engineer and a company that wanted the gorge empty. Your father died protecting the truth of it, and you nearly did the same, and now it's going to be open, and honest, and stamped. I think he'd want you to stop standing at my counter and sign the damn thing."

Isandro picked up the pen.

Her father's pen — dark walnut, brass fittings worn bright by forty years of his hand. She had found it wrapped in oilcloth inside the folio box, along with something else she had not expected: a letter, unsent, addressed to her, dated three days before he went up the escarpment for the last time. She had read it perhaps fifty times since. Most of it was engineering — bearings, flow rates, the load tolerances of the old gorge pilings — instructions disguised as a letter, because that was how her father loved: by handing you tools. But the last line was not instruction. It read: *A line isn't yours because your name is on it. It's yours when you've walked every meter of it and can answer for every meter of it.*

She had walked every meter. In the autumn, with Teo hauling the survey chain and the two Halvard survivors — Wren Ashby and the old radio man, Duclos — riding shotgun in the skiff, she had run the full sixty-one kilometers of the reopened route, sounding every channel, re-shooting every bearing, correcting her father's folio where the river itself had changed and confirming it everywhere it hadn't. The river had moved less than anyone expected. Her father's lines held. Of course they held. He had answered for every meter.

She signed.

The flourish was smaller than the one she'd used at Meridian Southern. Steadier, too. The ink went down clean and archival, and when it dried she saw that it looked exactly like what it was: not a promise made in hope, but a promise made in knowledge.

Senn blotted it, countersigned, and rang the little brass bell on his desk that he otherwise reserved for ship arrivals. Somewhere outside, someone whooped.

* * *

The salvage award came through in the spring, and Isandro paid her debts the way she paid all her debts now — in full, in order, oldest first, with receipts.

It took most of a morning at the credit office in Corvane's home port, sitting across a scarred table from a collections clerk named Brack who had spent seven years sending her letters with red borders. He laid the ledger out between them with the ceremony of a man unveiling a monument, and read the totals aloud in a voice pitched for humiliation. Isandro listened to every figure. When he finished, she slid the bank draft across the table.

Brack stared at it, then at her. "You're clearing all of it?"

"All of it. Principal, interest, penalties. The schedule's itemized in the second envelope."

He opened the second envelope, checked, and something in his professional posture faltered. People did not do this. People negotiated, defaulted, vanished upriver. They did not walk in off the morning boat and extinguish seven years of debt in a single instrument. "The Montrose name," he said slowly, "is going to mean something different in these books after today."

"It already does," Isandro said. "This just makes the paper match."

Outside, Teo was waiting on the office steps, eating roasted chestnuts out of a paper cone and radiating the particular patience of a man who has agreed to be somewhere and will be there until the being-there is done. He had stayed on after the gorge. Not with Ferran's fleet — Ferran's fleet was gone, its vessels impounded, its contracts voided, its broker awaiting trial on charges that now included conspiracy, falsification of survey documents, and the abandonment of the Halvard crew — but with Isandro, in the loose, argumentative partnership they had fallen into somewhere around kilometer thirty of the escarpment run. He called himself her deckhand. He was, in fact, becoming her chief of operations, mostly by virtue of being the only person alive who could tell when she was about to make a decision out of guilt rather than judgment, and say so, rudely, in time.

"Paid?" he asked.

"Paid."

"Every cent?"

"Every cent, plus the interest they tacked on during the inquiry years. Which I contested and lost, and which I paid anyway, because it was accurate."

Teo nodded slowly, chewing. "You know what I did with my share of the award?"

"Bought your mother a house with a roof that doesn't sing in the wind."

"Bought my mother a house," he confirmed, "with a roof that doesn't sing." He offered her the chestnut cone. "We're a couple of sentimental idiots, you know that? Everyone else in the fleet took their money and bought boats and whiskey."

"Everyone else in the fleet didn't spend five tides listening to a glacier decide whether to kill them."

"Fair." He tipped the cone toward the harbor. "Come on. The frame shop closes at four, and you've rescheduled twice."

* * *

The frame shop smelled of cut glass and pine sizing. Isandro had brought two items, and the framer — a narrow, meticulous woman named Gaudet — laid them side by side on her workbench and studied them over her spectacles with the gravity of a physician comparing X-rays.

The first was her father's folio: the sealed survey of the escarpment line, its canvas cover cracked with age, its spine broken at the pages he had annotated in the last months of his life. Those annotations were visible even closed — the edges of the pages furred with his cramped marginalia, the corrections and flow-notes and the single line, repeated three times in three different hands of urgency, that had started everything: *the narrows are sound — the failure report is false.* The folio bore the scorch mark from the campfire at kilometer forty, and the water stain from the fifth tide, and the thumb-wear of a dead man's final season. Gaudet pronounced it frameable if — only if — it was mounted unpressed, floating on acid-free board, nothing touching the surface.

The second item was Isandro's own new survey of the reopened line: sixty-one kilometers of soundings, bearings, piling inspections, and calving-season hydrology, bound in blue buckram, crisp as frost. On the title page, beneath the certification seal, sat her signature — the small, steady one.

"They'll hang together?" Gaudet asked. "Father and daughter?"

"Together," Isandro said. "His on the left. Mine on the right. Same wall, same height."

Gaudet smiled faintly. "And where is this wall?"

"The pilot house of the *Corvane*." Isandro said it evenly, though the words still felt new in her mouth. "I'm buying her. Refitting her as a survey and pilotage vessel. First contract's already signed — the Riverways Board wants quarterly inspections of the escarpment route, and they want them done by the engineer of record." She paused. "By me."

Teo, who had been examining a display of gilt corners with elaborate disinterest, turned around. "You signed a contract? Voluntarily? With your own name?"

"Don't make it strange."

"It's already strange. You're hanging your father's ghost in the wheelhouse and steering ships past it for a living." But he was grinning, and the grin was the whole of what he meant: *look at you. Look what you did with it.*

That evening, back in Vell's Landing, they mounted the frames themselves in the *Corvane*'s pilot house, because Isandro trusted no one else's hands near the folio. The old tug rode gently at her moorings, freshly painted, her engines torn down and rebuilt, her name retained — Teo had argued for keeping it, on the grounds that a vessel should carry her history the way a river carries its silt: visibly, and without apology. Through the wheelhouse windows the harbor lights came on one by one across the black water, and beyond them, invisible in the dark but present in every line of the charts racked beside the helm, the escarpment waited, open now, lit by beacons at every third kilometer, carrying its first honest traffic in forty years.

Isandro stepped back from the wall. Father's folio on the left, water-stained and fire-marked and true. Her survey on the right, blue and new and true. Between them, mounted on a small brass plate, the compass — her father's compass, the one she had lain awake holding in the first chapter of all this, the needle swinging and settling, swinging and settling, until it found its bearing and held.

It pointed, as it always had, upriver.

Teo appeared at her shoulder with two cups of chicory, having apparently decided the harbormaster's brand was now a tradition. "First inspection run's in three weeks," he said. "Calving season opens early this year, they're saying. Big melt."

"Then we'll be the first through."

"We're always the first through. That's the whole job." He clinked his cup against hers. "To the line."

"To the line," Isandro said — and then, because the words had been waiting in her for months, quietly, the way her father would have said them: "and to answering for every meter of it."

Below them the tide turned, and the river ran open, all the way to the horizon.
