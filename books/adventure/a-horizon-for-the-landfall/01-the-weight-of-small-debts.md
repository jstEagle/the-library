## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The storm came off the salt flats at four in the afternoon the way they always did — first as a bruise on the horizon, then as a sound, a long low grinding like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and only last as rain.

Elodie Zhou was already moving.

She'd been parked on the levee road for two hours with her engine idling and her instruments humming, watching the pressure trace its slow dive across the barograph like a held breath finally let out. The flats south of Corren's Landing were the best storm theater in the delta — flat as a griddle for forty kilometers in every direction, nothing to interrupt the wind but drainage ditches and the skeletons of old shrimp boats — and the charter clients loved it. They paid by the minute for proximity, and Elodie had built a modest reputation on knowing exactly how close *close* was without becoming a headline.

"Talk to me," she murmured to the anemometer, though it couldn't hear her yet. The wind was still ten minutes out, maybe twelve. She had time to do the thing she always did before a run: check the tie-downs twice, wipe the salt haze off the camera housing, and look at the compass.

The compass sat in its bracket on the dash, brass-cased, older than she was. Her brother's. Jun's compass, salvaged from his effects along with a duffel of clothes that didn't fit her and a ledger of debts that fit her all too well. It pointed north the way it was supposed to, steady and dull, and every morning she checked it anyway, the way you'd check on a sleeping animal you didn't entirely trust.

The radio crackled. Not the weather band — the other one, the one she kept turned down low because listening to it too loudly felt like inviting something in.

"—Zhou. Zhou, pick up. We know you're running today."

Elodie's jaw tightened. She reached over and turned the volume down another notch, which was a childish thing to do to a radio, and keyed the weather band instead.

"*Corren's Landing advisory, four-oh-two p.m.* Cell forming over the southern flats, tracking northeast at thirty knots. Hail possible. All small craft clear the—"

"Yeah, yeah," she said, and killed it too.

The client today was a weather researcher named Okafor, a nervous academic type who'd paid double for a front-row seat to a mesocyclone and triple to have the footage be exclusively his. He was strapped into the passenger seat now, white-knuckled, wearing a rain shell two sizes too big and an expression like a man attending his own funeral.

"Is it supposed to be green?" he asked, nodding at the wall cloud stacking up ahead of them. It did have a greenish cast, that particular sickly teal that meant hail and meant business.

"It's supposed to be whatever it wants," Elodie said. "We're just audience."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be." She dropped the truck into gear and pulled onto the flats road, tires humming on packed salt crust. "Rule one: I drive, you film, nobody argues with the sky. Rule two: when I say we're leaving, we're already late."

Okafor swallowed audibly. Good. Nervous clients followed instructions; confident ones died stupidly.

She took the road at speed, the flats unrolling gray-white on either side, and behind her the storm stood up. That was the only word for it — the cell went from lying along the horizon to standing, a rotating column of slate and green-black climbing until it owned the sky from edge to edge. The light changed. It always changed first, going that strange bruised yellow that made everything look like an old photograph, and Elodie felt the familiar tightening in her chest that wasn't fear exactly. It was closer to relief. Out here there was only the storm and the driving and the numbers on her instruments, and none of those things knew her name or what she owed.

The radio crackled again. The other band. She ignored it.

It crackled again. And again. Patient. Rhythmic. Like knocking.

"You going to get that?" Okafor asked.

"No."

*Three weeks,* she thought. Three weeks since the Vantara Syndicate's collectors had started calling on that frequency — not the public bands, never the public bands, because Vantara understood that embarrassment was leverage and leverage worked best in private. Three weeks since the first message, delivered in a woman's voice so calm it was almost kind: *"Miss Zhou. Regarding the outstanding balance of your brother's estate. We should talk."*

Jun had been dead eleven months. Drowned, officially — his salvage skiff found swamped in the lower channel, no body, just the boat and his kit bag and, wedged under the console where he'd clearly hidden it, the ledger. Pages of numbers in his cramped handwriting. What he'd borrowed. From whom. And the interest, compounding monthly, written in a different hand, neat and patient as arithmetic performed by something that never slept.

Two hundred and forty thousand. It had been eighty thousand when he borrowed it. She'd checked the math a dozen times hoping she'd misunderstood how interest worked. She hadn't.

The collectors had come to her door twice. The second time there had been three of them, and one had picked up a photograph of her and Jun from the shelf — two kids on a dock, gap-toothed, sunburned — and looked at it for a long moment before setting it face-down.

That was the language Vantara spoke. Not threats. *Arrangements.*

So Elodie ran storms. She ran them fast and she ran them well, because motion was the only solvent she'd found for dread, and because every contract bought another month, and every month the number grew anyway, patient as ice.

"Left here?" Okafor asked, camera up, filming the wall cloud through the windshield.

"Right. We want the southeast flank — precipitation core stays west of us, we get the rotation without the hail." She swung the wheel and the truck fishtailed slightly on wet salt, caught, held. In the mirror the storm's base was lowering, organizing, a dark shelf crawling toward them with rain curtains dragging beneath it like hemmed skirts.

The radio knocked again. Then, for the first time in three weeks, it spoke without waiting to be answered.

"Miss Zhou. We know you're working. We'll keep this brief."

The voice was the calm woman's. Cass, the others called her — she'd introduced herself once, at the door, with a smile that never reached anything above the cheekbones. *Cass, Vantara Recovery Services.* As if the debt were a lost dog.

"The seasonal window is closing," Cass said. "Glacier calving starts in six weeks, and after that the upriver routes shut down until next year. Which means certain assets currently stranded upriver become permanently inaccessible. The Syndicate dislikes permanent losses. So do you, I think, given your situation."

Elodie's hands were steady on the wheel. She made them steady. Lightning walked down the storm's flank, a stutter of white, and thunder arrived four seconds later, rolling across the flats like furniture being moved upstairs in a giant's house.

"There's a contract," Cass continued. "Salvage and recovery. The Meridian expedition, silent three weeks now. Terms are generous. Terms would resolve certain balances." A pause, precisely measured. "Or don't take it. But understand that patience has a season too, Miss Zhou. Ours ends when the ice does."

Static. Then silence.

Okafor was staring at her. "Who was—"

"Eyes on the storm," Elodie said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of that.

Ahead, the tornado warning tone began its ugly wail on the weather band, and she let it wail, because the funnel was dropping right on schedule two kilometers off their port bow — a gray rope thickening to a stovepipe, dust ring blooming at its feet where it touched down on the flats and began to walk. Beautiful. Terrible. Honest. It wanted nothing from her except to not be in its way, and she could respect that.

"Get your shot," she told Okafor. "Ninety seconds. Then we run parallel and let it pass ahead."

He filmed. The wind arrived and leaned on the truck and she leaned back, engine roaring, holding their distance with the instinctive precision of someone who had spent years learning exactly how much the sky could push. Rain hit in sheets. The wipers thrashed uselessly and then usefully and then uselessly again. Somewhere in the roar Okafor was shouting something joyful and terrified, and Elodie almost smiled.

This was the part she was good at. This was the part where standing still would kill you and so she simply didn't stand still, and the world simplified into vectors and margins and the next hundred meters of road. No ledgers. No face-down photographs. No dead brother's handwriting explaining, in his careful way, exactly how much love could cost if you spent it on the wrong people.

Jun had borrowed the money for her. That was the part nobody at Vantara knew, or cared about, or would believe. His little sister's charter boat, sunk in a squall with the insurance lapsed by nine days — nine days, a clerical cruelty — and Jun showing up at her door with cash and a smile and *don't worry about it, Els, I've got a line on a salvage job, big one, upriver.* And then the boat was replaced and the line dried up and the interest kept compounding, and eleven months ago the river took him and left the ledger, and Elodie had learned that grief and debt arrive together sometimes, in the same envelope, and only one of them ever gets forgiven.

The tornado roped out and dissolved into rain. The storm moved off northeast, trailing its anvil across the evening sky, and the light went soft and ordinary again. Elodie slowed the truck. Her shirt was stuck to her back. Her ears rang pleasantly.

"Best ninety seconds of my life," Okafor said faintly, reviewing footage with shaking hands. "You do this every week?"

"Every week the sky cooperates."

"And the rest of the time?"

The rest of the time she counted days. She said, "The rest of the time I maintain equipment," and left it there.

* * *

They got back to Corren's Landing at dusk, the harbor lights coming on one by one along the pilings, gulls settling into their noisy roosts. Elodie walked Okafor to his car, took payment — half up front, half on delivery of the edited footage, standard terms — and watched him drive off before she let her shoulders drop.

The *Kestrel* was moored at slip fourteen, her charter boat, forty feet of patched fiberglass and stubborn engineering. Elodie climbed aboard, went below, and allowed herself the ritual: kettle on, ledger out, calculator. Not Jun's ledger. Hers. The smaller, sadder one. Fuel. Moorage. Insurance — never lapsed again, never — and the monthly payment she sent to Vantara, which was less than the interest, which meant she was bailing the ocean with a cup, which meant the number grew while she slept.

She ran the figures anyway. Every night. It was a kind of prayer, or penance.

The knock came at 8:40 p.m., three soft raps on the cabin door, and Elodie was on her feet with the marlinspike off the chart table before her brain caught up with her hand.

"Easy," said the calm voice through the wood. "Just conversation. You have my word, which I know isn't worth much to you, but it's the only currency I deal in honestly."

Cass stood on the dock in a gray coat that probably cost more than the *Kestrel*, dry despite the drizzle that had started falling, hands visible and empty. Behind her, at the head of the pier, a second figure waited beside a black sedan, pretending to study the harbor. Elodie noted the figure, noted the sedan, and stepped aside just enough to make the doorway narrow.

"You used my working frequency," Elodie said. "That's harassment. There are laws."

"There are." Cass smiled her cheekbone smile. "May I?"

Elodie didn't answer, which Cass took as invitation, because people like Cass took everything as invitation. She stepped into the cabin, glanced at the ledger on the table without appearing to, and declined the chair Elodie didn't offer.

"You're good at moving fast," Cass said. "I watched the storm feed this afternoon. Local news carried your client's footage — 'veteran chaser Elodie Zhou,' very flattering. You position well. You leave early enough. You don't gamble with other people's lives." She tilted her head. "Which tells me you understand risk. Real risk. Calculated versus catastrophic. So I'm going to speak to you in your own language."

"I'm not interested."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I know how your terms work. They start generous and end with someone picking up photographs off my shelf."

Something flickered behind Cass's eyes — acknowledgment, maybe, or amusement. "Fair. Then let me skip to the end instead. The Meridian expedition went silent twenty-three days ago. Research vessel, eight crew, contracted through channels the Syndicate insures. Their last known position is upriver, past the narrows, in territory that closes completely when calving season opens — six weeks, maybe five. After that, no route in or out until next spring. Whatever happened to those eight people, whatever they were carrying, whatever they *found*" — a small emphasis, deliberate — "becomes unreachable. Permanently. The Syndicate's exposure on that policy is significant. Yours, Miss Zhou, is two hundred and sixty-one thousand and change, as of yesterday's accrual."

The number landed in the cabin like a third passenger. Elodie kept her face still. Outside, the drizzle ticked against the windows, and somewhere down the dock a halyard slapped its loose rhythm against a mast.

"Recover the Meridian or confirm its loss," Cass said. "Bring back the crew if they're alive, evidence if they're not, and manifest documentation regardless. Six weeks, in and out before the ice. Do that, and the balance is struck. Not reduced. *Struck.* Zero. Your brother's account closed, your name cleared, your frequency yours again." She paused. "Refuse, and we continue as we have been. Monthly payments. Monthly accrual. And eventually, when the number stops making sense even to us, other arrangements."

"You'd send me upriver alone? One boat?"

"You'd assemble a crew. You're good at that too — I checked. Tomas Reyes pilots out of Ninebark Basin. Imani Okonkwo reads water better than any hydrologist on the coast. Both available, both affordable, if the money's right." Cass smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, which was somehow worse. "And you'd carry a Syndicate observer. Standard practice on insured recoveries. For everyone's protection."

An observer. Elodie filed that away — a watcher, a leash, a set of eyes reporting back to the gray coat standing in her cabin drinking nothing.

"And if the Meridian's crew is dead because of something the Syndicate insured *against*," Elodie said slowly, "you want me to bring back proof so you can deny the claim."

"I want you to bring back the truth," Cass said pleasantly. "What anyone does with it afterward is above my station." She moved to the door, unhurried, and paused with her hand on the frame. "Contract's at the harbormaster's office, under my name, until Friday. After that the offer goes to someone else and your number keeps growing." She looked back once, and for a single unguarded instant the calm slipped and Elodie saw underneath it — tiredness, real tiredness, the look of a person who also served something patient and hungry. "For what it's worth, Miss Zhou, I hope you sign. The river's kinder than we are."

Then she was gone, footsteps fading down the wet dock, the sedan pulling away with a sound like a held breath released.

Elodie stood in her cabin for a long time. The kettle had gone cold. On the table, the ledger lay open to the same page it always opened to, and beside it, in its bracket by the window, Jun's compass pointed north, steady and dull, toward the upriver country, toward the narrows, toward the closing ice.

She looked at it for a while.

"Don't," she told it.

But the needle didn't move, and neither, she was beginning to suspect, would she.
