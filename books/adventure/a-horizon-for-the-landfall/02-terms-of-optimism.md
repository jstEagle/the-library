## 2. Terms of Optimism

The Syndicate's office in Port Merrow didn't have a sign. That was the first thing Elodie noticed, standing across the canal with her collar up against the drizzle, counting the ways a building could pretend it wasn't what it was. No plaque, no nameplate, just a gray stone facade with brass fittings gone green and a door that opened for her before she touched the handle.

Inside smelled of cedar oil and old paper, which was a lie too. The Syndicate dealt in debt, and debt had no smell at all until it came due.

"Ms. Zhou." A woman rose from behind a desk that was too clean to be used — Cass, though Elodie wouldn't learn the name for another ten minutes. She was maybe fifty, silver at the temples cut short and practical, wearing a coat worth more than Elodie's boat. Her smile arrived a half-second before the rest of her expression did, like it had been sent ahead as an advance party. "Thank you for coming. I know you had options."

"I didn't."

"Even better." She gestured to a chair. "Please. Sit. Can I offer you tea? Coffee? Something stronger? It's early, but I find salvage people keep their own hours."

"Just say what you want."

Cass sat back down, unhurried, and folded her hands on the immaculate desk. Up close there were lines around her eyes that suggested she'd once been out in weather, actually out in it, before graduating to rooms like this one. "Straight to business. Good. I'll respect your time because I'm asking you to respect mine, and we both know time is the only currency either of us can't counterfeit." She slid a folder across the desk. "You owe the Vantara Syndicate four hundred and twelve thousand, six hundred marks. Principal plus accrued interest on a loan taken out in your brother's name, which you assumed after his death. You've missed two payments. The third would have triggered collection proceedings."

Elodie didn't touch the folder. "I know what I owe. I've been paying."

"You've been *servicing*. There's a difference, and the difference is why you're here." Cass opened the folder herself, turned it around, and tapped a page. "Your storm-chasing contracts net you, what, nine hundred marks a run after fuel and maintenance? At that rate you'd clear this debt in roughly thirty-eight years. Assuming no engine failures, no medical emergencies, no bad seasons. And assuming," and here her voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse than cruelty, "we didn't raise the rate last quarter, which we did. You read the amendment?"

Elodie had read it. She'd read it three times at her kitchen table with the lamp turned low so she couldn't see the harbor out the window, because seeing the harbor while reading numbers like those felt like being mocked by the sea itself.

"What do you want?" she said again.

"The Meridian."

* * *

The name landed in the room and sat there. Everyone in Port Merrow knew the *Meridian* — or knew of it. An expedition vessel, privately funded, well-found, that had gone upriver six weeks ago toward the high glacial country where the maps went thin and the radio went deader than dead. Three weeks of silence now. The company that chartered her had posted a reward for information. The families of the crew had posted something less formal and more desperate: photographs stapled to the harbor board, faces sun-bleached and curling at the edges.

"Gone silent," Elodie said. "That's not salvage. That's a search. Searches don't pay debts."

"This one does." Cass pulled a second sheet from the folder and laid it flat. "Full contract recovery. You locate the *Meridian*, assess condition, recover any cargo or documentation of value, and render assistance to survivors if there are any. In exchange: the debt is struck. Not reduced. Struck. Clean ledger, clean boat, clean you."

Elodie looked at the number at the bottom of the page. It was written out in words as well as figures, the way they did when they wanted you to feel the weight of it in your mouth. *Four hundred twelve thousand, six hundred marks, forgiven in full upon completion of terms.*

"And if I refuse?"

Cass spread her hands, an open gesture, entirely false. "Then nothing happens today. Nothing happens tomorrow. But calving season opens in five weeks, Ms. Zhou, and when it opens, the upper river closes. Ice comes down off the glacier face in pieces the size of city blocks. No pilot alive runs that water during calving, and after it settles, the channels shift so badly the charts are worthless until midsummer. So the window is five weeks wide, and it's closing, and you are the best river pilot working these flats — yes, we checked, we always check — and every week you spend chasing storms for pocket money is a week the window narrows." She leaned forward. "Refusal isn't a door. It's just standing still in a room that's getting smaller."

There it was. Elodie had known it would come to this shape eventually; she'd simply hoped for more time before the walls became visible. The Syndicate never threatened. Threats left marks, and marks invited magistrates. They offered instead. They laid opportunity on the table like a meal and waited for you to notice there was no fork, that you were expected to eat with your hands, that everyone watching had already eaten this way themselves.

"Three weeks of silence," Elodie said. "On a river where silence usually means one thing."

"It means several things. Scattered radios. Dead batteries. A crew that decided it preferred its own company. Or the thing you're thinking of." Cass said it without flinching, without softening, and Elodie gave her credit for that much honesty. "We're not asking you to believe they're alive. We're asking you to go look. What you find is what you find."

"And the cargo manifest? What am I recovering, exactly?"

Cass slid a third page across. The manifest was long, technical, mostly survey equipment and provisions — but Elodie's eye caught the line items near the bottom, the ones that made no sense for a scientific expedition. Sealed transit cases, contents unspecified. Insurance riders written in language so dense it might as well have been cipher. A charter clause granting the Syndicate first claim on "all recovered materials of commercial interest."

"That clause," Elodie said, tapping it. "Commercial interest. Defined how?"

"Defined by our assessors, post-recovery."

"So I do the dangerous part, and you decide what anything's worth."

"I do the paperwork part," Cass corrected mildly. "You do the part you're good at. That's what employment is, Ms. Zhou. Division of labor. Nobody enjoys all of it."

* * *

They walked. That surprised her — she'd expected the negotiation to happen in the cedar-scented office, sealed and airless, but Cass had put on a plain rain cloak over her expensive coat and led her out along the canal toward the harbor, talking the whole way in a low, even voice that the drizzle seemed to soften rather than interrupt.

"You want to know about me," Cass said. It wasn't a question. "Everyone wants to know about the person holding their paper. It makes the debt feel more human. I'll tell you the relevant parts. I started on the water, same as you. Ran cargo upcoast for eleven years. Then a season came along — bad ice, bad luck, bad partners — and I ended up owing money to people very much like the people I work for now. I paid it off the only way it ever gets paid off. I stopped being the one who owes and became the one who's owed." She glanced sideways at Elodie. "It's not a promotion. I want to be clear about that. It's a change of address."

"Why tell me that?"

"Because you're going to sign anyway, and I'd rather you sign knowing what you're signing with. Resentment makes crews sloppy. Understanding makes them punctual." They reached the harbor board, where the photographs of the *Meridian*'s crew fluttered in the wet wind. Cass stopped in front of them. "Look at them. Really look."

Elodie did. Eleven faces. A woman with laugh lines and a captain's cap worn at a careless angle. A young man whose photograph had clearly been taken at a graduation, gown and all. Two people who might have been a couple, standing close enough that their shoulders overlapped. Someone had written under one photo, in a child's handwriting: *come home papa.*

"Three weeks," Cass said quietly. "Their families hear nothing from us, because we know nothing. The charter company knows nothing. The magistrate's office has declined to fund a search — the river's too far up, the season's too late, the odds are too poor. So the only people who will go looking are people who are paid to, and the only people who will pay are people who want something back." She turned to Elodie. "I won't insult you by pretending this is charity. It isn't. But it is also not nothing. Somewhere up that river there may be eleven people who are alive, and you may be the only reason anyone finds that out before winter closes the whole question permanently."

"You rehearse that," Elodie said. "The speech."

"I've given versions of it before. To other pilots, other debts." Cass met her eyes, and for a moment the broker's polish thinned and something older showed through, something that had also once stood in front of a wall of strangers' faces and calculated the cost of caring. "It doesn't make it untrue. That's the trap of this work, Ms. Zhou. Everything true I just told you is also bait. Both things at once. You'll have to decide which one you're biting."

* * *

She spent the afternoon not signing.

She walked the flats where her storm-chaser, the *Kestrel*, sat on its mooring with fresh paint over old scars. She checked the bilge she'd already checked that morning. She talked to the harbormaster, who owed her a favor from the year of the flash floods, and asked him, casually, what he'd heard about the *Meridian*. He spat over the rail. "Nothing good. Company's acting strange about it. Won't post the full manifest. Won't say what they were really doing up there. My cousin ran supply for them at the last lock — says they loaded crates marked with a seal he didn't recognize, heavy ones, handled careful." He looked at her sidelong. "You asking because someone's asking you?"

"Maybe."

"Then ask who benefits. River rule." He tapped his temple. "Who benefits, and who drowns. Usually different people."

At dusk she went home and took down the tin box she kept on the top shelf, behind the spare line and the expired flares. Her brother Jun's box. Inside: his compass, the brass one with the dented case he'd carried on every run since he was sixteen; a photograph of the two of them on the *Kestrel*'s bow, squinting into sun; and the letter from the Syndicate's lawyers that had arrived eleven days after the accident, informing her that as next of kin and co-signatory she had inherited his obligations along with his belongings. Four hundred twelve thousand marks. She'd read that number so many times it had stopped being money and become weather — something ambient, something you dressed for.

Jun had borrowed from the Syndicate to buy his half of a salvage partnership. The partnership had failed inside a year. He'd been running storm charters to service the debt when the squall line took him off Cape Verren — a run he'd only have taken if he needed the money that badly, which he only needed it that badly because of the loan, which existed because a man in a clean office had once smiled at him and called it opportunity.

She held the compass in her palm. The needle swung, settled, pointed north with the small persistent tremor it had always had, like a pulse.

"They want me to go get them," she said aloud, to the compass, to Jun, to the empty room. "And I don't even know if 'them' is alive."

The needle trembled. North. Always north. Never any answers in it, just direction.

* * *

She signed the next morning.

Not because the terms improved — they didn't; Cass conceded nothing except a fuel stipend and a salvage bonus structured so baroque that Elodie suspected she'd need a lawyer to understand what she'd agreed to, and the Syndicate's lawyers worked for the Syndicate. She signed because of arithmetic. Five weeks of window. Thirty-eight years of payments. A brother in the ground and a debt above it like a second grave marker, and somewhere upriver, possibly, eleven people breathing fog and waiting for a hull sound they'd recognize.

Cass brought the contract to the harbor itself, which Elodie understood immediately as theater — witnesses, openness, the appearance of a fair deal struck in daylight. Two clerks, a notary with a portable stamp, and a folding table set up on the quay with the drizzle beading on its surface while Cass's assistant held an umbrella over the documents and not over Cass.

"Any questions before you commit?" Cass asked, pen extended.

"A hundred. None you'll answer straight."

"Then we understand each other perfectly." The pen was heavy, silver, warm from Cass's pocket. Elodie took it and read the signature page one final time — *completion of terms to the satisfaction of the Vantara Syndicate,* there it was, the trap's teeth, satisfaction defined by whom, defined how, defined whenever they pleased — and signed anyway, because refusing wasn't survivable and because some part of her, the part that had stared at the harbor board photographs until the child's handwriting blurred, wanted to go up that river regardless of what paper said.

Cass countersigned. The notary stamped. One of the clerks produced a copy still warm from some portable printer, and then it was done, and Elodie stood on her own quay holding a contract that weighed less than the compass in her pocket and bound her a great deal tighter.

"One more thing," Cass said, gathering her clerks. "You'll need crew. Skeleton crew, fast boat, people who can keep their mouths shut. You have four days to assemble and provision. And—" she paused, and the advance-party smile returned, "—the Syndicate will be sending an observer. Standard practice for high-value recoveries. He'll board tomorrow. His name is Rell. Treat him as crew."

"Treat him as crew," Elodie repeated. "Or treat him as yours?"

Cass looked at her for a long moment, and the drizzle filled the silence between them, ticking softly against the umbrella and the table and the water below.

"Yes," she said, and walked away.

Elodie stood at the rail and watched the gray water move. Upriver, past the last locks, past the edge of the charts, the glacier waited with its three weeks of silence, and somewhere between here and there a stranger named Rell was packing a bag with whatever observers packed.

Four days. She turned toward the harbor board, where eleven faces fluttered, and began making a list of names in her head — a pilot, a deckhand, anyone desperate enough or brave enough or broke enough to say yes.

The river, at least, would be honest about what it was.
