## 3. Crew of Strangers

The harbor at Lowmark smelled of diesel and rotting kelp, and by seven in the morning Elodie had already been refused twice.

The first refusal came from a crane operator who wanted triple rate for a rush load and didn't care that her contract window was closing. The second came from the harbormaster's clerk, a thin man with ink-stained cuffs who slid her paperwork back across the counter with two fingers, as if it might bite.

"Salvage upriver," he said. "In calving season." He said it the way other men said *plague ship*.

"Insurance is current," Elodie said.

"Insurance doesn't cover what happens up there." He stamped something anyway — a provisional clearance, good for seventy-two hours — because the Syndicate's seal on her contract was the kind of thing clerks learned not to argue with. "Find yourself a pilot who's done the reach before. That's free advice."

"Nothing's free in this town."

"No," he agreed, and turned to the next person in line.

She walked the length of the working docks with the contract folded against her ribs like a splint. The *Kestrel* waited at berth nine — forty feet of river hull, shallow draft, twin screws, older than she was and meaner about it. Her brother had loved that boat. Elodie mostly loved that it floated and could be repossessed by no one but the bank, and the bank was the least of her creditors.

The posting board near the chandlery still had her notice pinned to it, curling at the corners: **CREW WANTED — UPRIVER SALVAGE — HAZARD PAY — DEPART WITHIN 48 HOURS.** Beneath it, three names had been penciled in overnight, which was either luck or the particular desperation that settled on Lowmark every time the glacier started talking.

* * *

Tomas Reyes found her before she found him.

He was sitting on an overturned crate outside the chandlery, drinking coffee from a dented thermos cup, a big man gone soft at the middle but not at the hands, which were scarred in the way rope and winches scar them. He looked at her the way he might look at a weather report he didn't believe.

"You're Zhou," he said. Not a question. "The one paying hazard rates for a suicide run."

"I'm the one offering steady pay for six weeks' work. Hazard rates because the work has hazards. That's how rates work."

"Hm." He drank his coffee. "I ran the upper reach eleven seasons. Piloted supply runs to the survey stations, the ice quarries, once a medical evacuation when the calving came early and we pulled nine people off a shingle bar in whiteout conditions." He set the cup down. "I also lost a crew on that stretch. Four people. So you should know that going in."

Elodie sat down on the crate beside him, uninvited, because standing over a man made negotiations worse. "What happened?"

"Fog. River shifted its channel overnight — it does that above the narrows, the gravel bars move like they're alive — and I trusted my memory instead of my soundings. Ran us onto a bar in fast water. Boat held. Two of them tried to wade for shore." He said it flatly, a story worn smooth from handling. "The river took them in under a minute. I've piloted below the narrows ever since. Ten years."

"And now you're answering postings for above it."

"Now I'm old and bored and my ex-wife owns half my pension." He almost smiled; it didn't take. "Also, your rates are stupid high, which tells me either you're rich or you're desperate, and nobody rich comes to Lowmark. So. Desperate people keep contracts better than rich ones. They can't afford to cheat you." He stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire. "I'll take pilot. But three conditions. One: I don't run water I haven't sounded, no matter what your schedule says. Two: if I say we tie off, we tie off, and you don't argue until we're tied. Three—" he looked at her, and behind the gruffness there was something tired and careful, "—nobody aboard keeps secrets about the route. Not from me. Last crew I lost, the owner was running cargo he hadn't told me about, and the weight distribution is why we hit that bar. You want my hands on your wheel, I want the whole truth in your manifest."

Elodie thought about the Syndicate's sealed addendum, the one she'd signed without reading past the liability clauses. *You have no idea what you're agreeing to keep quiet,* Cass had said, smiling like a door closing.

"Manifest's clean," she said. It wasn't entirely a lie. The manifest was clean. It was everything around the manifest that wasn't.

Tomas studied her for a long moment, the way pilots study weather. "That's the first thing you've said that I don't believe," he told her. "But it's also the only lie you've told me today, and liars usually can't stop at one. I'll take it." He stuck out a hand like a boat cleat. "Six weeks. Where do I sign?"

* * *

Imani Okonkwo came recommended by the woman who sold chain, which was as close to a character reference as Lowmark produced. She was waiting at the berth at noon, a small wiry woman with a canvas duffel and a string of river-glass beads knotted around her wrist, watching the *Kestrel* with an expression Elodie couldn't read — something between hunger and grief.

"You're hiring deckhands," Imani said. It wasn't quite a question either. Everyone in this town spoke in statements; questions were for people with time.

"I'm hiring one deckhand. Why do you want it? Hazard pay's good but the work will try to kill you."

"My mother worked the river boats twenty years. She said the water gives back what it takes, if you're polite about asking." Imani touched the beads at her wrist — green glass, sea-worn, old. "These were hers. She drowned when I was fourteen, up past the second bend, and the river gave her back on the third day, gentle as anything, laid out on a sandbar with her hands folded. So yes, I know the work tries to kill you. I also know it negotiates." She looked up, and her eyes were very direct. "You're going up past the narrows. Everyone at the market says so. Nobody who goes up past the narrows comes back the same, and some don't come back at all. I'm not afraid of that. But I have terms too."

"Everyone has terms today."

"Good. Terms mean people plan on surviving." She counted them off on her fingers. "One: I sleep ashore when we make landfall. Never aboard. My mother's rule and mine. Two: when I say the river's wrong — wrong smell, wrong sound, wrong light — you don't laugh at me, you just slow down. Three: nobody whistles aboard. Whistling calls the wind, and the wind up there answers to someone else." She said the last part without embarrassment, without any performance of superstition, just as fact, the way Tomas had listed his dead.

Elodie had worked with superstitious deckhands before. The good ones were worth double their wages, because their superstitions were just attention wearing ritual clothing — they noticed things, noticed them early, and noticed them honestly. "Done," she said. "All three."

Imani nodded once, satisfied, and swung her duffel aboard without waiting for permission, claiming the forward bunk by right of arrival. Then she stopped at the rail and looked back down at Elodie. "Your boat's got a name problem," she said.

"It's called the *Kestrel*."

"A kestrel's a bird that hangs in the wind watching for mice. Fine name for a hunter. Bad name for a boat going up to visit the ice." She shrugged. "Boats grow into their names or die of them. We'll see which."

* * *

Rell arrived at four o'clock, and Elodie knew what he was before he said a word, because the Syndicate dressed its observers in expensive restraint: a gray coat that cost more than her winch, boots too clean for a dock, and a smile calibrated to seem friendly without ever committing to warmth.

"Ms. Zhou." He extended a hand. His grip was dry and precise. "Corwin Rell. I'm told I'm your observer."

"Told by whom?"

"The same people who told you to expect me." The smile widened a fraction. "Clause fourteen of your contract. Vantara reserves the right to place an observer aboard for the duration of any recovery operation. I'm the duration."

Elodie had signed clause fourteen. She'd signed it the way she'd signed everything Cass put in front of her — fast, because reading slowly made the trap feel real. "And what exactly are you observing?"

"Contract compliance. Cargo handling. General seaworthiness." He strolled along the rail, touching nothing, cataloguing everything — the new line coiled on the pin rails, the patched fenders, Imani's beads hanging from a hook inside the wheelhouse door. "Think of me as ballast. Every boat needs some."

"Ballast stays in the hold and doesn't talk."

"That's the spirit." He laughed, easy and bright, and it was the laugh that unsettled her most — because she'd grown up around collectors and brokers and their enforcers, and none of them laughed easily. Men like Rell smiled the way fishermen baited hooks: generously, patiently, with the barb hidden in the curve. "Look, Ms. Zhou, I know what you're thinking. You're thinking *Syndicate spy*, and fair enough. But here's my honest pitch: I'm not here to make trouble. I'm here so that nobody else has to come make it. One quiet observer on a quiet boat, and everyone gets paid and everyone goes home. That's a deal I'd think a woman in your position would appreciate."

*A woman in your position.* The debt, then. They'd briefed him on the debt. Of course they had. Somewhere in a Vantara office her brother's ledger sat open on a desk, and every number in it was a hand on her throat, and this pleasant man in the gray coat was one finger of that hand.

"Berth's aft," she said. "Aboard means you work. Observers who stand around get assigned to the bilge pump."

"I've pumped bilge before." He picked up his single bag — light, she noted, much lighter than a man planning to stay six weeks should pack. "And Ms. Zhou? For what it's worth, I hope we find these Meridian people alive. Genuinely." And the strange thing was that she believed him — believed he hoped it, at least. What he wanted *done with* the finding was another question entirely.

* * *

They spent the evening loading, and the loading was where the friction lived, because friction always lives in the details.

Tomas rejected the first plan for the cargo trim — "you've got the weight too far forward, we'll dig the bow in the chop" — and redrew it on the deck in chalk, moving crates in his head with the authority of a man who had felt a hull answer wrongly and never forgotten it. Imani inspected every coil of line personally, running each one through her hands like scripture, and threw back two lengths as "unlucky" — frayed, actually, though only barely, and Elodie let it go because the result was correct even if the reasoning was hers alone. Rell watched all of it from the rail with his notebook, writing steadily, and when Elodie caught herself tracking the notebook instead of the work, she made herself stop.

At dusk they ate together in the wheelhouse because it was the warmest space aboard — tinned stew, hard bread, coffee Tomas brewed strong enough to strip paint. Nobody talked much. The silence had texture: Tomas eating with his eyes on the river mouth, already reading tomorrow's water; Imani turning her mother's beads over in her fingers, lips moving through something that wasn't quite a prayer; Rell asking cheerful, harmless questions — *how long have you had the boat, is that a compass over the binnacle, family piece?* — each one landing a little too precisely, like a man tapping walls for hollows.

It was the compass question that snagged. Elodie heard herself say "family piece" in a voice that closed like a door, and Rell smiled his easy smile and wrote something down, and she hated him a little for it, and hated herself more for giving him the page.

Later, when the others had turned in, she stood at the rail in the dark and looked upriver. Fog lay on the water in long pale bands, and beyond it, sixty miles north, the glacier sat in its valley doing whatever it did — growing, groaning, remembering. Somewhere up there, three weeks silent now, the *Meridian*'s crew was alive or wasn't, and her brother's debt hung around her neck like wet rope, and the only way out ran straight through the worst water on the coast.

"You're doing the math," Tomas said from the wheelhouse doorway. He hadn't slept either. Old pilots never did, the night before new water.

"Does it come out?"

"Never does." He handed her a cup of coffee. "For what it's worth — I checked. Your observer's bag weighed maybe ten pounds. Man travels light when he expects to be home soon, or when somebody else is carrying the heavy things for him." He drank. "Watch that one."

"I am."

"We all watch each other, then. That's a crew." He said it like a joke, but it landed like a keel — the first true thing holding the boat steady. "First tide's at five. Sleep if you can."

She couldn't. At four she went down to the wheelhouse to check the charts one last time, and found the Syndicate's sealed addendum where she'd left it, and finally — with the fog pressing at the windows and the river muttering against the hull — broke the seal and read it through.

Then she read it again, slower, and her hands went cold.

Because clause fourteen didn't end with *observer*. It ended with *recovery of specified materials*, and the materials were listed by number, not name, and the numbers matched nothing on any salvage manifest she'd ever seen — except that the last line instructed her, in the Syndicate's bloodless legal script, that the *Meridian*'s own records were to be surrendered intact, and that any crew member of the *Meridian* recovered alive was to be considered, for contractual purposes, *cargo*.

Up on the foredeck, in the gray beginning of morning, Rell stood at the rail looking north, and he was smiling.
