## 4. Upriver Silence

The fog came up the river like a held breath.

Elodie stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Wake* and watched it swallow the banks whole — first the reed beds, then the leaning pilings of an old fish weir, then the hills themselves, until there was nothing left in the world but brown water, gray air, and the sound of Tomas's engines turning over slow and patient below her feet. The compass sat in its bracket on the dash, her brother's compass, its brass case worn to a dull shine by twenty years of his thumb. It pointed north with the calm certainty of something that had never once been wrong.

She wished she could say the same for herself.

"Radio's still dead," Imani called from the bow, where she'd been standing for two hours with one hand on the rail and the other trailing a knotted sounding line through the water, hauling it up, reading it, letting it down again. "Third dead band since the narrows. Either every tower on this stretch went down at once, or somebody doesn't want us talking."

"Or the towers were always a rumor," Tomas said from the engine hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last decade. "Half these upriver stations exist on paper so the port authority can collect fees."

Imani didn't laugh. She wound the line around her fist, coil by coil, and looked at the water the way other people looked at scripture — like it was saying something, and the sin was in not listening. "River's running shallow on the left," she said. "Gravel bar moved after the spring melt. Keep her right of the dead willow."

Elodie eased the wheel over. The *Kestrel's Wake* drew less than most salvage boats, but not so little that she could afford to argue with a deckhand who read silt like a ledger. Behind them, the fog closed over their wake as if they'd never passed at all.

That was the thing about this stretch of river nobody put on the charts: it didn't feel empty. It felt *attended*. Like a room where someone had just stopped talking when you walked in.

* * *

Rell appeared on deck an hour past dawn, which was to say he appeared out of nowhere, because Elodie was starting to suspect Rell slept standing up somewhere out of sight and simply materialized whenever conversation turned interesting. He had a thermos of coffee he never seemed to drink from and a smile calibrated to the half-degree.

"Morning," he said. "Quiet water."

"Quiet everything," Elodie said.

"That's what I keep noticing." He leaned against the rail beside her, casual as a man discussing weather. "You know what struck me about the Meridian contract? The manifest. Cass sent over the cargo declaration before we shipped out — standard procedure, sure, but did you actually read it?"

"I read it."

"Did you read it *carefully*?" Rell's smile stayed exactly where it was. "Because survey expeditions carry survey equipment. Coring rigs, sample crates, spectrometers if they've got funding. The Meridian's manifest lists all of that. But it also lists forty cases of archival film stock. Sealed canisters. Climate-controlled storage, marked fragile." He tapped the rail once with one finger. "Film, Elodie. In this decade. Who's shooting film upriver?"

"Maybe Dr. Sefa likes the grain."

"Maybe." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. They were doing arithmetic. "Or maybe the Syndicate knows exactly what kind of cargo it is, and that's why they want it back so badly. Salvage contracts don't usually come with a bonus clause for *intact documentation*."

The engines thumped beneath them. Somewhere off to starboard, hidden in the fog, a bird cried twice and stopped, as if corrected.

"You ask a lot of questions for an observer," Elodie said.

"It's my job to observe." Rell straightened, unhurried. "Asking questions is just observation with better posture." He raised the thermos in a small salute and drifted aft toward the stern, where Imani was coiling line, and Elodie watched him go and thought: *he's not here to watch the river. He's here to watch us watching it.*

Her brother had a word for men like Rell. He'd had a lot of words, most of them now carved into a headstone she couldn't afford to visit. The debt the Syndicate carried in her name wasn't even hers — it was his, signed in his name against hers, a signature she'd found only after the funeral, buried in paperwork like a hook buried in bait. Forty thousand owed to people who never sent letters, only calls. The phone in her cabin hadn't rung since they left port. That silence should have been a mercy.

It felt like being watched instead.

* * *

They made the first lock by midday, or what would have been the lock if the lock still functioned; the gates stood rusted open, wedged with timber some previous crew had jammed there years ago, and the river ran through unimpeded, higher than any chart allowed for. Imani took them through on the sounding line alone, calling depths in a low, steady voice like a woman reciting psalms — *six feet, six and a quarter, six, five and three-quarters, hard right* — and Tomas answered with the throttle, and Elodie steered, and for twenty minutes the three of them moved as one animal through the flooded ruin of somebody's engineering.

It was the closest thing to trust she'd felt in months, and it lasted until they cleared the lock and Rell started asking questions again.

"The manifest," he said, appearing at her elbow. "Item sixty-one. Twenty drums of drilling fluid, but the drum count doesn't match the weight displacement Cass's numbers imply. There's maybe eight hundred kilos unaccounted for."

"Maybe the manifest's wrong."

"Manifests are never wrong. People lie on them, but they're never *wrong*." He smiled at her. "You're not curious? A salvage captain, not curious about eight hundred missing kilos?"

"I'm curious about staying alive long enough to spend my fee," Elodie said. "Curiosity's a luxury good. You should check the price before you buy."

Something flickered across his face — not offense. Recognition, maybe. As if she'd quoted something he'd been told once, by someone whose opinion had mattered. Then the smile reassembled itself and he wandered off toward the bow, thermos under his arm, and Elodie realized her hands had gone tight on the wheel and made herself loosen them, finger by finger.

The fog thickened as the afternoon wore on. Not thinning with the sun the way fog should, but gathering, condensing, rolling down from the glacier country ahead in slow gray shoulders. By four o'clock the visibility was fifty meters. By five it was thirty. The radio bands stayed dead — all of them, even the emergency frequencies, which was not possible, which was not how radio worked, and yet there it was: the speaker hissing static like sand pouring through an hourglass, no tower, no beacon, no voice anywhere on the dial.

Tomas came up from the engine bay and stood listening to it for a long moment. "I don't like this," he said. Not to anyone. To the river, maybe. "I ran this stretch eleven years ago. Radio was spotty, sure. Never *dead*. Dead means somebody's jamming, and jamming means somebody's got equipment, and equipment means—"

"Means what?" Elodie asked.

He didn't finish. He looked at the fog the way Imani looked at the water, and whatever he saw in it made him go back down into the engine bay without another word, and Elodie heard him checking the bilge pumps twice.

* * *

They anchored for the night in a cut behind a gravel island, engines off, running lights doused per Tomas's old habit — "lights draw eyes, and eyes draw questions" — and the fog pressed against the hull like something breathing. Elodie took first watch because she didn't expect to sleep anyway. She sat in the wheelhouse with the compass glowing faintly green on the dash and the dead radio hissing and the river sliding past the anchor chain with a sound like slow applause.

At some point Imani climbed up and sat across from her without asking, which was apparently how Imani did everything.

"You felt it too," Imani said. It wasn't a question.

"Felt what?"

"The quiet." Imani wrapped both hands around her knees. On shore, back home, she wore charms — little braided cords at her wrists, a coin on a string at her neck — and Elodie had privately filed her under *superstition*, a harmless eccentricity you hired anyway because she was the best reader of shallow water on the coast. Out here, in the fog, the charms looked less like superstition and more like equipment. "My grandmother ran barges on the upper reaches. She said the river has moods, and you learn them like a person's moods. This—" she nodded at the fog, the static, the utter absence of birdsong since sundown, "—this isn't a mood. This is a held tongue. River's keeping a secret. Or somebody's making it."

"The Syndicate's collectors aren't due for days," Elodie said. "And they'd come from downstream."

"Then upstream." Imani said it simply, the way you'd note the weather. "Something's between us and where we're going. Something that doesn't want company, or wants very specific company." She glanced at the compass. "Your brother's?"

Elodie's hand moved to the brass case before she could stop it. "You know about that?"

"Tomas talks when he's worried. Talks about the boat, mostly, but tonight he talked about you." Imani shrugged. "Brother's compass. Brother's debt. Brother's route, some might say, since you're following a needle he owned into water no chart admits exists." Her dark eyes were steady, not accusing. Curious. "Does it ever drift?"

"No." Elodie heard how fast the answer came and hated it. "It's steady. Steadier than anything else on this boat."

"Mm." Imani unfolded herself and headed for the ladder, then paused. "My grandmother also said: when the river goes quiet, it's not gone. It's listening. Best not to say anything you wouldn't want repeated." She climbed down into the dark, and her footsteps faded along the deck, and Elodie sat alone with the static and the needle and the fog.

She tried the radio one more time. All bands dead. She keyed the mic anyway, out of some stubborn animal need to hear a human voice, even her own.

"*Kestrel's Wake*, position—" She stopped. Listened to the static pour back at her. Somewhere inside it, so faint she might have invented it, there was a rhythm. A crackling, arrhythmic but regular, like ice shifting far away, like something enormous clearing its throat.

She set the mic down and did not key it again.

* * *

Dawn came without sunrise — just a gradual brightening of the gray, the fog lifting ten meters, twenty, enough to show the river running narrow and fast between walls of black spruce hung with mist. And enough to show the raft.

It came around the bend downstream of them, riding the current toward their anchorage, and for one absurd second Elodie's heart leapt — survivors, supplies, contact — before her mind caught up with the details. No one aboard. Lines neatly coiled. Cargo net lashed down with seamanlike care. An expedition supply raft, intact, abandoned, drifting home alone like a dog that had slipped its leash.

Tomas was already on deck. Imani was already reading the current, calling the intercept. Rell stood at the rail with his thermos, and his smile, for the first time since they'd shipped out, was gone.

"Meridian stencil," he said quietly, reading the hull marking through the binoculars. "That's their forward cache raft. It shouldn't be here. It should be *upstream*, with them."

"Then why is it coming down?" Elodie asked.

Nobody answered. The raft turned slowly in the current, presenting its flank, and Elodie brought the *Kestrel's Wake* alongside with hands that were steadier than she felt, and Imani threw the line, and together they hauled the empty raft against their hull — and that was when Elodie saw the logbook, lashed to the tiller post under oilcloth, and opened it to the last written page.

The entry ended mid-sentence. Mid-*word*. The handwriting was neat and professional right up to the final line, where it simply stopped, the pen stroke trailing off the edge of the page as though the writer had been interrupted by something that required both hands.

Or something that required no hands at all.

Behind her, Rell read over her shoulder, and when he spoke, his voice had lost every ounce of its polish.

"Get me the manifest," he said. "All of it. Every line."
