## 5. What the Water Gave Back

Tomas Varga had a rule about rivers: they never lied, but they never told you everything either. Forty years on the water had taught him to read the surface the way other men read faces — the dimples where current fought current, the pale seam of a submerged bar, the way driftwood rode high and fast when the channel was deep. The river upstream of Kessel Bend was telling him something now, and he didn't like its tone.

The fog had burned off by midmorning, which was itself unusual. This stretch ran cold even in high summer, fed by meltwater off the glacier fields, and it usually kept a skin of mist until noon. Today the air was clear and hard-edged, the light flat as hammered tin, and every sound carried too far. The slap of water against the hull. The creak of the mast step. Imani's bare feet on the deck plates as she moved from bow to stern with that unhurried walk of hers, reading the banks.

"Dead water ahead," she called back. "Big eddy behind the point. Something's turning in it."

Tomas eased the throttle down. Behind him, Rell looked up from the manifest he'd been studying for two days straight — the Meridian's cargo list, annotated in the Syndicate's cramped bureaucratic hand. The observer had asked about it eleven times by Tomas's count. What was aboard. What was insured. What the expedition's insurance would pay out if instruments were recovered versus if they weren't. He asked the questions pleasantly, always pleasantly, the way a man asks directions before robbing you.

"Something in the eddy," Rell repeated, coming forward. His smile was already in place, but his eyes were working. "Could be debris."

"Could be." Tomas cut the engine to idle and let the boat drift. The current here was lazy, swollen with early melt, and the river widened below the point into a long slow pool fringed with alder. He'd run this water twice in his life, both times without incident, both times with the feeling of being counted. Some stretches of river you passed through. Others passed through you.

Imani stood at the bow rail, one hand raised, palm out, the way she did when she wanted silence. She was the most superstitious woman Tomas had ever shipped with — salt over the threshold, no whistling aboard, a small iron nail taped under the helm console that she refused to explain — but her superstition was really just attention, disciplined and total. When Imani said the river felt wrong, the river was wrong.

"There." She pointed.

It came around the edge of the eddy slowly, turning end over end in the slack water like something asleep. A raft — not a log raft but an expedition supply raft, inflatable pontoons crusted gray with river silt, a low platform lashed across them, a broken oar trailing from one lock. It rode low but level. Whatever had put it in the water had done so deliberately.

"That's Meridian issue," Rell said quietly. All the pleasantness had gone out of his voice, replaced by something tighter. "That's their pontoon pattern. That's—"

"I know what it is." Tomas swung the wheel and brought the boat alongside with the gentlest touch he could manage, fending off with a boathook. Up close the raft smelled of mud and cold and something else, faintly sweet, like flowers left too long in a closed room. He didn't have a name for that smell and didn't want one.

Elodie came up from below decks, pulling her jacket on. She'd been sleeping badly — he heard her moving most nights, pacing the narrow corridor, sometimes standing at the chart table doing nothing at all. Debt did that to people. He'd seen it hollow out better sailors than her. "What have we got?"

"Their raft," Tomas said. "Stripped."

She saw it immediately, because Elodie Zhou missed nothing that mattered. The raft's cargo netting hung empty. The instrument mounts along the platform rail — drilled holes, stainless brackets — held nothing. No depth sounder, no GPS puck, no radio antenna. Even the tie-downs had been cut clean rather than torn, each strap coiled and left on the platform in neat loops, like a gift or a message.

"Somebody took everything off this," Elodie said. "Carefully."

"Or somebody needed everything," Imani said, "and left the straps so we'd know they meant to come back for it."

Nobody answered that. The river said nothing either, just turned the raft gently against their hull with a soft rubbery knock.

* * *

They hauled it aboard with the winch, and it took three of them to drag the platform to midships, silt streaming off the pontoons in brown ropes. Rell photographed everything — the mounts, the coils, the serial numbers stamped into the pontoon valves — with the methodical patience of a man building a case file. Tomas let him. Let him think the photographs were the important thing.

Because Tomas had found the compartment.

It was under the platform decking, a shallow waterproof locker built into the frame, the kind of place crews stashed things they didn't trust to the main hold. The latch was shut. Not locked — shut, with the care of someone who expected the locker to be opened again, just not by them.

He waited until Rell was aft photographing the oar and Elodie was at the wheel holding them steady against the current, then he knelt and opened it.

Inside: a logbook, oilskin-wrapped. A tin cup, dented. And a single bootlace, coiled tight, the aglets bitten off.

Tomas sat back on his heels with the logbook in his hands and his heart going like a bilge pump. He knew the bootlace trick. Every old river man knew it. You left your lace when you went somewhere you might not come back from — not as superstition, exactly, though Imani would have called it that. As a marker. *I was here. Count me.* He'd done it himself once, twenty-six years ago, on a river not four hundred kilometers from this one.

He unwrapped the logbook.

The handwriting started confident, slanted right, a surveyor's hand recording depths and bearings and fuel states in tidy columns. Three weeks of entries. The Meridian running upriver, mapping, sampling, the leader — Sefa, the manifest said, Dr. Ansel Sefa — noting water temperatures that made no sense, dropping by degrees in places where they should have risen. Notes about fog that behaved strangely. About radio bands going dead in specific bends of the river and nowhere else. About a side channel that appeared on none of their charts, wide enough to float a barge, that their own sonar insisted did not exist.

Then, eleven days ago, the columns stopped. The handwriting loosened. The last entry wasn't an entry at all. It read:

*We are going in tomorrow. If anyone reads this, do not follow the needle past the split rock. The valley is not on any map because it does not want*

And there it ended. Mid-sentence. The next page was blank. The page after that was blank. The rest of the book was blank, all the way to the back cover, as if whatever had been writing through that hand had simply been switched off.

Tomas closed the book and sat very still on the deck of his own boat and listened to the river go by.

*Do not follow the needle.*

He thought about Elodie's compass. Her brother's compass, the brass-cased thing she checked more often than her fuel gauge, the one she'd sworn was drifting. He'd watched her take bearings with it three days ago and frown and take them again. He'd assumed grief. Grief makes instruments lie; he'd seen men steer whole seasons by a dead wife's watch.

But maybe the needle wasn't lying. Maybe it was leading.

"Tomas." Elodie's voice, close. He hadn't heard her cross the deck. She was looking down at him, at the open locker, at the book in his hands, and her face had gone carefully neutral in the way of people who've learned that showing fear costs money. "What is it?"

He stood. His knees cracked. He was fifty-eight years old and suddenly every one of those years was present and accounted for.

"It's theirs," he said. "Sefa's log. It ends mid-sentence." He handed it to her and watched her read, watched her lips move slightly over the last line, watched the color change in her face by degrees, the way the water temperature had changed in Sefa's columns. "There's a warning in it. About following a needle past a split rock."

Elodie's hand went, without her seeming to notice, to the pocket where she kept the compass.

"And there's something else," Tomas said. "A bootlace. Left in the locker. Somebody on that crew wanted to be counted."

* * *

They anchored that night in the lee of a gravel bar, engines cold, a single lantern lit in the cabin because Imani said full dark on dead water invited company. Supper was rice and tinned fish eaten mostly in silence. Rell excused himself early, saying he needed to check his equipment, and Tomas noted that the observer's equipment had needed checking every night since Kessel Bend, always alone, always aft.

Afterward, Elodie stayed at the table with the logbook open between them, and Tomas poured two fingers of the plum brandy he kept for funerals and pushed the glass across to her.

"You want to ask me something," he said. "You've been circling it since the raft came aboard."

She turned the glass but didn't drink. "You knew. When you saw that raft. Before you found the log. You knew what it meant. You went white as the sail."

Tomas looked out the cabin window at the black water. The stars were out, hard and numerous, doubled in the river's stillness so that the boat seemed to hang between two skies.

"My third season running charter," he said. "I was thirty-two. Had a crew of six taking scientists up the Varo stretch — different river, same country, same ice feeding it. Same kind of expedition. Survey work." He turned the brandy glass once on the table. "Their lead man was a glaciologist named Ondrej Marek. Good man. Careful. The kind who triple-knotted everything."

"What happened?"

"He got interested in a side channel." Tomas heard his own voice go flat and distant, the voice he used for weather reports. "Not on the charts. Sonar wouldn't hold bottom in it. Ondrej wanted to see where it went. I told him no. Told him uncharted means unnavigable means we turn around. He argued. Politely. For two days. And on the third day I woke up and the raft was gone — their gear raft, their instruments, Ondrej and two of his team. They'd gone up the channel in the night without me."

Elodie was very still. "Did you find them?"

"We found the raft." He met her eyes. "Three days later, floating. Stripped of instruments. Straps coiled neat. And a bootlace in the locker." He tapped the table where the logbook lay. "Same as this. We searched nine days. Helicopters, dogs, the whole country turned out. Nothing. No bodies. No camp. Six years later a fisherman found Ondrej's field notebook wedged in a root ball forty kilometers downstream, dry inside a plastic sleeve, and the last entry was the same kind of sentence. Unfinished. Like the man was writing and something reached in and took the pen out of his hand."

"What did the entry say?"

Tomas recited it. He had never once had to look it up. "*The ice here is not weather. It is record. It has been keeping us.*"

The lantern flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there. Neither of them moved.

"After that," Tomas said, "I stopped running the upper rivers. Twenty years, Elodie. Flat water, coastal runs, ferry contracts. Safe as a parking lot. Then your Syndicate broker puts money on my table with this contract, and I tell myself: old man needs the work, the route's changed in twenty years, the ice has retreated, whatever took Ondrej is gone or was never anything at all." He laughed, short and without humor. "And then their raft comes around the bend looking exactly like his did, and I'm thirty-two again, and the river is counting us."

Elodie finally drank. She set the glass down with a click. "The log says don't follow the needle past the split rock."

"Yes."

"And your friend Ondrej followed a needle into a side channel."

"Yes."

"So we don't follow it." She said it firmly, and then her hand went to her pocket again, that unconscious gesture, and Tomas watched her take out the compass and set it on the table between them without meaning to, the way a person sets down a weight they've forgotten they're carrying.

The needle was moving.

Not swinging — drifting, slow and deliberate, ticking off degrees like a clock that had decided, after decades, to start keeping a different kind of time. It settled pointing upstream and to port, toward a wall of darkness beyond the gravel bar where the charts showed nothing but unbroken forest and, if the paper was honest, solid ground.

Neither of them touched it.

"Your brother's?" Tomas asked quietly.

"My brother's." Elodie stared at the needle the way you'd stare at a stranger wearing a loved one's face. "It's never done this. In fifteen years it's never—" She stopped. Started again. "The log says the valley doesn't want to be on maps. Maybe it doesn't mind being on compasses."

"Or maybe," Tomas said, "it wants company."

Above the river, far upstream, so faint it might have been imagination, came a sound neither cracking nor thunder — a long resonant groan out of the ice fields, rising and falling, almost rhythmic. Almost voiced.

Imani appeared in the doorway without a sound, looked at the compass, looked at their faces, and said only: "It started ten minutes ago. It's been getting closer."

Tomas looked at the needle. At the woman who owned it. At the dark water running north toward whatever the river was keeping.

In twenty-six years he had run from this. He was done running. Fear was a current like any other — you couldn't stop it, but you could damn well steer.

"All right," he said, and heard his voice come out steady, which surprised him. "Tomorrow we find the split rock. But we do it my way — daylight, lines rigged, everyone roped above deck. Nobody follows any needle anywhere until I've seen what we're steering into with my own eyes." He picked up the logbook and closed it gently, like closing a door on a sleeper. "Ondrej Marek went in the night, alone, without telling his boat. We are not going to make his mistake. Whatever took him isn't taking this crew."

Elodie nodded slowly, and for the first time since Kessel Bend, Tomas saw something in her face that wasn't debt or dread. It was purpose, hard and bright as a keel.

From aft came the soft click of a hatch — Rell, returning from checking his equipment, or transmitting through it, or both. The groan from the ice sounded again, longer this time, and Imani crossed herself with two fingers and murmured something in her mother's language that Tomas didn't understand but agreed with completely.

The needle held its bearing, patient as a promise, pointing at the dark.
