## 6. The Compass Points Wrong

The fog came up the river at dusk like something poured, thick and slow, filling the channel from bank to bank until the water and the sky were the same gray animal. Elodie stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Grace* with one hand on the spokes and the other closed around her brother's compass, and she watched the needle do a thing needles were not supposed to do.

It had started an hour ago. A tremor first — the kind of jitter you'd blame on the engine's vibration, on iron fittings, on your own tired eyes. She'd blamed all three in turn. But the *Grace* was running quiet through the dead water, her engine idled to a murmur so they could listen for anything under the fog, and still the needle moved. It swung off north with the deliberateness of a creature turning its head. Then it settled, steady as a plumb line, pointing not downriver along their charted route but hard to port, toward a wall of fog where the charts showed nothing but the unbroken shoulder of the valley slope.

She held it flat on her palm and watched it hold that bearing for a full minute. Two minutes. Five.

"Jun," she said quietly, to no one. "What are you doing?"

Her brother had carried that compass through six years of river work and two years of dying slowly in a hospital ward while the Syndicate's interest compounded against his name. He'd pressed it into her hand in his last lucid week, closing her fingers over the brass case with a strength that surprised her. *"It doesn't lie,"* he'd said. *"People lie. Maps lie. This thing only knows one direction, and it always knows it honestly."* She'd thought he was being sentimental. Jun had been many things — reckless, generous, bad with money in ways that had cost them both everything — but he had never been sentimental about instruments. An instrument that lied got you killed. He'd drilled that into her before he'd taught her to read water.

So when the needle pointed at blank fog, she did not assume the compass was broken.

* * *

Tomas found her there near midnight. He came up from the engine room wiping grease from his hands with a rag that had been black longer than it had been cloth, and he read her posture the way he read weather — instantly, and without kindness.

"You haven't slept."

"I'm fine."

"That's what people say when they're not." He leaned against the rail beside her, close enough that she could show him the compass without making a ceremony of it. She opened her hand. His eyes went to the needle and stayed there, and she watched his face do the arithmetic: the chart said straight on, the needle said left, and the man holding the instrument was the same woman who'd signed a Syndicate contract three days ago with a look on her face like she was swallowing glass.

"Your brother's?" he asked.

"His."

Tomas was silent a long moment. Out here, in the fog, with the river running brown and slow beneath them and the glacier somewhere ahead making its small sounds in the dark — out here, a compass that disagreed with the chart wasn't a curiosity. It was a decision waiting to be made.

"The Meridian's last confirmed position was forty klicks ahead on the main channel," he said. "That's where the contract sends us. That's where Rell says we're going."

"I know where the contract sends us."

"Then you know we can't go chasing brass needles into side channels. Fog like this eats boats. I've seen it happen." His voice didn't rise; it never rose. It just got flatter, more final, like a hatch being dogged shut. "We run the route. We find the wreck or we don't. We go home."

"She's not a wreck."

"You don't know that."

"I know the Syndicate doesn't send salvage crews after wrecks with a negotiator aboard." She nodded toward the cabin where Rell had turned in, or pretended to. "They sent us to bring back something alive, Tomas. And my brother's compass is telling me that something isn't on the main channel."

The old pilot looked at the needle again, then out at the fog, and Elodie saw something move behind his eyes that he didn't let reach his mouth. Grief, maybe. Or memory. He'd lost people on this river — she'd heard the story in fragments from Imani, who told it like scripture and refused to elaborate. Whatever the main channel had taken from him, it had taken it in fog not unlike this.

"Sleep on it," he said finally. "Decide at dawn with a clear head. That's not cowardice, Elodie. That's seamanship."

She agreed because agreeing cost nothing. Decisions made at midnight were just fears wearing a captain's hat. But she stood at the wheel long after Tomas went below, and the needle never wavered, and neither did she.

* * *

Rell was waiting for her at breakfast.

That was the thing about the Syndicate observer — he was always already there, seated at the little table in the galley with coffee poured for two, smiling his easy smile, as if the boat existed inside his schedule rather than the other way around. He wore the Syndicate's gray like it fit him well. Everything about him was pleasant, and everything about him was positioned.

"Morning, Captain." He slid a folded chart across the table. Her own chart, annotated overnight in neat red pencil. "I marked our progress. We're making good time. Another day and a half on this heading puts us at the Meridian's last known position right on schedule."

"Right on schedule," she repeated.

"Cass will be pleased." He sipped his coffee. "She does value punctuality. Among other virtues."

Elodie sat across from him and did not touch the chart. "Rell. What's the Meridian carrying?"

The smile didn't falter, but it thinned, the way ice thins over moving water — still bright, suddenly less solid. "You've asked me that twice now."

"And you've dodged me twice. Cargo manifest came through Cass's office with half the line items redacted. Survey equipment, they say. Core samples, they say. You don't send a gunboat escort request for core samples."

"We're not sending—"

"Not yet," she said. "But you've asked Imani twice about our fuel reserves and once about how much ballast we can take on. You've asked Tomas about the narrows downstream — whether they'd hold a vessel heavier than ours. You think nobody notices, but Imani notices everything and tells me everything, and Tomas pretends not to care and remembers every word." She leaned forward. "So I'll ask plain, once. When we find the Meridian crew — if we find them alive — what happens to them?"

For the first time since Port Vessa, Rell looked genuinely young. Just for a second. Then the smile reassembled itself, brick by pleasant brick.

"My job is observation, Captain. Not outcomes."

"Everybody's job is outcomes. That's the whole business."

He held her gaze, and she gave him credit for one thing: he didn't blink first. "Stay on route," he said softly. "That's all I'm authorized to tell you, and it's all you need to do. The main channel reaches the survey site. The Syndicate's maps are not wrong, Elodie. They're expensive."

* * *

Imani read the river the way other people read scripture, and that morning she read it aloud.

"Water's wrong," she announced from the bow, where she'd been standing for an hour with her bare feet braced wide and her eyes on the current. "Wrong since before dawn. Main channel's starving."

Tomas looked up from his charts. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the river's giving its strength away somewhere else." Imani turned, and her dark eyes found Elodie at the wheel with the certainty of someone who had already known what she would see there. "There's a second mouth eating the flow. Big one. The main channel runs shallow ahead — feel it in the hull, Captain? Shallow and getting shallower. Rivers don't shrink in flood season unless something bigger is drinking."

Elodie had felt it. The *Grace*'s draft had been whispering against the bottom since midmorning, a soft dragging kiss of silt that Tomas kept pretending not to hear. The charts showed ample depth for another thirty kilometers. The charts, Rell had said, were expensive.

Expensive. Not correct.

At noon they stopped at a shingle bar to take on water, and Elodie walked the gravel with the compass open in her hand, away from the boat, away from Rell's watching eyes. The needle swung as she walked and then steadied, always, on the same bearing: west by northwest, into the valley wall, where the fog stood in columns like the pillars of some drowned temple. Where, according to every map she owned, there was nothing but rock and ice.

Except that rivers didn't shrink in flood season unless something bigger was drinking. Except that her brother had died owing money to people who erased things for a living, and had handed her an instrument that didn't lie, and had said *it always knows honestly* with the particular emphasis of a man passing on a warning he wouldn't live long enough to explain.

She stood on the bar until the cold came up through her boots, and then she went back to the boat and made her decision the way she did everything: fast, because standing still meant reckoning.

* * *

She called the crew together at the rail at fourteen hundred hours. All four of them — herself, Tomas, Imani, and Rell, who arrived last and stood slightly apart, the way observers stand.

"There's a side channel," she said. "Half a kilometer back, on the port side, hidden in the fog. The main channel's failing — Imani called it, the hull confirms it, and by tomorrow we'd be dragging bottom with a full load. My compass has been pointing into that channel since last night, steady, like it's nailed there. There's nothing on any chart. I'm taking us in."

Rell spoke first, smooth and immediate. "Captain, I have to formally note that deviating from the contracted route constitutes—"

"Note it. Write it down. Put it in your report with today's date and my name spelled correctly." She kept her voice level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse for him. "But you should also note that the Syndicate paid for this boat to reach the Meridian, and the Meridian is not on your expensive maps. If I'm wrong, we lose half a day. If you're wrong, we lose the contract, the season, and possibly the boat. Do the arithmetic you're so fond of."

"This is exactly the kind of improvisation that gets crews killed."

"So is obedience," said Imani quietly, and even Rell glanced at her.

Tomas said nothing for a long moment. He stood at the rail looking at the fog wall, jaw working, and Elodie understood that he was fighting not the decision but the memory — whatever the river had taken from him on this stretch, however many years ago, it had taken it because somebody had made a call in fog just like this. Finally he spat over the side, an old pilot's benediction, and said:

"If we ground her, you're swimming ahead to sound the channel yourself. Every meter."

"Fair."

"And if there's nothing there—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "If there's nothing there but rock, we turn around and we never speak of the needle again."

"Deal."

Rell wrote in his log. Elodie watched him write it — *captain deviating from route against observer's formal objection* — and saw, beneath the neat handwriting, a man recording his own alibi. Fine. Let him build his paper fortress. Paper hadn't saved anyone on this river yet.

* * *

The side channel opened out of the fog the way a door opens in a dream: suddenly, silently, impossibly wide.

One moment the *Grace* was feeling her way along a fog wall at dead slow, Elodie at the wheel with the compass open on the binnacle beside her and the needle straining westward like a hound at a leash. The next, the gray simply parted, and there was water where water should not have been — a broad, deep, fast-flowing arm of the river sliding out from between cliffs that no chart acknowledged, running green-black and cold and heavy with glacial flour, pulling at the hull with a strength that made the wheel come alive in her hands.

"Soundings," she said, and Imani was already swinging the lead line, chanting depths like verses.

"Six fathoms. Seven. Nine — deep water, Captain, deep and getting deeper. This channel's older than the main one. This channel's the *real* one."

Tomas stood at her shoulder, silent, watching the cliffs slide past. They were close enough to touch in places, sheer granite streaked white with mineral stain, and cut into them — Elodie saw it and made herself keep looking — cut into them were marks. Regular marks. Squares and lines, too deliberate to be natural, too high on the rock face to be anything recent, half-swallowed by lichen and centuries of high-water stain. Doorways, maybe. Windows. The architecture of people who had built their lives against this stone and been forgotten so completely that even the stone had stopped telling.

Nobody spoke about it. But she saw Tomas see it, and Imani cross herself with two fingers, quick and old-fashioned, and Rell — Rell stood very still at the stern rail with his notebook open and his pen not moving, and for once he wasn't smiling, and for once Elodie didn't hate him for it.

The compass needle held true. West by northwest, deeper in, and the fog behind them closed over the entrance to the main channel like water closing over a dropped stone.

By late afternoon the channel bent south, and the air changed. Colder. Sweeter. Under the mineral smell of the glacier water came something else — woodsmoke, faint and impossible, and beneath the woodsmoke something older, the smell of a place that had been sealed a long time and recently opened. Imani lifted her head like a deer.

Ahead, the cliffs fell away. The fog thinned to rags. And the compass, which had pointed faithfully, stubbornly, dishonestly-according-to-every-map-in-existence for eighteen hours, finally went still — dead still, trembling with a kind of satisfaction, pointing at the place where the valley opened before them like a held breath.

Elodie raised her binoculars.

On the far shore, among pale shapes that might have been ruins or might have been buildings — whole, intact, impossible — there were tents. Canvas and rope and cook-smoke rising straight in the windless cold. And moving among the tents, small with distance but unmistakable, human figures, one of whom had apparently seen them first, because a figure climbed onto a rock at the water's edge and stood there, arms crossed, waiting, as though they had been expected for a very long time.

"Captain," Tomas said hoarsely. "Tell me you see it."

"I see it."

"Is that—"

"The Meridian camp." Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Alive."

Behind her, she heard Rell's pen finally start moving again, scratching fast, and she thought: *write it all down, observer. Write down that her brother's compass knew the way when your expensive maps didn't.*

The figure on the rock raised one hand — not waving, Elodie realized. Gesturing. *Come in.* The way you greet guests you set a place for.

The needle trembled once against the glass, and rested.
