## 7. Voices in the Calving Ice

Imani had a rule about rivers: the water told you everything if you listened long enough. It told you where the sandbars shifted overnight, where deadfall lurked just under the surface wearing a skin of calm, where a channel had decided to become something else entirely. She'd learned it from her grandmother on the delta runs, learning to read brown water like scripture — every ripple a verse, every eddy a psalm with a warning folded inside it.

The river they were running now didn't speak that language.

It spoke something older, and it was learning to speak through the ice.

She heard it first at dusk, while she was coiling line on the aft deck and pretending not to watch Rell, who was pretending not to watch her. The sound came down the valley like a knock at a far door — a crack, then a pause, then two cracks closer together, then a long groan that seemed to come up through the hull rather than across the air.

Tomas appeared at the wheelhouse door with a mug of something that smelled like burnt chicory. "Glacier settling," he said. "They do that."

"They don't do *that*," Imani said.

The pattern came again. Crack. Crack-crack. Groan. A silence exactly as long as the first silence. Then it started over, note for note, like someone practicing a sentence until they got it right.

Tomas stood very still, his mug halfway to his mouth. Steam curled off it and bent sideways in the wind coming off the ice fields upstream. "Huh," he said finally, which from Tomas was a full paragraph of unease.

Elodie came out last, pulling her jacket on one-armed, her brother's compass hanging from a lanyard against her chest. Imani had watched her check it eleven times since noon. The needle sat steady now, pointing upriver into the dark toward the glacier's white shoulder, patient as a dog waiting at a door.

"Count it," Elodie said quietly.

So Imani counted. Four beats, rest, four beats, rest. The rhythm held for six full cycles before the glacier broke pattern — a long cascading rumble somewhere deep in its throat, an avalanche of small ice shedding off some unseen face — and then, when the echo died, the rhythm resumed as though nothing had interrupted it.

"Water does this," Tomas said, but his voice had gone flat, the way it did when he was lying to himself. "Freeze-thaw. Thermal contraction. There's a whole—" He stopped. Started again. "There's science."

"There is," Imani agreed. "And none of it repeats itself in fours."

Nobody said anything after that. The four of them stood on the deck listening to the glacier practice its sentence, and Imani felt the hair on her arms rise, not from cold, though the cold was real enough to bite, but from the particular feeling she'd only had twice before in her life: once in a church where the congregation had gone silent mid-hymn, and once in a flooded house in the lowlands where she'd sworn she heard breathing under the floorboards and never went back to find out.

The river was watched. She'd written that in her head three days ago, back in the fog. Now she amended it: the river wasn't watched. The river was *watching back*.

* * *

She refused to sleep aboard.

She said it plainly, over dinner, standing in the galley doorway with her bedroll already rolled and strapped, her boots already on. "I'll take the bank. There's a gravel shelf fifty meters up, above the flood line, treed enough to break wind."

"It's minus six," Tomas said.

"Minus eight by midnight." She'd been reading the sky all evening; the stars had that hard, polished look they got before a clear freeze. "I've slept colder."

"Imani." Elodie set down her fork. "The boat's the safe place. Steel hull, stove, three people within shouting distance—"

"That's why I'm not sleeping here." Imani kept her voice level. This was the part she'd rehearsed on the aft deck while coiling line, and it still tasted bad in her mouth. "Something on this boat isn't right, and until I know what it is, I'm not going to lie unconscious next to it."

Rell looked up from their ration tin with that easy smile, the one that arrived a half-second too fast every time, like a card trick performed by someone who'd practiced the shuffle but not the patter. "You think one of us is the problem?"

"I think somebody aboard has been asking questions about cargo manifests nobody needs answered twice. I think somebody's radio work takes longer than radio work takes. And I think," and here Imani let herself look directly at Rell, because there was no kind way to say it and she'd never been good at cruelty dressed as tact, "somebody smiles like they're being paid for it."

The smile stayed on Rell's face, but something behind it moved — a shutter, a weight shifting from one foot to the other. "I'm Syndicate observer. It's my job to ask questions."

"Then answer one. What did you record last night?"

Silence. Tomas's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. Elodie went very still in the way she did, Imani had noticed, when she was deciding whether to be angry or afraid.

"I don't know what you mean," Rell said.

"The handheld recorder. You keep it in your coat's left pocket. You took it out at 2300 hours and again at 0400, both times on deck, both times facing upriver." Imani shrugged. "I sleep light. I wake easy. And I count things. It's what I do."

"You sleep light," Rell repeated, "and yet you're leaving the boat."

"I sleep light *here*. On land I sleep like the dead, because the dead don't lie to me."

That landed harder than she'd intended. She saw it hit Elodie — the flicker, the flinch — everyone aboard knew about her brother by now, or knew the shape of him without the name. Imani almost apologized. She didn't. Apologies were for accidents, and she'd meant every word.

Tomas cleared his throat. "Nobody's recording anything on my boat without telling me."

"Your boat," Rell said mildly, "is leased against a contract that specifies observation."

"My boat," Tomas said, rising, "has a woodstove and a winch, and I have thrown larger men than you off smaller boats."

"Enough." Elodie's voice cut clean. She looked at Rell for a long moment, and Imani watched her do the arithmetic she could see behind her eyes — the debt, the contract, the calving season ticking down like a fuse. "Rell records whatever Rell wants. That's the deal we signed. But Rell plays it back in front of all of us tomorrow morning. Every minute. That's the deal we're signing now."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow morning you can explain to Cass why the salvage crew lost a day arguing about it." Elodie picked her fork back up. "We're burning daylight either way. Imani, take the radio handset. Check in at midnight and 0400. If you don't, we come looking."

Imani nodded once. At the door she paused, because fairness mattered even when suspicion didn't leave room for much else. "For what it's worth — I hope I'm wrong. I've been wrong before. Once I accused a fisherman of stealing nets that turned out to be eaten by otters."

"What happened to the fisherman?" Rell asked.

"He wouldn't speak to me for two years." She pulled her hood up. "Worth knowing what it costs."

* * *

The gravel shelf held her well enough. She built no fire — fire drew eyes, and she'd had the feeling of eyes since the fog banks, days ago now, a pressure between her shoulder blades like a hand that hadn't yet decided whether to push. Instead she wedged herself into the roots of a leaning spruce, bedroll beneath her, radio handset wired to her belt, and she listened.

The glacier talked all night.

Not constantly. In movements. It would go quiet for an hour, the way a congregation goes quiet, and then begin again — those four-beat phrases, sometimes five, sometimes stretched so long between cracks that she lost the thread and had to start counting over. Around two in the morning it changed key. The cracks came sharper, higher, ringing off the valley walls, and underneath them she heard — or believed she heard, and she was honest enough with herself to hold both possibilities at once — a tonal quality, a rise and fall, that no thermal contraction had any business producing.

Her grandmother had a word for sounds like that. Not a word from any hymn book. An old word, delta-water word, for when the river spoke in a voice that wasn't the river's. *Mambo la maji*, the water's business. You didn't interpret it. You didn't answer it. You acknowledged it, you kept your hands visible, and you got out of its way.

At midnight she keyed the handset. "Aboard. All quiet. Glacier's singing."

Static, then Elodie's voice, low and rough with tiredness. "Singing how?"

"Like it's rehearsing." Imani hesitated. "Elodie. Your compass. Is it moving?"

A pause long enough that she thought the transmission had dropped. "Half a degree west since dinner. Maybe less. Could be my hand shaking."

"Could be."

"Get some sleep, Imani."

She slept in pieces, the way she always did in strange country, surfacing at every change in the ice's voice. Somewhere in the smallest hour she dreamed of her grandmother standing hip-deep in green delta water, holding up one finger, saying *listen* — and woke with her heart slamming because the glacier had gone completely, utterly silent, a silence so total she could hear her own blood in her ears and the tiny tick of frost forming on the spruce bark above her head.

Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty.

Then, faint and far and unmistakably rhythmic: crack, crack-crack, groan. Rest. Again. Rest. Again.

It hadn't stopped talking. It had stopped talking *to itself*.

* * *

Morning came up gray and glittering, rime furred on every branch, breath hanging like gauze. Imani broke camp fast and was back aboard before the kettle boiled, stamping ice off her boots, accepting coffee she didn't taste.

Rell was waiting on the aft deck with the recorder.

To their credit — and Imani was keeping a strict ledger of credits and debits these days — they didn't prevaricate. They set the device on the coil box, pressed play, and stood with their hands visible, palms out, while the recordings filled the cold morning air.

Most of it was what Imani expected: the glacier's voice, hour after hour, the four-beat phrases rendered tinny and strange through the little speaker. Timestamps murmured in Rell's flat careful voice. Wind. Hull creak. Once, Elodie's footsteps crossing the deck at 0310, pausing, retreating.

Then, at the 0400 entry, something else.

Underneath the glacier's rhythm — faint, buried, but there — a second rhythm. Metallic. Regular. Coming not from upriver but from *aboard*, from somewhere near the bow, a soft percussive tapping in groups of four. Tap. Tap-tap. Pause. Tap. Tap-tap. Pause. Perfectly matched to the ice.

Imani felt the bottom drop out of her stomach.

"Play that again," Tomas said. His voice had gone very quiet, which was worse than shouting.

Rell played it again. Three times. On the third, Elodie reached out and stopped the playback with one finger on the button, holding it there as though pinning something down.

"That's the anchor chain locker," Tomas said. "Nothing's in the chain locker. I checked it myself before we cast off. Nothing's *been* in the chain locker since I bought this boat."

"Someone was tapping along with the glacier," Imani said. "In the dark. At four in the morning. While the rest of us slept." She turned to Rell. "Where were you?"

"In my bunk." No smile this time. For the first time since they'd met, Rell's face was simply a face, stripped of performance, and what was underneath looked young and frightened and genuinely at sea. "I heard the tapping too. I thought — I assumed it was the hull. Ice in the water, striking the plating. Thermal stuff. Tomas said there was science."

"There is science," Imani said. "Open the locker."

They opened it together, all four of them crowded at the bow hatch with the morning fog breathing around them. The chain lay in its coils, rust-brown and ordinary. Empty space beneath. A smell of bilgewater and old paint.

And scratched into the inside of the hatch cover, low down where only someone crouched in the dark would put it, fresh enough that the raw metal still shone:

Four short lines. Four marks. Then a gap. Then four more.

Counting the same way the glacier counted. Counting the way someone counts nights, or messages, or the beats of a song they're trying to remember.

Nobody spoke. Upstream, muffled by distance and fog, the glacier cracked out its phrase — tap, tap-tap, pause — patient, unhurried, like something that knew it would be answered eventually.

Imani looked at Elodie. Elodie's hand had gone to the compass at her throat, and Imani saw, with a clarity that settled over her like frost, that the needle was no longer pointing upriver.

It was pointing at the bow of the boat.
