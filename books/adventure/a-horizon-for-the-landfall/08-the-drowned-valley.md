## 8. The Drowned Valley

The compass had been wrong for six hours, and Elodie had stopped pretending it wasn't.

She stood at the wheel with the brass instrument open on the binnacle beside her, its needle canted a stubborn fifteen degrees off true, quivering toward a wall of fog that no chart in Tomas's cabin acknowledged. Behind that fog, according to every map ever drawn of the upper Vessel, there was nothing but rock and ice and a river that simply ended. The needle disagreed. The needle had been disagreeing since dawn, and every mile they'd followed it, the pull had grown stronger — not drifting anymore but *leaning*, like a dog at the end of a leash.

"You're sure about this." Tomas stood at her shoulder, arms crossed over his oilskin, not a question so much as a man putting his objection on the record one last time.

"No," Elodie said. "But it hasn't lied to us yet."

"It hasn't told us anything yet. That's not the same."

"It's close enough."

Tomas grunted and went forward to check the lines, which was his way of saying he'd stay. That was the arithmetic of this crew so far: everyone stayed, and nobody said why. Imani crouched amidships with her hand flat against the deck plating, reading vibration the way she read the river — by touch, by feel, by some private scripture she'd never offered to translate. Rell stood at the rail with his hands folded behind his back, smiling at the fog the way he smiled at everything, and Elodie had learned in three days that the smile meant nothing except that Rell was awake.

The river narrowed. That was the first thing. The cliffs came down to meet the water in gray shoulders, and the current picked up its pace as if the river itself were being swallowed. The fog thickened until the bow was a rumor and the stern a memory, and Elodie ran on sound alone — the slap of water against hull, the mutter of the engine, Imani calling depths in a low voice like a woman counting prayer beads.

"Shallows to port. Rock, starboard, ten meters. Channel holds center." Pause. "Channel *holds* center."

The last part was new. Rivers this far north didn't hold center. Rivers this far north wandered, split, died in braided shallows. But the water under them ran deep and smooth and strangely warm, and when Elodie trailed her fingers over the side she pulled them back fast, because the temperature was wrong — not glacial melt, not snowmelt, but something older, something that had been sitting underground a long time and hadn't forgotten it.

The fog broke all at once, the way a curtain breaks.

There was no gradual thinning, no softening of gray into gray. One moment the world was milk, and the next it was torn open, and Elodie was looking at a valley that did not exist.

It ran north-south, maybe three kilometers wide, walled by cliffs sheared so clean they might have been cut with a wire. The river widened into a long lake at its floor, and the lake was the color of old bottle glass, green-black and utterly still, holding the reflection of the glacier at its head like a coin held under the tongue. The glacier itself — that was the thing that stopped her breath. It had retreated, was still retreating, a wall of blue-white ice a kilometer back from the waterline, and the land it had uncovered lay bare below it in terraces, dark soil and pale stone, steaming faintly in the cold air as if the ground itself were exhaling after a long held breath.

And on the terraces, there were buildings.

Not ruins. Elodie had seen ruins — she'd grown up on a coast that manufactured them, storm-broken piers, the ribcages of fish plants. These were not that. These were walls with their roofs on. Whole structures of dark timber and fitted stone, two and three stories, standing in rows along streets that were still streets, cobbled, edged with curbing. There were bridges over a stream that ran down to the lake. There were lamps on posts, their glass intact. There was a bell tower, and hanging in it, a bell.

"Imani," Elodie said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Tell me you're seeing this."

"I'm seeing it." Imani had come to stand at the rail, and her hand, the one that read the river, was pressed flat against her own sternum now. "I'm seeing it and I don't like it."

"Why not? It's—"

"Because nothing dies that clean." Imani's eyes moved along the shoreline, the streets, the bell tower, and Elodie watched her catalog it all with the same flat attention she gave to water. "Towns drown in mud or burn or fall down slow. This one just — stopped. Like somebody set the table and turned off the lights and walked out between one minute and the next."

Rell said nothing. When Elodie glanced over, he was leaning on the rail with his smile gone — actually gone, for the first time she'd witnessed — and his face beneath it was young and frightened and doing arithmetic he clearly didn't like the answer to.

The compass on the binnacle was spinning. Slowly, lazily, like a thing that had finished its work and no longer cared where north was. Then it stopped, needle steady, pointing straight down the axis of the valley at the glacier's blue wall, and stayed there.

"Dock," Tomas said from the bow. "There's a dock."

There was. A stone quay ran along the near shore, and moored at it, riding at lines that had clearly been set by living hands, was a raft — no, two rafts, and a survey boat, and a cluster of tents pitched on the cobbles above the waterline, and smoke rising from a stove pipe, thin and gray and unmistakably human.

Elodie's relief lasted the length of one heartbeat. Then the rest of it arrived.

*Alive.* The Meridian expedition, silent three weeks, was alive, and camped in a town that had no business existing, and had not, in three weeks, managed to send one word downriver. Alive and *staying*. She thought of the logbook in the supply raft, the entry that ended mid-sentence — *the ice is not what we thought, it is not ice at all, it is* — and the relief curdled in her chest and turned into something colder.

"Nobody goes ashore armed," she said. "Tomas, keep the engine warm. Imani, you're with me. Rell—"

"I know," Rell said. "I'm the hostage."

"I was going to say you're the negotiator. You smile better than any of us."

"Same job," Rell said, and there was something under the joke this time, something that sounded almost like a warning.

* * *

The water in the shallows was so clear it was disorienting, and Elodie could see the cobbles of a drowned street running out under the keel, continuing the town into the lake as though the waterline were an arbitrary administrative decision rather than a catastrophe. She kept looking at it and kept making herself stop.

The quay was old stone, but the bollards were new — steel eyes bolted down recently, with modern lines through them. The Meridian had made themselves at home. That was its own kind of message.

They were waiting for them. That was the second thing Elodie understood, and she understood it before anyone spoke. There were perhaps a dozen figures at the top of the quay steps, in expedition gear gone gray with wear, and none of them were surprised. No one was running down to the water shouting questions. No one had a rifle. They stood in a loose half-circle with the patience of people who had been expecting a boat for days, and at their center was a broad-shouldered woman in her sixties with a silver braid and a face like a cliff — weathered, vertical, impossible to get a purchase on.

"Dr. Sefa," Elodie said, before the woman could speak. "You're a hard crew to find."

"Dr. Zhou." Sefa's voice was low and even, with an accent Elodie couldn't place and didn't try to. "You're a hard crew to lose. We heard your engine two days ago. The valley carries sound strangely — it arrived before you did, in a sense." She came forward and offered her hand, and her grip was dry and strong. "Welcome. We'd have lit the lamps, but we've been rationing fuel."

"You knew we were coming."

"I knew *someone* would come. Eventually. The Syndicate's salvage notice was posted in every port office on the river." Something moved at the corner of Sefa's mouth — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "We assumed they'd send whoever was most desperate."

Elodie felt Rell shift beside her, felt the smile she couldn't see but knew was there. "That would be us," Rell said pleasantly.

"Then you'll want to rest before you argue with me. Everyone does." Sefa turned and gestured up the street, and the half-circle of her crew parted to let them through, and that was when Elodie got her first real look at the town from inside it.

The cobbles under her boots were worn smooth in two parallel lines, wheel ruts, centuries of cart traffic pressed into stone. Doorways stood open on either side. Through one she saw a kitchen — pots on hooks, a table, bowls. Through another, a workshop with tools on a wall, each one hanging in its outline painted on the board behind it. A child's coat on a peg. A ledger on a counter in what had been a shop, its pages stiff and pale but legible, columns of figures in a script that matched nothing Elodie had ever seen and yet was obviously, unmistakably, *bookkeeping*.

"Four hundred years," Sefa said, walking beside her without looking at her, the way a guide talks when they've given this tour to themselves many times. "Give or take a generation. The ice took the valley whole. Cold, dry, no light, no oxygen worth mentioning. Nothing rotted. Nothing rusted. The glacier didn't crush it — it sealed it, the way you'd press a flower." She stopped at the mouth of a street that climbed toward the bell tower. "Every archive in the world says no one lived here. Every map says this valley doesn't exist. And yet."

"And yet you've been here three weeks and told no one," Elodie said.

"Yes."

"People are looking for you. Families."

"I know." For the first time, Sefa's evenness cracked, just at the seam — a flicker of something that might have been grief or might have been guilt, gone too fast to name. "I've written the letters. They're in my tent. I've written twenty-two of them, and I will send them, I swear to you, when it's safe to send them. But the moment I radio a port, the moment I say the word *valley* into a transmitter, this place has a location. And everything that has a location can be sold."

"Sold to who?"

"Whoever pays." Sefa looked at her then, straight on, and her eyes were the flat gray of the glacier lake. "You took a salvage contract from the Vantara Syndicate, Dr. Zhou. You know better than most what gets bought."

Elodie didn't answer. There was no answer that didn't hand over something.

They walked up through the streets, Imani silent at her shoulder, Rell three steps back and very quiet, and the town opened around them like a book someone had left face-down. A fountain, dry, with a stone bird on its rim. A schoolhouse — she knew it was a schoolhouse because of the small desks, the small chairs, the slate board still bearing chalk ghosts of a lesson no one had finished. In the door of one house a woman's boot stood alone, upright, as if its owner had stepped out of it and out of the world in the same motion.

Imani touched Elodie's elbow. "You feel that?" she murmured.

"Feel what?"

"Nothing." Imani's voice was barely breath. "No birds. No rats. Nothing living in this whole town but us and them. Four hundred years and not so much as moss on the north walls." She shook her head slowly. "This place isn't preserved, Elodie. It's *kept*."

Elodie wanted to say that was superstition, that cold and dry explained everything. She wanted to, and she couldn't, because her brother's compass was in her pocket, and it was warm. Not sun-warm, not pocket-warm. Warm the way a hand is warm. She had been carrying it for eleven years and it had never once been warm, and she had decided, somewhere over the last six hours of following it into fog, that she was not going to think about that until she was safely downstream.

The camp occupied the square below the bell tower. Tents, a galley tarp, instrument cases stacked and lashed — and, at the center, a long table under an awning, on which lay the reason for everything: cores. Cylinders of ice and sediment in labeled sleeves, dozens of them, and beside them photographs, and beside those, notebooks in a dozen hands.

Sefa stopped at the table and rested her palm on one of the cores the way another woman might rest a hand on a sleeping child's back.

"The ice is layered," she said. "Everything is layered. The valley floor, the lake bed, the glacier — it all records. And the record doesn't match the world's records. There was a civilization here that traded up and down this river for eight hundred years, and it is in no archive, no genealogy, no oral tradition we can find. It was removed. Not destroyed — *removed*, deliberately, thoroughly, from every memory that survived it. Except this one." She tapped the core. "The ice kept what everyone else agreed to forget."

"Why would anyone do that?" Imani asked. "Erase a whole people and leave the town standing?"

"Because the town was never the danger." Sefa's hand closed around the core. "The *memory* was. We've spent three weeks reading what's in these buildings, and I will tell you now, before you find it yourself, because you will find it — there are things in that schoolhouse and that ledger hall that certain people would burn the world to keep forgotten, and other people would burn the world to own. We stopped answering the radio because we realized that the only thing protecting this valley is that nobody knows where it is." She looked at each of them in turn — Elodie, Imani, and last, longest, at Rell. "So. Before we go any further. Who did the Syndicate send you to find, and what exactly were you told to bring back?"

The square was very quiet. Somewhere up the valley, deep in the glacier, ice cracked — a long, resonant report that rolled down the cliffs and across the lake and came back changed, almost like a word, almost like a name being called. Imani's hand found the rail of the awning and gripped it.

Elodie took a breath and told the truth, because she was suddenly, desperately tired of every other currency. "We were told to find you and confirm salvage. That's all the contract said. But the man who wrote it doesn't send observers unless he wants more than confirmation." She tilted her head, without looking, toward Rell. "And we've got one aboard."

Rell's smile appeared — that easy, practiced thing — and then, for the second time that day, it failed him. He looked at Sefa, at the cores, at the open door of the schoolhouse with its small empty chairs, and when he spoke, his voice had no performance in it at all.

"You should know," he said, "that we weren't the only ones looking. There's a gunboat on the river. It left port the day after we did."

Sefa did not move. But every one of her crew did — a small, terrible stillness passing through them in the wrong direction, like wind bending wheat the wrong way.

"Then you'd better tell me," Sefa said quietly, "everything."
