## 9. What It Found Before Us

Sefa had given the same welcome to every visitor the valley had ever received, which was to say: none. Until now.

She stood at the edge of the Meridian camp with her hands loose at her sides and her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out, watching the new boat's crew come ashore. Four of them. A tall woman with storm-chaser's eyes who moved like she was cataloguing exits. An old pilot who touched the gunwale of his skiff the way a man touches a coffin lid. A deckhand woman who kept glancing at the glacier with an expression Sefa recognized from her own mirror — reverence laced with fear. And a fourth, younger, neat, whose smile arrived a half-second after his eyes finished calculating.

Sefa had spent thirty years learning to read people the way other scientists read strata. This one was a fault line.

"Dr. Sefa," she said, extending her hand to the tall woman. "You found us faster than I expected."

"Elodie Zhou." The grip was dry, strong, brief. "Your radio's been dark three weeks. Your syndicate backers hired us to pull you out. Or your bodies. The contract wasn't specific."

"Hired you." Sefa let the word sit in the cold air between them. "The Vantara Syndicate sent you."

"They own the debt on my boat. Same thing."

Behind Elodie Zhou, the one called Rell was already turning in a slow circle, and Sefa watched him do it — watched the way his gaze snagged on the exposed rooftops down-valley, on the black timber frames rising from the gravel like ribs, on the survey stakes and sample cases and the long tarpaulin shelter where her team worked by lamplight through the short grey days. He was counting. She knew counting when she saw it.

"You should come inside," she said. "All of you. There are things you need to understand before anyone makes a decision they can't take back."

* * *

The shelter had been the Meridian's mess tent once. Now it was something closer to a chapel, though Sefa would never have used that word aloud. Lamplight fell across tables crowded with the valley's gifts: ceramic shards glazed in colors no kiln this far north should have produced; a child's wooden top, its paint still bright under two thousand years of ice; bundles of birch-bark scrolls flattened between glass plates; and the thing her team called simply the Wall — a slab of packed earth and reed plaster lifted whole from a dwelling, its surface incised with tens of thousands of tiny characters in a script that matched nothing in any archive on earth.

She watched the newcomers enter. Watched the deckhand — Imani, someone had said — stop dead three steps in, her hand going to her collarbone. Watched Tomas the pilot remove his cap without seeming to decide to. Watched Rell's eyes go straight past the artifacts to the locked instrument cases at the back, because of course they did.

"Sit," Sefa said. "Please. What I'm about to tell you took my crew eleven days to believe, and they had the advantage of being here."

They sat. Elodie Zhou perched on the edge of a bench like a woman who expected the floor to be pulled away. Sefa poured tea from the dented kettle — real tea, hoarded, offered deliberately, because hospitality was also a kind of argument — and began.

"The valley is forty kilometers long," she said. "The ice began retreating from it nine years ago. Faster every season. What it's giving back—" she gestured at the tables, at the Wall, at everything— "is a civilization. Whole towns. Roads. Irrigation channels still holding their shape. We've mapped eleven settlement clusters so far and we've barely started."

"A lost tribe," Tomas said. "Happens. Ice keeps things."

"No." Sefa shook her head slowly. "A lost *people*. Not a village that wandered off the map. A culture. Tens of thousands of lives, centuries of them — writing, music, law, trade goods from four hundred kilometers south. And here is what has kept me awake since the second week." She leaned forward. "There is no record of them. Anywhere. Not in the imperial archives downstream. Not in the monastic libraries. Not in the oral traditions of the river peoples, and I have interviewed elders in eleven communities. Every culture along this water remembers floods, wars, kings, plagues. None of them remember *this*."

Imani spoke for the first time, her voice low. "That's not possible. People remember. Even when nobody writes it down, somebody remembers."

"That's what I believed too." Sefa held the old woman's gaze. "So we looked harder. And we found something worse than absence." She rose and crossed to the Wall, laying her palm flat against the incised surface. "We found erasure. Look at the base of this wall section. Under the plaster there's an older layer — same script, same hands, but chiseled out. Deliberately. Not weathered. *Chiseled.* And beneath the floor of the great house in the third cluster, we found a cache of these scrolls, buried face-down, stacked, and sealed under a curse-mark we've seen repeated at every site. Someone didn't just lose this civilization. Someone worked very hard, over a very long time, to make sure it stayed lost."

The silence in the tent had weight. Outside, the glacier ticked and groaned, the sound Sefa had stopped hearing weeks ago and now heard again through the newcomers' faces.

"So the valley remembers," Elodie Zhou said slowly, "because everyone else was made to forget."

"Yes. And now the ice is undoing their work." Sefa laughed, but there was no humor in it. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? In ten years, maybe fifteen, the retreat will expose all of it to satellite survey anyway. But right now, today, there is a window — narrow, closing — in which what happens to this place gets decided by the handful of people standing in this tent. That window is why my crew went silent."

Rell shifted. "Went silent how, exactly?"

Sefa turned to him and gave him the full weight of her attention, the way she'd give it to a core sample that had come up wrong. "Deliberately," she said. "I killed our transponder myself. Day nineteen. Before that, we reported only partial findings — geological survey, glacial monitoring, nothing about the settlements. On day nineteen I understood enough to know what would happen if the full picture reached certain ears, and I made a choice." She let the pause stretch. "I know what you're thinking, Mr. Rell. You're thinking: she's confessing to me. Why? Because you're Syndicate, and I want you to carry the confession back whether you choose to or not. Consider it insurance."

For the first time since coming ashore, Rell's smile fell off his face entirely. It was, Sefa thought, an improvement.

* * *

She walked them down-valley in the flat grey afternoon light, because she had learned that telling was not enough — people had to stand inside it.

The path followed the old riverbed, and the settlement rose around them gradually, the way truth rises: first a corner of wall, then a doorway, then a street. Houses of black timber and white plaster, their roofs long collapsed but their walls standing shoulder-high, doorposts carved with spirals. A granary with its bins still holding carbonized grain. A well. A shrine with offerings still arranged on its ledge — dried flowers gone to lace, a cup, a small clay figure of a bear wearing what might have been a crown.

Imani knelt at the shrine and did not touch anything. Her lips moved. Sefa pretended not to see, and approved of it.

"We think the final abandonment came fast," Sefa said as they walked. "No mass graves. No burn layer. Doors left unlatched. Whatever ended them, they walked out expecting to come back." She stopped at the crest of a low rise and swept her arm down the length of the valley. "Four hundred dwellings visible from here. Behind that ridge, a paved road runs eight kilometers to a bridge that still carries its own weight. Somewhere under the ice tongue at the head of the valley, we believe, is the city center. The archive itself. Their library, if they had one — and the script says they did."

"And the Syndicate," Elodie Zhou said. Her voice was careful, flat, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "Buyers who erase history for profit. That's a real market?"

"It's the oldest market there is." Sefa faced her. "Conquest has always had two phases. First you kill the people. Then you kill the fact that there were ever people. Ask anyone whose grandparents were boarded into schools to have their language beaten out of them. Ask why certain archives burn and others miraculously survive. Provenance laundering. Land claims dissolved. Treaties invalidated because the people who signed them can be recast as never having existed. A find like this isn't just valuable, Ms. Zhou. It's *dangerous*, and dangerous things sell to exactly the buyers you're imagining." She nodded back toward the camp, toward the Wall. "Every character on that plaster contradicts somebody's official history. Do you see? This valley isn't an asset. It's an accusation. And accusations get bought and buried like anything else."

Tomas spat, deliberately, into the old riverbed. "Then we burn it ourselves," he growled. "Better us than them."

"No." Sefa said it more sharply than she meant to, and softened. "No. Destruction is just erasure with better manners. I won't be the second hand that chisels the wall." She looked down the valley, at the low sun striking the glacier face until it burned white-gold, calving-season light, the light of endings. "My grandmother taught school in a country that decided her language was a mistake. They took the books. They couldn't take what she carried in her head, and on the night she died she recited poems to me in a tongue I'll never speak, and those poems exist now only because she spent them on me before the dark took them." Her throat tightened; she let it. "That is what I believe. Memory survives by being carried. Not archived. Archived memory belongs to whoever owns the archive. Carried memory belongs to the people who love it."

"So what's the plan, Doctor?" Elodie Zhou asked. "Because I've got a Syndicate contract in my pocket and a collector who expects a delivery, and 'carried memory' doesn't weigh much on a scale."

"The plan," Sefa said, "is that you and I are going to have a much harder conversation than this one. But not tonight. Tonight you eat, you sleep, and you look at what we've found with your own eyes until you understand that some choices don't come with a clean side." She turned back toward camp, then paused. "One more thing. Your observer — Rell. He recorded our conversation. Fob in his left pocket, running since the moment he stepped aboard your boat, if I had to guess."

She felt rather than saw the stillness pass through the group.

"I'm not telling you to do anything about it," Sefa said quietly. "I'm telling you so that whatever you decide, you decide with open eyes. That's the only kind of decision this valley respects."

* * *

Night came down blue and enormous. Sefa sat outside her tent with the last of the tea, listening to the glacier work — that deep rhythmic cracking, almost speech, that had driven two of her own researchers to sleep ashore in tents rather than aboard the Meridian. Across the fire, the newcomers' voices murmured. She caught fragments. *Debt. Contract. What Cass does to people who default.* The tall woman's silhouette, motionless, facing the ice.

Sefa had made her choice nineteen days ago and had not slept properly since, and she did not regret it, and she was afraid, and all of these were true at once. Conviction, she had learned, was just caution that had finally picked a side.

The young researcher, Oyelaran, came and sat beside her, hugging his knees. "They'll vote," he whispered. "Zhou's crew and ours. Expose it or seal it. I heard them talking."

"I know."

"And if they vote wrong?"

Sefa watched the glacier face glow faintly under the stars, a white line like a held breath. "Then we learn what my grandmother's poems were worth," she said. "And I pray we never find out."

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of an engine that belonged to no boat of theirs.
