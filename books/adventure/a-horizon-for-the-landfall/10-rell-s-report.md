## 10. Rell's Report

Rell had always been good at waiting. It was the first thing they taught you in observer training — patience as a professional discipline, the ability to sit in a room for six days and let other people forget you were furniture. He'd been furniture on a dozen contracts before this one. Furniture didn't get knifed. Furniture got paid.

But he'd never waited anywhere like this.

The valley had gotten under his skin the way damp gets into a hull — slowly, invisibly, until one day the whole structure groans. Three days now since they'd come through the hidden channel behind Elodie's drifting compass, three days of camping among buildings that predated every archive Rell had ever been paid to read. The Meridian crew moved among them like caretakers. Dr. Sefa spoke about memory layered in ice the way bankers spoke about interest: compounding, patient, inevitable.

And Rell had spent all three days doing what he was built to do. Observing. Recording. Transmitting.

He told himself it was just the job. That was the trick of it, the thing that made observers possible at all: you never called it betrayal while you were doing it. You called it reporting. You called it due diligence. You called it a man with a handheld unit crouched behind a collapsed granary at two in the morning, keying coordinates to a relay buoy he'd seeded downstream on the second night, when everyone else slept and Imani's superstitious dread kept her awake on deck where she couldn't see him.

The message was short. Coordinates. Depth of the channel. Estimate of the settlement's extent. A note on the Meridian's condition — *crew alive, cooperative, led by Sefa, ideologically committed to concealment*. And then the line Cass had specifically requested, the line Rell had typed with his jaw tight:

*Site viable for extraction. Recommend early survey team before calving.*

He pressed send. The relay chirped once, a sound swallowed instantly by the glacier's slow conversation with itself. Up on the icefield, something cracked — that rhythmic, almost syllabic breaking Imani refused to sleep near — and Rell flinched hard enough to nearly drop the unit.

*Get a grip,* he told himself. *You're the predator here. Remember that.*

Except he wasn't sure anymore that he was.

* * *

He almost made it back to his bedroll.

Almost. The path from the granary wound between two structures whose purpose nobody had determined yet — low stone walls, doorways sized for people slightly shorter than anyone alive — and Elodie Zhou was standing in the gap between them, arms crossed, backlit by the pale pre-dawn glow off the ice.

"Evening stroll?" she said.

Rell's mind did what it was trained to do: it reached for the smooth answer, the easy smile, the deflection dressed as charm. *Couldn't sleep. This place gives me the creeps.* Something light. Something that made him furniture again.

What came out instead, because he was tired down to the marrow and because the valley had gotten into him, was: "How long have you known?"

"Since the second night." She stepped forward. In her right hand, loose and casual, was the filleting knife she used on the fish Tomas caught — a long blade, river-worn handle, very sharp. "Your bunk smells like salt water after we've been in fresh current for a week. You've been rinsing something. And you walk different when you think nobody's watching. Lighter steps going out than coming back."

"You've been counting my footsteps."

"I count everything. It's how I stay alive." She tilted her head toward the granary. "Relay buoy?"

He said nothing, which was an answer.

"Here's what happens now," Elodie said, conversational, terrible. "You're going to walk with me to the boat. We're going to have a conversation below decks where Sefa can't hear us and Imani won't start praying. And if at any point I believe you're reaching for that transmitter, or for anything else, I'm going to use this." She lifted the knife slightly, an apology in the gesture. "I don't want to. But I've got eleven people up here whose lives are currently sitting in your pocket, and I like them more than I like being polite."

Rell looked at her — really looked, the way he'd been trained to look at assets and marks, cataloguing threat vectors — and found, to his genuine surprise, that he wasn't afraid of the knife.

He was afraid of everything else.

"All right," he said.

* * *

The hold of Tomas's boat smelled of diesel, rope tar, and the ballast stones they'd taken aboard at the last port. Elodie shut the hatch and sat on a crate across from him, knife across her knees, lamp turned low.

"Talk," she said. "All of it. And understand that I'll know if you're shaping it."

So Rell talked. And because there was no way to shape it — because the truth was a single ugly block with no seams — it came out whole.

"The Vantara Syndicate doesn't salvage ships," he said. "That's the cover. What they actually do is acquisition. Historical acquisition. There are buyers — I've never met them, I've seen encrypted payment trails only — who pay extraordinary sums for things that shouldn't exist. Sites that were erased. Records that were suppressed. Whole histories that somebody, somewhere, paid to make disappear." He rubbed his face. His hands wouldn't quite steady. "A drowned valley that isn't on any chart, preserving a civilization that isn't in any archive? That's not a discovery to these people, Elodie. That's inventory. And inventory gets moved."

"Moved where?"

"Private collections. Or worse — destroyed. Sometimes the buyers don't want the artifacts. They want the *absence* maintained. If a site proves that a history was deliberately erased, it proves someone powerful did the erasing, and their descendants pay very well to keep that proof from surfacing." He laughed, a thin, awful sound. "Do you know what my job description actually says? Not 'observe.' Not 'report.' It says *facilitate transfer of assets*. Assets. Sefa is an asset. The tin boxes, the wall carvings, the bones in the burial houses — assets."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. The lamp flame leaned in some draft from the river.

"What did you send?"

"Coordinates. Channel depth. Confirmation the Meridian crew is alive and that Sefa intends concealment. And—" He stopped.

"And."

"A recommendation for an early survey team. Before calving closes the route." He met her eyes because looking away felt worse than the words. "Cass wanted a timeline. I gave her one. If that message went through clean, there could be a gunboat moving upriver already. Maybe two days out. Maybe less."

The silence that followed had weight, physical weight, like ballast shifting.

"They'll kill them," Elodie said finally. Not a question. "Sefa's people. They'll be witnesses."

"Or they'll be disappeared along with the site. Rockslide, flood, tragic calving-season accident. The Syndicate is very good at geography doing its work." Rell heard his own voice go flat and clinical and hated it — that was the trained voice, the voice that made the work bearable — and he tore it open deliberately. "I've facilitated four transfers before this one. Four. I told myself they were ruins, relics, dead things. Nobody hurt. Paperwork and provenance. But I watched Sefa yesterday showing that young researcher how the ice layers record drought years, famines, songs carved into lintels so children would know them — and I thought, *this is what I sell*. Not objects. Continuity. I sell the cutting of continuity." His breath shuddered. "I'm not telling you this to save myself. I know what I am standing in front of you holding a knife over me. I'm telling you because you asked for all of it, and because for the first time in six years, somebody asked."

Elodie studied him. He had the sudden, humiliating sense of being observed himself — catalogued, weighed, assessed for threat vectors — and understood that she'd been doing it since the day he boarded.

"Why tell me about the gunboat?" she said. "You could have stayed quiet. Let it happen. Played furniture until the end."

"Because I keyed that recommendation at two in the morning behind a granary," Rell said, "and I couldn't sleep afterward, and I realized I've never once lost sleep over a report before. That's either conscience or cowardice, and I genuinely don't know which. But I know that if I sit silent and those people die, I'll have chosen it. Actively. With my eyes open." He spread his hands. "So. Here I am. Knife-point. Ask me anything and I'll answer true, because lying to you would just be one more transfer."

* * *

She didn't lower the knife for a while. He respected that. Then she did — slid it into her belt, not away entirely, which he also respected — and leaned forward with her elbows on her knees.

"Here's your problem, Rell," she said. "Even if I believed every word — and I mostly do, which frightens me — you're still the reason there's a gunboat coming. Cass doesn't move without confirmation. Your message was the confirmation."

"I know."

"So 'I'm sorry' doesn't fix it. What fixes it is making your next transmission worth more than your last one." Her eyes narrowed, working the problem the way she worked weather — reading pressure, reading current. "Can you send a false report?"

His stomach dropped. "If Cass finds out—"

"Cass will find out exactly what we want her to find out. That's the entire point." Elodie stood, pacing the narrow hold, energy suddenly crackling off her. "You tell her the site is smaller than reported. Marginal. Flood-damaged, mostly silt, nothing worth a survey team's risk before calving. You tell her the Meridian crew is hostile, armed, and has already cached the portable artifacts somewhere unknown. You give her a reason to wait. To negotiate instead of seize. Because negotiation takes time, and time is the one thing the glacier is about to take away from everybody."

Rell stared at her. "You want me to run a counter-operation against my own employer."

"Former employer. As of the moment you press send." She stopped pacing and faced him. "Understand what I'm offering, because I'm only offering once. You defect — really defect, not halfway, not keeping a little insurance in your pocket — and you're one of us. Imani will take a week to stop glaring at you. Tomas will take a month. But you'll be crew. You betray us again, even once, even a little—" She touched the knife handle. "No third conversation."

It should have been a hard decision. Six years of training said it was suicide; the Syndicate did not forgive deserters, and the rivers were full of unmarked graves. But Rell found that the decision had already been made, sometime in the last three days, without consulting him — made in the granary shadows, made listening to the glacier speak, made watching a child's tin box pass hand to hand among people who wept for strangers dead five hundred years.

The terrifying part wasn't choosing defection. It was realizing he'd already defected, alone, in the dark, and that this was the first moment he'd stopped doing it by himself.

"There's one more thing," he said. "If I send the false report, Cass won't fully buy it. She's careful. She'll send the gunboat anyway — smaller, or slower, or just to verify. That's her pattern. Verification first, force second." He swallowed. "Which means whatever you're planning, you have maybe four days. Five if the river cooperates."

"Then we'd better spend them well." Elodie extended her hand. Hesitated. "One question first. Truthfully. Why did you sign on to this contract at all? Before any of this. Why does a man sell continuity?"

Rell thought about it honestly, because she'd earned honesty and because he was done curating himself.

"Because I grew up with nothing that lasted," he said. "Moved eleven times before I was fifteen. Nothing stayed, nothing stuck, no place remembered me. Working for people who erased things felt like — power over the thing that hurt me. If nothing lasts, nothing can be taken from you." He gave a broken half-smile. "Then I walked into a valley where everything lasted five hundred years, and I understood I'd been on the wrong side of the only war that matters."

Elodie took his hand. Her grip was calloused and strong.

"Welcome to the right side," she said. "Now show me exactly how your transmitter works, because in about an hour, you're going to lie to the most dangerous woman on this river — and you're going to be very, very good at it."

Above them, muffled through the deck planks, the glacier cracked its slow syllables — and beneath the cracking, faint but unmistakable, came the deep-throated growl of an engine far downstream, running dark against the current.
