## 11. Debt Collectors on the Water

Tomas heard the gunboat before he saw it — a low diesel thrum riding up the river under the wind, the kind of engine note that didn't belong to anyone honest.

He was on deck with a mug of coffee gone cold an hour ago, taking the dawn watch because sleep had stopped coming easily since they'd entered the valley. The fog was lifting off the water in slow rags. Behind him, up the hidden channel, the drowned settlement lay in its bowl of ice and stone, and somewhere in that bowl Dr. Sefa's people were still cataloguing a civilization no archive remembered. Ahead of him, downstream, where the channel bent around the shoulder of the moraine, something with too much horsepower was coming upriver against the current.

He set down the mug. He didn't spill it. Twenty-six years on the water taught you which sounds to spill coffee over and which ones to stand very still for.

This one, you stood still for.

The thrum deepened, flattened, went wide. Not one engine. Two, maybe three, running in formation. Recreational boats didn't run in formation. Survey boats didn't run in formation at dawn in calving season, when every sensible captain had already tied off and prayed.

"Rell," Tomas said quietly, to no one, because Rell's cabin was aft and locked and Tomas had stopped knocking two days ago. Then louder, into the companionway: "Elodie. Get up. We've got company."

* * *

They gathered on the foredeck in various states of dressed — Elodie pulling her jacket over yesterday's shirt, Imani barefoot and wide awake the way she always was before sunrise, as if sleep were a country she'd emigrated from. Sefa came down from the camp with two of her researchers, moving fast but not panicked, and Tomas noted that. Not panicked. People who'd been expecting this, or something like it.

"How far?" Elodie asked.

"Five, six kilometers," Tomas said. "Running slow. They're sounding the channel." He nodded at the water, where the faint pulse of a depth transducer carried through the hull like a knuckle tapping on wood. "They don't know exactly where the mouth is."

"They know it exists," Rell said.

Everyone turned. He stood at the top of the companionway ladder, and for once the easy smile was gone. In its place was something rawer — a man watching his own reflection come up the river at him.

"You told them," Imani said. It wasn't a question.

"I sent reports. Coordinates." Rell's voice was flat, stripped of performance. "That was the job. What I didn't send — what I couldn't send, because I didn't understand it until I got here — is that they wouldn't wait for negotiation. Cass doesn't do negotiation first. She does possession first, terms after." He looked at Elodie. "I tried to buy time in my last transmission. Told them the site needed assessment, that early approach risked losing everything to the calving. It bought us days. Maybe not this many."

Elodie's jaw worked. Tomas watched her decide — watched the calculation move behind her eyes like weather — and then she let it go, whatever she'd been about to say to Rell, because the thrum was getting louder and there wasn't time for justice this morning. Only for survival.

"Tomas," she said. "How long before they reach the bend?"

"At their pace? Forty minutes. Less if they stop being careful."

"And how long to seal the channel?"

Tomas looked upstream, at the narrow throat of rock where the valley opened onto the main river — the slot of gray stone and old slide debris that they'd threaded on the way in, barely wide enough for the boat, invisible from the main channel unless you knew the angle. The Meridian's survey charges were still cached on the south wall. He'd helped carry them. He'd noticed, even then, what a man like him noticed: that the overhanging shelf above the slot was cracked like old porcelain, held together by habit and permafrost.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "If everyone does exactly what I say and nobody asks why."

* * *

Sefa resisted for exactly ninety seconds.

"You'll bury the approach," she said. "Possibly the site itself. The lower terraces are unstable —"

"The lower terraces are unstable anyway," Tomas said, shouldering a charge case up the switchback trail. "In three days the glacier opens up and takes them regardless. This way we choose what falls."

"That's not your choice to make."

"No," Elodie said, climbing beside him, breath ragged in the thin cold air. "It's ours. All of ours. And I'm making it, Doctor, because in forty minutes there will be men down there with a gunboat and a mandate, and they won't be choosing anything. They'll just be taking."

Sefa fell silent. Her researchers kept climbing. Behind them all, carrying his share without complaint, face pale and hands steady, came Rell.

Tomas had decided something about Rell during the climb, though he hadn't said it aloud yet. A traitor who lied to save himself ran. A traitor who climbed back up into the hills with the people he'd betrayed, carrying explosives toward the thing he'd sold — that was a different animal. That was a man trying to outrun his own report. Tomas had been that man once, on a different river, with a different dead crew, and he recognized the stride.

The cache was where they'd left it, tarped under scree. Tomas knelt and began unpacking with hands that remembered this work better than they wanted to. Emulsion charges, det cord, a firing system older than half the crew. The Meridian had used them for seismic survey — reading the ice's memory layer by layer. Now the same tools would write the valley out of the map.

"I need the shelf assessed," he said. "Imani — you've got the best eye for stone. Where's the fracture plane?"

Imani crouched at the lip of the overlook, studying the slot below and the wall above. Wind pulled at her braids. Below them the river ran green-gray and silent, and beyond the bend, faint but growing, the diesel thrum.

"There." She pointed to a seam running diagonally across the overhang, pale as a scar. "And there. The frost's been working that crack for years. You put your charges along the upper seam, the whole face wants to come down into the slot. But—" she hesitated.

"But?"

"But if you drop the whole face, you dam the channel with rubble. River backs up. When it breaks through — and it will — it comes out as a flood wave. Downstream. Toward wherever that gunboat is sitting."

Tomas considered this. Considered, briefly, whether he cared.

"We're going to be downstream too," Elodie said quietly.

"Then we don't drop the whole face." Tomas started placing charges along the seam, spacing them by feel and by twenty-six years of instinct. "We drop enough to bring the loose stuff down — the scree, the slabs, the debris fan. Choke the slot, hide the mouth, make it look like what it should look like: a slide. Recent, natural, boring. Nobody runs a salvage contract past a fresh rockslide in calving season. They turn around. They report the channel's closed. Cass waits for the glacier to do the real work."

"And if she doesn't wait?" Rell asked.

Tomas pressed the last charge into its cradle of stone and looked at him. "Then she finds out what this river does to people who don't respect it."

* * *

The first collapse came while they were running the wire.

Not theirs — the glacier's. The sound rolled up the valley like a door slamming in the belly of the world, a deep concussion followed by the long white roar of ice letting go, and every bird on the slopes went up at once in a shrieking cloud. Tomas froze with the detonator box open in his hands, heart slamming, waiting to learn whether the mountain had decided to make his plan irrelevant.

Dust bloomed off the far glacier face, a mile upstream. Through the binoculars he could see the calving front shedding house-sized blocks into the meltwater lake, the water leaping up in gray fists.

"First blood," Imani murmured. "Season's opening."

"It's early," Sefa said, and there was fear in it now, real fear, the fear of a scientist watching her data arrive ahead of schedule. "The models said five days. Six. If the calving front is going now—"

"Then we're out of time everywhere." Elodie took the binoculars and swept downstream, toward the bend. And went rigid. "Tomas."

He took the glasses. The gunboat had come around the bend early — impatient, or confident, or both. It was a hard-chined patrol hull, Syndicate gray, twin mounts forward covered with canvas that fooled nobody, and it was no longer sounding the channel. It was running straight for the slot, wake fanning white behind it, close enough now that Tomas could see figures on the flybridge. One of them raised binoculars. Glass caught the sun.

"They've seen the mouth," he said.

"They've seen *something*," Elodie said. "Fire it, Tomas. Fire it now."

His thumb found the firing key. And here was the thing about Tomas, the thing he'd never told any crew, the thing he'd carried up this river like ballast: the last time he'd stood at a detonator with lives downstream, he'd hesitated. Four seconds. Five. Long enough for the flood to take the punt, take Okafor and Lindqvist and the Brandt twins, take the part of himself that ever trusted a river to wait. He'd spent eleven years telling himself the charge timing had been wrong, the geology wrong, the luck wrong. Anything but the truth, which was that his thumb had frozen.

He did not freeze now.

He fired.

* * *

The mountain came down the way Imani had read it — not all at once, but in a rolling cascade, the upper seam cracking, the slabs peeling free, the whole debris fan of decades sliding into the slot with a sound like the end of an argument. Dust boiled up white and enormous. The river below vanished under falling stone. The shockwave slapped across the water and hit the boat's hull like a fist, and somewhere behind them the glacier answered with another calving roar, as if the two events were talking to each other in a language older than names.

When the dust began to sift down, the slot was gone. In its place: a raw gray scar of fresh slide, tons of rubble fanned across the channel mouth, indistinguishable from a hundred other scars on a hundred other walls. The valley's entrance had become geology. Anonymous. Boring.

The gunboat was half a kilometer out when the slide finished. Tomas watched through the binoculars as it slewed, slowed, stopped. Figures moved on deck. The binoculars on the flybridge swept the new scar, the dust, the settling slopes.

For a long moment nothing happened. The river ran brown with silt around the toe of the slide. Somewhere upslope, stones were still ticking down.

Then the gunboat's engines changed pitch — and instead of turning away, it nudged forward, cautious, nosing toward the rubble field, and a figure in the bow unshipped a boathook and began probing the debris, looking for a gap, a channel, a way through.

"They're not stopping," Imani said.

"They're assessing," Rell corrected, voice tight. "Cass's crews assess. Give them an hour, they'll find the slide's shallow on the north side. Give them two, they'll have a path. She'll want eyes on the valley before she commits the full team, but she *will* commit. That's not a patrol boat anymore, that's a claim stake."

Elodie lowered her own binoculars slowly. Tomas watched her do the same arithmetic he was doing: hours until the slide was passable, days until the calving made the whole river a death trap, one boat between here and downstream, twelve people and a secret worth killing for.

"Then we don't wait for her to come through," Elodie said. "We go down to meet her."

Every head turned.

"She expects us to hide," Elodie went on, and Tomas heard it in her voice now, the storm-chaser's calm, the voice of a woman who'd spent her life driving toward the thing everyone else drove from. "She expects us to bury the valley and pray. So we don't. We take the boat out, we run the flag of parley, and we put me on her deck with an offer she can't refuse yet. Because right now all she has is coordinates and a rockslide. Right now, I'm still worth more to her free than buried."

"That's insane," Sefa said.

"That's Tuesday," Imani said.

Tomas was already coiling the wire, stowing the detonator, moving toward the descent trail. His hands were steady. His heart was doing something complicated that he'd sort out later, or never. Eleven years ago he'd stood on a bank and watched a river eat his crew because he'd waited, because he'd calculated, because some cowardly prudent corner of him had hoped the problem would solve itself.

Not this time. This time the strangers on his deck — the debtor, the superstitious woman, the smiling liar, the stubborn doctor — had handed him charges and asked where to put them, and then done exactly what he said, no questions, no hesitation, and the mountain had listened. That wasn't a crew of strangers anymore. Whatever it was, it had happened sometime in the last hour, in the dust and the thunder, and nobody had voted on it.

"Elodie," he said, catching up with her on the trail. "If Cass doesn't take the parley — if that boat so much as looks sideways — you get behind me. Understood? I've lost one crew to this water. I'm not losing this one."

She glanced back at him, and for a second the storm-chaser mask slipped and he saw the exhausted, terrified, determined woman underneath, and he thought: *there she is.*

"Understood," she said. "Get us down the hill. I'll deal with the devil."

Below them, the gunboat's boathook scraped against stone, patient as a debt collector, and far up the valley the glacier groaned again — longer this time, deeper, a sound like something vast clearing its throat to speak.
