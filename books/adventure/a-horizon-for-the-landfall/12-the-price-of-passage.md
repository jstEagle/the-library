## 12. The Price of Passage

The white flag went up at dawn, and Elodie hated it on sight.

It wasn't cloth — it was a survey tarp, the kind the Meridian had used to cover instrument crates, and it snapped in the cold wind off the glacier like something trying to escape. Below it, the Syndicate gunboat sat in the middle of the channel with its engines idling, low and black, water sliding off its hull in long silver sheets. A launch detached from its side, small as a beetle, and began the crossing toward their anchorage in the drowned valley's mouth.

"They're supposed to wait for an answer," Imani said. She stood at the rail with her arms crossed tight, knuckles white against her sleeves. "That's how parley works. You signal, you wait."

"Vantara doesn't wait," Tomas said. He'd come up from the engine room with grease still on his hands and hadn't bothered to wipe it off, as if he wanted Cass to see exactly what kind of operation she was walking into. "Waiting is for people who think they might lose."

Elodie watched the launch cut the gray water. Two figures aboard. One was broad, helmeted, carrying a rifle slung with theatrical care across his back — decoration, or a message. The other was slighter, coat collar up, and even at this distance she knew the posture: shoulders squared like someone stepping onto a stage.

"Cass," she said.

"You know her?" Rell asked. He'd appeared beside her without sound, the way he always did, and she still hadn't decided what to make of that. Three days since she'd found his transmitter, three days since the knife and the bargain on the deck, and he'd kept his word so far — no more signals, no more lies that she could catch. But trust, she was learning, was less a door you opened than a wall you leaned against to see if it held.

"I've talked to her forty times," Elodie said. "Every one of them by radio. She has a voice like warm oil. She once told me my brother would have wanted me to pay his debts, and she said it gently, like a kindness." She turned away from the rail. "Get Sefa. And get everyone else below or out of sight. If this is negotiation, we do it with a small table."

* * *

They met in the Meridian's mess tent, which Dr. Sefa's crew had pitched on the gravel shelf above the waterline among the ruins — because of course they lived among the ruins now; Elodie had stopped being surprised. The tent smelled of diesel and instant coffee and the strange mineral cold that came off the ice walls up-valley, a smell like coins left out in rain.

Cass ducked through the flap and paused just inside it, letting the light find her. That was deliberate too. Everything about Vantara Osei-Cass was deliberate. She was smaller than her voice had led Elodie to imagine, neat in a charcoal coat, hair pinned back with mechanical precision, and her eyes did the thing they must have been trained to do: they swept the room once, cataloguing faces, exits, valuables, and then settled on Elodie with an expression of genuine, practiced warmth.

"Captain Zhou." She extended a hand. "In person at last. You have no idea how many hours of your voice I've listened to."

"That's a strange thing to admit," Elodie said, and didn't take the hand.

Cass let it hang there a moment, then folded it away without embarrassment. "Fair. Where shall we sit?"

"Nowhere. Say what you came to say."

Behind her, the armed man took up position by the tent flap, and Elodie felt rather than saw Rell shift his weight near the far wall — close enough to intervene, casual enough to pretend otherwise. Sefa sat at the folding table with her hands wrapped around a tin cup, watching Cass the way a geologist watches a fault line: not afraid, exactly. Measuring.

"The flag means terms," Cass said. "So here are mine, plainly, because I respect plainness and I suspect you do too." She reached into her coat and produced a slate — actual paper, Elodie noticed, actual ink, because the Syndicate liked documents the way old religions liked relics. She laid it on the table but didn't push it forward. "Your debt. Principal, interest, penalties, the whole ugly arithmetic your brother left you. As of this morning it stands at a number I won't insult us both by saying aloud. It will never be paid by chasing storms. We both know that."

"We both know that," Elodie agreed. Her heart was doing something quiet and traitorous in her chest.

"Strike it." Cass said the two words lightly, like setting down something fragile. "Gone. Not restructured, not deferred — struck. Clean ledger. You walk into any port on this coast and there is nothing behind you. No collectors, no liens, no letters. You could buy a boat. You could buy two." Her smile deepened by a degree. "You could stop moving, Captain. I've listened to enough of your voice to know you don't remember what that feels like."

The tent was very quiet. Outside, the glacier spoke — a long groan, distant, like a door opening in another house.

"And the price?" Elodie said.

"Dr. Sefa comes with me. Willingly, ideally — I'm authorized to make that comfortable. Research position, full funding, whatever equipment she wants. The Syndicate is generous to talent." Cass's gaze moved to Sefa, who did not blink. "And the core samples. All of them. Every cylinder, every logbook, every recording your people made inside the valley. In exchange, you and your crew run downstream free and clear, and when the calving closes this route nobody will ever ask you where you've been."

"You want her memory," Sefa said quietly. It was the first time she'd spoken, and her accent put strange music under the words. "You said buyers. On the radio, before — your man Rell told us. Buyers who erase history for profit."

"I want my employer's property delivered," Cass said, unbothered. "What my employers do with it afterward is above all our pay grades. Including mine." She looked back at Elodie. "I'll be honest with you, because you strike me as a woman who prices honesty correctly: if you refuse, none of this gets worse for you today. The debt stays. The route closes. And next season, when the ice opens again, someone else will be sitting where I'm sitting, and they won't have brought a flag."

There it was. Elodie had known it was coming — had known since the launch crossed the channel — but knowing a blade's trajectory didn't stop it from arriving. Freedom, laid out on a folding table between coffee cups. All she had to do was hand over a woman who trusted her, and a valley full of dead children's songs, and walk away light.

She thought about the number. She thought about it the way you probe a broken tooth — compulsively, wincingly. Eleven years. Radio calls at 3 a.m. The way her stomach dropped every time an unknown number lit her screen. The way she'd taken salvage contracts that should have killed her, because standing still meant reckoning. One signature. One word. *Done.*

"Can I see the valley?"

Cass blinked. Of all the responses, Elodie could tell, that one hadn't been in her script.

"The samples are sealed," Cass said carefully.

"I know. I'm asking to see them anyway. Through the wrapping. I want to look at what I'm selling before I sell it." She kept her voice flat, bored almost, a storm chaser's voice, the voice that meant weather data and nothing personal. "You want a clean transaction. So do I. Nobody signs blind — isn't that Vantara doctrine? I've read your contracts. They're very proud of their clauses."

A pause. Cass glanced at the guard, then back, recalibrating. "Ten minutes," she said. "Accompanied."

* * *

The core shed was a repurposed supply tent at the edge of the settlement, and Sefa walked ahead of them with the unhurried gait of someone leading enemies through a cathedral. Inside, racked in foam-lined cradles, lay the cylinders: pale plastic sleeves over dark ice cores, each tagged in Sefa's cramped handwriting with dates and depths.

Elodie had expected instruments. Data. What she hadn't expected was that some of the cores weren't uniform.

"This one," Sefa said, lifting a sleeve and tilting it to the lamplight. The ice within was banded — dark layer, pale layer, dark again — and suspended in the third band down, unmistakably, was ash. Fine black sediment laid down in a single season, centuries ago. "A burning year. Whatever burned, burned everywhere at once. Above it, the ice is clean. Below it—" She set it down gently. "Below it, the chemistry changes. Different air. Different world. This is the year they were erased, Captain. It's in the ice like a scar in skin."

Elodie looked at the core and thought of the tin box Imani had found — the lullabies, the arguments, the ordinary voices of people who had sung to children in a language no archive remembered. The ash layer was those people's last day, pressed flat and kept.

"Beautiful, isn't it," Cass said softly, beside her. Too close. "I mean that sincerely. I've moved cargo for eleven years and most of it was worth nothing. This is worth everything. Which is why—"

"Which is why you can't have it."

The words were out before Elodie had decided to say them, and she heard them land in the tent like a dropped tool. Cass went still. Even the guard by the entrance shifted.

"Captain," Cass said, and the warm oil was gone from her voice now, replaced by something cooler and more honest, which was somehow worse. "Think about what you're refusing. Not for me. For you. That number follows you to your grave. It follows you into every room you'll ever stand in. I am offering you the only exit that has ever existed from it, and it costs you—" she gestured at the cores, at the tent, at the whole buried valley beyond the canvas, "—*ice*. Ice and the ambitions of a doctor who chose a hole in the ground over the living world. You'd trade your whole life for that?"

"Yes," Elodie said.

It surprised her how easily it came. How little ceremony. Somewhere between the tin box and the vote below decks and Imani sleeping ashore because the ice talked at night, the arithmetic had changed underneath her, and she hadn't noticed until now, standing here, weighing a number against a lullaby and finding the number weightless.

Cass studied her for a long moment. Then, remarkably, she smiled — a real one this time, thin and tired, almost admiring. "You know what the worst part is? I believe you." She straightened her coat. "Then we're finished negotiating. I'm sorry, Captain Zhou. Genuinely. I liked listening to you."

"Wait," Elodie said.

Cass paused at the tent flap.

"One question. When you go back to that boat — what happens now?"

Cass didn't turn around. When she answered, her voice was almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened Elodie more than any threat had.

"What always happens. The tide comes in." She lifted the flap. Cold light poured in. "You have until the water reaches your anchor line to move your people off that shelf. After that, I can't promise where the river puts anyone. Not you. Not the doctor. Not the ice."

The flap fell shut behind her.

* * *

They stood in the core shed in the ringing silence, four people and a rack of frozen scars.

"She'll give us an hour at most," Rell said. His voice was steady but his hands weren't; he'd gone pale in a way Elodie had never seen him manage in all his smiling. "The gunboat won't shell the settlement directly — too much risk of collapsing the channel and trapping themselves. They'll force the narrows. Push us against the calving face and let the season do the work. It's textbook Vantara. Deniable. Natural causes."

"How do you know the textbook?" Tomas asked from the doorway, and there was no friendliness in it.

"Because I wrote part of it," Rell said. He met the pilot's glare and didn't flinch. "And because I've spent three days trying to figure out how to unsay everything I ever reported, and this is the first time knowing it might help instead of hurt. Use me or throw me overboard, but decide fast."

Sefa was already moving, pulling sample cases from the rack with swift, decisive hands. "The cores stay with us. If the valley goes, the evidence goes somewhere — ballast, bilge, sewn into mattresses, I don't care. Dr. Anselm's team takes the north trail over the shoulder with the survey charges. If they mean to force the narrows, then we will give the narrows teeth." She looked up at Elodie, and her eyes were calm in a way that made Elodie's spine cold. "You understand what she offered you. And what it cost you to refuse."

"I understand the invoice," Elodie said.

Outside, the glacier groaned again — longer this time, deeper, a sound with weight behind it, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Downriver, faint through the cold air, came the change in engine note they'd all been waiting for: the gunboat coming off idle.

Imani appeared at the tent entrance, breath steaming, eyes wide. "Captain," she said. "The water's rising. And the ice—" She swallowed. "The ice has started talking back."
