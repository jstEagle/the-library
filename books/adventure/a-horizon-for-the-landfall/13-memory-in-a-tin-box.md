## 13. Memory in a Tin Box

Imani had never trusted the ground of that valley. Not because it was unstable — though it was, in places, the permafrost going soft at its edges like old fruit — but because it was too quiet. Rivers talked. Glaciers groaned. Even dead towns made noise if you stood among them long enough: wind through broken windows, the tick of settling timber. This place did none of that. The buildings stood whole, their doors shut, their windows glazed with ice that had melted and refrozen so many times it looked like poured glass. Walking between them felt like walking through a held breath.

She'd told Tomas she didn't sleep aboard anymore. That was true, but it wasn't the whole truth. What she hadn't told anyone was that she'd started sleeping badly on land, too — that the rhythmic cracking from the glacier came even into her dreams now, patient as a heartbeat, like something counting. Like something waiting to finish a sentence.

So when the others were arguing below decks about survey charges and Syndicate gunboats, Imani went walking. She told herself it was to clear her head. She told herself a lot of things lately.

The settlement sat in three tiers along the valley wall, streets climbing away from the drowned riverbed like ribs from a spine. The lower tier was flooded still, black water standing in what must have been a market square, and she kept to the higher paths where the ground was firm. Dr. Sefa's people had mapped most of it already — chalk arrows on doorframes, little tags of colored tape marking structures they'd catalogued. *Dwelling. Workshop. Cistern.* As if naming things could keep them safe.

The house she found was on the third tier, small, half-shouldered into the hillside, with a lintel carved in a pattern she didn't recognize — interlocking spirals, worn shallow by hands long before the ice came. The door wasn't locked. It wasn't even latched. It had simply been closed, the way you close a door when you mean to come back.

Inside: one room, maybe two, the second collapsed behind a fall of roof beam. A stone hearth. A table with a bowl still on it, silt dried to a crust in the bottom. A bed frame with the ghost of a straw mattress pressed into it like a fossil. And against the far wall, on a low shelf built at the height of a child's reaching hand, a tin box.

Imani knew it was a child's shelf before she knew anything else about the room. Her grandmother had built one just like it for her in Port Meridian — low, so a small person could reach their treasures without asking. She stood in the doorway a long time before she crossed the floor.

The box was dented on one corner, painted once in colors the cold had eaten down to rust and shadow. It weighed more than it should have. When she lifted the lid, the hinge cried out — a thin, high sound in all that silence — and she flinched like she'd woken someone.

Inside were cylinders. Wax and fiber, dozens of them, each wrapped in a scrap of cloth, each labeled in a cramped, careful hand in a script she couldn't read but recognized as loving. Some of the labels had little drawings beside the words: a bird. A pot. A face with its mouth open, singing.

She knew what they were. Every river kid knew what they were — her uncle had run a recording parlor in the port district, cutting voices onto wax for sailors who wanted to send songs home. These were recordings. Voices. Ordinary lives, pressed into wax by people who had no idea the ice was coming, or who knew exactly and had decided that what mattered was not the warning but the singing.

Imani sat down on the cold floor with the box in her lap, and she did not move for a long while.

* * *

She carried it back to the Meridian's camp the way you carry a sleeping child, both arms, no sudden movements. Sefa met her at the edge of the dig site, and when she saw what was in Imani's arms, something moved across her face — not surprise. Recognition. Grief arriving on schedule.

"House on the third tier," Sefa said quietly. "Spirals over the door."

"You knew it was there."

"We knew there were boxes. We've found eleven." Sefa touched the lid without opening it, the way you touch a coffin rail. "We stopped listening to them after the first week. It was—" She paused, choosing the word with the care of a woman defusing something. "It was costing us more than we could pay and keep working."

Imani looked down at the box. "They're labeled. Somebody loved these. Somebody sat up nights writing on every one."

"Somebody did," Sefa said. "That's the whole argument, isn't it. That's everything my crew is fighting about, in one box."

Below decks on the *Vela*, the argument was still going. Elodie and Rell had the chart spread out; Tomas was sharpening a knife he didn't need sharpened; the young researcher from the Meridian — Nade, all elbows and certainty — was saying the word *evidence* like a hammer says nail. Nobody noticed Imani come aboard until she set the tin box on the galley table, dead center, and opened the lid.

The talking stopped.

"What is that?" Rell asked, and for once his voice had no broker-smoothness in it. Just a question, plain as hunger.

"Voices," Imani said. "From the town. From before."

Tomas leaned in first. He was the closest thing the boat had to an engineer of old things, and he found the playback rig in the Meridian's salvaged kit within ten minutes — a hand-cranked stylus assembly, meant for core-sample logs, close enough to the old parlor machines that Imani could make it work. She'd spent two years of her childhood watching her uncle do this. Your hands remember what your head forgets.

She chose a cylinder by its drawing — the open-mouthed face — because it seemed the honest thing, to start with a song.

The crank turned. The stylus settled. For a moment there was only the hiss of wax, the sound of a hundred years of cold, and then—

A woman's voice. Singing. Low and unhurried, a lullaby in a language none of them spoke, and beneath it, faintly, a child making sounds back — not words yet, just the music of being answered. Someone in the room beyond the recording laughed. A spoon clinked against a bowl. The whole ordinary evening of a household that had been dead longer than any nation Imani could name, playing out of a tin box on a boat deck in a valley that remembered everything.

Nobody spoke. The crank turned under Imani's hand, steady, because if she let it waver she would stop it altogether.

The song ended. In the silence after, the child giggled, and then the recording ran out into hiss, and then there was nothing but the river and the glacier counting somewhere above them, and eight people standing around a table not looking at each other because looking would have broken whatever had just happened to them.

Elodie sat down slowly, like her knees had made the decision. "Play another."

Imani chose one marked with a pot. An argument — that was what it turned out to be, a man and a woman going at each other about something domestic and enormous, water rights or a broken kiln or a mother-in-law, their voices rising and overlapping until suddenly both of them started laughing mid-fight, helpless, the way people do when they realize the fight was never about the fight. Nade translated fragments — the script on the labels matched inscriptions Sefa's team had partially decoded — but the translation barely mattered. Everyone at that table had been in that exact kitchen. Everyone had been both of those voices.

Tomas lasted four recordings. On the fifth — a man alone, reciting something rhythmic, maybe a prayer, maybe a work song, his voice cracking on one repeated phrase — Tomas took off his cap, held it against his chest, and said, in the voice of a man reporting a death, "I lost a crew on this river. Eleven years back. We never found the bodies." He put the cap back on. "This is what we didn't find. This is what's always missing, when the water takes people. Not them. *This.*"

Rell was crying. He wasn't hiding it well and he wasn't trying very hard. He stood at the edge of the lamplight with his arms folded tight, and when Imani caught his eye he said, "I used to do this. For the Syndicate. Listen to recordings of debtors' calls, build profiles, find the leverage." He shook his head slowly. "I never once thought about what was on the other end of the line. What kind of room. Whether there was a kid."

"And now?" Elodie asked, not unkindly.

"Now I'm thinking about it constantly," Rell said, "and it turns out it's unbearable."

* * *

They listened until the lamp burned low. Imani turned the crank until her forearm ached and refused all offers to take over, because it felt like a duty she'd been given — not by the dead woman who'd labeled the cylinders, exactly, and not by Sefa, but by something in between. A stewardship. Her grandmother had a word for it, a river word: *the keeping*. You kept what could not keep itself. Songs. Names. The fact that a child once giggled in a kitchen in a valley that the maps erased.

Around midnight, Nade brought out the last cylinder from the box — the one with the bird drawn on the label — and said, "This one's different. The label's dated later than the others. Whoever packed this box added to it over years. It was a living thing. They kept adding their life to it."

"Play it," Elodie said.

It was a morning recording. Birds, real ones, the valley alive with them. A child older now, maybe six, chattering breathlessly about something — a dog, a boat, a festival — and a parent's voice answering with the warm half-attention of someone frying breakfast. And then, near the end, the child said a sentence that Nade translated in a whisper, halting:

*"When I grow up, will I remember today?"*

And the parent said: *"I'll remember for both of us."*

The recording ended. No one moved. Above them, distantly, the glacier cracked twice, three times — the rhythm they'd all learned to dread — and for the first time Imani heard it differently. Not counting. Not waiting. Keeping. The ice doing what the parent promised. Remembering for both of them.

Elodie broke the silence finally, her voice rough. "Cass wants this valley's coordinates. Buyers who erase history for profit." She looked around the table, at each of them. "I want everyone to understand what we're actually deciding about. Not samples. Not data. *This.*"

"We can't save it all," Sefa said from the doorway. She'd been there a while; Imani wondered how much she'd heard. "That's the truth nobody wants. The calving season won't negotiate. Whatever we don't carry out, the flood takes. So the question isn't whether we can keep everything." She stepped into the lamplight and rested her hand, gently, on the tin box. "It's whether we're the kind of people who decide what's worth remembering, or the kind who let men with ledgers decide for us."

"The box comes out," Imani said. She hadn't planned to speak. "Whatever else happens. However many we have to choose. This box goes home with us. All of it. Every voice."

Sefa studied her for a long moment. "You understand that's testimony, not rescue. The valley stays. The people stay dead. Carrying the box doesn't undo any of it."

"It's not supposed to undo," Imani said. "My grandmother kept her sister's voice on a cylinder for forty years after the fever took her. Played it every solstice. She said it wasn't for the dead." She looked down at the box, at the labels written in a hand that had loved what it labeled. "It's for the living. So we know what we are."

Tomas nodded once, slow, like a verdict. Elodie put her hand flat on the table next to the box — not touching it, just near, the way you stand near a fire. Even Nade, who had been arguing all day for cores and archives and the world's right to know, said nothing, because there was nothing to argue. Some evidence is a number. Some evidence is a lullaby, and only one of them tells you why any of it matters.

They sealed the box with wax from the galley stores and Imani wrapped it in her own blanket, and stowed it in the hold's driest corner, behind the water casks, in the spot a sailor knows is safest because she has slept against it in storms.

She was climbing back on deck for the last watch when Rell fell into step beside her. He walked a few paces in silence, then said, "The transmission I sent. Before. The coordinates."

"I know about it," Imani said. "Whole boat knows."

"If the gunboat comes up that channel," Rell said, "everything we just — everything in that box, the houses, all of it — they'll strip the site in a week and burn what they can't sell. I've seen their crews work." His jaw worked. "I can send a correction. False coordinates. It'll buy days, maybe. But they'll check my earlier signal logs against it, and when they do—"

"When they do, you're done," Imani finished. "Deserter. Or worse."

"Or worse," Rell agreed. He almost smiled, and it was the first honest expression she'd ever seen him wear. "Funny. Three weeks ago I'd have called that an unacceptable outcome."

Imani looked at him — really looked, the way her grandmother taught her to look at rivers before trusting them — and saw not the Syndicate's smiling observer but a man standing on the last plank between two ships, deciding which one to jump to.

"Do it," she said. "Send your false coordinates tonight. And Rell — when the reckoning comes, and it will come, because debts always do — you stand with us. Not behind us. With."

Downriver, far off past the valley's mouth, a light blinked twice on the black water and went dark. Neither of them said what they both thought it might be. But Imani went below afterward and lay with her hand resting on the deck above the hold, over the tin box, listening to the glacier count, and for the first time since entering the valley, she slept.
