## 14. A Vote Below Decks

Sefa had chaired symposiums in halls built to hold four hundred people. She had defended her dissertation before a panel that wanted her to fail, had argued with ministers and funders and men with titles longer than their patience. She knew how to hold a room.

She had never held one like this.

The Meridian's main hold had been cleared of crates to make space, and still it was too small for twenty-three people. The combined crews sat on upturned supply barrels, on coiled line, on the deck itself, knees drawn up, faces lit by two work lamps strung from the overhead beam. The lamps swung faintly with the river's breathing beneath the hull, so that shadows moved across the walls of the drowned valley's salvaged artifacts — the crates Sefa had not yet been able to make herself repack — like something alive turning over in its sleep.

Outside, the glacier spoke. It had been speaking for two days now, longer reports than before, deeper cracks, the rhythm of a thing gathering itself. Everyone in the hold heard it between sentences. Nobody mentioned it. They didn't need to.

Elodie Zhou sat across from her, arms folded, watching Sefa with the particular attention of someone who has already decided what she thinks and is waiting to see if you'll prove her wrong. Tomas stood by the door because he seemed to be a man who never fully sat down. Imani had brought the tin box and set it on her knee like a sleeping child she refused to put down. Rell sat at the edge of the group, apart, the way a person sits when they've been forgiven but haven't yet forgiven themselves.

And Junie Okonkwo — twenty-six years old, three years on Sefa's team, the best ice-core analyst Sefa had ever trained — stood up before Sefa could even call the meeting to order.

"We should vote first," Junie said. "Before anyone talks anyone out of anything."

A few uneasy laughs. Sefa felt something twist under her sternum, because she recognized the move. She'd taught it to her, years ago, in a seminar on scientific ethics: *say your position before the room can pressure you out of it.* She had meant it as armor for Junie. She hadn't expected it to be turned on her.

"Fine," Sefa said. "Vote first. Then we talk."

* * *

Here is what Sefa had learned in nineteen years of digging things out of the ground: every discovery is a door, and you do not control who walks through it once it is open. You can stand in the doorway. You can describe what's inside. You cannot choose the visitors.

She had found the valley the way all real discoveries happen — by accident, by stubbornness, by refusing to log an anomaly that would have brought survey drones within the month. The settlement below the retreating ice was intact in ways that violated everything she knew about preservation: wooden doors still on their hinges, textiles holding dye, a granary with grain in it. And beneath the buildings, in the compressed layers where the ice had pressed eight centuries of seasons into stratigraphy, the valley kept its own archive. Not writing, exactly. Not only. Impressions. Resin casts. A civilization that existed in no chronicle, no trade record, no genetic database — erased so completely that its only surviving testimony was the testimony it had left for itself, sealed in cold.

For eleven days the Meridian crew had documented it. For nine days after that, they had debated whether to keep documenting.

Three weeks ago, Sefa had made the call that defined everything since: she ordered the radio silenced. Not broken — silenced. A deliberate dark. Because she had run the scenarios the way she ran core samples, layer by layer, and every scenario ended the same way. If the valley's coordinates entered any database — academic, corporate, governmental — the site would be stripped within five years. Not preserved. *Processed.* Buyers existed who purchased erasure the way other men purchased art: a rival nation's founding history, an inconvenient indigenous claim, a lineage that embarrassed a dynasty. The valley remembered people whom powerful present-day interests preferred forgotten. That was not paranoia. That was arithmetic.

So: silence. Carry the testimony out in bodies and notebooks and memory, publish slowly, carefully, decades from now if necessary, when the site could be protected or when the evidence could no longer be disentangled from its context. Let the valley keep its own history a while longer. It had kept it for eight hundred years. It could keep it a little more.

It had been a clean decision, alone in her tent at night with the glacier ticking like a clock.

It was not clean with twenty-two other people in the room, half of whom had nearly died getting here, one of whom had been sent by the Syndicate, and all of whom now understood that the Syndicate's gunboat was somewhere downstream, patient as debt.

* * *

"Those for opening it," Junie said. "Full disclosure. Coordinates, samples, everything, to every institution we can reach at once. Flood the zone so no single buyer can take it."

Four hands went up. Junie's. Two of her Meridian colleagues — Okafor and Lindqvist, both young, both brilliant, both with careers that a discovery like this would make. And, after a long moment, Rell.

Sefa watched Rell's hand rise and thought: *there it is.* The observer who had transmitted their coordinates already, who had confessed it at knifepoint, who had wept on the deck afterward — voting for truth. Of course. People who have betrayed something often need to betray it again, properly this time, to believe themselves repaired. Sefa did not hold it against her. But she noted it.

"And those for sealing it," Sefa said. "Testimony only. We carry out what we can carry in our heads and our notebooks, and the valley stays closed."

Nine hands. Her own crew, mostly. Elodie. Imani. Tomas, who raised his hand the way he did everything, without ceremony, like closing a valve.

Ten votes total. Twenty-three people in the hold. Sefa counted twice and understood: the rest were abstaining. Not undecided. Refusing.

"You can't abstain," Junie said, turning. "This isn't — you don't get to just—"

"They absolutely do," Elodie said quietly, and the hold went still, because Elodie Zhou did not speak often and when she did the room bent toward her like grass toward light. "Some of them are Syndicate contract hands. Some of them have families whose addresses the Syndicate knows. Asking them to vote on the record is asking them to pick a side in something that might follow them home." She looked at Sefa. "You know that. You've been counting it all night."

Sefa had been counting it all night. "Then we're ten and four with nine silent," she said. "Which means nobody has a mandate, which means we talk. Junie. Make your case."

Junie didn't hesitate. That was the thing about her — Sefa had hired her partly for it, and now it cut both ways. "Dr. Sefa, I love you, and you're wrong."

Someone exhaled sharply. Sefa kept her face level.

"You're wrong the way good scientists are always wrong at the end of a long project — you've fallen in love with the integrity of the sample over the reality of the world." Junie's voice shook and she let it shake; she'd learned that from Sefa too, and Sefa winced inwardly at the inheritance. "Eight hundred years. An entire people who sang songs and lost children and argued about grain prices, and they exist nowhere. Not in archives, not in languages, not in anyone's grandmother's stories. The only reason we know they lived is because the ice forgot to destroy them. And your plan is to re-bury them. To make the erasure complete after all — just with better intentions."

"The plan is protection—"

"Protection for how long?" Junie spread her arms at the hold, at the artifacts, at the tin box on Imani's knee. "You said decades. Say fifty years. In fifty years the ice will have retreated past the lower terraces anyway — the valley exposes itself, Dr. Sefa. It's already exposing itself. The calving season is doing in one summer what would take us a decade to map. We are not choosing whether the world finds this place. We are choosing whether the world finds it *documented* or finds it *looted.*"

Silence. The glacier cracked outside, long and rolling, and everyone waited it out like thunder.

"That's the strongest version of your argument," Sefa said finally. "I want you to know I hear it. Here's mine."

She stood. Her knees complained; she was fifty-eight and the field took its tax. She walked to the crate wall, to the resin cast she'd propped there — a child's handprint pressed into sealant eight centuries ago, small fingers splayed, preserved perfect.

"A man came to my dig in the Karakoram when I was thirty," she said. "Government liaison. Very polite. He looked at what we'd uncovered and he said, 'Doctor, this is very interesting, and it would be a shame if it became inconvenient.' That was the whole conversation. I published anyway. Six months later the site was a quarry. My notes were seized as 'national heritage materials' and I never saw them again. Four local families who had helped us were relocated. One of them — the Kader family — the father died in the transit camp." She touched the handprint, lightly, with one finger. "I have spent nineteen years telling myself that publishing was the ethical act and the consequences were someone else's sin. I am done lying to myself. When we open a door, we do not get to choose who walks through it. Junie is right that the valley is exposing itself. So we document everything — every measurement, every photograph, every recording — and we carry it out. All of it. But we do not hand coordinates to institutions, because institutions are made of people, and people are made of pressures, and somewhere upstream of every archive there is a polite man saying *it would be a shame if this became inconvenient.*"

"And the people in the valley?" Junie's voice cracked clean through. "They don't get a vote? They left their handprints in the doorframe, Dr. Sefa. They *wanted* to be remembered. That's why you press your hand into wet sealant. That's not storage. That's a message."

Sefa looked at the print for a long time.

"Yes," she said softly. "That's a message. And I intend to deliver it. Carried, not broadcast. There's a difference, and the difference is who holds it on the way."

* * *

The argument ran another hour. It was not a bad argument — Sefa had sat through enough academic conferences to know the difference between disagreement and performance, and this was the real thing, people risking their actual convictions in front of each other. Okafor argued that testimony without verification was indistinguishable from fiction; Lindqvist pointed out that Sefa's own career proved publication could be survived if the timing was right; Sefa answered with the Kader family until Lindqvist stopped talking. Elodie laid out the Syndicate's logistics with a salvage pilot's precision — response times, patrol patterns, what a gunboat could seize in six hours — and even Junie couldn't argue with the arithmetic, only with the conclusion.

Imani spoke once, near the end, and it landed harder than anything else said all night.

"I listened to that box," she said, nodding at the tin on her knee. "There's a woman on the third recording arguing with somebody about whether to leave. Whether the valley was dying and they should go downriver. And then there's a lullaby, later, same voice, singing to somebody small. She stayed. She stayed and she sang." Imani looked around the hold. "We don't know what she'd want. But she trusted this valley with her whole life. Seems to me the least we can do is not sell the address."

Tomas said nothing at all. He had taken out a knife at some point and was cleaning his nails with it, which those who knew him understood to be a form of deep thought. When Sefa caught his eye, he shrugged one shoulder — *your call, Doctor* — and went back to the knife.

Rell spoke last, and her voice was steady in the way of someone who had decided to spend her fear rather than hoard it.

"I've seen what buyers do," she said. "Not theoretically. I processed acquisition contracts for two years before they made me an observer. There is a client list, and it is exactly what Dr. Sefa fears, and I signed away sites to it." She swallowed. "I voted for disclosure because I owe the truth. But I'll say this plainly: if we broadcast these coordinates tonight, by morning the Syndicate knows, and by next week there are three more boats on this river, and none of them come to listen to lullabies. I'm voting truth and arguing silence. Both things are honest. I'm sorry. That's what I've got."

Nobody laughed. Everybody understood.

* * *

In the end they voted again, because Junie demanded it, and Sefa allowed it because she wanted to lose honestly if she was going to lose.

Twelve for sealing. Seven for disclosure. Four still silent, and Sefa met the eyes of the four — two Syndicate hands, a Meridian engineer, young Petros who hadn't spoken all night — and gave them each a small nod that said: *your silence is also a choice, and I will not make you account for it.*

No mandate. Not really. Twelve of twenty-three, with the opposition unconvinced rather than converted. Sefa had won committee fights with thinner margins and called them victories. This one tasted like a fracture.

Because that was the thing nobody warned you about, when you built something out of strangers in a hard place: the family forms fast, and it forms along fault lines you don't choose. Three weeks ago these people had been two crews and a spy. Now they were something else, something with a shared vocabulary of fear and lullabies and river-craft, and it was precisely because they had become a family that the split hurt the way family splits hurt — not like politics, like bone.

Junie packed her notebook with sharp movements. "I'll abide by the vote," she said, not looking at Sefa. "But I'm keeping my own copy of everything. And if this valley gets looted anyway — if we seal it and it doesn't matter — I want it on the record that some of us said so."

"Your copies stay with the crew," Sefa said gently. "That's all I ask. Testimony carried together."

Junie finally looked at her, eyes red-rimmed and furious and loyal underneath it, which was the worst combination, the one that kept you up at night. "Together," she repeated, like a word in a language she was deciding whether to keep speaking.

* * *

The meeting broke up near midnight. People filed out into the cold in twos and threes, low-voiced. Imani carried the tin box to the Meridian's dry locker and locked it, and Sefa watched her test the lock twice.

Sefa stayed behind in the emptying hold. The lamps swung. The glacier spoke its long slow syllables. On the crate wall, the child's handprint caught the lamplight, eight hundred years old and reaching for nothing, reaching for whoever came next.

Elodie hadn't left. She leaned against the doorframe now, arms crossed, watching Sefa watch the handprint.

"You knew you'd win," Elodie said. It wasn't an accusation. It was a question wearing one.

"I hoped I'd win. Hoping and knowing feel identical at the podium and nothing alike at three in the morning." Sefa lowered herself back onto the barrel. "Ask me tomorrow whether I did the right thing and I'll give you a different answer than I'd give you tonight. That's not weakness. That's just what decisions weigh."

Elodie studied her for a moment. Then she pushed off the doorframe and crossed the hold, and held out a folded scrap of paper.

"What's this?"

"Watch rotation," Elodie said. "Yours is third shift. And Doctor—" she paused at the door, silhouetted against the grey river-light, "—the gunboat moved an hour ago. Tomas saw running lights through the narrows glass. Heading upriver."

The glacier cracked, close now, impatient.

"So whatever we decided down here," Elodie said, "the river's about to have an opinion."

Sefa sat alone in the swinging lamplight, with the handprint and the silence and the sound of the ice coming undone, and began, calmly, to calculate how much a valley weighs.
