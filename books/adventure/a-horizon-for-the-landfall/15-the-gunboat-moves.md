## 15. The Gunboat Moves

Tomas heard the gunboat before he saw it — a low, wrong note under the river's voice, like a knuckle cracking somewhere beneath the world.

He was on the *Vela*'s foredeck with a mug of coffee gone cold in his hand, doing what he did every dawn now: listening. Thirty years on the water taught you that rivers talked. They gurgled and gulped and muttered over their stones, and if you paid attention long enough you could hear the difference between a river running easy and a river running scared. This one had been scared for two days. The glacier upvalley had been groaning all night, long tectonic complaints that rolled down the drowned valley like furniture being dragged across an upstairs floor.

And underneath it, faint but getting less faint by the hour: engines. Diesel. Big ones.

"Imani," he said, not turning around. She was already there. She always was, these days, when the river changed its mind about something.

"I hear it." Her voice was flat, which for Imani meant she was frightened. "Downstream past the narrows. Two engines, maybe three. Not running fast. Prowling."

"Prowling." He tasted the word. Yes. That was exactly it. A boat coming to do honest work ran at a speed. A boat prowling moved the way a man moves through a house he means to rob — slow, patient, memorizing.

Behind them, from the settlement, came the sound of Dr. Sefa's people breaking camp. Not fleeing — Sefa didn't flee anything — but consolidating. Crates carried down to the boats with the unhurried discipline of people who had already decided what they could live without. Three weeks of silence, of guarding a valley full of other people's voices, and now it was going to come down to whether they could outrun or outthink one armored hull.

Cass had left at first light under her white negotiation flag, and she had left with Elodie's refusal sitting in her like swallowed glass. Tomas hadn't heard the whole conversation — Elodie kept it between herself and Cass, mostly, though the word *no* carried beautifully across water — but he'd seen Cass's face as she boarded the Syndicate skiff. It wasn't anger. Anger would have been easier. It was recalculation. The look of a woman moving a problem from the *negotiate* column to the *solve* column, and knowing what solving cost.

"She'll be back," Elodie had said afterward, standing beside him at the rail, watching the skiff shrink downstream. "Not her. Something bigger."

She'd been right. She usually was, about the ugly things.

* * *

They gathered in the Meridian's main tent at midmorning — Tomas, Elodie, Imani, Rell, Sefa, and the young researcher whose name was Okafor and who had argued loudest for telling the world everything and had lost the vote and hadn't stopped glowering since. A camp table held the Meridian's survey maps, weighted at the corners with core sample boxes they were no longer allowed to keep.

Sefa spread the river chart flat. Her hands were steady. Everything else about her was tired.

"The narrows," she said, tapping a place where the river pinched between two shoulders of dark stone, half a kilometer below the valley mouth. "It's the only way in or out by water. The gunboat has to pass through it."

"It won't fit through at speed," Tomas said. "I've run that pinch twice in my life. Once in daylight with a light boat and a prayer. You go through at idle or you don't go through."

"Which is precisely why it interests me." Sefa looked up. "Mr. Villalobos. Your opinion. If we wanted to make sure nothing larger than a skiff ever passed those narrows again — how would we do it?"

The question sat in the tent like a lit fuse.

"You're asking me to close a river," Tomas said.

"I'm asking you how it would be done. Whether we do it is a separate question."

He looked at the chart. He knew every line of it without looking, because this stretch of river had lived in his head for nineteen years, renting space, refusing to leave. The narrows. He'd run them once going up, full of youth and contract money, and he'd run them once coming back down with a boat nine tons lighter than she should have been because the river had taken four of his crew out of her in the canyon above.

He had never gone back. Nineteen years of charters, and he'd drawn his routes around this drainage like a man walking around a grave.

"There are survey charges," Rell said quietly. Everyone turned. Rell had been almost useful since the night Elodie caught him transmitting — quieter, watchful, doing deck work nobody asked him to do, as if honesty were a debt he was paying off in labor. "The Meridian brought them for ice coring. Seismic work. I inventoried them my second day here, before—" He stopped. Before I betrayed everyone aboard, hung the rest of the sentence, unsaid. "There are eleven charges. Enough shaped explosive to bring down a section of the south wall. The geology there is fractured already. Sefa's own surveys say so."

"Sefa's surveys also say the whole valley is ready to let go," Okafor said bitterly. "You want to set charges under a glacier that's already calving?"

"I want to set charges where the rockslide closes a channel," Sefa said. "Not where it brings down the ice. There's a difference, and Mr. Villalobos will tell us where it is, because he is the only person in this tent who has put explosives in a river and lived to be old about it."

Tomas set down his coffee. His hands, he noticed distantly, were perfectly steady, and that felt like a betrayal of something — of the young man he'd been, who had screamed until his throat tore when the canyon took Marisol and Devi and the Okonkwo brothers, who had spent two decades making sure his hands never shook because shaking hands sink boats.

"You're gambling," he said. "All of you understand that? You're gambling that the calving comes before the gunboat does. You drop that wall into the narrows and the river backs up, redirects, tears a new channel — or it doesn't, and you've got a wall of water with nowhere to go but through your own escape route. And if the timing's wrong — if the ice lets go while you're still inside the valley—"

"Then we lose everything," Sefa said simply. "Yes. I've done the arithmetic. But here is the term I cannot get past: if the Syndicate takes this valley, the memory in it doesn't get studied. It gets strip-mined. Buyers pay for erasure, Mr. Villalobos. Whole histories, deleted, so that land titles can be laundered and inconvenient ancestors can stop existing. We have stood inside a civilization that every archive on earth says never happened. If we let armed men into this valley, we are complicit in a second death. I have spent three weeks listening to these recordings. I will not hand those people to the Syndicate twice."

Silence. Somewhere outside, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, and everyone in the tent glanced upward at nothing.

Elodie spoke last, which was her habit. "Tomas. Can it be done?"

He thought about lying. About saying no, too risky, we run for it instead — except running wasn't real. A loaded gunboat in open water caught a pair of salvage boats the way a hawk catches sparrows. There was no version of this where they outran anything.

"It can be done," he said. "Badly, easily. Well, only one way. The charges go in tonight, at slack water, from the water — no lights, quiet engines, because the gunboat's ears are better than ours. Eleven charges, placed along the fracture line on the south wall, tamped and wired, and then we wait. Nobody touches the firing circuit until the calving starts. Because here's the truth of it—" he tapped the chart, the great white shoulder of the glacier upstream, "—that ice is going to come down regardless. Days from now, hours, maybe tomorrow. When it does, it sends a surge down this valley that'll raise the river two meters in minutes. The rockslide doesn't have to dam the river. It just has to be loose and waiting, so the surge water does the heavy lifting, scours the rubble into the channel, and chokes the narrows for good. Nature swings the hammer. We just load the gun."

"And if the calving comes early?" Imani asked. "While the crews are still placing charges?"

Tomas met her eyes. "Then the river takes whoever it takes. Same as it always has."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Elodie nodded, once, the way she signed contracts she hated.

"Rig it," she said.

* * *

They went downriver in the dark with engines at a whisper.

Two boats: the *Vela* carrying Tomas, Elodie, and Rell; the Meridian's inflatable carrying Imani and two of Sefa's people, Adeyemi and the quiet one called Brann, who handled explosives the way monks handled scripture. The moon was a thin nail-paring behind cloud. The river ran black glass, and the cold came off it in sheets, and every man and woman aboard was silent in the particular way of people who have agreed not to name what they're afraid of.

Tomas steered by feel and by the sound of the water against the hull. He knew this reach. God help him, he knew it blind. Nineteen years ago he'd come down this exact line with a dead boat and a dying engine and four empty bunks behind him, and he had sworn on whatever altar drowning men swear on that he would never again hear this water under his keel.

Now here he was, going back down toward the narrows in the dark, carrying explosives.

"You don't have to be the one placing them," Elodie murmured beside him. She'd read him somehow — she was good at that, reading people the way Imani read water. "Brann knows the work."

"Brann knows the rock," Tomas said. "I know the river. Different things." He adjusted the wheel a degree. "Besides. I owe this stretch of water a visit. Been avoiding it half my life. Avoiding a thing doesn't pay what you owe it. Believe me — I've seen the interest rates."

Ahead, the canyon walls rose out of the dark like the shoulders of something sleeping upright. The river narrowed. The sound changed — tightened, quickened, the mutter becoming a hiss — and Tomas felt the old fear rise in him, familiar as a scar, and for the first time in nineteen years he didn't push it down. He let it sit in the boat with him. It weighed something, but so did the cargo, and he'd carried heavier loads than this into worse places.

"Slack water in twenty minutes," Imani called softly from the inflatable, ghost-gray alongside. "Water's holding its breath."

"Then we hold ours," Tomas said, and put the helm over.

* * *

The placement took four hours.

It was brutal, precise, freezing work. Brann and Adeyemi worked the wall itself, roped together, setting shaped charges along the fracture line Sefa's seismic surveys had mapped — a seam of rotten stone running forty meters along the south shoulder, angled so that when it let go, the mass would slide *into* the channel and not merely down the cliff face. Rell ran wire. Elodie ran the tender back and forth in the dark, ferrying charge crates, and Tomas ran the *Vela* in slow circles holding station against the current, and every hour on the hour the glacier upstream made its sound — that long dragging groan, closer now, more conversational, as if the ice were talking to someone.

To them, maybe. Imani would have said so. Imani had stopped sleeping aboard three nights ago and sat up on deck instead wrapped in tarps, listening, and this morning she'd told Tomas, very calmly, that the cracks were coming in patterns now. Repeats. Almost like a warning given twice for emphasis.

He hadn't laughed at her. He was past laughing at Imani.

At 0300, Brann swam the last gap to the boat, hauled himself over the gunwale streaming river water, and said the word they'd been waiting on: "Placed. All eleven. Wired back to the firing point on the north bank — hundred meters of det cord, shielded, above any flood line. We fire it from shore or we don't fire it at all."

"Test circuit," Rell said, and Brann nodded, and somewhere in the dark a small box blinked green eleven times.

Tomas looked up at the south wall. Invisible now, but he could feel it hanging over them — forty meters of doomed stone, patient as everything else in this valley. Below it, the narrows hissed and sucked, indifferent, the same water that had eaten his crew eating its way toward the sea.

"Come on, then," he said quietly, to the river, to the wall, to the nineteen-year-old ghost at his elbow. "One more job."

* * *

They were hauling anchor at the valley mouth when the sky upstream turned white.

Not lightning. Not dawn. Ice-dust — a plume of it rising off the glacier face, catching the first gray light, and underneath it a sound that Tomas felt in his sternum before he heard it with his ears: a crack like the world's spine adjusting, followed by a rumble that did not stop, that built and built, and the river under the keel began, very gently, to move backward.

"The surge," Sefa said, appearing at the rail with her binoculars, her voice stripped of everything but function. "That's the face going. That's the whole face going. How long?"

Tomas did the math the way he'd done it in nightmares for two decades — volume, gradient, distance, the choke points the flood would have to squeeze through. "Twelve minutes," he said. "Maybe ten. Maybe less if the upper ice follows the lower."

"Get everyone to the boats. Now. Everything that floats, everything that matters, on the water in five minutes." Sefa was already turning, shouting names, and the camp exploded into motion — crates abandoned mid-carry, the tin box Imani clutched like an infant, Okafor sprinting for the inflatable with armloads of notebooks.

Elodie grabbed Tomas's sleeve. "The charges. Do we fire now or—"

"When the surge hits the wall." He was already at the wheel, throat tight, hands steady, hands steady, hands steady. "Loose rock plus floodwater, that was the plan. We fire the second the water reaches the base of the south wall. Not before. Not after."

"And if we misjudge—"

"Then we find out what kind of sailors we are." He looked at her, and found, to his mild surprise, that he was almost smiling. "Elodie. Get your people aboard. All of them. Both crews, one river, one chance."

Above them the glacier roared like a door being torn off hell, and the first white wall of water came around the valley bend with the morning light inside it, and Tomas Villalobos turned the *Vela* to face the narrows one last time — the same water, the same walls, the same cold wind off the ice — and this time he was not running from it.

This time he had loaded the gun.

"Everyone hold on," he said, and gave the engines everything she had.
