## 16. Calving Season Opens

The charges went off at dawn, and the mountain answered.

Elodie felt it before she heard it — a deep concussion through the hull of the *Kestrel's Mercy*, up through her boots and into her teeth, like the river itself had been struck a bell. Then the sound arrived: not the sharp crack of Tomas's survey charges in the narrows behind them, but something vaster, a groan that seemed to come from under the world. The glacier at the valley's head was letting go.

"Run the rapids," she said. "Now."

Tomas didn't need telling twice. The old pilot had been awake all night with his hands on the wheel, waiting for this the way a man waits for a verdict. He threw the throttle forward and the *Mercy* surged into the current, the Meridian's flat-bottomed tender lashed alongside on a double towline, both boats riding high because their ballast was no longer stone.

It was evidence now. Eleven crates of core samples, sealed in wax and oilcloth, packed tight against the keel where cargo should be, wrapped in tarps and fish-netting so that any Syndicate inspector who looked would see nothing but ballast weight. Sefa had insisted on the arrangement. *If we don't make it out,* she'd said, *the river keeps them honest.* Elodie hadn't asked what she meant. Some things you didn't say aloud on open water.

Behind them, the gunboat was coming.

They'd heard its engines an hour before dawn — a low diesel growl rolling down the valley throat, Cass aboard, done with negotiation, done with flags of parley. Rell had stood at the rail listening to that engine note and gone gray around the mouth. *"That's the Vantara-class hull,"* she'd said. *"Twin screws. It'll run four knots faster than us loaded."*

So they'd blown the narrows.

It wouldn't stop the gunboat — Tomas said it might slow it, might force them to pick a line through the rubble — but it bought minutes, and minutes were the only currency left. And it had woken the glacier. That was the gamble they'd made, the one Imani had argued against with her whole body, standing on the foredeck with her arms crossed against the cold: *You don't ring a bell in a house you're still standing inside.*

Now the house was falling.

Elodie braced herself at the stern rail and looked back. The valley entrance — the hidden channel between the rock walls that no chart acknowledged, the drowned valley's only door — was visible for one last moment in the graying light. The glacier above it had sheared along a fault line half a kilometer wide, and the face of it was moving. Not collapsing. Moving. A wall of ice the color of old bone, calving in slabs the size of buildings, and each slab hit the meltwater lake at the head of the channel and threw up spray that caught the first light like burning paper.

The displacement wave was coming down the channel toward them.

"Tomas—"

"I see it." His voice was flat, professional, terrifyingly calm. "Imani, cut the tender loose if the towline fouls. Rell, get below and lash those crates. Elodie—" he glanced at her, and for the first time since she'd known him there was something in his face that wasn't grim competence. It was fear, and he wasn't hiding it. "Hold onto something that doesn't let go."

The wave reached the narrows.

She heard it before she saw it — a roar like a freight train running through water, the sound bouncing off the canyon walls and doubling back on itself until the air itself seemed to shake. Then the *Mercy* lifted. Not rocked — *lifted*, the whole boat rising on a swell that shouldn't have existed this far downstream, and for three seconds Elodie was weightless, watching the riverbank drop away beneath her, watching a pine tree that had stood on the shore for fifty years go under without a splash.

Then the bow came down and the world became noise.

Water came over the rails in sheets. She lost her grip on the rail, slid across the wet deck, and slammed into the winch housing hard enough to see white. Somewhere aft, Imani was screaming — not fear, Elodie realized dimly, but names, a litany of saints and river-spirits and dead pilots, the deckhand reading the flood like scripture even as it tried to read her. The tender's towline snapped taut with a crack like a rifle, held, then parted strand by strand, and Rell came up from below decks just in time to throw the emergency lashings free before the tender yawed sideways and rolled.

They got the line back on. Elodie never knew how. Later, Tomas would say that in forty years on the water he'd seen maybe five minutes of true seamanship, and most of it happened in the next ten.

The wave passed. The river kept its new height, swollen and brown and furious, carrying ice — small floes, then bigger ones, chunks the size of trucks spinning past them in the current. One clipped the tender's stern and left a gouge in the hull you could put a fist in. Imani stood at the tender's helm alone, having volunteered to run it solo through the rapids rather than risk a fouled tow, and when Elodie shouted across the water to ask if she was all right, the deckhand gave her a look that said *ask me when we're ashore.*

Ahead, the river narrowed into the lower rapids — the stretch the charts called the Throat, the stretch Tomas had lost his first crew to twenty years ago. He'd told her about it once, drunk, on the third night out: how the water funneled between two granite shoulders and dropped two meters in a hundred, how the standing waves could flip a loaded boat like a leaf, how he'd surfaced downstream alone and listened to the silence where voices used to be.

He was steering straight for it.

"Tomas," Elodie said, climbing to the wheelhouse, dripping, one arm pressed to ribs that might or might not be cracked. "We can portage. There's a landing above the—"

"There's no time." He didn't look at her. Behind them, distantly, the gunboat's engines were audible again — deeper now, strained, fighting the flood surge but coming. "Cass is behind us and the calving is ahead of us and the Throat is the only door. We go through or we drown in the doorway."

"The *Meridian's* tender is taking water."

"Then we bail and we steer and we pray to whatever Imani prays to." He fed the throttle everything the engine had. "I ran this water once and it killed everyone I loved. I'm not giving it a second crew."

The bow entered the Throat.

What followed, Elodie would spend the rest of her life failing to describe. The river dropped away beneath them and the *Mercy* went airborne — actually airborne, keel clear of water for a full second — and landed nose-first in a trough that swallowed the foredeck whole. She was thrown against the wheelhouse door. Tomas rode the wheel like a man wrestling a live thing, hands bleeding, saying nothing, making corrections too fast and too precise to be thought — pure muscle, pure grief, twenty years of a dead crew teaching his hands what to do. The tender came through beside them, Imani upright at her helm, hair whipping, screaming something joyful or furious or both.

A standing wave took the tender broadside.

Elodie saw it happen in perfect, horrible clarity: the little boat lifting, tilting, Imani thrown clear in a spread-eagle arc, the tender rolling once, twice, righting itself half-flooded and wallowing. Then Imani hit the water and was gone.

"*IMANI!*"

Rell was moving before Elodie finished the word. The Syndicate observer — the spy, the traitor, the defector who'd spent three weeks smiling too easily — tore off her jacket, kicked off her boots, and dove off the stern into floodwater full of ice. Elodie got a line, paid it out, watched the brown water for a shape, a hand, anything. Five seconds. Ten. Her lungs burned in sympathy. Fifteen.

Rell surfaced with an arm locked under Imani's chin, both of them streaming, and Elodie hauled the line in hand over hand while Tomas held the boat steady in water that wanted to kill them all. They came over the transom together, Rell gray-lipped and shaking, Imani coughing up half the river, alive, alive, *alive* — and the tender, unmanned, swept into the rocks downstream and broke apart like kindling.

Eleven crates of core samples, in its bilge. In its ballast.

"No," Sefa said. She'd come up from the *Mercy's* hold, and she was looking at the wreckage receding behind them, and her voice was very quiet. "No, no—"

"We'll find them," Elodie said. She didn't know if it was true. "The crates are sealed. They float. They're waxed, they're netted — they *float*, Sefa. We find a eddy downstream, we drag the shingle bars—"

The glacier spoke again.

This time there was no mistaking it for anything else. The second calving — the true collapse, the one the first wave had been only a knock at the door for — came down the valley behind them with a sound like the sky tearing. Elodie turned in time to see the last of the entrance channel vanish: the rock walls sheared, folded, buried under a hundred thousand tons of ice and displaced stone, the drowned valley's door closing as thoroughly as if it had never opened. Dust rose in a column. The river jumped its banks. And somewhere in that white chaos, small and stubborn and human, the gunboat's engines were still audible — then not.

Silence, except for the water.

Nobody spoke. Imani knelt on the deck, soaked and shivering, and made a sign against her chest. Rell stared back upstream at the place where the Syndicate's money and guns and certainty had just been erased by geology. Sefa stood with her hand pressed flat against the rail, watching the valley keep its own memory, and Elodie saw her mouth move — a word, or a goodbye, or both.

Tomas steered them out of the flood channel and into slower water, and only then did his hands start shaking.

* * *

They found the first crate six kilometers downstream, wedged in a willow thicket on the inside of a bend, wax seals intact. The second was snagged on a gravel bar with a dead otter beside it, which Imani insisted was an omen and Rell insisted was coincidence, and neither of them was really arguing about the otter. By midday they had seven. Seven of eleven. Sefa catalogued each one silently, handling them like relics, and when the seventh came aboard she sat down on the deck amid the nets and tarps and laughed — a short, broken sound that was either relief or mourning.

"Four are in the river," she said. "Four layers of someone's history, somewhere under the water."

"The river remembers," Imani said quietly. "That's what today taught me. It takes and it keeps. We just don't get to choose which."

They dragged bars until the light went long and gold. They found nothing more. Elodie organized the search with the same weary competence she brought to storm runs, because standing still meant reckoning, and the reckoning here was that they'd survived something that should have killed them, and survival had a price tag she couldn't read yet.

It was Rell who finally said it, sitting on the crate stack as the sun dropped, watching the water slide past with the expression of someone recalculating everything.

"Cass was on that boat."

"I know."

"Not just the boat. The ledger. My reports were routed through her terminal — my *real* reports, the ones I started writing after the knife, after the bargain. Everything I documented about the seizure order, the buyers, what the Syndicate intended for the valley." Rell's voice was steady, but her hands weren't. "It's all at the bottom of the flood channel. Along with Cass."

Elodie looked upstream, at the wall of dust still hanging over where the valley used to be accessible, at the brown water carrying ice southward toward the sea.

"Maybe," she said.

"You don't think she's dead."

"I think," Elodie said slowly, "that Cass has survived every bad decision she ever made by being the last person to leave the room. And I think that if she's alive, she washed downstream, same as our crates. Same as us." She turned back to Rell. "And I think that changes what we do next. Because a dead Cass is a closed file, and a living Cass is a witness — ours, if we get to her first."

Imani looked up from the crate she was re-sealing. "You want to rescue her."

"I want to *find* her," Elodie said. "What happens after that depends on what state she's in when we do. Half-drowned brokers make very reasonable negotiators."

Tomas appeared at the wheelhouse door, hands bandaged, face carved from exhaustion. "There's a shingle bar two kilometers down, wide enough to camp. Tide's turning. If we're going to search, we search tonight or not at all."

Elodie nodded and looked downriver, at the darkening water and the glittering line of the glacier far behind them, already forgetting them.

"Then we search tonight," she said. "All hands. Because somewhere between here and the sea, the Vantara Syndicate either drowned or didn't — and I need to know which before I decide what to tell the world."

They came around the bend, and on the long shingle bar ahead, half-buried in driftwood, something pale caught the last of the light.

It moved.
