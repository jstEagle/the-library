## 17. What We Carried Out

The river set us down the way a dog sets down a broken toy — without ceremony, without apology, on a shingle bar that hadn't existed last spring and wouldn't exist next summer. Gravel and gray water and a sky the color of wet slate. I came ashore on hands and knees, coughing glacial melt out of my lungs, and the first thing I did was count.

That's what debt does to you. It teaches you to count.

"Tomas." Nothing. "Tomas!"

"Here." His voice came from downstream, cracked and raw, and then his head broke the surface of the shallows and he stood up out of the water like something the river had decided, for once, to give back. He had a gash over one eyebrow that bled into his eyes, and he wiped it with his sleeve and kept walking toward me, because Tomas had never in his life stopped walking toward a problem.

"Imani?"

"Here." From the bar itself — she'd made the bank ahead of us, dragging the tin box against her chest with both arms like a woman carrying an infant out of a fire. She set it down on the gravel and then sat down beside it, shaking, her lips going purple in the wind off the ice. "Here. I'm here. The box is here."

"Sefa?"

A hand went up near the treeline, where the Meridian's people had washed ashore among the dwarf pines — three of them, four, five, someone helping someone else stand. Sefa's voice carried across the gravel, hoarse but level, the voice of a woman who had spent three weeks teaching herself not to panic. "All accounted. Yuen took water but he's breathing. Count your own, Captain Zhou."

I counted. Rell — there, flat on his back ten meters down the bar, coughing, alive, one arm raised in a thumbs-up that looked more like a surrender flag. The young researcher from the Meridian, the one who'd argued for the truth — Anouk — dragging herself out of the shallows. The deckhand. The engineer.

Fourteen souls on a bar of gravel the size of a tennis court, and the river running high and brown and murderous on both sides of us, and behind us — I turned, and everyone turned — the valley.

The rockslide had done what we'd asked of it, or the calving had, or the two of them together had conspired in a way none of us would ever be able to untangle. The narrows where the entrance had been were gone. Not blocked — *gone*. Half the valley wall had come down in the flood, and what remained was raw stone and churning water, a wound in the landscape with no door in it anymore. Somewhere behind that wall, under a hundred meters of new rock and old ice, the settlement sat in the dark with its layered memories, its drowned streets, its civilization that no archive had ever bothered to remember. Sealed. The way Sefa had wanted it sealed. The way the vote had gone, mostly, though the vote had never felt finished to me and it didn't feel finished now.

The glacier above it all was still talking. Even from here, even over the river, we could hear it — the deep, rhythmic cracking, the almost-voice Imani had refused to sleep near for a week. It didn't sound like speech anymore. It sounded like a door closing.

"Core samples," Anouk said. She was standing at the water's edge, staring at the flood with an expression I recognized from mirrors — the look of a person doing arithmetic they already know the answer to. "The cases were in the ballast. In the *ballast*, we put them in the — " She stopped. She didn't finish, because finishing meant saying it.

"They're gone," I said. "The ballast flooded. Everything in the hold flooded."

"Twenty years of work."

"I know."

"Eleven cores. The stratigraphy. The — " Her voice cracked down the middle, and she turned away from me and stood with her arms wrapped around herself, watching the water take the valley's whole history somewhere none of us could follow.

I let her grieve. There was nothing else to offer. The tin box was saved; the cores were not; that was the inventory, and the river had made the decisions for us, the way it always does, the way it had made them for Tomas once before, the way it had made them for my brother. You don't argue with a flood. You count what's left.

* * *

We built a fire out of driftwood and the emergency kit from the waterproof canister Imani had lashed to her back before we ever entered the narrows — because of course she had, because Imani read the river like scripture and scripture says prepare for the flood. We got a tarp strung between two pieces of driftwood, got the wettest people nearest the flames, got hot water going in a dented pot. Nobody spoke much. The work of staying alive is its own conversation.

Tomas sat apart, on a stone at the bar's upstream point, holding a compress to his eyebrow and watching the water. I went and sat with him. Neither of us said anything for a long time. The river ran. Somewhere downstream — not far enough downstream — the Syndicate's gunboat was either at the bottom of it or scattered along its banks in pieces, and I found I couldn't make myself care which.

"You ran the narrows," Tomas said finally.

"Barely."

"Nobody runs the narrows during a calving. Nobody runs them *into* a calving." He shook his head slowly, wincing at the cut. "I lost a crew on this river. Twenty-two years ago. Six people. I've run every stretch of it since, looking for — I don't know what. Forgiveness. Bodies. A reason." He looked at me, and his eyes in the firelight were the flattest I'd ever seen them, which for Tomas meant he was feeling everything at once. "Today I stopped looking. You understand? Today the river gave me a reason. It said: this is what I take. This is what I keep. Now get off my gravel."

"That sounds about right," I said.

"Your brother's compass," he said. "It's in your pocket?"

I took it out. The case was scuffed, the glass fogged with river water, but the needle swung free and steady, and it pointed — I checked, out of habit, out of twenty years of habit — upstream. Toward the valley. Toward the wall of stone that no longer had a door.

"Still pointing," Tomas said.

"Still pointing."

"Maybe it's just a compass now."

"Maybe," I said, and put it away, and didn't believe it yet, but wanted to.

* * *

We found Cass an hour before dark.

Imani spotted her — a pale shape snagged in the rootball of a fallen spruce on the far shingle, moving. Barely moving. Imani didn't hesitate; that was the thing about Imani that Rell had never understood and that the Syndicate would never understand, that she'd spent her whole life on water where the rule was *everyone comes out or nobody does*, and superstition and morality had worn down into the same smooth stone in her. She was in the shallows before I'd finished standing up, and Tomas and I followed, and the three of us dragged Vantara's senior negotiator out of the river by her coat while the flood tried to keep her.

She was half-drowned in the truest sense — lungs full, mind somewhere else, hands blue. Imani turned her on her side and got the water out of her, and Tomas wrapped her in the emergency blanket, and I crouched over her while she coughed and shivered and slowly, slowly came back to the surface of herself.

Her eyes found mine. There was a long moment where I watched her remember who she was — Cass, broker, collector, the woman with the contract and the smile — and then watched her understand where she was: on her back on a gravel bar, wrapped in a stranger's blanket, at the mercy of the people whose mercy she'd been sent to end.

"The gunboat," she rasped.

"At the bottom of the river," I said. "Or in a hundred pieces along it. Same thing."

"My crew."

"Some of them made the banks downstream. Tomas saw movement. If they're smart they'll walk out. If they're not, that's not our problem tonight."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them again she was, incredibly, almost herself — the broker's machinery grinding back to life behind the exhaustion, assessing, calculating. But the machinery had nothing to work with. No gunboat. No collectors. No leverage, no contract, no Syndicate within a week's hard travel. Just a woman on a bar with a blanket and a debt she couldn't collect and a debt, I remembered, she couldn't pay either — because Cass owed the Syndicate for this expedition's failure the way I'd owed them for my brother's folly, and the river had just drowned her collateral.

"You could leave me," she said. It wasn't an accusation. It was an audit.

"We could," I agreed.

"You won't."

"No," I said. "We won't. That's not how we do things. You wouldn't know anything about that."

Something moved behind her eyes — not gratitude, nothing so clean. Recognition, maybe. The look of a person seeing the shape of the trap they've been living in from the outside for the first time. She turned her face away and let Imani feed her hot water, and she shook, and she said nothing more, and I stood up and left her to the fire and the strangers who'd saved her life.

I didn't feel triumphant. I want to write that I did — that there was some hot clean satisfaction in seeing Vantara's iron hand pulled dripping from the water — but what I felt was closer to grief. Cass was what the Syndicate made instead of what it broke. She'd been me, once, maybe: someone with a debt and a boat and no good options, who'd chosen the side with the money because the other side drowned. The river didn't care which side you'd chosen. The river had come for all of us equally, and it had taken what it wanted — the cores, the gunboat, the entrance to the valley, the version of Elodie Zhou who thought paying a debt and doing right were two different projects.

* * *

Night came down cold and total. We huddled around the fire in a loose ring — my crew and Sefa's, fourteen of us plus one half-drowned broker, sharing heat and the last of the Meridian's emergency rations. The tin box sat in the middle of the circle, between Imani and the flames, drying out. Nobody had suggested opening it. Nobody needed to. We all knew what was in it, and we all knew what it had cost the people who'd made it, and we all knew it was the only thing — the *only* thing — that had come out of the valley intact.

Anouk sat staring at the fire with her hands around a tin cup. Sefa sat beside her and put a hand on her shoulder, and Anouk didn't shrug it off, and I watched the two of them hold a whole conversation in silence: *I'm sorry. I know. It had to be this way. I know. I hate it. So do I.*

Rell came and sat next to me. He was still dripping. His transmitter was gone — lost in the flood with everything else, and I watched him realize, in real time, that the device he'd been terrified of and ashamed of and finally honest about was simply gone, dissolved into the same water that had taken the gunboat and the cores. He looked lighter and more frightened at once, a man whose leash had been cut while he was still learning to walk without it.

"Officially," he said, "I'm going to be listed as a deserter by morning. If anyone from Vantara is alive to file the paperwork."

"Probably."

"Unofficially, I don't know what I am."

"You're crew," I said. "You ran the narrows with us. You held the line on deck when Cass's people boarded. That's the whole job description. The rest is paperwork."

He looked at me for a long moment, and his easy smile — the smile that had made me distrust him from the first day — wasn't there. What was underneath it was younger and rawer and, I thought, probably the real face. "Thank you," he said, and it came out cracked, and he looked away fast, at the fire, at the water, anywhere.

Downstream, faint, there was a light. One light, bobbing, moving with the current's edge — a survivor's signal lamp, maybe, one of Cass's crew who'd made it to a bank. Tomas saw it too. He stood, joints cracking, and looked at it for a while.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Tomorrow we go down and pick up whoever the river left, and we walk out together, and we tell the story we agreed to tell. Wreck. Rescue. Nothing more." He looked around the fire, at all of us, at Cass wrapped in her blanket at the ring's edge. "All of us. That includes you."

Cass looked up. For a second I thought she'd argue — that the broker would surface one more time, tally her options, look for the angle. But the firelight showed me something else in her face: exhaustion so complete it looked almost like peace. She nodded, once, and lay back, and let the river's silence take her.

I stayed awake last, as I always do. The glacier spoke its closing door. The tin box sat in the firelight, holding a lullaby and an argument and a hundred ordinary evenings of people no archive ever named. My brother's compass rested against my hip, needle still, pointing nowhere it needed to go.

Tomorrow we'd walk out and start lying — kindly, carefully, together. But tonight, on the gravel, I took inventory one last time. Fourteen alive. One box. One broker, beaten by water instead of by us, which was somehow better. Zero cores, zero gunboat, zero debt I could see the shape of anymore.

And one thing the river had kept, that I'd have to decide what to do about in the morning — because Cass, half-drowned, delirious, being dragged from the rootball of that spruce, had grabbed my wrist with both hands and said four words I hadn't told the others about, four words she'd repeat when she was herself again, or wouldn't, depending on what I did next:

*"I know about Rell."*
