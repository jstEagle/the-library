## 18. Negotiating with the Drowned

Cass coughed river water onto the shingle bar and Elodie let her.

She crouched a few feet away, arms wrapped around her knees, watching the Syndicate's broker retch the flood out of herself in long, shuddering heaves. Cass had always looked expensive — tailored field coat, boots that cost more than Tomas's engine, hair pinned back like she'd stepped off a trading floor instead of a gunboat. Now she lay on her side on gray gravel, soaked through, one boot missing, her comms unit a dead black brick of drowned circuitry beside her hand. The flood had taken everything else. It had left her.

Imani stood behind Elodie with his arms crossed, saying nothing. He'd pulled Cass from the water himself, diving into the churn after the gunboat went over, and he hadn't said a word since. That was its own kind of statement.

"You're alive," Cass rasped, when she could speak at all. She pushed herself up onto her elbows and squinted against the pale light coming down the valley mouth. "Congratulations to us both."

"Don't mistake it for kindness," Elodie said. "Tomas pulled you out because leaving people in the water is how crews go bad. Not because anyone here wanted you breathing."

Cass laughed — a wet, broken sound that turned into another cough. She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and looked around the shingle bar: the two boats beached and listing, hulls scored from the rapids; the Meridian survivors huddled under tarps; Sefa standing apart, watching the collapsed valley mouth where the rockslide had sealed the entrance into a scar of fresh rubble. Somewhere upstream, the glacier was still talking to itself, deep concussions rolling down the canyon like a door being locked from the inside.

"Where are my people?" Cass asked.

"Two made it to the far bank before the surge hit. Rell saw them swimming. The other four—" Elodie shrugged. "The river has them."

Cass closed her eyes. For a moment — just a moment — something human moved behind her face, some grief or fear that wasn't about balance sheets. Then it was gone, filed away, and the broker looked up at Elodie with the flat professional calm that had followed her for three weeks like a smell.

"So," Cass said. "Here we are."

"Here we are."

* * *

They moved up the bar, away from the others, to a driftwood log bleached white as bone. Elodie sat. Cass didn't, at first — she stood there dripping, arms folded, as if standing gave her height and height gave her leverage. But there was no leverage on this gravel bar. There were eleven people who wanted her gone, a flooded canyon between them and anywhere warm, and no boat but Elodie's.

Cass sat down. Her remaining boot squelched.

"I'll open with the obvious," she said. "You need me. My office holds your debt instrument. Whatever you think you've won today, I'm still the only person on this river who can make your life cheap again."

"That's true," Elodie agreed pleasantly. "It's also the last true thing you're going to say, so enjoy it."

Cass's jaw tightened. Upstream, the glacier boomed again, and both of them flinched — old instinct, new context. The sound that had been their enemy all week now sounded like an ally.

"Let me tell you what I see," Elodie said, and her voice came out level, almost gentle, which surprised her. She'd expected anger. What she felt instead was something colder and cleaner, like the first breath after surfacing. "I see a broker who launched an armed seizure of territory she had no legal claim to. No survey license. No mineral concession. No emergency writ — I've read enough Syndicate paper to know what those look like, and you never filed one, because filing one would have put the valley's existence on record, and the whole point was that nobody would ever know." She ticked the points off on her fingers. "I see four dead collectors whose families will ask questions the Syndicate cannot answer without admitting they were here. I see a gunboat registered to a shell company that traces back to Vantara holdings, sitting at the bottom of a canyon. And I see you, Cass, half-drowned, with no comms, no witnesses on your side, and no way home except in my boat."

"The boat can be replaced by—"

"By whom?" Elodie let the question hang. "Who's coming? You sent everything you had upriver before calving season. The route's closed behind us now — you know that better than anyone, you timed your operation against it. Nobody is coming up this river for months. Maybe ever, the way the channel's shifted." She shook her head slowly. "You're not a broker right now, Cass. You're a castaway. And castaways don't negotiate. They plead."

For a long moment Cass said nothing. Water dripped off her sleeve onto the gravel, each drop a small dark star. Down the bar, someone had gotten a stove going; the smell of heated tin and tea leaves drifted over them, absurdly domestic.

"You're enjoying this," Cass said finally.

"No." And Elodie found that it was true, and that the truth of it settled something in her chest. "I spent two years being afraid of you people. Every call, every letter, every polite voice asking about my brother's balance. I used to rehearse conversations with Vantara brokers in my head the way other people rehearse arguments with the dead. So no, I'm not enjoying this. I'm just — done being the one without options. It turns out it's not complicated. It's just a different seat at the same table."

* * *

Cass tried twice more, and Elodie let her, because a negotiation where the other side isn't allowed to fight isn't a negotiation — it's just a sentence being read aloud.

First Cass offered money. Real money, not debt relief: a payout, cash on delivery once they reached port, enough to bury the debt twice over, if Elodie would simply confirm that the gunboat had been lost to the calving and nothing more. Elodie listened all the way through, then said, "I don't want your money. Your money comes with your ledgers attached, and your ledgers come with you. I've had enough of you in my accounts to last a lifetime."

Then Cass tried fear, quieter and more skillfully. She pointed out that the Syndicate did not forgive; it deferred. That names went into files and files went into drawers and drawers stayed shut for years until the day they didn't. That Elodie was thinking about tomorrow when she should be thinking about the next decade.

"I know how the drawers work," Elodie said. "My brother thought about the next decade too. He kept paying, kept deferring, kept believing there'd be a year things got easier. The river got him before the Syndicate did, but only barely — and honestly, some days I'm not sure which one was killing him faster." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and held Cass's gaze. "So here's the trade. Listen carefully, because I'm only making this offer once, and after this we're just two wet people on a rock."

* * *

The terms were simple, and Elodie had built them the way Tomas built a rigging knot — every line load-bearing, nothing decorative.

One: the debt. Not deferred. Not restructured. Struck. The full instrument, Jun Zhou's name and hers, dissolved at source, with written confirmation transmitted the moment they had signal — because Elodie knew better than to trust anything that lived only in Cass's memory or Cass's goodwill.

Two: Rell. Every report Rell had filed — the coordinates, the manifest queries, the observations of the Meridian crew, all of it — buried. Not archived. Buried. Cass would certify the reports destroyed and swear the observer's file closed. Rell had turned, had stood on a deck with a knife at their own throat and chosen honesty over survival, and Elodie did not intend to let the Syndicate reach upriver and collect that particular debt forever.

Three: silence. This was the part that made Cass blink, because it ran backwards. Elodie would not expose the seizure attempt. No testimony, no records, no story sold to the coastal papers about a Syndicate gunboat running an illegal land grab up a closed river. The four dead collectors would be logged as lost in a survey accident, which was even almost true. The valley would stay exactly as hidden as the Syndicate had wanted it — except now it would be hidden from the Syndicate too.

"You're offering me cover," Cass said slowly. "Why?"

"Because exposure burns everyone in the room," Elodie said. "It burns you, sure. It also burns Sefa and her people, whose names end up in depositions. It burns Rell, who'd testify or obstruct, either of which ends their life. It puts the valley on the public record in a hundred documents I can't control, and somewhere in those hundred documents is a coordinate, or a bearing, or a buyer smart enough to read between lines. I watched what your buyers do to history, Cass. I'm not handing them a map by accident." She sat back. "You get to walk away clean-ish. We get to walk away free. And the valley gets to keep being a rumor nobody can price."

"And if I refuse?"

Elodie gestured downstream, at the brown floodwater still running high and fast between the canyon walls. "Then we put you ashore at the first settlement with a working radio, and I make some calls of my own. I know people now. People who were on that raft. People who heard you order the approach on an open channel." She paused. "Also, frankly, you'd be walking into the interior with one boot. Your call."

It wasn't much of a threat and they both knew it — Cass was already ruined, and ruined people are dangerous precisely because threats stop mattering. But that was the point Elodie was counting on. She'd learned it watching her brother drown by inches: a man with nothing left to lose will still take the exit that hurts least, if you build it well enough and don't gloat while pointing at it.

Cass stared at the river for a long time.

"You understand," she said at last, "that this doesn't end Vantara. Someone above me will eventually ask why the Meridian contract closed with no salvage fee and no report. Files have gravity. They pull."

"Then you'll handle it," Elodie said. "You're good at handling things. It's the only thing I've ever admired about you."

Something flickered across Cass's face — not quite a smile, not quite a wound. She extended her hand. It was still shaking from the cold, and Elodie took it anyway, and the grip was brief and businesslike and final, two people closing a deal neither of them would ever be able to talk about.

"Struck," Cass said. "Buried. Silent." She exhaled, and it seemed to take ten years with it. "You know what the worst part is? If you'd come work for us instead of against us, you'd have been running half the coastal contracts inside two years."

"Yeah," Elodie said. "That was the worst part of my week too."

* * *

They broadcast from the high bank that evening, where a sliver of signal survived in the rain shadow of the canyon wall. Elodie stood within earshot while Cass dictated confirmation numbers to her office in a voice stripped of all its polish — account struck, instrument voided, observer file closed, materials destroyed, expedition concluded with loss of vessel, no further action required. Each phrase landed in Elodie's chest like a stone dropped into deep water. When Cass read out her brother's name — *Jun Zhou, obligation released, zero balance* — Elodie had to turn and look at the glacier so no one would see her face.

Zero balance. Two words. Two years of dread, paid off not with money but with a secret, held cleanly, spent cleanly. Justice without law. It didn't feel like victory. It felt like setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy, and discovering her spine still worked.

Sefa appeared beside her while Cass was still transmitting. The old scientist watched the light die on the ice fields above the collapsed valley mouth.

"It's done, then," Sefa said.

"It's done."

"They'll wonder, someday. About the gap in their records. About the contract that closed wrong."

"Let them wonder," Elodie said. "Wondering is harmless. Coordinates aren't."

Sefa nodded slowly, then touched Elodie's shoulder — brief, dry, warm — and went back down toward the fires.

Behind them, Cass finished transmitting and lowered the radio. She looked smaller somehow, wrung out, a woman whose whole architecture of leverage had been washed downstream along with her gunboat. She caught Elodie looking and lifted her chin, salvaging the last scrap of dignity the river had left her.

"We're your passengers now, I suppose," Cass said.

"For the run downriver," Elodie agreed. "After that, we never met."

"After that," Cass echoed, "we never met."

Upstream, the glacier boomed one last time in the failing light — not angry, not threatening, just the sound of something enormous settling into a new shape. Elodie turned toward the fires, toward the voices of her strange, patched-together family arguing about tea rations, and started walking.

There was still a river between them and home. But for the first time in two years, none of it belonged to anyone else.
