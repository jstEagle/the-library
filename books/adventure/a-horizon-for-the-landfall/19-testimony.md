## 19. Testimony

The harbor at Vell's Landing smelled of diesel and drying nets, the way it always had, and Sefa stood at the rail of Tomas's boat and let it wash over her like a tide she had been away from too long. Three weeks upriver. A drowned valley. A glacier that had closed its own door behind them with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Behind her, on deck, the others were arguing.

She could hear them through the wheelhouse glass — Elodie's voice low and level, Rell's higher, defensive, then not; Imani's steady as a keel; Tomas rumbling underneath like ballast shifting. The Meridian survivors were there too, six of them, thinner than when they'd gone in, sunburned and quiet in the way of people who had agreed to something enormous and were still learning its weight.

They were deciding what to tell the authorities.

Sefa did not go down to join them. Not yet. This was the part she had dreaded more than the calving, more than the gunboat, more than the moment the valley entrance came down behind them and she understood, finally and completely, that there was no going back — not for the site, not for the samples, not for any version of the truth that fit inside a report.

She watched a gull hang motionless over the breakwater and thought about archives.

* * *

Her mother had been an archivist. That was the irony nobody aboard knew, the joke the river had played on her specifically. Her mother had spent thirty years in the national repository in Ondrel, cataloguing things in acid-free boxes, believing — genuinely, devoutly believing — that preservation was a moral act. That anything recorded was anything saved. That memory, properly indexed, outlived forgetting.

Then the Consolidation came, and the repository's funding moved to a ministry that preferred certain drawers empty, and Sefa watched her mother's life's work reclassified, restricted, and eventually pulped — legally, with signatures, with a paper trail so meticulous you could have archived the destruction itself.

Her mother died two years later. Sefa had stood in the empty reading room where the boxes used to be and understood something that had taken her up a river and into a valley of ice to fully believe:

A record can be seized. A record can be bought. A record is only ever as safe as the hands that hold it, and hands can be pressured, purchased, or broken.

But a thing carried — carried in the body, in the voice, in the stubborn private keeping of people who love it — that cannot be subpoenaed. That cannot be auctioned to buyers who erase history for profit. That survives the way songs survive: badly, imperfectly, changed a little with every singing, and therefore alive.

That was what she would tell them, if they asked her opinion. She suspected they would.

* * *

The port authority office sat at the head of the quay, a squat building with a green door and a clerk named Odile Ferrant who had known Tomas since he was a boy stealing dinghies. They went in as one delegation — Elodie at the front because this had been her contract and her boat, Sefa beside her because the Meridian was her expedition and her responsibility, Rell hovering at the back with the expression of a man attending his own sentencing.

Odile looked up from her ledger. Looked at them. Looked at their faces — salt-cracked, sleep-starved, marked — and Safa saw the exact moment the old woman decided not to ask certain questions.

"The Meridian," Odile said. "Reported overdue twenty-six days."

"Wrecked above the Kessin narrows," Elodie said. "We found survivors. Brought them home."

"Cause?"

"Ice." Elodie let the word sit there, plain and unadorned. "Calving season opened early this year. The river took the boat. We got the people out."

Odile wrote it down. Her pen scratched in the quiet. "Cargo?"

"Lost with the vessel."

"Instruments? Samples? Anything of scientific value?"

"Gone," Sefa said, and her voice did not shake, though it wanted to. "Everything. The river keeps what it takes."

Odile looked at her over the spectacles for a long moment — a look Sefa recognized, the look of a woman who had processed forty years of river stories and knew precisely which ones were incomplete. Then she stamped the page.

"A wreck and a rescue," Odile said. "Six survivors recovered. Salvage rights to be filed by the recovering party." She set down the stamp. "I'll need statements from each of you. Short ones. And Miss Zhou — the Syndicate will have their own paperwork here by week's end. Whatever arrangement you made on the water, make sure it's in writing before they arrive."

"It's in writing," Elodie said.

"Good." Odile closed the ledger. Then, quieter, without looking up: "My grandmother used to sing a song about the high valleys. Nobody else in this town knows it anymore. Some things don't need to be in ledgers to be kept."

Sefa felt something loosen in her chest, some knot she'd been carrying since the ice. *They know,* she thought. *The ones who matter know how to keep.*

* * *

Outside, the light had gone long and gold across the water. The crews dispersed to eat, to sleep, to stand in doorways of boarding houses and remember how beds worked. Sefa found herself walking the quay alone, past the fish sheds, past the chandlery, all the way to the seawall at the harbor mouth where the breakwater met the open sound.

Elodie found her there an hour later. Of course she did. Sefa was beginning to understand that Elodie Zhou found people the way her brother's compass found north — inevitably, and perhaps not entirely by her own choice.

"You voted with us," Elodie said, sitting on the cold stone. "Back on the boat. Silence. But you gave Odile nothing either way. You let her decide what to write."

"She'd already decided. People like Odile keep things by knowing when not to ask." Sefa turned the tin box over in her lap. It had traveled from the settlement in Imani's coat, then in the ballast with the evidence, then in Sefa's own arms through the rapids. Dented now. One hinge sprung. Inside, the recordings of lives no archive had ever held — songs, arguments, a man laughing at his own joke, a woman counting a child's fingers, lullabies sung in a language that existed nowhere else on earth because the valley had been erased from every map and every memory except its own.

"You could have told the world," Elodie said. "The young researcher — Anselm — he'll carry that argument the rest of his life. Truth as justice. Let everyone see what was done to them."

"Yes. And the Syndicate's buyers would have seen it too. Every exposure is also a set of coordinates." Sefa opened the box. The little cylinders lay nested in cloth, each labeled in a hand long dead. "My mother believed recording was saving. I spent my whole career believing it. Then I watched men with contracts try to buy a valley's grave site while we were still standing in it." She closed the box. "Anselm isn't wrong. He's just early. Maybe in fifty years the world will be safe enough for that truth. But testimony isn't only documents, Elodie. Testimony is what we do with it. Who we trust with it."

"And you're trusting me."

"I'm trusting the crew." Sefa lifted one cylinder free — the last one, the one she had copied alone in the Meridian's ruined lab the night before the gunboat came, working by lamplight while Imani kept watch at the tent flap. "There are nineteen voices in this box. Songs, quarrels, a wedding, someone teaching a child the names of stars. I've listened to all of them more times than I can count. But there's one I can't keep to myself anymore, and I can't put it in a drawer either. It has to be carried. That's the whole point. That's the only way it stays true."

She held it out.

Elodie didn't take it at first. "Why me?"

"Because you paid your debt with silence instead of selling it. Because you ran the rapids to get these people home when every rational part of you should have cut loose and saved yourself. Because your brother taught you that some instruments point at people, not places." Sefa smiled, tired and certain. "And because you'll play it once a year, maybe twice, and each time you'll remember that a whole world sang, and you'll be angry about what was done, and the anger will keep you honest. Memory doesn't survive by being archived, Elodie. It survives by being carried. By being loved inconveniently, in private, by people who have no duty to it except that they choose it."

Elodie took the cylinder. Held it like something live.

"What is it?" she asked.

"A lullaby. A mother named Yeva singing to a daughter who grew up and became a weaver, and whose granddaughter — as far as we could reconstruct — may have been alive when the valley was flooded and struck from the record." Sefa looked out at the darkening water. "It's three minutes long. It took me four days to copy it without damaging the original. And I've decided that's the right size for a civilization's ghost. Small enough to hide in a coat pocket. Large enough to be a whole people."

* * *

They sat until the harbor lights came on, saying very little. At some point Elodie asked, "Does it feel like losing? Sealing it?"

Sefa considered honestly. "It feels like burying someone you love in ground you chose instead of ground chosen for you. There's grief in it. But there's agency too. The Syndicate wanted to decide what the valley meant — inventory, asset, coordinates. We decided instead. That's not erasure. That's authorship." She paused. "Anselm will publish someday. When it's safe, or when he can't hold it anymore, whichever comes first. And when he does, everything he says will be true, because we made sure of it. But it won't be everything. What's missing will be missing on purpose, and the ones who carry it will know why."

"And me? What am I carrying?"

"A mother's voice," Sefa said. "Sing it back into the world once in a while. Quietly. Where the water can hear."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the boarding house had gone silent and the tide had turned — Sefa stood at her window and watched a single lamp burn aboard Tomas's boat at the quay. Elodie's lamp. Listening, probably. Or not listening yet, holding the cylinder the way you hold a letter you're afraid to open.

Down the coast, invisible in the dark, a wall of ice stood over a hidden valley, sealing itself year by year, keeping its own counsel. Somewhere south, a broker named Cass was disappearing upcountry with a debt struck and a silence purchased. Somewhere in a drawer in the port office, a lie with a stamp on it read *wreck and rescue*, and it was even mostly true.

Sefa pressed her hand flat against the cold glass.

Her mother had been wrong about archives, but not entirely wrong about remembering. Remembering was a moral act. It just couldn't live in boxes. It lived in a clerk who wouldn't ask, in a superstitious deckhand who'd heard the ice speak and never stopped listening, in a defector learning to be crew, in a storm chaser with a compass that pointed at people.

Nineteen voices in a tin box. One voice given away freely, to be carried.

It was enough. It would have to be enough.

Below, the lamp on the boat went out — and then, a moment later, flickered on again, and Sefa caught, faint across the black water, the first thin notes of a machine winding up, a needle finding its groove, a voice three hundred years dead beginning, softly, to sing.

She smiled, and let the dark keep the rest.
