## 20. Settling Accounts

The paper came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Rell had always imagined that the moment a debt died, it would happen in some place of consequence — a courthouse with bad lighting, a broker's office smelling of ozone and old coffee, maybe at gunpoint on open water. Not a Tuesday. Not a courier in a gray jacket who wouldn't come aboard, who held out the envelope across the gap between dock and deck like it might bite him.

"Vantara Holdings," the courier said. "Signature required."

Rell signed it because Elodie was below decks and someone had to, and because his hand was already moving before his mind caught up — old training, the reflex of a man who'd spent two years making other people's paperwork real. The courier left without looking back. Nobody ever looked back at Rell. That had been the job: be the smile at the edge of the frame, the man taking notes who was never in the photograph.

He stood on Tomas's deck — *their* deck now, maybe, though nothing was settled yet — and turned the envelope over twice before opening it.

Inside: one page, Syndicate letterhead, the seal pressed deep enough to feel with a thumb. *Re: account of E. Zhou, deceased debtor J. Zhou. Obligation discharged in full. All claims, liens, and collection actions withdrawn.* A signature he didn't recognize. A date. Nothing else.

No mention of the valley. No mention of the gunboat, or the flood, or Cass being dragged half-drowned from the shallows and handed back her own life by the people she'd been sent to break. No mention of what Elodie had traded for this — the silence, the leverage, the clean blade of it. The Syndicate didn't write down its losses. It wrote down only what it intended to collect later.

Rell read the letter three times anyway. He had a habit of reading things three times; it was how you caught the second document hidden inside the first, the clause under the clause. There wasn't one. Or there was, and it was so deep in the architecture of the thing that no amount of reading would surface it.

"You're going to burn a hole in it," Imani said from the gangway, a crate of dried fish on her shoulder. She didn't startle anymore when she found him standing somewhere quiet. That was new. Two weeks ago she'd have put the crate between them and kept her eyes moving.

"It's clean," he said.

"Nothing from Vantara's ever clean." She thumped the crate down on the deck boards. "It's just paid for."

* * *

Elodie read it sitting on the rail, feet hooked through the stanchions, the way she read everything — like the paper might try to run. The harbor moved behind her: gulls, diesel, the long slow breathing of water against pilings. Somewhere upriver, past the last bend anyone charted, a glacier was calving into a valley that officially did not exist, burying itself deeper with every ton of ice that came down.

"Well," she said finally. "That's that."

"That's never that," Rell said. "I worked for them. They don't discharge debts. They convert them."

She looked at him then, and he braced for it — the flat assessment, the weighing, the question of whether he was still an instrument that happened to be lying on their deck. But what crossed her face was something more tired and more human. "You think there's a hook in this."

"I know there is. I just can't see it."

"Then we do what we've been doing." She folded the letter once, twice, and tucked it inside her jacket, next to where the compass used to ride. "We live like it's true until it isn't."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then they come find us," Elodie said, "and they'll wish they hadn't."

It should have sounded like bravado. From her it sounded like weather forecasting.

* * *

Cass was gone by Thursday.

Rell learned it the way he learned everything now — sideways, through the harbor's nervous system, the net-menders and fuel clerks and barkeeps who saw everything and reported to no one, which made them the only intelligence network he'd ever trusted. She'd taken the morning packet boat upcountry, alone, one bag. She'd paid cash. She'd looked, according to the ticket clerk, "like a woman going somewhere to stop being somewhere else."

Rell understood that condition intimately.

He walked out to the packet dock after she'd gone and stood where she'd stood, and tried to imagine the arithmetic that had brought her to it. He could, actually. That was the worst part. He'd sat across from her in the flooded aftermath, wrapped in a blanket, hair plastered flat, and watched her understand in real time that the Syndicate's failure on the river would land on her ledger and nowhere else. A broker whose operation drowned was not a broker anymore. She'd looked at Elodie across the shingle bar and Rell had seen the exact moment the arithmetic finished — not hatred, not even fear. Just the cold recognition of a woman who'd been outmaneuvered by someone holding better cards, and who knew the only move left was off the board.

Elodie had offered her the trade anyway. Silence for silence. *You were never here. We were never here. Go.*

Cass had said one thing before boarding the packet boat, according to the clerk. Rell made himself ask what it was.

"She looked back at the river," the clerk said, "and she said, 'Tell the little observer he was right about the manifest.'"

Rell stood on the empty dock a long time after that, wind coming off the water with the first bite of the season in it.

He had been right about the manifest. Three weeks upriver, fog on the water, and he'd asked too many questions about cargo weights that didn't balance — and the truth underneath had been that the Meridian was carrying survey charges and core drills and a science team that had already decided, before it ever left port, that some answers were going to stay upriver. He'd reported none of it. He'd burned his own reports in a coffee can the night before the gunboat came, and stood watch while Tomas wired the narrows, and when the flood took the entrance and everything in it, Rell had felt something crack open in his chest that he still didn't have a name for. Relief, maybe. Or the death of the man who would have reported it.

Cass knew. Of course she knew. Brokers always knew; it was the whole profession. And she'd carried his name upcountry like a coin she chose not to spend.

He owed her for that, and he hated owing anyone anything, and both of those facts sat in him all afternoon like swallowed stones.

* * *

The vote — if you could call it that, since nobody called a vote and everybody voted — happened at Tomas's table on Friday night, over a stew Imani made with too much pepper because grief and celebration got the same seasoning in her kitchen.

Tomas laid it out the way he laid out everything: short, blunt, hands flat on the wood. "Boat's mine. Was mine. Engine's tired, hull's honest, and I'm too old to single-hand her another season." He looked around the table. "So. Shared venture. Charters, salvage, running the safe water. Four shares. Anybody wants out, says so now and owes nobody an apology."

Imani said yes before he finished, because Imani always answered questions about belonging faster than questions about danger.

Sefa wasn't at the table — the Meridian crew had scattered to their own reckonings, most of them drifting toward the university town where Dr. Sefa intended to spend years writing reports about a wrecked expedition and a failed season, every word technically true and every word a wall. But Sefa had sent word through Elodie: *the river gave you to each other; don't waste the gift arguing over shares.*

Which left Rell.

Everyone looked at him, and he felt the old machinery spin up — the smile, the deflection, the easy line that kept the frame's edges soft. He let it spin and didn't use it. That was the discipline now. Every day, choosing not to be the thing he'd been built to be.

"I'm officially a deserter," he said. "Vantara doesn't forgive desertion. It waits. There'll be a file with my face in it, and someday somebody will walk down this dock carrying it."

"Probably," Tomas said.

"There's no version where this is clean. You take me on, you take the risk that comes with me."

"Boy," Tomas said, and the word landed with surprising gentleness from a man built entirely of angles, "I ran this river thirty years. I've taken on superstitious deckhands, debt-haunted pilots, and a boatload of scientists who lied to my face about their cargo. You think a file scares me?" He tapped the table once. "You stood the watch when the charges went in. You didn't sleep, you didn't flinch, and you threw your radio in the flood with your own hands. I saw it. That's the whole interview."

Rell's throat did something he pretended was a swallow.

"The share's yours," Tomas said. "If you want it."

And there it was — the actual question, the one under the question. Not *may I stay*, but *do I want to*. He'd spent two years wanting nothing except to finish assignments and remain unphotographed. Wanting a place was dangerous. Wanting people was the kind of exposure no tradecraft covered.

"Yeah," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I want it."

Imani grinned and dumped more stew in his bowl without asking, which he understood, in the grammar of that table, to be a ceremony of some kind.

* * *

He couldn't sleep that night, so he did what he always did with sleeplessness: he worked. He went through the boat's papers by lamplight in the wheelhouse — registration, insurance, Tomas's cramped handwriting on maintenance logs going back a decade — reorganizing, cross-checking, building the boring skeleton of a legitimate venture. It was the first paperwork of his life that wasn't a weapon. He found himself slowing down over each page, handling them like artifacts.

Elodie found him there near midnight. She didn't announce herself; she just filled the doorway, and he knew better than to pretend he hadn't heard her.

"You checked that letter four times today," she said.

"Five."

"And?"

"And it's clean. Which bothers me more than a dirty one would." He set down the logbook. "Vantara doesn't lose. It reallocates. Somewhere upstairs, someone decided your debt was worth less than the cost of pursuing it — or worth more as a favor owed than money collected. Either way, we're in someone's ledger. Just on a different page."

Elodie leaned against the doorframe and looked out at the black water for a while. "Then we keep living small," she said. "Keep the boat honest. Keep our names off manifests that matter." A pause. "You could run. Deserters run. Nobody would blame you."

"No," Rell said, and heard how certain it was, and let it stand. "I'm done being nowhere."

She studied him a moment longer — that pilot's assessment, reading water ahead of the hull — and whatever she saw seemed to satisfy her. "First charter's in three weeks. Tourists. Rich ones, bored ones, the safest kind." She pushed off the doorframe. "Get some sleep, crewman."

*Crewman.* He turned the word over after she'd gone, the way he'd turned the discharge letter over, hunting for the trap in it. There wasn't one. Or the trap was simply that he believed it now, and belief was the one vulnerability he'd never been trained to defend.

* * *

On Sunday he walked upcountry himself, not far — just past the last streetlamp, to the rise above the packet dock where you could see the river make its first big bend. The water ran high and brown with the season's melt, carrying ice scraps down from somewhere no map admitted to. Somewhere up that bend, behind rock and flood and a glacier's slow conspiracy, a valley was keeping its own counsel. He'd seen it for eleven days. He'd transmitted its coordinates exactly once, and then he'd helped kill the road to it, and he would carry both facts forever, and he was starting to learn that carrying things was what people did instead of being clean.

Below him, the boat rode at her moorings, lamp lit in the wheelhouse where Imani was doing the inventory she never trusted anyone else to do. Tomas's silhouette crossed the aft deck, checking lines he'd checked an hour ago. Elodie sat on the rail with her boots hooked through the stanchions, watching the water like it owed her an answer.

His people. The phrase arrived uninvited and he didn't reject it.

Then he saw the figure on the far dock.

Not moving. Not approaching. Just standing at the end of the commercial pier where the couriers tied off, wearing a gray coat, face turned toward the boat with the patient stillness of a man confirming an address. Even at this distance, even in the failing light, Rell's trained eye catalogued it automatically: the way the figure stood with weight even on both feet, the bulge of a document case under the coat, the fact that he'd arrived on foot from the depot rather than by launch.

A courier. Or something wearing a courier's posture.

The figure looked at the boat a moment longer. Then, deliberately — deliberately enough that Rell understood it was meant to be seen — he raised one hand, not in greeting but in acknowledgment, touched two fingers to his hat brim, and turned away up the dock road, unhurried, disappearing into the dark between the lamps.

Rell stood very still on the rise, the cold coming up off the river, and did the arithmetic he'd hoped never to do again.

They weren't done. Someone upstairs had signed the discharge and sent a watcher in the same week, and the watcher wanted them to know he'd come — wanted *him*, specifically, the deserter, the observer, to see it.

Down at the mooring, oblivious, his people's lamplight burned steady against the water.

Rell started down the hill, fast now, already composing what he'd say and how much of the truth it would hold.
