## 1. The Radio Goes Quiet

The skull came out of the riverbank at four in the afternoon, and by four-fifteen Hazel Yarrow had already been told it was worth nothing.

"Too degraded," said Petrakis, the site supervisor, not looking up from his clipboard. "No provenance worth the paperwork. Bag it and move on."

Hazel crouched in the trench with the thing cradled in both hands — a human cranium, river-bleached, one orbit staring up at him through two hundred years of silt. Somewhere under the levee spoil and the diesel stink of the pumps, this had been a person. Someone had buried them, or failed to bury them, and now the river was giving them back and the only ceremony they'd get was a zip-lock bag and a man with a clipboard who couldn't be bothered.

"Bag it," Hazel said. "Right."

He bagged it. He moved on. That was the job. The Meridian Salvage Group didn't do archaeology; they did recovery of assets prior to inundation, which was what you called it when a hydroelectric consortium paid you to strip anything sellable out of a valley three weeks before they drowned it. Hazel had been on the crew eleven months. Long enough to stop flinching at the word *assets*. Not long enough to stop hearing his father's voice every time he did.

*You're digging through other people's grief for pocket change,* Marcus Yarrow would have said, if Marcus Yarrow had spoken to him at all, which he hadn't in six years.

The trench wall behind Hazel wept groundwater in slow brown threads. Above it, the September light lay flat and yellow on the water tanks, the generator shed, the rows of tagged crates waiting for the auction house. A helicopter thumped somewhere downriver, hauling survey gear for the dam crews. Everything here was numbered, weighed, priced. Hazel wiped his hands on his thighs and thought, as he did most afternoons, that his mother would have hated this place — and then, as he did every afternoon, he made himself stop thinking about her, because the alternative was thinking about why she'd died, and the trench was no place for that.

He climbed out, signed the find sheet without reading it, and walked toward the mess tent because the shift was ending and there was coffee, and coffee was the only ritual left that asked nothing of him.

The radio room was a converted shipping container at the edge of camp, and it was already occupied when he got there — Devlin, the comms tech, sitting with his feet up, frowning at the HF set like it had insulted him.

"No luck?" Hazel asked, mostly to hear a voice.

"Kestrel station's not answering." Devlin said it lightly, but his feet came off the desk. "Scheduled check-in was oh-eight-hundred their time. Nothing. I've tried every hour since."

Hazel stood in the doorway with his tin cup going cold in his hand. The Kestrel River ran north out of the mountains eight hundred kilometers away, into country he knew only from maps and from a childhood of listening to his father talk about it the way other men talked about women or God. The Yarrow Expedition. Twelve people. Sixteen months of funding fights and permit delays, and then Marcus had gone upriver in June with a guide, a cook, a surveyor, and enough satellite equipment to file reports twice a week like clockwork, because the university's insurers demanded it and because Marcus Yarrow, whatever else he was, was punctual about his obligations to institutions. It was his obligations to people he'd never managed.

"They'll have a dead battery," Hazel said. "Or a storm. Or they're in a canyon — canyons eat signals."

"Yeah." Devlin turned back to the set, keyed the mic, ran through the call sign again. Static answered, thick and unbroken, the sound of an empty frequency. "Except they checked in Tuesday. Clean transmission, strong signal. And the weather window's been open all week. I called the met office."

"So call the university."

"I called the university." Devlin's voice had gone carefully flat, the way voices go when someone is trying not to hand you bad news too fast. "They said the expedition's field insurance lapsed in August. They're calling it an independent venture now. Their liability ends at the trailhead."

Hazel heard the words and felt them land one at a time, like stones dropped into a well.

"Lapsed," he repeated.

"Budget dispute, apparently. Your father pulled the team's funding rather than accept conditions on the survey rights." Devlin shrugged, apologetic, as though he'd personally drafted the contract. "So there's no institution to notify. No rescue coordination. Officially, there's no expedition."

The generator coughed somewhere across camp. In the static from the radio, Hazel could almost believe he heard breathing.

"He wouldn't miss a check-in," Hazel said. "Not two." He corrected himself before Devlin could: "One. He missed one today. One isn't anything."

"One so far." Devlin looked at him with the particular kindness of a man who processed emergencies for a living and had learned to ration his own. "Look — I'll keep the window open. I'll run the schedule all night. But you should know, I also called the regional authority upriver, and they told me the last confirmed position of any party on the Kestrel was a trading post logbook entry, three weeks ago. After that, nothing. That country doesn't have roads, Hazel. It barely has rivers you can count on in autumn."

Hazel drank the cold coffee. It tasted like the bottom of the pot, which is to say like every day for eleven months.

His father was sixty-one years old and had been walking into remote places since before Hazel was born. He'd been caught in flash floods, quarantined, detained at two borders, and once — a story Hazel had grown up on, before the stories curdled — had spent nine days in a collapsed cave system with a broken wrist and come out having mapped it. The man was a cockroach. The man outlived everything.

The man also hadn't returned a single letter in six years.

*He owes you nothing,* Hazel reminded himself. *And you owe him less.*

But the arithmetic didn't hold and he knew it. Because there was no one else. That was the thing that sat down inside his chest while Devlin keyed the mic again and static poured out like sand. The university had washed its hands. The funders were anonymous — Hazel had learned that much from the expedition's public filings, a shell company, an antiquities consortium, names that meant lawyers. The twelve people upriver had families, presumably, but families were scattered across three continents and none of them knew the river, and knowing the river was everything. On the Kestrel in late September, with the freeze coming, distance wasn't measured in kilometers. It was measured in days, and the days were running out at both ends.

Nobody was going to go look.

Unless somebody who knew the river went. Unless somebody with nothing better holding him to the world than a salvage dig he despised packed a bag and caught the mail boat, which left — he made himself remember — Tuesdays and Fridays, from the dock forty kilometers downstream, and took four days to reach the last settlement before the gorges.

"Hazel." Devlin had turned around in his chair. "I have to ask. Next of kin on the expedition manifest. It lists you."

"I know. He filed it in March." Before Hazel had decided whether to answer the one letter he'd sent — a single page, formal, listing itinerary and check-in schedule, no greeting, no signature beyond the typed name. His father had known he wouldn't reply. He'd listed him anyway. Hazel still hadn't decided what that meant, and now he might never get to.

"That makes you the contact of record. If I report them overdue tonight, it triggers a search-and-rescue assessment. Which will take a week to authorize, minimum, and cost money nobody has committed." Devlin paused. "Or you tell me to wait, and we keep trying the radio, and maybe tomorrow morning some grad student's voice comes out of the static complaining about the beans."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then you've lost a day. Maybe two." Devlin held his eyes. "I can't make this call for you. I'm sorry. I genuinely don't know what the right answer is."

Hazel set the cup down on the desk. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Underneath the steadiness, something was moving — the old resentment, six years of it, the whole archive of unanswered letters and a mother's funeral his father had attended standing at the back like a stranger paying respects to someone else's family. He reached for that anger the way you reach for a coat in cold weather. It was familiar. It kept a man warm. It required nothing of him.

*Let the river keep him,* the anger said. *He chose it over you. Let it.*

And then, underneath, quiet as groundwater: *the cook's name was Amara and she wrote to ask if your father ate enough, and you never answered her either.* And: *he's sixty-one.* And: *nobody else will go.*

That was the truth of it, the one that curdled the anger even as he held it. Resentment was a luxury of people who could afford to wait. Out past the last settlement, winter didn't care whose fault anything was. The snow would come down off the high country in October like a lid closing, and whatever was on the wrong side of it would stay there until spring, and by spring —

By spring, if anyone was alive, they wouldn't be.

"Keep the window open," Hazel said. "Don't file anything yet. Give me until Friday's boat."

Devlin nodded slowly. "You're going."

"I'm going to the dock." Hazel heard how it sounded and didn't correct it. "There's a guide works out of Ferro Landing — Sefa. My father hired her for the approach. She'll know the river better than anyone. If she won't take me up—" He stopped. He hadn't gotten as far as *if she won't*. "She'll take me."

"You haven't spoken to your father in years, have you." It wasn't quite a question. Devlin had been on the dig long enough to have heard the stories, or invented his own version of them; camps made their own weather.

"Six years," Hazel said. "Give or take."

"And you're going up a river that eats signals, ahead of the freeze, for a man who wouldn't cross the street for you."

Hazel looked at the radio, at the green glow of the frequency readout, at the static that was either emptiness or distance or twelve people being very busy very far away. He thought about the skull in its bag, the person it had been, the ledger where Petrakis had written *no value* beside a human life. He thought about how easy it would be to stay. Staying required nothing. Staying was a kind of death you could do with your boots on.

"He crossed an ocean for me once," Hazel said. "When I was nine. I don't remember what for. My mother used to say he was a man who showed up for the things he couldn't explain and vanished from the ones he could." He picked the cup back up just to have something in his hands. "Maybe this is the thing he can't explain. Maybe I finally show up for it."

Devlin studied him for a moment, then swiveled back to the set and began logging the failed contacts, timestamping each silence, building the paper record that would matter later if later ever came. "Friday's boat," he said. "If you hear anything before then—"

"You'll raise me on the shortwave at the landing. Yeah." Hazel paused in the container's doorway. The evening had gone copper over the spoil heaps; the pumps droned; somewhere a crew was laughing about something. The world down here went on being cheap and loud and survivable. "Devlin. If they check in — if it's my father's voice on that set — don't tell him I'm coming. Just tell him the boat leaves Fridays."

He walked out before he could hear whether Devlin agreed.

* * *

He packed that night, which didn't take long. Eleven months at a salvage dig teaches you to own nothing that can't be carried at a run. Two shirts, the good knife, his mother's compass — brass, dented, still true, the one object he'd taken from the house afterward and never explained to anyone. At the bottom of his rucksack, wrapped in a plastic sleeve, was the letter. He took it out and read it again by headlamp, though he knew it by heart.

*Field itinerary follows. Check-ins 0800/2000 local, Tuesdays and Saturdays, frequency as below. Do not attempt contact outside schedule. — M. Yarrow*

No *Dear Hazel*. No *your father*. As if the relationship were a formality the document couldn't accommodate. He'd read it forty times looking for the message underneath the message. There wasn't one. Or there was only this: *do not attempt contact*, which was Marcus Yarrow in miniature — a man who loved you best from a distance he controlled.

Hazel folded the letter away and lay in his bunk listening to the generators, and sleep came slow and shallow, full of brown water and a voice on a frequency that almost resolved into words.

At dawn he gave Petrakis his notice. The supervisor didn't argue; men left salvage digs the way rats left ships, and Petrakis had stopped pretending to be surprised by any of them. He counted out Hazel's pay in cash, which was how the company preferred grief to be settled.

"Yarrow." Petrakis said it as Hazel shouldered the pack. "The Kestrel. That's serious country this time of year."

"I know."

"Do you?" Petrakis looked at him with something that wasn't quite sympathy. "Because half the people who go up that river in autumn are looking for something. And the river doesn't care what it is. It takes the ones who hurry and the ones who hesitate same as each other."

"Then I'll try to be neither."

Outside, the morning was cold enough to see his breath. Forty kilometers to the dock, two days if he walked fast along the haul road, and the Friday boat waited for no one. Hazel set off with the sun in his eyes and his father's letter against his spine, and behind him the radio container shrank into the flat yellow morning, its antenna raised against the sky, listening for a voice that did not come.
