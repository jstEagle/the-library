## 2. What the River Keeps

The mail boat was a flat-bottomed thing called the *Marta Lee*, and it smelled of diesel, fish guts, and the particular despair of men who had lived too long in one place. Hazel Yarrow sat on a crate of engine parts with his knees against his chest and watched the Kestrel River unspool behind them, brown and patient, carrying everything downstream except the things that mattered.

Three days since the telegram. *EXPEDITION OVERDUE TWO CHECK-INS STOP FAMILY NOTIFIED STOP.* He'd read it forty times on the bus north, looking for something between the lines. There was nothing between the lines. There never was. The river company sent the same telegram when a boat ran aground as when a man drowned; grief was not their department.

He'd told himself he wasn't going. He'd told himself it for two full days while he packed.

"You're the son, then." The pilot was an old man named Brannic who spoke without turning his head, hands steady on the wheel. "Yarrow. The archaeologist."

"His son."

"Heard about you." Which meant Marcus had talked about him, which meant — what? That his father mentioned him to strangers on rivers? It shouldn't have landed the way it did, a small hot coal under the breastbone. Six years of silence, and apparently Hazel existed somewhere in the man's mouth.

"What did you hear?"

Brannic shrugged. "That he paid well and tipped better. That he asked questions nobody else bothered to ask." A pause, the wheel turning a few degrees against the current's shove. "That he went too far up this river for anybody's good."

"How far is too far?"

"Past the trading post, there isn't any far. There's just farther." Brannic nodded at the water sliding past. "River keeps what it keeps. Men come up here thinking they'll take something out. River decides."

Hazel didn't answer. He'd spent six years learning not to answer things like that — the kind of folk wisdom people offered instead of help. His mother would have loved it. She'd been the one who believed in places having moods, in weather meaning something. Marcus had believed in stratigraphy and carbon dating and the clean, cold religion of evidence, and somehow between the two of them they'd raised a boy who believed mostly in leaving.

The banks narrowed. Spruce crowded down to the waterline, black-green and dripping. Somewhere past the second bend the current picked up and the *Marta Lee* began to labor, and Brannic said, "Trading post's another hour. I don't go past it. Nobody goes past it this time of year, not with the water dropping."

"I need to get to Kestrel Fork. Base camp."

Brannic looked at him then, finally, a long assessing look. "Your father's camp."

"Yes."

"Hired guide came through last week. Woman. Bought supplies, took a canoe upstream." He turned back to the wheel. "Alone, which is its own kind of stupid, or its own kind of determined. Couldn't tell which."

Hazel filed it away. "Did she say anything? About the expedition?"

"Didn't ask. Up here you learn not to ask. People tell you or they don't." He spat over the side. "You want the Fork, there's a cutline trail from the post. Fourteen kilometers if the mud's frozen, longer if it isn't. Or you find her canoe."

* * *

The trading post was three buildings and a fuel drum orchard, run by a broad silent woman named Okonkwo who sold Hazel a coil of rope, a tin of matches, and a look that said she knew exactly why he was here and thought poorly of the plan. The cutline trail was worse than the river. It was fourteen kilometers of boot-sucking muskeg and deadfall, and by the time he saw smoke ahead through the trees his legs were shaking and his hands were bleeding in four places from spruce bark.

But it was smoke. Smoke meant people, and people meant answers, and he came into the clearing at a half-run before his body could argue him out of it.

Then he stopped.

Base camp should have been tents — four of them, Marcus traveled heavy — a cook shelter, supply crates stacked on pallets against the wet, a generator, a radio mast. What was left was wreckage arranged with a kind of terrible tidiness. The tents were slashed, not collapsed. Slashed along one wall each, deliberate, so everything inside was exposed but nothing carried off carelessly. The crates had been opened, their contents lifted out and laid on the ground in rows: trowels, brushes, sample bags, a rock hammer, jars of preserved food. Someone had gone through all of it, item by item, and taken almost nothing.

Almost. Hazel walked the rows slowly. No survey equipment. No camera cases. No field notebooks — he could see the outline where they'd sat in a waterproof box, a rectangle of dry wood in a lid furred with damp. And no oilcloth-wrapped anything, though he wouldn't have known to look for oilcloth yet. He only knew that someone had searched this place the way he searched a site: patiently, completely, with a list.

Not weather. Weather didn't slash tent walls. Weather didn't leave the food and take the paper.

"Touch anything and I'll break your wrist."

The voice came from the treeline, low and even, and Hazel turned with his hands already rising — some instinct older than thought. She stood between two spruces with a canoe paddle held like a spear, and she did not look like someone bluffing. Mid-thirties maybe, dark hair cut short and practical, a face burned brown across the cheekbones and pale around the eyes where a hood had done its work. Her jacket was expedition-issue, the kind Marcus bought for his crews, and it hung on her like it had been worn too many days without washing.

"I'm not touching anything," Hazel said. "I'm looking for my father."

She didn't lower the paddle. "Name."

"Hazel Yarrow."

Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, recalculation. "Marcus Yarrow's son."

"Yes."

"The son who doesn't speak to him."

The words went in clean, no armor anywhere. "Who are you?"

"Sefa." She let the paddle drop to rest against her shoulder, which was either trust or exhaustion, and Hazel suspected exhaustion. "I was their guide. I'm the reason your father's expedition got as far as it did, and I'm the reason it won't get any farther, because everyone I was guiding is gone."

"Gone where?"

"That," she said, "is the question I've been walking fourteen kilometers of mud to answer badly." She jerked her chin at the wrecked camp. "Come look at what they left me. Then we'll talk about gone."

* * *

She made him tea first, on a pocket stove at the edge of the clearing, because — she said — angry people make bad witnesses, and she needed his eyes fresh. It was control dressed as kindness, and Hazel respected it and resented it in equal measure. They sat on a fallen log facing the ransacked camp, two cups of bitter spruce-tip tea steaming in the cold, and she talked while he looked.

"They broke camp eight days ago. Moved upriver toward the canyon — the one every map calls impassable, because it is. Your father had a theory about a pre-colonial route through the range. Trade passage. He'd been chasing it for years." She glanced at him sideways. "You knew that much, surely."

"I knew he chased things," Hazel said. "I stopped keeping track of which."

Sefa absorbed that without comment. "They had a week of good weather left, maybe ten days. I told him that. I told him the canyon approach was a two-day push and the return trip needed margin. He said he understood." Her jaw tightened. "On day five he came to me and said the schedule had changed. He was sending me back downriver. Paid me through the month, gave me a bonus, said the remaining work was technical and light and he didn't want to risk me on the descent."

"And you believed him?"

"No." She said it flatly. "I've been fired before. This wasn't firing. Firing comes with reasons, and reasons come with arguments, and he wouldn't argue. He just — closed. Like a door." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "So I went downriver like a good employee, and I got as far as the trading post, and I sat there for two days telling myself it wasn't my business. And then I bought a canoe and came back up. Because I know what 'technical and light' means when a man won't look at you while he says it. It means he knows something you don't, and it's bad."

Hazel stared at the camp. The neat rows of abandoned tools. The dry rectangles where notebooks had been. "And when you got back—"

"Empty. Three days empty. Cold fire rings, no tracks worth reading — it had rained. Tents slashed, supplies sorted, nothing of value gone unless you count paper." She set down her cup. "So I did what I'm trained to do. I read it. And here's what it says, Mr. Yarrow. Whoever did this wasn't robbing them. They were inventorying them. You take food and fuel when you're desperate. You take instruments when you're a thief. You take notebooks and drawings and leave the rest—" she tapped the log twice, "—when you're looking for something specific, and you want the owners to know you found it."

"Found what?"

"If I knew that, I'd be less angry." But her voice said otherwise; she had a guess and was rationing it. "There were seven people in that expedition. Your father, six crew. Cook, two grad students, a surveyor, a photographer, and a general hand named Pell who'd worked with your father before. I've found signs of maybe four of them heading upriver after the camp was hit. Boot prints, old ones. Heading up, Mr. Yarrow. Toward the canyon. After whatever happened here."

"Some of them could be running from whoever did this."

"Or being driven ahead of them." She stood, restless, and looked north through the trees where the river bent away toward country neither map nor mercy covered. "Here's what I can't get past. Whoever hit this camp knew the expedition's schedule. Knew when the camp would be empty enough to search. That's not luck. That's information, and information up here travels by mouth or by radio, and I was the mouth, and I never talked." She turned and looked at him straight on, and her eyes were the gray-brown of the river itself, and very tired. "Which leaves radio, or a leak inside the crew, or someone who's been following them since the railhead. Any way you cut it, your father didn't lose control of this situation. He handed it away. Or had it taken."

Hazel felt the old resentment shift under him like scree. All summer he'd built a clean structure out of the silence: Marcus was careless, Marcus was obsessed, Marcus valued old bones over living family, and if the man had finally gotten himself killed up a river that didn't want him, it was tragic and it was typical and it was not Hazel's fault. Clean. Load-bearing. And now this woman was pulling stones out of it one at a time, showing him the mortar was somebody's intent.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "You don't know me."

"No." She considered him, head tilted, the way she might consider a feature in a trench — provenance unknown, value undetermined. "But I'll tell you what I see. A man who took a mail boat and a death-march trail alone, with no plan I've heard and gear I could fit in a bread bag, to a place he's never been, for a father he doesn't talk to. That's not a rescue party. That's a man running on something he hasn't named yet." She picked up her paddle. "I know that condition. I have it myself. So here's my offer, and it's the only one I'm making. We go upriver together tomorrow at first light. Two sets of eyes, one canoe. You want your father; I want my crew back, because I delivered seven people into this valley and I will not be the guide who brought home zero. We're not friends. We're not trusting each other past the next bend. But we're both furious at men who decided things without us, and fury paddles well."

Hazel looked at the ruined camp a moment longer. At the tidy rows. At the space where his father's handwriting used to live in a waterproof box, carried off by hands that knew exactly what they wanted.

Six years of not answering letters, and now the letter was written in slashed canvas and missing paper, and the only person who could read it was standing in front of him holding a paddle.

"One condition," he said. "When we find them — if we find them — I talk to my father first. Before anyone. Before you decide what's your business and what isn't."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Fine. And mine: nobody drowns us being brave. I've seen what this river does to brave."

They shook on it, her grip hard and cold, and it was the first honest thing Hazel had held in six years.

Above the bend, far off, thin as a wire, someone fired a single shot. Once. Then nothing.

Sefa's head came up. Hazel watched the tiredness drain out of her face and get replaced by something older and harder, and he understood that whatever this valley was about to cost them, the price list had just been posted.

"That," she said quietly, "isn't hunting season."
