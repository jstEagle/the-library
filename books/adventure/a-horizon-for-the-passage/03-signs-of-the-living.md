## 3. Signs of the Living

Sefa woke before dawn because the cold made sleeping past it impossible, and because the dead camp had a way of pressing on her chest all night, like a hand that meant no harm but didn't know its own weight.

She built the fire small. Small fires were a habit of poverty — you learned early that fuel was money and smoke was a message, and she had spent enough of both in her life to be careful with them now. She fed the flames dry larch bark from the bundle she'd cut yesterday, watching Hazel Yarrow sleep in his father's tent with his boots still on.

He'd slept in the dead man's camp without asking whose bedroll he was taking. That told her something about him. Either he didn't know, or he knew and decided not to care, and she hadn't figured out which yet. The guide who'd hired her — Brandt, the expedition's logistics man, a soft-handed talker out of the capital — had put her up in a proper dome tent at base camp and paid half up front, which should have been her first warning. People who pay half up front are buying something they're ashamed of.

She'd been three days into provisioning when the expedition came down off the water to resupply, and she'd met Marcus Yarrow exactly twice. Once in passing, once across a map table. He was a tall man gone stooped at the shoulders, gray in his beard, with hands that looked like they'd been broken and set by someone in a hurry. He'd looked at her for a long moment when Brandt introduced them — not the way men usually looked at a woman guide, checking her for decoration, but the way you look at a bridge before you drive a truck over it. Assessing whether she'd hold.

"Your reputation says you know the Kestrel headwaters," he'd said.

"My reputation is accurate."

"The maps don't." He'd said it flatly, no smile, and then he'd rolled up the map and walked away, and she'd spent the rest of the evening trying to decide if she'd been insulted or complimented.

Now she knew. Now she stood in the wreck of his camp, among the scattered tins and the slashed duffels and the radio with its antenna bent double like a straw, and she understood that Marcus Yarrow had known things about this river country that weren't on any map she owned, and that whatever those things were, they had gotten his people scattered into the trees like seed.

Hazel stirred. She heard him wake the way she'd learned to hear clients wake — the change in breathing, the rustle, the pause where a person remembers where they are and wishes they weren't. Then the tent flap opened and he came out into the blue-gray light looking like a man who'd slept in his clothes on purpose so he could start running the moment his eyes opened.

"Coffee?" she said.

He crouched by the fire instead of answering, held his hands out, stared into the flames. Up close, in daylight, he looked younger than his voice. Late twenties, maybe. Dark hair going untidy, a face that was handsome in a way that seemed to annoy him. There was a bruise-yellow shadow under one eye where he'd walked into a guyline in the dark last night, and he hadn't mentioned it and she hadn't offered sympathy. They had an understanding already, two days old and unspoken: no fussing.

"We need to talk about where they went," she said.

"I've been thinking about that." He turned a tin cup in his hands without drinking. "The camp's ransacked but the boats are all here. Three boats. Nobody leaves an expedition and takes nothing."

"They left in a hurry."

"Or were made to." He glanced at her. "You were their guide. You know this stretch better than anyone. Where does a team of nine go from here, on foot, without boats?"

Sefa poured herself coffee and sat on an overturned supply crate, choosing how much to give him. This was the part she'd been turning over since yesterday, the part she'd held back while they buried the cook's belongings and searched the camp and said wary, polite things to each other across a fire. Not because she wanted to withhold it. Because saying it out loud would make it real, and real things required decisions.

"There's only one direction," she said. "Up. Into the canyon."

"The maps call that section impassable."

"The maps call a lot of things impassable." She blew on her coffee. "The maps were drawn by surveyors who came through in high water season, saw the gorge walls come together, and turned around. But there's a shoulder on the east side where the rock steps down. My grandfather ran traplines up there. It's hard walking, but it's walking. The old people used it before the river changed course. There are places up there where you can still find their cairns."

"And you never mentioned this to Brandt?"

"He never asked about canyons. He asked about distances and dates." She shrugged. "Men like that want the river to be a schedule."

Hazel almost smiled. Almost. "So they went up the shoulder. Nine people, full packs, into terrain your own grandfather needed a lifetime to learn."

"Not nine." She let that sit. "I counted the tracks yesterday, before the light went. Boot prints heading up the east shoulder, fresh enough to be four days old. I counted six sets. Six, Hazel. And there were nine on the manifest."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the river a branch cracked under the weight of frost, loud as a shot, and neither of them moved until it settled.

"Three missing," Hazel said finally.

"Or three who stopped being able to walk. Or three who went somewhere else. Tracks don't tell you why." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "But here's what I keep coming back to. When they resupplied, Marcus Yarrow asked me one question I didn't understand at the time. He asked if I'd ever carried anything heavy up the east shoulder in October."

"October."

"It's September now. In October the shoulder gets ice on the north faces by mid-morning. Carrying anything heavy up there in October is either stupidity or desperation." She looked at him across the fire. "He wasn't asking as a curiosity. He was planning something. And whatever he was planning, he wanted it kept off the manifest, because when I inventoried their loads coming back upriver, there was a crate on the list that weighed forty kilos and was listed as 'survey equipment.'"

"You think it wasn't survey equipment."

"I think survey equipment doesn't get wrapped in oilcloth and tarred at the seams." She'd seen it herself, briefly — the crate open in Brandt's tent while he argued with the cook about rice weights, and inside, beneath a layer of false packing, bundles wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with pitch, each one the size of a large book. She hadn't touched them. She'd been paid to move cargo, not read it. "Three bundles. Heavy paper stock. Whatever they were, someone had taken care to make them waterproof, fire-resistant, and anonymous."

Hazel had gone very still. It was a particular kind of stillness she recognized from clients who'd just realized the mountain was bigger than they'd budgeted for. "Oilcloth," he repeated.

"You know something about that?"

"No." Too fast. Then, slower: "My father used to wrap things in oilcloth. Field notes, mostly. Photographs. He had a whole ritual about it — tarred seams, waxed cord, the works. I used to help him when I was a kid. He called it 'making things survive.'" His jaw worked. "I haven't thought about that in years."

"People don't carry forty kilos of survival up an impassable canyon unless what's inside has to survive more than weather," Sefa said. "Whatever's in those bundles, he treated it like it was worth lives."

"Whose lives?"

"That's the question, isn't it."

* * *

They broke camp fast, working around each other with the wordless efficiency of two people who'd discovered, in the space of two days, that the other one didn't do anything stupid. Sefa struck the fire and scattered it; Hazel repacked the tents and, after a moment's hesitation, folded the dead cook's bedroll and carried it himself rather than leave it for the animals.

They reached the foot of the east shoulder by midmorning. From below it looked like nothing — a wall of gray rock and spruce falling steeply to the water, seamed with scree chutes. But Sefa found the entrance the way her grandfather had taught her, by looking for what wasn't there: a line where the trees grew differently, older growth spared because generations of travelers had kept the path clear. A trail that had never been on a map because the people who used it had never needed one.

She started up and heard Hazel stop behind her.

"Sefa."

She turned. He was standing with one hand on a boulder, looking back down at the river, and his face had changed — closed in, private, the look of a man arguing with someone who wasn't there.

"When did he ask you about October?" he said. "Marcus. When did he ask?"

"At the resupply. Eleven days ago."

"Eleven days." He laughed, short and bitter, no humor in it. "Eleven days ago my father was standing on a dock asking a stranger whether she could haul forty kilos of secrets up a cliff in freezing weather. And eleven days ago I was—" He stopped. Started again. "I got a letter from him in June. First one in six years. Four sentences. It said the dig was going well, it said he was well, it said he hoped I was well. I threw it in a drawer. I was going to answer it. I kept meaning to."

Sefa said nothing. There was nothing useful to say, and she'd learned long ago that useless comfort was worse than silence.

"He trusted you with the question," Hazel went on. "He trusted Brandt with the crate. He trusted some salvage crew he barely knew with whatever's in that oilcloth — enough to drag them into a canyon the maps won't even admit exists. And his own son gets four sentences and a hope-you're-well." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, she could see him hating it, jaw tightening against the crack like a man bracing a split timber. "What does that make me? What did he think I was, that I couldn't be told?"

"Maybe he thought you were the one thing he couldn't afford to lose," Sefa said.

It came out plainer than she intended. Hazel looked at her sharply, and she wished she'd dressed it in something more deflective, because now it was just sitting there between them, true and exposed, and neither of them knew what to do with it.

"That's worse," he said quietly. "If that's true, that's so much worse."

"Then maybe it isn't true. Maybe he's just a man who's bad at the people he loves. Those exist. I've met several." She shouldered her pack. "Come on. Six sets of tracks went up this shoulder four days ago, and three of your father's people aren't accounted for, and we're standing here talking about feelings while the light's good."

Gratitude moved across his face — gratitude for the escape she was offering — and he took it, and they climbed.

The shoulder was everything her grandfather had promised and less. Loose rock over solid, spruce roots grabbing at boot edges, sections where the path narrowed to a ledge with the river a hundred meters of white noise below. Sefa led, reading the old cairns where they appeared — low stacks of flat stone, moss-eaten, some collapsed, one rebuilt recently enough that the stones were clean underneath.

She stopped at that one.

"Hazel."

He came up beside her. The cairn stood knee-high at the edge of the ledge, and on top of it, wedged under the capstone against the wind, was a strip of red survey tape. Fresh. And beside the cairn, placed deliberately in the center of the path where no one could pass without seeing it, lay a stone the size of a fist with letters scratched into its face — shallow, hurried, but legible.

Hazel crouched. Read it. Went pale in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"What is it?" Sefa said. "What does it say?"

He didn't answer right away. He picked the stone up carefully, the way you pick up something that might still be alive, and turned it toward her so she could see the scratched letters in the flat morning light.

Six words, cut deep:

**NOT THE WAY WE CAME.**

And beneath them, smaller, almost gouged through the stone:

**TRUST THE GUIDE.**

Hazel looked up at her. The wind came up the canyon and pulled at their jackets, and somewhere above them, in the gray maze of the high shoulder, six sets of tracks waited in the frost — and now, it seemed, so did a message that had been left for whoever followed.

For him.

"Trust the guide," Sefa repeated slowly, testing the words. Her eyes went from the stone to Hazel's face. "He wrote this before whatever happened to them happened. Which means your father knew someone might come after him." She paused. "And he knew it wouldn't be safe to send word ahead."

Hazel was still holding the stone. "No," he said quietly. "It means he knew *who* would come after him."
