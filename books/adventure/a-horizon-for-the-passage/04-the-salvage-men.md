## 4. The Salvage Men

The trading post announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and tallow and the sour sweetness of a cache going soft in the heat. Then the river bent, and there it was: a squat timber building with a false front painted the color of old milk, a dock of split pilings, and a yard littered with the archaeology of commerce. Fuel drums. Crates stenciled in three alphabets. A dog chained to a sledge runner, watching us with the flat patience of something that had learned not to expect much.

Sefa brought the boat in against the current, easy, like she'd done it a hundred times, and maybe she had. She hadn't said more than forty words since the base camp. Most of them had been instructions.

"Rules," she said now, cutting the engine, letting the current swing the bow toward the dock. "You don't talk about the camp. You don't talk about your father. You don't talk about what we're doing. If anyone asks, we're running supplies up to a fishing cabin. Mine. You're my hired hand."

"I can lie."

"You lied to me twice yesterday and I caught both. You lie like a man reading from a script he wrote an hour ago."

"That's not—"

"Your ears go red." She stepped onto the dock, coiling the painter without looking at it. "It's a tell. Don't talk."

The dog barked once, decided against enthusiasm, and lay back down.

Inside, the post was dim and dense — shelves to the ceiling, a counter of scarred pine, a woodstove ticking away though the day was mild. A woman with forearms like hawser rope sat behind the counter doing something complicated to a rifle part. She looked up, took us in — the boat, the gear, the fact that we'd come upriver rather than down — and went back to the rifle. Up here, that was courtesy.

"Two of the freeze-dried," Sefa said. "The white fuel. And whatever batteries you've got that aren't dead."

"Check the dates yourself." The woman nodded at a shelf. "Half this post is a museum that charges money."

While Sefa gathered supplies, I did what I always do in unfamiliar rooms: I looked for the exits, and then I looked at the people, and then I made the mistake of letting my eyes rest too long on the third thing.

There were four men at the corner table. They had the specific stillness of people who are pretending not to watch you, which is a stillness I knew from salvage camps — the contractors who came through at season's end, the ones who asked about what else was on the site, who else had claims, whether the permits were clean. They wore the uniform of the river: canvas and rubber and wool, nothing to distinguish them. But their boots were wrong. Too new. Too clean for men who'd supposedly come up this water. And there was a fifth chair at their table, empty, with a pack leaning against it that had a satellite phone antenna sticking out of the top like an accusation.

I looked away too late. The big one — red-bearded, hands like slabs of frozen meat — was already watching me with the mild interest of a man pricing something.

Sefa appeared at my elbow with the supplies and a look that said, without words, *don't*.

We were almost to the counter when the big one stood up. Not fast. The way a door closes.

"Morning." His voice was friendly the way ice is friendly — smooth until you test it. "You folks come up from the delta?"

"Fishing cabin," Sefa said. "Up past the narrows."

"Narrows." He nodded slowly, as though filing it somewhere. "Good water. We're a rescue party ourselves." He gestured at his table, his men, the pack with the antenna. "Looking for an expedition that's gone quiet. Archaeology outfit. Six of them. You seen anything on your way up? Camp gear, boats, sign of passage?"

Here it was. The question I'd been dreading since the dock, arriving in the mouth of a man who was almost certainly the reason it needed asking.

"No," Sefa said.

"Shame." He scratched the red beard. "Name's Voss. Meric Voss. We're contracted through the family. The expedition leader's got a son, they say. Estranged, they say, but blood's blood." His eyes moved to me, and they were pale and patient and absolutely without warmth. "You'd be him, then. Hazel Yarrow."

The name landed in the room like a dropped pan. The woman behind the counter stopped working on the rifle. The dog outside barked once at nothing.

I felt my ears go hot and knew, with a clarity that would have been funny under any other circumstances, that they were turning red.

"No," I said.

Sefa's hand found my arm. Light. Almost nothing. A full sentence in four ounces of pressure.

"Name's Cole," I said, which was the worst possible thing to say, because it was a lie I hadn't prepared, and my voice did the thing it does when I'm lying, which is to go up half a register at the end like a question.

Voss smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, the kind of smile a man practices in a mirror until it works on everyone except the people who matter.

"Cole," he said, tasting it. "Sure. Funny thing, Mr. Cole. The Yarrow boy's got a scar." He tapped his own jaw, left side. "Here. From a dig in the Tarthes when he was nineteen. Fell on a trowel, the story goes." His eyes went to my jaw, where the scar sat in plain view, and stayed there for a long, comfortable moment. "You've got one just like it."

"Common injury," I said. "Around trowels."

One of the men at the table laughed — a short, hard bark — and Voss turned his head just enough to silence him, then back to me, and the smile was still there but the temperature behind it had dropped ten degrees.

"Well," Voss said. "Small world. Lots of scarred men on this river." He stepped back, hands open, all friendliness again, and the performance was so smooth it made my skin crawl, because I understood suddenly that this was a man who had decided something and was in no hurry about it. "If you see that camp — or that boy — you'll send word downriver. The post here takes messages. There's a reward. Substantial." He said the word the way other men say *love* or *God*. "Family's worried."

"We'll keep an eye out," Sefa said.

"Appreciate it." He turned, then paused, and looked back over his shoulder with an expression of almost paternal kindness. "One more thing, Mr. Cole. The expedition carried a survey kit. Oilcloth wrapping. If that turned up — separate from the people, I mean — that'd be worth more than the reward. To the right buyer." His eyes flicked to Sefa, then back. "People are fragile. Paper keeps. A man in your position might find that arithmetic attractive."

"I'll remember the arithmetic," I said, and my voice cracked on *arithmetic*, and I wanted to die.

Voss laughed — genuinely, it sounded like — and clapped his hands once. "Good man. Good luck with the cabin."

We bought the fuel and the food and the batteries. The woman behind the counter rang it up in silence, and when Sefa counted out the bills, the woman said, very quietly, without looking up, "There's a back way out. Through the cache shed. Comes out above the dock." Then she went back to her rifle, and I understood that upriver, neutrality had a price, and sometimes it was paid in directions.

Sefa took the back way. I followed. Behind us, through the wall, I heard Voss's voice, easy and carrying: "—so the narrows, then. We'll want to check those cabins anyway. Be thorough."

* * *

We didn't speak until the bend took the post out of sight behind us. Sefa ran the boat wide around a snag, then let the current take us and finally looked at me.

"Cole," she said.

"It was the first name I—"

"You told a professional liar your name was Cole while your ears turned the color of the fuel drums." She shook her head, but there was no contempt in it. Something closer to recalculation. "He knew. The second you opened your mouth, he knew. He was never asking if you were Hazel Yarrow. He was asking what you'd do when he said the name."

"And what did I do?"

"You lied badly, and then you stayed at the table instead of running, which is the only reason he let it go." She steered around a deadhead with her knee. "He let us go because he's not sure. Because a man doesn't kill over a maybe when there's a river full of witnesses at that post. But he'll check the narrows. There are no cabins at the narrows, Hazel. There's one cabin at the narrows and it belongs to a man who winters in the delta and will tell Voss anything he wants for a bottle of whiskey. We have maybe a day before he knows we lied. Less if he flies."

"Flies?"

"Salvage crews charter. There's a strip at the mouth of the Kestrel canyon." She said it flatly, but I saw her jaw tighten. "I've flown out of it."

The river ran on, brown and indifferent, carrying us north toward the canyon the maps called impassable, toward whatever my father had sealed in oilcloth and carried into the high country like a man carrying a live coal.

"He offered it to me," I said. "The oilcloth. Right there. He thought I might sell."

"He was testing whether you knew what it was. Now he knows you know." She was quiet a moment. "That's worse, Hazel. Before, you were a stray. Now you're a competitor."

The current picked up as the valley narrowed, the water going from brown to a hard, glassy green, the banks closing in like a book being shut. Somewhere ahead, in the high silence, my father was either dead or running, and the man behind us had a plane and a satellite phone and the patience of someone who had already done his arithmetic about people.

Sefa opened the throttle. The boat climbed up onto its wake and the wind took the words out of my mouth before I could say any of the things I was thinking — that I was sorry, that I'd nearly gotten us killed with two syllables, that I had never in my life been so afraid of a smiling man.

Instead I watched the water and did my own arithmetic. Voss had a plane, four men, money, and a head start. We had a boat, a guide who'd been abandoned by my father's expedition and hadn't said why, and a scar.

The race, I understood, had started days ago — the morning the radio went quiet, maybe the morning my father sealed that oilcloth — and we were not in it. We were behind it, chasing the sound of it up a river that was already turning cold.

Sefa's eyes flicked to the sky once, twice, checking for the drone of a charter engine, and then fixed forward on the canyon mouth opening ahead of us like a door to somewhere the maps had given up on.

"Hold on," she said. "We're done being polite."
