## 5. Cold Camp

**HAZEL**

The temperature dropped like a verdict.

By four in the afternoon the light had gone thin and gray, and Sefa stopped walking without announcing it, the way she stopped for everything—no discussion, no vote. She stood in the lee of a granite shoulder where the Kestrel's tributary cut down through the trees, and she said, "Here," and that was the whole of the negotiation.

Hazel dropped his pack and felt six years of bad decisions in his shoulders.

They'd been following the river for three days. Three days of Sefa reading sign he couldn't see—broken alder, a boot heel pressed into mud, the ghost-logic of men moving fast and scared. Three days of him asking questions and her answering exactly as many as she chose. The camp had come apart at a bend in the river, tents slashed, a stove smashed open like a ribcage, and whatever had happened here had happened in a hurry, and Sefa had looked at the wreckage and said one word: "Voss," and then said nothing else for four miles.

Now she was building a fire in a way Hazel had never seen: a pit first, dug down below the frost line, then a screen of green boughs angled to eat the smoke. She worked without wasted motion, and he watched her the way he'd been trying not to watch her for three days, which was to say thoroughly and with a growing sense of trouble.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm learning."

"You're staring." She struck a match and fed it into a nest of birch bark. "Learning is fine. Staring costs extra."

The fire caught. In the pit it burned low and hot, a red coin in the dark, and the cold pressed in from every other direction like something with hands. Hazel got the tarp strung between two trees while Sefa melted snow in the pot, and when they finally sat, backs against the granite, knees almost touching, the silence between them had a texture he was starting to recognize. Not hostile. Not comfortable. Suspended.

She handed him a tin cup of water that tasted of smoke and pine needles. He drank. The heat of it went through him like a wire.

"Your father," she said.

Hazel looked at her.

"You flinch every time I say his name. You've done it since the trading post." She turned her cup slowly in her hands. "I'm not asking you to tell me anything. I'm telling you that I've noticed, and that noticing is my job, and you should assume I notice everything."

"That's a terrible quality in a person."

"It's an excellent quality in a guide." She took a sip. "It keeps clients alive."

Hazel turned the cup in his own hands. Above the trees, the first stars were coming out, hard and white, the kind of stars that made the sky feel less like a ceiling and more like a depth. His father had loved stars. Had taught him their names when Hazel was small enough to still want to learn them—before the digs got longer, before his mother's illness became the family's whole weather, before the funeral where Marcus stood at the graveside with dirt on his boots because he'd come straight from a trench and hadn't thought to change, or hadn't cared to, and Hazel had looked at that dirt and felt something in him close like a door.

"Six years," he said.

Sefa didn't move. That was the thing about her—she never pounced on an opening. She just held it open and waited.

"Six years since I've spoken to him. Not a letter. Not a call. He wrote twice, the first year. I sent the second letter back unopened." The words came out flat, and he was surprised at how easy they were to say, after how hard they'd been to carry. "He missed the funeral. He was on a dig. He sent a telegram. *Deeply sorry. Work unavoidable.* Four words. I did the math on that for a long time—what it costs a man to reduce his wife's death to four words, and what it means that he paid it."

"And then the expedition went quiet, and you came."

"The radio man at the post said nobody else would go. The university washed their hands—said the expedition was over budget and overdue and that the family should be notified." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm the family. That's the whole reason I'm here. Not love. Logistics."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. The fire ticked in its pit.

"You came up a river in November," she said, "after a man you hate, to find him."

"I told you. Logistics."

"You told me logistics. I watched you buy the boat ticket." She set her cup down. "You bought it before the radio man finished talking."

Hazel didn't answer. Because she was right, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them in the firelight, warm and unbearable.

**SEFA**

Sefa had learned a long time ago that the fastest way through a cold night was to give the cold something to work against, and the second fastest was to give the silence something to stand on. Hazel had given her his six years. It would be dishonest, and worse, tactically stupid, to let him carry the whole ledger.

"My turn," she said.

He looked over. In the pit-light his face was all planes and shadow, younger than he pretended to be and more tired than he admitted.

"I took the guiding job because of a debt," she said. "Not a metaphor. An actual debt. To a man named Rurik Voss."

Hazel went still.

"You know the name," she said. "Of course you know the name. You've been saying it like a curse for three days."

"He's the one who—"

"Who bought the salvage rights on the lower Kestrel, yes. Who employs the men who smashed your father's camp, if it was his men, and it moves like his men." She kept her voice level, the way she kept everything, because level was the only register that had ever kept her safe. "Two years ago my brother took a loan to buy a boat. The boat sank. My brother sank with it, which the river seemed to consider settlement enough, but Voss did not. Debt passes to family, where I come from. Not in law. In practice."

"How much?"

"More than a season of guiding pays. Less than a life is worth, though Voss would disagree." She fed a stick into the pit and watched it catch. "Your father's expedition needed a guide who knew the upper river. The pay was triple. The timing was a miracle. I said yes before I finished reading the contract."

"And then they went up the canyon and left you at the base camp."

"Left me with the boats and half the food and a note." She heard her own voice flatten further and couldn't stop it. "Marcus Yarrow wrote the note. He wrote that he was sorry, and that he'd pay me double for the days he didn't use me, and that if anyone came asking, I was to say I'd never been hired." A pause. "He was protecting me. I know that now. At the time it felt exactly like what it was—being made into a lie so a man could disappear."

Hazel was quiet. Then: "He does that. Makes people into lies. It's practically his method." The bitterness was old and worn smooth, a stone he'd been turning in his mouth for years. "You at least got a note. I got a telegram."

"Your mother," Sefa said. "The one from the funeral."

He looked at her sharply. "I never told you about a funeral."

"You told me about a telegram. Four words." She shrugged. "I notice everything. I told you."

The fire had burned down to a steady orange heart. Somewhere across the river a branch cracked under the weight of the cold, a single sharp report, and they both went still until the woods settled again. When Hazel spoke, his voice had changed—lower, stripped of its irony.

"She was the archaeologist, actually. Did you know that? Everyone assumes it was him. She was better. He'd say so himself, when he was drunk, which was the only time he told the truth about anything." Hazel's thumb moved along the rim of his cup, around and around. "She got sick on a dig in the eastern range. Bad water, bad winter, bad luck. She was dead in five weeks. He kept digging. Two more weeks. He finished the survey." He stopped. Started again. "I've told that story a hundred ways in six years and it never once made sense. Tonight it made sense for the first time and I hate it."

"Why tonight?"

"Because of the note he left you." Hazel's jaw worked. "He left you at the camp to keep you off the mountain, and he wrote you a lie to do it, and you're alive and angry about it. That's the shape of him. That's the exact shape." He looked into the fire. "I've spent six years deciding he didn't love her enough to come home. It's possible he loved us too much to come home, and I don't know which is worse, and I'm so tired of not knowing."

Sefa said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie or a cruelty, and she had a rule about both. Instead she reached over and took the empty cup from his hands, because his fingers had gone white around it, and refilled it from the pot, and handed it back, and the small transaction said what she wasn't going to say out loud: *I'm here. The night is long. We'll get through it the way everything gets got through—one necessary thing at a time.*

He took the cup. Their fingers touched on the metal, briefly, and neither of them moved away fast enough for it to mean nothing.

* * *

**HAZEL**

He woke once in the black hours, and she was already awake.

They'd slept in shifts, her insistence, and it was his turn, but she was sitting up against the granite with her blanket around her shoulders, watching the dark where the trees ended. He crawled over beside her. She didn't tell him it wasn't his shift. The cold was total now, the kind that made a person understand why animals shared dens, and he sat close enough that their shoulders touched, and the touch was less warmth than the promise of warmth, and it was enough.

"My brother's name was Teo," she said, out of the dark, unprompted. "You didn't ask."

"I was going to."

"No, you weren't. You were being polite. It's fine. Nobody asks, because asking makes it real." She pulled the blanket tighter. "He was twenty-three. He thought the river owed him a living because our grandfather had taken one from it. The river doesn't owe anybody anything. That's the first thing it teaches you and the last thing anyone learns."

Hazel watched the stars through the gap in the trees. They'd wheeled while he slept, the whole slow machinery of the sky, indifferent and enormous, and he thought about his mother lying in a tent in the eastern range looking up at this same machinery, and about his father looking at it too, on the other side of a canvas wall, finishing a survey.

"Sefa."

"Mm."

"Thank you. For covering for me at the trading post. I lied like a man reading from a cue card and you—" 

"You lied like a scholar," she said. "It's a different skill set. You'll never have it. That's fine. I'll always have it." A pause, and then, quieter, in a voice he hadn't heard from her yet: "Voss's men asked about the oilcloth. You remember that. They used the word *oilcloth*. Nobody uses that word unless someone told them it exists."

The cold moved through Hazel like a current.

"Someone on the expedition told them," he said.

"Someone on the expedition," she agreed, "or someone who talked to someone. Either way, they knew before we knew. Which means they weren't following the river looking for survivors." She turned her head, and in the starlight her eyes were dark and steady and very close. "They were following the river looking for a package. And everyone who knew about the package is dead, or left behind, or—" she gestured, small and grim, at the two of them, at the fire pit, at the whole frozen dark—"out here."

Hazel felt the night reorganize itself around that fact. The cold, the silence, the three days of trail—they weren't a search anymore. They were a race, and they'd been losing it since before they started.

"We should sleep in turns," he said. "Real turns. I'll take the rest of yours."

"You'll fall asleep."

"Probably."

She almost smiled. In the dark he saw it happen, a small rearrangement at the corner of her mouth, gone as fast as it came, and he understood that he would think about it later, and that thinking about it later was a problem he was not equipped to solve.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll wake you when the sky changes."

He lay back against the granite with his shoulder against hers because moving away had become, somewhere in the last hour, unthinkable. The fire ticked. The river ran on under its skin of ice, patient, keeping what it kept.

And it was just before dawn, in the gray hour when Sefa finally woke him with a hand on his arm, that they both saw it at the same time: on the far bank, upstream, a fire. Someone else's fire. Small, screened, built exactly the way she built hers—dug low, smoke-eaten, professional.

Sefa's hand closed on his arm and did not let go.

"That's not a trapper's fire," she whispered. "Trappers don't hide their smoke from other trappers."

The fire across the river burned on, a red coin in the gray light, and someone beside it moved, and the movement was the movement of a man standing watch.
