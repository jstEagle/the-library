## 6. The First Grave

We smelled him before we saw him.

Not decay — the cold had seen to that — but woodsmoke and old fat, the ghost of a cook's life clinging to his coat. Sefa stopped on the trail ahead of me, one hand up, and I watched her read the ground the way I'd learned to read a stratigraphy section: layers, disturbance, what didn't belong.

"Smoke," she said. "Two days old, maybe three."

"There's no camp here."

"No," she agreed quietly. "There isn't."

The river bent wide below us, gray-green and patient, and in the crook of the bend stood a lean-to of driftwood and torn canvas, half-collapsed, the kind of shelter a man builds when he thinks he'll only need it for one night. A thin thread of ash at its mouth. A pot overturned beside it, frozen into the mud.

And at the base of the rock face behind it, crumpled like something dropped from a great height, lay a man.

I don't remember deciding to run. I was just suddenly moving, boots skidding on scree, Sefa's voice behind me saying *careful* in that flat way she had that meant she thought careful was a waste of breath.

He was face-down. Big man, heavy through the shoulders, hands blunt and scarred from thirty years of kitchen work. His parka was expedition-issue, olive drab, and stitched inside the collar where the others had their names he had only initials: **T.O.** Tomas Okafor. I'd never met him, but I knew him from the manifest Sefa had copied out of the base camp office — the cook, forty-one years old, second season with my father's surveys. In the photograph taped to the manifest he was laughing at someone off-camera, holding up a fish like a trophy.

Now his neck was bent wrong and one arm lay beneath him at an angle arms don't go.

Sefa crouched beside him without touching anything. She'd been doing that since the base camp — this discipline of hers, hands hovering, eyes working. "Don't turn him yet," she said. "Look first."

"Look at what?"

"At everything. Then we turn him."

So we looked. The scree slope above the lean-to rose maybe sixty feet to a shelf of broken rock. Stones had come down — there was a scatter of fresh-fallen rubble across the shelter's roof canvas, a smear of displaced gravel down the slope's throat. It told a simple story. Man shelters under cliff. Cliff sheds rock in the night. Man dies.

It was a good story. That was the first thing that bothered me. On salvage digs I'd walked past a hundred staged scenes — looters rearranging a site to look like natural collapse, poachers dressing a snare line as windfall. People who lie with landscapes all tell the same kind of story: too clean, too convenient, every detail pointing exactly where they want your eye to go.

"Sefa." I kept my voice low, though there was no one for miles. "The rocks came down. But when?"

She understood immediately. Her jaw tightened. She picked up a fallen stone near the body — a chunk of shale the size of a loaf — and turned it over. The underside was dry. Dusty. Unmarked by rain or frost-heave.

"It hasn't rained in nine days," she said. "No frost since the cold snap started. If these fell two days ago, the undersides would be damp. They'd have taken moisture from the ground." She set the stone down with exaggerated care, like returning evidence. "These were placed. Or dropped from above by someone standing on the shelf."

The wind moved through the canyon and I felt it go through me too, cold finding every seam in my clothing.

"He was killed," I said. It wasn't a question anymore.

"Or he was dead already and someone wanted it to look like the mountain did it." She finally reached out and turned him, gently, the way you'd turn a log to see what lived underneath.

His face was calm. That was somehow worse than horror would have been. There was frost in his beard, gray shot through black, and around his left temple a wound I might have believed was rock-caused if I hadn't already stopped believing in coincidences — except that the blood had flowed *downward* along his cheek toward his ear, pooling and freezing in a way that meant he'd been lying differently when it happened. Moved after. Arranged.

Sefa went through his pockets with the same terrible gentleness. A folding knife. A tin of matches, half full. A stub of pencil. And folded into an inner pocket, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth — the same material, I noticed, that my father supposedly carried his secrets in — a piece of paper gone soft with handling.

She opened it and held it so I could see.

It was a list. Names. Seven of them. Six struck through in different inks, different pressures of hand. The seventh name was written but not struck: *M. Yarrow.*

Below the names, in a shakier script, someone had written: *who is left. who do we trust.*

"That's his handwriting?" Sefa asked.

"I don't know. I've never—" I stopped. The truth was ugly and simple: I wouldn't know my father's handwriting if it wasn't signed. Six years of silence will do that. You forget the small things first. "Maybe. Probably."

"Six crossed out," Sefa said slowly. "Six people accounted for. Dead or scattered, we don't know which. And Marcus Yarrow still unaccounted for." She looked up at me, and her eyes in the flat light were the color of the river, unreadable and moving fast underneath. "Your father was the last name on a dead man's list of who to trust."

* * *

We buried him because there was nothing else to do.

The ground fought us the whole way. Sefa found a spot above the flood line where a stand of birch grew thin and crooked out of the gravel, roots having done some of the digging for us over decades of freeze and thaw. Even so, the pickaxe bit an inch and rang. Bit an inch and rang. We took turns, and each turn got shorter, and neither of us said much.

I kept thinking about the photograph. The fish held up like a trophy. The laugh aimed at somebody off-camera. A man who cooked for strangers in the wilderness for two seasons running, who wrote lists of names to remind himself who was left alive, who trusted my father enough to follow him into a canyon the maps called impassable.

A man whose own mother probably still expected letters.

"You can stop," Sefa said. I'd been swinging longer than my turn. Sweat had frozen at my hairline.

"I'm fine."

"You're not. Give it here."

I gave it to her. Watched her work — economical, furious strokes, her breath tearing white out of her. She'd lost people; I knew that much about her, gathered in fragments since the base camp. She never offered details and I never asked, because the asking would have required answers in trade, and I wasn't ready to pay.

The grave, when it was done, was barely eighteen inches deep. We laid him in with his coat buttoned to the chin and the tin of matches in his pocket, because it seemed wrong to take them, and Sefa stacked stones over the shallow mound until it looked less like a grave than a cairn, which is what travelers build for the living, to say *this way, keep going.* Maybe that was the right instinct. Maybe the dead can point the way too.

I stood over it longer than I needed to.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about grief you can't afford: it doesn't wait politely outside while you handle survival. It comes in anyway, sits down in your chest, and makes itself comfortable while you're trying to calculate how many miles to the next water and whether your boots will last the pass. Every minute I spent hating whoever had put Tomas Okafor under those rocks was a minute I wasn't spending thinking about Voss's crew, about the days we'd lost, about the snowline creeping down the peaks like a tide coming in.

"We should mark the location," I said finally. My voice came out rough. "For whoever comes after. Police, surveyors, anyone."

"With what? We have no paper to spare." But she was already unpicking a thread from her cuff, and I watched her tie it to a birch branch — a small red flag no bigger than a fingernail, visible only to someone standing exactly where we stood. It wasn't much. It was what we had.

"And the list," I said. "The names. We should take it."

"We are taking it." She patted her breast pocket. "You think I'd leave it under stones?"

* * *

We made four more miles before dark, putting distance between ourselves and the grave because Sefa said staying near a body was staying near whoever made it one. We camped in the lee of a bluff, cold food, no fire, and I lay awake listening to the river and doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Six names struck through. One expedition of eleven. My father carrying secrets sealed in oilcloth, trusting a guide he then abandoned, writing warnings in places only pursuers would find. And somewhere behind us or ahead of us, men who killed a cook and dressed the mountain for it.

Someone was erasing witnesses. Working their way down a list. And the list had my father's name on it — last, unfinished, waiting.

I rolled onto my side. Across the dead fire ring, Sefa was a shape against the fainter shape of the sky, and I knew she wasn't asleep either, because her breathing had that deliberate evenness of someone pretending.

"Sefa."

"Mm."

"The way those rocks were placed. The blood. Whoever did it took time. Twenty minutes, maybe more, arranging a scene." I stared at the black bulk of the bluff. "That's not panic. That's not a fight gone wrong. That's a professional making sure the next people to find the body believe a story."

Silence. Then, quietly: "Yes."

"So they're not behind us cleaning up. They're *ahead* of us. Working the trail in order." My heart was doing something unpleasant against my ribs. "Which means they know the route. Which means either they caught someone from the expedition and made them talk—"

"—or someone from the expedition is helping them," Sefa finished.

The words sat between us in the dark like a third person at the fire.

I thought of the manifest again. Eleven names. Six struck through. Four unaccounted for besides my father — and any one of them could be walking this same canyon a day ahead of us, or a day behind, wearing the same boots, carrying the same gear, indistinguishable at a distance from a friend.

"How do we tell?" I asked. "If someone comes up the trail toward us. How do we know?"

Sefa was quiet long enough that I thought she'd gone to sleep after all. Then:

"We watch how they walk," she said. "People who belong in this country walk like they forgive the ground. People who are lying walk like they're managing it." A pause. "And we watch their eyes when they see us. Relief is easy to fake. Recognition isn't."

I pulled my sleeping bag tighter and looked up at the strip of stars the canyon allowed. Somewhere above the pass, my father was either alive or becoming the seventh name on a dead man's list, and the only inheritance he'd ever given me was a trail of warnings meant for strangers.

Tomorrow we'd climb. Tomorrow we'd find whatever he'd left at the fork in the river — if he'd left anything, if it hadn't already been found and carried away by hands that arranged rocks for a living.

But tonight, in the dark, one image kept returning: Tomas Okafor's list, six names struck through, and the seventh written carefully, hopefully, in a dying man's hand.

*M. Yarrow.*

Still unstruck. Still trusted.

I intended to find out why — before someone else decided the list was finished.
