## 7. Cairn at the Fork

The river forked at dawn, and Sefa knew before they reached it that the maps had lied.

Not lied, exactly. The survey she'd memorized in the trading post office — the one the expedition's outfitter had photocopied for her, creased and coffee-ringed — showed a single channel braiding into three, all of them marked as dead ends above the gorge. But rivers up here didn't end. They went underground, or they went vertical, or they waited behind a wall of scree for someone stubborn enough to look twice. She'd grown up with water like this. It kept its own counsel.

Hazel stood at the gravel bar where the channels split, hands jammed in his coat pockets, breathing white. He hadn't slept well. Neither of them had, but he wore it differently — she hoarded her exhaustion like currency, spent it carefully; he let it sit on him openly, a debt he'd stopped trying to hide. Six days upriver from the ransacked camp, and his face had begun to take on the color of the country: gray stone, gray light, gray water moving under ice skin at the edges.

"Which one?" he asked.

She crouched and touched the current in the middle channel. Cold enough to burn. "None of them, probably."

"Probably."

"Your father didn't come up a river channel with pack animals and eleven people." She straightened. "He came up something that could be walked. Which means he came up the bank, or he came up—"

She stopped. Because she'd seen it an hour ago and filed it without thinking, the way you file things that don't fit until they do: a shape on the left-hand rise, upstream where the valley pinched against the canyon throat. A pile of stones. Man-made. Deliberate.

"—or he told us," she finished.

* * *

The cairn stood chest-high on the bench above the fork, built from flat river slabs stacked with a mason's patience, capped by a stone the size of a loaf. Someone had wanted it found. Someone had also wanted it to last through weather that ate everything else — tents, radio masts, people.

Sefa circled it once. Hazel hung back, and she understood why without asking. You could read a thing like this two ways: as a message, or as a grave marker. After the cook's body they'd buried two days ago — Tomas, the cook, folded into frozen ground with Hazel swinging the pick until his palms bled — neither of them was eager to assume the kinder reading.

"It's not a grave," she said. "No mound. No depression. And it's too neat."

"You're sure."

"I've seen both kinds." More than she'd ever wanted to. She set her hand on the capstone and felt the cold of it through her glove, deep and mineral, and beneath the cold something else — a ridge of wax, a seam. "There's a tin under this."

They worked the capstone off together. Beneath it, wedged in a cavity lined with birch bark, sat a battered mess tin, its lid soldered shut over a strip of oilcloth. Sefa pried the lid with her knife and drew out a roll of paper wrapped in waxed canvas, tied with copper wire, and a second object: a strip of leather, soft with handling, stamped with letters that had been painted and repainted so many times they'd gone glossy.

She turned it over. *M. YARROW.* An old expedition tag, the kind universities issued twenty years ago, back when Marcus Yarrow was still a name on grant applications instead of a rumor her own employers argued about.

"That's his," Hazel said. His voice had gone strange. Flat, careful, the voice of a man carrying something breakable across ice. "That's — I've seen that tag. On his kit. When I was a kid."

"He carried it up here," Sefa said. "On purpose."

"Why would a man carry a twenty-year-old luggage tag up a river nobody surveys?"

She didn't answer, because the paper was answering for him. She unwound the wire, unrolled the oilcloth, and there was the handwriting — angular, left-slanting, pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath. She'd never seen Marcus Yarrow's hand before, but she knew it instantly anyway. It was the handwriting of a man who expected to be doubted.

It said:

*If you are reading this, you followed the river, which means you did not know me, or you knew me badly. Either way, listen.*

*We came up the north bank to the gorge mouth and no further. The canyon below the col is watched — not by us. Do not come the way we came. There is a pass east of the twin peaks, marked on no map I trust, that crosses above the icefall. It is hard and it is high and it is open maybe three more weeks. Take it or turn back. Those are the only honest options.*

*I have left what matters where only someone who knows me could find it. If you are my son: I am sorry. That will have to do for now.*

*Do not trust anyone who asks about drawings.*

*Sefa — if it is you — the debt is paid. Get him over the mountain.*

Sefa read it twice. The second time wasn't for comprehension. It was for the weight of it, the way you weigh a stone in your hand before deciding whether to throw it or keep it.

Then she handed it to Hazel and watched him read his father's apology for the first time in six years.

* * *

He read it the way he seemed to do everything since the base camp — like a man defusing something. Slowly. Lips moving slightly on the hard words. When he reached the line with his name in it, his jaw worked once and went still.

"She knew about me," he said finally. "You. He wrote to you."

"He hired me. Of course he—" She stopped herself. That wasn't what Hazel meant, and pretending otherwise would be its own small cruelty. "Yes. He knew who I was. He knew we'd end up together on this trail, or he guessed it. He planned around it."

"Six years." Hazel's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Six years I got nothing. Not a letter. Not a phone call answered. My mother died and he sent flowers with a card signed by his department secretary, and I told myself that was who he was — a man who couldn't be bothered. And meanwhile he's up here writing notes to a stranger about getting his son over a mountain."

"I'm not a stranger."

"No." He looked at her then, and there was something raw under the anger, something that hadn't decided yet whether it was gratitude or grief. "No, apparently you're the person he trusted. That's the part I can't — six days I've been telling myself he changed, that the man who cut us off was some version of him that happened after Mom. And now there's a note in his handwriting proving he was exactly the same man. Careful. Thorough. Thinking three moves ahead. He just did it for other people."

Sefa crouched and began restacking the capstone, giving him the privacy of a task to watch. It was a kindness her grandmother had taught her without ever naming it: when a person is breaking, put something in their hands.

"You think he cut you off," she said to the stones. "Maybe. Or maybe you're reading a letter written by a man who already knew someone was following him, killing his people, and asking about drawings — and you're angry he didn't write you a different letter, a warmer one, from inside a war zone."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Most true things aren't."

* * *

They walked the north bank through the morning, following the route the note described, and Sefa found herself cataloguing evidence of Marcus Yarrow the way she'd once catalogued game trails: here, a blaze cut into a spruce at shoulder height, healed over but recent; there, a flat stone placed in a stream crossing so boots wouldn't fill with water; farther on, a cache barrel wired into the roots of a leaning birch, empty but intact, its lid closed properly because even fleeing men leave the wilderness tidier than they found it if they were raised right.

Every sign said the same thing. This was not a man blundering into the mountains with a doomed expedition. This was a man moving deliberately through country he understood, leaving a trail designed to be read by friends and missed by enemies.

"He anticipated them," Hazel said at midday, when they stopped to melt snow for tea. "Voss's crew. He knew they'd come. How long has he known?"

"Longer than us." Sefa fed the stove a pinch of fuel and watched the blue flame catch. "The note says the canyon is watched. Past tense. He'd already been warned when he built that cairn."

"Warned how?"

"How do you think?" She let it hang. They'd both done the arithmetic after Tomas's body — the fall that was too clean, the rocks arranged just so, the absence of the man's boots, which they'd found half a mile downstream snagged in a willow, dry. Somebody had taken a dead man's boots and walked away in them. "Someone talked. Someone inside, or someone close enough to inside to know the route and the schedule."

Hazel stared into the flame. "My father doesn't inspire that kind of loyalty. People worked for him because he paid and because the work was interesting. That's what my mother used to say. She said he collected brilliant people the way other men collect knives — useful, sharp, and you never quite know which way they'll point."

"And your mother? What did she collect?"

Something moved through his face — surprise, then a grief that was older and easier than the new kind. "Records," he said. "Music. She had this wall of them, hundreds, alphabetized. She said you could tell everything about a person by what they played when they thought no one was listening." He almost smiled. "She'd have hated it up here. No electricity. She'd have hated it and then she'd have learned every bird call within a week and started correcting the guidebooks."

Sefa found she wanted to hear more of that — the mother, the records, the life Hazel had come out of — and caught herself wanting it, and filed the want away with the other things she wasn't ready to spend. "Your father loved her," she said instead. "I saw it in the way he talked about home. Once. At the outfitter's, before we shipped out. He asked if I had family and I said no, and he said, 'Neither do I, anymore,' and the way he said it — that wasn't a man who'd forgotten. That was a man serving a sentence."

"You don't know that."

"No," she said. "But I know men who've lost things, and I know men who've thrown things away, and they carry themselves differently. He carried it like loss."

Hazel was quiet a long time. The stove hissed. Somewhere above them, on the scree slopes, a rock clattered down — just frost release, just the mountain shrugging — but they both looked up, and their eyes met over the flame, and for a moment neither of them pretended it was nothing.

"We should move," he said.

"Yes."

* * *

The twin peaks came into view an hour before dark, two broken teeth against a sky going the color of pewter, and between them, barely visible at this distance, a notch of paler stone where the pass cut through. It looked impossible from here. Everything worth doing looked impossible from a distance; that was practically the definition.

Sefa studied it through her binoculars while Hazel made camp in the lee of a granite shelf, and what she saw made her lower the glasses slowly and stand very still.

"What?"

"There's smoke," she said. "Or there was. Look at the col — see the dark smudge on the snow, left side, in the shadow of the rock?" She passed him the binoculars. "That's a fire site. Recent. Days, not weeks."

Hazel found it. She watched his shoulders change.

"The note said three weeks," he said. "Open maybe three more weeks. That was written — when? Before the camp was hit. Before Tomas. We're inside that window, but the edges are closing."

"Then we climb tomorrow," Sefa said. "Hard day, long day. Above the icefall by nightfall tomorrow or we don't go at all."

"And if Voss is already on it?"

She took the binoculars back and swept the pass one more time, patient, thorough, the way her father had taught her to sweep a horizon looking for stock that had strayed. The smudge of old fire. The clean white of the upper snowfield. And then — at the very lip of the notch, so small she might have invented it — movement. A figure, or the suggestion of one, upright, brief, gone.

She lowered the glasses.

"Sefa."

"Someone's up there," she said. "One person. Watching the approach." She met Hazel's eyes. "Whoever it is, they're standing exactly where your father's note told us not to stand — where anyone coming the way we came would be visible. Which means either they haven't read the note..."

"...or they wrote it," Hazel finished.

The wind came down off the peaks then, first taste of the real cold, the kind that didn't negotiate, and Sefa pulled her collar up and thought about the last line of the letter again. *Get him over the mountain.* As if Marcus Yarrow had known — had calculated, the way he calculated everything — that by the time his son read those words, the trail behind him would already be a lie.

Tomorrow they'd find out which lie.

Tonight, she banked the fire small and took first watch, and above the twin peaks the sky began, very quietly, to snow.
