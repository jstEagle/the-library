## 8. Voss Catches Up

The river told us before the man did.

Sefa heard it first — a change in the water's voice, a flattening where the Kestrel spread wide over gravel shallows before pinching back into its gorge. She stopped on the trail with her head cocked, listening the way other people read, and said, "Crossing ahead. Good one. Half a day's walk."

"Good how?"

"Good enough to take a loaded boat across in summer. Good enough for us in October." She adjusted the strap of her pack and looked at me sideways. "Which means it's good enough for anyone following us."

I hadn't told her anyone was following us. I hadn't had to. We'd been finding signs for two days now — a cigarette butt pressed into mud that hadn't been there when we'd passed going upriver, boot prints overlapping ours on the portage trail, once, impossibly, the smell of woodsmoke drifting down through the pines when there was no camp marked anywhere on Sefa's maps. She'd catalogued each one without comment, filing it away behind those dark, patient eyes, and I understood she was waiting for me to say it out loud.

"They're behind us," I said now. "Aren't they."

"Not behind." She started walking again. "Beside. Across the river, maybe. Voss doesn't chase people, Hazel. He herds them."

* * *

We came down to the crossing late in the afternoon, with the light already going bronze and long across the water. The river ran maybe eighty meters wide here, shallow enough to show its gravel ribs, fast enough in the channel to make a white seam down the middle. On the far bank the forest climbed away toward the pass in dark ranks of spruce. It was, as Sefa had promised, a good crossing — which meant it was also the only one for miles, and we both knew what that made it.

A trap with a floor of moving water.

"We go through the shallows on foot," Sefa said, crouching at the bank, reading the current. "Stay together, lean upstream, and don't stop in the channel no matter what. Twenty minutes and we're on the far side."

"And if someone's watching?"

"Then we're already being watched, and standing here arguing about it changes nothing."

She was right, which was becoming a habit I both relied on and resented. We were halfway through pulling off our boots to tie them around our necks when the voice came from the trees behind us.

"You'll want to keep your boots on for this conversation."

I turned so fast my pack swung me half off balance. Three men stood at the treeline, maybe forty meters back, arranged with an ease that wasn't ease at all — the tall one on the left had a rifle slung loose across his chest, barrel down, and the one on the right was chewing something slowly, watching us with the flat interest of a man watching weather. The man in the middle was smaller than I expected. Neat. Grey at the temples, a face like something carved for a bank advertisement, hands folded in front of him like a man waiting for a train.

Voss.

He looked nothing like the picture the word *salvage crew* had built in my head. No scars, no swagger. He looked like a man who did crossword puzzles and tipped well, and somehow that was worse.

"Hazel Yarrow," he said. My name in his mouth sounded like a file number. "Marcus's boy. You have his jaw. And his stubbornness, I'm told, though I understand you two weren't close enough for him to have shared much of anything with you."

Every word was designed. I could feel the design of them, the way you feel a hook working under skin. Beside me Sefa had gone very still, and I saw her hand drift, casual as adjusting a cuff, to rest near the knife at her belt.

"You have me at a disadvantage," I said. "You know my name."

"I make it my business to know names." He took a few steps closer, unhurried, stopping where the gravel was dry. "Elias Voss. My associates are Renn and Cole. We're doing salvage work upriver — geological survey, some historical recovery. When word reached us that Professor Yarrow's expedition had gone silent, we naturally offered assistance." He smiled without engaging any part of his face above the cheekbones. "You're looking for him too, I imagine. Family loyalty. Admirable. Though I confess I'm curious why you came armed with a guide and a grudge instead of calling the authorities."

"The authorities are four days' travel from here," Sefa said evenly, "and the river doesn't wait."

"Practical," Voss said, inclining his head to her. "You must be Sefa. Also admirable. Your last employer spoke highly of you right up until he stopped paying you. I hope that's not a pattern."

Something moved behind her eyes, quick and cold, and I realized he'd done his homework on both of us. He knew about the abandoned contract. He knew exactly which wounds to press.

"What do you want?" I said.

"Directness. Thank you. Marcus raised you better than you think." He clasped his hands again. "Your father found something in that canyon. Something old, something valuable, something that belongs — by every legal instrument I hold — to the interests I represent. He took certain documents with him when he left camp. Survey drawings, field notes, perhaps artifacts. I want to know where he cached them."

"And if I don't know?"

"Then this is a short conversation and a wasted walk for all of us." His tone didn't change at all; that was the frightening thing. "But I don't think it's short. I think Marcus told you something, or left you something, or trusted you with something — because whatever else that man is, he is not careless, and men who aren't careless don't send their sons coded messages through trading post radios."

My stomach dropped. He knew about the radio message. Of course he did. The trading post was a bottleneck; everything that moved up or down the river passed through it, including words.

"He sent me a note saying he was fine," I lied.

It was a bad lie. I felt it leave my mouth already dead. Voss didn't even blink.

"He sent you coordinates," Voss said gently. "Or a description. Or a name. It doesn't matter which. Here's what does matter." He reached into his coat and produced a slim envelope, held it up between two fingers. "Fifty thousand. Now, for the location of the cache. Another fifty when the documents are recovered. That is more money than you will earn in twenty years of digging in other people's garbage, Mr. Yarrow, and it requires nothing from you but a sentence."

Behind me, the river ran on, indifferent. Somewhere above the treeline a bird called once and went quiet, as if even it understood the terms of the meeting.

"He's my father," I said.

"Yes," Voss said. "And he abandoned you for six years to dig holes in the ground. Forgive me, but I've read the same letters you have — or rather, the absence of them. Loyalty is a lovely word, but it spends poorly. Fifty thousand spends beautifully." He let the envelope drop onto the gravel between us, deliberate, almost gentle. "Think of it this way: you're not betraying him. You're simply being paid what he never paid you in attention."

I looked at the envelope. I'm not going to pretend I didn't look. Fifty thousand dollars lying on a riverbank, and six years of resentment whispering in my ear that I owed that man on the ground nothing, less than nothing, that he'd chosen dirt and dead civilizations over his own son and here at last was the world offering me compensation with interest.

And underneath the whisper, quieter but older, my mother's voice: *Hazel. Some things you don't sell, because once they're sold they're never yours again.*

I picked up the envelope. Voss's smile widened a fraction, expectant.

I walked three steps to the river's edge and threw it in. The current took it instantly, a pale flash swallowed by the brown water, gone before the ripples closed.

"No," I said.

For the first time, something flickered across Voss's face. Not anger. Interest. The look of a man revising an estimate upward.

"Well," he said. "There it is."

"There what is?"

"Sentiment." He sighed, genuinely regretful, a man closing a file. "It's always sentiment. Money moves mountains, Mr. Yarrow, but family moves fools." He glanced past me at the river, then at the light dying in the west, and made a small calculation with his eyes that I couldn't follow. "Renn. Cole. We're done here."

"That's it?" I heard my own voice crack with disbelief. "You're just letting us go?"

Voss looked at me then, fully, and I understood that I had misread everything about this encounter. This had never been a negotiation. It had been a measurement.

"Letting you go," he repeated, tasting the phrase. "Mr. Yarrow, I don't need to hurry. Winter will do my work for me — it always does. Every day you climb toward that pass, the snowline comes down to meet you, and snow erases trails, buries caches, kills the unprepared. Your father learned that lesson years ago. Perhaps you'll learn it faster." He began turning away, then paused, courteous to the last. "One courtesy, since you declined mine. The cook — Anselm — fell at the second canyon. A tragedy. Accidents happen so often in country like this. Do be careful."

The name hit me like cold water. We hadn't told anyone about the grave. We hadn't told anyone we'd found it.

Sefa's hand was on my arm before I could speak, gripping hard, a warning transmitted directly through muscle. I swallowed whatever I'd been about to say, and it went down like a stone.

"Walk north," Voss said pleasantly, already retreating into the trees, his men folding around him like water closing. "Or don't. Either way, I'll see you at the top."

And then the forest had him, and there was only the river, and the envelope's ghost somewhere downstream, and the sound of my own breathing too fast in the cold air.

* * *

We didn't cross that evening. Sefa wouldn't allow it — not with the light failing and three men somewhere in the woods who knew exactly where we'd sleep. We went back up the trail forty minutes in the gathering dark, off the main path, and made a cold camp in a hollow of granite with no fire and no talk, sharing a blanket against a night that dropped below freezing before full dark.

I lay staring at the black shapes of the spruce and replayed it, frame by frame, hating every frame.

"I threw fifty thousand dollars in a river," I said finally.

"Yes."

"It seemed like the right amount of dramatic at the time."

"It was stupid," Sefa said, without heat. "And it was the only thing you could have done. Both things are true." She shifted beside me, and I felt more than saw her checking the darkness beyond the hollow, the way she'd been checking it since the treeline. "He let us go because he wanted to. Understand that. If Voss wanted us dead, we'd be dead. He's telling us something."

"I know what he's telling us. He doesn't need to hurry."

"Do you understand what that means, though?" Her voice was low, stripped of everything but the facts. "It means he thinks time is on his side. It means he believes winter will kill us or turn us back, and either way the mountain delivers your father's secrets to him in spring, wrapped and frozen and free of charge." A pause. "He's almost right. Almost. What he doesn't account for is that we might be faster than he thinks, and stupider than he plans for."

Despite everything, despite the fear sitting in my chest like swallowed ice, I laughed — a short, involuntary bark that I muffled against my sleeve.

"Stupider than he plans for," I repeated. "Put that on a headstone."

"If we're careless, it won't need engraving." But I heard the ghost of a smile in it. Then, quieter: "He knew Anselm's name, Hazel. He knew how the cook died. Which means either Voss's men killed him—"

"—or someone from the expedition told him," I finished. The thought had been circling me all evening like a wolf I refused to look at directly. Someone who'd been there. Someone who'd stood at that graveside with frozen hands and then walked away to report everything to the man paying best. "Someone's still alive out here. And they're working for him."

The wind moved through the spruce above us, and for a moment it sounded almost like footsteps.

"Sleep," Sefa said. "In shifts. I'll take first."

I didn't sleep anyway. I lay in the dark doing arithmetic I didn't like: four days to the pass if the weather held, and the weather was not holding — the clouds stacking up in the northwest had a weight to them I was learning to read, a bruised, pregnant grey. Behind us, a man with money and rifles and infinite patience. Ahead of us, a canyon wall with a secret carved into it, and somewhere on the mountain between, a traitor wearing a dead expedition's boots.

And my father, alive or dead, at the end of all of it, holding answers I'd crossed a country to get and wasn't sure I could survive receiving.

Near dawn, Sefa shook my shoulder and took her rest, and I sat watch with the rifle across my knees, watching the east go from black to iron to a thin, bloodless rose. On the far side of the river, faint in the first light, I could see smoke rising from a camp I couldn't see — one thin thread of grey, unhurried, climbing into the cold morning air.

He really wasn't hurrying.

I woke Sefa when the sun cleared the ridge, and we broke camp without words, and went down to the crossing with our boots tied around our necks and the river running cold and quick between us and everything my father had tried to tell me.

Halfway across, waist-deep in water that burned like a lie, Sefa grabbed my arm and pointed upstream.

On the far bank, staked upright in the gravel where the current would keep it visible, was a second envelope. Weighted with a stone. Waiting for us.

Voss hadn't even bothered to write a new note.
