## 9. The Passage Wall

The canyon narrowed for two days until the walls leaned so close they swallowed the sky, and then, on the third morning, Sefa stopped walking because the river did.

It didn't end. It went *into* the mountain — a black slot in the rock where the Kestrel poured itself through a gap no wider than a wagon, roaring, and came out the other side, somewhere below, in a country neither of them had ever seen. She stood at the lip of it with the spray cold on her face and understood why every map she'd ever owned drew this place as blank paper. The cartographers hadn't been lazy. They'd been honest.

"Maps call it impassable," Hazel said behind her. He was breathing hard from the climb, and his voice had that flat careful quality it took on when he was trying not to hope.

"The maps are right." She turned. "For anyone trying to follow the river."

"But not for anyone following my father."

She didn't answer. She'd learned over nine days of trail that Hazel Yarrow needed silence to think, the way other men needed talk. So she let him stand there working it out while she scanned the canyon wall instead — north face, where the sun hit longest and the lichen grew thinnest — and it was because she was looking at the rock and not at him that she saw it first.

"Hazel."

He came to her shoulder. She pointed.

At first glance it was nothing: a seam in the stone, a shadow, a trick of the morning light running down the wall like water. But the light moved and the shadow didn't. It climbed. Straight up, a thread of darker stone stitched into the grey, rising from a shelf of rubble at the base of the cliff toward a ledge two hundred feet above where something — a shape, a regularity, an *intention* — broke the natural line of the rock.

"That's not a crack," Hazel said.

"No."

"That's steps."

"It might be steps."

They stood looking at it the way you look at a word written in a language you almost know. Then Hazel dropped his pack right there on the scree and started down toward the base of the wall without another word, and Sefa followed, because there had never really been any question of following or not following. There had only been the question of how far.

* * *

The rubble shelf was older than the slide that had half-buried it. That was the first thing Sefa understood, crouching at its edge, brushing frost-grit away with her bare hand. The slide had come off the wall above sometime in the last century — she could see the fresh scar of it, pale rock against dark — but beneath the fallen stone the surface was worked. Dressed. Fitted. Blocks laid in courses so fine she couldn't have slid a knife between them, and each block cut with a shallow channel that channeled snowmelt away from the stair's foundation the way a roof gutter channels rain.

Whoever built this had known winter. Had built *for* winter, a thousand years before anyone thought to draw a map that admitted the place existed.

"Hazel." Her voice came out strange. "Come look at this."

He was already ten yards along the shelf, standing very still beside a slab of stone that leaned against the cliff face like a door left ajar. As she reached him he reached out and touched it — just touched, fingertips, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal — and the slab rocked slightly on some balance point hidden in the rubble, and beneath it, protected from a hundred winters by its own fall, the stair began.

Steps cut into the living rock. Narrow, steep, each one worn into a shallow concave bowl by centuries of feet, climbing the wall in a switchback sequence that hugged the cliff like a vine. Above them the thread of shadow she'd seen from the rim resolved into balustrades — carved posts, waist-high, their tops rounded smooth as river stones — and above those, impossibly, the stair kept going, up and up, until it vanished into a fold of the canyon wall where the morning light hadn't yet reached.

Nobody spoke. The river roared through its slot and the spray drifted and the stair climbed, patient, indifferent, magnificent, and Sefa felt her eyes sting and didn't bother hiding it because Hazel wasn't looking at her anyway. He was looking up. His mouth was open. He looked, in that moment, exactly like a boy, and she understood suddenly that everything about this man — the guardedness, the sarcasm, the way he held his father's name in his mouth like something spoiled — all of it had been built over this. Over a boy who had wanted, once, to be taken along.

"It's pre-colonial," he said finally, and his voice cracked on the second word and he didn't care. "Sefa, do you understand what — nobody has documented anything like this in the whole range. Nobody has even *theorized*—"

"I understand."

"This is—" He laughed, one short helpless note. "This is what he found. This is what he wouldn't tell me about."

And there it was, the other thing underneath the wonder, surfacing the way bodies surface. She watched him hold both at once — the awe and the wound — and she put her hand briefly on his arm because there was nothing else to give him that would help.

Then she looked past him, down the shelf, and stopped.

"Hazel," she said again, and this time her voice had gone flat and hard, the guide's voice, the voice that meant danger, and he turned, and followed her gaze, and whatever the boy in him had been feeling drained out of his face between one breath and the next.

There was a tent among the rocks. Or what had been a tent. It was collapsed, shredded along one side, its poles snapped, half-buried under the same slide that had spared the stair. And beside it, arranged with a neatness that made her stomach turn, were the things of a man's last camp: a cook pot. A boot. A jacket folded over a stone.

And a body.

* * *

He was lying on his back in the shelter of the leaning slab, and the cold had kept him. Not well — the weeks had done their work, and Sefa had seen enough death on the river to know what time and weather did — but enough. Enough to see that he was a big man, grey-bearded, dressed in expedition gear with a torn sleeve, and that his head was wrong. Turned too far. Lying at an angle no fall explains and no sleep allows.

Hazel crouched beside him and for a long moment didn't touch anything. His breath came out white and slow.

"Do you know him?" Sefa asked.

"No." Then, quieter: "Yes. From photographs. Okafor. Daniel Okafor. He was the surveyor." Hazel's hand hovered over the man's collar, not touching. "My father sent me a team photo three years ago. Christmas card. I threw it out." He swallowed. "I remember faces anyway."

Sefa knelt on the other side. She made herself look — properly look, the way her grandmother had taught her to read a river, reading the ground instead. The body lay under the slab's overhang, dry, sheltered. But the slide debris around it was disturbed in ways the slide itself didn't explain. Boot prints, old, frozen into the mud and then frozen over: several sets, coming and going. And near the dead man's outflung right hand, pressed into the dirt where someone had stepped hard, the clear print of a boot heel with a distinctive crescent-shaped wear mark across the tread.

She'd seen that mark before. Three days ago, in the frozen mud outside the cook's grave, she had knelt in the dark with a torch and memorized it, and said nothing to Hazel, because there had been nothing yet to say.

Now there was.

"Hazel. Look at the ground. Don't touch him yet."

He looked. She watched him find it — the prints, the heel mark — and watched him arrive, step by step, at the thing she'd already arrived at, and she watched him refuse it, and then stop refusing.

"He didn't fall," Hazel said.

"No."

"Somebody was here. After. Somebody checked the body." His jaw worked. "Somebody turned him over."

"Yes."

"Why would Voss's men—" He stopped himself. Started again, slower, colder. "They were looking for something on him. Papers. Or they were making sure of something."

"Or both."

Hazel sat back on his heels. Above them the stair climbed into the shadow-fold of the wall, beautiful and silent, and Sefa saw him look at it and saw the moment the wonder curdled — saw the discovery become a crime scene in real time, the way a room changes when you learn what happened in it. Two hundred feet of ancient engineering, a thousand years of patient human hands, and at its foot, a man with a broken neck and strangers' footprints all around him.

"They died finding this," Hazel said. "And somebody's using it. Using *them*. Every body we find is a signpost pointing us deeper in, and whoever's ahead of us knows we're coming, because we're following the same trail they're leaving."

"Okafor didn't die here." Sefa said it carefully. "Look at the ground under him. No blood, no struggle. The rocks under his shoulders are undisturbed. He was carried here. Laid out." She paused. "Arranged."

"Why?"

"To be found." She met his eyes. "Or to be counted. Someone is keeping track of their own work, Hazel. This isn't panic. This is bookkeeping."

Hazel stood abruptly, walked three paces, stood still. When he turned back his face had settled into something she recognized from the trading post, from the river crossing — the look of a man deciding what kind of person he was going to be about a thing.

"We bury him," he said. "Like the cook. Properly."

"There's no time to dig frozen ground—"

"We bury him." Flat. Final. "We're not leaving him laid out like an exhibit for the next set of boots that comes through."

So they buried him. It took two hours with the ice axe and the folding shovel and it took something out of Hazel that Sefa could see he didn't have to spare, and when it was done — a cairn at the base of the great stair, stones gathered from the slide, the surveyor's boot placed on top because Hazel refused to leave it in the dirt — the light had shifted and the shadow-fold in the canyon wall had opened, and the upper reaches of the stair stood lit for the first time since morning.

And that was when Sefa, squinting up against the sun, saw the second body.

* * *

It was higher. Much higher — perhaps halfway up the stair, on a landing where the switchback turned, a small dark shape that might have been a boulder if boulders wore red.

They climbed. There was no discussion about it; the discovery was above them and the dead were above them and the day was burning away, and Sefa went first because she was the better climber and because she wanted, selfishly, to reach the shape before Hazel did. She failed. The stair was narrow and they climbed nearly abreast, hands on the ancient balustrade, boots fitting into bowls worn by feet a thousand years gone, and it was impossible — *impossible*, with the dead below and the dead above — not to feel the strangeness of it, climbing a miracle in the company of corpses.

The steps were sound. That astonished her most. A thousand years of freeze-thaw should have shattered them, but the builders had cut drainage channels into every tread, angled the balustrades to shed water, chosen the seam lines in the rock itself so the stair rose where the stone was strongest. It wasn't just a passage. It was an argument — a thousand-year-old argument that this canyon could be crossed, made in stone by people who had never needed anyone's permission or anyone's map.

The landing opened without warning: a platform maybe twelve feet wide, sheltered by an overhang, and on it, curled on his side as though asleep, the second man.

Younger. Dark-haired. Anorak with a torn hood. He had been dead longer than Okafor — the cold had taken him further down that road — and there was no question of arrangement here. He had fallen. Or been pushed. He lay where he'd landed, and below the landing's edge the drop went straight down forty feet to the rubble shelf, and Sefa looked at the drop and then at the body and then at the drop again and did not say what she was thinking, because Hazel was already saying it.

"He fell." Hazel's voice was thin. "From higher up. During the climb."

"Or he was thrown."

"Why would Voss throw him? If they wanted the drawings, they'd want him talking first."

"Maybe he didn't talk." Sefa crouched. The man's hands were bare, frost-bitten black at the fingertips. His pockets had been turned out — not searched, *turned out*, methodically, both of them, the linings hanging — and his pack was gone. Whoever had found him had taken everything and left him for the weather. "Or maybe he talked and it didn't matter. Or maybe—" She stopped.

"Maybe what?"

"Maybe he wasn't killed by Voss at all." She heard herself say it and knew how it sounded. "Hazel. We've been assuming one group. One enemy. But the cook's grave was staged. Okafor was arranged. This man was robbed and dropped. Those aren't the same hands."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. Below them the river thundered through its slot in the mountain, and above them the stair went on, up into the fold of the wall, toward whatever pass the builders had carved a thousand years ago.

"You're saying someone from the expedition is still alive," he said slowly. "And moving."

"I'm saying I don't know. I'm saying the ground doesn't match, and the ground never lies." She stood. "One set of those boot prints at Okafor's body matched the ones at the cook's grave. Crescent heel. But there were others. At least two more sets, different treads, older. And this man—" she nudged the turned-out pocket with her toe, hating herself for it, needing the evidence more than the dignity, "—this man was searched by someone in a hurry, or someone careless, or someone who'd already found what mattered and didn't need to be thorough."

"So Voss has the drawings already?" Hazel's voice cracked upward. "Then what are we doing, Sefa? Why are we climbing toward—"

"Because if Voss had the drawings, Voss would be gone. Downriver, gone, sold, done." She said it with more certainty than she felt. "He's still here. He's still hunting. Which means what he wants isn't in his hands yet. Which means someone else is carrying it." She looked up the stair, into the blue shadow where it vanished. "Someone who's still ahead of us."

The wind came down the canyon then, the first real wind of the afternoon, and it carried the smell of snow in it — that clean mineral emptiness that Sefa had known her whole life and had learned, the hard way, to respect. She judged it without wanting to: two days, maybe three, before the weather closed. Four before the high pass became a coffin.

"We can't take him down," Hazel said. He was standing over the young man, and his face had gone the grey of the stone. "We can't carry two days' climb down and then dig. We don't have the time."

"No."

"So we leave him."

"We cover him. With stones from the landing. It's what there is." She touched his elbow. "Hazel. Look at me. Grief is a luxury up here. You can pay it later. In spring. In a warm room. Right now it will kill you faster than Voss will."

He looked at her then, and something passed between them that had no name yet and didn't need one — the recognition of two people who had both learned to postpone their own hearts, and who recognized the technique in each other like a shared scar.

They built the cairn in twenty minutes, working silently, passing stones hand to hand. When it was done Hazel stood with his palm flat on the topmost rock for a moment, saying nothing, and Sefa gave him the moment and didn't rush it.

Then she turned and looked up the stair.

The last visible stretch climbed out of the sunlight into the fold of the canyon wall, and there, at the limit of sight, something caught the light. Metal. Small, glinting, wedged between two balustrade posts maybe sixty feet above them.

She climbed toward it before she decided to. Sixty feet, thirty, ten — and then she was standing over it, and her stomach dropped through the thousand-year-old stone.

A carabiner. Expedition-grade, modern, bright alloy winking in the sun. And clipped through it, fluttering faintly in the wind, a strip of fabric torn from a jacket sleeve — red, matching the dead man below.

But it was what was scratched into the rock beneath the carabiner, in a hurried hand, with the point of a knife or an ice screw, that made her call down the stair with a voice she barely recognized as her own:

"Hazel! Get up here!"

Three words, freshly cut, the stone dust still sitting in the grooves:

**NOT ALONE.**
