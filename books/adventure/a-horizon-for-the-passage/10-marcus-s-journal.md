## 10. Marcus's Journal

The cache was where the cairn said it would be.

Sefa had read the marker twice — *third stone from the fork, buried below the frost line* — and then she'd knelt in the scree and started moving rocks with the unhurried efficiency of someone who had dug things out of cold ground before. Hazel stood over her feeling useless, which by now was a familiar condition, and watched her hands work.

"Your father cached supplies like a man expecting company," she said. "Bad company."

"He knew Voss was coming."

"He knew *someone* was coming." She lifted a flat slab, set it aside, and kept digging. "There's a difference. Men who know exactly who's after them leave notes with names. Men who only suspect leave caches."

The tin was an ammunition box, olive drab, its latch crusted with river silt. Sefa worked it free of the earth and passed it up to him without ceremony, and it was heavier than it should have been — heavier than rope and cartridges and tea, anyway. He carried it to the lee side of a boulder where the wind couldn't get at them, and for a long moment he just held it on his knees.

"You going to open it?" Sefa asked.

"I'm deciding whether I want to know what's in it."

"That's not how knowing works."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

He broke the latch. Inside: a brick of tea wrapped in waxed paper, a coil of lamp wick, two tins of condensed milk, a box of matches that had stayed dry — his father had always been good at keeping things dry — and beneath all of it, wrapped in a canvas map case gone stiff with cold, a journal.

Not a field notebook. Not one of the little survey books Marcus Yarrow filled with elevations and grid coordinates in handwriting so small it looked typeset. This was a proper journal, leather-bound, the kind Hazel's mother used to give him every Christmas because she said a man who wrote things down could be trusted with other people's memories. Hazel had bought him one himself, once, the last Christmas before everything went wrong. He didn't know if this was that one. He wasn't sure he wanted to find out.

Sefa made tea. She did it without being asked and without looking at him, giving him the privacy of the page while staying close enough that he wasn't alone with it, and he understood that too was deliberate. She had a way of doing kindnesses that left no fingerprints.

He opened the journal.

The early pages were ordinary. Dates from the spring, entries about permits and porters and the price of diesel in the last town with a pump. His father's handwriting, tight and upright, each letter standing at attention. Hazel read it the way you'd read a stranger's mail — with attention but no claim on it — and then, three weeks into the expedition, the entries changed.

*May 14.* The canyon is real. I have been saying "if" for six months and I am done with "if." The wall above the second bend is dressed stone under the talus. Dressed. Someone built here, and everyone who ever surveyed this river walked past it and saw nothing but rockfall. We are nothing but rockfall ourselves.

*May 19.* Cut through the first collapse today. It took nine hours and cost us a winch cable and Okafor's composure. Behind it: a stairway cut into the living rock, ascending the canyon wall. Pre-colonial. Older than pre-colonial, maybe — the tooling isn't anything I've seen in the literature. I sat down on the third step and put my hand flat on the stone and I thought, nobody has touched this in five hundred years, and then I thought, no, that's wrong, somebody touched it yesterday, because I did.

Hazel turned the page. The wind came off the glacier above them and rattled the boulder they were sheltered behind, and Sefa poured tea into the tin cup and set it at his elbow without comment.

*May 23.* I have been sitting with this for four days and I need to write it plainly or I never will. The passage goes through. Not around, not over — through. A corridor engineered through the spine of the range, graded, drained, with ventilation shafts I can see daylight through when the sun is right. Whoever built it moved armies across these mountains, or trade, or whole communities fleeing something. It rewrites the maps. It rewrites the history of the entire cordillera. And I am the only person alive who knows it exists.

That is no longer true. That stopped being true when I sent the report.

*May 24.* Mistake. My mistake. I transmitted a summary to the institute — coordinates redacted, but the existence confirmed, the significance flagged. I told myself it was procedure. Procedure. Six days later a man named Voss arrives at base camp with a satellite phone, a budget that makes ours look like pocket change, and a contract for "salvage documentation rights." He smiled at me like we were already partners. He called the passage an asset.

I told him the summary was exaggerated. He looked at me the way a man looks at a lock he intends to pick.

Hazel stopped reading. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he'd expected them to shake. What shook instead was something underneath the steadiness, some load-bearing assumption giving way quietly, floor by floor.

Voss. The name meant nothing to him and everything at once. At the trading post — ten days ago now, another life — the men who'd asked their wrong questions had mentioned a boss. *Mr. Voss doesn't like loose ends,* the big one had said, smiling, and Sefa had lied for him smoothly and gotten them out the door, and Hazel had filed the name away as a threat shaped like weather, distant, general. Now it had a face and a signature and a place in his father's handwriting, and the distance collapsed.

"Read it out loud if you want," Sefa said quietly. "Or don't. But your face is telling me the story changed."

"It changed." He took a breath. "My father found the passage. Weeks ago. And a salvage outfit came for it before anyone else even knew."

"The men at the post."

"Their boss. By name." He looked at the page again. "He refused them. He hid the drawings. And then—" He stopped. The next entry was dated later, and the handwriting had changed, the letters leaning now, written fast, maybe walking.

*June 2.* They searched camp while we were in the canyon. Went through everything, polite as customs officers. Didn't find the drawings because the drawings aren't in camp. I've split them. Three sets. One stays with me. One is in this tin, under the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth — if you're reading this, you found the cache, which means either my people followed my markers or someone else did, and I'm sorry, because I can't control which.

The third set is somewhere no one will think to look, and I'm not writing down where.

Hazel ran his thumb along the inside lip of the ammunition box. There — a seam, hairline, almost invisible under the silt crust. He pressed, and a false bottom tilted up on a hidden edge, and beneath it lay a bundle in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, dense as a brick. He didn't open it. He set it on his knee and put his hand flat on it, the way his father had described putting his hand on the stairway stone, and understood that he was touching the thing men had already died for. The cook, folded at the bottom of a ravine with his neck broken at an angle falls don't make. Whoever else lay ahead of them along this trail.

"They're in there," Sefa said. "The drawings."

"Yes."

"And Voss wants them."

"Voss needs them. There's a difference." Hazel heard his father's cadence in his own mouth and hated it and didn't stop. "You can want a thing and walk away from it. If he needed them, it's because the passage is worthless without proof of location. My father made sure of that. He made himself the only map."

"Then your father is the most dangerous man on this mountain," Sefa said, "and Voss can't afford to let him reach the valley."

Hazel turned back to the journal. The remaining pages were few. His father had stopped writing daily sometime in June; the entries came in clusters now, written in the margins of exhaustion, and the prose had shed its academic polish until what was left was just a tired man talking to paper because paper was the only listener he trusted.

*June 11.* Two of the crew want out. I can't blame them. I gave them the choice honestly: go downriver and say nothing until spring, or stay and share whatever happens. Both chose to stay, which shames me, because I wouldn't choose to stay. I'd go home. I'd sit in a kitchen across from my son and say all the things I haven't said, and none of this would have happened, because I'd never have come.

But I didn't go home. I never do. That's the disease. Everything else is symptoms.

Hazel read that paragraph three times. Around him the light was failing, the canyon walls going blue, the temperature dropping the way it did now every evening, winter leaning its weight against the season. Sefa banked the fire small and sat close, shoulder almost touching his, and he was distantly aware that he angled toward the warmth of her without deciding to.

*June 17.* Voss made his real offer today. Not money. Leniency. He has people in the regional office — I checked, and the check frightened me more than the offer. Permits pulled. Insurance voided. A customs flag on my name that would follow me to every border on earth. He can't buy the drawings, so he'll take my livelihood, my reputation, and if necessary my freedom, one instrument at a time, and he'll do it legally, or legally enough.

I told him no. He thanked me. That's the part I keep turning over. He thanked me, sincerely, like I'd done him a favor, and I understand now: refusal confirms value. Every day I hold out tells him the passage is worth more than he thought.

*June 18.* Moved the oilcloth bundle tonight. New cache. If you're reading this and the bundle is here, I moved it once and moved it back, or I'm dead and someone smarter than me arranged it. Either way: the drawings are real, the passage is real, and the men chasing both are real, and I am sorry. I am sorry to whoever inherits this. I tried to keep it contained and I failed, and containment was always the fantasy. Things like this don't stay buried. That's why they were buried with stairs.

Hazel laughed — a short, involuntary sound with nothing in it. Sefa glanced over. "What?"

"He apologized to whoever found the cache." He tapped the page. "He's apologizing to me. He just doesn't know it's me."

"Maybe he does."

"He hasn't spoken to me in six years. He doesn't know anything about me. He knows less about me than he knows about Voss." The bitterness came out ready-made, six years aged, and it tasted different than it used to. Thinner. Like a coat that no longer fit and that he kept wearing because taking it off would mean admitting how much colder he'd gotten.

Sefa said nothing. She was good at that — at letting silence do work words would only undo. She fed the fire a stick and waited.

There was one page left.

The last entry was undated, or the date had been scratched out and rewritten, and the handwriting had changed again — slower, larger, the letters of a man writing carefully because he might not get a second attempt at clarity. Hazel felt his pulse in his throat before he read a word.

*If Hazel reads this.*

His name. His own name, in his father's hand, at the top of the final page, and the world narrowed to the width of the paper.

*I don't know if you're reading this, and I have to write as if you are, because if you are then it means you came looking, which means the radio silence worked on you exactly the way I feared it would and hoped it would. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry for using you. I told myself I kept you away from my work to punish you, or because you'd chosen your mother's side of the estrangement, or because we were both too proud — and some of that is even true. But it isn't the truth.*

*The truth is that knowing things is dangerous. You know that better than anyone, though you may not know why yet. Your mother knew things. She was on the Callao dig because she knew things, and she is not alive because of it, and I have spent eleven years making sure my son knew nothing worth killing him for. Silence was the only inheritance I could give you that no one could take away. I told myself it was protection. Maybe it was cowardice wearing protection's coat. Both, probably. I was never able to tell those apart in myself.*

*If you're reading this, the protection failed, and I'm glad, and I'm ashamed to be glad. Come find me if I'm alive. Forgive me if I'm not. Either order works.*

Hazel closed the journal.

He sat with it shut on his knees for a long time, long enough that the fire burned down a stage and Sefa rebuilt it, long enough for the first hard stars to come out over the col. He was aware of grief, but it was a strange grief, grief with the floor removed — because the story he had carried for six years, the story that had held him upright through the funeral and the estate sale and the unanswered letters, was that his father had chosen the work over him. Cleanly. Coldly. A man who preferred stones to sons. Hazel had built his whole resentment on that foundation, and the foundation was gone, and he was falling through the space where it had been.

His father hadn't neglected him. His father had hidden him. Had kept him ignorant the way you keep something valuable out of the light, and the ignorance Hazel had nursed like a wound was the wound itself — self-inflicted, inherited, designed. Every Christmas he hadn't answered. Every letter returned unopened. All of it had been Marcus Yarrow holding his son at arm's length to keep him outside the blast radius, and it had hurt worse than neglect ever could, because neglect you could hate. Protection you had to forgive, and forgiveness was a country Hazel had no map for.

"Hazel." Sefa's voice, low. "Your hands."

He looked down. He was gripping the journal hard enough that the cover had creased. He made himself let go, finger by finger.

"She knew things," he said. "That's what he wrote. My mother knew things, and that's why she died. On the Callao dig — I was nineteen. They told us it was a ferry accident. Everyone told us it was a ferry accident." He heard his own voice go flat and strange. "Was it a ferry accident?"

Sefa was quiet a moment. "I was a child when Callao happened. But my uncle ferried supply boats on that river, and he never took passengers again after that year, and he never said why." She met his eyes. "So. Probably not."

The wind picked up, moaning through the notch above them, and somewhere in that sound Hazel heard the expedition's radio going quiet, the two missed check-ins, the whole chain of events that had started the moment a man named Voss decided a secret was an asset. His father had spent eleven years building a wall of silence around his son, and Voss had knocked it down in six days with a satellite phone and a smile.

And the last line — *come find me if I'm alive* — sat in Hazel's chest like a coal.

He repacked the tin with hands that finally, properly, shook. Drawings in the oilcloth, journal inside his jacket against his ribs, where he could feel it with every heartbeat. Sefa doused the fire. Above the eastern ridge, past the black teeth of the pass they'd have to cross, the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the air had acquired an edge it hadn't had that morning — a mineral smell, cold iron and coming snow.

"Sefa," he said. "How many days to the high pass?"

She looked at the sky the way sailors look at water, reading it, and he watched the answer arrive in her face before she spoke it.

"Fewer than we had yesterday," she said. "We move at first light. And Hazel — we're not the only ones who can read weather. Whatever Voss is doing right now, he's doing it faster."

Far downslope, in the blue dark below the canyon mouth, something glinted. Once. Like lamplight. Then gone.

Neither of them said what they were both thinking.
