## 11. Snowline

The first snow came at dawn, and it came wrong.

Sefa knew snow the way she knew her own pulse — by its weight, its smell, the way the air went still before it. This was mountain snow, high-country snow, and it had no business falling this early in the season. It should have been three weeks out, maybe four. Instead it came down fine and dry through the canyon's throat while Hazel slept, sifting onto the ledges of the carved stairway above them, filling the grooves the old stonemasons had cut into the living rock a thousand years before anyone now alive had a name for this place.

She stood at the mouth of their shelter — a shallow overhang below the stairway where the two bodies lay covered with stones they'd carried up one by one — and watched the flakes come down without wind, straight as plumb lines, patient.

That was the worst part. Snow that fell straight meant cold air sitting on the range like a lid. Snow that fell straight meant it would keep falling.

Behind her, Hazel stirred in his bedroll. She heard him wake the way he always woke now — a sharp intake of breath, a hand moving to the knife under the canvas, then the slow exhale when he remembered where he was and that the man who'd died on the stairway two days ago was not going to get up again. He hadn't slept well since the ambush. Neither of them had. But his body was learning things his mind refused to say out loud.

"You're watching it come down," he said. Not a question. His voice was rough with sleep and cold.

"It started an hour ago."

"Is that bad?"

Sefa didn't answer right away. She crouched and picked up a handful of the powder that had already gathered on the stone lip, let it sit in her palm until the warmth of her skin began to kill it. "It's early," she said finally. "Three weeks early. Maybe more."

Hazel came to stand beside her. He'd stopped shaving somewhere below the snowline and the beard made him look older, harder, less like the man who'd stepped off the mail boat six weeks ago clutching a telegram like it might bite him. He looked up at the sky the way people look at something they've been told is beautiful.

"So what does that mean? In days."

She'd been doing the arithmetic since before he woke. She did it again anyway, because the numbers kept getting worse and she wanted to be sure. "The pass is two days' hard walking from here if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't. After we cross, another day down to the valley country." She turned her palm over and let the dead snow fall. "But once this starts in earnest — once the storm systems set in over the col — nobody crosses. Not guides, not rescue parties, not fools. The pass closes and it stays closed until thaw."

"How long?"

"Five months."

He was quiet for a moment. Below them the canyon dropped away in terraces, the river a gray thread now, and somewhere down that thread, behind them, Voss was either dead or regrouping or waiting for exactly this — for the mountains to do his work for him.

"And if we don't cross," Hazel said. "We winter here."

"We winter here. With what food we have left, which is nine days' worth if we're careful. With no fuel except what we can scavenge from the expedition caches. With Voss's men knowing this canyon better than we do by now." She looked at him. "Or we go up."

"Up." He said it flatly, testing the word.

"Up. Four days. We leave within the hour, while the snow's still soft enough to read tracks in and light enough not to break our ankles on the ice underneath."

* * *

They broke camp fast, the way they'd learned to, each of them moving through the other's routines without discussion anymore. Sefa coiled rope; Hazel kicked dirt over the fire ring and scattered the ashes wide. It had taken weeks to build this wordless machinery between them and neither of them had noticed it happening. That was the thing about almost dying together repeatedly — you stopped keeping accounts.

The stairway rose above them as they climbed, switchbacking up the canyon wall in great weathered flights, and Sefa found herself touching the carved edges as she passed, the way you'd touch a banister in a house where someone you loved had lived. Marcus Yarrow had walked these steps. Whatever else the man had done — whatever silences he'd built, whatever secrets he'd carried like stones in a pack — he had found this, and he had understood what it was worth, and he had been willing to die rather than sell it.

She understood Hazel better now because of it. The son was the father's silence given legs.

At the top of the first flight they paused, and Hazel looked back down the canyon the way he always did, scanning the terraces below for movement. He'd developed the habit after the ambush. He checked the way behind them every twenty minutes whether she reminded him or not.

"Nothing," he said.

"There won't be. Not yet. If Voss has any sense he's waiting for us to lead him to the drawings." She adjusted her pack straps. "Which means we have one advantage. He thinks we're going where his map says to go."

"Are we?"

"That depends on your father."

"My father," Hazel said, "is either dead in a crevasse or alive in a hole somewhere, and either way he's spent six years not telling me a damn thing, so I'm not holding my breath for directions."

It came out harder than Sefa thought he intended. She let it sit. There was no use smoothing it over; the anger was load-bearing now. Take it away and she wasn't sure what would hold him up.

They climbed.

* * *

By midday the snow had thickened, fat wet flakes that stuck to everything and erased the world a few yards at a time. The trail left the stairway where the ancient engineers had cut it into a ledge and followed the canyon rim north toward the col — a notch in the ridgeline, two days off, that the maps marked with a contour so tight it looked like a scar. Sefa navigated by memory and by the cairns Marcus's team had built, most of them still standing, their stones furred white now, like small animals gone dormant.

They ate standing up, frozen biscuit and cold tea from a thermos that had lost its argument with the altitude. Hazel chewed mechanically, eyes on the ridgeline.

"Sefa."

"Mm."

"If I turned back. If I said turn back, winter in the trading post town, come back in spring with a proper party—" He stopped. Started again. "Could you do it? Alone?"

She considered the question honestly, which was the only way she knew how to consider anything. Could she walk out alone, downriver, past the graves, past the trading post, past the whole smoking ruin of this autumn, and wait five months for a thaw?

"Yes," she said. "I could turn back alone. I've traveled alone most of my life. It's not hard. You just keep walking and don't talk to anyone about anything that matters."

He nodded slowly, looking at the snow.

"But," she said, and heard her own voice change, and couldn't stop it, "I wouldn't want to."

The words hung there in the falling snow between them, small and white and impossible to take back.

Hazel turned to look at her. His face was doing several things at once and none of them well — surprise, and something rawer underneath it, and the particular panic of a man who has just realized the ground has shifted under him and he doesn't know which direction is down.

"I haven't wanted to be alone," he said, "for a while now. I think maybe since the base camp. Since you were standing in the wreckage of my father's expedition looking like you wanted to burn it down too." He laughed, short and unsteady. "I told myself it was just — circumstance. Proximity. Two people thrown together."

"And now?"

"Now I think circumstance doesn't make you check the trail behind someone every twenty minutes. Circumstance doesn't make you count how many days of food she has left in her pack." He looked down at his hands, red and cracked from the cold. "I don't want to turn back alone. I don't want to be anywhere alone. I want—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. The snow filled the sentence in for him, falling between them, covering the ground they'd stood on all autumn, the wary alliance, the shared anger, the cold camps and the graves and the ambush — all of it dissolving into something that had been there the whole time, waiting, like the stairway under its centuries of rubble.

Sefa reached out and took his hand. Her fingers were freezing; so were his. It wasn't romantic. It was two cold hands holding on, and it was the truest thing either of them had said in weeks.

"Four days," she said quietly. "We cross in four days, and then we find your father, and then—" She stopped herself. Then what, she didn't know. Then everything. Then nothing. Then whatever the world allowed.

"Then we figure out the rest," Hazel finished for her.

* * *

They made eight miles that afternoon, which was good going in fresh snow, and camped at dusk in a hollow below the ridge with the wind starting to moan over the col ahead of them. Sefa got the tent up while Hazel melted snow for water, and they ate huddled together inside the nylon with the stove hissing between them, and for a little while there was nothing in the world but the small circle of heat and the sound of the storm gathering outside.

Then Hazel said, "There's something I keep thinking about."

"Mm."

"The journal entry. The last one. He wrote my name, Sefa. At the end of everything — the buyers, the threats, all of it — the last thing he wrote was my name." Hazel stared into the stove flame. "And I keep trying to decide if that makes it better or worse. That he was thinking of me. That I was the last thing."

Sefa fed another pinch of fuel to the stove. Outside, the wind rose a register, and somewhere far off down the canyon, a serac cracked and boomed like distant artillery.

"Both," she said. "It's both. That's what loving someone is. Better and worse at the same time, and you don't get to pick."

He looked at her then, really looked, and in the orange light of the stove she saw something settle in his face — not peace, nothing so clean as peace, but a decision. The kind of decision you make with your whole body.

"Whatever we find in that valley," he said. "Alive or dead. Whatever he tells me or doesn't tell me. I'm glad you're here. I need you to know that. Whatever happens on the other side of that pass — I'm glad it's you."

The wind screamed over the col. The stove hissed. Sefa leaned her head against his shoulder and felt the exhaustion of six weeks settle over her like a second bedroll, warm and heavy, and for the first time since the mail boat she slept without dreaming of the men on the stairway.

She woke once, deep in the night, to the sound of Hazel breathing beside her and the storm hammering the tent, and she lay still and listened to the mountain and did the arithmetic one more time, the way she always did, because the numbers were hers to carry.

Four days to the pass. Four days of food. One killer somewhere behind them in the white.

And the snow, falling straight down, patient as anything, showing no sign at all of stopping.
