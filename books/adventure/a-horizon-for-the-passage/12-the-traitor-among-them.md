## 12. The Traitor Among Them

The boot print was small. That was the first thing I noticed, and the last thing I wanted to.

We'd made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder where the trail bent away from the river, and Sefa had gone back fifty yards to a flat shelf of mud beside a seep — one of the few places above the snowline where the ground held what touched it. She crouched there for a long time without saying anything, and when she finally waved me over, she didn't look up from it.

"Tell me what you see."

I saw a boot print. Nine inches, maybe ten. A lug sole with a distinctive bar across the heel — the kind of tread you'd find on expedition-grade boots, the kind my father's team had worn because Marcus bought all their gear himself and bought it good.

I saw that the print was fresh. The edges hadn't crumbled. The mud at its center still glistened.

And I saw that it pointed downriver. Toward us.

"Somebody passed here," I said. "Recently. Heading toward the crossing."

"Solo," Sefa said. "One set. No pack straps dragging." She rose and looked past me, up the trail, into the blue shadow of the pass approach. "Light on his feet. He knew we were behind him."

*He.* I noticed she'd said it too.

* * *

Here is what I want to be honest about, since honesty is apparently the only currency I have left: for most of that evening I convinced myself it was nothing. A hunter. A prospector. One of Voss's men ranging ahead, which was bad enough but comprehensible — Voss had men, men walked trails, trails were where men walked. I built the explanation like a wall and stood behind it while Sefa cooked rice over a flame no bigger than a cupped hand, and I almost believed it.

Then I took off my own boot to rub my blistered heel, and Sefa looked at it, and went very still.

"What?" I said.

"Turn it over."

I turned it over. The sole was worn smooth at the ball of the foot, cracked along the arch, and stamped with a tread pattern I recognized now because I'd spent an hour staring at a photograph of it in my mind — the same bar-across-the-heel lug sole, the same make, the same size nine and a half that had been pressed into the mud by the seep.

"These are expedition issue," I said stupidly.

"Marcus outfitted eleven people," Sefa said. "You told me. Cook, surveyors, porters, the geologist, your father. Ten sets of these boots went up this river in June." She fed a stick to the fire. "Two of them are in graves we dug or found. Your father is ahead of us. That leaves seven unaccounted for — and one of those seven just walked past our camp going the other way, alone, fast, and quiet."

"He could be running," I said. "A survivor who got clear of Voss—"

"Running from what? Toward what? There's nothing downriver but the trading post and Voss's boats." She looked at me across the fire, and her face in the flame-light was patient in a way that made it worse. "Hazel. The print at the cook's grave. You remember?"

I did remember. I'd tried not to. The cook — Ondrej, his name had been Ondrej, I'd learned it from a supply manifest three days after burying him — had been found at the base of a bluff with his neck broken, and everything about it had been arranged to say *fall*, except that a man doesn't fall uphill, and a man doesn't fall onto his own dropped hat placed neatly six feet from his body. And pressed into the frost-hardened ground near the grave we'd dug for him, half-scuffed by our own knees as we worked, there had been a print. Bar across the heel. Size nine and a half.

I had told myself then that it was one of ours. Mine, maybe. Sefa's.

Sefa wore size seven.

"You thought it was mine," she said, reading my face the way she read water. "At the time. Don't apologize. I'd have thought the same." She turned the rice pot. "But it wasn't yours either. Yours are worn through at the outer edge; you favor your left leg when you're tired. The print at the grave didn't favor anything. It was a man walking carefully, carrying something heavy, and taking his time about it."

"Carrying what?"

"I don't know. Supplies. Rope." Her jaw tightened. "Or a body."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I lay in the tent with the flap open a hand's width, watching the stars wheel over the col, and I let the thing I'd been refusing to think take shape in the dark.

Someone from my father's expedition was alive. Someone had survived whatever happened at the canyon — had survived Voss, or worse, had never been in danger from Voss at all — and was now moving through these mountains wearing boots my father had paid for, leaving prints at the graves of the people he'd eaten with for months. Working with Voss. Guiding him. Telling him where the caches were, where the bodies were, where two strangers following the trail would camp and when they would sleep.

Which meant Voss didn't need to hurry. Which meant Voss had known every step we'd taken since the river fork, possibly before we took it.

And which meant — and here was the thought that sat on my chest like a stone until dawn — that the only person whose movements Voss *didn't* know was the person lying next to me on the far side of the fire.

I want to be clear: I'm not proud of what my mind did next. But grief does strange bookkeeping, and fear keeps terrible accounts, and somewhere around three in the morning I found myself reviewing Sefa. How she'd appeared at the ransacked base camp so conveniently, the abandoned guide with a grievance ready-made. How she always seemed to know the trail. How she'd covered for me at the trading post with a fluency that could have been loyalty or could have been practice. How she'd steered us to the high pass — the route Marcus's cairn had pointed to — before I'd even finished reading the warning carved into the bark.

*Not the way we came.*

How had she known to look for the cairn at all?

I lay there hating myself for the arithmetic and doing it anyway, and the worst part — the part I still can't write cleanly — is that some cold clerk in the back of my skull kept whispering: *your father trusted strangers with his secrets. Maybe one of them was closer than you think.*

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the kind of light that makes distances lie. Sefa was already up, breaking camp with her usual economy, and I watched her roll her bedroll and thought: *she rolls it left to right. Would a traitor be so careless as to leave the print by the seep, or so skilled as to plant it?*

That's where paranoia takes you. Into questions with no floor.

"We should talk about the print," I said.

She paused, a guy-line coiled in her hands. "All right."

"It was expedition-issue boots. Size nine and a half. Same as the grave site."

"Yes."

"And there are seven members of my father's team unaccounted for."

"Six," she said. "Your father makes seven, and he's ahead of us."

"How can you be sure of that?"

The question came out harder than I meant it, edged with something I hadn't decided to put there, and I saw her hear it — really hear it — and set the guy-line down slowly, the way you set down something breakable.

"Hazel."

"Because if someone from the team is working with Voss, then someone from the team has been feeding him the trail since the fork. Every cache we've found, he found first or knew about. And the only variable he couldn't predict—" I stopped. I heard how it sounded. I said it anyway, because paranoia is a coward and it makes cowards of us all. "The only variable is you. You were hired by the expedition. You were there when they pushed on. You knew about the oilcloth before I did."

The silence lasted four heartbeats. I counted them against my own pulse.

Then Sefa laughed. Not warmly.

"You think I'm leading Voss to your father." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "Me. Who buried Ondrej with you in frozen ground and read the service out of a mail-order prayer book because neither of us knew one. Who took a knife-cut across the forearm getting the journal tin out of the cache. Who hasn't slept more than four hours since the trading post because I keep hearing boots on scree." She stepped closer, and I made myself not step back. "Ask me the question properly, Hazel. If you're going to do it, do it properly."

"Where were you the night the cook died?"

"Three days behind you, coming upriver, alone, cursing the man who hired me and left me." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing. It got quieter. "You know this. You've known this since the base camp. But you weren't asking because you believe it's me. You're asking because you're scared, and scared men need the danger to have a face, and mine is the only one out here that isn't trying to kill you." She picked up the guy-line again. Coiled it. "So. Six missing men, one of them your father, one of them a traitor in borrowed boots — and you spent the night doing sums about the woman carrying half your load."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She shouldered her pack. "You want to know who walked past our camp? Then walk. Because whoever it is knows this terrain, knows Voss's plan, and is between us and the col. And if he reports our position—" she glanced at the sky, reading weather in it the way I read resentment in a man's handwriting, "—we'll meet more than footprints at the pass approach."

* * *

We climbed. All day, in that gray windless light, up switchbacks cut into the mountainside by hands long dead or long gone, and I turned the problem over and over like a stone in a pocket.

Six names. I said them to myself as I climbed. Brandt, the surveyor. Okonkwo, the geologist. Reyes and Lindqvist, porters. Tam, the junior archaeologist. And Deverell — Marcus's second, his right hand for fifteen years, the man who'd taught me to stratigraphically sort pottery shards when I was nine years old and my father was too busy to notice I was there.

Deverell. Big Deverell, who used to carry me on his shoulders across stream crossings. Whose boots I had laced once as a boy, sitting on his knee, because the knots amused me.

Size nine and a half. I'd have bet the salvage money on it.

"Deverell," I said aloud, and Sefa stopped climbing.

"You know him."

"I grew up around him." My mouth had gone dry. "My father's second. Fifteen years. He carried me across rivers, Sefa. He—" I stopped, because the sentence that wanted to come out was *he's family*, and family was a word that had done nothing but lie to me for six years.

"Then he knows you," Sefa said quietly. "And he knows you'd recognize his stride, his habits, his gear. If Deverell is Voss's man, then he's been counting on sentiment to slow you down." She looked back down the trail, the way we'd come, toward the mud by the seep. "Or he left that print where we'd find it deliberately. A message. Or bait."

"For what?"

"For exactly what you're doing right now," she said. "Standing still."

* * *

We found the sign an hour before dark, and it ended the argument about whether we were being followed, because you don't leave sign unless you want company.

A cairn — not one of Marcus's careful markers, but a rough pile of stones, recent, thrown together — with a strip of oilcloth weighted under the top rock. Sefa lifted it out. On the inside, written in charcoal, in a blocky engineer's hand I didn't know:

**TWO DAYS BEHIND YOU. FOUR MEN. HE WANTS THE OLD MAN ALIVE.**

No signature. No name. Just that, and below it, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the cloth:

**TRUST THE ONE WHO BURIES THE DEAD.**

Sefa read it twice and handed it to me without a word. I read it, and the cold that went through me had nothing to do with altitude.

Four men. Two days. And a sentence that could mean Sefa — the one who'd knelt in frozen ground with me and said words over Ondrej — or could mean the man who'd dug the graves in the first place. Whoever wrote it knew about the burials. Knew things only someone close to both the dead and the living could know.

"Trust the one who buries the dead," Sefa repeated softly. She was looking at me. "That's you, Hazel. That's us."

"Or it's him," I said. "Whoever wrote it. Whoever buried them." I folded the oilcloth into my jacket, next to the journal, next to my father's last entry with my name in it. "It's a message from a man who wants us to trust somebody. People who want to be trusted, at this point in this story, are people who want something."

Below us, far down the valley, a thread of smoke rose from the tree line where no camp should be. Thin. Deliberate. A signal, or a lure.

Sefa saw it at the same moment I did. We stood together in the failing light, two days from the col, four men and a traitor somewhere behind us, and my father somewhere ahead with a secret worth killing for strapped to his dying body.

"Two days," Sefa said. "We rest four hours and climb through the night."

I nodded. And as we broke camp in the dark, I caught myself checking the ground behind us for prints — and finding, pressed fresh into the frost beside our own cold fire ring, a single boot print. Bar across the heel. Size nine and a half.

Pointing toward the pass. Waiting for us to follow.
