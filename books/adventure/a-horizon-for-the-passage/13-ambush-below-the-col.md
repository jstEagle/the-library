## 13. Ambush Below the Col

The col showed itself an hour before we reached it, a pale notch in the black spine of the ridge, and I hated it on sight. Not because it looked dangerous — though it did, a saddle of scree and old snow pinched between walls of rock — but because it was the only way through, and the only way through is where you die if someone wants you dead.

"Too open," I said.

Hazel crouched beside me at the lip of the moraine, breathing hard from the climb. Four days above the snowline had pared him down to something leaner than the man who'd stepped off the mail boat, all soft hands and city anger. The cold had done its work. Grief had done the rest.

"It's the only open ground for miles," he said. "Which means it's the only place they can be sure of finding us."

"Or the last place they'd look."

"You believe that?"

I didn't answer. We both knew what Voss was. A man like that doesn't guess. He waits at the door you have to use.

We crossed the lower slopes with the wind at our backs, pushing us toward the col like a hand between the shoulder blades. The sky was the color of dishwater, low and heavy, holding its snow. Sefa's weather, my grandmother would have called it — sky that keeps its secrets until it's ready to spend them. I kept checking the light on the rock faces, reading the mountain the way my father taught me before I could read words: where the wind funneled, where a man could hide, where the shadows would pool when the sun went behind the ridge.

There were three places. Maybe four.

"Hazel." I touched his arm. "If anything happens—"

"Nothing's going to happen."

"If anything happens, you go up and over without stopping. You don't come back for me. You don't stand over me and argue with the mountain. You climb."

He looked at me then, and there was something raw in his face that he hadn't learned to hide yet. "You've been giving me that speech since the canyon wall."

"And you've been ignoring it since the canyon wall."

"I'm not leaving you."

"That's not courage, Hazel. That's arithmetic. One of us knows this range. If you're alone, you still might live."

He opened his mouth — I watched the argument form behind his teeth — and then the wind shifted, and I smelled woodsmoke.

Not ours. Ours had died two valleys back. This was fresh, sharp, close. Someone had a fire burning within a kilometer, sheltered from the wind, and there was only one reason a man lights a fire below a pass instead of above it.

I put my hand flat against the scree and felt it before I heard it: a shiver in the loose stone, faint, rhythmic. Boots. More than one pair. Moving upslope on our left, where the broken rock ran in long fingers toward the trail.

"Down," I breathed, and pulled him off the path into the shadow of a boulder the size of a house.

We lay flat, cheek to frozen stone. Below us, maybe three hundred meters down, the trail bent around a spur — and along that bend came three men, moving fast, roped loosely together like they'd done this before. Not Voss. I knew Voss's walk now, the deliberate patience of him. These were the others. The ones he kept like tools in a drawer.

They stopped at the bend. One of them raised binoculars and swept them slowly across the slope above us.

I pressed myself into the stone and thought about nothing at all, which is the hardest thing a person can do. Beside me Hazel had gone very still, the particular stillness of a man discovering that his body is made of parts that want to make noise — breath, heartbeat, the click of a jaw clenching. I put two fingers on his wrist and squeezed once. *Still.* He understood.

The binoculars passed over us. Paused. Passed on.

Then the man lowered them and said something to the others, and all three turned and began climbing directly toward our position.

Not toward the col. Toward *us*. They hadn't seen us. They'd seen something — a track, a glint, the wrongness of undisturbed snow in one place and disturbed snow in another — and they were coming to correct it.

* * *

There are perhaps ten seconds in any fight that decide everything, and you spend nine of them deciding whether to run.

I measured the distance. Two hundred meters of open scree between us and the col. They were closer to the bottom of the slope than we were; if we ran now, uphill, in deepening snow, they'd reach the trail junction ahead of us and simply wait. If we stayed, they'd find us in minutes.

But there was a third thing. My father's people had a word for it — the small path, the one the mountain shows you only when it has no other choice. Above us and to the right, a gully cut up through the rock band, choked with old avalanche debris. It wasn't a route. It was a crack. But cracks go somewhere, and men following tracks do not follow cracks.

I pointed. Hazel nodded. We moved.

For twenty minutes we climbed like animals, hands and boots, no words, the wind covering our noise. The gully narrowed. The debris field gave way to bare ice, and I cut steps with the axe while Hazel fed me the rope, and all the while I listened — not for them, but for the mountain. For the sound a slope makes when too many feet cross it.

It came just as we topped out onto the shoulder below the col: a deep, structural groan under the wind, the sound of the gully deciding it had carried enough.

"Avalanche!" I shoved Hazel toward the rocks at the shoulder's edge. "Move — *move*—"

The mountain exhaled behind us. I felt it more than heard it, a pressure like a giant's palm flattening the air, and then the roar arrived, filling the world, and the gully we'd climbed became a white river pouring down toward the trail below.

Toward the three men.

I turned in time to see it take them. Not all of them — the lead man had reached the shoulder of stable rock and thrown himself flat, and the slide split around him like water around a stone. But the other two were caught in the open, and the mountain does not negotiate. They went under white and did not come up. The sound went on and on, ten seconds, twenty, and then the silence after it, which is worse.

Hazel stood beside me, rope still coiled in his fists, staring down at the burial site. His face was gray.

"They're dead," he said. "Because of us. Because we led them—"

"We didn't lead them anywhere. They chose to follow."

"They're *dead*, Sefa."

"Yes." I took his arm. "And if we stand here discussing it, we'll join them. Come on."

But even as I said it, I saw the third man rise from the rocks below, dust himself off, and look up.

He'd seen us. There was no mistaking the angle of his head, the way he went still, the way his hand dropped to the rifle slung across his back. And he was between us and nothing — below us, yes, but off to the flank, with a clear line to the col approach, and he was already unlimbering the weapon.

"*Run,*" I said.

* * *

We ran.

The col was four hundred meters of rising ground, scree and crusted snow, the worst possible footing at the worst possible pace. Behind us the first shot cracked off the rocks — high, a warning or a miss, impossible to know — and then a second, lower, and something stung my left side like a whip of fire and my leg went wrong beneath me.

I didn't fall. That was the strange part. I stumbled, corrected, kept running, and it was only when Hazel grabbed my jacket and hauled me behind a fin of rock at the top of the col that I understood why my side felt wet and hot and distant all at once.

"Sefa." His voice came from far away. "Sefa, look at me. Where?"

"Left. Under the ribs. It's—" I tried to laugh and it came out as a cough. "It's a graze. I've had worse from a mule."

He didn't laugh. He stripped his outer jacket off, then his shirt, wadding the shirt against my side with hands that shook so badly I had to put mine over them and hold them still.

"Breathe," I told him. "You bind a wound the way you tie any knot. Slow hands, fast mind."

"Shut up," he said, without heat, and kept working. He was better at it than he believed — the salvage digs had taught him field dressings, he said later, though at the time he seemed to be operating purely on terror and instinct. He tied the shirt tight, dragged his jacket around my shoulders, and got us both moving again, my arm over his neck, his body taking half my weight without comment.

Behind us, on the slope, the shooter had stopped firing. I understood why before I wanted to: he was descending, circling, coming for us the slow certain way. Wounded prey doesn't climb well. He had time.

The far side of the col dropped into a shallow bowl of wind-carved snow, and beyond it the trail descended toward a shelf of rock where the path narrowed against a cliff face. If we reached the narrows, one man couldn't flank us. If we didn't—

Hazel set me down against a boulder and turned to face back the way we'd come.

"What are you doing?"

"Buying time." He picked up a loose stone the size of his fist, weighing it like he didn't know what it was for. "Go on ahead. Slide down to the narrows. I'll hold him here."

"With a rock."

"With a rock."

"Hazel." I made my voice level, though the pain had begun to organize itself into something serious. "Look at me. You've never—"

"I know what I've never done."

"He has. He'll shoot you before you're within thirty meters, and then he'll walk down and finish both of us, and everything your father bled for dies in this bowl of snow. Is that the trade? Is that worth your pride?"

His jaw worked. Below us, faintly, I could hear the man climbing — unhurried, methodical, the scrape of boots on stone carrying in the thin air.

"Then what?" Hazel whispered.

I pointed to the slope above the trail, where a cornice of wind-packed snow hung over the narrows like a held breath. All morning I'd been reading the mountain, and the mountain had shown me this: a ledge of loaded snow, sitting wrong, waiting.

"The cornice," I said. "Someone crosses beneath it, loud enough, long enough..." I met his eyes. "I can bring it down. On him. On the narrows. But I need you to get me to the ledge above it, and I need you to not ask me afterward whether I hesitated."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he shouldered me again, gentler than before, and started climbing.

* * *

The ledge above the cornice was a shelf of bare rock no wider than a table, and getting me onto it cost Hazel more than it cost me — I could hear him swearing under his breath every time my weight pulled at the wound, each curse bitten off like he was afraid the mountain would hear him complain.

Below us, the shooter entered the narrows.

He was careful. I'll give him that. He moved along the cliff base in short rushes, using the rock, keeping his rifle ready. A professional. The kind of man Voss collected. He paused halfway through, directly beneath the cornice, and lit a cigarette — I could see the small orange bloom of it, absurd, obscene, in all that white — and stood smoking while he scanned the slopes above.

Hazel lay beside me on the shelf, our shoulders touching, our breath smoking in the wind. He had found a stone. Of course he had. He held it in his fist like a talisman, and I understood suddenly that he wasn't planning to throw it at the man below. He was planning to throw it at the cornice. He'd read my mind, or the mountain's, or both.

"On three," I whispered.

"No," he whispered back. "Together. Whenever you're ready. I'm ready whenever you're ready."

So we lifted our stones together, and I aimed for the seam in the snowpack where the wind had undercut the cornice, the weak point, the place where the mountain was already holding its breath — and we threw.

The first stones did nothing. The second did less. But the third — Hazel's, thrown with all six years of estrangement and four days of grief and whatever terrible new thing was growing between us — struck the seam squarely, and the cornice sighed, and let go.

It came down like the end of the world. Not an avalanche — smaller than that, tighter, a focused white hammer of two tons of packed snow dropping thirty meters onto the narrows. The crack of it was tremendous. The cloud it threw swallowed the cliff face whole.

When the air cleared, the narrows were gone. Buried. The trail itself reshaped, a smooth white rampart where the path had been.

No movement below. No cigarette ember. Nothing.

Hazel knelt at the edge of the shelf, staring down, and I watched his face do something I will remember for the rest of my life. It didn't harden and it didn't break. It went *empty*, the way a room goes empty when the furniture's been carried out, and I knew that some part of him had been hoping — right up until the snow fell — that the man would scramble clear. That there would be a chase, a struggle, something with rules. Something a civilized man could survive with his story intact.

Instead there was just the white mound, and the wind, and the two of us.

"He might have—" Hazel began.

"No," I said. "He didn't."

"He was just *standing there*. Smoking. Like it was over already. Like we were already—"

"Hazel."

"—and we killed him. I killed him. I threw the stone that—"

"*Hazel.*" I took his face in both my hands and made him look at me. His eyes were wet and furious and terrified, and underneath all of it, grieving. "Listen to me. That man came up this mountain to kill us. Not to scare us. To kill us, and take what your father found, and leave our bodies for the spring melt to explain. Whatever happened on that ledge, it wasn't murder. It was the bill coming due."

He closed his eyes. A tear slipped free and froze almost immediately on his cheekbone, a small bright bead, and I wiped it away with my thumb.

"It doesn't feel justified," he said quietly. "That's the worst part. I keep waiting to feel justified, and I just feel... heavy."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've carried it longer than you have." I leaned my forehead against his. "And because the feeling doesn't mean you're guilty. It means you're still someone who ought to feel it. The day it stops hurting is the day to worry."

He laughed then — a broken, disbelieving sound, half sob — and wrapped his arms around me, carefully, carefully, mindful of the wound, and held on like I was the last solid thing on the mountain.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow below the col's far side, out of the wind, with the buried narrows somewhere behind us and the descent ahead. I sat propped against Hazel's pack while he worked by headlamp, cleaning the wound properly now that we could stop moving. The bullet had gouged a furrow along my ribs — ugly, bleeding freely, but shallow. A centimeter deeper and I'd be telling this story to someone else.

He was gentle. That surprised me, though I couldn't have said why. The same hands that had thrown the killing stone now moved over my skin with a tenderness so precise it was almost formal, like a ritual being performed for the first time by someone who'd only ever read about it. He taped the dressing, checked the circulation past it twice, adjusted my layers around it, and then sat back on his heels and looked at what he'd done with an expression I recognized: the look of a man auditing his own soul.

"I've never hurt anyone before today," he said. "Not really. Not on purpose."

"I know."

"And now I've—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a version of me that existed yesterday, and he doesn't exist anymore, and I didn't get to say goodbye to him."

"No one does. That's not how it works."

"My father would know what to say right now. Some archaeological platitude about how violence is just stratigraphy, everyone's built in layers over the older selves." He shook his head. "God, I used to hate when he talked like that."

"And now?"

"Now I'd give anything to hear it." He finished taping the last edge of the bandage, smoothing it flat with his thumb, and his hand stayed there, warm through the fabric, over the wound. Over my heartbeat. "Does it hurt?"

"Only when I breathe, laugh, or move."

"So, constantly."

"Constantly," I agreed.

He settled beside me then, arranging my good side against his chest, his chin resting on the top of my head, his arms a cage of warmth around me. The wind moaned over the col behind us. Somewhere below, under two tons of snow, a man was keeping his own kind of vigil, and neither of us spoke of him again that night.

But I felt the change in Hazel as clearly as I felt the throb in my ribs. Something had been spent that could not be refunded. He had crossed a line that doesn't appear on any map, and he knew it, and the knowing had aged him years in an afternoon.

"There's blood on your sleeve," I murmured, near sleep. "Mine. You should wash it before it sets."

"I'm not washing it out."

"Why not?"

A long pause. The wind. The tick of cooling rock.

"Because it's evidence," he said finally. "That today happened. That I was there, and it was real, and I didn't imagine the part where I became someone else."

I didn't argue. There was no arguing with a man reasoning his way through his own transformation, and besides — I understood. Better than he knew.

We slept in shifts, as always. But when my shift ended and I woke him, he surfaced slowly, blinking, and before he fully woke he reached for me in the dark, found my wrist, and held on — not gripping, just *holding*, the way you hold something you're afraid the mountain will take.

Neither of us named it. Neither of us needed to. The mountain had already heard everything.

Dawn came gray and cold, and with it, sound from below: voices. More than one. Carrying up the valley on the still morning air, from the direction of the narrows — men digging, shouting to each other, uncovering what the cornice had buried.

Voss hadn't sent three men.

He'd sent five.
