## 14. What Loyalty Costs

The man had been running downhill when Sefa's snare took him, and he had not stopped screaming since.

Not screaming, exactly. Hazel corrected himself as he picked his way down the scree with the rifle loose in his hands. Moaning. A low, continuous sound, like wind through a crack in a door, the kind of noise a body made when it had given up on dignity and was simply reporting its condition. The man lay on his side at the bottom of the gully, one ankle cinched in the loop of braided cord Sefa had set across the game trail at dawn, his other leg bent under him at an angle that made Hazel's stomach climb.

Sefa stood over him with the axe. Not raised. Just held, the way you'd hold a tool you hadn't yet decided to use.

"Don't," the man said. "Please. Please don't."

Hazel knew the voice before he knew the face.

It came back to him in pieces, the way the camp had come back to him two weeks ago — the mail boat, the scattered tents, the cook's staged fall — assembled from fragments he hadn't known he was keeping. A voice calling across the base camp in the early morning, laughing about the coffee. A voice reading out coordinates while Hazel had stood at the edge of the tents, reading his father's ransacked maps, feeling the resentment curdle into something worse.

"Okonkwo," Hazel said. "Sam Okonkwo."

The man stopped moaning. He turned his head, and Hazel saw his face properly for the first time — hollowed, frost-burned at the cheekbones, the beard grown in patchy and grey. The expedition's surveyor. The one whose boot prints had matched the graves. The one whose survival Hazel had spent three days dreading, because a survivor working with Voss was worse than a stranger with a knife. A stranger wanted your supplies. A survivor wanted your silence.

"You're the son," Okonkwo said. "You're Yarrow's boy."

"I'm the son."

"He talks about you." Okonkwo laughed, and it broke in the middle and became something else. "God. He talks about you all the time. Did you know that? Every camp, every night. *My son, my son* — like a prayer nobody answered."

"Shut up," Hazel said, and was surprised at how much it cost him to say it, and how much it cost him that it cost anything at all.

Sefa hadn't moved. Snow was starting again, fat slow flakes that landed on the axe head and didn't melt. "He was at the cook's grave," she said quietly. Not to Hazel. To the gully, to the mountain, to the record. "The prints were his."

"I know whose prints they were," Hazel said.

"Then you know what he did."

"I know what the prints said." He crouched, keeping the rifle between them, not pointing it anywhere in particular and pointing it everywhere at once. "I don't know what he did. There's a difference."

"There's a dead cook," Sefa said. "There are two dead men at the wall. There's a difference between a man who kills and a man who stands there while it happens, and it is not a difference that matters to the dead."

Okonkwo had begun to cry. Not loudly. The tears just came, freezing in the creases of his eyes, and he didn't wipe them because his hands were shaking too badly to be trusted near his own face.

"Voss has my brother," he said.

The gully went quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Dar es Salaam," Okonkwo said. "Voss's people found him there. Owed money to the wrong men — the same men Voss's company uses for, for clearing, for problems. Voss bought the debt. All of it. And then he sat across a table from me at the trading post with a photograph of my brother and a cup of coffee he'd bought me, and he said, *Sam, I'm not a monster. All I want is the drawings.*" His voice climbed, cracked, dropped. "I said no. I want you to know that. I said no for six days. I said no until they sent me the photograph again with a date written on the back of it. A date, and nothing else. Just a date."

Hazel looked at the man's ankle in the snare, at the leg bent wrong beneath him, at the frost creeping into the grey of his beard, and felt the whole moral architecture of the last two weeks — the clean, load-bearing anger that had gotten him over the pass — begin, very quietly, to sag.

"You led them to us," Sefa said.

"I led them to the trail. I drew them a map of where I thought you'd cross." Okonkwo's eyes came up, and there was something in them worse than fear — the flat, exhausted honesty of a man who had stopped negotiating with himself. "I didn't know they'd hit the ambush point at the col. I told Voss you'd come down the eastern route. I was trying to buy you time, do you understand? I was trying—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It doesn't matter what I was trying. Men are dead. I was there when the cook fell. I could have—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The sentence finished itself in the space between them, in the snow, in the silence of a man who had watched and said nothing because a photograph existed somewhere with a date on its back.

Hazel stood up. His knee cracked. The rifle felt suddenly very heavy and very stupid in his hands, a thing designed to end arguments he was only beginning to understand.

"Sefa," he said. "A word."

They walked ten paces up the gully, out of Okonkwo's hearing — though Hazel doubted the man could have heard a rockslide just then. Sefa kept the axe. She didn't seem to notice she was holding it, which was somehow worse.

"We can't carry him," she said. "Not with my arm. Not over the pass. You know the arithmetic."

"I know the arithmetic."

"Then say what you're actually asking me."

Hazel looked back down the slope. Okonkwo lay where he'd fallen, a dark shape in the white, and the snow was already doing its patient work, softening his outline, beginning the process of erasing him that the mountain performed on everything eventually and on some things ahead of schedule.

"He's a witness," Hazel said.

"He's a murderer."

"He's a coward who was blackmailed, and a coward who was blackmailed can testify." He heard his own voice go flat and precise, the voice he used at salvage digs when he was pricing someone's grief, and he hated it, and he used it anyway, because it was the only voice that could carry what he had to carry. "Think about what happens if we leave him. Voss's men find him, or the mountain finds him, and either way he's gone, and everything he knows goes with him. Who Voss's buyers are. What they offered my father. What was said at the cook's grave. Sefa — my father is dying in a hut at the bottom of this valley. I watched him breathe last night and I counted the breaths. When he's gone, the only person alive who can put Voss in a room with a photograph and a debt is lying in this gully with his ankle in a snare."

"And if he's lying?" Sefa's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the frightening thing about her anger; it came down like a barometer. "If Voss sent him alone on purpose? If the brother, the debt, the photograph — if it's all a story shaped exactly like the one a man would tell two people holding an axe?"

"Then we've lost nothing but our chance to kill him."

"We've lost more than that." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were bright and furious and afraid. "We've lost the version of us that didn't have to decide this. Hazel. I set that snare for Voss's men. All of them. I set it and I walked away and I slept. If we let him live, we have to live with him — feed him, carry him, trust him not to put a knife in your back at the col. And if we kill him, we have to live with *that*. There is no version of today that we walk away from clean. So don't stand there and tell me about witnesses. Tell me which weight you can carry. Because I've carried men out of the mountains before, and I've buried them too, and the carrying is harder. It's always harder. That's the trap of it. Mercy is just a debt you agree to owe."

Hazel said nothing for a long time. The snow came down between them, indifferent, filling the gully, filling the world.

He thought about his father. Not the dying man in the hut — the man in the journal, the one who had found the passage and hidden the drawings and said nothing for twenty years, not even to his own son, because knowing had gotten Hazel's mother killed. Marcus Yarrow had spent his whole life choosing silence over safety, loyalty over truth, and the bill had come due in a shepherd's hut with his lungs filling and his son counting his breaths. Loyalty to the dead, to the secret, to the expedition, to the past — his father had paid it in full, in every currency there was, and what had it bought? A canyon full of graves and a son who hadn't spoken to him in six years.

The dead didn't need loyalty. That was the thing nobody told you. The dead couldn't spend it. It just piled up in the accounts of the living, compounding, until it crushed the living flat.

But truth — truth could be spent. Truth could be carried down out of the mountains and put on a table in a courtroom, in a newspaper, in the trading post over the radio, and it could buy things. It could buy a brother's freedom. It could buy a heritage designation and a locked gate. It could buy the cook's name spoken aloud, at last, by someone who hadn't stood silent while he fell.

"Okonkwo," Hazel called down the gully.

The man's head came up. Hope and terror in the same face, fighting for space, neither winning.

"Voss promised you leniency," Hazel said. "If you delivered the drawings."

"Yes."

"Did he promise it in front of witnesses?"

Okonkwo blinked. "What?"

"Witnesses. Did anyone else hear him offer it? The trading post — was anyone there? Names, Sam. I need names."

"There was — the postmaster heard part of it. And Voss's man Ferro, he was at the table, he did the talking about the debt, he—" Okonkwo's voice was steadying, finding its footing on this new ground, the ground of testimony. Hazel watched it happen and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that this was the man's only way down the mountain alive, and that he had known it, and that some part of Okonkwo had been waiting two weeks for someone to ask him exactly this question.

"And the cook," Hazel said. "His name was Aldous Brandt. I read it in my father's journal. Tell me what happened at the grave. All of it. In order. And then you're going to tell it again, at the bottom of this valley, in front of the radio at the trading post if we have to carry you there on a litter made of tent poles. You're going to be the most inconvenient witness Voss's money ever bought."

Okonkwo was crying again, but it was different now, and Hazel turned away from it because watching a man be relieved was somehow harder than watching him be afraid.

Sefa hadn't moved.

"You're serious," she said.

"I'm serious."

"You understand what you're choosing. Not him. *You.*"

"I understand." He looked at her, and let her see all of it — the arithmetic, the anger, the sagging architecture of the last two weeks, and underneath it, the thing he'd finally built in its place. "My father was loyal to the dead his whole life. It got everyone killed. I'm not going to finish his expedition for him, Sefa. I'm going to finish his *testimony*. There's a difference, and I think it's the only difference that matters."

The axe head was white with snow now. Sefa looked down at it for a long moment, and then she did something Hazel had never seen her do: she set it down, deliberately, in the snow, handle upright, like a man planting a claim.

"All right," she said. "But hear me. If he slows us past the snowline, if his ankle goes and he can't walk, I make the call. Not you. You've spent your loyalty for today, and it was the right spend, but I'm not spending mine twice."

"Agreed."

"And Hazel." She picked up the axe, slung it through her belt, and something in her face shifted — not softening, exactly. Recognition. "The man who decides that mercy is a debt he's willing to owe, in a gully, in the snow, with a killer behind him and a dying father below him — that man is not the man who got on the mail boat. I want you to know I noticed."

He didn't have an answer for that. He wasn't sure one existed. He went down the slope instead, and knelt in the snow beside Sam Okonkwo, and cut the snare, and splinted the broken leg with an ice axe handle and the lacing from his own boot, and the whole time Okonkwo talked — the trading post, the postmaster's name, Ferro, the date on the photograph, the cook's fall, all of it, in order, pouring out like water finding the crack in a dam — and Hazel listened and memorized and did not forgive him, because forgiveness wasn't his to give. It belonged to the dead, and the dead could wait. The living could not.

They made camp that night in the shelter of the gully's lip, three of them now, and the fire was small and mean and Sefa sat with her back to a stone and her wounded arm bound tight and watched Okonkwo the way a hawk watches a field, and Hazel took first watch because he knew he wouldn't sleep.

It was near midnight when Okonkwo spoke from the darkness beyond the fire.

"Your father," he said. "In the journal. The last entry. Did you read it?"

Hazel didn't answer.

"He wrote one more," Okonkwo said. "He didn't put it in the tin. He gave it to me. The day before the cook died. He said, *If anything happens to me, give this to my son, and if my son won't take it, burn it.*" A pause. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, on the ridgeline, snow slithered off a rock with a sound like a sigh. "I was supposed to give it to you at the base camp. Before all of this. I kept it instead, because I thought — I thought it might be worth something. To Voss. I thought it might buy my brother back." Hazel heard him shift, heard the splinted leg drag, and then a hand came into the firelight holding an oilskin packet, small, sealed with wax, gone soft and grey at the edges from being carried against a man's skin for three weeks.

"I'm done buying things," Okonkwo said. "Take it. And when you read it — when you read what he asks you — I need you to remember that you chose to let me live. Because what's in there, Yarrow. What your father asks you in there. You're going to need to be that man again."

Hazel took the packet. The wax was cold. His father's thumbprint was pressed into it, faded but unmistakable, the same print that must be on a hundred survey drawings in a canyon below the snowline, the same hand that had held a pen over a last entry and written his son's name.

He broke the seal.

* * *

The page was thin, the kind of paper a man carries folded against his heart until it takes the shape of him. Marcus Yarrow's handwriting, usually so precise it looked printed, slanting downhill now, hurried, the letters of a man writing against a deadline he could feel in his chest.

*Hazel. If you're reading this, then either Sam found his courage or you found him, and either way I'm proud, which you'll have to take on faith because I was never any good at saying it out loud.*

*The drawings in the oilcloth are not the only set. There is a second set, and I buried it where no one will look for it, and I am going to tell you where, and then I am going to ask you for the hardest thing I have ever asked anyone, which is a long list, God knows.*

*Voss's buyers are not salvage men. They are not treasure hunters. Two of them sit on the heritage board that will decide the passage's future, and if the drawings come down this mountain through official hands, they will be altered, or lost, or ruled a forgery, and the passage will be opened by permit within five years and stripped within ten. I have watched it happen before. I watched it happen to your mother's site. It is why she is dead, though I have never had the courage to write that sentence until now.*

*So here is what I ask. Don't bring the drawings down. Don't burn them either. Bury them. Both sets. Let the passage keep its own secret, the way it kept it for four hundred years before us, and let the mountain be the only witness, because the mountain is the only witness that can't be bought.*

*I know what I'm asking. I'm asking you to choose the dead over the living one more time. I'm asking you to be me. And I know what it cost you, being my son, and I know you came up this river to find the truth, and I am telling you, Hazel, the truth is a thing that gets people killed, and the only mercy I ever learned is that some truths keep better in the ground.*

*But you were always better than me at knowing which debts to pay. So I'll leave it in your hands. Burn them, bury them, broadcast them — whatever you choose, choose it as yourself, not as me. That's all I ever wanted. That's the whole confession. The rest is just geography.*

*Your father, who loved you the whole time and said it too late,*

*M.*

The fire had burned down to coals. Sefa was watching him from across it, and Okonkwo was watching him, and the snow came down on all of them alike, patient, indifferent, keeping its own counsel.

Hazel folded the page along its old creases and put it inside his jacket, against his chest, where it would take his shape.

Below them, far down in the valley, a light moved in the dark. One light, then two, descending the far slope toward the shepherd's hut — torches, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew where they were going and didn't think anyone was watching.

Voss hadn't been waiting for the drawings to come down the mountain.

He'd gone down to get them himself.
