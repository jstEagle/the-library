## 15. The White Crossing

**Hazel**

The snow started an hour after they left the col behind, and by the second hour it had erased everything that could be called a trail.

Hazel walked with his head down, following Sefa's boots because he couldn't follow anything else. The world had contracted to a gray circle three feet wide — her heels, his own hands, the rope between them. Above that circle there was nothing but white moving sideways, and a wind that didn't howl so much as grind, like something enormous chewing slowly on the mountain.

"Talk to me," Sefa shouted over her shoulder.

"What?"

"So you don't stop talking. People who go quiet up here start walking in circles. Then they sit down." She turned back into the wind. "So talk."

He talked. He told her about the salvage dig in the south, the one he'd hated — pulling bullets and belt buckles out of a riverbank for a developer who wanted to build condos on a massacre site. He told her about the foreman who called him Professor because Hazel had once used the word *stratigraphy*. He told her anything, everything, filling the air the way she'd asked, and underneath all of it ran the thought he wouldn't say: *my father is somewhere on this mountain, or beyond it, and I am lost above the snowline with four days of food and a woman I've known for two weeks.*

They camped at what Sefa judged was midafternoon, though the light gave no argument either way. They dug into a drift until they'd hollowed something like a cave, lined it with the tarp, and crawled in together because separate shelters meant two people freezing instead of one person cold.

Her back was against his chest. He could feel her shivering even through both their coats.

"You're shaking," he said.

"I'm conserving conversation." A pause. "Your heart is very loud."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. It's the warmest thing here."

* * *

**Sefa**

She woke to darkness that wasn't night — just the storm sealing them in, no difference between the hours. Her shoulder throbbed where the bullet had grazed her, a deep hot pulse under the bandage, and she lay still and catalogued it the way her grandmother had taught her: pain that pulses is alive; pain that spreads is trouble; pain you can name is pain you can carry.

Hazel was awake too. She knew it from his breathing, which was the breathing of a man pretending to sleep.

"The food," he said finally.

"I know."

"How much is 'I know'?"

"Two days. Maybe three if we're honest about portions, which we won't be."

Silence. Outside, the wind found a new register, higher, almost conversational, as if the mountain were muttering to itself.

"My mother died in weather like this," Sefa said.

She hadn't meant to say it. It came out the way things come out in whiteouts — because there was nothing else in the world, nowhere for words to hide.

Hazel's arm tightened around her, careful of the shoulder. "You don't have to—"

"She was a guide too. Not like me. She guided pilgrims up to the lake shrines, the old routes. There was a party that went late in the season against everyone's advice, a rich man who'd paid enough that the outfitter said yes." Sefa watched her breath fog and vanish. "She went because we needed the money. Because my father had debts, and debts in our valley are not a thing you decline twice. The storm took four of them. It took her last, they said later, because she kept giving her coat away. Kept giving pieces of herself to keep the others walking."

"Sefa—"

"I was fourteen. My father never forgave himself, which is its own kind of dying, slower. And I learned that mountains don't take sides. Everyone talks about the mountain's cruelty, the mountain's judgment. It has neither. It's just honest in a way people can't afford to be." She stopped. "That's why I took your father's job. Not only the debt. I wanted to be up here where honesty lives."

Hazel was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"I haven't been honest in six years," he said. "Maybe longer."

* * *

**Hazel**

The second day they moved anyway, because staying put was its own death sentence and Sefa's arithmetic of food left no room for cowardice. They climbed when she said climb, traversed when she said traverse, and twice she stopped dead and made him stand silent for full minutes while she listened to a landscape that sounded identical in every direction.

The second time, she said, "We're off the route."

"How far?"

"I don't know. That's what 'lost' means." But her voice was steady, and steady was contagious, and they walked on.

That night the cave was smaller, the cold more intimate. His hands had stopped hurting, which frightened him more than pain would have, and Sefa noticed — of course she noticed — and pulled his fingers inside her coat against her stomach without a word, the way you'd handle something already broken.

"I hated him," Hazel said.

It came out flat, a fact laid on the table between their bodies.

"For six years I hated him. After Mom died, he didn't call. Didn't come to the funeral — no, that's not true, he came, he stood at the back like a man attending a stranger's service, and he left before the dirt went in. Six years, Sefa. Birthdays. Christmases. The anniversary. Nothing. I built a whole man out of that silence. Cold. Ambitious. A father who chose old rocks over his own family." His breath shook. "And then I find his journal in a supply tin and learn the silence was protection. Learn that knowing him — knowing what he knew — is what got my mother killed in the first place. Some buyer's people, years ago, an accident arranged to look like one. And Marcus carried that alone rather than let me carry any part of it."

Sefa said nothing. She had learned, somewhere in these weeks, when silence was the right answer.

"And here's the thing I can't say to anyone living," Hazel went on, "so I'll say it to you, in a hole in a glacier, where it can't hurt anyone. I loved him the whole time. Every day of the six years. I hated him and I checked the mail for letters from him and I hated myself for checking and I loved him for making me hate myself, and now he's dying on the other side of this range and all I want—" His voice failed. He started again, smaller. "All I want is one hour. One honest hour. And I'd trade every year of the anger for it."

The wind ground on outside. Sefa's hand found his in the dark, his thawing fingers, and held them.

"He'll have his hour," she said. "We're going to get you there."

* * *

**Sefa**

The third day the storm broke for ninety minutes around noon — a blue seam tearing open above them, showing a ridgeline they did not recognize and a valley below that ran the wrong way. Sefa stood in the sudden light and felt the old fear, the pilgrim-party fear, rise in her throat like water.

Then she killed it. That was the skill nobody praised, the real inheritance from her mother: not courage, but the speed of killing fear before it finished being born.

"We drop east," she said. "There'll be tree line by evening. Trees mean fuel, fuel means a fire, fire means we live to be lost tomorrow instead of dying lost today."

They dropped. The snow got deeper and then wetter, and by late afternoon they heard it — water, running under the white, a stream feeling its way downhill — and followed it down through thinning drifts into a stand of black pines bent like old men under their loads.

The fire took on the third match. They sat as close to it as burning allowed, drying socks, melting snow in the dented pot, eating half a ration each and lying to each other about not being hungry.

"Hazel." She watched the flames. "What you said last night. About loving him while hating him."

"Yeah."

"That's not a contradiction. That's just love, in the wild. Love isn't the warm thing people think it is. It's the thing that keeps walking when walking is stupid. It's giving your coat away." She looked at him then, across the small fire, and the thing she had been carrying since the canyon wall — since the stairway, since the graves, since the ambush below the col when he'd bound her wound with hands that shook and apologized to her for existing — rose in her chest and would not be swallowed. "I turned back alone once. At base camp, the first morning, I nearly did. I told myself I'd guide you to the fork and leave you there, take my fee, settle my debt, forget your face."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you lied badly to Voss's men and I covered for you, and when we walked away I realized I'd chosen you over my own safety without deciding to. That's not how I operate. I decide everything." The fire cracked; a knot burst. "I've been deciding ever since, every morning, to stay. And I'm tired of deciding. So I'm saying it once, in the teeth of this mountain, where honesty lives, so neither of us can pretend it was said gently or casually or anywhere safe."

She reached across and took his hand, the burned-and-frozen one, and held it the way you hold something you intend to keep.

"I love you, Hazel Yarrow. Say something or don't, but say it honestly."

* * *

**Hazel**

He thought of a dozen answers and discarded all of them — the clever ones, the deflecting ones, the ones shaped like jokes. The mountain had stripped him of everything except the truth, and the truth was simple and enormous and had been true for longer than he'd admitted.

"I love you," he said. "I think I have since the trading post, when you lied better than I did."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, half sob — and then she was around the fire and in his arms, careful of her shoulder, careless of everything else, and they held each other while the pines shed their snow in soft collapses and the stream ran on beneath the ice toward wherever streams go when no one is watching.

Later, wrapped in the tarp with the fire banked low, her head on his chest, he felt something he hadn't felt since before his mother died: unguarded. The word arrived and he tested it and it held.

"Tomorrow," Sefa murmured, already half asleep, "we find the pass. Your father's cairns will start again below tree line. He was thorough. Thorough men leave trails."

"And if Voss is ahead of us?"

"Then we're ahead of him in the ways that matter." She tilted her head up. "He has men and guns. We have each other and the truth. In the long run, Hazel, we are better armed."

He wanted to believe her. He was learning to.

But sleep, when it finally took him, brought a sound with it — and near dawn he surfaced from a dream of his father's voice to hear it again, faint but unmistakable, drifting up the valley from the direction of the pass:

A shot. Then another. Then, far off and closing, the barking of dogs.
