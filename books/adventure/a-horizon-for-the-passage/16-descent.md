## 16. Descent

The far side of the pass was a different country.

Sefa felt it in her knees before she saw it — the angle easing, the rock giving way to scree, then to the first stubborn grass pushing up through old snow. Below them the valley opened like a held breath finally let out: a long green throat of a place, the river running silver through it, willows crowding the banks, and somewhere down there, smoke.

She stopped and looked at it for a long time. Not because she doubted. Because she'd learned, on this mountain, that good news deserved to be checked twice.

"Smoke," Hazel said behind her. His voice had gone rough over three days above the snowline, scraped raw by wind and by everything they'd said to each other in the white. He came up beside her, and she watched him see it — the thin gray thread rising off the valley floor, maybe two miles down, near where the river bent against the western slope.

"A hut," Sefa said. "Shepherd's, probably. They use them in summer, move the flocks up high." She paused. "It's occupied."

Hazel didn't answer right away. She understood why. For eleven days now the smoke had meant one thing: Voss's men, a fire built by someone hunting them. But Voss's men were behind them — or dead, or scattered across the col where Hazel had done the thing he still couldn't talk about. The man they'd taken had given them what he knew before Sefa sent him walking downslope with half his rations and all of his shame: Voss himself was coming around the southern route with the rest of his crew, two days behind at best.

Two days. Maybe less.

"Could be herders," Hazel said anyway. Wanting it to be, the way a starving man wants a stone to be bread.

"It could be," Sefa said, and started down.

* * *

The descent took them most of the day, and the mountain made them pay for every meter of it. The snow turned to slush, the slush to mud, and by late afternoon they were picking their way through willow scrub with their boots soaked through and their hands cut from the alder. Sefa led, because she always led on ground like this, reading the slope the way other people read a page. Hazel followed in her tracks without complaint. He'd learned that much about her in the storm — when to argue and when to simply put his feet where hers had been.

Twice she stopped him with a raised hand and listened. Once it was a ptarmigan exploding out of the brush, and they both stood there with their hearts hammering, feeling foolish, feeling alive. The second time it was nothing at all, just wind moving through the valley, but she kept them still for a full minute anyway, because the valley was too quiet. No sheep bells. No birdsong from the river flats. A working valley in autumn should have been loud with preparation for winter.

"Someone scared everything off," she murmured.

"Or someone's already here."

They found the hut an hour before dark.

It sat against the slope like something the mountain had tried to swallow and failed — low stone walls, a turf roof sagging in the middle, a corral of broken fence posts behind it. The smoke rose from a chimney crack, thin and steady. A fire tended by someone who wasn't going anywhere.

Sefa circled wide, the way she would have if she were alone, and Hazel followed her lead without being told. She noted the details as they came: no pack animals, no snares set, a single pair of boots outside the door, cracked and salt-stained and too large for any of Voss's crew. Expedition boots. She'd seen their like at base camp, lined up neat as teeth before everything went wrong.

She put her hand on Hazel's arm. He was staring at the door like it might open on anything — a gun, a grave, a stranger wearing his father's coat.

"Hazel." She said it quietly. "Whoever's in there has been alone a long time. Alone changes people. Go in slow, and go in kind."

He looked at her, and for a moment she saw how young he was under all that damage, and how tired, and how much he loved a man he'd spent six years learning to hate.

Then he pushed the door open.

* * *

The man inside was not the Marcus Yarrow from the photographs Sefa had glimpsed at base camp — the broad-shouldered one standing beside a trench wall, sunburned and grinning, one hand resting on his son's shoulder in a picture taken before either of them knew how the story would end.

This man was folded into the corner of the hut like a letter someone had crumpled and decided not to send. Gray-faced. Cheeks hollowed. Hands wrapped in filthy bandages, frostbitten past saving on two fingers, if Sefa's eye was any judge, and her eye was good. He'd dragged himself here somehow — she could see the story written in the hut itself: the melted snow line on the wall where he'd propped himself between the fire and the door, the tin plate with weeks of scraps, the crutch made from a tent pole lashed to a ski pole. He had crawled, walked, and crawled again, and the mountain had let him live out of what — indifference? amusement?

He looked up at the light spilling through the open door, and for a moment his eyes were empty, the flat stare of a man who had stopped expecting anything. Then they focused. Then they widened.

"Hazel," Marcus Yarrow said.

Not *my son*. Not *you came*. Just the name, said the way you'd say the name of a country you'd dreamed about from a prison cell — unable to believe it and afraid to.

Hazel stood in the doorway with the wind at his back and said nothing at all.

Sefa stayed by the door. This was not hers. She watched the two of them look at each other across twelve feet of packed earth floor, father and son, and thought: *this is what damage looks like when it's had years to cure.* Not shouting. Not tears. Just silence with a whole history inside it, both of them waiting for the other one to speak first, neither one knowing how.

"You're alive," Hazel said finally. It came out almost angry. "You're actually— we dug a grave for your cook. We buried two more men at the canyon wall. I crossed a mountain thinking I was following your body home."

"I know." Marcus's voice was a ruin, barely there. "I heard the shots. At the col. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I couldn't signal. If Voss's people were watching the valley—"

"So you sat here. In the dark. While I—" Hazel's jaw worked. "While everyone thought you were dead."

"Yes." No excuse in it. No apology either. Just the fact, laid down like a stone.

Sefa saw Hazel's hands curl at his sides and uncurl again. She saw him take one step into the hut, then stop, as if the distance between them were physical, a river he didn't know how to ford. Six years of silence, and now the man who'd caused it was ten feet away and dying — she could see the dying from the doorway, the yellow tinge at the collar, the way his breathing caught and hitched. Whatever was wrong with him was deep and getting deeper, and they had days, maybe less.

"You could have told me," Hazel said. Quietly now, which was worse than shouting. "Any of it. The passage, the buyers, whatever you were running from. You told strangers. You trusted a guide you'd known a month more than you trusted me."

"I trusted you with nothing," Marcus said, "because nothing was the only safe thing I had to give you."

"That's not—"

"It is." He shifted against the wall, and the movement cost him; Sefa saw him grip the crutch until the tendons stood out in his wrist. "You think I don't know what it cost you? Your mother's death, and then me going silent? You think I chose it?" His breath whistled. "I chose the only version of your life where you got to keep living it."

And there it was — the thing Sefa had pieced together from journal pages and graves and a captured man's confession, said aloud at last, ugly and plain. Hazel went very still.

"Don't," he said. "Don't make it noble. You weren't protecting me, you were hiding from me. There's a difference."

"Yes," Marcus said. "There is. And I've had a lot of time in this hut to work out which one I did." He looked down at his ruined hands. "Mostly the second one."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind moved through the willows with a sound like slow applause. Sefa found herself holding her breath, willing Hazel toward something — she didn't even know what. Forgiveness was too big a word. Maybe just acknowledgment. Maybe just *I'm here.*

What Hazel said was: "We brought food. And antibiotics, from the supply cache. You need to eat, and those fingers need proper cleaning, and then you're going to tell me everything, start to finish, and this time nobody leaves anything out."

Marcus looked up. Something moved across his face — relief, or grief, or the strange gratitude of a man being ordered to live by the last person he'd expected to order it.

"All right," he said.

It wasn't catharsis. Sefa had imagined, during the long white nights, what a reunion might look like — embraces, weeping, six years dissolving in a moment. What happened instead was smaller and harder: Hazel kneeling by the fire without a word, heating water; Sefa cutting away the rotten bandages while Marcus stared at the roof beam and breathed through his teeth; the three of them eating stew in a silence that was not peace but at least wasn't war. Two people who loved each other and had forgotten, or never learned, the grammar for saying so. A stranger in the room who could see exactly what was broken and knew none of them had time to fix it.

But once, while she was rewrapping his hands, Marcus looked at her — really looked, with the appraising eye of a field scientist cataloguing something unexpected — and said, "You got my son across the Kestrel Range in November. That shouldn't be possible."

"He's stubborn," Sefa said.

"So am I," Marcus said. "Look how well it served me." And for a second, just a second, the ghost of the grin from the photograph surfaced through the gray face, and Sefa understood that Hazel had inherited more from this man than his jaw.

* * *

Full dark came early, the way it did now. Sefa stepped outside to check the sky — habit, discipline, the guide's litany — and found the stars hard and brilliant in a windless black. Good weather. Which meant good travel weather. For anyone.

She counted what she knew: Voss on the southern route, two days out, maybe one. One man lost at the col, one sent down in disgrace, and Voss himself too proud and too invested to turn back with the prize a day's walk away. The oilcloth was somewhere in this hut — she'd seen the shape of it under Marcus's sleeping mat, the way he'd positioned his body between it and the door even unconscious, even dreaming. A dying man guarding paper with his last strength.

Inside, voices. Low. Father and son, talking past each other in the careful way of people defusing something old and unstable. She caught fragments through the turf and timber: *your mother* — *I never wanted* — *you don't get to decide what I can survive*. Not reconciliation. Inventory. Two men counting what remained.

Sefa breathed the cold air and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

They could not stay. Staying meant Voss arrived to find all three of them in one hut, wounded and cornered, and Voss did not leave witnesses — the graves on the trail had settled that question forever. But Marcus could not travel. She'd cleaned his hands; she'd seen the infection climbing his arms, the fever banked in his eyes. Moving him would kill him. Staying would kill all of them. And somewhere between those two certain deaths lay a third option she hadn't found yet, and the clock on it was ticking down from two days.

Behind her, the door opened. Hazel came out and stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, which had become, somewhere in the storm, a language of its own.

"He's sleeping," Hazel said. "Finally. He talks like a man paying off a debt, one coin at a time, and he's terrified of running out of coins before he finishes."

"Did he finish?"

"No." Hazel stared out at the valley, at the river catching starlight. "He says tomorrow. He says there are things I need to hear sober and rested, and things—" His voice caught. "Things about my mother. About why she was really on that dig in Talis nine years ago. Sefa, he said her name like it was a debt too. Like he owed her something worse than silence."

Sefa said nothing. There was nothing yet to say. Tomorrow would bring the confession or it wouldn't, and either way—

That was when she saw it. Down-valley, where the river bent west and the tree line began. A light. Small, moving, shuttered — the disciplined glow of a lantern carried by someone who didn't want to be seen.

Not one light. Three.

She took Hazel's sleeve and turned him toward it and felt him go rigid beside her.

"They're a day early," she said. "Maybe two. Whoever's scouting ahead." Her mind was already moving, laying the terrain over the problem like a hand over a map. "The corral fence is broken on the north side. There's a drainage cut behind the hut that runs up toward the high pasture. If we're quiet, and if the mountain is kind—"

"We can't carry him up a mountain."

"No," Sefa said softly. "We can't." She watched the lights move, patient as fish under ice, closing on the bend of the river. "So tonight, before they get here, your father finishes telling you everything. And then we decide what's worth more — his life, or the truth in that oilcloth. Because I don't think we'll get to save both."

The lights went out, all three at once.

Down in the dark valley, the river kept talking to itself, indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea.
