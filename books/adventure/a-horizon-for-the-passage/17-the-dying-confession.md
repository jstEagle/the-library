## 17. The Dying Confession

The shepherd's hut smelled of woodsmoke and old wool and the sweetish rot of a body negotiating its own surrender.

Hazel had not been able to look at his father for the first hour. He'd busied himself instead — feeding the stove, melting snow in the dented kettle, checking the oilcloth bundle where it lay wrapped in the corner like a sleeping animal — anything to keep his hands moving so they wouldn't have to do the other thing, which was hold on.

Marcus Yarrow lay on the cot beneath two blankets and a sheepskin, propped against a saddle that had seen better centuries. He was sixty-one years old and looked carved from something worse than stone. The wound in his side had been bound with strips of shirt and packed with moss, Sefa's fieldwork, competent and grim, and it had bought him time the way a leaking boat buys you distance from shore: some, but not enough.

"You always were," Marcus said, "a terrible liar."

Hazel looked up. His father's eyes were open, fixed on him with an clarity that seemed almost cruel, as if dying had burned away everything except the part of Marcus that had spent forty years reading people in pits and trenches and river camps.

"You knew," Hazel said. "When we came through the door. You knew it was me."

"I heard you arguing with the guide about the map. You argue exactly like your mother." A breath that might have been a laugh, if lungs still did that. "She never lost an argument in her life. She just waited until you ran out of words."

"Don't." Hazel set the kettle down harder than he meant to. "Don't do that. Don't make her a— a technique."

Marcus closed his eyes. When he opened them again there was something in them Hazel had never seen there before, and it took him a long moment to name it, because it had no business being on his father's face.

It was fear.

"Sit down, Hazel."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit down." And then, quieter, in a voice scraped down to the grain: "Please. I have maybe a day in me. Maybe less. I've spent six years deciding what to tell you and I'm going to spend all of it now, and I'd rather you were sitting when I do."

Hazel sat. The stool wobbled on the uneven flagstones. Outside, the wind worried at the hut's dry-stone walls the way a dog worries a bone it can't crack, and somewhere below them in the valley a stream ran loud with snowmelt, absurdly alive.

* * *

"I found the passage in the second week," Marcus began. "You know that much. The stairway in the canyon wall — pre-colonial, older than anything in the surveys, a trade route through the range that every academic in this hemisphere would have wept over. What you don't know is what happened next, because what happened next is that I made a radio call."

"To the university."

"To the university, yes. And the university has a development office, and the development office has friends, and within nine days there were men at my camp who had never held a trowel in their lives asking to see my survey drawings." He shifted, and the pain of it moved across his face like weather. "Voss wasn't the buyer. Voss is the errand. The buyers are three names on a letterhead in the capital — antiquities brokers with museum boards and government contracts, respectable men whose fingerprints never touch anything. They wanted the route mapped, documented, and then buried. Sealed. Because a passage like that isn't just a discovery, Hazel. It's leverage. It's a corridor through mountains that certain people would very much like to move things through without customs officers or journalists asking questions."

"The drawings."

"In the oilcloth. Every survey line, every elevation, every entrance. Enough to find it again from either side of the range." He looked at the bundle in the corner. "I told them no. I told the university no. I pulled our permits, I filed to make the site protected heritage, and I thought — God help me, I thought paperwork would protect it. That the law was a wall."

"And they sent Voss."

"They sent money first. Then threats. Then Voss." Marcus's hand moved on the blanket, restless, tracing something. "Two of my team took the money. I don't blame them the way I should. One of them is dead now — Okafor, our cook. He refused. They staged his fall at the scree slope and I've been carrying his name up this mountain like a stone in my boot."

Hazel thought of the grave they'd dug in ground hard as iron, of the cairn at the fork, of *not the way we came*. "So you ran."

"I retreated. There's a difference, though I'll grant it's thin." Marcus's mouth twisted. "I sent the team downriver with pay and silence agreements. I kept the drawings. I came up here to wait out the season and file from the valley post in spring. And then—" He stopped.

The wind filled the pause. Hazel let it.

"And then," Marcus said, "you came looking for me. Which I had specifically, explicitly, in writing, forbidden."

"You didn't forbid anything. You went silent. Two missed check-ins. Nobody would go. So I went."

"I know why you went." His father's eyes were wet now, and he didn't wipe them, didn't seem to notice. "You went because despite everything — despite six years of nothing, of returned letters, of me canceling every visit, missing every birthday, letting the silence grow between us like scar tissue — you still came. And that is the thing I cannot forgive myself for, Hazel, because I did all of it on purpose. Every bit of it. The estrangement wasn't neglect. It was architecture."

"What are you talking about?"

Marcus turned his head on the saddle and looked at him, and what was in his face then made Hazel's chest go tight and cold.

"Do you remember how your mother died?"

The question landed like a blow. "Avalanche. On the Torvane dig. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said avalanche." Marcus's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Because I signed the report that said avalanche. Because I was twenty-nine years old and I had found something on that dig too — smaller, but the same shape. A cache. Documents. Things that certain families in this country had paid good money to keep underground, literally underground, and I was young enough to think that truth was a shield instead of a target. I talked about it. At a conference. In a bar. To people I thought were colleagues."

Hazel stood up. The stool went over behind him and he didn't pick it up. "Say it plainly."

"She was on the survey team above us. The route she was taking that morning was changed the night before. New plan, new assignment, and your mother — careful, methodical Elena, who double-knotted her own boots — followed the changed plan without question because it came from the expedition lead, and the expedition lead had been reached." Marcus's breath caught, held, released. "Three days after I gave a paper on the Torvane cache. Three days. I've done the arithmetic ten thousand times and it never comes out any different."

"That's not—" Hazel's hands were shaking. "That's not possible. There was an inquiry. There were reports—"

"There were reports I co-signed. Because by then I understood what had happened, or most of it, and I understood that the only thing standing between those people and doing it again was whether anyone knew I knew. So I became the safest kind of witness. A silent one. And when you were born — when *she* was gone and you were all I had left — I made a decision that I have hated every single day since, which was that the further you were from me and my work, the further you were from the bill that comes due for knowing things." He swallowed. "Every canceled visit. Every unanswered letter. I watched you learn to hate me and I let you, because a son who hates his father keeps his distance, and distance, Hazel, was the only protection I had left to give."

The stove ticked. Snowmelt ran. Somewhere in the walls a marmalade-colored cat that Sefa had befriended yesterday shifted and resettled, indifferent to the ruin of human lives conducted around it.

"You let me think I wasn't worth staying for," Hazel said. His voice came out low and cracked and he hated it. "Sixteen years old, burying my mother, and my father decides the best way to love me is to disappear. Do you know what I built out of that? Do you know what I decided I was?"

"I know exactly what you decided you were. I read your salvage reports. All of them. Under other people's names so you wouldn't find out." A ghost of the old stubbornness. "You're brilliant, and you're angry, and you take jobs that punish you because some part of you believes punishment is what you're owed. I did that. Not the buyers. Me. I chose the architecture over the boy."

"Don't." Hazel's vision blurred. "Don't you dare make this clean. You don't get to confess your way into forgiveness. She's dead. Okafor's dead. Those men at the wall are dead. And you sat on the truth for twenty-five years like it was yours to sit on—"

"It was never mine," Marcus said. "That's the whole confession, son. None of it was ever mine. Not the truth, not the silence, not even the choice. I just kept paying interest on a debt other people opened in my name, and I let you believe you were the debt." He coughed then, deep and wet, and pressed his hand flat against the binding on his side, and when it came away dark Hazel felt the floor tilt under him.

"Sefa!" he called, hoarse. "Sefa—"

"I'm fine." Marcus caught his wrist with shocking strength. "I'm fine. Sit. Listen. There's not much time and there's one more thing and it's the hardest one."

Hazel sat. He took his father's hand because there was nothing else to do with his own, and Marcus held on like a drowning man, and went on.

* * *

"The buyers will come for the drawings," Marcus said. "Not Voss alone — Voss is a blade, but blades get replaced. The letterhead sends more. As long as the survey exists in one piece, in one place, we are one bribe away from losing it forever. Officials here are already bought; I told you that. The heritage filing will die in a drawer. So I need you to hear the option I couldn't bring myself to write down, even in the journal."

"Burn them," Hazel said. It wasn't a question. "Destroy the survey. If nobody can find the passage—"

"If nobody can find it, nobody can exploit it. And nobody can protect it, or study it, or stand inside it and understand that people eight hundred years dead were engineers and artists and traders who crossed these mountains when our civilization was a rumor." Marcus's grip tightened. "Burning it protects the dead and blinds the living. Publishing it saves the discovery and paints a target on everyone connected to it. Your mother is dead because someone once chose publication. I am dying because someone once chose silence. I have spent my whole life on that hinge, Hazel, and I am telling you honestly, as a man with hours left: I do not know which way to push it. I only know I can't push it anymore. It's yours."

"That's not fair."

"No," his father agreed. "It's monstrous. Welcome to inheritance."

Hazel laughed — an awful, involuntary sound, grief and fury tangled past separating — and bent forward over their joined hands, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the wind outside rose and fell like something breathing.

"I hated you," Hazel said finally. "For six years I got up every morning and hated you efficiently, professionally, like it was my job. And every night I went to sleep hoping you'd call. Both things. At the same time. Every single day."

"I know." Marcus's thumb moved once across his son's knuckles, clumsy, unpracticed, a man handling something breakable for the first time. "I did the same math from my side. Love and hate, same ledger. I just never had the courage to show you the books."

Outside, light was failing. Sefa appeared in the doorway with an armload of cut firewood and snow in her hair, took one look at the two of them — the cot, the darkened bandage, Hazel bent double over his father's hand — and withdrew without a word, closing the door softly behind her, giving them the room the way she gave everything: completely, and without being asked.

"Dad." The word came out strange, unused, a tool recovered from a collapsed dig. "Dad. I'm not going to decide tonight. But I need you to know — whatever I choose, it won't be to punish you. I'm done punishing you. I don't know what I am instead, yet. But I'm done."

Marcus smiled. It transformed his face, briefly, into the man from the old photographs — the one kneeling in a trench with a brush in his hand, young and lit up with wonder, the father Hazel had loved before he'd learned to hate him.

"That," Marcus whispered, "is more than I hoped to carry out of this world." His eyes drifted toward the window, toward the darkening valley below. "More than I—"

He stopped.

His hand tightened around Hazel's, sudden and hard, and his gaze fixed on something past the glass — down-valley, where the last light was dying off the peaks.

"Hazel," he said. "How many men did Voss have left?"

Hazel's blood went cold. "Four. Maybe five. Why?"

"Because," Marcus breathed, "there are lanterns coming up the valley road. And they're not stopping at the village."
