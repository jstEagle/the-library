## 18. Voss Arrives

### Hazel

The hut smelled of woodsmoke and iodine and the sweetish rot that Hazel had learned, over the last two days, to stop pretending he didn't recognize. His father lay on the cot beneath a sheepskin that had gone gray with age, and every breath Marcus Yarrow took now came with a sound at the end of it, wet and small, like water finding its way through gravel.

Hazel sat on the floor beside the cot because there was nowhere else to sit and because standing up meant looking down at his father, and looking down at his father meant seeing how little of him was left. Six years of silence had prepared him for anger. Nothing had prepared him for this — for the way Marcus's hands had shrunk, for the paper dryness of his lips, for the fact that the man who had once carried a hundred pounds of survey equipment over scree without breaking stride now weighed less than Hazel's pack.

"Say it again," Hazel said.

Marcus's eyes opened. They were still sharp. That was the cruelest part — the mind burning bright inside a body already half extinguished.

"Burn them."

"The drawings."

"The drawings." A pause for breath. "You'll want to keep them. I know. You came all this way for them. You crossed the range in November for them." His mouth twitched, which on Marcus passed for a smile. "Stubbornness is hereditary. I'm sorry about that too."

"They're the only proof—"

"They're bait." The word cost him; Hazel watched it move through his chest like a stone dropping through water. "Voss isn't coming for me. He doesn't care if I die. He cares about paper. Paper can be sold, copied, traded in Zurich or Singapore or wherever his buyers drink their coffee. As long as it exists, we're worth chasing. Burn it and we become what we should have been all along." Another breath, longer. "Witnesses. Nothing but witnesses. And witnesses are expensive to hunt."

"And the passage? Who protects the passage?"

"The state will protect it when it's embarrassed into protecting it. That's what testimony is for. Yours. Sefa's. Even the man you let live — especially him. Drawings get buried in archives, Hazel. Testimony gets printed." His hand moved on the blanket, an inch, an effort that visibly emptied him. "I spent twenty years believing the work could speak for itself. It can't. It never could. People speak for it, or people bury it."

Hazel looked at the oilcloth bundle where it sat on the shelf above the hearth, wedged behind a tin of lamp oil. It had traveled farther than any object he'd ever owned. His father had carried it out of the canyon with three cracked ribs and a punctured lung. Sefa had bled on the pass so it could keep moving. A man was dead below the col because of it, and Hazel had killed a man because of it, and somewhere down-valley, four men with rifles were climbing toward it through the last of the light.

"You knew Mom's death had something to do with your work," Hazel said. "And you never told me."

"I told myself I was protecting you."

"Were you?"

Marcus closed his eyes. For a moment Hazel thought he'd lost him, and his heart did something ugly and desperate against his ribs. Then the eyes opened again.

"No," Marcus said. "I was protecting myself. From having to watch you hate me while knowing the whole truth. Silence was cowardice wearing a coat of armor. I've had six years to learn the difference and I learned it too late to matter." He turned his head on the pillow, and for the first time since Hazel had arrived, he looked directly at his son and held it. "Don't inherit it. Whatever you do with those drawings — do it awake."

Outside, Sefa's voice cut across the yard, low and hard: *"Hazel."*

He was moving before the second syllable landed.

* * *

### Sefa

She had heard them before she saw them — not voices, not engines, but the dogs. The shepherd's dogs, which had been nervous all day in the way dogs are nervous before weather or wolves, went silent all at once, and silence from dogs is louder than barking.

Sefa stood in the doorway of the byre with her wounded arm strapped tight against her ribs and her rifle resting on the doorpost, and she counted them coming up the valley track: four figures in the blue-gray dusk, spaced like men who had done this before, walking where the frozen ruts would hide their footfalls. Behind them, farther down, a fifth man held the horses. She put the rifle's sights on the lead figure and made herself breathe.

Voss.

She'd seen him twice — once at the trading post, playing the concerned rescuer with his clean boots and his practiced frown, and once across the river, letting them go with that terrible patience of his. He walked the same way both times. Unhurried. Like a man arriving to collect a debt rather than fight a war.

"Hazel," she said, without raising her voice, and heard him come out of the hut behind her.

"Four," she said. "One holding back with the horses. They'll stop at the fence line. He wants to talk first. Talking costs him nothing and tells him everything."

"How much time do we have?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe fifteen. He'll let us see him counting our guns." She glanced back at him. In the last light his face was hollowed and gray, and she understood that some part of him had been waiting for this hour since the trading post, the way you wait for a storm you've watched building on the horizon for days. "How is he?"

"He's dying, Sefa. Today or tomorrow. He knows it. He wants—" Hazel stopped. Started again. "He wants me to burn the drawings."

The words landed in her chest like cold water. She looked at the hut, at the smoke rising thin and straight from its chimney, and then down the valley at the four dark figures steadily closing the distance, and she felt the whole shape of the trap they were standing in.

"If you burn them, we have nothing to bargain with."

"We were never going to bargain. You said it yourself — he doesn't need to hurry. Men who don't need to hurry don't negotiate."

"Then why hasn't he shot at us yet?" She kept her voice flat, clinical, the guide's voice, because the other voice — the one that had started saying *we* and meaning something larger — couldn't be trusted right now. "Because he wants the drawings intact. Fire is the one thing he can't buy back. Which means right now, that bundle is the only reason we're still breathing."

"Or it's the reason everyone else has stopped breathing."

Below them, the lead figure raised one arm — not a weapon, just an open hand, a greeting performed for an audience. A voice carried up the valley, pitched to carry: *"Yarrow! I'm coming up alone! We should talk before the light goes!"*

"He's lying," Sefa said.

"Obviously."

"But he'll do it. He'll come alone, because he believes he doesn't need the others yet, and that belief is the only advantage we have left." She worked the bolt on the rifle, checked the magazine, closed it. Her arm screamed where the strap bit the wound, and she let it scream silently. "Go tell Marcus what's happening. Then come back out here and decide what kind of man you're going to be in the next hour. Because I've seen both versions of you now, Hazel Yarrow, and I need to know which one is standing next to me when Voss reaches that fence."

He looked at her for a long moment. Something passed between them that neither of them named — the same thing that had been passing between them since the storm, wordless and load-bearing, like a beam under a roof.

"I'll be right back," he said, and she believed him, and hated how much easier that made everything.

* * *

### Hazel

Voss came up the track exactly as promised: alone, unhurried, gloved hands visible, a smile on his face that had all the warmth of a ledger. He stopped ten paces from the fence line, close enough to talk without shouting, far enough that Sefa's rifle would have to reach across real distance to touch him. He'd measured it. Of course he had.

"Mr. Yarrow." He tipped his head toward the hut. "Your father inside?"

"He is."

"I'd like to pay my respects."

"He'd rather you didn't."

"Yes," Voss said pleasantly, "I imagine he would." He shifted his weight, a man settling in for a negotiation he'd already won in his own mind. "Let me save us all some frostbite. Your father took something off my clients' property. Survey drawings, pre-colonial transit route, considerable value. I've been authorized to offer — and I want you to appreciate the number, Mr. Yarrow, because it's the last generous number anyone will say out loud in this valley — fifty thousand for the bundle. Cash. Tonight. Plus transport out for you, your father, and your guide, before the pass closes for good. Everyone lives. Everyone wins. The mountains keep their secrets, which frankly they're better at than we are."

Hazel stood very still. Behind him, at the edge of his vision, Sefa had not moved from the byre door, and the rifle had not moved either.

"My father says you killed his cook."

Something flickered across Voss's face — annoyance, quickly filed away. "Your father says a great many things. Dying men are unreliable narrators, Mr. Yarrow. I'd take everything he tells you with a grain of salt." He spread his hands. "Fifty thousand. Or I take the bundle and we find out what your guide's arm is worth at two hundred yards. I'd rather not. But winter doesn't care about my preferences either."

Here it was, then. The fulcrum. Hazel could feel it under his feet like a plank over a drop.

Every version of the next hour arranged itself in his head and fell apart. Take the money and run — and spend the rest of his life knowing the passage would be stripped, the graves forgotten, the cook's name unspoken. Refuse and fight — and watch Sefa bleed again, maybe worse, for paper. Burn it — his father's voice, *burn them* — and stand unarmed in front of armed men with nothing left but the truth in their mouths.

His mother's face surfaced, unbidden. Not the hospital face. The dig face — sunburned, laughing, kneeling in a trench with a brush in her hand, treating a two-thousand-year-old potsherd like it was a letter from a friend. *Things survive because someone bothers to write them down,* she used to say. *Everything else is just weather.*

"Mr. Yarrow," Voss said, and there was the first hairline crack in the pleasantness. "It's getting cold."

"You're right," Hazel said. "It is."

He turned and walked back toward the hut, and he heard Voss shift behind him, heard the faint creak of leather, heard Sefa's voice crack across the yard like a whip — *"That's far enough"* — and then he was through the door, into the smoke and the lamplight, kneeling at the cot.

"They're here. Four men. He offered fifty thousand."

Marcus's eyes opened. "And?"

"And I'm asking you one more time. Tell me why burning them is right and not just safe."

His father looked at him for a long moment. Then he lifted one hand — it shook, but it lifted — and pointed past Hazel, at the shelf, at the oilcloth bundle sitting in the firelight.

"Because I found the most important thing I will ever find," Marcus said, each word bought and paid for in breath, "and I hid it. I hid it from the world, I hid it from my partners, I hid it from you. And hiding it got Amara killed, got Okafor killed, got two boys at the wall killed, and it is going to kill me tonight in this hut, and none of it — none of it — protected anything." His hand fell. "Truth doesn't survive in the dark, Hazel. It dies there quietly, like everything else. Light it or lose it. Those are the only choices left, and I am begging you — I have never begged anyone — choose the fire."

Hazel took the bundle down from the shelf. It was lighter than it had any right to be. Twenty years of his father's life, folded into oilcloth.

He carried it to the hearth.

* * *

### Sefa

From the byre door she watched Voss watch the hut, and she read him the way she read weather — the stillness before the front comes through. His smile hadn't moved, but his weight had shifted forward onto his toes, and his right hand had drifted, casually, terribly, to the hem of his coat.

They're out of time, she thought. Whatever is happening in that hut, it ends in the next thirty seconds, one way or the other.

She put her sights on Voss's chest and began, silently, to count down the distance she'd let him close.

Inside, she knew, Hazel was standing at a hearth with his father's life's work in his hands. She knew what Marcus had told him to do — she'd heard enough through the walls these past two days. And she knew what it would cost: the proof, the leverage, the thing that had dragged them both across a mountain range through snow and blood. Burning it meant walking into the spring with nothing but words. Words against money. Words against guns.

But she had grown up in a valley where the old road through the hills had been quarried away for gravel before anyone thought to photograph it, and she had watched her grandmother try to describe it to children who would never believe it had existed. Some things, once the evidence is gone, are gone forever. And some things — she looked at the lit window of the hut, at the silhouette moving against it — some things live in people, if the people live, and if the people are brave enough to keep saying them out loud.

In the hut, the firelight flared.

Through the window she saw Hazel feed the oilcloth into the flames, saw the bundle catch, saw the light jump gold across his face — and saw, in that same instant, Voss's hand come out of his coat, and heard him shout something to the tree line that was not a word but a command—

Sefa fired.

The shot rolled up the valley like thunder finding its echo, and the world broke open all at once: muzzle flash in the trees, the dogs erupting, Hazel bursting from the hut with a burning brand still in his fist, and Voss, hit high in the shoulder, spinning down into the snow with his unfinished order dying in his throat.

"Sefa!" Hazel's voice, raw. "The treeline!"

She was already moving, reloading one-handed, her arm on fire, her heart strangely, appallingly clear. Three of them left in the trees. One hut. One dying man. One burning bundle whose ashes were drifting up the chimney into the dark.

Come on, then, she thought, settling behind the stone wall as the first bullets chewed into it. Come and see what the truth costs.

The night answered with gunfire.
