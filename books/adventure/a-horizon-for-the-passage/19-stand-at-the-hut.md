## 19. Stand at the Hut

I heard them before I saw them — not voices, but the absence of birdsong in the birches below the hut.

"Sefa." I said it low, and she was already moving, crouched at the window with her rifle cradled against her shoulder like something she'd been born holding. Outside, the light was going grey and blue, that hour when the mountains stop pretending to be kind. Snow had been falling since noon, soft and steady, erasing everything — which meant our tracks were gone, and so were theirs, and we'd know where they were only when it was too late.

"How many?" Marcus asked from the corner. He'd propped himself up against the stone wall with the oilcloth bundle across his knees, as if he could defend sixty years of silence with his hands. He looked worse than yesterday. The cough had gone wet.

"Four," Sefa said. "Maybe five. They'll split."

"You can't know that."

"I grew up here." She didn't look at him. "This valley funnels. There's one approach worth taking in snow, and they don't know this ground, so they'll take it anyway. Two will come up the creek bed because it's sheltered. One will circle wide through the trees thinking he's clever. And Voss—" she paused, adjusting her grip, "—Voss will walk straight up the path because he believes the world makes way for him."

"And you'll let him," I said.

"No," Sefa said. "I'll make the world stop making way."

* * *

We had spent the day preparing, and every preparation had been hers. She'd shown me how the slope below the hut broke into three terraces where the shepherd's family had once cut hay — a drop of six feet at each terrace edge, hidden under new snow, invisible unless you knew to look. She'd cut deadfall branches and laid them flat under the powder along the creek bed, a lattice that would hold a man's weight for exactly one step. She'd moved the woodpile to block the window on the north side and dragged the table against the door, then thought better of it and left the door loose.

"They'll expect it barred," she said. "A barred door is a fortress. A loose door is an invitation. Men die of invitations."

Marcus watched her work with an expression I couldn't read — something between grief and recognition. "You've done this before," he said.

"I've been hunted before. It's different." She drove a stake into the frozen ground by the woodshed and didn't elaborate, and I understood that the debt she'd fled, the life before the guiding job, had teeth in it I would never fully see. Maybe someday. Maybe not. Love, I was learning, did not require full disclosure. It required showing up with a rifle when the world came through the trees.

Now, in the failing light, we took our positions. Sefa at the south window with the long sightline down the path. Me at the east wall, above the creek bed, with the shotgun Marcus had kept wrapped in sacking under the floorboards — a shepherd's gun, old, but it threw a heavy pattern and I only needed it to work once. Marcus stayed inside with the oilcloth and a pistol he held like a man holding a pen: correctly, formally, without conviction.

"Hazel." His voice stopped me at the door. "If it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong, you take the drawings and you run east. There's a game trail behind the hut. It doesn't matter what happens to me. It never has to matter what happens to me. That's the whole point of what I—"

"Dad." The word came out strange, rusty, a tool I hadn't used in years. "Shut up and hold the door."

He almost smiled. In the grey light, with death already sitting patiently in the corner waiting its turn, he almost smiled.

* * *

They came the way she said they would.

First the man in the trees — I heard him before I saw him, a snap of frozen brush off to the northwest, too deliberate to be an animal. Sefa must have heard it too, because a moment later a single shot cracked out of the south window and the snapping stopped. Not killed, I'd learn later — winged, dropped into a drift, screaming until the cold shut him up. But at the time I believed she'd killed him, and the knowledge sat in my chest like swallowed ice.

Then the creek bed. I saw nothing for a long minute, just white on white, and I let myself believe she'd been wrong. Then the snow shivered. A boot punched through the deadfall lattice and a man went down hard, leg folding wrong, and his shout of pain turned into a curse turned into silence as he realized what it meant: *she knew this ground and we did not.* He started crawling back the way he'd come, dragging the ruined leg, and I raised the shotgun and found I couldn't fire at a crawling man's back.

Sefa could have. From her angle she had the shot. She didn't take it either. Later she said, "Dead men make martyrs. Cripples make confessions." I think there was more to it than tactics. I think we were both learning, in real time, exactly how much killing we could carry, and the answer was: less than survival required, more than conscience allowed.

That left Voss.

He came up the path alone, unhurried, hands visible, no weapon drawn, walking through falling snow like a man strolling to a business meeting. Which, in a sense, he was. He stopped twenty yards from the hut, well short of the terrace drops, and called out in a voice pitched to carry.

"Yarrow! I know you can hear me. I've got two men down and I've got all night, and your father hasn't got that. Send out the drawings and everyone walks away. Everyone. Even the guide. I give you my word."

"He gave my mother his word once," Marcus said quietly beside me. "In Lima. Said the permits were clean."

The words landed like a blow. I filed them away — *Lima, permits, later* — because there was no room for them now.

"Come see him!" I shouted back. "Come see what your word costs!"

"I don't need to see him. I need paper." Voss took a step forward, testing, and Sefa fired a warning shot into the snow at his feet. He didn't flinch. That frightened me more than a weapon would have. "You're a salvage man, Yarrow. You worked the Meridian claim. You know how this ends. Everything gets sold eventually. The only question is who signs."

And here is the thing I will carry to my own grave: for about four seconds, I considered it. Four seconds of arithmetic — my father dying anyway, Sefa bleeding if it went badly, a tube of oilcloth traded for three lives. Four seconds. Then I heard Marcus cough behind me, that wet rattle, and I understood that he had done this math for thirty years, alone, always choosing the quiet trade, always selling small pieces of the truth to buy safety for people he loved, and it had bought my mother a grave and bought him a canyon and bought me six years of silence. The arithmetic never balanced. It only ever moved the cost onto someone who wasn't in the room.

"No deal," I said.

"Pity." Voss stepped back into the trees, and the world went very quiet, and then Sefa said, "Down," and the night tore open.

* * *

They flanked us. Of course they flanked us — the wounded man in the creek bed had been the diversion, or the third man hadn't been as hurt as he'd sounded, and while we watched the path they came around through the rocks on the east side, ten feet from my window. The first shot took out most of the window glass and the second hit the wall a hand's width from my head, and then it was noise and powder smoke and Sefa returning fire and me rolling across the floor toward the door because the east wall was a losing position.

Through the broken window I saw one of them coming low and fast across the open ground, and I saw Sefa track him, and I saw her hesitate — that half-second of *can I* — and I fired past her, over her shoulder, and the man spun and went down into the snow and did not get up.

I want to tell you I felt something decisive. I felt my own heartbeat and the taste of gun oil and a certainty arriving cold and complete: *this is what it is now, this is the country we're in.* Sefa glanced at me — one look, half thanks, half grief for what I'd just become — and then we both heard Marcus.

He was outside.

The old man had gone out the back, through the game trail door, around the woodshed, and he was standing on the second terrace with the pistol raised and the oilcloth bundle tucked under his arm like a courier from another century, and he was firing at the treeline where Voss had gone, and he was shouting — actually shouting, this silent careful man — "It's here! Voss! It's here and you'll never read it!"

Drawing their fire. Drawing it away from the hut, away from us, putting his body between the bullets and the truth one last time because it was the only fluency he had left.

"No," Sefa breathed. "No, no—"

The shot came from the rocks. It caught Marcus high in the chest and turned him halfway around, and he sat down slowly in the snow, still holding the bundle, still upright, as if even dying he refused to let the drawings touch the ground unguarded.

Everything after that happened in pieces.

I went through the door without deciding to. Sefa covered me — I heard her rifle working, deliberate shots, driving the shooter back among the rocks. I crossed the open ground with snow flying up around me and slid the last six feet on the terrace and got my hands under my father's arms and hauled him back against the woodshed wall. His breath was coming in bubbles. Blood on his lips, bright against the white world.

"The rocks," he managed. "Left side. Behind the split boulder."

Sefa had already seen it. Her next shot sparked off stone and there was a cry, and then footsteps crashing downhill, fast, retreating — Voss's voice cutting through the trees, calling his men off, calling them off with the same terrible calm, because Voss did not spend men he could spend later. The night emptied by degrees. Gunsmoke drifted. Snow kept falling, indifferent, beginning its patient work of covering everything.

I knelt in the drift beside my father and pressed my hands against the wound and blood came up between my fingers, warm, shockingly warm in all that cold.

* * *

Inside, by lamplight, we did what could be done, which was nothing. The bullet had gone where bullets go to finish things. Sefa cleaned and bound it anyway, because refusing to stop is its own kind of prayer, and I sat holding his hand and watching his face, which had gone calm in the way of men who have finally run out of reasons to keep fighting.

But Marcus Yarrow had one fight left, and it wasn't with his body.

"The tin," he whispered. "Under the floor. Not the oilcloth. Under it."

I pried up the board. Inside, beneath the oilcloth bundle, lay a second package — a battered trading-post radio, the kind every post upriver used for weather and freight schedules, and folded around it, pages of handwriting.

"Open channel," Marcus said. Each word cost him. "Channel four. Everyone listens to channel four."

I stared at him, and then I understood, and understanding went through me like the bullet had gone through him.

Not burning. Not surrendering. *Broadcasting.*

"You can't sell it," Marcus breathed, "if everyone owns it. You can't bury it if every post from Kestrel Mouth to the coast has heard the coordinates. Voss's buyers want exclusive. Kill exclusivity and you kill the price. Kill the price—" a bubble of blood, a wet laugh, "—and you kill the war."

I thought of chapter after chapter of my own resentment — *he told strangers, he trusted guides, he never told me* — and here was the answer, the whole architecture of his silence, handed to me in a dying whisper: secrets kept people safe; publicity kept truths alive; and he had spent his life unable to do both at once. So he'd chosen secrecy, and it had cost my mother, and now, at the end, with nothing left to protect except the truth itself, he was choosing the other road and handing me the key.

"Read it," he said. "Read the coordinates. Read what's carved on the stair. Read the names of the buyers. All of it. Say my name when you say mine."

Sefa tuned the set. Static filled the hut, that oceanic hiss, and then she found the frequency and nodded at me.

I picked up the pages. My hands shook. I began to read.

*"Attention all stations on the Kestrel net. This is Hazel Yarrow, transmitting from the upper valley. I am reading from the survey journal of Marcus Yarrow, archaeologist. What follows are the coordinates of a pre-colonial passage through the range, discovered this summer, intact, unmapped..."*

Outside, the snow fell on the terraces and the deadfall and the men in the drifts, covering everything equally, keeping nothing. Somewhere downvalley, in some freezing camp, Voss was listening to his fortune dissolve into static and airwaves, becoming common property, becoming history, becoming the one thing he could never own.

My father listened to every word. Near the end, when I read the passage naming the buyers — the names in Lima, the names behind the permits — his eyes closed, and I couldn't tell if it was peace or pain, and I decided it could be both, because I was learning that from him.

When I finished, the hut was very quiet. Sefa's hand found my shoulder and stayed there.

Marcus opened his eyes. "Good," he said. Just that. *Good.* The last review of his career.

Then his gaze went past me to the dark window, to the east, where the storm was building over the col, and his brow creased, and he said, in a voice suddenly sharp with fear:

"Hazel. The fourth man. I never saw the fourth man come down."
