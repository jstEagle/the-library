## 20. Ashes and Snow

Marcus Yarrow died the way he had lived most of his last year: quietly, stubbornly, on his own schedule.

Hazel had kept vigil through the night in the shepherd's hut, one hand wrapped around his father's, the other feeding the stove just enough wood to hold back the cold. Sefa slept sitting up against the far wall, her bandaged shoulder rising and falling, rising and falling. Outside, the wind had finally spent itself somewhere around two in the morning, and after that there was only the enormous silence of the valley — a silence so complete it felt less like an absence of sound than like sound being held in reserve.

Marcus surfaced once, near three. His eyes found Hazel's with an effort that seemed to cost him something physical.

"You're still here," he said.

"Where would I go?"

Marcus's mouth moved toward a smile. It didn't quite arrive. "You always were a bad liar." His fingers tightened, a bird's grip, all tendon. "The drawings. You know what to do."

"I know what you want me to do," Hazel said. "That's not the same thing."

"No," Marcus agreed. "It never was, with us." He closed his eyes. "Good. Keep arguing with me. Keeps you honest."

He didn't wake again until dawn, and then only for four minutes. Hazel counted them without meaning to. In those four minutes Marcus said nothing at all — just looked at the window, where the first light was coming off the eastern peaks in a long rose-gold blade, and then looked at Hazel's face as if memorizing it, or perhaps checking his work. Then his hand went slack inside Hazel's, not all at once but by degrees, like a rope paying out through a cleat, and the room was very quiet, and the mountains stood up white against the morning, and Marcus Yarrow was done.

Hazel didn't move for a long time. He kept holding the hand because letting go felt like a decision, and he wasn't ready to make any decisions. The stove ticked. Snow slid off the roof in soft slabs. Somewhere down the slope, water ran under ice, patient as arithmetic.

Sefa woke without being woken. She crossed the room, knelt beside him, and put her good hand over both of theirs. She didn't say *I'm sorry*. She didn't say anything. That was the right call. Every condolence Hazel could imagine sounded like a coin dropped into a well with no bottom.

"He waited for dawn," she said finally.

"He'd have hated dying at night. No light to work by."

A breath of laughter escaped her, half sob. "Yes. He would have."

* * *

Voss was gone.

They confirmed it within the hour, moving carefully along the tree line with the rifle Hazel still couldn't look at without feeling sick. The tracks told the story plainly enough: six men had come up the valley the day before, and five men had gone back down it before first light, moving fast, leaving their dead behind them under a cairn of stones they hadn't bothered to build well. Voss himself had left nothing but boot prints and, wedged in the drift near the hut's woodpile, a single leather glove, split at the seam — a man dressing in the dark, in a hurry, in defeat.

But it wasn't quite defeat. That was the thing Hazel kept turning over. Because the night before, while Marcus lay dying, Hazel had sat at the trading post radio forty miles away — no. He corrected himself. He had sat at the *radio*, which Sefa had rigged to the post's frequency from the hut using wire and nerve, and he had read the statement aloud into the static. Names. Dates. The buyers in the capital. The bribed surveyor general. The passage itself, its location given to the nearest degree, its significance stated flatly, without poetry, because poetry could be dismissed and coordinates could not. Copies existed now in three places: the transcript at the trading post, the provincial records office that monitored the frequency, and the mind of every operator within range who understood what they'd heard.

Voss hadn't fled from them. Voss had fled from that. A man can kill witnesses; he cannot kill a broadcast. By now the name Aldous Voss was attached, in official ink, to three graves on the Kestrel and a salvage operation that had murdered its way up a river to steal a heritage out from under a country. There would be warrants. There would be questions at every crossing south. Voss's advantage had always been time, and Hazel had taken it from him with a microphone and ten minutes of shaking voice.

"He'll try for the drawings anyway," Sefa said, crouched over the tracks. "Before anyone reaches him. Proof burns."

"Then he'll come back up this valley."

She looked at him. "And we'll be here."

"Yes," Hazel said. "We'll be here."

It wasn't bravery. It was simpler than that. His father was going into the ground today, and whatever else happened, Marcus Yarrow was not going to spend eternity alone in a hole dug by strangers.

* * *

They buried him at noon beside the passage.

Getting there took the better part of the morning — Marcus had lost too much weight in the hut for Hazel to carry him far, and the carved stairway lay a mile up the canyon wall, and the snow was knee-deep and lying over scree that shifted like bad conscience. They wrapped him in the oilcloth.

That had been Hazel's decision, made at the stove with the tin open in front of him. Inside, beneath the journal and the sealed tube of drawings, the oilcloth had been doubled — an outer wrapping and an inner one, and between them, folded small, a photograph Hazel had never seen: his mother, thirty years old, squinting into sun at some dig site, laughing at whoever held the camera. Laughing, Hazel realized with a jolt, at Marcus. At young Marcus, who must have been behind the lens, saying something ridiculous. She was wearing the scarf Hazel remembered from the hall closet of his childhood, the green one, the one he'd assumed meant nothing.

His mother had known about the passage. Had maybe helped find it. And Marcus had wrapped the proof of it around the thing that got her killed, and carried it for years, and never said a word — because saying words was how people died, in his arithmetic. Hazel had raged about that in the night, silently, furiously, while holding his father's hand. He expected the rage to outlast the man.

It didn't. That surprised him most of all. The rage had needed Marcus alive to push against, and now there was nothing to push against, and what remained underneath was just grief, plain and unadorned, the kind that doesn't ask to be justified.

So: the oilcloth. Marcus went into the ground wrapped in the discovery of his life, with his wife's laugh folded against his chest.

The grave was hard work. The ground at the stairway's base was sheltered rock, and they broke through the frost crust with the pickaxe from the hut and then it was gravel and old flood silt, diggable, barely. Sefa worked one-armed and refused to stop. Twice Hazel told her to sit. Twice she looked at him with an expression that ended the conversation before it started. They took turns. The hole grew. The mountains watched.

When it was deep enough, Hazel lowered his father in on ropes, and for a moment Marcus lay there looking smaller than Hazel had ever seen him — smaller than the man who'd taught him to read stratigraphy at nine, smaller than the stranger who'd missed every birthday for six years, smaller than the dying man who'd gripped his hand and argued with him about honesty. Just a body. Just a father. Just a man who had made terrible choices for defensible reasons and defensible choices for terrible reasons, and who had loved his son in the only dialect he'd ever been fluent in, which was silence.

Hazel knelt at the edge.

"I'm not going to say I understand," he said. His voice came out rough. "Because I don't, fully. Maybe I will someday and maybe I won't. But I forgive you." The words felt enormous and strange in his mouth, like stones he'd been carrying in his pockets for years and was finally setting down. "Not because it was okay. It wasn't okay. But because I'm tired, Dad. And because Mom would've wanted you to hear it, and because—" His throat closed. He breathed through it. "Because somebody should get to rest easy in this valley, and it might as well start with you."

Sefa stood behind him with her head bowed. When he finished, she spoke the few words of her grandmother's language that she'd offered the cook's grave, weeks ago and a lifetime ago, the blessing for travelers who have arrived somewhere. Then they filled the grave together, and set the stones, and Hazel built the cairn the way Marcus had built the one at the fork — careful courses, fitted tight, a structure meant to outlast weather and men.

On the flat stone at the top, he carved with the pickaxe point:

MARCUS YARROW
ARCHAEOLOGIST
HE FOUND THE WAY THROUGH

No dates. Dates belonged to paperwork. This was for the mountain.

* * *

They stayed at the stairway while the light went long and gold.

Up close, freed of horror, the passage wall did what it had been doing for six hundred years: it held its silence and its beauty. The steps climbed the cliff face in a switchback of cut stone, half-buried, worn concave by feet that had stopped climbing centuries before any mapmaker drew this range. Above them, the opening into the rock — the way through — showed as a dark seam, drifted shut with snow. Below, the valley fell away in blue shadow. Whoever had built this had looked at an impassable canyon and disagreed.

"They'll seal part of it," Sefa said. "For protection. Once the survey teams come."

"Probably."

"Good." She leaned into him, careful of her shoulder, and he put his arm around her. "Some things shouldn't be a road. Some things should just be a place."

Hazel thought about that. About his father spending his last years keeping a road closed. About the difference between finding a thing and owning it, and how Marcus had finally learned the difference at the exact price of everything.

"He'd have liked you," Hazel said.

"He threatened to shoot me our second day out."

"He liked you."

She laughed — really laughed, the first time since the ambush, and the sound went up the canyon wall and came back changed, and Hazel thought: *this is what the valley does. It takes what you give it and returns it altered.* Grief, laughter, gunshots, confessions. Nothing came back the same. The wilderness forgave nothing — it kept every score, held every body, remembered every lie — and somehow that was why forgiveness meant something here. It wasn't cheap. It was the hardest stone in the range, and he had just laid it on his father's grave.

Snow began again around four, fine and dry, ticking softly on the stones. Not a storm. Just the mountain breathing.

"We should go down," Sefa said. "Storm's coming tomorrow. Real one."

"In a minute."

They stood another minute. Then another. Then Hazel touched the cairn once, flat-palmed, the way you'd touch a shoulder, and turned downhill.

* * *

At the hut, the radio crackled to life at dusk.

They both froze. Sefa reached it first, hand on the dial, and the voice that came through was thin and officious and impossibly welcome: the provincial operator, reading back the transcript of Hazel's broadcast for confirmation, and then adding, in the tone of a man delivering news he didn't entirely believe: *"—and be advised, party identifying as Voss surrendered this morning at Fort Meridian crossing. Two of his men in custody. Warrants issued. Repeat, Voss in custody. Also be advised, survey and recovery team dispatched upriver, estimated arrival your position eleven days. Confirm your status and numbers, over."*

Sefa looked at Hazel. Hazel looked at the stove, at the bunk where his father had died, at the window going dark over the pass they'd crossed half-dead.

Eleven days. For the first time since the radio went silent in September, someone was coming *up* the river instead of down it, and they were coming slowly, officially, with shovels and clipboards and the law behind them.

He took the handset. His hands were steady.

"This is Hazel Yarrow," he said. "Two survivors at the shepherd's hut, upper valley. We have graves to show you." He paused, and heard himself add, calm as a man reporting weather: "And we have a passage to walk you through."

Outside, the snow thickened against the glass, and the mountains stood quiet over the cairn, keeping what they keep, giving back what they choose.
