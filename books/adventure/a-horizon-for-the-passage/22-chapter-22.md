## 22. Chapter 22

The river in spring was a different animal than the one I'd hated all winter.

Sefa stood at the rail of the mail boat and watched the Kestrel run brown and loud with snowmelt, carrying whole trees downstream like it was clearing its throat after a long sleep. Six months ago she had come up this water with a man who paid her to guide strangers into country she loved and then left her standing on a bank with nothing but her gear and her anger. Now she was coming back down it with Hazel Yarrow asleep against her pack, his face slack, his hands still blistered from the crossing, and the whole valley behind them holding a secret that would be public knowledge by summer's end.

She should have felt lighter. She kept waiting for it — the lightness. They had made it. Marcus was buried where he'd wanted to be, above the passage, where the stone stair climbed out of the canyon wall like something the mountain had grown rather than something men had cut. Voss was gone, fled south ahead of the story, and the story was already moving faster than he could: the trading post radio had done its work, and by the time they'd descended into the valley there were surveyors from the heritage commission already camped at the fork, asking questions with notebooks open. Hazel had given three days of testimony. She had given two. The man they'd captured below the col — Anselm, the expedition's surviving member, the traitor with the familiar boots — had given six, and would give more when the trial came.

It was done. It was won, if you wanted to call it that.

So why did she keep looking back upriver?

She felt him wake before he moved — some small change in the weight of him against her shoulder, the way his breathing shifted. He didn't say anything for a while. That was one of the things she had learned about Hazel Yarrow in the white crossing and everything after: he didn't fill silence. He let it sit there like weather, and when he finally spoke it was because he had something true to put into it.

"The commission wants me back in the fall," he said. "Consulting. They want me to walk the whole passage with their people, document every chamber." A pause. "I told them I'd think about it."

"Will you?"

"I don't know." He sat up, rubbed his eyes, looked out at the water the way she'd seen him look at it a hundred times now — like it owed him an answer and he wasn't sure he wanted to hear it. "Every time I think about going back up there, I see the stairway. And then I see the graves. Both at once. I don't know how to separate them yet."

"You don't," Sefa said. "You just carry both."

He glanced at her. In the months since the storm, since the hut, since the dawn Marcus died with his son's hand around his fingers, she had watched Hazel learn to be looked at again. At first he'd flinched from it, turned away, done the thing where he made himself useful so no one would make him be present. Now he held still under her gaze, and something in her chest did the quiet painful thing it always did when he managed it.

"My mother used to say the mountains keep what you give them," he said. "I thought that was just something mothers said."

"What do you think now?"

"I think she was right, and I hate it." He almost smiled. "I gave those mountains my father. I gave them a version of myself I'm not sure I want back. And they kept all of it."

The boat rounded the bend below the old base camp — ransacked, abandoned, a ruin now that the commission had photographed and catalogued and left to the weather. Sefa watched it slide past on the bank, tents collapsed, the supply tarp shredded by ravens. It looked smaller than she remembered. Everything up here had looked enormous in winter: the cold, the danger, the distance between herself and any life she recognized. Now, in the green flood of spring, the whole country seemed almost gentle. Almost forgivable.

Almost.

* * *

They reached the trading post at dusk.

It was the same post where Voss's crew had smiled at them over coffee and asked the wrong questions, where Hazel had lied so badly that Sefa had stepped on his foot under the table to shut him up. The same woman ran it — Marta, broad-shouldered, sharp-eyed, the kind of person who missed nothing and commented on less. She looked at the two of them coming through the door, mud to the knees, packs worn gray, and set down what she was doing.

"You're the ones from the radio," she said. Not a question. Everyone within two hundred miles knew them as the ones from the radio now; Hazel's voice crackling across the frequencies, naming names, reading out coordinates, saying *the Kestrel passage is real and there are men who will kill for it and I am telling everyone at once so no one can bury it.* It had been, Sefa thought, the bravest stupid thing she'd ever heard, and it had worked, and she had kissed him afterward in the snow with the transmitter still warm.

"We're the ones," Hazel said.

Marta studied him a moment longer, then nodded toward the kitchen. "There's stew. There's beds. There's a letter came for you three weeks ago, been sitting in my drawer because I didn't know if you were alive and I don't forward mail to dead men."

Hazel went very still. "From who?"

"Postmark's from the coast." She fetched it, held it out. "Yarrow. Same name as yours."

Sefa watched him take it the way you take something that might bite. He turned it over twice. His mother's handwriting — or what remained of her hand, the letters she'd written years ago that relatives had found and forwarded and re-forwarded until they landed here, at the end of the world, addressed to a son who hadn't known they existed.

He didn't open it at the counter. He carried it to the corner table like it was made of ice, and Sefa sat across from him and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't his to find first.

He opened it with his thumbnail, careful, and read, and Sefa watched his face do several things in sequence — confusion, recognition, and then something raw and unguarded that he would once have hidden and now, slowly, was learning not to.

"It's from before," he said quietly. "Before the expedition. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "She wrote it the year she got sick. She asked a friend to hold it and send it if Dad ever went back into the field without me. Like she knew. Like she knew exactly how it would go — that he'd shut me out, that I'd stop trying, that someday I'd come up this river hating him and find out too late why."

"What does it say?"

He slid it across the table. His hands weren't steady.

Sefa read it. It was short. Mothers who know they're dying write short letters; there isn't time for anything else. *If you're reading this, your father has done the thing he does, and you are angry, and you have every right to be. But listen to me, Hazel. Your father loves the way stone loves — slow, deep, and impossible to move. He will never tell you he's protecting you because protecting you means admitting the danger, and admitting the danger means admitting what happened to me. So he'll be silent, and you'll be hurt, and you'll both be wrong about each other for years. Don't wait for him to say it first. You're the brave one. You always were.*

Sefa set the letter down. Across the table, Hazel had his jaw tight and his eyes bright and was staring at the middle distance like it contained his mother, his father, and six wasted years, all at once.

"She knew," he said. "She knew everything. About the buyers, about the earlier dig, about why he went silent. She knew and she never told me either."

"Maybe that was her way of protecting you too."

"Everyone protected me." His laugh came out cracked. "That's the joke, isn't it? I spent six years thinking nobody gave a damn, and the whole time I was the thing everybody was guarding."

Sefa reached across and took his hand. It was calloused and scarred now, the rope burn from the crossing still healing along the palm, and she held it the way she'd held it in the storm — firmly, without ceremony, because ceremony would have broken him.

"So what do you do with it?" she asked. "Being the guarded thing?"

He was quiet a long moment. Outside, the last light was going off the ridgeline, and somewhere down the valley a loon called across the water, that wild rising sound that always made her homesick for places she'd never lived.

"I think," he said slowly, "you stop being it. I think you become the person who does the guarding instead. Or—" he turned his hand over and laced his fingers through hers, "—the person somebody else guards. I haven't figured out which one I am yet."

"Neither," Sefa said. "You're the one who tells the truth on the radio when it would be easier to burn the drawings. That's a third thing. We'll invent a word for it eventually."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he'd started doing, unflinching — and she saw the question arrive in him before he spoke it, the way questions arrived in Hazel: slowly, heavily, like freight coming up the river.

"Come with me," he said. "In the fall. When they walk the passage. Come as the guide. Nobody knows that country like you, and the commission will need someone, and I—" He stopped. Breathed. Started over, plainer. "I don't want to walk back into that canyon without you. I don't want to walk anywhere without you. I've been trying to find a way to say it that sounds less terrifying and there isn't one, so there it is."

Sefa had imagined this moment, in the way you imagine things you refuse to admit you're imagining — on watch, in the dark, during the long gray hours of the descent. In her imagination she had answers ready, clever ones, deflecting ones, the kind you keep loaded for exactly this.

None of them came. What came instead was the memory of the stairway half-buried in the canyon wall, the wonder of it, and the bodies at its base; the memory of Hazel binding her wound below the col with shaking hands; the memory of Marcus's last morning, when he'd gripped her wrist and said, *take care of him, he doesn't know how to ask.* She had promised a dying man. She had also, somewhere between the pass and the valley, stopped doing it out of obligation and started doing it out of something that had no name she trusted yet.

"Yes," she said.

Just that. No armor on it.

Hazel exhaled like a man surfacing, and his grip tightened on her hand, and for a while neither of them said anything, because the truest things had been said and the rest was just weather.

* * *

They stayed three days at the trading post, and on the second day Hazel wrote two letters.

The first was to the commission, accepting the fall work, naming Sefa as lead guide. The second took him most of an evening, and he wouldn't let her read it, and when he sealed it she saw the address was a coastal town she'd never heard of — his mother's sister, the aunt he'd mentioned once, drunk on Marta's plum brandy, and never mentioned again.

"What are you going to say to her?"

"That I'm sorry I disappeared. That I understand now what she probably tried to tell me and I wouldn't hear." He weighed the envelope in his hand. "That I found out what my parents were protecting me from, and it nearly killed me, and I'd do it again anyway, because not knowing was worse."

"And if she writes back?"

"Then I'll have family who writes back." He said it lightly, but Sefa heard the weight underneath, the same weight that had been in his voice in the hut when Marcus told him everything — the buyers, the bribed officials, the earlier dig, the accident that had not been an accident, the reason a man spends six years being stone. "That's more than I had in the spring."

On the third morning they walked down to the dock together, and the river was high and fast and brown, and the mail boat was loading, and the pilot — a young man who had heard the radio broadcasts like scripture — shook Hazel's hand with both of his and said, "My uncle worked a dig with your father, years back. Said he was the straightest man on the river." Hazel thanked him and Sefa saw him carry the words aboard like cargo, something to be stored carefully and opened later.

The boat pulled away from the dock and the trading post shrank behind them, and then the bend took it, and then there was only the river and the ranges on either side, green to the treeline, white above, the passage hidden somewhere in the high country like a sentence half-erased.

Sefa stood at the stern. Hazel came and stood beside her, and their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away.

"Can I ask you something?" he said. "And you can tell me it's none of my business."

"That's usually how your questions start."

"When we go back in the fall — when I stand in that passage with their surveyors and their instruments — what do I do about him? My father. Do I talk about him like a hero? Like a man who hid things? Both?"

Sefa watched the water for a while. A log turned slowly in the current, riding the melt down from the high country, going wherever the river decided.

"You tell it the way it happened," she said. "That's what you taught all of us on the radio. The truth doesn't need him to be clean. It just needs him to be true. He found something magnificent and he was afraid and he loved you badly and he died buying you ten seconds with a rifle. All of it. All at once. Same as the graves and the stairway."

"Both at once," Hazel said. "You just carry both."

"Now you're learning."

He laughed — really laughed, the first full one she'd heard from him since the valley, since before, maybe since ever — and put his arm around her, and they stood like that while the boat ran the brown water down toward the lowlands, toward the coast, toward the fall and the passage and whatever came after.

The mountains fell away behind them by degrees, patient as stone. Somewhere up in that white silence a stairway waited in the rock, older than any nation, and in September they would climb it together, and Sefa found — testing the thought the way you test new ice — that she was not afraid of it. Not of the country, not of the past, not even of the future, which had always frightened her more than any storm.

Because the future, for the first time in her life, was not a place she was fleeing to.

It was a horizon. And they were walking toward it side by side, and the river, which keeps everything, carried them down to meet it.
