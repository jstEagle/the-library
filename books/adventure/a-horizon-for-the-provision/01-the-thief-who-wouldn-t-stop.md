## 1. The Thief Who Wouldn't Stop

The canyon had a name on no map Sebastian trusted, and by the time he reached its throat the sun had gone the color of an old bruise. Red stone rose on either side of him in walls close enough to touch with both hands spread wide, and the light fell into the slot of it like water poured down a well — thin, late, and going fast.

He had been chasing Wren for six months, eleven days, and however many hours he refused to count anymore because counting them made the arithmetic obscene. Six months of her boot prints in alkali mud, of half-eaten meals abandoned at waystations when she caught wind of him, of the particular insult of finding her campfires cold but still warm enough to hold his palm against. She was good. That was the worst of it. She was better than good, and some traitorous part of him had come to admire the craft of her running the way a man admires a storm from under proper shelter.

Today, for the first time, the storm had run out of country.

He heard her before he saw her — the scrape of a boot sole on sandstone, high up where the canyon narrowed to a chimney of rock. He climbed. His hands knew the holds without being asked; six months will do that, teach a man another person's habits so thoroughly that he begins to anticipate them like his own heartbeat. Wren favored the left wall in ascending corners. Wren rested her weight on her heels. Wren, at this moment, was twenty feet above him and moving like water finding its level, and she did not yet know he was below.

He let her get to the ledge. Let her pause there, silhouetted against the last copper strip of sky, one hand braced on stone worn smooth by a century of flash floods. Then he pulled himself over the lip of the climb and said, "End of the road, Wren."

She didn't startle. That was the second-worst part. She turned her head just far enough to show him the edge of a grin, sharp as a split fig.

"Ranger Thornbury." Her voice carried the dry drawl of someone who'd never once been impressed by authority. "You look terrible."

"You look cornered."

"Mm." She glanced past him, down the canyon, then up at the darkening sky with an expression he couldn't read — not fear, exactly. Calculation. "You want to know something funny? I've been waiting for you to catch me for about two months now. Kept leaving signs a deaf mule could follow. You're slower than your reputation."

"I'm thorough," he said. "There's a difference."

"There is." She turned fully now, and he saw she had her back to the canyon wall and nothing behind her but a forty-foot drop into shadow. "Thorough gets you here. Slow gets you here *late*." Something in her tone shifted, dropped its playfulness like a coat shrugged off. "Sebastian. Listen to me. We need to get out of this canyon. Tonight."

"You don't give orders. I do the giving. You do the coming along quietly."

"Look at the sky."

He looked. He wished he hadn't. Over the western rim, above the sun's grave, the clouds had gone a strange green-black, stacked and boiling, the kind of sky the old desert hands called *a loaded gun*. The air had gone heavy and sweet, tasting of ozone and wet stone, and somewhere beyond the canyon walls thunder cleared its throat.

"It hasn't rained in this district in fourteen months," Sebastian said.

"So it's owed." Wren's eyes were very steady. "Flash flood in a slot canyon doesn't send a warning letter. It sends a wall of water moving at the speed of a falling horse. I've seen it take a pack train and leave nothing but boots." She held out one hand, palm open, an absurd gesture of truce after half a year of theft and pursuit. "Come on. High ground. Arrest me on top of the mesa if you have to. I'll even let you."

Every instinct he owned told him it was a trick. Every instinct he owned had also kept him alive through nine years of ranger service, and those instincts looked at the green-black sky and screamed.

He took her hand.

It was the right decision and it saved his life, and it changed nothing, because the water came anyway.

* * *

It came as a sound first — a low, grinding roar from up-canyon, like the earth itself clearing its throat of everything it had swallowed for fourteen months. Then the light died all at once, the green-black sky closing over the slot like a lid, and then the flood arrived: not water so much as a moving landmass, brown and violent and full of trees, boulders, cattle bones, the whole loose inventory of the high desert torn loose and hurled downward through the throat of the canyon.

They were climbing. They were both climbing, fast, toward a shelf Wren had shouted about, and the water hit the walls below them with a concussion he felt in his teeth. Spray went up like smoke. The roar filled the world, erased thought, erased the last six months, erased everything except the animal imperative of *up*.

He reached the shelf. He hauled himself onto it, gasping, and turned to pull Wren up beside him —

— and the flood, in its indifferent genius, chose that moment to give him what he'd spent half a year wanting.

The water had found something. Down in the black churn, where the canyon bottom had scoured itself clean, the flood had torn open a seam in the rock floor — and out of that seam, tumbling, rolling, snagged briefly on a boulder, came a chest. Iron-banded, lead-sealed, small enough for one strong person to carry and no more. It spun in the torrent and slammed against the shelf's edge, wedged there, and by the lightning's stuttering glare Sebastian saw what was stamped into its lid.

A crest. A sun split by a sword, crowned in laurel.

Karavel.

His breath stopped. Everyone in the borderlands knew the shape of that seal, the way you know the shape of a scar on your own family. Karavel had been a nation once, a coastal one, rich and proud and fat on trade — until twenty years ago, when it had fallen in a single season to conquerors whose names were now printed on every map where Karavel used to be. Erased. Not defeated — *erased*, its cities renamed, its records burned, its people scattered like seed grain on wind. There were Karaveli refugees in every river town on the coast, and they still, quietly, in their own houses, drew that crest on their doorposts.

And here it was, dredged up out of the desert's memory by a wall of dirty water.

"Wren!" he shouted over the roar. She was ten feet away on a lower spur of rock, soaked, hair plastered flat, watching the chest with an expression he would think about for the rest of his life. Not surprise. *Recognition.*

"Don't—" he started.

She dove.

Later, he could never explain it — not to himself, not to the inquiry he imagined some clerk someday conducting. She went off that spur into the flood like it was a swimming hole in July, cut across the current with three strokes that should have killed her, got a hand on the chest's trailing band, and rode the water down-canyon into the dark.

He stood on the shelf with the rain hammering him and the lightning showing him the whole impossible scene in strobing frames: the flood, the thief, the dead nation's seal flashing white in the water. And he understood, with a clarity that felt like a second drowning, that the chase had just changed shape entirely. This wasn't about stolen ledgers anymore. Whatever was in that chest, the desert had guarded it for twenty years, and Wren had known it might exist, and she had gone into a killing flood for it without a moment's hesitation.

He went in after her.

* * *

The flood carried them both down out of the highlands and dumped them, half-dead, into the broad gravel washes of the lowlands, where the water spread thin and lost its temper. He lost her twice in the dark and found her twice by lightning. The third time he found her, dawn was coming up gray behind the mountains, and she was sitting on a gravel bar with the chest between her knees, wringing out her shirt, watching him approach with the weary courtesy of someone greeting a dog that keeps following the wagon.

"You could have drowned," he said.

"You could have stayed on the shelf." She patted the chest's lid. Water streamed off the lead seal. "Do you know what this is?"

"I know whose mark is on it."

"Then you know it isn't mine to sell and it isn't yours to confiscate." She stood, swaying with exhaustion, and squared up to him in the gray light, and for the first time in six months she looked him full in the face instead of over her shoulder. "So here's my question, ranger. And think hard before you answer, because I'm only asking once. Are you still chasing me? Or are you chasing this?"

He opened his mouth to say *you*, to say *the warrant*, to say the words that had fed him across four hundred miles of salt and stone —

— and found, to his genuine horror, that nothing came out. Because standing there in the dawn with his lungs full of river and his badge feeling like a stone sewn into his coat, he realized he could not remember why catching Wren had ever mattered. Not truly. Not the why underneath the habit. Somewhere in six months of pursuit the reason had worn through like a boot sole, and he'd kept walking on the bare nail of it because stopping would mean admitting the walk had become the whole point.

Wren read his silence the way she read lock mechanisms — quickly, expertly, and without mercy.

"That's what I thought," she said softly. She hoisted the chest onto her shoulder. "Monsoon season's starting. Nobody crosses the flats for two months. There's a ferrywoman downstream, on the river — Odessa. Leaky boat, worse coffee, takes labor for deck space." She turned and began walking along the wash, then paused, not looking back. "You can arrest me when the skies clear. Until then, you're welcome to pump bilge."

Sebastian stood alone on the gravel bar, dripping, empty-handed, and watched her go. The horizon ahead of her was the color of pewter, and the first fat drops of the monsoon were already printing dark circles in the sand around his boots.

After a long moment, he followed.
