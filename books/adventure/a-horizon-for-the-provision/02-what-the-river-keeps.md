## 2. What the River Keeps

The river announced itself before Sebastian saw it — a green smell riding the dry wind, the distant complaint of frogs, and then the water itself, wide and brown and slow as spilled honey. After four months of salt flats and canyon stone, it looked like a lie.

He came down off the last ridge at dusk with his boots full of sand and Wren's trail finally, blessedly cold. She'd gone to ground somewhere in the river settlements; he could feel it. Thieves always did when the monsoon clouds stacked up black over the mountains. Even the restless stopped running when the sky turned to a wall.

The ferry sat half-sunk at the settlement's edge, listing hard to port, its hull crusted white where the waterline had climbed and fallen across twenty years. A woman stood on its tilted deck coiling rope with the economy of someone who had done nothing else for two decades. Gray shot through her black braid. Her forearms were bare and scarred like old driftwood.

"You're the second one today," she said, without looking up.

Sebastian's hand drifted toward his knife out of habit. "The first one being?"

"A thief. Small, quick, lies about her name." The woman — Odessa, the paint on the hull had once read, though only *-essa* survived the weather — finished her coil and set it down. "You want her?"

"I'm a ranger."

"Rangers don't chase thieves for four months." She looked at him then, and her eyes were the flat gray of the river before a storm. "I've watched men chase things they couldn't name. You've got that look. Deck space costs labor. Bilge pump, patching, hauling. I don't care what you're running from. The boat doesn't care either."

He should have walked on. There were other landings upstream, other shelters. Instead he heard himself say, "I can work."

* * *

Wren knew the ranger was aboard before she saw him. She felt it the way she felt a lock's tumblers — a wrongness in the arrangement of things. She'd taken the forward hold under the tarp, claiming it by arriving first, and now she lay in the dark listening to him pump bilge forty feet aft, the rhythmic groan of the handle carrying through the hull like a heartbeat.

Four months he'd chased her. Four months, and she still didn't know why. She'd stolen from him exactly once — a satchel, in a border town whose name she'd already forgotten — and whatever was inside hadn't been worth this. Nobody chased like that over a satchel.

She'd opened the chest twice since the flood gave it to her. Both times she'd stopped at the wax seals on the inner packets, because some part of her — the part that had grown up in Karavel's refugee camps, singing songs about a country that existed only in past tense — understood that some doors don't close again.

Now the door was going to open anyway. She could hear it coming: the ranger's boots on the deck, Odessa's voice, low and transactional, and then silence, and then footsteps approaching the tarp.

"I know you're there," Sebastian said.

"Congratulations." She pushed the tarp back. In the lamplight spilling from the cabin he looked worse than she remembered — thinner, sun-scoured, the badge-shaped absence on his chest more visible than any badge had been. "Come to arrest me? You'll need a badge for that. Or a reason."

Something moved behind his eyes. "I need to know what's in the chest."

"So do I."

They regarded each other across the flooded hold, thief and ranger, and Wren thought: *he doesn't know either.* Whatever had driven him across the salt flats had burned down to embers somewhere in those canyons, and he was still walking because stopping meant admitting the fire was out.

"The chest," she said slowly. "It's Karaveli work. The seal's real. And if it's what I think it is—"

"It belongs to Karavelis."

She blinked. Of all the sentences she'd imagined from him, that wasn't among them.

"My grandmother," Sebastian said, "came from the coast. Before the annexation." He said it like a man confessing to a crime. "I haven't thought about it in years. But when I saw that crest in the canyon—" He stopped. Started again. "Open it with me. If we're going to decide anything, we decide it together."

* * *

Odessa fed them salt fish and river rice on the tilted deck, and pretended not to listen while pretending very hard to listen.

The chest sat between them, rain beginning to speckle the planks around it. Wren cut the first seal with a knife she'd cleaned twice, an odd formality none of them remarked on. Inside: oilcloth packets, dozens of them, each stamped with the same dead crest, each holding documents in a hand so fine it might have been written by a jeweler.

Ledgers. Correspondence. A manifest of payments, dated across three years leading up to the annexation, signed in a flowing confident script: **Aldous Veyran**. Beside the signatures, itemized sums. Beside the sums, in another hand, annotations — garrison strengths, patrol schedules, the locations of Karavel's granaries and armories. Everything a conqueror needed to know, sold by the man entrusted to keep it.

"There were rumors," Wren whispered. "In the camps. That Karavel didn't fall. That it was handed over." She touched the page as though it might burn. "Veyran's family still rules the coast. They built their houses on our harbor."

Sebastian read in silence, turning pages with a ranger's care for evidence. "This isn't rumor. This is proof. Names, dates, amounts." He looked up, and the lamplight caught something raw in his face. "Twenty years the survivors have been called liars. Traitors' kin. And the truth's been sitting under a canyon the whole time."

"And now every hired blade between here and the delta will want it," Odessa said quietly. She refilled their bowls without being asked. "Men who sell countries don't let their grandfathers' letters surface. Eat. Then pump the bilge. Truth keeps better in a dry boat."

* * *

The monsoon arrived three days later and did not leave.

Rain became the world's whole architecture. Sebastian learned the bilge pump until his shoulders screamed and his dreams filled with the sound of water finding ways in. Wren patched seams with tar and canvas, her quick fingers — made for picking locks — suddenly good for something else. They worked in shifts, passed each other in the narrow companionway, and somewhere in the passing, the hostility wore thin as the hull's old paint.

He learned she hummed when she worked, tuneless, a habit from a camp where singing aloud was punished. She learned he ate slow, savoring, because his grandmother had taught him hunger was a thing you respected even after it left. Neither of them mentioned the chest for six days. It sat sealed in the dry locker, and both of them found themselves checking on it anyway, the way you check on a sleeping animal that might yet bite.

On the seventh night the rain eased to a whisper and Odessa cooked actual spices into the fish, and the three of them sat on deck under a sky wrung out and dripping.

"Why'd you really chase me?" Wren asked. "All those months. It wasn't the satchel."

Sebastian turned his bowl in his hands. "I got my badge revoked for refusing to clear a village for a mining concern. Since then I've been—" He searched for the word. "Moving. Like if I kept moving I was still a ranger somewhere. Still enforcing something." He glanced at her. "Then I saw the crest in that canyon and it was the first time in months I wanted to catch something for a reason."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know what catching means anymore."

Wren laughed — short, surprised out of her. "Welcome to my entire life."

Odessa said nothing, but she smiled at the river, and the river said nothing back, keeping what it keeps.

* * *

Two mornings later, Wren woke to the smell of woodsmoke that wasn't theirs.

She went up the ladder quiet as her namesake and saw them across the brown water: three men at the landing, asking questions of the fisherfolk, showing something around — paper, or coin. One of them wore a coat too fine for the settlement, river mud to his knees, and the way he held himself said *paid to be patient.*

She dropped back down into the hold and found Sebastian already awake, already listening.

"They're here," she breathed. "For the chest."

Sebastian reached for the locker. Wren caught his wrist.

"Not yet. Odessa first. We go downstream, through the rapids, before they cross." Her eyes were bright and terrified and certain. "Because I know who these papers belong to. And I owe them."

Above them, thunder rolled down the valley, and the river began to rise.
