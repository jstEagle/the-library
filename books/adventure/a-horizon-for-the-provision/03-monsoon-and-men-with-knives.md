## 3. Monsoon, and Men With Knives

Odessa had lived on the river long enough to read its moods the way other people read faces, and on the morning the knives came, the river was sulking — low gray light, a wind out of the west that smelled like iron and drowned things. She stood at the rail of the *Provision*, her father's ferry, her father's ruin, and watched three men walk down the mud track into the settlement with the particular gait of men who were paid to be patient.

They weren't river folk. River folk walked like they were apologizing to the ground. These three walked like the ground owed them money.

The tallest one wore his blade openly, a long curved thing slung across his back, and he stopped at the first fish-drying rack and asked the old woman tending it a question. Odessa couldn't hear the words over the wind, but she saw the woman's shoulders climb toward her ears, saw her shake her head too many times. Questions about strangers, then. Questions about a chest.

Odessa turned and went below without hurrying, because hurrying told watchers what you feared.

* * *

Below decks, the *Provision* smelled of bilge and lamp oil and the two strangers who'd been living in her hold for eleven days now. Sebastian Thornbury sat cross-legged on an overturned crate, cleaning a rifle that hadn't fired since before Odessa bought it off a drowning man, and Wren lay flat on her back atop the sealed chest itself, arms behind her head, staring at the ceiling beams as if they might spell something out.

"They're here," Odessa said.

Sebastian's hands didn't stop moving. "How many?"

"Three. Walking like tax collectors."

Wren sat up in one motion, cat-quick even half-asleep. "For the chest?"

"For whatever pays them." Odessa braced a hand against the doorframe as the boat rocked. "Nobody walks into a monsoon settlement for anything else."

Sebastian set the rifle down. In the lamplight his face looked older than it had when he'd arrived, hollowed out around the eyes, like a man who'd been chasing something so long the chase had eaten the reason. "Options," he said.

"Run downstream," Odessa said. "Through the narrows."

Wren laughed — a short, disbelieving sound. "Through the narrows? In *this*?" She slapped the hull beside her, and the whole boat groaned in answer, a long wet complaint from seams that had been weeping since before Odessa owned her. "She'll come apart."

"She'll come apart slower than you'll die if those men find you," Odessa said. "I've run the narrows in worse. Once."

"Once," Wren repeated.

"I'm still here."

Sebastian looked between them, then at the chest, then at the small round window where rain had begun to stutter against the glass. "Then we go now," he said, "before the water gets high enough to make it suicide instead of just stupid."

"That's not comforting," Wren said.

"It wasn't meant to be."

* * *

They cast off in the worst hour of the morning, when the rain came down in ropes and the settlement disappeared behind them in thirty yards of gray. Odessa took the wheel herself; no one else knew how the *Provision* argued with current, how she wanted to swing her stern into every wave like a mule balking at a fence. The boat fought her and she fought back, and behind her she heard the sounds of two people who had spent months trying to catch each other now working in wordless rhythm — Sebastian hauling on the bilge pump, Wren bailing with a bucket where the pump couldn't reach, both of them soaked through, shouting measurements of water depth over the storm.

The narrows announced themselves as a change in the river's voice. It went from a growl to a roar, and the banks closed in, black rock slicked green, and the water stood up in places where water had no business standing.

"Hold something!" Odessa screamed, and then the river took the wheel out of her hands for a while.

She remembered it afterward in pieces: the bow lifting until the masthead seemed to touch the clouds. The crack of something — a rail, a plank, maybe part of her heart — somewhere aft. Wren sliding across the deck and Sebastian catching her wrist at the last second, the two of them slamming into the cabin wall together. Water coming over the gunwales in cold fists. And through all of it, the chest, lashed to the mainmast by Odessa's own rope, holding fast, holding its dead nation's secret against the liveliest river in the territory.

Then the narrows spat them out, and the river went back to merely being angry, and the *Provision* floated — lower in the water, listing to port, but floating.

Odessa realized she was laughing. She couldn't remember starting.

"You're insane," Wren said. She was draped against the mast, gasping, hair plastered to her skull, grinning like a lunatic.

"Both of you are," Sebastian said, but he was leaning on the pump handle, breathing hard, and there was something in his face Odessa had never seen in the weeks of watching him — something loose. Almost young.

* * *

That night they anchored in the lee of a bluff, and the rain gentled to a steady whisper, and the three of them sat in the cabin around a single lamp eating smoked fish and hard bread while the pump ticked away in the corner like a patient clock.

It was Wren who broke first. Odessa had learned that about her — the thief talked when she was tired, and the flood had wrung her out.

"I knew Karavel," Wren said, to the lamp more than to either of them. "Before it fell. I was a child. My mother sold cloth in the harbor market." She turned the fish bones over in her fingers. "After, when I was older — I went back. To the survivors. The enclave upriver, the ones who kept what was left. They took me in when I had nothing." Her jaw tightened. "And I stole from them. A reliquary. Small. Silver. I told myself it didn't matter, that they had nothing anyway, that silver was silver. I sold it in Vessik and ate well for a month."

Sebastian said nothing. Odessa said nothing. The river said everything, knocking soft against the hull.

"The chest," Wren went on, "I stole it from a dealer in Vessik who'd bought it off a grave robber. But when I opened it — when we opened it —" she looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet — "the names in it. The men who sold Karavel. I know those names. Their grandsons govern the coast now. And the survivors, the people I robbed — they've spent twenty years being told their country deserved to die. This chest says otherwise. So yes. I owe them. Not the courts. Them."

The lamp hissed. Odessa found she was holding her breath.

Sebastian set down his bread. When he spoke, his voice was flat in the way voices go flat when they're carrying weight.

"My badge was revoked," he said. "Two years ago. There was a settlement — Ashford Crossing. Squatters, mostly. The magistrate wanted them cleared for a rail line, and when they wouldn't go, he ordered us to burn them out in winter." He stared at the wall. "I refused. Stood in front of the rangers who would do it and refused. They took my badge on the spot. Called it insubordination." A pause. "The crossing burned anyway. Eleven dead."

Wren reached across the space between them — not touching him, just laying her hand palm-up on the crate, an offer, or maybe a question.

Sebastian looked at it for a long moment. Then he put his hand over hers, briefly, like a man testing ice.

"So," Odessa said, because someone had to say it, "we're a disgraced ranger, a thief with a debt, and a ferrywoman with a sinking boat."

"And a chest full of truth nobody wants found," Wren said.

"Then we'd better not get caught," Odessa said, and raised her tin cup of river water in a toast, and to her surprise, they raised theirs.

* * *

Near dawn, Odessa woke to silence — the wrong kind. No rain. She climbed to the deck and found the sky torn open and dry gold light spilling down the river valley, the first clean morning in forty days. The dry season, opening like a door.

And on the south bank, half a mile back the way they'd come, three figures stood at the waterline. Waiting. One of them raised an arm, and the light caught a long curved blade.

Behind them, far off but closing, came the sound Odessa dreaded most: engines. Boats. More than one.

She put her hand on the wheel and turned the *Provision*'s bow toward the delta, toward Karavel's scattered children, and rang the bell below to wake the others.
