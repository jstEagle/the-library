## 4. A Horizon for the Provision

The dry season came the way a verdict comes — not all at once, but with an inevitability you could feel in your bones before you heard it spoken. For three days the rain had been thinning, the river dropping, and Odessa had spent those days with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder, bailing and patching and muttering to her boat as though it were a sick animal she refused to let die. By the fourth morning the sky over the delta had gone hard and blue, and Sebastian could see the salt haze rising off the flats where the water gave up its argument with the land.

They came down the last channel with the current doing half their work and the bilge pump doing the rest. Wren stood at the bow, one hand on the fraying line, watching the delta open before them like a wound — braided channels, mudflats smoking in the sun, and far out, where the river finally surrendered, the gray line of the coast. Somewhere along that coast sat the courts that would take the chest and lose it. Somewhere past them, in the drowned hills north of the estuary, sat the enclave.

"You're sure about this," Wren said. It wasn't a question. It hadn't been a question since the night on Odessa's deck when she'd told him about the family in Karavel whose grain she'd stolen, whose trust she'd spent like counterfeit coin, and how she'd been running from that debt ever since — faster even than she'd run from him.

"No," Sebastian said honestly. "But I'm done being sure of things that don't matter."

Odessa snorted from the stern without looking up from her tiller. "The ranger's learned philosophy. Next he'll learn to bail."

* * *

The pursuers found them at the delta's throat, where the channels narrowed between walls of grey clay and the boat had no room to maneuver. There were six of them, hired blades in dust-colored leathers, spread across the mudflats on both banks, and their leader was a broad woman with a scarred jaw who had been following them since the rapids and knew patience better than they did.

"Thornbury!" her voice carried flat across the water. "The chest. That's all. Walk away and nobody writes your name down."

Sebastian brought the boat alongside the clay bank and stepped out into shin-deep mud, and Wren was beside him before he could tell her not to be, and Odessa behind them both with a boathook held like the weapon it had always secretly been.

"You've got the wrong buyer," Sebastian called back. "We're not selling."

"Everyone sells." The woman shrugged. "It's just a question of price."

"That's what the men who sold Karavel said too." He let that sit. He watched it land — watched two of the six exchange glances, because even hired blades had grandmothers, and grandmothers remembered. "Read the names sometime. Ask yourselves who paid you, and what their grandfather did."

What followed was short and ugly and nothing like the clean duels of storybooks. It was mud and shouting and Odessa swinging her boathook with the economy of a woman who'd defended a ferrydeck for thirty years. Wren fought the way she ran — low, fast, always already somewhere else. Sebastian took a blade across the forearm that would need stitching and gave worse than he got, and when it was over, three of the six were sitting in the shallows holding themselves, two had fled into the reeds, and the scarred-jawed woman stood ankle-deep in the tide with her sword point-down in the mud, looking at him with something that was almost respect.

"Karavel," she said. "My grandmother sang songs about Karavel. I thought they were just songs."

"They were," Wren said quietly. "That's why the chest matters."

The woman looked at the chest a long moment — at the dead crest stamped in its lead seal — then pulled her sword from the mud, sheathed it, and walked away up the flats without another word. Sebastian understood that she would not be reporting to whoever paid her. Not today. Perhaps not ever.

* * *

The enclave was nothing like a nation. It was forty roofs of woven reed and salvaged timber on a bluff above the northern marshes, smoke rising thin from cookfires, goats tethered among the salt grass, children who stopped their games to stare at strangers with the particular stillness of children who have learned that strangers arrive carrying either help or harm.

An old man met them at the path's head, leaning on a staff, his face seamed like dried riverbed. When Wren lifted the chest from her shoulders and set it down in the dust between them, he did not reach for it. He looked at it the way a man looks at a grave.

"Where did you find this?" he asked.

"The salt flats," Sebastian said. "The flood dug it up. The flood, or whatever decides these things."

The old man knelt slowly, joints cracking, and pressed his palm flat against the lead seal. Around him the enclave gathered — silently at first, then not silently, because word travels through a crowd faster than any runner, and someone had seen the crest, and someone else had recognized it, and by the time the chest was opened in the light of the cookfire that evening, there were more people than roof-space to hold them.

Sebastian stood at the edge of the firelight while the documents passed hand to hand. He watched faces change as they read — the names of the men who had opened Karavel's gates, sold its granaries, signed its death in exchange for coastal titles that their grandchildren still held. He watched an old woman read a name aloud and begin, very quietly, to laugh, the laugh of someone who has carried a weight so long she'd forgotten it was there. He watched a boy of perhaps ten ask his mother what the names meant, and watched her answer, and understood that the answer would now be told at every cookfire from here to the coast, and that no magistrate's gavel could ever un-tell it.

No courtroom. No decree. Just truth moving through people the way water moves through land — finding every channel, filling every hollow, impossible to recall.

Wren found him at the fire's edge. She didn't say anything for a while. Neither did he. That was the thing about her he'd never managed to hate properly: she knew when words were the wrong tool.

"I keep thinking I should go," Sebastian said finally. "There's nothing keeping me here. My badge is gone. My warrant died with the flood. I could walk out tomorrow and be anyone."

"Could you?" Wren asked.

He turned the question over and found it heavier than it looked. Behind him, Odessa was arguing cheerfully with three enclave fishermen about the seaworthiness of her boat, which she had apparently decided was now theirs, or theirs hers, the distinction having dissolved somewhere on the river. An old woman pressed a bowl of fish stew into his hands without asking whether he wanted it, because in this place you fed people; that was simply what hands did. A child was showing Wren a knot she'd invented, and Wren was learning it with the grave concentration of a thief studying a new lock.

For months — years, if he was honest — Sebastian had measured every road by how quickly it let him leave it. Stillness had felt like drowning. But standing here, stew warming his palms, fire on his face, he felt something he had no ranger's training for. Not peace exactly. Something more active than peace. The feeling of a man who has stopped running not because the road ended, but because he'd found a place worth arriving at.

"The dry season's opening," Wren said. "Roads will be clear all the way to the coast. If you're going, tomorrow's the day."

Sebastian looked out past the firelight, to where the marshes darkened under the first stars, and beyond them the invisible sea, and beyond that everything he'd ever fled toward.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I'm going to learn to bail a boat properly. Odessa says I'm hopeless."

Wren smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one he'd chased across four hundred miles of salt without ever once managing to catch. He didn't need to catch it anymore. It had come to him.

Above the enclave, the stars came out over the drowned hills, steady and unhurried, and for the first time in as long as he could remember, Sebastian Thornbury was not watching the horizon for what might come next. He was simply in it.
