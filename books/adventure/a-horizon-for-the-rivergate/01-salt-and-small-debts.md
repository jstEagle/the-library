## 1. Salt and Small Debts

There was a saying among the rangers of the Rivergate Basin that Silas Pemberton had never much cared for: *the desert forgives*. He'd spent nine years learning exactly the opposite. The desert didn't forgive anything. It just kept excellent records.

Every footprint out here was a ledger entry. Every broken salt crust, every displaced stone, every place where a lizard had paused and thought better of it — the flats wrote it all down in a hand only patient men could read. Silas had made his living being patient. It wasn't a virtue. It was more like a habit he'd never found a reason to break, the way some men chewed the same pipe long after the tobacco was gone.

The thief he was following had left him a very chatty ledger indeed.

She'd come through the night market at Corren's Landing two days back, lifted a tin of dried fish, a spool of copper wire, and — this was the part that had gotten under his skin like a splinter — a compass off the belt of a man who hadn't even noticed it was gone until Silas pointed it out to him. She'd cut north across the flats toward the old pilgrim road, moving light and fast, and she'd made one mistake in forty miles of empty country.

She'd stopped to sleep.

*Stopping costs more than moving.* That was the other philosophy, the one Silas actually lived by, though he'd have been embarrassed to say it out loud. Out here, motion was safety. A person who kept walking was a rumor; a person who camped was an address. The land remembered where you'd been, and so did everyone who wanted something from you. So you moved. You kept moving until the moving itself became the point, until you couldn't have told anyone — least of all yourself — what you were moving *toward*, only what you were moving away from.

Silas knew that road well. He'd been walking it for nine years.

He crouched at the edge of her cold camp now, in the gray hour before sunrise, and read the story of her evening like a letter from an old friend. She'd built no fire — smart. She'd slept in a hollow behind a shelf of wind-carved saltstone, with her back to the rock and her face to the open flat — smarter still, because nothing could come at her without crossing ground she'd see. She'd eaten half the fish. She'd mended a tear in her sleeve with three stitches of the stolen wire, which struck him as either thrift or a joke, and he was beginning to suspect she was the sort of person who didn't know the difference.

And she'd left, maybe two hours ago, walking northwest.

Northwest. Toward the Kettlewash. Toward the last stretch of open route between here and the river country where Silas had grown up, wading the flood channels with his grandmother's nets, before the concession permits came down like fences drawn on paper and closed every path he'd ever known.

His jaw tightened. He made himself stand up slowly instead.

"Northwest," he said to nobody. His voice came out rusty. He talked to himself a lot on the flats; there wasn't usually anybody else to talk to, and the one time he'd tried talking to his horse, the horse had given him a look of such profound judgment that they'd agreed to keep things professional between them.

The horse — a rangy, mud-colored mare named Biscuit, because she was brown and slightly burnt around the edges of her temperament — stood dozing at the base of the saltstone shelf. Silas saddled her, took a long pull from his water gourd, and set off at a walk, letting the mare pick her way over the crusted pans while he watched the horizon for the small dark smudge of a person on foot.

A petty theft. That's all this was. Fish, wire, a compass. In the old days — by which he meant any year before the permits — a ranger would have shrugged it off. The nomads had their own ways of settling debts; you'd find the thief, she'd return what she could carry, somebody's aunt would yell at everybody involved, and the matter would end with tea. But the new rules said every theft on concession-adjacent land went into a ledger of its own, and ledgers became warrants, and warrants became men in gray coats riding out to make examples. Silas had learned that if he caught the small ones first, gently, the big machinery mostly stayed home. It was a stupid way to spend a life — plugging leaks in a dam with his own two hands — but it was the work he had, and the work was the only thing standing between him and thinking too hard about anything else.

Besides. The compass bothered him.

It was an old service compass, brass-cased, the kind the authority hadn't issued in fifteen years. The man it was stolen from — a drover named Pell, fat and cheerful and entirely unobservant — swore he'd bought it fair at a junk stall. But Silas had held it, and he knew the maker's mark stamped inside the lid, because he'd seen that same mark on the compass that used to ride his own belt, back when he wore a badge instead of just a hat.

Somebody's old service compass, sold or lost or traded, ending up on a junk stall in Corren's Landing. The basin was full of ghosts like that. Most of them stayed buried. This one had grown legs and run off across the salt.

* * *

He caught sight of her at midmorning, a speck against the white glare, and spent the next hour closing the distance with the slow, unglamorous patience of a man watching paint dry in a very large room. She was good. He'd give her that. She walked in the wheel-ruts of an old survey track to spare the crust, she kept to low ground where the heat shimmer would blur her outline, and twice she stopped and simply stood, reading the country behind her, and both times Silas was already down behind a rise with Biscuit's nose in his jacket, waiting.

The second time she did it, he found himself almost smiling. It had been a while since anybody out here had made him work.

By early afternoon the flats began to break up — low mesas the color of old bone, dry washes choked with tamarisk, the first shy green of the season's false start. She ducked into a wash to rest through the worst of the heat, and Silas, who had done this exact thing approximately four hundred times, dismounted a quarter mile back and walked in on foot, boots quiet on the sand, hat brim low.

She was sitting with her back against the wash wall when he rounded the bend, and to her credit, she didn't scream, bolt, or reach for a weapon. She looked up at him with the flat, evaluating calm of a cat deciding whether the new furniture was worth the effort, and said:

"You're the ranger."

"I'm the ranger."

"The one from Corren's. Pell's been telling everyone about you. Says you're the only law left out here who says please." She brushed sand off her knees. "I was going to sell the compass and mail him the money, if that helps."

"It doesn't help."

"I figured." She sighed, as if he were the inconvenience. "Fish and wire I'll give back. Compass I want to keep."

"That's not how debts work."

"You'd be amazed how many ways debts work," she said, "depending on who's doing the counting."

She was young — twenty, maybe twenty-two — with sun-cracked lips, a scarf gone the color of dust, and eyes that did all the talking her mouth was trying to avoid. There was a sharpness under the tiredness that Silas recognized, because he saw it in his own reflection some mornings: the look of someone running a ledger of their own, and losing.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Does it go in the report?"

"Everything goes in the report."

"Then I'm Nobody From Nowhere, and I was born in a ditch." She tipped her head at his belt. "You've got water on you. Real water, not that alkali stuff. I can smell it."

"You can't smell water."

"From here I can." And there it was — the flicker, the thing under the calm. Not greed. Hunger, worn smooth by practice into something that looked like indifference. Silas had seen that look on men twice her age and it had always ended one of two ways, and neither way ended well.

He crouched, keeping his hands loose and visible, an old habit from dealing with spooked animals and spooked people, which were frequently the same animal. "Here's how today goes," he said. "You give me the compass and the wire. You keep the fish — Pell overbought anyway, the man salts everything like he's embalming it. We walk back to Corren's, I write it up as recovered goods, voluntary surrender, no charges. You get a warning. Everybody lives."

"And if I say no?"

"Then it gets complicated, and I hate complicated. Complicated means paperwork."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. Almost a smile. "You really are the polite one."

"I'm the *tired* one. Politeness is cheaper than the alternative." He held out his hand. "Compass."

She looked at his hand for a long moment. Then, slowly, she reached into her coat — and moved faster than he'd have credited, because what came out wasn't the compass. It was a fistful of fine white salt dust, thrown straight into his eyes, and by the time he'd reeled back swearing and blinking tears, she was already past him, up the wash bank, and she'd taken his water gourd off his saddle along with the compass, and Biscuit — traitor, coward, horse — hadn't done so much as flick an ear.

"Sorry!" she called from the top of the rise, and she didn't sound sorry at all. She sounded delighted. "Pell was right about you! You *do* say please!"

Silas knelt in the sand, eyes streaming, listening to her footsteps fade — quick, light, already a rumor again — and laughed. It surprised him, the laugh. It came up from somewhere he'd thought had been packed away with the rest of his belongings, years ago. It hurt a little, like a joint remembering it could bend.

"Biscuit," he said thickly, wiping his face. "You had *one job*."

The mare regarded him with vast equine serenity, as if to say the whole affair had been between him and the girl, and she wished not to be included.

* * *

He caught up to her again near sundown, because of course he did. Nine years of reading salt gave a man certain advantages, and one of them was knowing that people fleeing across open flats always, always aimed for cover, and the only cover for thirty miles was a tamarisk stand along a dead channel called the Sighing Wash — named, according to local legend, for the sound the wind made through it, and according to Silas, for the sound rangers made when they had to search it.

She was there, of course. Sitting against a trunk with his water gourd in her lap, drinking in small, careful sips, rationing like a professional. When he stepped into the wash, she didn't run. She just looked at him, and then at the sky going orange behind him, and something in her shoulders came down an inch.

"You followed me through the heat," she said.

"I napped through most of it."

"Liar. Your shirt's got salt lines up the back. You walked." She turned the gourd in her hands. "Nobody walks the heat for a compass and some fish."

"No," Silas agreed. He lowered himself onto a rock opposite her, joints announcing their various grievances, and let the silence sit a moment. The tamarisk sighed overhead. Somewhere far off, a bird made its opinion known about the state of the world. "Tell me about the compass. Where'd you find it?"

"Why?"

"Because it's not Pell's. It's authority issue — old authority issue, older than me, nearly. And I'd like to know how a girl who was born in a ditch came to be carrying it."

For a moment he thought she'd throw salt at him again. Instead she was quiet for a long time, turning the brass case over in her fingers, thumb finding the worn spot on the lid where a badge number had once been stamped and later ground away.

"My brother carried it," she said finally. "He guided survey crews. Last spring he went out with one — up past the drowned canyons — and none of them came back. No bodies. No tracks. The authority sent one man to look around for two days and then wrote them off as desertion." Her voice stayed level, which is how Silas knew how much it was costing her. "This was his. It got sold off with his effects, like he was dead, like his things were *surplus*. So I took it back. That's my debt. That's my counting."

The wind moved through the tamarisk, sighing. Silas found he had no paperwork for this.

"There's no tracks," he said quietly. "None at all? Six people?"

"Seven with my brother. And nothing. Not a boot print, not a dropped strap. The flats don't lose seven people, ranger. I grew up on these flats. They *keep*." She looked up at him, and there it was again — the hunger, except now he understood it had never been about water. "Everyone says stop looking. Everyone says the desert forgives, the desert takes, the desert whatever. But the desert keeps records. You're the one who taught me that — well, not you. The other rangers, the old ones. Before the permits."

Before the permits. Before the paper walls went up across routes his family had walked for six generations, before his own signature started appearing on documents that fenced off the sky. Silas looked at this thin, stubborn, salt-dusted girl holding a dead man's compass, and felt the strange vertigo of seeing his own philosophy walking around outside his body, younger and angrier and still willing to throw things.

"The gourd," he said at last. "Keep it. You'll need it more than me."

Her eyebrows went up. "That's not how debts work."

"No," he said, standing, brushing off his trousers. "Turns out there's a lot of ways debts work, depending on who's counting." He whistled, and Biscuit came ambling down the wash with the resigned air of an employee who suspected overtime. "Corren's Landing is east. There's a cartographer there, government woman, name of Oduya. Hired me yesterday to look into a missing survey team."

He watched it land. Watched her go absolutely still, the gourd halfway to her lips.

"Up past the drowned canyons," he said. "Seven people. No tracks."

The sun went down behind the mesas, and the Sighing Wash filled up with shadow, and Naya — though he wouldn't learn her name until tomorrow, when she appeared at his campfire uninvited, sat down without asking, and handed him back his water like a queen returning a borrowed crown — stared at him across the dying light with her brother's compass open in her palm, its needle trembling, pointing steadily at something that, as far as any map in the basin agreed, was not there at all.
