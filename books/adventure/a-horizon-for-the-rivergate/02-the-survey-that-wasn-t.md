## 2. The Survey That Wasn't

The woman from the capital arrived at my door before the sun did, which in the Rivergate Basin is a genuine achievement. The light out here takes its time. It likes to sit on the salt flats and warm up first, like an old man easing into a bath. Anyone who shows up earlier than that is either desperate or government, and Wren Oduya managed to be both.

I'd heard her boat coming up the slough for a good ten minutes—the particular arrhythmic sputter of someone who hadn't yet learned that engines in this country need to be talked to, not commanded. By the time she knocked, I had tea going and my boots on, because a ranger learns early that the fastest way to end a conversation you don't want is to look already busy.

"Silas Pemberton?" She stood on my porch with a leather document case clutched to her chest like a shield, dust on her spectacles, and the expression of a person who had rehearsed her opening argument on the boat and was now watching it evaporate.

"That depends who's asking."

"Wren Oduya. Bureau of Cartography." She produced credentials with the crisp efficiency of someone dealing cards. "I need to hire you."

"I don't do permits anymore," I said. "I don't do evictions, boundary disputes, or whatever it is they're calling land theft this season."

"I know what they call it." Something flickered across her face—too quick to name, gone before it settled. "This isn't that. A survey team went missing. Four people. Near the drowned canyons, up past Corren's Landing. They've been dark for six days."

"People go missing in the basin all the time," I said. "Usually because they didn't ask anyone who lives here how the basin works."

"These people asked me. I drew their route maps myself." She said it flatly, but her thumb was pressing a groove into the edge of that document case, worrying the leather like a worry-stone. "Silas Pemberton. Nine years of service. Clean record. You know that country better than anyone still wearing a badge."

"Not wearing one anymore, as it happens."

"So I've heard." She looked past me, into the house, at the single cup on the table, the bed made with military corners, the walls bare except for a faded survey plaque I kept meaning to take down and never did. She was cataloguing me. I recognized the habit; I owned the same disease. "I can pay. And I'm told you never turn down work that keeps you moving."

That landed harder than she could have known. I thought about the last legal route home through the eastern flats, closed off in spring by a concession permit with a number instead of a name. I thought about how a man can spend nine years walking away from things and start to mistake the motion for a life.

"Tea's hot," I said, which in the Rivergate means yes.

* * *

We took my boat upriver, and I learned three things about Wren Oduya in the first hour.

One: she got seasick on rivers, which struck me as a design flaw in a cartographer of waterways, and she handled it with the grim dignity of someone refusing to acknowledge her own body's opinions.

Two: she talked when she was nervous, and she was nervous about something more than a missing team. She told me about the drowned canyons—how the new dam works upstream had swallowed three canyon systems whole, how the old nomad routes through them now existed only on charts marked SUBMERGED, how the water rose a little more every month like a debt being collected.

Three: she watched the banks the way I did. Not the scenery—the ground. Reading it. When we passed a stretch of mud printed with wader tracks, she said, "Those are from yesterday. Water's filled the toe impressions halfway." I decided then that if the missing team turned out to be a fool's errand, I might hire *her*.

The drowned canyons announced themselves before we saw them. The river widened, slowed, went the color of steeped tea, and the cliffs along the banks changed character—old flood lines visible on the stone like rings on a tree, each one higher than the last. Somewhere under our keel were trees that had been forest, paths that had been roads, and a way of life that had been a way of life.

The survey camp sat on a shelf of rock above the waterline, tucked against a cliff face where the mapmakers had evidently decided the rising water couldn't reach them yet. From fifty meters out, everything looked fine. That was the first wrong thing. A camp abandoned in panic looks wrong from a distance—scattered, torn, birds circling. This camp looked *tidy*, and tidy was worse.

I beached the boat and told Wren to stay put until I'd walked it. She stayed put for approximately forty seconds.

Four tents, all staked properly. Four bedrolls inside, rolled or half-rolled, nothing dragged, nothing bloodied. Cooking gear stacked. A instrument case open on a camp table, brass fittings catching the afternoon light—and here I stopped, because the case was open but nothing was scattered around it. Nobody grabs equipment in a hurry and leaves the case neatly open. Either they left unhurried, or they left without using their hands at all.

There was a kettle on the fire ring. I crouched and touched it. Cold, but not long cold—not rain-washed, not sun-baked to the temperature of the stone. The ash beneath it was intact, undisturbed, the way ash looks when nobody has broken it apart to bank coals or bury a fire. Somebody had made tea, set the kettle aside, and then—

And then what?

"No tracks," Wren said behind me. She'd done a slow circuit of the camp perimeter while I worked the interior, and her voice had gone carefully level, the way voices do when the speaker is holding something heavy and doesn't want to drop it. "Silas. There are no tracks."

"There are always tracks."

"Not here. Look." She pointed at the soft silt at the camp's edge, ground that should have held prints the way wax holds a seal. Six days of four people living, eating, hauling water, and the ground recorded none of it. Not faded prints, not wind-blurred ones. None. As if the camp had been lowered into place by crane and lifted out the same way.

I've spent nine years reading ground. It's the only conversation the desert ever has with you, and I flatter myself I speak it fluently. Ground tells you who came, how many, how fast, how scared, how heavy laden. Ground told me once that a horse thief limped, twice that a bride ran off willingly, and a hundred times that men lie and dirt doesn't.

This ground was lying. Or rather, it had been *made* to hold its tongue.

I stood very still in the middle of that impossible camp and felt the unease in my chest change species. This wasn't the professional discomfort of a hard search ahead. This was older and colder—the feeling you get when you realize the story in front of you has been arranged, and arranged by someone patient. Tracks don't erase themselves. Wind blurs them, water washes them, but six days of weather doesn't produce a clean floor. Someone had swept this site. Swept it the way you sweep a kitchen, calmly, thoroughly, with a broom and time.

Nobody sweeps a crime scene unless they own the aftermath.

"The instruments," Wren said. She'd reached the open case and gone quiet in a different way now—not nervous quiet, offended quiet. She lifted out a transit scope and sighted through it at the cliff line, and I watched her face do something complicated. "These readings are wrong. All of them. The calibration's been shifted—it would put every elevation off by—" She stopped. Recalculated. Her lips moved with the arithmetic. "By enough to move a river."

"Move a river?"

"Move a *boundary*." She set the scope down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you handle a thing you're angry at. "If you surveyed from these instruments, you'd draw maps that were wrong in a consistent direction. Consistently wrong isn't error. Error wanders. This is aimed."

I looked at the cold kettle. At the neat tents. At the ground that refused to testify. Four people had come to this shelf of rock to measure the world honestly, and somewhere between the tea and the tents, the world had measured *them*.

"Any sign of them?" Wren asked. "Bodies, blood, a note?"

"No bodies. No blood. No note." I turned a slow circle, giving the cliffs their due attention. "Which leaves three possibilities. They walked out and someone erased every trace of the walking. They were taken out, same condition. Or—" I paused, because the third possibility was the one I liked least, and saying it aloud gives it weight. "Or they found something here worth making disappear, and the disappearing started with them."

Wren had gone pale around the mouth, but her hands were steady as she opened her document case and pulled out her own field copy of the route map. She spread it on the camp table, weighted the corners with stones, and compared it against the survey team's remaining papers. I watched her frown deepen by degrees, a cartographer's frown, the frown of a woman meeting an error she couldn't explain.

"This journal page," she said. "It's the team leader's. Ilsa Voss. She logged a position here—" her finger tapped a coordinate, "—and it doesn't match anything. It's not on any chart I've ever drawn, and I've drawn all of them."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning either Voss made a mistake, which I doubt, because her work is beautiful, or she found a place that isn't supposed to exist." Wren looked up at me over her spectacles, and behind the dust and the seasickness and the bureau polish there was something harder and hungrier. "Pemberton. What kind of country makes a government survey team vanish and sweeps the ground after?"

I knew the answer. I'd been watching the answer arrive for nine years, permit by permit, fence line by fence line, each one drawn by offices in the capital by people who had never tasted the salt air out here and never needed to. The kind of country where somebody owns the map. And whoever owns the map owns everything the map says exists—including, apparently, the places it says don't.

I picked up the cold kettle and poured the last of the team's tea into my cup. It was bitter and stale and I drank it anyway, partly for the ritual and partly because I wanted to feel close to whoever had poured it first. Four people had stood here planning a day of honest work. The tea was still in the pot. That was the detail that would keep me up tonight, I suspected—not the missing tracks, not the tampered scopes, but the tea. You don't abandon a hot drink unless the world stops making sense.

"We'll need supplies," I heard myself say. "And we'll need to talk to anyone who works this stretch of river. Smugglers, fishers, salt-gatherers. If four people left this camp, somebody saw them, and if nobody saw them, that's an answer too."

"You're taking the job, then." It wasn't quite a question. Wren was already repacking her case, brisk again, purpose like a spine straightening.

"I'm taking the job," I said, and discovered I meant it, and discovered further that meaning it felt less like duty than like relief, which told me something about the state of my life I chose not to examine just then.

I was turning toward the boat when I saw it—a glint at the base of the cliff, half-tucked under a stone where the sweepers' broom had missed it. Small. Brass. I crouched and worked it free of the grit.

A compass. Old pattern, service issue, the kind the ranger authority handed out a decade ago before they switched to the new concession-stamped models. The lid was scored with initials scratched by hand, clumsy and young: *T.V.*

I turned it over twice in my palm. It swung lazily, settled, and pointed—steadily, stubbornly, ignoring the magnetic north I knew to be true—toward the drowned canyons, deep into country that appeared on no map Wren Oduya had ever drawn.

"Well," I said. "That shouldn't happen."

Wren came and looked over my shoulder, and neither of us said anything else for a long moment. The river moved brown and slow below us, keeping whatever it kept. Somewhere under that water lay canyons, roads, and maybe four surveyors—or the reason there weren't four surveyors anymore.

The compass pointed into the impossible, and I pocketed it, because I had a feeling that following it was going to cost me far more than stopping ever had.
