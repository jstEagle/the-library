## 3. A Compass That Lies

Wren Oduya had handled a great many instruments in her career—sextants with cracked mirrors, theodolites older than her father, one barometer that had been dropped down a well and still insisted, stubbornly, that the weather was fine. She liked instruments because they were honest in a way people rarely managed to be. An instrument either told you the truth or it was broken, and broken things could be fixed.

The survey team's transit case opened with a click that sounded too clean for gear that had supposedly been abandoned in a panic.

She knelt in the dust of the vanished camp, sleeves rolled, and lifted the theodolite out with both hands. It was beautiful work—brass and glass, government-stamped, maintained by someone who cared. She turned it over, checked the leveling screws, sighted along the barrel toward the salt horizon out of pure habit.

Then she stopped.

"Silas," she said.

He was across the campsite, crouched over the cold fire ring, doing whatever it was rangers did instead of admitting they were worried. "Mm."

"Come look at this. And bring your hands, not your opinions."

He came over with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once hurried anywhere on principle. He leaned over her shoulder. She felt him go still.

"The bubble level," she said. "Look at it."

The spirit vial sat in its brass housing—and the housing was sitting a full two degrees off true against the instrument's own reference plate. Anyone using this theodolite would have leveled it perfectly to a false standard. Every reading taken with it would drift, gently, consistently, in the same direction. Not enough to notice in the field. Enough to ruin a map.

"That's not damage," Silas said slowly. "Damage falls. This was *set*."

"Someone adjusted it deliberately." Wren set it down and pulled open the map case beside it, and her stomach did something complicated and cold. The maps inside were lovely. Crisp linework, confident hachures, the drowned canyon country rendered in careful ink. Too careful. She'd drawn maps of this basin herself; she knew how the drainage actually ran, and these maps showed channels where she'd personally stood in dry ground, and dry ground where she'd once lost a boot to standing water.

"They're forgeries," she said. "Good ones. But I've surveyed half this terrain, and half of it isn't where these say it is."

Silas picked up one sheet and held it to the light, angling it the way he seemed to angle everything—like he expected it to lie to him and wanted to catch it at it.

* * *

*From the recovered journal of Survey Leader Ilsa Voss, entry dated the 14th of the Wet Season:*

*Day nine, and I no longer trust my own equipment, which is a sentence I never thought I'd write. Pell checked the theodolite against the stars last night—the old way, the slow way—and swears our baseline has drifted east by more than the instrument could account for. Either the ground moved (it didn't) or something in our kit is lying to us.*

*I've started carrying my own compass, the one my brother gave me, sewn into my coat lining like a paranoid grandmother. If the day comes when I can't trust what's in the cases, I want one honest needle left in the world.*

*Tomaso laughed at me. Then he helped me sew the pocket deeper.*

* * *

Silas read the journal page twice. Wren had found it folded into the false map case, along with three others—a habit of Voss's, apparently, keeping a paper trail inside the thing most likely to be stolen. Smart woman. The kind of smart that made Silas uneasy, because smart women who kept paper trails usually needed them for a reason, and reasons like that had a way of ending badly.

"Her brother," he said.

"What?"

"She mentions a brother gave her the compass." He turned the page toward Wren. "You said the missing guide's family filed the inquiry. What was his name?"

Wren frowned, digging through her satchel for the file. "Kellam. Kellam Voss. Why?"

"Because the thief who robbed me two weeks ago stole an old service compass," Silas said, "and fought me like it was worth more than the water she took with it. I've been wondering about that ever since. Water's life out here. Nobody fights harder than for water." He tapped the journal. "Unless they're fighting for family."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. "You think Naya is Voss's sister."

"I think the basin's smaller than the government thinks it is."

He said it lightly, but the thought settled in his chest with weight. Nine years he'd been chasing small crimes across these flats, and somewhere in all that moving he'd learned that nothing out here was ever just itself. A stolen compass was a grief. A vanished team was a message. A forged map was a door, and behind every door somebody was standing there hoping you wouldn't knock.

He turned back to the campsite evidence—or rather, the elegant absence of it. No tracks. He'd walked the perimeter twice more that morning, widening the circle each time, and the salt crust around the camp lay unbroken in every direction except the one their own boat had come from. Six trained surveyors did not leave a site without tracks. They did not leave their tea cold in the pot, their tents staked, their boots—boots, by God, six pairs of them—lined up neat as churchgoers outside the mess fly.

Unless they'd meant to make it look like they'd been taken. Unless leaving *was* the plan, and the plan required a stage.

And someone had dressed the stage before they left. Someone had doctored the instruments and swapped the maps so that whoever came looking would find exactly the wrong country documented in beautiful, official ink.

"Who benefits?" Wren asked. She'd pulled out her notebook and was sketching the false drainage lines from memory, quick and precise. "Bad maps of the drowned canyons. Who wants the canyons mapped wrong?"

Silas crouched and picked up the corner of the topographic sheet again. He wasn't a cartographer, but he'd spent nine years reading documents—permits, warrants, boundary filings, the small bureaucratic weather that decided who could walk where. He knew handwriting the way Wren knew contour lines. And forgery, like any craft, had habits. A forger's hand betrayed them in the flourishes, in the way they drew a river fork or a boundary stone, in the little inherited tics of whatever office had taught them to draft.

He'd seen this style before. Recently. In the stack of concession filings he'd been forced to serve on the nomad families last spring—the permits that had closed his own route home, among other things. The boundary lines on those permits had a particular look: the forks drawn with a slight upward flick, the hachures stacked like fish scales, the north arrow rendered as a star with eight points instead of four. A house style. An office style.

"This matches the concession office drafting conventions," he said. "Exactly. Not similar. Exact. Same hand training, same template, same—" he turned the sheet, "—same eight-pointed star."

Wren went very quiet. "The concession office drafted the survey team's field maps."

"Or drafted the replacements."

"Silas." She set down her pencil. "If the concession office is forging the survey maps, then the permits were drawn from forged surveys. Which means the boundaries themselves are fiction. Which means—" She stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things had consequences, and consequences out here tended to arrive on horseback with rifles.

"Which means somebody powerful is lying on paper," Silas finished for her, "and the survey team found out, and now the survey team is gone without tracks." He stood, knees cracking, and looked out at the empty flats shimmering under the early heat. "I've spent nine years arresting people for stealing goats. I'd very much like to know what the punishment is for stealing a landscape."

* * *

*From the recovered journal of Survey Leader Ilsa Voss, entry dated the 19th of the Wet Season:*

*Found it. Or rather, Tomaso found it—he has always been luckier than he deserves. One of the porters brought a duplicate set of base maps from the Corren's Landing office, and Tomaso, being Tomaso, laid them side by side with ours out of boredom. They don't match. Ours show the aquifer readings we took in the deep canyon; theirs show nothing there at all. Blank. As if forty miles of water-bearing limestone simply declined to exist.*

*I've written to the Concession Office asking for clarification. Reeve's deputy replied within the hour—within the HOUR, which for that office is faster than fire—and assured me the discrepancy was a printing error and requested I surrender my field copies for 'correction.'*

*I have surrendered copies. Copies. My originals are in the lining of my coat now, next to my brother's compass. Let them correct their own lies. I am going to finish this survey honestly if it kills me.*

*In hindsight, I should have chosen less dramatic phrasing.*

* * *

Wren read the entry three times. Around her, the dead camp ticked and settled in the heat—the canvas flies breathing in the wind, a tin cup rolling a slow half-inch in the grit. Somewhere out on the flats a bird cried once and thought better of it.

"Aquifer," she said finally. "Under the drowned canyons."

"You know what that means?"

"It means the whole basin might be sitting on water." Her voice came out flat with concentration. "Real water, permanent water—not monsoon runoff, not the seasonal rivers. If the government knew, and the concession permits were drawn to keep everyone away from the canyon country..." She trailed off, pencil hovering. "Permits aren't just paper, Silas. Permits decide who gets to be *there*. If you control the maps, you control who believes anything is there at all."

Silas was staring at the lined-up boots again. Six pairs. Neat. Patient. A stage dressed by professionals.

"And Voss wouldn't play along," he said. "So they made her disappear. Except—" he gestured at the untouched camp, the cold tea, the impossible absence of tracks, "—this isn't how you disappear people. This is how you make people *think* someone disappeared them. There's a difference. The first gets you a body. The second gets you a story."

"What story?"

"The kind that keeps other surveyors home." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Nobody volunteers to map a place where teams vanish without a trace. Fear does your fencing for you. You don't need permits to keep people out of the canyons if the canyons are haunted."

Wren looked at him with an expression he couldn't quite parse—something between alarm and admiration. "That's a very cynical theory."

"I'm a very cynical man. It comes free with the badge."

"Except," Wren said, and here her voice did something delicate, "if Voss staged her own vanishing, then she's not dead. And if she's not dead, she took her originals with her. And if she took her originals—" she held up the journal page, "—then somewhere out there is a set of maps that tells the truth, and a woman who knows exactly how much it's worth, and exactly who wants her dead."

The wind moved across the flats, carrying the faint mineral smell of the salt, and beneath it—so faint Wren might have imagined it—the green promise of coming rain. The monsoon was early this year. Everyone said so. The nomads had been saying it for a month, watching the sky like creditors.

"We need that thief," Silas said.

"Naya."

"If she stole Voss's brother's compass, she may know things the file doesn't. Where the guide was from. Who he wrote to. Whether Ilsa mentioned where she'd run." He paused. "Also she has my water ration, and I take that personally."

Despite everything, Wren smiled. "Of course. The conspiracy can wait. The water ration cannot."

"Wren." He said it seriously, and she stopped smiling. "There's a river market two days south where a girl like that would surface eventually. Thieves always come back to water. But if what's in these journals is true—if there's an aquifer under those canyons and men willing to kill over paper—then we're not looking for a missing team anymore. We're carrying something hot. You understand that? From here on, every person we talk to is either help or hazard, and some of them will be both."

Wren packed the journal carefully into her satchel, next to her own instruments, which she had that morning checked against the stars out of a new and lasting paranoia. They were honest. For now.

"Then we'd better find the one person in this basin with a reason to help us," she said, "and a grudge big enough to keep her brave."

Silas looked south, toward the river, toward the market smells and the crowd noise and a thief who fought like grief had hands.

"Kellam Voss's sister," he said quietly. "Let's hope she's as stubborn as her family."

* * *

*From the recovered journal of Survey Leader Ilsa Voss, final entry, undated:*

*If anyone finds this: the originals exist. The aquifer is real. The permits are built on maps that were drawn after the land they claim to describe, which is a trick only possible if the people drawing them already knew what they wanted the land to say. Follow the old nomad route past Drowned Bend. Trust the river. Trust my brother's compass—it points somewhere that shouldn't exist, and that somewhere is the truth.*

*Do not trust the office. Do not trust the friendly ones.*

*Trust whoever stayed.*
