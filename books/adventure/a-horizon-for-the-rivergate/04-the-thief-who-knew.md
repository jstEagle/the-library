## 4. The Thief Who Knew

Naya had been lifting purses in the Rivergate markets since she was nine years old, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things worth knowing.

First: never steal from anyone who looks like they're enjoying the market. Happy people count their money later; miserable people count it now.

Second: always know where the water is. Every market in the basin was built around a river landing or a well or a cistern, and every escape worth making ended at water, because water forgave everything—footprints, blood, a dropped knife.

Third: if a ranger ever got close enough to touch you, you were already caught, so you might as well say something memorable on the way down.

She was thinking about all three of these rules, and about how thoroughly she was about to break the third one, when the tall ranger in the dust-colored coat stepped out from between two stalls of dried fish and said, in a voice like gravel poured slowly, "You have my compass."

The morning market at Sallow Bend smelled of cardamom smoke and river mud and the particular sweetness of overripe melons nobody would admit to selling. Naya stood with her back to a stack of woven baskets, her hand still warm from the purse she'd lifted not four minutes earlier—a good purse, fat with ferry tokens—and the ranger was looking at her the way a man looks at weather he'd been promised.

"You're mistaken," she said. "I don't have your anything."

"I know what I have," he said. "I'm telling you what you have."

That was the thing about Silas Pemberton that the market gossip had gotten wrong. They said he was slow, that he was tired, that he tracked petty thieves across the flats out of some stubborn habit his body wouldn't give up even after his heart did. All true, probably. But nobody mentioned that when he finally cornered you, he didn't shout, didn't reach for his irons, didn't do any of the theater rangers loved. He just stood there being correct at you until correctness wore you down like water on salt.

Naya's eyes flicked left—the fish stalls, the crowd, the gap behind the melon cart that ran down to the landing.

"Don't," said Pemberton.

"I wasn't going to."

"You looked at the melon cart."

"Everyone looks at the melon cart. It's a very dramatic melon cart."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, filed away somewhere under nine years of dust. "Give me the compass, and we can discuss whether the melon cart is dramatic."

And here was the problem, the whole rotten beautiful problem: she couldn't give him the compass. Not because it was useful—though it was, God knew it was, that needle pointing at nothing like a drunk pointing at the moon—but because of whose it was. Because of the initials scratched into the brass case in a boyish hand that had never learned to write small. Because of the dent in the lid from the night Tam fell off the ferry dock laughing at his own joke and swore the compass had broken his fall.

Because Tam had walked into the drowned canyons with a survey team six weeks ago and had not walked out, and this compass was the last piece of him that moved.

"No," Naya said.

Pemberton exhaled through his nose. Around them the market flowed on, oblivious—women haggling over river salt, a boy chasing a goat with the confidence of someone who had chased this goat before and lost. "You know who I am."

"Everyone knows who you are. You're the ranger who catches everybody. The story they tell children so they'll hold onto their purses." She tilted her chin. "You're also the ranger who hasn't caught anybody in two years, because there's nothing left to catch. The permits closed the flats. Your whole territory is a fence now, and you're the dog that keeps patrolling a yard nobody lives in."

The words landed harder than she'd meant them to. She watched them land. Watched something go out of his face—not anger, worse than anger. Recognition.

"That's a lot of talk," he said quietly, "for someone standing next to a stolen purse."

Naya's hand went to her belt before she could stop it. The purse. The fat, stupid, ferry-token purse she'd lifted while running from a man she should have been running toward—

Wait.

*Running from.* That was the thought that snagged, sharp as a fishhook. He hadn't grabbed her. He'd had every chance—she'd counted three in the first ten seconds alone. A ranger with his reputation could have taken her wrist before she finished her first denial. Instead he'd stood there and *talked*, which no ranger did unless talking was the point.

"You followed me here," she said slowly. "From the flats. Three days ago."

"I've been following you for three days, yes."

"Why? You had me at the salt pans outside Corren's Landing. I know you did. I saw your tracks doubling mine." She'd spent half a night panicking about it, actually, lying awake in a culvert wondering why a legendary tracker would let a thief sleep. "You could have taken the compass back then. Taken me too. Why wait?"

Pemberton was quiet long enough that the goat escaped its boy entirely and knocked over a basket of limes, to general applause.

"Because you weren't running like a thief," he said at last. "Thieves run light. You ran like someone carrying something heavy. And you kept checking the compass. Every hour, sometimes more often. Thieves check their loot to make sure it's still there. You checked yours like you were asking it a question." His eyes, pale as river-worn stone, held hers. "So I wanted to hear the answer."

Naya's throat went tight. This was the danger of the Rivergate, she thought wildly. Not the enforcers, not the permit men with their stamped paper and their soft boots. The danger was that everyone out here was lonely enough to see straight through you.

"It belonged to my brother," she said, and hated how her voice cracked on *brother*, like a wheel hitting a rut.

*

They ended up at a tea stall at the edge of the landing, which was the last place either of them should have gone—a ranger and a known thief sitting shoulder to shoulder on a bench built for smaller crimes—but the stall's owner was deaf as a post and loyal to no government but the tea's, and Naya needed to sit down before her legs figured out she'd stopped running.

Pemberton paid for both cups without comment. She decided not to read anything into that, then read seventeen things into it immediately.

"His name was Tam," she said, turning her cup. "Tam Oduya."

The cup stopped halfway to the ranger's mouth.

"Oduya." He set it down. "Your brother was the guide. On Voss's survey."

"He grew up on those rivers. Before the dam works, before the canyons drowned, he knew every channel and every deadfall from Sallow Bend to the capital landing. The survey office hired him because nobody else could read that water." Her hands were steady. She made them be steady. "Six weeks ago the team went upriver. Fourteen days ago, the office announced they'd vanished. No bodies. No wreckage. No word." She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle as sun-baked clay. "No tracks. Isn't that what they said? No tracks at all?"

Pemberton said nothing, but his silence had a shape, and the shape was agreement.

"The compass was his. Our father gave it to him, and our father got it from a ferry captain who won it in a card game he swears he lost on purpose. It's junk, honestly. The needle's been wrong since before I was born—it points twenty degrees east of anywhere real. But Tam carried it everywhere. Said a compass that lies teaches you better than one that tells the truth, because you have to learn the land itself instead of trusting the needle." She swallowed. "When the survey office packed up his quarters, they sold his things. Sold *him*, effectively—his kit, his letters, everything, into a bin at a surplus auction like he was equipment that hadn't come back. I bought back what I could find. The compass wasn't there. I traced it. It had been resold twice, and the second buyer was a man camped on the flats north of Corren's Landing."

"With a water ration he didn't guard properly," Pemberton said.

"With a water ration he didn't guard properly," Naya agreed. "I'm sorry about that, by the way. You looked thirsty."

"I was."

"And the irons—I didn't take those. I want that noted. I have standards."

"Noted."

She turned the compass over in her hands, thumb finding the dent in the lid. "Here's what I can't make fit, ranger. Tam sent me a letter the week before he went upriver. Just a line—he was always just a line, postage cost money—saying *if anything happens, the needle will point true for once*. I thought it was a joke. He joked about that compass his whole life." Her jaw ached from clenching. "Then he vanishes with eleven other people, leaving no tracks in country where I once tracked a one-legged heron for two days, and the survey office burns his belongings, and the concession office announces new permit boundaries drawn by mapmakers who apparently surveyed land nobody walked. And I keep asking this compass where my brother is, and it keeps pointing northeast, into the drowned canyons, at something that isn't on any map I've ever seen."

She looked up. "So. What are you doing out here, Ranger Pemberton? Really? Because I've watched you chase me across forty miles of salt, and a man doesn't spend that kind of effort on a stolen compass unless the compass is the least strange thing in his week."

For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he reached into his coat and brought out a folded sheet of paper, worn soft at the creases, and laid it on the bench between them like a man laying down a card he wasn't sure won the hand.

It was a page from a journal. Fine, cramped handwriting. *The instruments disagree with each other. Wren says it's the heat. I say instruments don't lie in chorus.*

"Wren?" Naya said.

"Cartographer. Government. Hired me to find the missing team." His mouth twisted. "We found their camp instead. Gear intact. Tea cold in the cups. Eleven people gone from hardpan ground without leaving so much as a heel print—which is impossible, and impossible things don't happen by accident. And this—" he tapped the journal page, "—was buried under a stone where only someone who knew to look would look. Someone on that team left it. Which means someone on that team expected to disappear."

Naya felt the hair rise on her arms, there in the hot market morning. "The needle points northeast. The canyons are northeast."

"I know."

"And you think—"

"I think your brother's lying compass might be the only honest instrument left in this entire basin." He said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "I think eleven people found something the concession office would drown the whole rivergate to bury, and I think somebody very tidy made them vanish, and I think the reason you and I have been circling each other for three days is that we're both doing the same thing."

"Which is?"

"Refusing to stop." He picked up his tea again, drank, grimaced at it—the tea at Sallow Bend was famously an insult—and Naya realized she was smiling, a small unguarded smile she hadn't worn since the announcement, since the bin of sold belongings, since the world had developed a hole in it shaped exactly like her brother.

"So what happens now?" she asked. "Officially speaking, you're supposed to arrest me."

"Officially speaking," Pemberton said, "I'm supposed to arrest you, recover the compass, file a report nobody reads, and go back to patrolling a fence." He looked out at the river, brown and patient, carrying the whole basin's secrets seaward one silt grain at a time. "But officially speaking, there's no such thing as a vanishing without tracks, and yet here we sit."

"There's a town called Corren's Landing," Naya said. "Where the permit office keeps its records. Where the boundaries were filed. If those maps are forged, the proof's in the dates—in the ink, in the paper, in whatever clerk signed off on an impossibility."

Pemberton glanced at her, and she saw it happen: the weary professional weighing the reckless amateur, and losing, and being glad about it.

"You've been reading permit filings," he said.

"I've been reading everything. Grief makes excellent study habits. You can't sleep anyway; you may as well learn property law."

That almost-smile again, surfacing like a fish. "Corren's Landing it is, then. But there are conditions."

"Of course there are conditions."

"One: you don't lift anything that isn't nailed down, and nothing that's nailed down either. Two: when I say run, you run. Three—" he hesitated, and for a moment the gravel voice went uncertain, human, "—when we find out what happened to your brother, however it turns out, you let me stand next to you when you hear it. Nobody should hear a thing like that alone."

Naya looked at him for a long moment—at the dust-colored coat and the tired eyes and the terrible tea going cold in his hands—and thought about her third rule, the one about saying something memorable on the way down.

"Deal," she said. "But I get the window seat on the boat."

"There's no window seat on a boat."

"Then I get the seat furthest from you, because you snore. I heard you the second night, outside Corren's Landing. Through a culvert. Through *earth*, ranger."

"I do not snore."

"You snore like a landslide with opinions."

He shook his head, but he was fighting the smile now and losing badly, and Naya thought: *there you are.* Underneath all that dust and duty, there you are.

She pocketed the compass—her compass, Tam's compass—and stood, and the market noise rushed back in around them, the haggling and the goat and the smell of cardamom, the ordinary world going on top of the extraordinary one like paint over rot.

"Northeast," she said quietly, touching her pocket, feeling the little brass lie tick against her hip like a heartbeat. "Whatever's out there, it's been waiting for us long enough."

Pemberton rose too, unfolding to his full height, and looked downriver toward Corren's Landing, where the permit office sat fat and white above the water like a spider that had learned architecture.

"One more condition," he said. "In Corren's Landing, you let me do the talking to officials. You have a face that argues."

"My face is *lovely*."

"Your face is a legal liability." He was already walking. "Come on, thief. Let's go find out what the government is afraid of."

And Naya Oduya, who had spent six weeks asking a broken compass where her brother was, followed the only honest lawman in the Rivergate down to the water—carrying her grief like a blade she'd finally learned to hold by the handle.

* * *

Behind them, unnoticed, a man in a concession office coat lowered the newspaper he'd been pretending to read, and wrote three words in a small neat notebook:

*The girl talks.*
