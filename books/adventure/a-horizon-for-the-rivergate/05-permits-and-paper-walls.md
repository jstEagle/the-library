## 5. Permits and Paper Walls

The records office at Corren's Landing occupied the ground floor of what had once been a granary, and it still smelled faintly of old wheat, as though the building itself remembered being useful. Wren Oduya stood at the counter for eleven minutes before anyone acknowledged her existence, which she considered almost generous by government standards. In the capital, you could wait a full day and be charged a fee for the privilege.

The clerk who finally emerged from behind a partition was a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the haunted expression of someone who had once loved his work.

"Cartographic survey access," Wren said, laying her ministry credentials on the counter. "Concession permits, Rivergate Basin, all filings since authorization."

His eyes flicked to her credentials, then to her face, then to something over her shoulder, as though hoping a supervisor might materialize to absorb this problem. "That's... quite a lot of paper."

"I'm aware of how paper works."

He flushed. "It's only that most people want the summary folios. The pretty ones. The full filings are—" he gestured vaguely toward the back of the room, where shelves receded into shadow like the entrance to a cave system, "—back there."

"Then I'll be back there."

What followed was an hour of increasingly creative bureaucratic resistance—the files required a form, the form required a countersignature, the countersignature belonged to a man who was "at lunch" in a way that suggested he might be at lunch until autumn—but Wren had spent six years navigating the Ministry of Lands and Survey, and she knew that every obstruction was just a door with a personality. She signed things. She waited politely through two false starts. She complimented the clerk's filing system, which was genuinely excellent and clearly the only thing in the building anyone cared about, and by mid-afternoon she was alone in the archive room with a lamp, a table, and nine years of concession paperwork stacked in front of her like a small defensive wall.

Silas had wanted to come inside. She'd told him no.

It wasn't distrust, exactly. It was that Silas Pemberton walked into rooms the way a hawk walks into a henhouse—not because he intended violence, but because everyone's reaction to him told her something, and today she wanted reactions undisturbed. Besides, someone needed to mind Naya, and "minding Naya" was a job Wren suspected would age her prematurely. The thief had spent the morning loudly declaring that Corren's Landing was full of things worth stealing and that she was *reformed now, obviously*, in a tone that convinced absolutely no one, least of all herself.

So: the archive. Just Wren and the paper.

She started with the boundary maps themselves, because that was her language. Each concession permit came with a survey plat—a formal drawing of the land being leased, its boundaries fixed by coordinates and landmarks. She'd reviewed dozens of these in her career. They were supposed to be boring. Boring meant accurate.

The first plat covered the eastern salt flats, forty thousand hectares granted to the Meridian Concession Company. Wren smoothed it flat under her palms and began, out of habit, checking the reference points.

Her habit caught something within four minutes.

A boundary marker described as "the split rock above the dry wash" — she knew that rock. Not personally, but professionally; it appeared on the 1889 basin survey, the foundational map every cartographer in the province learned from. Except the 1889 survey placed the split rock *two miles north* of where this plat put it. And the plat wasn't wrong by accident. Its whole coordinate grid was shifted, subtly, elegantly, so that the concession line swallowed a corridor of land that the original survey showed as open route — nomad territory, unclaimed, unmapped by any government hand.

She pulled the next plat. The same shift, again, in a different quadrant. And the next. Nine plats, nine concessions, and every single one of them had absorbed the same corridors — the old caravan routes, the well roads, the seasonal crossings. Someone had redrawn the basin so that everything the nomads used to move through it now legally belonged to the Meridian Concession Company.

Wren sat back. Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

*This is the quiet part,* she thought. *This is what they're not saying out loud.* The permits weren't about grazing rights or mineral claims or whatever justification had been printed in the gazette. The permits were a fence made of ink. Draw the routes as private property, and the people who walked them became trespassers on their own history.

But it was worse than that, and it took her another hour, three cups of terrible office tea, and one increasingly sympathetic visit from the clerk (who kept finding reasons to bring her things — more tea, a better lamp, a cushion that smelled of wheat) before she found the thing that turned unease into dread.

Plats had to cite their source surveys. It was standard practice: *boundaries per Survey of [date], filed under [reference].* Wren flipped to the citation page of the fourth plat and read:

*Boundaries per detailed hydrographic survey of the Rivergate Basin, dated 14th of the Dry Month, Year 901.*

She checked the permit's own filing date. *Filed 2nd of the Green Month, Year 900.*

Wren stared at it. Read it again. Held the page up to the lamp as though the light might correct her eyes.

The boundaries were drawn from a survey dated fourteen months *after* the permit that cited it. The map was older than its own source. Unless someone had built a time machine out of bureaucracy — and Wren put nothing past the Ministry, but even they had limits — this was impossible.

Unless the survey hadn't existed when the permit was written. Unless the permit was written first, claiming land nobody had properly measured, and the "source survey" was manufactured afterward to make the claim look legitimate. Backwards. The whole edifice backwards.

She went through all nine plats with a pencil, marking dates. Seven of the nine carried citations from surveys that postdated their own permits. One cited a survey that didn't exist in the provincial registry at all — she checked twice, then a third time, her hands moving faster now. The ninth cited a real survey, but the numbers on the plat matched nothing in it.

Someone had forged the foundation. Not one document. The entire architecture. And they'd done it competently enough to pass review, which meant either the reviewers were fools or the reviewers were paid, and Wren had met enough ministry reviewers to bet confidently on the second option.

She copied the citations by hand, date against date, permit against source, until her page looked like a ledger of impossibilities. Then she sat very still for a moment, thinking about a missing survey team in the drowned canyons, and a compass that pointed somewhere that shouldn't exist, and a thief whose brother had walked into the basin carrying instruments that might have recorded the truth.

Ilsa Voss's team had been doing hydrographic work. Detailed. Basin-wide.

*Oh,* Wren thought, with the particular cold clarity of a cartographer realizing the map she's standing on is a lie. *They found it. They found exactly this.*

* * *

She emerged from the archive blinking in the late-afternoon light like something exhumed, clutching her notes, and nearly walked into trouble wearing a very good coat.

He was standing by the records office door talking to the clerk — the haunted one, who was laughing, actually laughing, at whatever the man was saying, which told Wren immediately that this stranger was dangerous, because that clerk had not laughed once in five hours and Wren had *complimented his filing system*.

"Aldous Reeve," the man said, turning to her with an easy smile and extending a hand. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in the kind of linen that announced its own price quietly. "Deputy Concessioner. You must be the ministry cartographer — the archivist mentioned a colleague from the capital doing research. I always like to meet the people who keep our maps honest."

Every word was warm. Every word was also, Wren noted, a small hook: *our* maps, *honest*, *colleague*. He was establishing, in three sentences, that they were on the same side, that the records were shared property, that her presence here was known and catalogued.

"Oduya," she said, shaking his hand. His grip was dry and precise. "Routine cross-referencing. Concession boundaries against historical surveys."

"Ah." Something moved behind his eyes — not alarm, nothing so crude. More like a man watching a scale settle. "Historical work. Important. Though I warn you, the old surveys are a mess. Half of them were drawn by drunk cavalry officers. That's rather why the concession program exists — to give the basin proper, modern documentation." He smiled again. "You've found the same, I expect. Errors everywhere."

"Errors everywhere," Wren agreed pleasantly.

It was, she reflected later, the most dangerous conversation of her life conducted entirely in polite agreement. Reeve walked her the length of the street toward the inn where Silas and Naya were waiting, chatting amiably about the monsoon forecast, the quality of the river fish, the tragedy of the missing survey team — "dreadful business, Voss was competent but headstrong, took risks" — each remark delivered like a gift and landing like a measurement. He was gauging her. How much she knew. What kind of person she was. Whether she could be flattered, frightened, or hired.

At the inn's porch he paused. "You know, Miss Oduya, a cartographer of your caliber is wasted on archival dust. The Concession Office maintains its own mapping division. Better funding than the ministry. Better equipment." He produced a card from somewhere with a small flourish. "If you ever tire of other people's errors, my door is open."

"That's generous," Wren said.

"It's practical. Good maps are the foundation of good order." He tipped an imaginary hat toward her. "Do enjoy Corren's Landing. It's lovelier in the dry season — assuming we get one."

And he strolled off down the street, pausing to exchange greetings with a shopkeeper, patting a child on the head, a beloved public figure making his evening rounds while the light went gold behind him.

Wren stood on the porch holding his card and feeling, distinctly, like a mouse that had been invited to dinner by the cat's personal secretary.

Inside, Silas looked up from cleaning his boots. One glance at her face and he set them down. "You found something."

"I found seven impossible documents, one nonexistent survey, and a job offer." She dropped onto the bench beside him and slid her notes across. "Reeve knows I was in the archives within hours. Possibly minutes. Either the clerk reports to him or the office does, and I couldn't tell which, which frightens me more."

Naya, draped across a chair with her boots on the windowsill, had gone very still — the stillness Wren had learned to read as attention. "He offered you a job?"

"He offered me better funding and better equipment. Which is how it works. First they offer you money. If you refuse, they offer you consequences."

"So we run," Naya said. "We have the dates. We go to the capital, we—"

"We go to the capital with a stack of arithmetic," Wren said, "and Reeve owns the men who'd receive it. Dates can be explained away. Clerks can be reassigned. I can be discredited by lunchtime." She rubbed her eyes. "Citations prove the permits are forged. But we need to know *why* — what's underneath the land they were so desperate to own on paper. Voss's team was doing hydrographic work. Water surveys. And then they vanished without tracks."

Silas had been reading her notes with the focused patience of a man who trusted maps more than words. Now he tapped the ninth plat — the one covering the drowned canyons, the deep interior. "This corridor here. The routes, the wells, all of it folded into one concession. That's not grazing land. There's nothing out there worth permitting."

"Unless there is," Wren said quietly. "Unless the reason the maps were forged is the same reason the survey team disappeared. The land itself. Whatever's under it."

The lamplight flickered. Outside, far off toward the flats, thunder muttered — the monsoon clearing its throat, weeks early. Naya swung her boots down and leaned forward, and for once the irony had drained out of her face entirely.

"My brother's last letter said they'd found something the water knew about," she said. "I thought it was poetry. He wrote a lot of poetry." Her voice caught, steadied. "It wasn't poetry, was it."

"No," Wren said. "I don't think it was."

Silas was already reaching for his pack. "Voss followed the old routes," he said. "Naya — your brother guided for her. Do you know which ones?"

Naya looked at him, and Wren watched something pass between them — grief recognizing grief, purpose recognizing purpose.

"I know every route he ever walked," Naya said. "Give me a map."

Wren spread the ninth plat on the table between them, the forged lines and stolen corridors glowing in the lamplight, and outside the storm rolled closer over the salt, coming early, coming fast, as if the land itself had heard what they'd found and meant to keep it.
