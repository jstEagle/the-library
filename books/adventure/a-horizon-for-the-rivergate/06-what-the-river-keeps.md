## 6. What the River Keeps

The route her brother left behind was not written. It was sung.

Naya hummed it under her breath as we walked the eastern rim of the basin, a low looping melody that rose when the ground rose and fell when the ground fell, and when I finally asked her what the tune was, she looked at me like I'd asked her why water was wet.

"It's a wayfinding song," she said. "Every nomad family has them. You don't write a route down because paper burns, and ink runs, and soldiers read. You sing it. The melody is the contour. The words are the water."

"The words are the water," Wren repeated, scribbling in her notebook, and I could see the cartographer in her having a small religious crisis. Nine years I'd watched government surveyors measure the Rivergate with chains and theodolites and an air of enormous confidence, and here was a woman in a patched coat carrying a map in her throat that was older than the concession office, older than the authority, older probably than the capital itself.

"Sing it again," Wren said.

"I'll sing it when we get there," Naya said. "Not before. My brother didn't teach it to strangers, and I'm not starting the tradition for two people I met through theft."

"I caught you," I said.

"You caught me once," she said. "Statistically, that's nothing."

We had been walking since before dawn, three days out from Corren's Landing, following the song's logic through country that looked, to my ranger-trained eye, like nothing at all. Salt pan giving way to hardpan, hardpan to scrub, scrub to the first shy green of the river's far reach. No trail. No cairns. Nothing a permit map would bother to record, because there was nothing here the permits valued.

That was the thought I kept circling, the way a tongue finds a cracked tooth. If there was nothing here, why had Naya's brother walked this route with a survey team? Why had Ilsa Voss's journal—the pages Wren had copied in Corren's Landing—referred to "the third listening" and "the place where the ground drinks"?

"The ground drinks," I said aloud, mostly to myself.

"What?" Wren said.

"Voss's journal. Page nine. *The ground drinks faster than the sky pours. Either the numbers are wrong or the basin is a cup.*" I'd read that line a dozen times in the last two days, and it had sat in my head like a splinter, working deeper every night.

Naya had stopped walking. She was standing with her head cocked, listening to something I couldn't hear, and after a moment I realized she was listening to herself—to the song, to the words inside the melody—and the words had brought her up short.

"This is the verse about the hollow stone," she said slowly. "My brother used to sing this one and laugh. I always thought it was a joke verse." She looked at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—not grief, exactly, but grief's cousin, the one that arrives when the dead turn out to have been right. "It's not a joke verse."

"How do you know?"

"Because the song says the hollow stone is a day's walk from the leaning fig, and there's the fig." She pointed.

I looked. There was, in fact, a fig tree—leaning, as advertised, at an angle that suggested it had spent its whole life eavesdropping on the ground and slowly given up standing straight. It grew out of a crack in a low ridge of limestone, the only tree in sight, green as an argument in all that pale country.

And beneath it, half-buried in the salt crust, was a stone with a hole in it.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the one habit nine years of ranger work never managed to beat out of me.

I expected a smuggler's cache. That's what I told myself we were walking toward—a dead drop, a box of papers, maybe a rifle wrapped in oilcloth. I'd found a dozen of them over the years, left by salt runners and permit dodgers, and I knew the grammar of them: buried deep, marked subtle, protected by nothing but the country's own indifference.

This was not that.

The hollow stone wasn't a marker. It was a door. Naya pulled it free—it weighed less than it looked, hollow as a drum—and beneath it was a shaft lined with clay jars, sealed with wax and pitch, and the topmost jar was full not of grain or gunpowder but of paper. Rolled, oiled, wrapped in waxed linen, dozens upon dozens of them, packed down among the lower jars like a nest of sleeping snakes.

Wren made a sound I had never heard a government cartographer make. It was close to a sob.

"Voss," she whispered. "These are Voss's originals. These are—" She had a roll unwrapped before I could tell her to be careful with her hands, and then she stopped talking altogether, which for Wren Oduya was the equivalent of a grown man weeping in the street.

I crouched beside her and looked over her shoulder, and that was when the awe arrived, and right behind it, quiet as a knife coming out of a boot, the fear.

The map she'd unrolled was a cross-section. I know cross-sections; every ranger does, because you can't chase anyone through country you don't understand, and understanding means knowing what's under the ground as well as on it. This one showed the Rivergate Basin the way a butcher shows a side of beef—sliced open, layer by layer, from the salt crust down. And beneath the salt, beneath the hardpan, beneath the clay, drawn in Voss's precise, impatient hand, was water.

Not a river. Not a spring. A sea of it. A lens of fresh water lying under the whole basin like a second sky turned upside down, fed by the monsoon rivers, held by the limestone, vast enough—I could read the figures along the margin, and I read them three times to be sure—to drink a million people for a century.

The aquifer. The whole basin, sitting on top of it, the way a sleeper lies on a fortune she doesn't know is under her bed.

"They knew," Wren said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when the alternative is shaking. "The concession office knew. Look at the date—this survey is four years old. Four years. They drew the permit boundaries *on top of this*." She spread another roll beside the first, one of the concession maps she'd pulled in Corren's Landing, and laid them edge to edge. The boundaries matched. Not approximately. Exactly. Every concession block sat precisely over the richest part of the lens, like a fence built around a well while the well's owner slept.

"They weren't selling grazing rights," I said. "Or salt. Or river access."

"They were selling the water," Naya said, "without telling anyone there was water." She was very still. "The nomad routes cross every block on this map. Every single one. My family's route, the Ferro route, the Saltwise people—" She stopped, and I watched her do the arithmetic I was doing: the permits weren't careless. They weren't bureaucratic greed or the usual fat-fingered theft of the frontier. They were a land grab of a kind I'd never seen, dressed up as paperwork, and every nomad family in the basin had been moved off the top of the treasure without ever being told the treasure existed.

"The ground drinks faster than the sky pours," I said. "Voss figured it out. The basin's a cup, and the government knew it, and the permits were the lid."

We sat there a long moment, three people crouched around a hole in the ground, holding the most dangerous paper in the Rivergate.

* * *

Here's a thing about fear that the ranger academy never taught me, though nine years of fieldwork did: it doesn't always arrive shouting. Sometimes it arrives as arithmetic.

I sat on the leaning fig's roots while Wren catalogued the cache—she couldn't help it, and honestly I didn't want her to, because if we lost any of this paper I wanted to know exactly what we'd lost—and I did the sums.

Voss had found this. Voss had hidden it here, in jars, under a singing stone, along a route only nomads walked. Which meant Voss had known, when she hid it, that the concession office couldn't be trusted. Which meant someone in the concession office had given her reason to know. And a team of six trained surveyors did not hide their life's work in a clay jar and then simply fail to come back for it unless coming back had become the most dangerous thing they could do.

"They didn't vanish," I said. "They were erased."

"Then why leave the maps?" Naya asked. She'd climbed the ridge and was scanning the flats behind us with the particular patience of someone who had spent her life watching for riders. "If Voss was hiding, why not take them?"

"Insurance," Wren said, without looking up. "You hide the original so that killing you doesn't settle anything. It's standard practice in disputed surveys. You leave a dead man's switch." She paused. "Except she never got to tell anyone where the switch was."

"Her guide did," Naya said quietly. "My brother. He's the one who knew this route. He's the one who could have carried word to the families." She didn't say the rest. She didn't have to. Whatever had happened to the team, it had happened between the hollow stone and the drowned canyons, and it had happened to her brother, and she had walked three days to stand over his last handiwork, and she was holding herself together the way you hold a full jar—carefully, and with both hands, and knowing that eventually something has to give.

I went up the ridge and stood next to her. I'm not good at comfort. My whole career was built on the principle that stopping costs more than moving, and comfort is nothing but stopping, turning around, sitting down beside a person in the middle of the road. But I stood next to her, and after a while she leaned, very slightly, so that her shoulder touched mine, and that seemed to be the correct amount.

"Silas," she said. "If this is what I think it is—if the government's been sitting on a sea of water under our routes—then the people who did this won't let three nobodies walk it into the capital. You know that."

"I know that."

"They'll send riders."

"I'd be disappointed if they didn't."

She looked at me sideways. "Is that a joke?"

"It's a ranger's way of saying yes."

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of the ridge, no fire, cold food, the maps redistributed between the three of our packs so that no single capture could take them all. Wren worked by the light of a shuttered lamp, cross-referencing, muttering, occasionally emitting small outraged noises like a kettle deciding whether to boil.

"The figures," she said at last. "Silas, the figures. Voss measured the recharge rate. The monsoon rivers don't just flood the basin—they *feed* it. The whole flood cycle, the one the dam works are supposed to control, the one the concession office uses to justify everything—" She looked up, and the lamplight made her look younger and angrier at the same time. "They don't want to control the floods. They want to *own* the refill. Every permit, every boundary, every displaced family—it's all plumbing. We've been reading it as land theft. It's a well theft."

"And the dam works," I said, "are the pump handle."

"And anyone who knows," Naya said, "is a leak."

I lay back and looked at the stars, which out there, away from the towns, are thick as spilled grain, and I thought about the word *leak*, and about how a man who has spent nine years plugging small leaks for a government that was itself the flood feels when he finally sees the shape of the thing he's been protecting.

I thought about my badge, back at my camp under a stone, and how light it had always felt in my hand, and how heavy it felt now, three days' walk away.

"We move at first light," I said. "Downriver. Odell Brack's water, if we can reach it—he knows the drowned canyons, and if Voss's team went anywhere after the hollow stone, it was there." I sat up. "And we travel like we're carrying what we're carrying. Because we are. This isn't a box of papers anymore. It's the basin itself, folded up small enough to steal."

Naya was quiet a moment. Then she began to hum—softly, the wayfinding song, the verse about the hollow stone—and this time she sang the words out loud, and I heard them properly for the first time:

*The river keeps what kings forget,*
*the stone remembers what the paper burns,*
*carry the water in your chest,*
*the land will take back what it learns.*

Wren had stopped writing. "How old is that song?" she asked.

"Older than the government," Naya said. "Maybe the nomads knew the water was there all along. Maybe they always sang about it, and everyone thought it was poetry." She smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile, but it was a fierce one. "It was a map. It was always a map."

It was very late, and very dark, and very far from anywhere, when I heard it—a sound I knew from nine years of night work, a sound I had made myself on a hundred quiet approaches and so knew the way you know your own footsteps.

Hooves. Many of them. Moving fast, out on the salt, toward the ridge.

I put my hand on Naya's shoulder before she could hum another note, and in the starlight I saw her eyes change, and I saw Wren's hand close around the maps, and somewhere out in the dark the riders came on, and I understood, with a calm that surprised me, that the arithmetic had finished.

They had sent the riders.
