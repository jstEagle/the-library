## 7. Riders on the Flats

The trouble with dusk in the Rivergate Basin, Silas had always told the new rangers, was that it lied to you. The sun went down behind the salt hills and the whole world turned the color of a bruise healing—purple, gold, that soft pink that made everything look gentler than it was. A man could look out at a flat that had been empty all afternoon and swear it was still empty, when in fact it had been filling up with trouble for the better part of an hour.

He was thinking about explaining this to Wren when he saw the dust.

They had made camp on a low shelf of salt crust at the edge of the flats, close enough to the river channel to hear it muttering in the distance. The channel was running high—higher than it should have been, three weeks ahead of any honest monsoon—and the sound of it carried across the salt like a rumor. Wren had spread Voss's maps out on the boat's hull, weighting the corners with stones, and was tracing the aquifer contours with one finger, her lips moving as she did arithmetic the land itself had been hiding for years. Naya was up on the shelf with Silas's battered spyglass, ostensibly keeping watch, actually eating the last of the dried figs she had sworn she hadn't taken from his pack.

"Riders," Naya said, around a mouthful of fig she had definitely not been given permission to eat. "North. Four of them. Maybe five."

Silas took the spyglass. The light was bad, which was a mercy and a problem both. He could make out shapes against the last glow of the sky—mounted, moving fast, and moving wrong. Nomads crossed open salt in loose lines, letting the land breathe around them. These came in a wedge, tight and purposeful, the way men moved when someone was paying them by the hour.

"Concession riders," he said. "Reeve's."

"You're sure?"

"Nomads ride like they're visiting. Those ride like they're collecting." He handed the glass back and started folding the maps with a care that surprised even him. Nine years of small crimes and petty debts, and here he was handling paper like it was made of eggshell. Well. It was, in a way. It was made of the truth, and the truth had turned out to be the rarest thing in the basin. "Wren. We're leaving."

Wren looked up, and he watched her do the same arithmetic he'd done—the distance, the light, the boat sitting in plain view like a beetle on a plate. "How long?"

"Ten minutes if they've seen us. Twenty if they haven't. They've seen us."

"Then we take the boat down the channel," Wren said, already rolling the maps into their oilcloth. "The water's high enough."

"The water's *high enough*," Silas repeated. "The channel's running three weeks early. That's not high enough, that's a river deciding it owns the calendar. Nobody sane runs the channel at night in a boat with a patched hull."

"Nobody sane is carrying those maps, either." Wren's voice was steady, but he saw her hand press flat against the oilcloth, the way you'd press a wound. "Silas. They didn't come to talk. Look at them."

He looked. The wedge had split—one rider peeling wide to the east, flanking. That was the move of a man who knew the ground and knew his quarry couldn't outrun him in a straight line. It was also the move of a man who had been told, in no uncertain terms, that there were to be no witnesses.

"Down," Silas said. "Both of you. Naya, the boat. Wren, the maps stay on your body, not in the boat. If we get separated, the maps go with whoever's breathing."

"That's a grim way to assign custody," Wren said, but she was already moving.

"I'm a grim man with a grim assignment. It's why they pay me nothing and respect me less."

Naya snorted as she slid down the shelf, and Silas felt something in his chest loosen slightly—just slightly—because if she was joking, then her hands weren't shaking. He'd learned to read fear the way other men read weather. Naya's fear came out sideways, as jokes and theft. Wren's came out as precision. His own came out, these days, as an ache behind the eyes and a stubborn refusal to sit down.

They got the boat to the channel in six minutes, dragging it over crust that cracked like old porcelain underfoot. The river had come up over its banks and was sliding brown and muscular through the channel, carrying sticks, foam, and one extremely surprised marsh bird. Silas got them aboard—Wren amidships with the maps strapped across her chest under her coat, Naya at the stern oar because she claimed, with some justice, that she was the only one of them who could row in a straight line when the world was ending.

The riders came over the shelf as Silas pushed off.

The first shot cracked across the salt, and the sound of it was enormous in all that emptiness—like the sky clearing its throat. The bullet hit the water a boat-length short, throwing up a white feather of spray.

"Down!" Silas shoved Wren flat in the bilge and took the bow oar himself. "Naya—midstream, as hard as you've got. Don't fight the current, *use* it. We ride it down to the bend."

"The bend is flooded," Naya said, rowing.

"The bend is *flooded*," Silas agreed. "Everything is flooded. That's the plan. Our plan is to be flooded."

"This is the worst plan—"

Another shot. This one hit the hull with a sound like a knuckle rapping a door, and Silas felt the boat shudder and knew, in the tired, professional way he knew most unpleasant things, that it had gone through and out again. They were taking on water. Of course they were. The basin had never once let him leave a scene without a complication, and it wasn't going to start being generous now.

The channel took them. That was the strange mercy of it—once the current had hold of the boat, the riders lost their clean shot, because the river bent and the banks grew up in reeds and dead tamarisk, and the dusk closed in around them like a hand. Silas rowed until his shoulders screamed, then kept rowing, because the alternative was drifting, and drifting was dying with better posture.

Behind them, faint and fading, he could hear hoofbeats along the bank. The riders weren't giving up. They were keeping pace, waiting for the channel to slow or shallow or spit them out somewhere convenient.

"They're following along the bank," Naya called.

"I have ears, yes."

"They're *keeping pace*, Silas."

"Then we'll have to be more interesting than they expect." He risked a look back. Wren was sitting up now, one arm around the maps, her face pale in the last light. "Wren. How's the hull?"

"Taking on water faster than I can bail," she reported. "But slower than I can worry, so we're balanced."

* * *

Full dark came down, and with it the rain.

It didn't start so much as arrive—the first drops fat and warm and scattered, then suddenly the sky simply gave up holding anything back. The monsoon, which by every almanac Silas had ever trusted was still three weeks out, came down on the basin like a debt collector. The channel swelled as he watched it, brown water climbing the reeds, the current going from muscular to furious. Somewhere upstream, whole watersheds were emptying themselves into the basin at once.

The boat was sinking. Slowly, steadily, in the manner of all honest failures.

Silas bailed with a tin pan while Naya rowed and Wren—Wren did the thing that made him, later, unable to sleep for a different reason than usual. She got the maps out from under her coat, unwrapped them from the oilcloth, and wrapped them instead around her own torso, binding them tight with the cloth itself, so that the paper lay against her skin under her shirt, and the oilcloth went over the top as a second skin.

"If we swim," she said, "the maps swim with me."

"The maps will drown you," Silas said. "Wet paper weighs like a stone. You go under, you'll never come back up."

"Then I'll come back up anyway." She said it simply, without drama, the way she said everything, and went back to bailing with one hand while the other held the boat's gunwale against the current's shoves.

The bank riders had fallen behind in the rain—or been lost, or given up—but Silas didn't trust it. He'd spent nine years learning how men quit, and men like these didn't quit. They *redeployed*. There would be a ford downstream, or a bend where the channel slowed, and they'd be waiting, and the boat would come to them like a parcel.

"We can't run the channel all night," he said. "They'll pick a spot ahead and be patient. Patience is the one thing they're paid for."

"So what do we do?" Naya shouted over the rain.

Silas looked at the water. It was the color of strong tea, churning with debris, and it was rising, and somewhere out in that rising dark was the drowned canyon country—the old canyons the dam works had swallowed, where the river now wandered through country that used to be dry land, full of drowned trees and drowned fences and drowned everything, waiting under the flood like furniture in a house where nobody lived anymore.

"We get off the channel," he said. "Before the bend. There's an old overflow cut on the west bank—my grandfather ran cattle through it. It'll be flooded now, but it's shallow flooding, over old ground. The riders can't follow through drowned country in the dark. They don't know it. I do."

"You *knew* it," Naya said. "Before it was underwater."

"Everything I knew is underwater. That's rather the theme of the season." He pointed with the bail pan. "There. See where the reeds break? That's the cut. Naya—hard to port, on my mark. Wren—hold onto something that's attached to the boat, and when we hit the shallow water, be ready to get out and *pull*. We're going to run her aground on purpose."

"On purpose," Wren repeated.

"I'm told that's the difference between shipwreck and navigation."

The boat swung for the cut. For one long moment the current fought them—the whole brown weight of the river objecting to being robbed of a passenger—and then they were through, into shallower water that still pulled and sucked but no longer commanded. The boat scraped over something that had once been a fence post, then something that had once been a wall, and then they grounded, hard, in waist-deep floodwater where an orchard had been, the drowned trees standing around them black and patient in the rain.

Silas got them out and they hauled the boat up onto the roof of what had been, in another life, a barn. Only the roof—everything else was under the flood, and the rain hammered down on the shingles as if demanding entry.

They stood there in the dark, three soaked people on a drowned barn roof in the middle of a flooded orchard, and Silas started laughing before he could stop himself. It wasn't good laughter. It was the laughter of a man who has just realized the size of the thing chasing him.

"They'll figure out where we went by morning," he said. "They'll know this country eventually—Reeve's people buy their maps, they don't learn them. But eventually is enough. We can't go back to the channel. We can't go back across the flats. And the boat—" he put his boot through the soft spot in the roof to make his point, and his boot went through to the water below— "the boat is a roof now. We have a roof. That's the good news."

"What's the bad news?" Naya asked.

"The bad news is that everything between here and the capital is drowned canyon country, the rain hasn't even *started* properly yet, and the only person I know who can read this flooded land is a hermit fisherman named Odell Brack who owes me a debt and is going to be *insufferable* about it."

Wren sat down on the wet shingles, maps still bound to her chest, and looked out at the black water standing in the drowned orchard. Lightning walked along the horizon, far off, and in its light Silas saw her face—pale, set, and underneath the exhaustion, something fierce and unextinguished.

"Silas," she said. "The riders on the bank. When they flanked us. Did you see their faces?"

"Some."

"One of them. The big one on the gray horse. He had a ranger's coat on. Old pattern, but a ranger's coat."

Silas went very still. He thought about the wedge formation, the flanking rider, the patience. He thought about a warrant with his name on it that he hadn't seen yet, and wouldn't see for days. He thought about nine years of small debts, and what it cost, finally, to stop.

"That would be Harl Voss's old coat," he heard himself say, before he could stop the words. "No. Not Voss. That's—Ilsa's brother-in-arms, from her survey. The one who sold them out." He rubbed rain from his eyes. "Which means Reeve didn't just send hired men. He sent the man who knows exactly what those maps look like and exactly who'd be carrying them."

Naya pulled her knees up to her chin, small and soaked and unafraid in the way that only genuinely frightened people can manage. "So he knows it's us."

"He's known since Corren's Landing," Silas said. "We just made it official."

The rain came down harder. Somewhere out in the drowned orchard, a tree groaned and settled deeper into its grave. Silas looked east, toward the drowned canyons and the man who knew them, and then west, toward the capital and the courts that Reeve owned, and understood with a tired, perfect clarity that the season had turned—not just the weather, everything. The dry years of small crimes and small debts were over. The flood was here, and they were in it, and the only way out was through.

"We move at first light," he said. "Odell Brack's camp is a day's walk through the shallows, if the shallows are feeling charitable. Wren—that coat stays against your skin. Naya—"

"I know, I know. Watch the north."

"Watch the water," Silas corrected. "From now on, the water's the only road. And everybody on it is either drowning or hunting."

* * *

They did not sleep, exactly. They dozed in shifts on the barn roof, which was less sleeping than a series of agreements to stop being afraid at the same time. Silas took the middle watch, as he always did, because he had never in his life trusted anyone else with the hours when the world was at its most honest.

It was on the middle watch, with the rain gone soft and the lightning far away, that he heard the shot.

One shot, far off to the east, back toward the channel. Then nothing. He lay flat on the wet shingles and listened until his ears ached, but the night gave him nothing more—no hoofbeats, no voices, no second shot. Just the water talking to itself in the drowned orchard, and the rain, patient now, settling in for a long stay.

He didn't wake the others. There was nothing to wake them for, and everything to wake them *from*. But he lay there with his hand on the old service compass in his pocket—the one Naya had stolen from him and he had taken back, the one that pointed, sometimes, somewhere that shouldn't exist—and he thought about a man in a ranger's coat riding a gray horse, and about how the basin had a way of collecting on every debt at once.

First light came gray and drowned. They ate the last of the figs—Naya admitted, under interrogation, that there had been more figs—and waded out into the shallows, hauling the dead boat behind them as a raft, because Silas refused to leave anything behind that could float. The water was warm as blood and rose, as they walked, from waist to chest.

By noon they could see, far off across the flooded flats, a smudge of dust that was not weather. Riders. Five of them now. Moving not toward them, but ahead of them—cutting the angle toward the drowned canyon country, toward the only road left.

Toward Odell Brack's camp.

"They're not chasing us anymore," Wren said quietly, maps bound to her chest, water to her ribs. "They're herding us."

Silas watched the dust settle over the drowned land and felt the last of the dry years wash off him for good. "No," he said. "They're racing us. And they don't know the water." He tightened his grip on the raft line and set his face toward the canyons. "Hold on to the maps, Wren. Naya—stay close. We're going to meet a hermit, and God help us all, he's going to be right about everything."

Behind them, the rain came on again, harder, and the season turned another degree toward the flood.
