## 8. Leaking Hull, Rising Water

The boat had been dying for hours before any of us admitted it.

I noticed first, because I notice things—it's the thief in me, the part that reads a room by its exits and a crowd by its pockets. The bilge water had climbed past my ankles while we bailed, and it wasn't going down anymore. It was coming up to meet us. Somewhere under the floorboards, the river had found a way in, and it was patient about it, the way rivers always are.

"Silas." I said his name quietly, because Wren was finally asleep, curled against the supply crates with her bandaged shoulder rising and falling too fast. "We have a problem."

He looked at me across the lantern light, and I watched him do the thing he does—take inventory of my face before he took inventory of anything else. Checking whether I was panicking. Deciding how much panic the situation deserved.

"How much water?"

"Enough."

That was all he needed. He didn't argue, didn't ask me to be more precise, which is one of the maybe four things I like about him. He just pulled up the floorboard nearest the stern and put his hand into the dark underneath, and when he brought it back out it was dripping, and there was a sound now that we could all hear even over the rain—a wet, sucking groan from the hull, like the boat clearing its throat before it said something we wouldn't like.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't wait for a convenient moment. It doesn't schedule itself around your emergencies. It sits in your chest like ballast, quiet for days, and then the moment you are trapped on a flooding river with a holed boat and a wounded woman sleeping three feet away, it chooses *that* night to start talking.

My brother Tam's name was on the letter. That's where this started, though I didn't know it yet. Tam, who could find water in salt flat with nothing but a bent wire and a squint. Tam, who taught me to pick a lock with a fishhook when I was nine because he said every skill is a door and doors should never be closed to you forever. Tam, who went off guiding for a government survey team with a handshake deal and came back as nothing at all—no body, no tracks, no word. Just gone, the way things go in the Rivergate, quietly, between one season and the next.

And then Silas Pemberton caught me stealing a compass that pointed somewhere that shouldn't exist, and it turned out the compass was the only honest thing left of him.

The river rose around us. We bailed.

* * *

The patch job was Silas's idea, because everything practical is Silas's idea and everything reckless is mine and somehow we keep needing both. He found the breach by candlelight—hairline split in a seam near the keel, opened wider by the strain of running the channel at dusk with enforcers shooting at us, thank you very much—and he wedged a rag into it with the tenderness of a man dressing a wound.

"That's not going to hold," I told him.

"It's going to hold until morning."

"And after morning?"

"After morning," he said, pressing the rag deeper, "we'll want something better than a rag." He glanced at the crates lashed amidships. "Melt some of that pitch. And wake Wren gently. She'll want to help."

Wren Oduya, who had taken an enforcer's bullet graze meant for the maps, who had not complained once, who was currently asleep sitting up like a camel. I crouched beside her and touched her good shoulder and she came awake instantly, eyes sharp, hand already moving toward the maps before her mind caught up with where she was. Even half-conscious, her first thought was the maps. I decided in that moment that if she died on this river I would burn down whatever was left of Aldous Reeve's concession office myself, personally, with my own two hands, and enjoy it.

"Leak?" she asked.

"Leak," I confirmed.

"Bad?"

"Manageable," Silas said, which from Silas means bad but survivable, and which from anyone else would mean catastrophic.

So we worked. Wren held the lantern steady with her uninjured arm and read the hull like she reads terrain, tracing seams with her fingers, calling out soft spots. I melted pitch over the little spirit stove and tried not to set us all on fire, which is harder than it sounds when the whole world is already wet. Silas pressed the hot pitch into the seam and held it there while it cooled, his hands in the black water, his face doing that thing where it shows absolutely nothing, which I have come to understand is its own kind of screaming.

The rain came down like the sky had a grudge. The river ran brown and fast around us, carrying branches, carrying fences, carrying somebody's roof from somewhere upstream. Twice we heard the deep boom of debris hitting rock below the surface, and each time we all went still, listening, the way you listen for a predator in the grass.

It was during the second wait, in that held-breath silence, that the ballast in my chest shifted.

"I have to tell you both something," I said.

They looked at me. The lantern guttered. Somewhere behind us, miles back, were men with rifles and warrants, and ahead of us was a drowned canyon country full of submerged trees and old dead channels, and I chose that exact moment to open my mouth, because apparently I am my brother's sister after all and we both have flawless timing.

"Tam sent me a letter," I said. "Before the survey. Before he vanished. It came to the market drop—we used a spice seller on the river road, he'd hold post for us when either of us was moving around." My voice did something strange in the middle of the sentence, cracked sideways, and I hated it and kept going. "I never opened it. I was—I don't know what I was. Busy. Angry at him for taking government work. There was a boy in Corren's Landing I liked better than I liked staying home, and I thought, oh, it's Tam, it's just Tam, he writes me letters all the time, it'll keep."

The rain filled the pause. The river filled the pause. Nothing else did.

"When they vanished, I went back for it. Opened it by lamplight in the spice seller's back room." I made myself look at them. Both of them. They deserved to see my face while I said it. "He knew, Silas. He *knew*. He wrote that the maps didn't match the land. That the survey was being steered, that someone above Voss wanted certain readings lost. He wrote that if anything happened to them, I shouldn't come looking—that I should take the compass and go north and forget the basin." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Forget the basin. Like that's a thing a person can do."

Wren reached over and put her hand on my wrist. Just that. Her grip was cool and steady and it anchored me better than any anchor on this leaking tub.

"If I'd opened it when it came," I said, "if I'd read it that same day instead of letting it sit under a sack of cardamom for three weeks—" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "Maybe I couldn't have saved them. Maybe. But I'd have known where to look sooner. I'd have known the compass mattered. Instead I stole it blind, off a ranger, with no plan, because I was chasing a ghost I'd already been warned about and ignored." I looked at Silas. "You spent nine years catching people like me. You should've turned me in at the market. You should have."

"No," Silas said.

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it," he said, and almost smiled, which for Silas is a parade. "For about a second. Then I remembered I'm a better judge of character than my warrant office is." He wrung out the rag in his hand, matter-of-fact, like we were discussing weather. "You didn't kill your brother, Naya. Men like Reeve killed your brother. You're the only one still trying to bring him home. That's not a debt. That's a job. And you're good at it."

I turned away so neither of them would see what my face did next, and pretended to check the pitch patch, and it needed checking anyway, so it wasn't entirely a lie.

* * *

The night got worse, because nights on the Rivergate during early monsoon have a competitive streak.

Around midnight the wind swung and started shoving us sideways toward the cutbank, and Silas and I poled off the mud while Wren, feverish and gray-faced, kept the bow pointed into the current by main strength and stubbornness. Around two, the pitch patch began to lift—the hull flexing in the chop, working the seam like a jaw working a gristle—and we re-melted and re-pressed it by feel, nearly blind, swearing in three languages between us. Around three, Wren fainted, quietly, mid-sentence, the way she does everything: efficiently, without fuss, and with evident annoyance at herself.

We laid her against the crates. Silas checked her wound with the lantern low. The bandage was seeping but not soaked. Fever, he said. Needs proper rest, dry clothes, and medicine, none of which the river was offering.

"She shouldn't have taken that shot for the maps," I said.

"She'd say the maps were worth it."

"The maps aren't worth *her*."

Silas was quiet a moment. Rain drummed the canopy. "No," he agreed. "But try telling her that. She'll draw you a diagram proving otherwise. With citations."

Despite everything—despite the leak and the fever and the fear sitting in my stomach like swallowed stone—I laughed. A real laugh, small and surprised, bubbling up out of the muck of the night. And I saw Silas hear it, and register it, and file it away somewhere, the way he files everything.

"Do you ever stop working?" I asked him. "Even in your head?"

"No," he said. "Working is easier than stopping."

"Why?"

He fed another stick to the spirit stove. The flame steadied. "Because when I stop," he said, "I have to be somewhere. And being somewhere means being someone, and I've spent nine years making sure I was only ever the man who was passing through." He glanced at me. "You know exactly what I mean. You've been running since Tam disappeared."

"I'm not running. I'm hunting."

"In the same direction, fast enough that nobody can tell the difference." He said it without cruelty. That's the trick of Silas Pemberton—he can say the truest, sharpest thing in the world and make it sound like a report on rainfall. "Tonight you stopped running. You sat down in a sinking boat and told the truth to two people. That's not nothing."

I didn't answer. But I moved closer to Wren, and tucked the blanket higher around her shoulders, and stayed there.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and with it, the discovery that the patch had held but barely, and that we had drifted farther than intended—down past the burned ferry pilings, into water I didn't recognize and Silas only half did.

"We're below the bend," he said, studying the drowned treeline with narrowed eyes. "Old country. This was canyon before the dam works backed the river up. Now it's..." He gestured at the flooded forest, the rooftops of dead hills showing here and there as muddy islands, the tops of trees standing in twenty feet of water like drowned men reaching up.

"Someone lives out here," I said. I'd seen it in the grey light and almost missed it: a thread of smoke, thin and deliberate, rising off a shelf of rock where the old canyon wall broke the surface. Not a campfire smoke. A chimney smoke. Someone lived here the way hermits live anywhere—on purpose, and wanting to be left alone.

Silas followed my gaze, and something crossed his face that I hadn't seen before. Recognition, yes—but also something older and heavier underneath it. Debt, maybe. Or memory.

"Odell Brack," he said slowly, as if the name cost him something to say aloud. "Fisherman. Hermit. Knows these drowned canyons better than the river does." He was quiet a moment. "He also knows these waters well enough to tell us where a survey team could hide and stay hidden. And I owe him a debt I've never paid."

"You? Owe someone?" I grinned despite myself. "I thought you were self-sufficient."

"I'm self-sufficient the way this boat is watertight," Silas said, and put his shoulder to the pole, and turned our leaking, patched, exhausted, impossible little vessel toward the smoke.
