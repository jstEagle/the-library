## 9. The Stranger at Drowned Bend

Odell Brack had lived at Drowned Bend long enough to know the river's moods the way a man knows his wife's—by the sound of her before he ever saw her.

So when the water started talking wrong at half past dark, he set down his mending and listened.

The monsoon had come early and rude this year, three weeks ahead of itself, and the river had spent the last two days swallowing everything the concession office had built too close to its banks: a fence line, a survey stake, a stack of permit signage that Odell had watched float past with more pleasure than a man ought to admit to. The water was brown and muscular and full of opinions. But tonight it had a new note in it. A knock. A soft, splintering knock, like a fist on a door made of driftwood.

He stepped out onto his porch, a crooked thing built from a boat that had failed him decades ago, and shaded his eyes against the rain. The lamp he kept burning in the window—the one the enforcers had told him twice now to douse, because a light on the water invited traffic—threw a weak yellow cone into the dark.

And there, riding the current sideways like a drunk leaving a funeral, came a boat.

He heard it before he saw it properly: the hollow slap of a hull taking water, the clatter of someone bailing with anything that would hold, and—cutting through all of it—a woman's voice, flat and furious, saying what sounded like, "I *told* you the patch would hold, I never said it would hold *well*."

The boat struck his landing stake with a crunch that Odell felt in his back teeth. Then it swung around, hung there a moment as if considering, and began to sink with the slow dignity of a thing that had made its decision and saw no reason to rush.

"Ah," said Odell Brack, and went to get the rope.

* * *

They came up his bank one at a time, the way drowning things do.

First was a young woman, thin as a whip handle, hauling a soaked canvas satchel she clearly valued more than her own breathing. Then a taller woman, olive-skinned, moving with the careful stiffness of somebody hurt and pretending not to be, one arm pressed against her ribs where a bandage had gone dark. Last came a man, big through the shoulders, gray at the temples, who came up the bank last because he was pushing the wreck ahead of him to keep it from washing off entirely—and who, when the lamplight finally found his face, made Odell Brack stop dead in the rain with a rope half-coiled in his hands.

"Well," Odell said. "I'll be damned."

Silas Pemberton looked at him the way a man looks at a door he'd hoped was painted on. "Hello, Odell."

"Hello, Odell," the hermit repeated, savoring it. "Nine years. Nine years, and that's the greeting. Not *Odell, you old river-rat, you're looking well*. Not *thank you, Odell, for hauling my sorry hide out of the water*. Just *hello*." He turned and bellowed up the slope, "Marta! Heat water! And the good blanket, not the one the moths got!"

An answering voice came from inside the stilt-house, unhurried, unimpressed. "We only have the one blanket, Odell."

"Then get the moths' blanket!"

The young thief—the satchel one—had stopped on the bank and gone very still, her eyes moving from Odell to Silas and back with the quick arithmetic of someone recalculating a debt. "You *know* him."

"He pulled me out of the channel at Sallow Reach," Silas said. His voice had gone carefully neutral, the way it did, Odell noticed, whenever anything true was about to happen. "Nine years ago. I was chasing him, actually."

"You were *what*?"

"Smuggling," Odell said cheerfully, waving them up the slope. "Medicine, mostly, for the nomad camps when the permits first started squeezing. Your ranger friend here ran me down in a storm, my boat swamped, and instead of arresting me he held my head above water for two hours and lied to his report about how he found me." He clapped Silas on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him. "Owed him a debt ever since. Hate it. Debts are for people who plan to die."

Inside, the house was small and smelled of woodsmoke, fish oil, and the particular damp of a structure that had made peace with the river rather than defeated it. Marta—Odell's sister, seventy if she was a day, with forearms like dock rope and the expression of a woman who had buried two husbands and found neither of them worth the trouble—put the moths' blanket around the wounded one without being asked and pressed a cup of something hot into the thief's hands with the firmness of a woman who would not be taking it back.

The wounded one was the cartographer. Wren. Odell had heard the name whispered up and down the river for a month now, the way you hear a storm name: the survey, the missing team, the maps. He'd also heard what the enforcers said about the ranger who'd gone bad, and he'd heard it with the particular contempt of a man who had been lied about by professionals his whole life.

"Ribs?" he asked her.

"Cracked, maybe. It's fine."

"It's never fine. Marta, the willow bark."

"It's *fine*."

"It's fine," Marta agreed, already grinding the bark.

The thief—Naya, Silas called her, and there was something in how he said it that Odell filed away for later, the way you file away a fish that jumped but didn't take the hook—had planted herself between the door and Silas, arms crossed, satchel still gripped. "How do we know this isn't a trap? Enforcers have been on the water since Corren's Landing. Everyone on this river is either bought or scared."

"Both, mostly," Odell said. "I'm neither, which is why I live out here with my sister and my grudges." He nodded at the lamp in the window. "You want to know how much I'm scared? They came in the spring. Two of them, nice as tax collectors. Said a light on the water invited unauthorized traffic. Said I should douse it. Said a man my age, alone out here, was vulnerable." He smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "I told them the light wasn't for traffic. It was for drowning men. And that if they touched it, I'd know exactly where to send the river next, because I've been reading this water since before their grandfathers could swim."

"And they left?" Wren asked.

"They left. Then they came back with a paper saying my stretch of the Bend was now concession water, and I'd need a permit to fish it." He shrugged. "So I fish it without one, and I leave the lamp burning, and every boat that goes down in the night knows there's one fool on the bank who'll come out for them." He looked at Silas. "That's the whole of my politics. It's not much. But it's mine."

* * *

They ate fish stew in silence broken only by the rain, which had settled into the long, patient drumming of a monsoon that intended to stay the season. Wren slept by the stove with the willow bark doing its slow work. Naya sat with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, and eventually, despite herself, her eyes lost the argument with exhaustion.

Silas stayed up.

Of course he did. Odell found him an hour past midnight out on the porch, wrapped in a tarp, watching the river eat the remains of his boat with the expression of a man watching a funeral he'd been expecting.

"She was a good boat," Silas said.

"She was a leaking argument with a sail. I sold her to you."

"You sold her to me at a fair price."

"I overcharged you shamefully and you knew it." Odell lowered himself into the chair beside him, joints announcing each stage of the descent. For a while they just listened to the water. Then: "The warrant's real, then. The one they're calling in on the ferries. *Silas Pemberton, wanted in the matter of the missing survey.*"

"It's real."

"Did you do it?"

Silas turned and looked at him, and there it was again, that flicker of something true underneath the weary competence. "Nine years I hauled people in for less than what they're saying I did. You want me to say the words?"

"I want you to hear yourself say them."

"I didn't do it." He said it flat, to the river. "And the part that keeps me up isn't the warrant. It's that the people who issued it know I didn't. That's the part that's new. I always figured the badge was crooked at the edges. I never figured it was crooked all the way down."

Odell nodded slowly. He'd had this conversation before, in one form or another, with half the river. "You know what a drowned canyon is, Silas?"

"A canyon with water in it."

"A canyon with a *past* in it." He pointed his pipe stem out at the black water. "Before the dam works, all this through here was high dry country. My father fished the Bend when it was a bend and not a grave. There's a canyon upriver—Kettle Canyon, we called it, because the current through the narrows boiled like one—that the government put under forty feet of water and told everyone it was empty. Except it wasn't empty. There were caves in the walls. High ones. Dry ones. Caves a man could live in if he knew the water wouldn't rise past the old flood line, and the water never rises past the old flood line, because I'm the one who's been measuring it for thirty years."

Silas had gone very still. "You know where Voss's team went."

"I know where a team of survey people went upriver in the spring, quiet as church mice, in a boat with no permit lights. And I know they didn't come back down. And I know that when the enforcers came asking, I told them I'd seen nothing, because I hadn't seen anything I cared to share." He tapped the pipe stem against the rail. "There's a cave in Kettle Canyon, third shelf up on the east wall, that you can only reach when the water's high enough to float you in and low enough that the entrance stands clear. Right now, with the monsoon running early and mean, the water's exactly wrong and exactly right. Which means if your missing surveyors are anywhere, they're there, and if you're going to find them, it's now or when the dam people finish their works and drown the shelf for good."

The rain filled the silence between them.

"Why?" Silas said finally. "Why tell me? You know what I'm carrying. You know what following me onto your porch could cost you."

Odell took his time lighting the pipe, cupping the flame from the wind, an old man's ceremony performed ten thousand times. "When you pulled me out at Sallow Reach," he said, "I asked you why. You were a ranger. I was a smuggler. It was your job to let me drown and file the paperwork. And you said—" he squinted at Silas through the pipe smoke, "—you said, *because the water doesn't check permits.* I've thought about that for nine years. Every time they brought a new map through here that erased another camp, another crossing, another family, I thought about it. The water doesn't check permits. The river never signed their papers, Silas. Neither did I."

He stood, joints complaining, and looked down at the man who had once held his head above the water and was now, by every official account, the worst kind of criminal.

"So. Tomorrow, when your thief wakes up and your cartographer can stand, you're going to tell me no, because you're a decent man and you'll want to keep your trouble off my porch. And I'm going to tell you to sit down and drink your tea, because I'm coming with you. I'm the only one alive who can read that water, and you'd all drown in the first narrows without me."

"Odell—"

"And one more thing." He leaned on the rail, and his voice dropped, and the humor went out of it entirely. "The enforcers came by again last week. Asking after a light on the water. Asking after *me*. They're not done with this river, Silas. Whatever you're carrying in that satchel of hers, they want it bad enough that they're sweeping every bend between here and the capital." He knocked out his pipe against the rail. "So sleep. Because I don't think they're far behind you. And I don't think they stop at the waterline."

Behind them, through the wall, Marta's voice drifted, dry as salt: "Odell Brack, if you're out there making promises to drowning men again, I'm putting the good blanket back in the moth chest."

Odell smiled into the rain.

"Wouldn't dream of it, Marta," he called back. And then, quieter, to Silas, watching the lamp burn steady in the window against all instruction: "Get some sleep, ranger. Tomorrow we go up into Kettle Canyon—and I'd bet this whole Bend my father left me that what's waiting in that cave is going to change whose side of the water everyone stands on."

Downriver, faint beneath the rain, Odell heard it—or thought he heard it: an engine, throttled low, running dark against the current.

Coming upriver.

Coming for the light.
