## 10. Into the Drowned Canyon

Wren Oduya had drawn maps of rivers she'd never seen, coastlines she'd never walked, and had always assumed that was the honest way to do it—paper before place, theory before mud. The drowned canyons cured her of that assumption in under an hour.

Odell Brack poled the patched boat up a channel that did not exist on any chart Wren had ever certified, and she sat in the bow with her wounded arm strapped tight and her field notebook open on her knees, feeling like a fraud with a fever.

"Your maps put the canyon mouth two miles south," she said. "I drew it myself. From aerials."

"Aerials don't know water." Odell's voice came out of his beard like gravel settling. He stood at the stern with a pole longer than Silas was tall, reading the current the way other men read letters from home. "River rose eleven feet when they closed the dam gates upstream. What your aerials show is what used to be. What you're floating on is what they made."

Silas crouched near the middle of the boat, one hand resting on the gunwale, watching the canyon walls slide past. They were red stone going gray at the waterline, and above the new high-water mark they still wore their old colors—streaks of ochre and rust, old bird nests like torn pockets, ledges where goats had once stood and now could only stand in memory. Below the line, everything was drowned and dark and dripping.

"It's wrong," Naya said quietly. She hadn't said much since dawn. She sat with her brother's compass open in her lap, though she wasn't consulting it. "The whole thing. It looks wrong."

"It is wrong," Odell said. "But it's useful wrong, if you know how to read it. Which is why your ranger friend came knocking on my door instead of the authority's."

"I knocked because you owe me," Silas said.

"I owe you a debt, boy, not obedience. There's a difference. A debt I pay how I see fit." Odell pushed off a submerged ledge; something pale and long slid away beneath the surface. "This is me paying it. Mind you remember that when we're all drowned or shot."

"Gentle humor," Wren murmured. "That's what this crew runs on. Gentle humor."

"We run on bail water and bad decisions," Naya said. "Humor's just what floats on top."

Wren laughed, then immediately regretted it, because laughing pulled at the bandage on her shoulder where the enforcer's bullet had scored her during the flight across the salt flats. Three days of fever had come and mostly gone, thanks to Odell's store of willow bark and an alarming tincture he called "snake medicine" and refused to explain further. Her arm still felt like it belonged to someone less careful. But her head was clear, and clear was what mattered now, because somewhere up this drowned channel was a cave, and in that cave—if Odell's knowledge and Naya's brother's route were both right—were survivors of the survey team that had vanished without tracks.

Two survivors, Odell said. Maybe three. He wouldn't say how he knew. He only said that a fisherman who worked flooded water learned things, and that some of those things had been sitting in his chest for months like swallowed hooks.

* * *

They camped that first night on a shelf of rock that had once been a shepherd's landing, forty feet above the old river level and now barely a hand's width above the new one. Odell built no fire. They ate cold smoked fish and flatbread gone soft, and Wren lay awake listening to the canyon drip.

It was a strange music. In her old life—the life of drafting tables and government ink—water was a line on paper, blue, obedient, labeled. Here it breathed. It rose against the stone and sighed back down, rose and sighed, patient as a creditor. Somewhere far off, a piece of the canyon calved and fell into the deep water with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"You're doing the thing," Naya whispered from beside her.

"What thing?"

"The cartographer thing. Where you look at everything like it's trying to lie to you."

"Everything *is* trying to lie to me," Wren whispered back. "That's my whole profession. I just usually get to be the one holding the pen."

Across the dead fire ring, Silas sat propped against a boulder with his hat over his eyes, ostensibly asleep. Wren had learned by now that he never actually slept first. He waited until the others' breathing slowed, and then he slept in shallow installments, like a man making withdrawals he didn't trust the bank to honor.

She thought about that. About a man who had spent nine years moving through the basin stopping small crimes, and whose entire philosophy—as far as she could extract it over cold tea and shared danger—was that stopping costs more than moving. And about how a man like that had now stopped. Stopped running, anyway. Was lying in a drowned canyon with a thief, a wounded mapmaker, and a hermit who called him *boy*, all for a set of maps worth killing for.

People, Wren had decided, were the hardest terrain there was. No contour lines. No legend.

* * *

The second day the canyon narrowed and the water deepened and went green-black, and Odell stopped poling and started steering by landmarks that weren't there anymore.

"There should be a bend with a split rock," he said, frowning at walls that gave him nothing. "Big as a chapel door. My father moored there every season."

"The dam works flooded the lower bend," Naya said suddenly. She had her brother's letters out—not the warning letter, which she kept separate now, wrapped in oilcloth like a wound—but the earlier ones, the ones full of route notes. "Tam wrote about it. 'Past the split rock, keep the wall on your left until the stone goes white.' White stone, he says. Like teeth."

Odell looked at her a long moment. "Your brother mapped this?"

"He guided for the survey team. He wrote it all down because—" Naya's jaw tightened. "Because he trusted paper more than people. Same as Wren."

"That's not fair," Wren said.

"No," Naya agreed. "But it's true, and truth gets to be unfair. Keep the wall on our left."

They kept the wall on their left. An hour later the stone changed—went from red to a bleached bone-white where mineral springs had run, before the springs too had drowned—and Odell grunted like a man greeting an old acquaintance wearing a stranger's face.

"White stone," he said. "Like teeth. Your brother had an eye." He swung the boat toward a shadow in the wall that Wren's eyes insisted was nothing: a darkness among darker darknesses, half-drowned, its lip lapped by green water. "And there's the cave. Or what the dam left of it."

The entrance was maybe eight feet above the waterline, a ragged arch with a rubble fan spilling down toward the flood. Odell backed them in against the base of it and held the boat steady with the pole while Silas climbed first, testing each hold, then reached down for Wren's good arm.

"Other arm," she said. "The shoulder's decorative this week."

He shifted without comment, took her wrist, and hauled her up onto the rubble with a care that pretended to be casual. Naya came up light as a cat. Odell tied the boat off to a root that had once been a tree and followed, breathing hard but game.

Inside, the cave opened wider than its mouth promised—a throat, then a chamber, dry-floored and dim, lit by a seam of daylight falling from a crack in the ceiling far above. And the chamber was not empty.

There were signs of habitation arranged with paranoid precision: a smoke-blackened corner where fires had been burned small and banked fast; snares made of wire and fishing line; a row of tin cups, each one nested inside the next, spotless. Blankets folded into squares sharp enough to cut. And along one wall, wrapped in canvas and stacked like ammunition crates, were instruments—survey instruments, brass and glass, the real ones, Wren realized, not the tampered sets from the abandoned campsite.

Naya made a sound low in her throat.

From the black depth of the chamber, a voice said: "That's far enough."

A lantern flared. Behind it stood a woman.

She was gaunt in the way that hunger carves rather than age—cheekbones like tent poles, hands steady only through visible effort. Her clothes had been mended so many times they were more patch than cloth. But she held a pistol in a grip that had not forgotten its training, and behind her, half-risen from the shadows, was a younger man with a bandaged leg and the wide eyes of someone who had been afraid for a very long time.

"Ilsa Voss," Wren said. It wasn't a question. She'd memorized the survey leader's file, her signature, her handwriting. "My name is Wren Oduya. I'm a cartographer with the—"

"I know who you are." Voss's voice was dry as the salt flats. "Government cartographer. Which means government-trained, government-vetted, and government-ownable. Put the notebook down. Slowly. On the floor. Kick it here."

Wren looked at Silas. Silas looked at Odell. Odell, magnificent, unbothered, lowered himself onto a rock with the groan of an old man and said, "Ilsa. It's Odell Brack. You sent the boy Tam's sister to find me, or have you forgot?"

"I sent no one anywhere." But the pistol dipped a degree. "Tam's sister?"

"Here," Naya said. She held up the compass. "You know this. He carried it every survey. He wrote me letters. I've got them. I've got his route notes, I've got—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, Wren could see her hating it. "I've got the letter he sent me that I didn't open in time. So don't tell me I'm bought. Nobody buys grief."

The silence in the cave was long. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, keeping time.

Then the young man with the bandaged leg said, "Ilsa. The hermit's right. Look at them. The girl's starving as bad as we are."

"She might be that good an actress," Voss said, but the pistol dipped another degree, and something in her face was fighting itself—nine months of vigilance against nine months of wanting exactly this and never daring to believe it.

"Ma'am," Silas said.

Voss's eyes snapped to him, and hardened all at once, like water going to ice.

"Oh, no," she said softly. "No, I know *you*. Ranger authority. Pemberton. You escorted our resupply convoy last spring. You wore the badge. You saluted the concession liaison like he paid your wages, because he did." The pistol came back up, steady now, aimed at the center of Silas's chest. "Everyone is bought, ranger. Everyone. That's the first lesson of the basin. The second lesson is that the ones who aren't bought yet are just waiting for a better price."

Silas didn't move. Didn't reach for the rifle slung on his back, though Wren saw his hand twitch toward habit and stop itself. He took his hat off—which somehow made it worse and better at once—and held it against his chest like a man at a funeral.

"Deputy Concessioner Reeve issued a warrant for me four days ago," he said. "Framed me for your team's disappearance. As of this morning I'm a fugitive with a price on my head. If I'm bought, ma'am, somebody got swindled."

"Warrants can be theater. Prices can be bait."

"They can," Silas agreed. His voice stayed level, weary, almost gentle. "You're right to check. I'd think less of you if you didn't. But I've spent nine years chasing thieves across flats where the only crime most days was thirst, and I never once brought a man in for something he didn't do. That's all I've got. It isn't much. But it's mine, and I haven't sold it."

Voss stared at him. The lantern flame shivered in some underground breath of air.

It was Wren who broke the standoff—or tried to, because her particular weapon had always been paper. She knelt, slowly, and laid her field notebook on the stone, and nudged it toward the lantern light.

"Page one," she said. "Corren's Landing permit records. The concession boundaries are drawn from maps dated *after* the land they describe. I found it myself, before any of this, before I even met Silas properly. I've been pulling that thread ever since. If I'm bought, then whoever bought me has an extremely roundabout sense of humor."

Voss didn't move. But the young man limped forward, snatched the notebook, and thumbed through it with shaking fingers. "Ilsa," he said again. "The dates. It's the same thing we found. It's the same *impossible dates*."

"I know what impossible dates look like, Ferro. I found them first." But her voice had changed—thinner, cracked down the middle, and Wren understood suddenly that this woman had been alone with the truth for nine months, rationing it like food, and the rationing was killing her faster than the hunger.

"Nine months," Wren said quietly. "You've been down here nine months?"

"Ten," said Ferro. "Since the monsoon ended. We were supposed to be five." His face did something complicated and terrible. "We were supposed to be five."

Voss finally lowered the pistol. She didn't holster it—Wren noticed she simply kept it in her hand, the way some people kept a rosary—and she sat down heavily on a supply crate, and for a moment she looked every hour of her ten months.

"Odell," she said. "Tell me honestly. Are these people fools, or are they mine?"

"Fools," Odell said comfortably. "The best kind. The kind who keep walking after sensible folk quit."

Voss laughed. It was a rusty sound, a hinge unused, and it turned into a cough, and Naya was suddenly beside her with a waterskin, and Voss let her drink, and that—that small surrender—was the moment Wren felt hope come back into the cave like warmth into a cold room. Not triumph. Just warmth. Just the possibility of it.

* * *

Later, while Ferro showed Naya the cache of real instruments and Odell doctored everyone's various damages with his snake medicine over vigorous protest, Wren sat with Voss near the lantern and watched the woman eat. Slowly, deliberately, the way you eat when your body has learned not to trust abundance.

"Why hide?" Wren asked. "Why not walk out? Ten months. You could have reached the capital."

"With what?" Voss said. "My testimony and my word? Reeve owns the courts, owns the press office, owns half the rangers. The moment I surfaced, I'd vanish properly. And the maps—" She glanced at the canvas-wrapped crates. "The originals stay hidden until they can hurt him in front of witnesses he can't buy. There's a difference between evidence and ammunition. Evidence sits in a file. Ammunition needs a firing position."

"And you found one?"

"I'm working on it." Voss studied Wren with eyes that missed nothing. "You pulled the permit records yourself. Before you knew anything. Why?"

Wren considered lying, decided it would be insulting to both of them. "Because the maps were wrong and it offended me. That's the whole story. Somebody drew a boundary that couldn't exist, and I couldn't sleep until I knew whether I was crazy or the paper was."

Voss smiled—thin, genuine, exhausted. "The paper was. It usually is." She leaned closer, and the lantern threw her shadow huge against the cave wall, a giant hunched over a secret. "Then listen, cartographer, because I'll say this once. We didn't just find forged boundaries. We found what the forgery was *for*. The aquifer surveys. This whole basin sits on enough water to green the Corren territories for a century, and the government has known since before the permits were signed. The concessions aren't about grazing rights. They're about owning the water before anyone knows it exists. And Reeve's plan, if the truth surfaces—" her voice dropped to almost nothing— "is to open the dam gates. Flood the canyons. Drown every record, every route, every witness. This cave included."

The drip of the water kept its slow time. Above them, through the crack in the ceiling, a thread of evening sky had gone the color of a bruise.

"So we're sitting in the flood zone," Wren said.

"You're sitting in the flood zone," Voss corrected. "I've been sitting in it for ten months. It grows on you." She looked across the chamber, at Silas standing watch at the cave mouth with his hat back on, at Naya laughing quietly at something Ferro said, at old Odell humming as he repacked his kit. Something moved in her face that might have been the beginning of trust, or the memory of it.

"But if you're serious," Voss said, "if any of you are serious—then tomorrow I'll give you my testimony. Sealed. Signed. Everything. And you'll carry it out of this canyon ahead of the water."

She paused, and her hand tightened around the pistol she still hadn't holstered.

"One condition. When we move, we move at first light. Because yesterday morning, Ferro heard engines on the ridge above the north rim." Her eyes went to the crack of sky overhead, dark now, and held there. "Somebody's already looking for us. And I don't think they're rangers."
