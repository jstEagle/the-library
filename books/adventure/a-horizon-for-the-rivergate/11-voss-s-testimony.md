## 11. Voss's Testimony

Ilsa Voss had spent eleven days in a cave learning the exact weight of her own name.

It was heavier than she'd thought. When she'd signed it onto survey documents for fifteen years, it had been nothing—a flourish at the bottom of other people's paperwork. Now it was the only thing between Aldous Reeve and a basin full of water, and it weighed enough that she could feel it in her shoulders when she woke, like a yoke she'd forgotten to take off.

She heard them coming before she saw them. Of course she did. Eleven days of listening to dripping stone had tuned her ears to anything that wasn't dripping stone, and what came up the flooded channel now was oar-knock, low voices, and—distinctly, absurdly—the smell of fish. Someone out there had been cooking.

"Kesh," she whispered to the shape huddled in the dark beside her. "Company."

Her junior surveyor lifted his head from his knees with the slow, pained dignity of a man who had eaten river snails for nine days and had opinions about it. "If they're enforcers," he croaked, "I want it noted that I fought bravely."

"Noted," said Voss. "You fought bravely while holding my leg."

"It's a good leg."

The light found them then—a lantern held high by a broad-shouldered woman with mud to the elbows, flanked by a tall man in a ranger's coat gone soft at the seams, and a wiry girl who moved like water finding its level. The lantern swung, caught Voss's face, and stopped.

The ranger stared at her the way men stare at ghosts.

"Survey Leader Voss," he said slowly. "Silas Pemberton. Rivergate Authority." He said it like an apology already in progress.

"I know who you are," Voss said. Her voice came out rusted; she hadn't used it for anything above a whisper since the flood took their boat. "Everyone in this basin knows Silas Pemberton. You're the one who writes tickets for grazing two hundred meters outside permit lines." She let that sit. "I used to think you believed in those lines."

Something crossed his face—not guilt exactly, but the raw place where guilt would eventually build its house.

"I did," he said. "Once."

"Well." She leaned back against the cold stone and looked at all three of them: the cartographer with a bandaged arm, the thief with her brother's compass clutched like a talisman, the ranger with his badge still on his chest as if it were a fact rather than a question. "Then you'd better sit down. Because I'm about to tell you why believing in lines is the most dangerous thing a person can do in the Rivergate."

* * *

They made tea first. This was Kesh's doing—he produced a dented tin from the wreckage of their supplies with the reverence of a priest revealing relics, and the girl, Naya, produced actual tea leaves from somewhere inside her coat, along with, apparently, half of everything else in the basin.

"You're a pickpocket," Voss observed.

"I'm a *collector*," Naya said, handing her a cup. "There's a difference. Collectors have taste."

Voss drank. It was terrible tea, over-steeped and gritty with cave dust, and it was the best thing she had tasted in eleven days. Warmth spread through her chest and loosened something that had been clenched there since the night the river swallowed their escape boat and everything in it except the oilskin packet strapped against her ribs.

She touched the packet. Still there. Still dry. Still heavy.

"Eleven days ago," she began, "my team was mapping concession boundary seventeen. Officially. Unofficially—" she paused, because unofficially was where the whole story lived, "—unofficially, we were doing what survey teams always do, which is more than we're asked. Reeve's office gave us six weeks and a corridor two miles wide and told us not to stray. So naturally we strayed."

Naya grinned. Wren Oduya—the cartographer, Voss knew her name from the guild registries, everyone in the trade knew Oduya's name—leaned forward with the particular hunger of a professional hearing about a colleague's fieldwork.

"The straying paid off," Voss said. "Day nineteen, Kesh noticed the seepage patterns in the canyon walls didn't match the hydrological charts. Not slightly off. Not surveyor's error. Off by an entire aquifer." She watched their faces. Only Oduya's showed real understanding; the others got there a beat later, and Pemberton's expression when it landed was worth recording.

"There's no aquifer on the charts," he said.

"No," Voss agreed. "There isn't. There is, however, an aquifer under the Rivergate Basin large enough to make the eastern provinces green for a century. And every concession permit drawn in the last three years sits directly on top of it."

The cave dripped. Somewhere far off, the new dam works groaned through their concrete throat.

"They knew," Pemberton said quietly.

"They've known for years. The original government surveys found it decades ago and buried the findings—literally buried them, sealed archive, restricted classification. Then Reeve's people drew the concession maps *around* the knowledge. Every boundary line, every permit, every 'unused wasteland' designation—it's all built on pretending the land is worthless so it can be bought cheap. The moment the aquifer goes public, the permits are worthless instead. Worse than worthless. They're evidence."

"And you found the old surveys," Oduya said. Her voice was careful, the way people speak when they're trying not to hope too loudly. "Where?"

"In a sealed annex under the Corren's Landing records hall, behind a wall that was supposed to be load-bearing and wasn't." Voss allowed herself a thin smile. "Your predecessor had a habit of hiding things in bad masonry. Bless him. We copied everything. Originals, dates, signatures, the lot. And then—"

"And then someone sold you out," Pemberton said.

Voss looked at him for a long moment. He had the particular stillness of a man who had already done this arithmetic and hated the answer.

"My deputy," she said. "Tomas Ferren. Twelve years at my side. I taught him how to read stratigraphy. I recommended him for his certification. I—" She stopped, surprised at how much of it was still in her voice, how fresh the cut remained. "He went into Corren's Landing to resupply. He came back with new boots, good coffee, and a story about a lucky card game. Three days later, enforcers knew our route before we walked it. So I did the only thing left. I faked our vanishing."

"You faked it," Naya said flatly. "People think they're dead."

"People are *supposed* to think they're dead. That was the entire point." Voss's voice hardened. "A missing team is a scandal. A murdered team is a martyrdom—and martyrs get investigated by whoever wins the investigation. But a team that simply *vanishes*, without tracks, without bodies? That haunts people. That makes clerks nervous. That leaves me alive to testify." She gestured at the cave around them, the drowned canyon beyond, the whole improbable architecture of her plan. "We scuttled our own boat upstream, walked the ridge at night, doubled back through the shallows where the current eats footprints. It was a good plan."

"And it went wrong," Pemberton said.

"Two things went wrong. First, the monsoon came early, which nobody could have planned for. Second—" she exhaled, "—Ferren didn't just sell our route. He sold the *story*. Told Reeve we'd found the archive. Which meant Reeve couldn't afford to let a vanishing stay mysterious. He needed bodies, or he needed us found and silenced. So now there are enforcers sweeping these canyons, and my beautiful bloodless vanishing has become a manhunt, and the only reason I'm not in Reeve's cellar is that I know these caves better than the man who flooded them."

Kesh snorted into his tea. "She does. She mapped half of them before the water came. Spent a whole season down here complaining about the humidity."

"A season well spent," Voss said. "Though I confess I never planned to live in my own field notes."

* * *

Pemberton asked the questions after that, and he asked them like a ranger—which is to say, precisely, and with the weary air of a man cataloguing a crime scene the size of a watershed. Dates. Names. Chain of custody. Where the copies were, where the originals were, who else had seen them.

Voss answered everything, and watched him grow quieter with each answer, and understood: he was realizing that the institution on his chest had known. Had to have known. No warrant gets issued this fast, this cleanly, against a decorated ranger, unless someone very high up signed it with a steady hand.

"You've been served papers, haven't you," she said, when he paused. "Or you will be. They'll call it desertion, or theft, or the disappearance itself. Reeve doesn't leave loose threads; he embroiders them into accusations."

Pemberton's jaw worked. "My commander told me to come in alone. No escort. No partner." He glanced at Oduya, whose bandaged arm was a whole testimony in itself. "I've been a ranger nine years. I know what 'come in alone' means."

"Yes," Voss said gently. "You do."

The girl, Naya, had been silent through all of it—silent in the coiled way of someone holding a spring down. Now she set down her cup. "My brother guided your team. Jomar. Jomar Nadeer."

Voss closed her eyes. "Yes. He did."

"He sent me a letter. I didn't open it until—" Her voice snagged, and she pushed through it the way she probably pushed through everything, headfirst and furious. "Until it was too late to matter. He wrote that the survey had found something, that Voss was moving the team off-route, that if anything happened I should find the woman with the red field case. That's you. That's the packet."

"That's the packet," Voss confirmed.

"Is he alive?" The question came out stripped bare, all the thief's cleverness burned off it. "Kesh. Is Jomar alive?"

Kesh and Voss exchanged a look that contained an entire conversation, most of it painful.

"He was alive when we split," Kesh said carefully. "The plan was three groups, three routes. Jomar took the southern channel with Denna and Okoro. We haven't seen or heard from them since. But the southern channel floods late, and Jomar knows that country better than anyone I've ever guided with. If anyone made it out—"

"If," Naya said.

"If," Kesh admitted. "But it's a good if. I'd bet on it."

Voss watched the girl absorb that—the way she held it, turned it over, refused both the comfort and the despair, pocketed the *if* like something she intended to spend later. A formidable creature. Voss had raised no children and mentored dozens of surveyors and recognized the type instantly: the ones who became formidable because the world had given them no other option.

"Jomar carried the second copy," Voss said. "Split custody. Standard procedure for findings this sensitive. If he reached anywhere with a post office, there's a duplicate already traveling under seal."

Naya's head came up sharply. "Where would he send it?"

"I don't know. He wouldn't tell me—that was the point. But he'd send it somewhere the concession office can't reach. Somewhere with its own law." Voss smiled faintly. "Somewhere like the nomad routes, perhaps. If such places still exist on any map your government honors."

The girl looked at Pemberton. Pemberton looked at the floor. Voss decided not to enjoy that quite as much as she wanted to.

* * *

Later, when the others had settled into an uneasy sleep among the stones—Oduya curled against her pack, Naya sitting watch out of pure stubbornness, Pemberton lying awake in the manner of a man rehearsing a resignation—Voss took out the oilskin packet and weighed it in her hands one more time.

Eleven days she had guarded it. Slept with it strapped to her body. Peeled apart moldy rations with one hand so the other could stay near it. She had told herself this was prudence. It was also, she admitted now, in the honest dark, fear wearing prudence's coat. As long as she held it, she was the only witness, and a lone witness can be erased. One cave-in, one fever, one unlucky enforcer, and the truth dies with her body, and Reeve's maps stand forever.

Truth kept in one pair of hands isn't truth. It's a hostage.

She crossed the cave to where Pemberton lay. His eyes opened before she'd taken the third step—he slept like a door left unlatched—and he sat up without a sound.

"Survey Leader," he murmured.

"Pemberton." She crouched, joints protesting, and set the packet on the stone between them. "Do you know what this weighs?"

He looked at it. "Paper and wax. A few ounces."

"Fourteen pounds," Voss said. "Give or take. I've been carrying fourteen pounds for eleven days, and before that, the weight of knowing for three weeks before that, and I am fifty-three years old and my knees have filed a formal complaint." She pushed it toward him. "Take it."

His hand didn't move. "It should go to the authority. Through channels—"

"Through *channels*?" For a moment her voice cracked across the cave like a whip, and Naya's head turned, and Voss lowered it again, fierce and quiet. "Pemberton. The channels are the thing that's broken. Your authority issued a warrant for the only honest ranger I've met in this basin. Your courts confirm Reeve's permits. Your archives hide behind false walls. There are no channels. There is a river, and right now it flows through three people in a wet cave, and one of them is you."

He was silent a long time. Lantern-light moved on his face, and Voss watched a man stand at the edge of the person he had been and look across at the person he might have to become.

"If I take it," he said finally, "it's not evidence anymore. It's a target. Everything that touches it becomes a target. Her—" a nod toward Oduya, asleep with her bandaged arm cradled like a bird with a broken wing. "The girl. Odell Brack, if he's fool enough to keep sheltering us. Me, obviously. I'm already embroidered."

"Yes," Voss said. "That's what it means to carry the truth. It doesn't make you safe. It makes you *responsible*." She tapped the oilskin once, softly. "Fifteen years I put my name on documents. Most of them were true and none of them mattered. This one matters. And I am done being its only keeper. Hand to hand, ranger. That's how truth survives—not in vaults, not in archives. Hand to hand, from people willing to hold it."

Pemberton picked up the packet. He did it carefully, the way you take something living, and Voss saw his arms register the weight she'd described—fourteen pounds, give or take, the whole drowned history of the basin in a rectangle of oilskin and wax.

"I don't know how to do this," he said. "I know how to follow rules. The rules are gone."

"Then you'll do what the rest of us do," Voss said, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling—tired, rusted, real. "You'll improvise. Badly, at first. With company."

From her post by the entrance, Naya spoke without turning. "For the record, I vote we leave tonight. Before first light. Enforcers swept the lower channel yesterday—I heard their engines through the rock."

"You heard engines through solid stone?" Pemberton said.

"I hear everything," Naya said. "It's a curse and a profession."

Voss laughed—actually laughed, a dry bark that startled even her, the first sound of its kind in eleven days—and Kesh stirred and muttered something about people having no consideration for the convalescent.

Outside the cave mouth, far downstream, the river ran dark and swollen toward the capital, carrying everything it carried. Voss looked at the packet in the ranger's hands and felt the strangest sensation: lighter. Not safe—she suspected none of them would be safe again—but lighter, the way a rope feels when the load is finally shared.

"First light is in four hours," she said. "Someone wake me when it's time to run. And Pemberton—"

He looked up.

"When you get to the capital, when you stand in front of whatever's left of the law and open that packet—" she held his eyes, "—say my name first. Say all our names. The vanished ones especially. Vanished people haunt best when somebody speaks for them."

"I will," Silas Pemberton said, and Voss Voss believed him, which after eleven days of trusting no one felt like setting down a second weight she hadn't known she was carrying.

Above the drowned canyon, the sky was beginning, very faintly, to consider dawn.
