## 12. The Badge They Take Back

The radio at Odell Brack's camp was older than Silas and had the temperament to prove it. It sat on a shelf between a jar of fishing hooks and a stuffed heron that had seen better decades, and it required a specific ritual: two slaps to the casing, a quarter-turn of the tuning dial past the marker, and a prayer to whatever patron saint watched over corroded wiring.

"You have to apologize to it first," Odell said from the doorway. "It can tell when you're insincere."

"I've spent nine years talking to government equipment," Silas said. "Insincerity is my native language."

He keyed the transmit switch and felt the old familiar weight settle onto his shoulders—the weight of the call sign, the protocol, the voice he'd be expected to use. *Ranger Pemberton, station four-one-one, requesting extraction.* Nine years of small crimes and long miles, all of it funneled through this one crackling channel back to the people who paid him in rations and suspicion.

The commander's frequency answered on the second try.

"Pemberton." Commander Hesk's voice came through flat and careful, which was wrong. Hesk was many things—stubborn, superstitious about weather, a man who salted his tea—but he had never once been careful with Silas. For nine years he'd opened every transmission with some variation of *you still alive, you impossible man?*

"Still alive," Silas said. "Barely. I need extraction, Hesk. Three of us. We've got documents that—"

"I know what you've got."

Silas stopped. The rain drummed on the tarp roof. Somewhere behind him, Wren was rewrapping her bandage with her teeth, and Naya was pretending very hard not to listen, which meant she was listening to every word.

"Hesk," Silas said slowly, "how do you know what I've got?"

A pause long enough to have weather in it. Then Hesk said, in the voice of a man reading from a paper he hated, "Silas Pemberton, you are advised that a warrant has been issued for your arrest. Charges are as follows: abduction of survey personnel, tampering with concession property, and—" here the paper seemed to stick in his throat "—the disappearance of the Voss team. There's a bounty attached. It's substantial."

The rain got very loud. Or maybe everything else got very quiet; Silas couldn't tell the difference.

"Say that again," he said.

"You know I can't—"

"Say it again, Hesk. Slowly. I want to make sure I understand exactly which direction this joke is pointed."

"It's no joke." And there it was, under the flatness—something that might have been grief, if grief were allowed on official frequencies. "Warrant went out three days ago. Reeve's office filed it personally. They've got statements, Silas. Witness statements placing you at the survey camp the night the team vanished. They've got your compass logged as recovered evidence from the site."

Silas looked down at his belt, where his old service compass hung—the one Naya had stolen from him in the flats, the one that had led them half across the basin, the one that had belonged to her brother. It swung gently on its lanyard, pointing its useless, lying north.

"They've got my compass," he repeated.

"The description matches. Serial number and all."

"That compass has been in three pockets since the survey camp, none of them mine by choice, and one of them belongs to a girl who's currently eating Odell's entire supply of dried fish." He heard Naya pause mid-chew behind him. "Hesk. You've known me nine years. You've signed off on every report I've ever filed. Are you telling me you believe I made six people disappear?"

Another pause. When Hesk spoke again, he'd dropped the official register entirely, and somehow that was worse. "I'm telling you that I filed a request to delay the warrant and it came back denied within the hour, from above my level, which doesn't happen, which means someone wanted it fast. I'm telling you that two of my own rangers asked me this morning whether they'd be paid the bounty if they brought you in, and I didn't have an answer for them that wasn't a lie. And I'm telling you, Silas, that if you're calling me on this frequency, then they already know roughly where you are, because we don't have encryption anymore—we haven't since the budget reallocation—and the concession office pays for our maintenance now."

Silas closed his eyes.

The rain. The heron. The smell of fish smoke and wet canvas. Nine years of his life arranged themselves in front of him like evidence on a table: every petty thief dragged to Corren's Landing in irons, every nomad family turned back from a route that had been their grandmother's grandmother's, every citation written in neat ranger's script enforcing lines on maps that, as it turned out, had been drawn by liars. He had been the hand. He had been proud of the hand. Steady, fair, tireless—the best hand the authority had in the basin, people used to say.

And now the arm it was attached to had turned around and pointed at him.

"Silas?" Hesk said. "You still there?"

"I'm here." His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Hesk. One question, and answer it straight, because I'll know if you don't. If I turned myself in tomorrow—if I walked into your station with my hands up and those documents under my arm—what happens?"

The silence stretched so long that Silas checked whether the connection had died.

Then Hesk said, quietly: "Don't turn yourself in."

That was the answer. That was the whole answer, and it told Silas more than any briefing could. The institution that had made him—fed him, trained him, given him a badge and a purpose and a reason to keep moving through nine years of empty country—could not protect him, would not hear him, and knew it. The machinery he'd served had been bought, bolt by bolt, and he'd been too busy chasing chicken thieves to notice the factory changing owners.

"There's a patrol rotation out of Corren's Landing," Hesk went on, low and fast now, a man spending something he couldn't replace. "They'll sweep toward Drowned Bend within two days. After that I can't tell you anything, because anything else I say will hang us both. Silas—"

"Don't," Silas said.

"—the badge was never supposed to cost you this much."

Silas looked at the radio. At his own reflection, warped in its cracked glass face. He thought about saying something dignified. Something like *it was an honor serving* or *I understand, old friend.* What came out instead was:

"You salt your tea, Hesk. All these years, and you never once fixed that."

He switched off before the old man could answer. Some conversations should end while they're still true.

* * *

He turned around and found both women looking at him.

Naya had, in fact, stopped eating the dried fish, which he suspected was the greatest tribute she was capable of paying. Wren stood with her half-tied bandage hanging loose, her cartographer's mind visibly working through what she'd overheard, arranging it into contours and boundaries the way she arranged everything.

"So," she said. "We're criminals now."

"We were always criminals," Naya said. "Some of us just hadn't been caught yet. Welcome to the club. Meetings are Tuesdays. There's no snacks because the government took them."

"Naya."

"What? He needs to laugh or he's going to do the thing where he stands very still and stares at the river and pretends he's fine, and honestly, I've seen him do it twice today and it's making me nervous."

She wasn't wrong, which was the irritating part. Silas became aware that he was standing very still and staring in the general direction of the river, and that his hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the rain. He made himself sit down on Odell's bench, which groaned in protest, and put his face in his hands.

Nine years. He tried to find the shape of it, the thing he'd been carrying all this time that he'd called duty, and discovered that when he pressed on it, it made a sound like hollow tin.

"When I was nineteen," he said, to his palms, "I arrested a woman for grazing goats on concession land. She was seventy, maybe. She looked at me like I was a flood coming, like I was weather, something natural and terrible that she'd just have to wait out. I wrote the citation anyway. I remember being proud of how neatly I wrote it." He lifted his head. "I spent nine years being weather for people who never did anything worse than exist in the wrong place. And now the same machine that handed me the citation wants me hanged for a crime I didn't commit, committed by men I helped make rich, and the only reason I'm surprised is that I wasn't paying attention."

Wren sat down beside him. She smelled of ink and fever-sweat and river water, and her shoulder against his was warm and solid.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I read the warrant language while you were talking. It's sloppy. 'Abduction of survey personnel'—they don't even name the personnel. Real warrants name everyone. This one's boilerplate with your name pasted in. Reeve's office drafted it in a hurry."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No. It's supposed to tell you something useful: they're scared. Careful men write careful warrants. Aldous Reeve is not being careful right now, and a man like that being careless means he thinks he's losing." She said it with the particular satisfaction of a woman who had spent her career finding errors in other people's maps and intended to enjoy finding errors in other people's justice.

"He's not losing," Silas said. "He owns the courts. He owns the rangers. As of ten minutes ago, he owns my face on every bounty board between here and the capital. He's winning so hard he can afford to be sloppy."

"Then why does he need us dead instead of arrested?" Naya said.

Silas looked at her. She'd set down the fish and was turning her brother's compass over in her fingers, and there was nothing flippant left in her face.

"Think about it," she said. "If he really believed you took the survey team, he'd want you alive. A trial. A confession. A show. Dead men can't confess, but neither can they contradict the story. So why is every enforcer in the basin going to shoot first?" She held up the compass. "Because he knows the team isn't missing. He knows where they went, or he knows why, and if you two get to a courtroom with Voss's maps, the whole beautiful lie comes apart in front of witnesses. He doesn't want to arrest you, Ranger. Arrests come with questions. He wants the story to end."

The rain filled the silence after that, patient and enormous.

Odell appeared in the doorway with a kettle and four chipped cups, because Odell's response to all crises, Silas remembered suddenly and with a strange ache, had always been tea. He poured without asking, handed the cups around, and settled onto a crate with the sigh of a man who had fished these waters through three governments and expected to outlast a fourth.

"My late wife used to say," Odell offered, "that a man's reputation is like a boat hull. Everyone pokes holes in it eventually. Question is whether you bailed it yourself or let somebody else decide when it sinks."

"That's grim, Odell."

"She also said the river keeps better records than any court. She was usually right about that, too." He sipped. "Point being, boy. You spent nine years believing the badge made you honest. It didn't. You were honest before it and you're honest after it. The badge was just a thing you wore. Like a hat."

"A hat with a warrant attached," Naya said.

"All hats come with something attached, if you wear them long enough."

Silas turned his cup in his hands. Tea, unsalted—Odell had heard the transmission too, apparently, or simply knew. And underneath the fear and the fury and the strange vertigo of having his own name turned against him, something else was settling into place, quiet but unmistakable, like ballast shifting in a hull.

For nine years he had kept moving because stopping cost too much. Stopping meant thinking. Thinking meant admitting the routes were closing, the basin was being sold out from under the people who loved it, and that the man with the neat handwriting was part of it. Movement had been his mercy and his hiding place both.

Now movement was taken from him too. Every road out had a bounty board on it. Every legal channel ended in Reeve's pocket. He was, officially and completely, the criminal he'd spent his life hunting—and the terrible, liberating truth of it was that almost nothing had changed. He was still in the basin. He was still holding the truth. The only difference was that the institution had finally shown him its face, and he was free to stop pretending otherwise.

"We go to the capital," he said.

Wren raised an eyebrow. "Through two days of monsoon, past a patrol sweep, with a warrant, a wounded cartographer, a thief, and a boat with a patch history I'd describe as ambitious?"

"Yes."

"And when we get there? Reeve owns the courts. You said it yourself."

Silas looked at Naya, who was watching him with an expression he was beginning to recognize—the look of someone waiting to see whether you'd finally stop running and start choosing. He thought about what she'd told him once, in the market at Corren's Landing, about her brother's letters and the network of families that moved word faster than any government telegraph, mouth to mouth, campfire to campfire, along routes that existed on no map because no map had ever bothered to include them.

"Not the courts," he said slowly. "Not yet. First we need people. People who know the water, the crossings, the places a patrol won't look. Naya—those families the permits displaced. The ones who used to run the ferry lines. How many of them would talk to us?"

Naya smiled, and it was not entirely a nice smile. "Ranger, half the basin owes me favors and the other half owes my brother apologies. Give me a day and a reason, and I'll have you a road that doesn't exist."

"Good," Silas said. "Because starting tonight, we're ghosts. No stations, no frequencies, no badges." He reached down to his belt, unclipped the ranger's star he'd worn for nine years, and held it a moment in his palm—heavy, scratched, warm from his body. Then he set it on Odell's shelf, next to the stuffed heron, where it gleamed dully in the lamplight like a coin left for a ferryman.

"Keep it safe, Odell. If I come back for it, I'll want it back. If I don't—" he shrugged "—sell it. Buy something that floats better than my last boat."

Odell looked at the badge, then at Silas, and something passed across the old man's face that was too complicated to be pity and too gentle to be respect alone. "Boy," he said, "that shelf has held junk for forty years. Be an honor to hold something worth keeping."

It was Naya who broke the moment, because it was always Naya, standing abruptly with her head cocked toward the tree line, one hand raised.

"Quiet," she said. "Do you hear that?"

Silas listened. Rain, river, wind in the drowned cottonwoods. And beneath it—faint, rhythmic, wrong for the weather—a sound he knew from nine years of night patrols and had hoped never to hear aimed at himself.

Engines. More than one. Coming up the river road.

"Odell," Silas said quietly, already moving, "how well do you know the back channels from your dock?"

"Well enough," the old man said, setting down his cup with a calm that sounded rehearsed and wasn't. "But you three take the boat and go now. I'll stay and greet our visitors. Nobody suspects a lonely old fisherman of anything except loneliness."

"Odell, no—"

"Go," he said, and picked up his lantern, and walked out into the rain to meet whatever was coming, leaving Silas with a debt he already knew he would spend the rest of his life repaying.
