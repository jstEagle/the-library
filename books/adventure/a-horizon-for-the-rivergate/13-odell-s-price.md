## 13. Odell's Price

Naya had learned to read a river the way other people read faces, and the river that morning was lying.

It lay in its calmness — flat brown water sliding past Odell Brack's stilt camp without so much as a ripple of opinion, herons standing in the shallows like bored clerks waiting for the day to end. It lay in the smell of frying fish and river-mint tea drifting from the cookfire, where Odell was humming something tuneless and content, turning fish with a stick he'd clearly owned longer than Naya had been alive.

"You're burning it," she said.

"I'm *crisping* it," Odell said. "There's a difference, and the difference is respect."

She sat on the edge of his dock with her feet in the water and let herself, for one dangerous hour, feel safe. That was the thing about safety nobody warned you about: it made you stupid. Nine days on the run had taught her to sleep in shifts, to count exits, to keep the satchel with Voss's sealed testimony strapped against her ribs like a second heartbeat. But Odell's camp had a way of loosening knots. The old man fed you, teased you, asked nothing, and somewhere between the second cup of tea and the third, you forgot you were carrying the most dangerous papers in the Rivergate Basin.

Silas was on the far bank, ostensibly checking the boat's patched hull, actually doing whatever Silas did instead of admitting he liked people. Wren lay in the shade of the lean-to with her wounded shoulder propped on a rolled net, fever finally broken, arguing with Odell about whether catfish could be trusted as a species.

"They're liars," Wren said. "They sit at the bottom looking innocent while eating everything smaller than them."

"Sounds like government work," Odell said.

"Sounds like concession work," Wren said, and then winced, because even now, nine days later, saying the word *concession* out loud felt like stepping on a nail.

Naya turned her brother's letter over in her mind — the unopened one, the opened one, the words she'd read so many times they'd worn smooth as river stones. *If anything happens to me, go to Drowned Bend. Find the man who owes a debt. He'll know.* Tam had known. Tam had always known things before they happened, which was either foresight or the simple arithmetic of growing up nomad in a basin being sold out from under you.

She missed him with a pain that had stopped being sharp and become structural, like a crack in a load-bearing wall.

That was when the dogs went quiet.

Odell kept three river dogs, mangy, loud, and devoted to the principle that all strangers were crimes in progress. They had been barking at herons all morning. Now, all at once, they stopped — not settling, but stopping, ears up, bodies low, staring downriver toward the reed banks.

Naya's feet came out of the water.

"What is it?" Wren asked, already sitting up, hand going to her bandaged shoulder.

"The dogs," Naya said. "Dogs don't get bored. Dogs get *smart*."

Silas appeared on the bank as if summoned by bad news, which, Naya thought, was essentially his job description. He stood still for a long moment, head tilted, listening to a silence the rest of them could only guess at. Then he said, very quietly, "Engines. Two of them. Idling upstream around the bend — they've cut back to drift speed."

"Concession boats?" Wren asked.

"Concession boats don't drift." Silas was already moving, scooping up the maps case, his face doing the thing it did when the ranger in him and the fugitive in him agreed for once. "Drifting means they want to arrive quiet. Quiet means they know exactly who's here." He looked at Odell. "How many ways out of this camp?"

"Water's the only honest one," Odell said. He didn't sound afraid. He sounded like a man doing sums he'd done before. "Reed channel behind the fish racks, too shallow for their draft if you pole the first two hundred yards. After that you're in the current and gone by dark."

"We're not leaving you," Naya said.

"Yes, you are," Odell said pleasantly, and handed her a wrapped parcel of fried fish, as though the end of the world were a picnic that simply required provisions. "And here's how it goes, girl, so listen, because I'm only going to say it once and I hate repeating myself almost as much as I hate concessions."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took four minutes to explain and relied entirely on the fact that Aldous Reeve's enforcers were, in Odell's phrase, "men who'd learned authority from a manual written by another manual."

Odell would stay. He would greet them at his dock with his nets spread and his grievances loaded — and Odell had grievances the way rivers had water, deep and renewing. He'd complain about the permit office, the dam works, the price of salt, the disrespect shown to his dogs. He'd stall them the way an old snag stalls a log drive: not by force, but by being impossible to move quickly.

"And when they figure out we're not here?" Wren asked.

"Then they take me in for questioning," Odell said. "Which will be a great relief to everyone involved, because I haven't had anyone to question me properly since my wife passed, and she cheated — she'd just walk out mid-argument."

"That's not funny," Naya said.

"It's a little funny," Odell said. "She'd have liked these three. She'd have liked you especially, girl. You argue like her. All elbows."

"Odell—" Silas started.

"Don't." The old man held up a hand, and for the first time there was no humor in it, just a plainness that hit harder than any speech would have. "Nine years ago I ran a cargo I shouldn't have, and a young ranger caught me at Last Light Crossing. Any other ranger writes the report, I do five years in the Corren work camps, my boys grow up without a father. This one wrote that the cargo was spoiled fish and not worth the paperwork." He nodded at Silas. "I've owed him a debt ever since, and debts in this basin are the only currency that doesn't inflate. Let me pay some of it. A man should get to choose what he's worth."

Naya wanted to argue. She built the argument carefully, plank by plank — *you're old, you're slow, they'll hurt you, we can hide you in the reeds, we can all fit in the boat* — and then she looked at the reed channel, and the two hundred yards of poling under open sky, and the three of them plus an old man plus a dog situation, and she watched the argument fall apart in her hands like wet paper.

Found family, she thought bitterly, meant having something to lose. She hadn't lost anyone in nine days and she'd gotten soft. She'd gotten *safe*. She'd eaten fried fish with her feet in the water.

"Take the dogs," she said instead, because someone had to say something practical or she was going to cry, and Thackeray women — well, no one, actually, but she'd decided on the spot that crying was for after.

"One dog," Odell bargained. "Biscuit. The other two are cowards and Biscuit bites enforcers, which is a public service."

* * *

They pushed off through the reed channel with the sun barely clearing the treetops, poling through water the color of steeped tea, the hull whispering against stalks taller than their heads. Behind them, muffled by reeds, they heard the engines arrive — two boats, gentle, deliberate — and then Odell's voice, carrying across the water with the practiced volume of a man who had spent sixty years making himself heard over rivers and knew exactly how far sound traveled.

"—and ANOTHER thing, your permit office sent a boy last month who couldn't tell a gill net from a hole in his head—"

Wren laughed, then pressed her good hand over her mouth.

"He's buying us time," Silas said, poling steadily, eyes forward.

"He's enjoying himself," Wren said.

"Both things can be true," Naya said. "That's what makes it unbearable."

They poled. Two hundred yards took forever. Odell's voice followed them the whole way — complaints about the dam, the dredging, the state of the ferry landings, an extended digression about a concession surveyor who had once measured his dock wrong and refused to admit it — and then, abruptly, a new voice, smooth and reasonable, and Naya's blood went cold, because she'd never heard Aldous Reeve speak but she knew the shape of that voice instantly, the voice of a man who apologized while taking things.

Then silence from the camp. Then, faintly, Biscuit barking — furious, indignant, bitten-off — and the sound of a dog being carried onto a boat, yowling protest the entire time.

"They took the dog," Wren whispered, horrified.

"They took the *witness*," Silas corrected grimly. "The dog's leverage."

Naya poled until her shoulders burned and the reeds ended and the main channel opened before them, wide and brown and fast with the early monsoon swelling it. She took up the steering oar. Nobody suggested they go back. Everybody thought it. She could see it in their faces — Silas's jaw, Wren's white knuckles on the gunwale — the same thought circling: *we left him.*

"We come back for him," she said aloud, because thoughts that stay thoughts rot. "After the capital. After the hearing. We come back with rangers and warrants and we take him and that horrible dog home."

"Biscuit is a fine animal," Odell's voice said in her memory, *he bites enforcers, which is a public service,* and she gripped the oar harder.

* * *

By midday the sky had gone the color of a bruise and the river had opinions.

The monsoon wasn't due for another week — Wren said so twice, checking the pressure in her aching shoulder like she could read the weather through the wound — but the basin had stopped caring about schedules the moment Reeve's people entered the story. The current had thickened overnight, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past them like the river was clearing its throat. Rain came in bands, hard, then harder, then a brief insulting drizzle, then hard again.

They ran downriver with the satchel of Voss's testimony lashed under the thwart and the maps case double-wrapped in oilcloth, and Naya steered, and guilt sat in the boat with them like a fourth passenger who wouldn't stop talking.

She'd been the one who insisted on Odell's camp. She'd been the one whose brother's letter led them there. Every mile of the trail that ended at Drowned Bend traced back through her — her theft, her grief, her refusal to stop — and now the trail had led to an old man on a dock, choosing to be arrested so that strangers could carry truth to a capital that might not listen.

"You didn't do this," Wren said, reading her face the way she read contour lines. "Reeve did this."

"I brought the map to his door."

"Reeve drew the map," Wren said. "We just finally read it."

Silas said nothing for a long time, which from Silas was practically a eulogy. Then, as the rain thickened again: "When I was a ranger, I told myself moving was cheaper than stopping. You keep ahead of everything — grief, mistakes, your own face in a mirror. Nine years I believed it." He looked at the brown water rushing past. "Turns out stopping is what costs. Stopping means somebody can be taken from you. Moving, you only lose yourself, and you get used to that."

"That's the bleakest thing I've ever heard," Wren said, "and I audit concession budgets."

"It's why we go back for him," Naya said. "All of it. The hearing, the exposure, all of it — that's just the road. Going back for Odell is the destination."

Silas glanced at her, and something passed between them that she couldn't have named — recognition, maybe, of two people who had spent their lives refusing to stop finally agreeing on a reason to.

The river bent east. The rain became a wall. And ahead, where the channel narrowed between drowned orchard trees, Naya saw something that stopped her breath: white water. Real rapids, born overnight, chewing at the banks where last week there had been a lazy green channel any child could run.

"Wren," she called over the rain. "Your charts. Is there a weir on this stretch?"

Wren unfolded the chart under the oilcloth, and Naya watched her face do the thing faces do when good news dies.

"There was," Wren said. "There's an old weir at the narrows — decommissioned, half-dismantled, it shouldn't be a problem at normal water." She looked up at the bruised sky, at the river running high and angry, and finished quietly: "This isn't normal water."

Behind them, faint under the rain, an engine note changed pitch. Not idling anymore. Coming.

Naya set her jaw, put the bow at the heart of the current, and steered for the roar.

"Hold on to the testimony," she said. "And hold on to each other. We're going through."
