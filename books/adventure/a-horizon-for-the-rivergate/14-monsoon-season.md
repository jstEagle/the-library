## 14. Monsoon Season

The river had stopped being a river sometime in the night. By dawn it was a brown animal the length of the horizon, and it wanted them dead.

Naya knew this the way you know a person's mood from across a market square — not by any single sign but by the whole of it: the way the water shouldered past the canyon walls instead of flowing, the way whole trees went by standing upright, roots first like drowned men walking, the way the boat — Odell's patched, patient, long-suffering boat — groaned every time the current shifted its grip.

"Is it supposed to look like that?" she called over the wind.

Silas was at the stern with the sweep oar, both hands white-knuckled on the wood, rain streaming off his hat brim. "Like what?"

"Like it's *angry*."

"It's a river, Naya. Rivers don't get angry."

A half-submerged acacia spun past close enough to touch, its branches combing the water like fingers.

"*Mostly*," Silas amended.

* * *

Wren was feverish again. She lay wedged between the supply crates in the bottom of the boat, wrapped in the oilcloth they'd rigged as a shelter, and her wound — the one she'd taken on the flats shielding the maps, the one she kept insisting was "basically fine" — had gone hot and angry in ways wounds shouldn't. Her skin was flushed dark under its brown, and when Naya pressed the back of her hand to Wren's forehead, the heat came off her like a stove lid.

"You're burning up."

"I'm *fine*," Wren said, with the particular conviction of someone who is not fine. "I'm just — the sun is out."

"The sun has not been out in two days."

"Then I'm radiantly healthy and we should discuss something else." Wren's eyes were glassy but sharp underneath, the way they always were, even now. Even half-drowned in fever, she looked at Naya like she was reading a map of her. "How are your brother's letters holding up?"

That was the question, wasn't it. That was the whole question.

Naya pulled the oilskin packet from inside her shirt — dry, always dry, she would let the river take the boat before she let it take those letters — and unwrapped it for maybe the hundredth time. Tam's handwriting. His looping, impatient, left-slanting scrawl that he'd never bothered to fix because fixing things took effort and Tam had always preferred to simply outrun his flaws.

*The channel bends hard left after the second red bluff,* he'd written, four months ago, in a letter she hadn't opened until he was already gone. *Don't take the right fork no matter how calm it looks. The calm water is where it eats people.*

She'd read that line forty times since Drowned Bend. *The calm water is where it eats people.* Tam had known this river the way she knew rooftops — intimately, illegally, and with a running commentary of jokes. Fourteen letters, sent over two years while he guided survey teams through country the permits said didn't exist. She'd stacked them unopened in her footlocker because opening them meant admitting he was doing something dangerous, and admitting that meant worrying, and worrying was a tax she'd told herself she couldn't afford.

The tax had come due anyway. It always did.

"Naya." Silas's voice cut through the rain. "I need you up here. I can't read water and steer at the same time, and my reading isn't good enough for this."

She tucked the letters away and crawled forward along the gunwale.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about navigating by dead brother: it works. That's the terrible part. It works beautifully. Every letter was a piece of the river, and together they were almost a map — bends and bluffs, snags and sandbars, the places where the current ran honest and the places where it lied. Tam had written it all down with the cheerful thoroughness of a boy who'd grown up memorizing escape routes, and now his little sister was steering three lives down a flooded hell-channel using nothing but his voice in her head.

*Past the leaning fig tree, hug the left bank until you hear the weir, then pray.*

There was no fig tree anymore. There was no bank anymore. The monsoon had redrawn everything, drowned the landmarks, turned Tam's careful instructions into poetry — beautiful, useless poetry.

Except not entirely. Because some things held. The high bluffs still stood. The deep channels still ran where deep channels had always run. And Tam hadn't just described the river — he'd described its *character*. Where it got greedy. Where it got lazy. Where it liked to pretend to be gentle.

"Hug left!" Naya shouted, pointing at a stretch of water that looked identical to every other stretch of water, all of it brown and furious and going somewhere very fast. "Tam says the right side has a snag field under the surface!"

"Your brother says, or your brother *said*?" Silas asked, and then immediately winced, because Silas Pemberton had exactly two modes — silent and tactless — and occasionally they fired simultaneously.

"He *says*," Naya snapped. "He says plenty. He's the only one on this boat who's actually run this water."

To his credit, Silas didn't argue. He hauled the sweep oar left, and the boat swung wide, and thirty seconds later something enormous and pale rolled past on their starboard side — a submerged cottonwood trunk the size of a temple pillar, its broken branches reaching up through the flood like a trap waiting to be sprung.

Silas looked at the snag. Then at Naya. Then back at the snag.

"Noted," he said.

"Don't apologize," Naya said. "Just listen."

"I'm listening."

"And stop steering like the river's a suspect you're chasing. Steer like it's a market crowd. You don't fight a crowd. You find the gaps."

Something flickered across Silas's weathered face — surprise, maybe, or the expression of a man discovering that the thief he'd once chased across the salt flats had opinions about hydrology. But he adjusted. The boat stopped fighting quite so hard. And when the next bend came, he asked, quietly, "Left or right?"

"Left. Always left here. *The calm water is where it eats people.*"

* * *

They ran the rapids at midday, if you could call it running. Really the river ran them. The boat went where the water decided, and Silas's oar and Naya's shouting only negotiated the terms.

It was the worst hour of Naya's life, and she'd once spent a night in a Corren's Landing jail cell with a man who sang sea shanties about his ex-wife.

The river dropped them through a chute between two rock ribs, and for eleven seconds — she counted, later, obsessively, the way you count things that nearly kill you — for eleven seconds the boat was less a boat than a cork with opinions. Water came aboard in sheets. The crates broke loose. The maps — Voss's maps, the maps worth killing for, the maps currently responsible for approximately all of their problems — slid toward the open gunwale like they had somewhere better to be.

Naya did not think. Thinking was for afterward. She threw herself flat, caught the map case by its strap as it went over the side, and ended up draped across the gunwale with her entire upper body hanging over brown chaos, one arm in the river up to the shoulder, while Silas screamed her name in a voice she'd never heard him use before.

Then his fist closed on the back of her collar and hauled, and she came over the side in a rush of river water and triumph, gasping, clutching the case to her chest like a stolen purse.

Which, she supposed, it was.

"You *idiot*," Silas said. He was kneeling over her, soaked, shaking — actually shaking, this granite man who never shook. "You absolute — you could have —"

"But I didn't." She held up the case. "Maps intact. You're welcome."

"That's not —" He stopped. Ran a hand down his face. Started again, slower. "Next time, the maps go in the river and you stay in the boat. Promise me."

"No."

"*Naya.*"

"They're not maps, Silas. They're Tam. They're the reason any of this happened, and they're the reason it'll matter. I'm not letting go of them." She wrung out her sleeve and met his eyes. "You chased me across nine miles of salt flat for a compass worth six coppers. Don't tell me you don't understand."

He stared at her for a long moment. Rain drummed on the boat. Somewhere behind them, the rapids continued their argument with the canyon.

"Yeah," he said finally. "All right. But hold on with your legs next time. Your arms are for catching."

"That's what Tam used to say."

"I know," Silas said. "Odell told me. I've been trying to steal his best material for years."

And there it was — the thing she'd been refusing to notice for days, arriving despite herself: warmth. Not comfort exactly. Something rougher and more durable than comfort. This man who'd spent his whole life moving so he'd never have to stop and feel anything was sitting knee-deep in river water, making jokes about her dead brother, because that was how he knew how to say *you matter to me now, and I'm bad at saying it.*

Naya decided to let him have it. People who were bad at saying things deserved credit for attempting.

* * *

They found a gravel shelf below the rapids where the current slackened, and beached the boat to bail and breathe. Wren had slept through the entire rapid run, which either meant the fever had her completely or she trusted them absolutely, and Naya genuinely could not tell which was worse.

She got Wren upright against a crate and fed her willow bark tea from the dented kettle, and Wren drank it and made a face like it had personally insulted her family.

"This tastes like punishment."

"It tastes like medicine. Same thing."

"In Corren's Landing the medicines taste like mint and money."

"In Corren's Landing the medicines are also priced like mint and money. Drink."

Wren drank. Then, quietly, without looking up: "You caught the maps today."

"Somebody had to. You were busy being radiant with health."

"Naya." Wren's fever-bright eyes found hers and held. "I've been keeping accounts since I joined the survey office. Debts, favors, who owes whom. It's how you survive a bureaucracy. And I want you to know that whatever happens at the end of this river — whatever this costs us — the account between you and me is settled forever. Paid in full. You understand? Whatever you need, after. However far you run. You call, and I come."

Naya hadn't expected it. Her throat did something embarrassing. She covered it by fussing with the tea kettle.

"You're feverish," she managed. "You're making dramatic declarations at a thief on a gravel bar. When you're well you'll deny everything."

"Probably," Wren agreed placidly, and lay back down, and was asleep within a minute, which was probably for the best, because her hand had found Naya's wrist on the way down and squeezed once, deliberately, and Naya needed a moment where nobody could see her face.

* * *

Silas took first watch, which meant he sat on a rock facing upstream getting rained on, radiating vigilance at a river that could not possibly sneak up on anyone. Naya sat beside him anyway, mostly because her body had decided it was done moving and sitting next to him required no decisions.

For a while neither of them spoke. The rain filled the silence the way water fills a cup.

"My brother wrote me fourteen letters," she said eventually. "I opened the first one the day after they disappeared. I've been living in them ever since. Like — moving into a house he built." She turned the oilskin packet over in her hands. "You know what the worst part is? They're funny. He's funny, in them. He complains about the survey food. He describes Voss like she's a force of nature. He says—" her voice caught, steadied— "he says the last letter, he says he's coming home after this job. Says he'll take me up the drowned canyons himself. Show me what the government's hiding."

Silas said nothing for a while. When he spoke, his voice was low, pitched under the rain.

"When I was twenty-two, my mother sent me letters. Eleven of them. I was posted out on the east flats and I was — busy. Important. Chasing rustlers and permit violations and feeling like the badge made me somebody." He watched the water. "She died in the spring floods. I found the letters unopened when I cleared out her house. Eleven of them. I've read them so many times I can recite them, and it doesn't change a single thing about how it went."

Naya looked at him. Rain ran off his hat brim and dripped steadily into the space between them.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you think you're the only one carrying it." He shrugged, awkward, a man handling something fragile with hands built for rope work. "You're not. Half this basin is walking around with unopened letters. We just don't show each other."

The river roared past, brown and endless, going wherever rivers go.

"We're going to make it," Naya said. Not a question. A decision. "Down this river, to the capital, with the maps and Voss and all of it. We're going to finish what Tam started. And then I'm going to sit on a roof somewhere and read all fourteen letters in daylight, slowly, like a person with time. And you're going to sit there too, because you owe me eleven recitations, and I want to hear how your mother signed them."

Silas's mouth did something complicated that on another man might have been the beginning of a smile.

"She signed them 'come home when you're ready,'" he said. "Every one."

"Well then," Naya said, and stood, and stretched, and felt the day's terror settle into something she could carry. "Best get ready, ranger. River won't wait, and neither will I."

* * *

They launched at dusk into water gone the color of strong tea, and made four miles before the light failed completely. Naya navigated by sound — Tam had taught her that much, in the letters, in the margins: *when you can't see, listen; the river tells you what it's about to do a full breath before it does it.*

She heard the bend coming. She heard the shallows on the right, the deep throat on the left. She heard —

She heard something else.

Faint, downstream, threading through the rain and the roar: a sound no river makes. A rhythmic, mechanical knocking, regular as a heartbeat. An engine. More than one.

She put her hand on Silas's arm. He was already listening, head cocked, and she watched the last of the ease go out of his shoulders.

"How far?" he murmured.

"Close. Around the next bend." Naya's mouth had gone dry. "Reeve's enforcers run engines on the flood channels. Tam wrote about it. They patrol the drowned canyons this time of year."

Silas looked back at Wren, sleeping in the bilge. At the maps. At the black water ahead, and the blacker water behind.

"Kill the lantern," he said quietly. "And Naya — whatever you hear around that bend —"

"I know," she said, easing the sweep oar into the water, steering them into the deepest, darkest channel her brother had ever described. "*The calm water is where it eats people.* Let's hope it eats them first."
