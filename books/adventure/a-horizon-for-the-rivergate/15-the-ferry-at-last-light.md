## 15. The Ferry at Last Light

The river told us we were close before we saw it.

You learn to read water the way you learn to read people — by what it does when it thinks nobody's watching. For two days it had been all muscle and temper, a brown torrent hauling trees and the occasional drowned fence post past us like a man late for an appointment. Then, in the last hour of light, it slowed. Widened. Went almost polite.

"Crossing," Naya said. She was standing in the bow of our patched, complaining boat with one hand on the stays, her hair plastered to her skull by spray, and she said it the way other people say *home*. "The Ferry at Last Light. My mother crossed here twice a year. There's a bell."

"A bell," Wren said from the bilge, where she'd been bailing with the grim rhythm of someone who has made peace with futility. She looked up. Her fever had broken that morning, which was the first good news we'd had since the weir, and she was still pale enough that her freckles looked painted on. "A bell. In this. On purpose."

"The bell isn't for the river," Naya said. "It's for the people."

I kept my eyes on the far bank and my hands on the tiller and said nothing, which is a skill I've practiced longer than any other.

Because here is the thing I hadn't said out loud, not to Wren, not to Naya, not to the boat, which I probably should have apologized to at some point: I knew this crossing. I had stood on this bank nine years ago — no, eight, the year the permits came through for the eastern routes — with a ranger's sash on my shoulder and a ledger in my hand, and I had watched a family argue with a concession clerk about whether their ferry was a *licensed transport operation*. It wasn't. It was a rope, two barges, and forty years of knowing the river's moods better than the river did. The clerk fined them. I stood there. I remember thinking the fine was excessive. I remember thinking that very clearly, and I remember writing my name on the form anyway, because the fine was legal, and in those days I still believed that legal and right were cousins who wrote to each other at midwinter.

The bell, when we heard it, was exactly as I remembered: not a church bell, not a ship's bell, something between — a bronze note with a crack in it, like a voice that had been shouting across water for so long it had made its peace with imperfection. It rang three times. Somewhere in the reeds on the far bank, lanterns began to bloom.

"Silas." Wren had hauled herself up onto the thwart beside me. She didn't say anything else. She didn't have to. She'd read my face the way I'd taught her to read water, back when I still thought I had things worth teaching.

"I know this place," I said.

"I figured."

"I wrote citations here."

"I figured that too." She squeezed my arm. Her hand was still fever-hot. "Then you'd better figure out what you're going to say, because we're about to need everything they have, and as far as I can tell, our assets are one stolen boat, one forged permit we're using as kindling, and a bag of maps that half the government wants drowned."

"And my badge," Naya said from the bow, without turning around. "His badge. Which I'm still holding as collateral, by the way. Sentimental value only. It's very shiny."

"It's not shiny, it's tarnished."

"It's *character*, Silas."

The current took us in then, gentle as a hand on the small of the back, and there was no more time for talking.

* * *

The Ferry at Last Light was not what the maps said it was, which was nothing, because the new maps said it wasn't there at all. It was a shelf of packed earth and moored barges, a long low house with a roof of woven reeds, drying racks hung with fish, and — this was the part that caught in my throat, though I'd have denied it under oath — children. Three of them, running along the bank with the lanterns, shouting to each other in the river-cant that the permits had officially classified as a dialect and unofficially as an inconvenience.

A woman came down to the water's edge ahead of the others. She was broad and gray-streaked and moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who has pulled drowning people out of this river and will again, and I knew her. Of course I knew her. Maren Osei. She'd been the one arguing with the clerk, eight years ago. She'd called me — I remember this with the terrible clarity of shame — *the polite one*. As in: *at least the polite one looks embarrassed.*

The boat touched the bank. I stepped out into the mud, and forty pairs of eyes — because the whole crossing had come down to watch, and why wouldn't they, strangers arriving by flood at last light — settled on me like weather.

"Maren Osei," I said.

"Ranger Pemberton." She said the title the way you'd say the name of a disease that's been mostly cured. Her eyes went past me to the boat: Wren swaying on her feet, gray-faced; Naya already scrambling to secure the line, competent as a deckhand twice her age; the patched hull; the wrapped bundle of maps lashed under the thwart, which everyone on this river had learned to read as either treasure or plague. "You're bleeding, you're burning, and your boat sounds like a kettle with opinions. What do you want?"

I had prepared a speech. I want you to know I'd prepared a speech. I'd been composing it for the last two miles of river, something about duty and justice and the truth, something that would make what we carried sound like it mattered to *them*, something that would make me sound like less of what I was.

What came out instead was: "I wrote you a fine."

The words just fell out of me, the way a stone falls out of a boot you've finally sat down to empty.

"Eight years ago. I stood on this bank and I watched them fine your family for crossing your own river, and I wrote my name on the paper, and I've thought about it every season since, and I don't—" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "I don't have a speech. I have a warrant out for my arrest now, so I suppose the institution and I have finally agreed on something. And we're carrying proof that every permit in this basin was built on forged maps, and I'm asking you — I, specifically, the polite one — to help us anyway."

Silence. The kind of silence a riverbank can hold is enormous. Behind me I heard Naya stop fidgeting, and Wren stop breathing, and somewhere a child ask their grandmother something in cant and get shushed.

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked at Naya.

"Whose girl is this?"

"Nobody's," Naya said. "I'm mine."

"She has Sefu's chin," Maren said. "Sefu Adisa's girl. He ferried with us two seasons, before the eastern permits." She said it simply, the way you say a name that has become a place in you. Naya's jaw did the thing it did when her brother came up — the clench, the swallow — and Maren saw it, because women who pull drowning people from rivers see everything. "He's the one who taught my boys to read water. Come up to the house, all of you. You'll eat. Then you'll tell me about these maps."

"And after that?" Wren asked. Her voice was rough with fever's leftovers.

"After that," Maren said, turning up the bank, "you'll sleep. Nobody drowns at my crossing, Ranger. Not even the ones who deserve to."

* * *

They fed us fish stew with river greens and a grain I hadn't tasted since I was a boy, and it was so good I nearly wept into the bowl, and I'm fairly sure Wren actually did, though she blamed the steam.

The long house was full in the way that only displaced places are full — every surface doing two jobs, every person holding three. An old man repaired a net by lamplight and listened to everything. Two teenage boys argued in whispers about whether the strangers were famous or fugitives, and settled, correctly, on both. The children had adopted Naya within minutes, drawn to her the way cats are drawn to the one person in the room who claims not to like cats, and she was showing them — I watched this with my heart doing something unprofessional — how to fold a map so it fits in a sleeve.

And then Maren sat down across from me with two cups of something strong and bitter, and said, "Tell me the part about the warrant."

So I told her all of it. The survey team, the compass, the forgeries, the aquifer, the ambush on the flats, Wren's shoulder, the weir, the boat, the canyon, Voss. I told it plainly, the way I'd file a report, except that no report I ever filed had my own name in the column where the criminal goes.

When I finished, Maren drank her tea and looked into the middle distance, which on this river is a trained skill.

"Do you know what the concession men said, when they closed the eastern routes?" she asked. "They didn't say the crossing was illegal. They said it was *redundant*. That word. My grandmother's ferry, redundant." She turned the cup in her hands. "They put a line on a map, and the line said this place was a mistake, and men with paper came to make the land agree with the paper. And now you arrive carrying the paper that proves the paper was a lie."

"That's the size of it."

"And you want us to carry you into the capital on it."

"I want you to get us across the lowlands. That's all. After that, Naya has a plan that doesn't involve your boats, and it's a better plan than any I'd have made, because every plan I'd have made starts with a ranger station, and I'm done with ranger stations."

Maren set down her cup. "Eight years ago you stood on my bank and you looked like a man swallowing a stone. I remember thinking: that one will either break or leave. Most of them leave." She looked at me, and her eyes were not unkind, which was somehow harder than if they had been. "You left. And now you've come back carrying the stone. Why should that buy you anything?"

"It shouldn't," I said. "It's not a payment. It's just the truth. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to let me be useful."

She was quiet for a while. Outside, the river went on saying the only thing it has ever said.

"You know what my grandmother used to say about the ferry?" Maren said at last. "She said: a crossing isn't a place, it's a promise. Anyone who reaches the water is already halfway to being someone new. Our job is only to not let the river finish the job first." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and raised her voice to the room. "Sefu's girl and her friends sleep here tonight. Boats at first light — the *Kestrel*, she's the only hull that'll take the lowland channels in flood. Toma, wake the pole crews."

A murmur went through the room, and I braced for the edge in it, the resentment, the *why should we*. What I heard instead, from the old man with the net, was: "The polite one. Huh. Always thought he'd come back."

I didn't sleep so much as surrender to it, on a woven mat between Wren's fevered breathing and the sound of Naya telling the children, in a whisper I was absolutely meant to hear, that yes, the tall one was a real ranger once, and no, he wasn't as scary as he looked, and yes, she'd stolen his compass and he'd only chased her a little.

* * *

First light came gray and soft, the storm spent down to a steady generous rain, and the *Kestrel* was a barge older than me with a new patch over her old wounds, which felt right. Maren herself took the steering oar. When I tried to object, she looked at me until I stopped.

The whole crossing came down to the bank to see us off, which they would have done for anyone, but they pressed things into our hands that they would not have done for anyone: dried fish, a jar of the bitter tea, a child's carved wooden boat "for luck, it worked for my uncle," and — from the old man with the net, pressed into Wren's hands with great ceremony — a fishing lure he described as "ugly but honest, like most useful things."

Naya stood at the rail beside me as the bank fell away. For a while she said nothing, which for Naya is a full conversation.

"He ferried here," she said finally. "My brother. Before the survey. I never came with him because I was—" She made a gesture that encompassed thieving, pride, and nine years of refusing to stop. "He wrote me that this was the one place the permits couldn't reach. He was wrong about a lot of things, but he wasn't wrong about that."

"No," I said. "He wasn't."

Maren, at the oar, spoke without turning. "The capital road's flooded past the weir, so you'll go by the old drover paths — high ground, nomad waystations every half-day. Word travels faster than water on those paths. By the time you reach the capital, half the basin will know what you're carrying." She glanced back, and there was something in her face that was almost a smile. "Including, I'd wager, the men who want it drowned. You've made noise, Ranger. Now you'd best make it louder."

We came around the bend into the open lowlands, and that was when we saw it — far upstream, where the river narrowed, a boat with a concession pennant snapping in the rain, and men in it, and glass catching the light.

Binoculars. Pointed at us.

Maren saw them too. Her hands never changed on the oar. "Well," she said calmly. "Toma was right. We should have left at dark."

Naya was already moving to the maps. Wren was already counting our food. And I stood at the rail of a barge full of other people's kindness, watching trouble come down the river toward the only family, found or otherwise, that had ever taken me in — and understood that whatever happened next, I was done running from the cost of stopping.

"Silas," Wren said quietly, "tell me you have a plan."

I watched the pennant grow. "I have a ferry," I said, "a grandmother with a steering oar, and a river that's on our side for the first time in nine years. It's a start."
