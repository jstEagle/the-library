## 16. What Reeve Wants

Aldous Reeve believed in many things, and this was precisely what made him dangerous.

He believed in the sanctity of ledgers. He believed that a man's word, properly notarized, was worth more than his life. He believed that the Rivergate Basin—six hundred square miles of salt, silt, and seasonal madness—could be made legible, and that anything made legible could be made profitable, and that anything made profitable could be made permanent. He believed these things the way other men believed in weather: completely, without argument, and with a certain resigned tenderness toward those who did not.

What he did not believe in was chaos. Chaos was simply order that hadn't been filed yet.

Which is why, on the fourth morning after the fisherman was brought in, Reeve found himself personally supervising a breakfast tray.

"Poached eggs," he said, setting it down on the crate that served as the man's table. "The cook over-salts everything, I'm afraid. Occupational hazard of a garrison kitchen. The tea is from the capital, though—actual leaf, not the dust they sell at Corren's Landing."

Odell Brack sat on the floor of the supply shed with his wrists bound in front of him and his back against the wall, and he looked at the breakfast tray the way a man looks at a snake wearing a hat.

"You've gone to trouble," Odell said.

"I have."

"For me."

"For you," Reeve agreed, and sat down on an upturned barrel across from him, arranging his coat with the care of a man settling into a theater seat. "You'll forgive the accommodations. We weren't expecting guests of quality."

"This is a shed."

"It is a shed," Reeve conceded. "With excellent drainage, which is more than most of the Basin can say this time of year." Outside, the monsoon hammered the tin roof like an impatient creditor. Water ran past the door in a brown ribbon. Somewhere upriver, the world was drowning, and here was Aldous Reeve, sitting on a barrel with a poached egg, conducting a conversation.

Odell looked at the eggs for a long moment. Then he looked at Reeve, and there was something in the look that Reeve found genuinely interesting—not fear, not hate, but the flat patient assessment of a man who had spent forty years reading water and had decided to read him instead.

"You're the one who owns the maps," Odell said.

"I own the *concession*," Reeve said. "The maps are government property. I merely—" he smiled, "—appreciate them."

"Appreciate."

"Deeply."

* * *

Reeve had interviewed perhaps two hundred people in his career. Smugglers, surveyors, clerks who skimmed, clerks who didn't skim but were inconvenient anyway. He had learned early that everyone wanted something, and that the fastest route through any conversation was to find out what, and then stand next to it.

The fisherman, by all reports, wanted nothing. This was rare enough to be charming.

"Let me tell you about my father," Reeve said, because he had learned that the second-fastest route through a conversation was to make it about yourself, briefly, sincerely, and then never again.

Odell said nothing, which Reeve chose to interpret as encouragement.

"He was a ferryman. Down south, on the Vell. A good one—the best, actually, though he'd have denied it, because ferrymen are constitutionally incapable of admitting competence in front of their customers. He knew that river the way you know your canyons. Every sandbar, every snag, every place the current turned treacherous after rain. Forty years of knowledge. And do you know what happened to it?"

"He died," Odell said.

"He died," Reeve agreed, "and within a season the river changed course, as rivers do, and every scrap of what he knew became worthless. Not wrong—*worthless*. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. His knowledge wasn't lost because it failed. It was lost because it lived only in him. It couldn't be owned, so it couldn't be kept, so it died with him." He leaned forward slightly. "I was eleven years old. I watched a lifetime of genius evaporate because nobody wrote it down. Because nobody could *hold* it."

"And that made you want to hold things."

"That made me understand that unheld things die," Reeve said. "Yes."

The rain filled the pause. Odell reached out—awkwardly, wrists still bound—and took a piece of bread from the tray, and ate it with the unhurried dignity of a man refusing to be rushed even in captivity.

"So you drew lines on maps," Odell said.

"So I drew lines on maps," Reeve said, pleased. "You say it like an accusation. Consider what the lines do. Before my permits, the Basin was used by everyone and maintained by no one. Nomads crossed where they liked; surveyors surveyed; fishermen fished. And every year the salt crept further into the grazing land, and the wells went brackish, and the floods took another village, because *everyone's responsibility is no one's*. My lines gave the land an owner. An owner plants. An owner dams. An owner drains. Order, Mr. Brack, requires someone to own the map entirely. Half-owned maps are how basins die."

"The nomads had routes older than your government."

"The nomads had routes," Reeve said, "and routes are not ownership. Routes are habits. Beautiful ones—I don't deny it. But a habit can't be audited." He spread his hands. "I am not a cruel man, whatever you've heard. I have displaced people, yes. I have paid them. Some of them are angry, and some of them are richer than they've ever been, and both groups would tell you I'm a monster, which tells you something about the impossibility of the work."

"You talk like a priest," Odell said.

"I talk like a man who has thought about it," Reeve said. "That's rarer, and more frightening, I know."

* * *

Here was the thing about Aldous Reeve that his enemies consistently got wrong: they assumed he lied.

He almost never lied. Lying was a tool for men without better tools. What Reeve did was select truths—arrange them, light them well, present them in sequence like courses at a dinner—and the selection was ruthless, but each individual dish was genuine. When he told Odell Brack that he respected him, he meant it. When he told him that the alternative to the concession was not freedom but entropy, he believed it down to his shoes. This was why clerks trusted him, why ministers signed his papers, why men who should have known better shook his hand and walked away feeling *reasonable*.

And it was why, now, he set down his teacup and said, quite honestly:

"I need three names, Mr. Brack. Or rather, I need one location, and I suspect the names come attached."

"The ranger," Odell said. "The cartographer. The girl."

"Pemberton, Oduya, and the thief, yes." Reeve nodded. "Though I'd caution you against 'thief.' She stole a compass that was arguably hers by inheritance. I find I respect that. There's a clarity to it."

"They're dead by now," Odell said. "River like this."

"Perhaps." Reeve let the word sit. "But the river has been generous to them so far, hasn't it? A boat that shouldn't have floated, floating. A channel that shouldn't have been open, open. I've stopped believing in coincidence on this river. It has opinions."

Odell chewed his bread. Rain drummed. Reeve waited, because waiting was free and talking cost.

"You're going to offer me something," Odell said finally.

"I'm going to offer you everything," Reeve said. "Freedom, first. You walk out of this shed this afternoon. Not a warrant, not a record, not a mark. Your boat—which we confiscated, and which I apologize for—is returned with a new coat of pitch and a repaired stern seam that frankly needed it before we ever touched it. Your camp on Drowned Bend stays yours. No permit required. Ever. In writing, sealed, mine to give."

"And?"

"And a stipend. Modest. Enough that winters stop being arithmetic." Reeve paused, and then, because sincerity was his sharpest instrument, added quietly: "And my word, which I have never broken, that no harm comes to you or yours for as long as I draw breath."

"That's a lot of everything."

"It is," Reeve said. "Because what I'm asking for is worth it. One location. Where did Pemberton take Voss's maps? Where is the testimony?"

Odell was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice had changed—not harder, exactly, but deeper, the way water gets deeper over a hole.

"My brother drowned in the Vell when I was nineteen," he said. "Ferry accident. Current took him right in front of me, and I couldn't reach him, because the river doesn't negotiate. After that I came up here, to the canyons, because I wanted to live somewhere the water couldn't surprise me. Learned every stone. Forty years, Mr. Reeve. Every stone." He looked up. "Then your dam works flooded them. Drowned my canyon the way the Vell drowned my brother. So don't talk to me about order. Your order is just a flood with paperwork."

Reeve absorbed this without flinching. "I'm sorry about your canyon."

"I know you are," Odell said. "That's the worst part. You mean it. And you'd drown ten more tomorrow and mean that too."

"Yes," Reeve said simply. "I would. Grief and conviction aren't opposites, Mr. Brack. They're colleagues."

* * *

He left the fisherman alone with the eggs after that. Not from frustration—from strategy. Men like Odell Brack didn't break under pressure; they broke under kindness, slowly, the way stone broke under frost. Reeve had time. The storm that trapped the fugitives also trapped them, narrowed the river to a throat, funneled everything downstream toward the few crossings that existed. He had enforcers at the weirs, at the ferry crossings, at the old ford below the dam works. The Basin, for once, was working *for* him—a map drawn in water, and every drop of it his.

In the evening he returned with more tea and found Odell looking out the shed's single window at the rain.

"May I ask you something?" Reeve said. "Not business. Curiosity."

"You may ask."

"The ranger. Pemberton. Nine years chasing petty thieves across the flats, enforcing the very permit edges he hated, and never once—according to his file—taken a bribe, cut a corner, or quit. Why does a man like that exist? What keeps him moving?"

Odell considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"Fear," he said at last. "Same as you. You're afraid of the map coming apart. He's afraid of stopping. Man who's afraid of stopping will run straight through hell if hell's in the direction he was already going." He glanced back at Reeve, and for the first time there was something almost like pity in the old face. "You should understand something about Silas Pemberton, since you're spending so much money trying to catch him. He doesn't stop for himself. Never has. But put someone behind him who needs protecting, and he'll stop anywhere. Stand anywhere. Die anywhere." Odell smiled, small and dry. "You haven't offered him anything yet. When you do, you'll learn what I just learned watching you with these eggs."

"Which is?"

"That some men can't be bought," Odell said, "because they already sold themselves to something cheaper-looking and worth more."

Reeve stood, brushed off his coat, and collected the tray. At the door he paused.

"The offer stands until the storm breaks," he said. "After that, the river decides, and the river works for me now. Think about your stones, Mr. Brack. All forty years of them. Ask yourself what they'd want."

"They'd want the water to stop rising," Odell said.

Reeve smiled, inclined his head, and stepped out into the rain.

Inside the shed, alone, Odell Brack finished the cold tea, and then—he had been working at it since the first night, patiently, with fingers that had spent a lifetime on knots—slipped the last loop of rope off his left wrist, looked at it, and put it back on.

Not yet, he thought. Not yet. But soon, and downriver, and God help the man at the weir.

Because Aldous Reeve had made one mistake, small and fatal, in all his beautiful ordering: he had assumed the fisherman's silence was for sale at any price, when in fact Odell had simply been waiting—forty years a student of water—to learn which way the current was going to turn.

Outside, the storm bent the trees along the bank, and far upstream, at a ruined weir where the brown torrent ran white over broken stone, three figures in a patched boat were about to come around the bend.
