## 17. Ambush at the Weir

I had begun to think of the boat as a person. A bad-tempered, leak-prone person with a limp and a grudge, but a person all the same. Wren Oduya did not name her tools—cartographers learn early that naming things makes you sentimental about them—but somewhere between the drowned bend and the third day of monsoon, I had named her anyway. *Stubborn.* She fit.

"Starboard seam's weeping again," Naya said from the bow, her voice pitched low under the drumming rain. She had her hand pressed flat against the hull the way you'd check a child for fever. "I can hear it."

"You can hear a seam weeping over a river that sounds like a landslide?"

"I can hear everything over a river that sounds like a landslide. It's the quiet I can't hear through." She glanced back at me, rain streaming off her chin, and grinned. It was a feral, exhausted grin, and I found, to my considerable alarm, that I had grown fond of it. "You're a mapmaker. You're supposed to notice things."

"I notice things on *paper*," I said. "Paper holds still. Paper has never once tried to kill me."

Silas, at the stern with the sweep oar, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough. Hard to tell, with Silas. He'd taken to steering with his left arm favored, though he claimed the right one was fine, and I had stopped arguing because arguing with Silas Pemberton about his own body was like arguing with a river about which way it intended to flow. You lost, and you got wet doing it.

We came around a bend of red clay bluffs, and there it was.

The weir.

I knew it from the old charts—the ones predating the concession, the ones I'd spent three years quietly correcting in the government archives while my supervisors looked the other way. A low stone weir, built a century ago to raise the river's level for a mill that no longer existed, its gates long rotted to stumps. On the old maps it was marked with a careful little symbol and a note in a dead surveyor's hand: *passable at high water, right channel only.*

On Reeve's maps, it did not exist at all.

"Right channel," I called to Silas. "There's a gap in the stones on the right. The old survey says—"

"I see it." His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when his eyes were doing three things at once. "Wren. Look at the bank."

I looked.

There were horses tied above the weir, under the leaning fig trees on the left bluff. Four of them. And men moving among the rocks along the crest of the weir itself, taking up positions with the unhurried economy of people who had done this many times and expected, this time, to be bored.

"They followed us from Odell's camp," Naya said. All the wildness had gone out of her voice. What replaced it was worse—cold, precise grief. "They took him and they *followed us*."

"Or they knew the route already," Silas said. "The weir's the only way through this stretch. If Reeve's people read the old charts the way Wren does, they didn't need to follow anyone." He shipped the oar halfway and looked back at us, rain running down his face, and for a moment he looked very tired and very old in a way that had nothing to do with years. "They're not shooting yet. That means they want something first."

"They want the maps," I said.

"They want the maps," he agreed. "And they want us to hand them over believing we'll live through the handing."

"And will we?"

Silas was quiet for a moment. The river roared around us, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past at a walking pace. "Reeve strikes me as a man who keeps his promises exactly as long as they're useful," he said. "So. Options."

"Go through," Naya said immediately.

"Through four armed men on a weir with one gap in it, in floodwater."

"You said the right channel's passable."

"I said the *old survey* says it's passable. The old survey was written before the dam raised the base flow by eleven feet." I hated the mathematics of it even as I said it. "The gap's still there. But the water over it will be standing waves and backwash. It's not a channel anymore. It's a throat."

"Then we go down the throat," Naya said.

"Or," Silas said, "we go down the throat while I make them think we're doing something else."

* * *

What he proposed was simple, the way most terrible things are simple. He would run the boat at the left bank—hard, obvious, surrender-shaped—close enough to the rocks for their leader to hail us. And while every eye and every rifle on that weir fixed itself on the ranger who was, as far as the warrants said, a murderer and a thief, we would swing hard at the last moment for the right channel and take our chances with the throat.

"They'll shoot," I said.

"They'll shoot at me. I'll be the one holding the oar, looking like a man who's given up." He said it lightly, but I had spent enough days with Silas Pemberton by then to hear the seam of something else under it. "Wren, the maps. Whatever happens, the maps go in the oilskin and the oilskin goes on you. You're the smallest and the best swimmer."

"I'm the *cartographer*. I'm the least expendable person on this boat. I'm the only one who can *read*—"

"You're the only one who can read them, which is exactly why they go on you." He held my eyes, and the gentleness in his face was worse than any argument. "Naya can navigate by her brother's letters. I can navigate by nine years of this river. You're the only one who can prove what's on those pages is true. That makes you the mission now, Wren. Not the boat. Not me."

I wanted to tell him he was wrong. I wanted to tell him that maps are just paper, that people are the point, that I had spent my whole career learning the opposite lesson—that a map is a person, drawn in ink, and that losing either one is the same loss. But the horses on the bluff were shifting, and a man on the weir crest had raised something to his mouth, and the river was not going to wait for my philosophy.

I sealed the maps in the oilskin roll, slung it across my chest, and cinched it tight enough to bruise.

"Right channel," I said. "On your mark."

* * *

The first hundred yards went exactly as Silas planned, which should have warned me.

He ran us at the left bank with the oar angled like a man steering for capture, and the figures on the weir rose up and waved—waved!—one of them calling something through the rain about surrender and safe conduct, and I saw Silas's shoulders drop into the shape of a beaten man, and I thought, with a strange, detached admiration, *he's good, he's so good at this, he's been playing at surrender his whole life—*

Then he heaved the oar over and the boat spun.

The river took us sideways. That was the first mistake—mine, not his. I'd calculated the current off the old charts, off the pre-dam figures, and the flood river laughed at my arithmetic. We came around the swing and the throat of the right channel was not where my mind had drawn it. It was ten yards further downstream, and the standing waves above it were not waves. They were a wall.

"They see us!" Naya screamed. "Silas—"

The first shot cracked flat and ugly across the water, and something punched through the hull below my knee. The second took the stern board apart in a spray of old wood. Silas threw his whole body across the oar, and the boat, *Stubborn*, the bad-tempered, leaking, beloved wreck of a person, gave us everything she had left. She drove for the gap.

The world went white.

Not metaphorically. The standing wave at the weir's crest stood taller than the boat, and we went through it the way a letter goes through a flame—briefly, loudly, and never quite the same. I remember the sensation of the river picking the boat up and considering it. I remember Naya's hand closing on my collar. I remember thinking, with the last coherent part of my mind: *the oilskin is cinched, the oilskin is cinched, the maps are—*

Then the hull hit stone and came apart, and the river had us.

* * *

Cold is a strange thing in monsoon water. You'd think brown floodwater in a warm country would be warm. It isn't. It comes off mountains somewhere behind the rain, and it is cold the way a fist is cold, and it closed around me and turned me over and over like a hand examining something small.

I have nearly drowned exactly once before, at nine, in a mill pond, and I remembered the lesson of it even as the river applied it again: *don't fight the water, find the water's plan and agree with it.* So I stopped thrashing. I let it roll me. I got my face up for half a breath, and in that half breath I saw the boat—what was left of it, a third of a hull, spinning in the backwash below the weir.

And I saw the oilskin's strap.

No. Not the strap. The *end* of the strap. The cinch knot I had tied in a shaking hurry on the deck had torn half free, and the roll was working loose across my chest, and the river was working it looser, and below me the backwash below the weir was a slow green carousel of debris going around and around and *down*.

I got one hand on the roll as the knot gave.

The river took it.

I want to be honest, because honesty is the only thing I have ever been any good at: I went after them. Not bravely. Not thoughtfully. There was no moment of decision, no noble arithmetic of one life against a nation's worth of truth. There was just a strap sliding away through brown water and my hand closing on it, and then the carousel took me under, and the world was roar and green-black and the taste of silt, and somewhere in it I understood, with perfect clarity, that I was going to die at a weir that didn't exist on any map worth having.

The roll was still on my wrist. I had gotten a hand through the loose loop, and the loop had jammed at the heel of my palm, and that stupid, beautiful, half-tied knot had made itself into a shackle. The water pulled. I pulled. My shoulder made a sound I felt in my teeth.

*Let go*, said a calm, reasonable voice in my head that sounded like my old survey professor. *Wren. Let go. It's paper.*

*It's Ilsa Voss*, I said back. *It's Naya's brother. It's every nomad route they've already inked out. It's the only true thing in this whole drowned basin, and I am not letting the river have it, because the river doesn't even want it. The river doesn't know what it has.*

I got my other hand on the strap.

The carousel turned. The backwash rolled us down and down, and the light above went from silver to gray to a rumor, and my chest began to burn the way a fuse burns, and I thought—this is the part I have never told anyone, the part I will carry like a stone—I thought, *so this is what it costs,* and I was not sorry. That frightened me more than the drowning. That I could be thirty feet under a flooded weir with my lungs full of fire and feel not regret but *certainty*, certainty like a compass bearing, that some things are worth going down for.

Then the water broke, and hands had me.

* * *

Silas had come down the throat after me.

I learned the shape of it afterward, in pieces, from him and from Naya, and the pieces still don't sit comfortably together. He had run the boat into the gap, taken a wave that broke the stern off entirely, and gone into the water already swimming for my last sighting point. Naya had caught the hull fragment and ridden the carousel twice—*like a fairground*, she said later, with that grin, because grief and laughter live in the same room in her—and come out on the downstream rocks. And Silas had found me by the oilskin strap, because of course he had; the man had spent nine years tracking people across empty salt by the width of a heel-print, and one white strap in brown water was a shout to him.

He got an arm around my chest and cut the strap with his knife—cut the strap, not my wrist, though I learned later how close the margin was—and we came up together into the rain.

We came up into gunfire.

They had moved along the weir crest while we were under, and now they were shooting at debris in the backwash, at the white strap, at anything that surfaced. The first round went through the water a hand's width from my cheek. The second—

The second hit Silas.

Not squarely. A graze, the bullet opening a furrow along the outside of his right arm, the arm he had already been favoring since the flats, the arm he had sworn was fine. He made no sound at all. What he did was turn his body—*turn it*, deliberately, putting his back between me and the weir—and fire four shots one-handed from his service pistol with the other, and I will never know, because I have never asked, whether he was shooting at men or at rifles or at the space just above their heads. Two of the muzzle flashes on the crest stopped.

"Downstream," he said into my ear, calm as a man reading a gauge. "Naya. Rocks. Swim, Wren. You've done the hard part."

"I nearly—"

"You did the hard part," he said again, and pushed me into the current.

* * *

The downstream rocks were a spine of granite the flood hadn't bothered to drown, and Naya hauled me onto it by both arms with a strength that seemed, as always, to come from somewhere outside her slight frame. Then she hauled Silas, who came up the rock with his arm hanging and his face the color of the water and his jaw set in a line that dared either of us to comment.

I commented anyway. I was shivering too hard for dignity.

"You're bleeding on the maps' chaperone," I said. "That's a crime in three jurisdictions."

"Four," he said, and sat down hard, and let Naya bind the graze with strips of my shirt, which was the last shirt, which meant that from here to the capital I would be wearing a boat sail, and I found I did not care even slightly.

We sat on the rock in the rain and took inventory, the way you do.

The boat: gone. All of her, every plank, every patch, every bad-tempered seam. Somewhere below the weir her remains were being distributed across the basin, and I grieved her more than I have ever grieved a vessel, because she had carried us through the throat when the throat should have kept us.

The maps: intact. Dry. Cinched—half-cinched—against my chest, and when I opened the oilskin with shaking hands and saw Ilsa Voss's careful lines still unblurred by the flood, I put my forehead down on them and shook, and neither of them said a word, which was the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me.

The people: one grazed, one half-drowned, one soaked and wild-eyed and already, already scanning the far bank for the next threat. Each of us had bled now. Silas for the flats and for me, twice over. Wren for the maps, nearly to the bottom of the river. Naya—Naya had been bleeding since the day her brother vanished, and had simply learned to do it invisibly.

"We can't go by water anymore," Naya said at last. "No boat. And they know the river now. They'll be watching every crossing."

"Then we don't go by water," Silas said. He was looking east, where the rain thinned toward a smudge of hills. "The lowlands are flooded, but the flood's shallow. Knee to waist, most of it. We walk."

"Walk to the capital?" I said. "That's nine days, Silas. Through flooded country, with enforcers on the roads, with your arm—"

"With my arm," he said, "which is fine."

"It is not *fine*, you impossible man—"

"Wren." He turned and looked at me, and the rain ran off his face, and under the exhaustion there was something I hadn't seen in him before—something settled, like a bearing finally taken. "Nine days ago I would have told you to turn back. Hand the maps to some magistrate and trust the paper to do its work. But paper's the whole problem. Reeve owns the paper." He looked at the oilskin roll in my lap. "What he doesn't own is people who've seen. So we go. All three. And we make them look."

Naya snorted. "You've been spending too much time with her. That's a cartographer's speech."

"It's a speech I stole from a cartographer," Silas agreed, and for a moment—just a moment—the three of us sat on a granite spine in the middle of a flooded world and laughed, raggedly, helplessly, the laughter of people who have very recently been underwater.

Then Naya stood, and shaded her eyes against the rain, and went very still.

"Silas," she said. "How long since we've seen their horses?"

I followed her gaze back upriver, to the weir, to the bluff with the fig trees.

The bluff was empty. The horses were gone. And on the crest of the weir, planted upright in a crack between the old stones where the flood would carry it down to us like a message, a single paper fluttered in the wind—white, official, and stamped with the seal of the Concession Office.

Silas got up, waded out, and brought it back. He read it once. Then he handed it to me without a word, and I read it, and the laughter died in my throat, because it was not a threat and it was not a demand.

It was a list. Our three names, written in a clerk's careful hand. And beside each name, a date and a place.

The place was the capital.

The date was tomorrow.
