## 18. The Long Road to the Capital

The water found us before the road did.

We'd been walking since dawn through what the old maps called the Merrow Lowlands and what the new maps, I suspected, called *Concession Parcel 44-B*, though nobody had gotten around to surveying it since the river decided to reclaim it. Ankle-deep at first. Then knee-deep. By midmorning we were wading a country that had forgotten it was ever land, the floodwater brown and warm as tea left out too long, carrying sticks, fence posts, a drowned chicken coop that drifted past us with more dignity than any of us felt.

Wren walked ahead of me, favoring her bandaged shoulder, using a surveyor's pole as a walking staff because she refused to use anything that wasn't an instrument of her trade. Naya brought up the rear, which was where she always walked when she was thinking hard — close enough to hear everything, far enough to pretend she hadn't.

The maps were wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, under my shirt, tied down like a second heartbeat. Voss's testimony rode in the same bundle. Between us and the capital lay ninety miles of flooded lowland, one ferry crossing if the nomads still held it, and every checkpoint Reeve's people could staff between here and there.

"Stop," Wren said.

I stopped. Old habit. Nine years of taking orders from the land will do that.

She pointed her pole at a rise ahead — a low spine of ground breaking the water, crowned with a dead acacia and, mercifully, dry enough to sit on. We climbed up onto it like three shipwreck survivors claiming an island the size of a kitchen.

Naya dropped flat on her back and stared at the sky. "I want it noted for the record," she said, "that I have now walked through a chicken coop."

"You waded past a chicken coop."

"I was inside its wake. That counts." She turned her head toward me. "How much farther?"

"Two days to the ferry. Three if the water keeps rising."

"And after the ferry?"

"After the ferry, four days to the capital, assuming the roads are passable, which they won't be."

Wren lowered herself carefully onto the roots of the dead acacia and began unwrapping the bandage on her shoulder to check it, because Wren checked things the way other people breathed. The wound beneath had gone from angry red to a sullen pink, which she pronounced "acceptable" in the tone of a woman grading a student who'd barely passed.

"We should talk about it," she said.

"We're talking."

"No, Silas. We should talk about *it*. Whether this is still worth doing."

I looked out over the water. Somewhere under all that brown was a road, and fences, and the little marker posts the concession office had planted two years ago with their cheerful painted numbers. The river was erasing them one by one. There was a lesson in that I didn't have the heart to say out loud.

"Say your piece," I told her.

* * *

Here is what Wren said, in the orderly fashion she said everything: Voss's testimony named names, but names could be disputed. The original maps proved forgery, but provenance could be attacked — how did we come by them? Stolen goods, recovered by a wanted man, delivered by nomads with no standing in court. Reeve owned the concession office, half the provincial judiciary, and, as far as we could tell, at least two of the three newspapers that printed in the capital. She laid it all out like contour lines on a chart, and every line ran downhill.

"They'll dismiss it," she finished. "Not because it isn't true. Because they can. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole problem."

Naya sat up. For once she didn't have a joke loaded. "So we turn back?"

"I'm saying we should consider whether turning back is the rational choice."

"Turning back to *where*?" Naya spread her arms at the flood. "Odell's camp? They took Odell. My brother's route? It ends in a cave with two starving people in it. The flats? Every ranger post between here and the salt has my face pinned to a board next to Silas's." She jabbed a finger at me. "No offense."

"Some taken."

"You know what I mean. There's no back to turn to. That's the whole trick of it. People like Reeve don't need to catch you. They just need to make everywhere behind you worse than whatever's ahead."

Wren rewrapped her bandage with short, precise movements. "That's not an argument. That's a mood."

"It's a *map*," Naya shot back. "You of all people should respect a map."

I let them go at it for a while, because sometimes the truth comes out of an argument wearing different clothes than it would wear to a meeting. But eventually the sun got high enough that I could feel it through my shirt, and my patience, never a deep well, ran dry.

"Both of you are right," I said.

They stopped and looked at me with identical expressions of suspicion, which was, I suppose, something they'd learned from each other.

"Wren's right that we can't win in their courts. Naya's right that there's nowhere behind us." I pulled the oilcloth bundle partway out of my shirt, just enough to rest my hand on it. "Which means we've been planning this wrong from the start. We keep trying to carry this truth *to* the system. Hand it to a magistrate, file it with a ministry, deliver it to someone whose job it is to care. And every step of that road belongs to Reeve. He built the road. He owns the tolls."

"So what's the alternative?" Wren asked. "Truth doesn't travel without roads."

"It travels without *his* roads."

Naya went very still. I'd seen her do that before — back at the river market, when I'd cornered her and she'd been deciding whether to run or talk. It was her body deciding something before her mouth caught up.

"The nomad network," she said slowly.

"The nomad network."

"You know about the network?"

"Naya. I spent nine years chasing petty thieves across the basin, and half of them were never caught. You think that's because I was bad at my job?" I tipped my hat back. "It's because the routes the permits erased didn't stop existing. They just went quiet. Salt caravans, river runners, the ferry families, the well-keepers. There's a web out there older than the concession office and it doesn't file paperwork with anybody. It moves letters, grain, grudges, and news. Mostly news."

"My brother used it," Naya said quietly. "His letters came through it. That's how—" She stopped. We all knew how that sentence ended. The letter she'd carried unopened for a season, the one that would have warned her, the one she'd finally read on a leaking boat in the dark. "That's how his last one reached me."

"Then you know it reaches places the government mail doesn't."

"But it's slow. And it's not a court. Nobody in that network can arrest Aldous Reeve."

"No," I agreed. "But they can make sure that by the time we reach the capital, everyone already knows what's in these maps. Every ferryman, every well-keeper, every family the parcels displaced. Not as evidence. As *story*. You can dismiss a document." I tapped the oilcloth. "It's harder to dismiss five hundred people who already knew, and know that you know, and are watching to see what you do about it."

Wren was frowning at the water, but it was her working frown, the one that meant gears were turning rather than doors closing. "A public reading," she murmured. "Before the hearing, not during it. Lay the originals beside the concession maps somewhere they can't be seized first. In front of witnesses who don't answer to him."

"In front of press," Naya added. "Real press. There's a woman at the *Capital Ledger* — Sefi Maro — my brother trusted her. Said she was the only reporter in the city who'd ever gotten a nomad name spelled right."

"There it is," I said. "That's the play."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. "Silas. If we do this, there's no official channel left for us. No surrendering to a friendly magistrate, no protective custody, no reduced charges for cooperation. Once we go around the system, we're outside it for good. You especially. You'd be throwing away any chance of—"

"Of what?" I heard my own voice come out flatter than I intended. "A pardon? A hearing where Reeve's judges weigh his word against mine? Wren, I've been playing their game since I was nineteen years old. I wore the badge. I followed the rules. And the rules were written by men who drew maps of places they'd never been, and I enforced those maps on people who'd lived there their whole lives." I shook my head. "I'm done asking permission from the thing we're trying to expose."

Nobody said anything for a while. A heron stalked past the base of our island with the unhurried confidence of a creature that had never once filed a permit.

It was Naya who broke the silence, and she did it, naturally, sideways. "You realize this means we have to actually *talk* to nomads. Formally. With manners." She grimaced. "There are protocols, Silas. Salt-gift protocols. You can't just walk up to a ferry family and start issuing instructions like a ranger."

"I know the protocols."

"You know the *enforcement* version. 'Move along, this crossing is closed.' That's not the same as knowing how to ask."

"She's got you there," Wren said, and there was gentle humor in it, but underneath the humor was something kinder — she was giving me a way to say the hard thing without having to say it alone.

And the hard thing was this: "I'll ask," I said. "On my knees in the mud if that's what the protocol wants. Those families remember me, Naya. Not fondly. I stood at their crossings with a warrant book and made their grandmothers unload their carts. When I ask them for help, I'll deserve every cold look I get, and I'll stand there and take them." I looked out at the drowned road. "Grace isn't something you get to demand. It's something you get given, if you're lucky, by people better than you. All I can do is show up honest and hope."

Naya studied me for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket and tossed something onto the oilcloth bundle — a small tin, dented, rattling faintly.

"What's that?"

"Salt. For the gift protocol. I stole it off a trader at the weir before the fight." She shrugged. "Habit. Don't look at me like that — this time it's for a good cause."

"For once," Wren said, "the theft is load-bearing."

* * *

We moved again in the late afternoon, when the heat broke and the light went long and gold across the water. The flooded lowlands were strange and beautiful in that hour — the dead acacias standing in rows like the masts of a sunken fleet, egrets rising in white sheets wherever our footsteps disturbed the shallows, the whole drowned world gone quiet except for water dripping off leaves and the far-off mutter of thunder building in the west.

The monsoon wasn't done with us. None of us said so, but we all walked a little faster anyway.

Naya ranged ahead to scout the causeway she remembered from her brother's letters — he'd mapped the nomad crossings in his head even after the official maps erased them, and she'd inherited the memory along with the grief. Wren kept pace beside me, and after a mile of comfortable silence she said, without looking over:

"You meant it. About being done with their game."

"Meant every word."

"It'll cost you. Whatever's left of your name, your pension, the chance to ever be a ranger again. You understand that going public doesn't just expose Reeve. It exposes you. Everything you did enforcing those permits. The press won't be gentle."

"No," I agreed. "They won't."

"And you're all right with that?"

I thought about it honestly, because Wren deserved honesty and because lying to a cartographer seemed uniquely foolish — they deal in true lines all day; they can smell a bent one.

"I spent nine years believing that if I just kept moving, kept chasing the small crimes, kept the edges tidy, it added up to something," I said. "It didn't. Tidiness isn't justice. It's just tidiness." I shifted the oilcloth bundle against my chest. "These maps cost Ilsa Voss her team. They cost Naya her brother. They cost Odell his freedom this morning, and Wren, they nearly cost you your life at that weir. I'm not going to hand all that to a bought judge and call it a plan. If my name is the price of theirs meaning something, then fine. It was never worth much anyway. I mostly used it to sign warrants."

Wren was quiet for several steps. Then: "For what it's worth, I think your name is worth more than you've been charging for it."

"That's the fever talking."

"A little," she admitted. "But only a little."

Ahead of us, Naya stopped on a hump of higher ground and raised her arm — the scout's signal, flat palm, hold. Beyond her, maybe half a mile across the flooded flats, I saw what she'd seen: a thin line of smoke rising straight up from a stand of trees, and beneath it, half-hidden by the flood-swollen channel, the shape of a ferry platform and moored boats.

The crossing. Real. Manned. Close enough to reach by dark.

And on the near bank, small with distance but unmistakable, figures moving among the trees. Watching us. They'd have seen us coming for an hour — three travelers wading through open water toward the only crossing for miles, one of them carrying something bundled against his chest like it mattered more than his own skin.

Nomad families. The people the permits displaced. The people I'd policed.

"Well," Naya said, pulling the little tin of stolen salt from her jacket and pressing it into my hand. Her voice had gone soft, and underneath the softness was steel — her brother's steel, the kind that grief hones instead of blunting. "Time to find out what kind of man asks forgiveness with his boots full of river."

I closed my hand around the tin and started forward through the water toward the fires and the watching faces, and the thunder in the west rolled closer, counting us home.
