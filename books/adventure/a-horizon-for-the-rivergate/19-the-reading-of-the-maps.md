## 19. The Reading of the Maps

The capital smelled of wet stone and frying onions, and after eleven days of flooded lowlands, Silas Pemberton thought it was the finest smell in the world.

They had come in on the last leg of the nomad network—hidden under tarps in a salt wagon, then walking the old drover road that the maps said didn't exist, which was, Wren pointed out, the entire point. Naya had gone ahead at dawn to find her people. She came back at midday with mud to her knees, a bruise on her cheekbone she refused to explain, and a grin that made Silas uneasy in the way good news often did.

"They'll do it," she said. "The Reading. Tomorrow, at noon, on the Steps of Records."

"Tomorrow?" Wren looked up from the oilcloth bundle she had been rewrapping for the hundredth time. Her wound was healing, but the fever had left her thin, and she guarded the maps now the way a mother hawk guards a nest. "Naya, we haven't even—"

"Tomorrow," Naya repeated. "Because Reeve's people are already asking questions at the ferry landings. Because Odell is in a cell somewhere and every day we spend rehearsing is a day they have to move him. And because," she added, with the particular gentleness she reserved for arguments she knew she'd already won, "my grandmother says the Reading has never once been scheduled more than a day out. You announce it when the truth is ready. Not before. Otherwise it goes stale, like milk."

Silas leaned against the doorframe of the safehouse—a weaver's back room that smelled of dye and lamp oil—and let the argument wash over him. He had spent nine years believing that the law was a slow, careful machine, and that patience was a ranger's chief virtue. He was only now, at thirty-four, learning that patience could also be a cage other people built for you.

"So how does it work, this Reading?" he asked.

Naya sat down cross-legged on the floor and pulled a piece of dried fish from her pocket, tearing it three ways without being asked. "Old law. Older than the concessions, older than the capital, maybe older than the government, if you ask my grandmother, and she will ask you to ask her, so be ready. When two maps disagree, either party may call a Reading. The maps are laid down side by side in public, before witnesses who cannot be bought—well. Before witnesses, anyway. Anyone may come. Anyone may speak. And no official may seize a map under Reading without answering for it in open hearing."

"Which is why it's never been used against them," Wren said slowly. "They never let the originals survive long enough to be read."

"Which is why," Naya said, "we're going to do it in front of two hundred people and every press-writer in the city. My cousin Sefi writes for the *Meridian Ledger*. She's terrible. She asks questions like she's stabbing something. She's perfect."

Silas chewed his fish and thought about the last time he'd stood on the Steps of Records. He'd been twenty-two, newly badged, holding his commission scroll while a deputy concessioner he couldn't remember the name of shook his hand. The steps had seemed so solid then. Marble, imported, cold underfoot even in summer. He wondered if they'd feel different tomorrow.

They would. He already knew they would.

* * *

The night before the Reading, nobody slept, and so they all pretended to.

Wren sat up with the maps, checking and rechecking the seals, the survey dates, the watermark of the old Hydrographic Office that appeared on Voss's originals and—this was the beautiful, damning part—*did not* appear on the concession maps that claimed to be copied from them. "It's like forging a signature," she murmured, "and forgetting to forge the hand."

Naya sat by the window with her brother's letter, the one she'd carried unopened for a season, and read it until Silas suspected she could recite it in her sleep. Once, near midnight, she said quietly, "He wrote that the canyons sang at night. The flooded ones. Water in the caves." She didn't finish the thought, and Silas didn't ask her to.

Silas sat by the door, because that was where Silas sat, and cleaned a rifle that he was not, by law, permitted to carry anymore. Somewhere in the city a warrant with his name on it was sitting in a clerk's drawer, or maybe already in an enforcer's hand. Aldous Reeve had made him the villain of his own story, and tomorrow Silas intended to walk onto the most public stage in the capital and hand himself over like a parcel.

"You could stay back," Wren said, without looking up. "At the edge of the crowd. There's no rule saying the accused has to attend his own ambush."

"There's no rule saying he can't be arrested on the steps, either."

"That's rather my point."

He set the rifle down. "Wren. If I stay back and it goes wrong, I spend the rest of my life being the man who hid while you two stood on the steps. I've been a lot of things. I'd rather not be that."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. "You've changed," she said. "Nine years of running in place, and now—" She stopped, and smiled a small, tired smile. "Now you run toward things. It's very inconvenient. I liked you better cynical."

"You did not."

"No," she admitted. "I didn't."

* * *

The Steps of Records at noon looked like a market day and a funeral had collided and agreed to share the space.

The nomads came in numbers Silas hadn't seen since he was a boy riding the basin with his father: Rivergate families in their traveling colors, Saltwalk clans with their silver bangles, herders, ferrymen, the displaced of a dozen erased routes. They packed the square below the steps and spilled onto the surrounding rooftops, and above them, on the marble, stood the officials in their gray coats and the press-writers with their ink-stained fingers, and between the two crowds, a strip of empty stone about six feet wide that everyone understood was a border of a kind.

Naya's grandmother, a small ferocious woman named Amara who walked with two canes and needed neither, opened the Reading with the old words. Silas didn't understand half of them—they were in the river-tongue, older than the trade speech—but he understood the shape of them. *We lay the land down. We let it speak. Whoever lies, lies against the land, and the land remembers.*

Then the maps came out.

Wren carried Voss's originals down the steps herself, though her hands shook, and laid them on the reading table beside the concession maps that a clerk from the Concession Office produced with the reluctant air of a man handing over his own teeth. And there they were, side by side in the noon sun: the land as it was, and the land as it had been sold.

Silas stood at the back of the officials' strip, in his old ranger coat, feeling every eye that slid toward him and away. Wren had argued for hours about whether he should be visible at all. In the end Naya had settled it: "If they're going to arrest him, better it happens where everyone can watch them do it."

Sefi, the terrible press-writer, worked the crowd like a pickpocket. Silas heard her corner a deputy registrar: "Sir, the concession boundary on the eastern flats follows a river that, per the original survey, is forty miles west of where you've drawn it. Did the Concession Office move the river, or just the map?" The registrar's answer was a noise like a kettle giving up.

For an hour it held. Voss's maps were read aloud—dates, seals, survey marks—and the crowd's murmur grew from a hum to a rumble. Amara's voice cracked across the square like a whip: "Read the dates! Read them again!" And the dates were read, and the impossibility of them sat in the sunlight where no clerk could file it away: permits drawn from maps that described land the surveys hadn't reached yet. Paper that remembered a future it had been sold from.

Silas let himself believe, for perhaps ninety seconds, that it might be that simple.

Then the black coats came.

* * *

They came up the sides of the square in files of eight, enforcers in concession livery, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had been told, correctly, that no one would stop them. The crowd's rumble dropped into something lower and more dangerous. Amara's canes came up off the marble with a crack like a rifle.

At their head, past the enforcers, walked a man Silas recognized from a wanted circular and a single bad memory in Corren's Landing: tall, gray-gloved, with the face of a man who found other people's grief administratively interesting. Reeve's senior man. Behind him, at the edge of the square, sat a concession carriage with its curtains drawn, and Silas would have bet his—his nonexistent—badge that Aldous Reeve was behind those curtains, watching his machinery work.

"The Reading is concluded," the senior man announced, in a voice pitched to carry. "The documents are the subject of a criminal inquiry and are hereby seized as evidence."

"Under what law?" Amara demanded.

"Under the law," he said, which told everyone everything.

The enforcers moved for the table. Wren stepped in front of the maps, arms spread, and Silas's heart went into his throat—she'd been shot once already shielding those papers, and she would do it again, he could see it in her stance, and this time there was no river to dive into.

And that was when Silas Pemberton stopped calculating and started moving.

He came down the strip of marble with his hands high and his voice carrying the way a ranger's voice is trained to carry, across wind, across water, across nine years of empty country.

"My name is Silas Pemberton!" The square went quiet in rings, like a stone dropped in a pond. "Ranger, Third District—formerly. There's a warrant out for my arrest. Aldous Reeve says I made a survey team disappear." He let that sit while two hundred people looked at him, and he looked back, and did not flinch, because flinching was the one thing he'd never been any good at. "I didn't. I found them. Or two of them. And I found these." He turned and gestured at the table, at the maps, at Wren standing guard with her chin up and her knuckles white. "And the reason Aldous Reeve wants those maps so badly he'd send armed men into a public square to take them—"

"Seize him," the senior man said, almost wearily.

"—is that those maps prove," Silas went on, louder, as the first enforcers reached the steps, "that his permits are forged, his surveys are false, and the land he sold belongs to the people standing in this square!"

They took his arms. He let them. That was the whole trick of it, and he understood it only as their grips closed: a quiet arrest in some back alley was a story Reeve could bury. But an arrest on the Steps of Records, in front of the press and the nomads and the gray-coated officials who were now, very visibly, doing arithmetic about which side of this they wanted to be found on—an arrest here was a *question*. And questions, once asked in public, had to be answered in public.

"You'll want a hearing, then," Silas said to the senior man, conversationally, as the cuffs went on. "Because you've just seized evidence and arrested a witness at a lawful Reading, in front of two hundred witnesses of your own. Under the old law." He nodded toward Amara, who was watching him with an expression he'd remember for the rest of his life—something between a blessing and a dare. "And under the old law, that hearing is public. Open. On the record. Every press-writer in this square gets a seat."

Sefi was already writing. He could see her pencil moving from ten feet away, fast as a water-strider.

"Sir," said one of the enforcers, to the senior man, in the particular tone of a man passing a problem upward, "the crowd."

The crowd was not doing anything. That was the remarkable thing. Two hundred nomads and half the capital's poor were simply standing there, watching, in the silence of people who had finally been given something worth waiting for. The senior man looked at them, and at the carriage with the drawn curtains, and Silas watched him understand, with visible distaste, that the arithmetic had changed.

"There will be a hearing," the senior man said at last, each word like pulling a nail. "Three days. The documents will be held by the Records Office pending—"

"They'll be held," Amara said, "by the Records Office, under Reading seal, where any tampering is a public crime, in front of the same public. Yes. We know the law. We wrote it."

They took Silas down the steps. At the bottom, as they turned him toward the carriage, he caught sight of Wren and Naya at the table—Wren rigid with fury, Naya with her hand on her cousin's arm, holding her back, holding herself back, her eyes locked on his with a message in them he read clearly enough: *We'll be there. All three days. Don't you dare lose faith before we do.*

The carriage door opened. And from the shadow inside it, Aldous Reeve leaned forward into the light—neat, unhurried, gray-gloved, wearing the mild expression of a man greeting a neighbor across a fence.

"Ranger Pemberton," Reeve said pleasantly. "Or should I say *former* ranger. You've made this so much easier than it might have been. A hearing is a controlled environment. I've won every one I've ever stood in." He smiled, and it was almost a kind smile, which was the worst thing about it. "I do wonder who you're planning to call. Your cartographer? The thief? The hermit?" A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "The hermit is *available*, you know. If you ask nicely."

The door closed. The carriage rolled.

And Silas Pemberton sat in the dark with his cuffed hands and his hammering heart, and understood that the hearing had already begun—and that the first witness Reeve intended to call was a man who owed Silas his life, sitting in a cell, being offered his freedom as the price of three names.
