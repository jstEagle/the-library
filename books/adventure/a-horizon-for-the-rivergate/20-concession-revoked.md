## 20. Concession Revoked

The hearing chamber smelled of beeswax and old paper and, underneath it all, the faint river-damp that three of us had carried in on our boots like a stowaway. Marble floors. High windows. A ceiling painted with the Basin itself—stylized, blue, harmless-looking, the kind of map made by someone who had never once been thirsty.

I stood at the defendant's rail, because that was where they'd put me. Nine years of service, and when I finally walked into the capital's Hall of Concessions, it was in irons they'd agreed to leave off as a courtesy. The courtesy of not chaining a man while deciding whether to hang him. The Rivergate was generous that way.

Wren sat behind me with her arm still bound from the weir, her good hand flat on the leather case at her feet like a mother resting a palm on a sleeping child. Naya sat beside her, uncharacteristically still, wearing the one clean shirt she owned and an expression I'd last seen on her face over a card game she intended to win by cheating.

"Stop fidgeting," Wren murmured.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"Your shoulder is fidgeting on your behalf."

I made my shoulder stop. It didn't want to. Neither did my heart, which had spent the whole boat ride downriver rehearsing every way this could go wrong: Voss too broken to testify, Odell dead in some concession cellar, the maps ruled inadmissible, Reeve smiling his way through another impossibility. I'd learned long ago that hope was just fear wearing its good coat.

At the front of the chamber, behind a desk carved with more eagles than any bird deserved, sat Magistrate Corvel—a small, dry woman with spectacles thick enough to start fires—and beside her, in the seat reserved for interested parties, Deputy Concessioner Aldous Reeve.

He looked wonderful. That was the first thing that struck me, and it struck me hard. Three weeks of monsoon, a drowned canyon, a sinking boat, and a bullet-graze across my own ribs, and Reeve looked like he'd been pressed that morning. Grey suit, silver pin of office, hands folded. He caught me looking and inclined his head slightly, the way a man greets a dog he once considered buying.

The clerk read the charges against me—unlawful flight, obstruction, theft of government property, suspicion in the disappearance of the Voss survey team—and I listened to my own life described back to me in the language of paperwork. It's a strange thing, hearing yourself become a file.

Then Magistrate Corvel said, "We will hear testimony," and everything changed.

* * *

Ilsa Voss came up the aisle on her own feet, which surprised half the room. She'd spent two months starving in a cave above the flood line of the drowned canyons; I'd expected her to be carried. Instead she walked like a woman who had decided walking was the point. Thin as a fence post, sunburnt, hair cut raggedly short with what I guessed was a survey knife. She reached the stand, took the oath in a voice like gravel settling, and then turned—not to the magistrate, but to Reeve—and held his gaze for a full five seconds before answering a single question.

I have seen men stare down rivers. I have seen them stare down each other. I had never seen a man look away first the way Aldous Reeve did.

She testified for two hours. She laid it out plain, no poetry, which is the hardest kind of speaking there is. Her team had found the aquifer surveys—deep water under the eastern basin, enough to green half the Rivergate. Enough to make the concession lands worth ten times what the government had sold them for. And the permits, she said, had been drawn from maps dated after the surveys were suppressed. Maps describing land as if it had never been anything but empty. Forged backward through time so thoroughly that the forgery *became* the record.

"The nomad routes," she said, "were erased from the working copies before the permits were drafted. Not disputed. Erased. Someone decided four thousand people had never crossed that ground, and then built law on top of the decision."

Corvel asked how she knew the originals were authentic.

Voss smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Because I drew them, Magistrate. Every contour. I can tell you where I stood when I measured each one, and what I ate that day, and which of my guides complained about the sandflies. A forger can copy a line. He cannot copy standing on it."

In the gallery, the press scribbled. Nomad families filled the back rows—the same families from the ferry crossing, come downriver to watch—and they did not scribble. They listened the way people listen when the words being said about their lives are finally their own.

Then Corvel said, "The defense will note that Surveyor Voss has been in hiding and may bear grievances," and Reeve's counsel rose, smooth as poured oil, and began the work of unwinding her.

He was good. I'll give him that. He found the seam in her—her deputy, the man who'd sold the team out—and he pulled at it gently, sympathetically, until Voss's certainty started to sound like paranoia. *You believed your own colleague betrayed you. You hid rather than report. Isn't it possible, Surveyor, that isolation has shaped your account?*

Voss's jaw tightened. I saw her grip the rail. And I saw Wren, behind me, go rigid, because we both knew the answer to the next question mattered less than the room's belief in the asker.

That was when the doors at the back of the chamber opened.

* * *

Odell Brack came in the way he did everything—slowly, damply, and as though he had all the time the river ever gave him. He wore a fisherman's slicker over prison clothes. His beard had gone another week greyer since I'd last seen him, and there was a fresh bruise along his jaw, but his eyes, when they found mine, were exactly as calm as the day he'd hidden three fugitives in his smokehouse and told the enforcers his cat had rabies.

"Magistrate," said the bailiff, hurrying, "this man claims—he says he escaped custody this morning from the Concession House cellars. He says—" the bailiff glanced at his notes, visibly losing faith in them, "—he says he walked out through a door someone forgot to lock, and that he would like to testify."

"He broke out of a holding cell," Reeve's counsel said, "which is—"

"Which is evidence," Odell said mildly, reaching the stand without waiting to be invited, "that somebody wanted me kept somewhere quiet. I've been fishing the Rivergate forty years, sir, and I know a stocked pond when I'm thrown in one."

Corvel peered at him over her spectacles. "State your name and occupation."

"Odell Brack. Fisherman. Occasional liar, when lying serves the innocent." A ripple went through the gallery. Somewhere in the back rows, a nomad grandmother laughed out loud, and the sound loosened something in the whole room.

Odell testified simply. He told them about the enforcers who'd come to his camp hunting three travelers. About being taken to the capital and held—not charged, *held*—in a cellar below the Concession House, where he'd heard, through a grate, men discussing the disposal of certain documents. He named names. Two of the names made Reeve's counsel stop taking notes.

Then Corvel asked the question I'd been waiting three weeks to hear asked.

"You claim the concession maps are forged. On what basis?"

"On dates, ma'am." Odell produced, from inside his slicker, a folded paper—water-stained, smuggled out of that cellar in a place I chose not to imagine. "This here's a requisition order for the survey plates. Dated the ninth of Thaw-month. But the permit boundaries were filed as drawn from those same plates on the second of Thaw-month." He let that sit. "Now, I'm just a fisherman, but even I know a fish can't be caught before it's swimming. Somebody drew lines from a map seven days before the map existed."

The chamber went very quiet. Paper does that to people. One impossible date, laid flat in the light, outweighed every speech Reeve had ever given.

Reeve's counsel objected—chain of custody, provenance, the reliability of a self-confessed escapee—but Corvel had stopped listening to him the way a river stops listening to a stick. She ordered the requisition entered into evidence, cross-referenced against the permit filings, and sent her clerks scurrying with instructions that made the clerks blanch.

Reeve himself rose then. Of course he did. He asked to address the chamber personally, and Corvel, to her credit or her vanity, allowed it.

He was magnificent. I'll say that for him, because it matters. He spoke about order, about the Basin needing stewardship instead of drift, about how maps were instruments of civilization and civilization required someone willing to hold the pen. He never raised his voice. He almost had me, and I knew better. In the gallery I saw young clerks nodding, and I understood for the hundredth time how a thing like the concession scheme gets built—not by villains twirling mustaches, but by reasonable voices explaining why the river would be better off owned.

"And I ask the chamber," Reeve concluded, spreading his hands toward the leather case at Wren's feet, "to consider who benefits from these so-called original maps. A disgraced ranger facing charges. A cartographer suspended pending review. A confessed thief. A hermit who admits to lying. Is this the company of truth, Magistrate? Or the company of convenience?"

It was a good question. It was the best question anyone asked all day, and for a moment the room tilted toward it.

Then Naya stood up.

* * *

She hadn't been called. Nobody had called her. She simply rose from her seat, walked to the case at Wren's feet, and lifted out the maps herself—which caused three bailiffs to move and Corvel to raise one finger, halting all of them, because even magistrates are curious creatures.

"I'm the thief," Naya said, loud enough for the gallery. "Since we're listing credentials. I stole a compass off this ranger—" she jerked a thumb at me, and I felt forty pairs of eyes swing over, "—because it belonged to my brother, who guided the Voss team and vanished. I've stolen other things too. Food. A boat. Once, a hat I regret." Laughter again, nervous this time. "But I never stole the truth. That was already stolen. I'm just the one carrying it back."

She set the maps on the evidence table and unrolled the top sheet with both hands, careful as handling a hatchling. The aquifer contours ran across it in Voss's sure ink, and beneath them, in the margin, the nomad routes—drawn in, dated, witnessed. Four thousand people's worth of comings and goings that the concession papers insisted had never happened.

"Magistrate," Naya said, "you can believe the deputy, who owns the pen. Or you can believe the paper, which doesn't belong to anybody yet. That's the whole choice. Everything else is weather."

Silence. Then Corvel said, dry as salt: "Thank you. That is the clearest statement of evidentiary principle I have heard in nineteen years on this bench."

And that should have been the end of it. That was the moment the room tipped for good—I felt it go, felt the weight shift like ballast sliding. Which is, I suppose, exactly why Reeve moved.

Later, everyone would describe it differently. The press wrote *lunged*. The bailiffs wrote *reached suddenly*. What I saw was simpler: a man watching his life's work get weighed and found counterfeit, and choosing, in one final flash of sincerity, to burn the scales. He crossed the floor in four strides, hand outstretched toward the maps—not toward a weapon, never toward a weapon, Reeve was far too controlled for that—fingers closing on the top sheet with the clear intention of tearing it, crumpling it, feeding it to whatever fire a determined man can find in a marble hall.

He got the corner of it.

He did not get the rest, because I was already moving. Nine years of chasing petty thieves across the flats had taught me exactly one physical skill beyond riding and complaining, and that was covering ground fast over bad footing. I came over the rail, took him around the chest, and we went down together onto the evidence table, which was undignified for both of us and fatal only to his dignity. The map slid free, unharmed. Wren snatched it up and sat on the case. Naya, I am proud to report, did nothing helpful whatsoever except cheer.

"Rangers," Corvel said, into the tangle of limbs and gasping counsel, and for the first time all day her voice carried real iron. "Arrest the Deputy Concessioner."

Nobody moved. One heartbeat. Two. The rangers at the doors looked at Reeve, then at me—still officially a criminal, still a name on a warrant—and I watched them weigh nine years of paperwork against thirty seconds of a man trying to shred the truth in open court.

I stood up off the floor, straightened my ruined shirt, and gave them the only thing I had left.

"My badge number is 4471," I said. "You took it three weeks ago. I served this Basin for nine years and I never once lied in a report, and I am telling you now: the maps are real, the dates are forged, and the man on the floor just showed you which side he's on. So. Do your jobs."

Ranger Hask—the same Hask who'd signed my warrant, whose face I'd been expecting to hate forever—looked at Reeve. Reeve looked back with that terrible reasonable calm and began, "Gentlemen, if you'll consider the context—"

Hask cuffed him mid-sentence.

They led him out past the gallery, past the nomad families, past Voss, who watched him go with an expression I couldn't read and didn't need to. At the doors he paused—just a moment—and looked back at the chamber, at the maps, at the blue painted river on the ceiling, and whatever he saw there, he shook his head slowly, like a man disappointed in a student. Then the doors closed, and the sound they made was the best sound I'd heard in nine years.

* * *

Afterward, the corridor outside the chamber was chaos—the good kind, shouting and handshaking and a reporter asking Wren whether she'd really sat on the evidence (she had; she confirmed it with a straight face and became, briefly, the most quoted cartographer in the capital). Naya vanished into the crowd and reappeared twenty minutes later with meat skewers for everyone, having somehow acquired them without paying, a fact she declined to discuss.

Voss found me by the window. Up close she looked older than the cave had made her, and tired in a way that wouldn't wash out.

"They'll offer you your badge back," she said. "By tomorrow. With a promotion attached, if you'll take it quietly."

"Will they?"

"They'll need you to take it quietly. It makes the story cleaner. *Loyal ranger vindicated* sells better than *the whole authority nearly hanged an innocent man*." She studied me. "What will you do?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I'd managed in years. I thought about a badge in a drawer, and a horizon, and a boat with a patched hull that needed a proper repair, and two people waiting to see what I'd say.

"I don't know yet," I said, and found that it was true, and found also that not knowing didn't scare me the way it used to.

Voss nodded slowly. "Good answer. The wrong ones come faster."

Down the corridor, Wren and Naya were arguing about dinner—Naya advocating for somewhere with a roof, Wren insisting the roof was non-negotiable but the establishment beneath it remained open to debate—and Odell stood between them, dripping on the marble, looking happier than any man had a right to look after a month in a cellar.

"Silas!" he called. "These two can't pick a tavern. Apparently it needs a ranger's judgment."

I looked at the three of them, and at the long window beyond, where evening was coming down over the capital and, far past it, somewhere east, the river running brown and free toward a horizon that belonged to nobody.

"Yeah," I said. "I know a place."

I just didn't know yet that it wasn't a place at all.
