## 21. A Badge Returned, Refused

The summons came on paper, which was how Silas knew it was official. Nobody in the Rivergate Basin wrote anything down anymore that they didn't want held against them later.

It was delivered by a junior ranger named Ferro, a boy of perhaps nineteen who stood in the doorway of Wren's rented room in the capital with his cap in his hands like a man arriving at a funeral he wasn't sure he'd been invited to. Silas recognized the posture. He'd worn it himself, once, standing on doorsteps all across the basin, telling nomad families their routes were now "administrative corridors."

"Ranger Pemberton," the boy said. "Commander Hesk wants to see you. Headquarters. Tomorrow, ten o'clock."

"I'm not a ranger anymore, Ferro."

"No, sir." The boy looked at the floor, then at Silas, then at the floor again, as though the conversation were happening somewhere between the two. "That's—actually, sir, that's what it's about."

* * *

Headquarters smelled the same as it had for nine years: lamp oil, ink, and the particular staleness of a building that had never once opened a window on principle. Silas walked the corridor to Hesk's office and noted, with a feeling he couldn't immediately name, that the warrant notice had been taken down from the board. In its place was a notice about the monsoon drainage schedule, and beneath that, someone had pinned a newspaper clipping: *CONCESSION REVOKED — RIVERGATE SCHEME COLLAPSES UNDER TESTIMONY.*

His own name was in the third paragraph. He'd read it so many times the words had stopped being words and become a kind of weather.

Hesk was older than Silas remembered, which was unfair, because Silas had only seen the man four months ago. Time, apparently, had been working on Hesk at a faster rate. The commander sat behind his desk with his hands folded, and on the desk, centered with the precision of a man who had rehearsed this moment, sat a badge.

Not Silas's old badge. That one had been logged as evidence, then quietly lost, then quietly found, then quietly retired to a drawer somewhere. This was a new badge. Shined. Engraved. *S. PEMBERTON, SENIOR RANGER, FIRST CLASS.*

"Sit down, Silas."

"I'll stand."

"Sit down anyway. You always were a mule about furniture." Hesk pushed the badge an inch forward. "Senior Ranger, First Class. There are six of those in the entire service. It comes with a posting here in the capital, a salary that'll make your eyes water, and a commendation for—what did the citation say—'extraordinary fidelity to the truth in the face of institutional failure.'"

"Institutional failure," Silas said. "That's a polite way of saying we took Reeve's money and hunted the people who caught him."

Hesk's jaw tightened. He was, in his way, an honest man, which was the most dangerous kind to have in charge of a dishonest institution. "Yes. It is. I signed the warrant for your arrest, Silas. I read the file Reeve's people built, and I believed it, because believing it was easier than believing that nine years of my own judgment had been for sale around me. That's not an excuse. It's just what happened, and I won't insult you by dressing it up."

Silas looked at the badge. It caught the lamplight and threw it back, bright and meaningless as a coin at the bottom of a well.

"The survey team," Hesk went on. "Voss's people. The service wants to rebuild it. Properly this time. Independent funding, public records, the whole architecture. Voss has agreed to lead it. And she'll only agree to one condition regarding staffing." He paused. "She wants you. And Oduya. And the Oduya girl—Naya, is it? As guides and field security. The service will formalize it. Badges all around."

"That's generous."

"It's the least we can do, and I'm aware the least we can do is a debt we'll be paying for a decade." Hesk leaned back. "So. Senior Ranger, First Class. Do we have an accord?"

Silas picked up the badge. It was heavier than he remembered badges being, or perhaps his hands had gotten stronger. He turned it over. On the back, the service motto: *WE HOLD THE LINE.*

He'd held a lot of lines in nine years. Most of them had been drawn by other people, on maps he hadn't made, for reasons he hadn't been told, and he had held them anyway because holding them was the job and the job was the man and if you took the man apart to see what was underneath you might find nothing at all.

He set the badge back down on the desk, gently, the way you'd set down something sleeping.

"No," he said.

Hesk blinked. In twenty years of command, Silas suspected, no one had ever declined a First Class posting, mostly because no one had ever been offered one and then asked to think about it. "No?"

"No. Thank you. But no." Silas stood. "You want to know the funny thing, Commander? For nine years, the badge was the reason. Bad season, bad case, bad night on the flats—didn't matter, because I had the badge, and the badge meant I was the kind of man who kept going. Then Reeve's people put out a warrant with my face on it, and the badge was gone, and I found out I was still the kind of man who kept going. Turns out it was never the badge. Turns out it was me the whole time, and I'd been renting myself out at a terrible rate."

Hesk was quiet a long moment. "The salary really would make your eyes water."

"I know. Wren already checked the pay scales. She made a chart."

"Of course she did." Something in the commander's face loosened—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might someday build. "What will you do?"

"Guide work. Independent. Wren's redrawing the basin maps—the real ones, with the nomad routes put back where they always were. Naya knows half those routes better than any survey team that ever walked them. And there's a fisherman at Drowned Bend who's threatened to teach me to catch fish properly, on the grounds that nine years of rangers never managed it." Silas paused at the door. "Voss can have my testimony, my maps, and my boots if the survey needs them. But the badge belongs to the line, and I'm done holding lines I didn't draw."

He left the badge shining on the desk like a small, disappointed sun, and walked out into a capital that smelled, for the first time in his memory, like rain that had somewhere better to be.

* * *

Wren was waiting outside on the steps, ostensibly reading a survey bulletin, actually watching the door with the transparent patience of a woman who had been watching it for forty minutes.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered me First Class."

"And?"

"I gave it back."

Wren lowered the bulletin. "You gave back a First Class posting. Silas. Those come with a hat. There's a *hat*, Silas. With a silver band."

"I know."

"A silver. Band."

"I'm at peace with the hat, Wren."

She folded her arms, but the corner of her mouth was fighting her. "Naya's going to be insufferable about this. She had a bet going. Ten silver on you taking it, five on you throwing the badge in the river, and one on you challenging Hesk to a duel."

"Which did she bet on?"

"The duel. Obviously. She said, quote, 'it's the only outcome with style.'"

* * *

They found Naya at the survey office, where she had taken up a semi-permanent residence on the windowsill on the grounds that, as she put it, "someone has to make sure you government people don't forge anything while I'm not looking." Voss's rebuilt survey had taken over the old concession offices—same desks, same ink-stained floors, entirely different air. The forged maps had been burned, officially, in a small ceremony that Naya had attended solely, she claimed, to make sure they burned.

Ilsa Voss looked better than she had in the drowned canyon, which was not a high bar—she'd looked, in the canyon, like a woman the river had tried to keep. She was thinner than Silas remembered from before the vanishing, and her hands still shook when she thought no one was watching, but there was a steadiness underneath it now, the steadiness of a person who had testified to a hearing full of powerful men with her voice never once cracking.

She looked up when Silas came in. "Well? Did you take it?"

"No."

Voss nodded slowly, and something that might have been approval, or might have been relief, crossed her face. "Good. I'd have had to fire you from the survey if you had. Conflict of interest. Can't have a Senior Ranger First Class running around with a bunch of independent mapmakers. Terrible for the optics." She returned to her papers. "You're hired. Same terms as before, which is to say there are no terms and no pay until the funding clears, at which point there will be modest pay and endless work."

"That's the worst job offer I've ever received."

"You've clearly never worked for the concession office." Voss unrolled a map across her desk—the new map, Wren's map, three months of work inked in careful lines. "Look at this. Tell me what you see."

Silas looked. The Rivergate Basin spread out beneath his hands, and it was wrong in all the right ways. The nomad routes were back—drawn as solid lines, not the dotted "historical interest" ghosts the concession maps had reduced them to. The wells. The grazing grounds. The ferry crossings. A whole country of people who had never officially existed, rendered undeniable by ink.

"I see the basin," he said. "All of it."

"That's what the concession maps couldn't do," Voss said quietly. "Reeve's people drew a basin with nobody in it. Empty land is free land. Filled land has to be answered for." She tapped the map. "The hearing ordered every permit boundary redrawn from this. Every one. It goes to the printers next week."

Naya, from her windowsill, made a sound that Silas had learned to interpret as suppressed emotion. "They put the Saltwalker route back," she said. "The whole route. Spring camp to the river, like it was before." She was quiet a moment. "My brother used to say the maps were the first thing they'd take and the last thing they'd give back. He'd have—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, and finished, with tremendous dignity, "He'd have complained that your line weights are inconsistent, Wren. He was insufferable about line weights."

"I know," Wren said softly. "He taught me."

* * *

They left the capital three days later, at the tail end of the monsoon, when the rain had thinned to long silver curtains and the lowlands were a hundred shallow lakes strung together by patient water. The boat was new—well, new to them; old in the way of all river boats, patched and repatched by Odell's cousin's son, who had charged them a fair price and thrown in a coil of rope out of what he claimed was generosity and what Naya claimed was guilt over the hull.

The basin opened up around them as they went west, green where it had been salt, loud with water where it had been silent. Silas stood at the tiller and let it all in. Nine years he had crossed this country seeing it as a ledger—routes to patrol, boundaries to enforce, small crimes to chase across the flats because stopping cost more than moving. He was looking at the same basin now and seeing something else entirely: not a territory but a home, and not his alone.

Wren sat in the bow with the new maps spread around her like a skirt, annotating. "The ferry crossing at Last Light needs resurveying," she called back. "The flood moved the channel forty meters east. And there's a nomad family camped on the north bank who've offered to show me the old fords if I put their well on the map by name instead of by number."

"Take the offer," Silas said. "Names matter more than numbers."

"I told them that. They said a ranger taught them that, years ago, and they'd assumed he'd died, because no ranger ever came back twice." She looked over her shoulder at him, eyes bright with mischief. "I said I'd vouch for you. I may have overstated."

"You absolutely overstated."

Naya was at the stern, feet up on a crate, mending a net with the fierce competence of someone who had learned every skill she owned out of necessity. She'd been quiet since the capital—not the old guarded quiet, but something easier. She caught Silas looking and held up the net.

"Odell's going to make us fish with this," she said. "He told me last time we passed through. He said, and I quote, 'the ranger rows, the map-woman watches, and the girl learns, because someone in this boat should be useful.'"

"He said that about all three of us?"

"He said it about you twice."

The river bent north, and around the bend the flats began—wide and shining, the salt crust broken now by shallow sheets of monsoon water that turned the whole world into a mirror. The sky came down to meet the ground and there was no telling, for a long stretch, where one ended and the other began. Somewhere out in all that brightness, Silas knew, Odell was at Drowned Bend with his nets and his grudge against modern fishing, and Voss's survey crews were walking routes that had never been walked officially before, and nomad children were learning the shape of a country that finally, on paper at least, belonged to them.

Wren had stopped annotating. She was just looking—out at the water-mirror, at the horizon that ran unbroken in every direction.

"It goes on forever," she said. "You can't map it. There's no edge."

"Every map needs an edge."

"Not this one." She rolled up her charts and patted them, satisfied. "This one's going to stay open. That's the whole point of it."

Naya snorted. "You two are going to be like this the entire trip, aren't you. Saying meaningful things at the water."

"Probably," Silas said.

"Good." She settled back against the crate, the net across her knees, and grinned at the sky. "I was worried it'd be boring."

The boat ran on, west, toward the drowned canyons and the fisherman and the long work of putting a country back on its own map—and Silas Pemberton, who had spent nine years moving because stopping cost too much, found that the moving itself had changed. It wasn't fleeing anymore. It was just going, with the people who stayed, toward a horizon that belonged to no one and was, for that exact reason, finally his.
