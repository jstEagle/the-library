## 22. A Horizon for the Rivergate

The monsoon ended the way it always did in the Rivergate Basin—not with a last storm but with a long exhale, the clouds thinning day by day until one morning Silas woke in Odell Brack's camp and the sky was simply blue again, enormous and unembarrassed about it, like a man who had been shouting all season and finally remembered his indoor voice.

He lay in his bedroll a while longer than he needed to. That was new. For nine years he had woken the way rangers wake: instantly, completely, already cataloguing the wind and the light and the small crimes waiting out there on the flats. Now he woke slowly, and the first thing he noticed was not wind or light or crime.

It was smell. Fish smoke. Wet rope drying in the sun. And underneath it, faint as a rumor, the green smell of the lowlands coming back to life after the flood—grass pushing up through silt, stubborn as anything.

Nobody was shooting at him. Nobody was hunting him. His name had been cleared at a hearing that had taken four hours and destroyed a deputy concessioner's entire career, and the strangest part—the part Silas still hadn't gotten used to—was that afterward, nothing had happened. No warrant appeared. No enforcers came over the ridge. The world had simply gone on, and it had gone on without needing him to run through it.

He got up, folded his bedroll with ranger neatness anyway, because some habits were load-bearing, and walked down to the riverbank where the boat sat on its cradle of logs.

They'd spent three weeks on her. The hull Wren had nearly drowned retrieving from the weir had been patched with tarred canvas and river clay and, at Naya's insistence, a strip of brass from the old concession office sign, which Naya had pried off the building herself during the hearing week "for sentimental reasons." The boat had a name now, burned into the stern plank in Wren's careful cartographer's hand: *Ilsa's Wake*.

Silas ran his thumb along the letters. The wood was warm already. Everything was warm. The basin in the dry season was an oven with delusions of grandeur, and he loved it the way you love a relative who shouts—completely, and slightly against your better judgment.

"You're talking to the boat again," Naya said behind him. She came down the bank carrying two mugs of Odell's coffee, which was less coffee than a threat delivered in liquid form. "Wren says if you keep doing it she's going to start drawing faces on the prow."

"Wren can draw whatever she likes. It's her boat."

"It's our boat." Naya handed him a mug. "That was the vote. You abstained, which Wren counted as a yes, which I counted as cowardice."

"I abstained because I was being interrogated by a magistrate at the time."

"Excuses." She sat down on the cradle log, knees up, mug balanced, and looked out at the river the way she looked at everything—like it owed her money and she intended to collect. But there was something softer under it this morning. The dry season did that to people. Took the edge off. Or maybe it wasn't the season.

Her brother's compass hung around her neck on a cord. It pointed nowhere useful anymore—it never had—but she wore it anyway, and Silas understood that too. Some things you carried not for the direction they gave but for the proof that someone had once tried to give you one.

"Big day," she said.

"Mm."

"You could pretend to be excited."

"I'm a ranger."

"You're an ex-ranger. You're allowed to have feelings now. It's in the paperwork. I read it."

Silas drank the coffee, which tasted like boiled leather and regret, and watched the water slide past, brown-green and heavy with the season's memory, running free toward the flats. Toward the horizon. Toward nothing that belonged to anyone, which after everything was the most beautiful thing he knew how to look at.

* * *

Wren came down mid-morning with a rolled chart under her arm and ink on three fingers, which meant she'd been working since before dawn, which meant she was happy. Wren Oduya's happiness had always been legible only to people who knew what to look for: ink stains, rapid speech, and a tendency to narrate her own mapmaking to no one.

"The final proofs," she announced, sitting cross-legged on the sand and unrolling the chart with the reverence other people reserved for scripture. "The new basin survey. Voss signed off yesterday. The printers in the capital start next week." She smoothed the paper flat. "Look."

Silas looked.

It was the Rivergate Basin as it actually was. Every nomad route restored, drawn in Wren's clean hand—the salt crossings, the well lines, the seasonal paths that the concession maps had erased with a stroke of forged ink. The aquifer marked honestly beneath it all, no longer a secret worth flooding a canyon for, just water under land, belonging to itself. In the corner, in small type: *Original surveys by I. Voss. Field verification by S. Pemberton, W. Oduya, and N. —*

"She wouldn't put your full name," Wren said. "Said thieves get initials. Her rule, not mine."

"Naya will be insufferable."

"Naya has been insufferable since the hearing. This just formalizes it."

Silas crouched and studied the routes. He knew them. That was the thing that caught in his chest every time he saw this map—he *knew* them, had grown up on half of them, had spent nine years enforcing the edges of the ones that remained legal, and here they all were again, laid back onto the land like a bandage on a wound that had finally stopped bleeding.

"There," he said, and touched a line near the drowned canyons. "The old ferry crossing. My father used it. Before the dam works."

"It's on there," Wren said quietly. "Everything's on there. That was rather the point."

For a while none of them said anything. The river said enough. It had opinions all season and it shared them freely, slapping against the bank, carrying sticks and silt and one extremely indignant heron downstream toward the flats.

Naya broke it, because Naya always broke it. "So when do we launch? Because Odell's been cooking since dawn, and when Odell cooks for an occasion, we launch before he adds the second fish, or we don't leave for two days."

"We launch at slack water," Wren said. "Which is—" she checked the sky, then the water, then some internal instrument Silas suspected she'd been born with, "—now, actually."

"Now?" Silas said. "We haven't loaded—"

"We loaded yesterday. While you were 'inspecting the perimeter,' which is ex-ranger for 'avoiding feelings near the boat.'"

He opened his mouth to object and found, to his mild alarm, that he didn't have one. They had, in fact, loaded yesterday. They had, in fact, been ready for days. The only thing that hadn't been ready was him, and they had known that too, and they had waited without saying so, the way people do when they've decided someone is worth waiting for.

"All right," he said.

"All right," Naya echoed, grinning, and went up the bank hollering for Odell.

* * *

Odell Brack waved from the bank the way he did everything: slowly, thoroughly, and with both arms, as though waving were a craft he'd been perfecting for decades and would not be rushed in its execution.

He stood on the mud shelf below his camp, fish smoke rising behind him, and he kept waving long after waving made sense—while they pushed off, while the current took the bow, while Wren shipped the oars and Naya took the tiller and the bank began to slide away. Silas raised a hand back and held it up, and the two of them waved at each other across the widening water like a pair of fools, and neither stopped first.

"He'll be fine," Naya said, watching the shore. "He's got the whole river to himself and a legal claim to it now. Voss's survey gave him standing. He said—" she dropped into a fair imitation of Odell's slow gravel, "—'first peace and quiet I've had in forty years, and I intend to be difficult about enjoying it.'"

"He means it," Silas said. "He'll be difficult about it for years."

"Good." She said it firmly, and Silas heard everything underneath it: the camp that had sheltered them, the stall that had cost Odell three days in a concession cell, the debt between them that neither man had ever named and both had paid. Found family, Wren called it once, and Naya had scoffed, and then Naya had started using the phrase herself within the month, casually, as though she'd invented it.

The current took them. The basin opened up.

Silas sat in the stern with his back against the gunwale and let the country come to him. The flats first, white and blinding, then the green smear of the recovering lowlands, then the far blue suggestion of hills that had been names on forged permits and were now, simply, hills. Somewhere out there were the nomad families from the ferry crossing, moving their herds along routes that existed on official paper again. Somewhere out there was Ilsa Voss, rebuilding her survey team from scratch, having interviewed every applicant with the warmth of a customs inspection. Somewhere out there was a magistrate who still sent Silas terse notes correcting his testimony grammar, which was apparently how that woman expressed affection.

And somewhere behind them, in the capital, was a cell where Aldous Reeve was learning what it cost to believe that order required one man to own the map entirely. Silas thought about him rarely now, and without satisfaction. That surprised him. He'd expected triumph to taste like something. Mostly it tasted like coffee Odell made, and mornings that asked nothing of him, and this.

The boat moved. The water held them. That was the whole of it, and it was enough.

* * *

They made camp that first evening on a sandbar Silas had known since he was nine years old—a low crescent of pale ground mid-river, good holding ground, dry even in high water. He hadn't been back since before the permits. He'd told himself for years that stopping anywhere was a risk, that stopping anywhere was a *cost*, that the moving was the safety. Nine years of that arithmetic. Nine years of small debts collected on empty flats, of water rationed and routes closed and a compass in his pocket pointing home to a home that was no longer legal to reach.

He built the fire while Wren argued cheerfully with Naya about whether the eastern channel was deep enough to run at dawn (it was; Wren had measured; Naya maintained that measuring was a form of opinion). He set the kettle on. He did the small, ordinary work of making a place safe for the night, and then—and this was the part that felt like setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing—he sat down inside the circle of it and stayed.

Not patrolling the perimeter. Not sleeping in shifts out of habit. Just sitting. Firelight on the water. Two voices bickering like sisters over a chart by lamplight. A river running past toward a horizon that belonged to nobody, which meant, he supposed, that it belonged to everyone, himself included, if he wanted it.

He wanted it.

"Silas." Wren, without looking up from the chart. "You're doing the face."

"What face."

"The one where you look like a man solving a crime that turns out to be his own life."

"He does do that," Naya said. "I've seen it. Usually right before he admits something."

"I'm not admitting anything."

"You're sitting down," Naya said. "At a camp. At nightfall. Voluntarily. For you that's practically a confession." She leaned back on her hands, firelight catching the brass compass at her throat, and grinned at him across the flames—that thief's grin, sharp and warm at once, the grin of a girl who had stolen a compass to find her brother and found, instead, two people who stayed. "Face it, ranger. You stopped."

Silas looked at the fire. At the river. At the two of them, framed against the darkening water like something out of a story he would never have believed if he'd read it.

"Yeah," he said. "I suppose I did."

Wren reached over and, with the precise hand that had redrawn an entire basin, turned his mug right side up. He hadn't noticed it was tipped over. That was the kind of partner she was.

"Dawn run down the eastern channel," she said. "Then the flats crossing, then the ferry families want us to bring the new charts to the autumn gathering. It'll take all season."

"Good," Silas said.

"That's it? No objections? No lecture about route risk?"

"The routes are on the map now." He settled back against the log, fire warm on his shins, and watched the water slide silver-dark toward the edge of the world. "All of them. Somebody verified every line personally."

"Somebody should be thanked," Naya said solemnly.

"Somebody is going to make you bail the boat tomorrow if you keep this up."

The laughter went around the fire and came back, the way laughter does among people who have bled for each other and earned the right to be ridiculous together. Above them the stars came out over the Rivergate Basin, thick and indifferent and beautiful, and below them the river ran on—unowned, unmapped in any way that mattered, running free toward a horizon that belonged to no one.

Silas Pemberton, who had spent nine years believing that stopping cost more than moving, closed his eyes at a camp with people who stayed, and slept.
