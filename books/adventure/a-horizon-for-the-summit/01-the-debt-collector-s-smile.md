## 1. The Debt Collector's Smile

The storm had cost Bram Okafor eleven thousand dollars, and it hadn't even had the decency to show up.

He stood in the doorway of the Salt & Cinder Bar in Port Calloway, rain sheeting off the awning behind him, watching the Vane brothers occupy a corner booth like weather of their own. They'd chosen the seat with their backs to the wall and a view of both exits—Bram noticed these things professionally now, the way other men noticed tide tables or barometric pressure. Silas Vane sat on the left, big through the shoulders, hands wrapped around a mug of coffee he never drank. Dorian Vane sat on the right, smaller, neater, smiling at something on his phone. The smile was the problem. Silas you could read like a falling barometer. Dorian smiled the way a calm sea smiles at a man in a leaking boat.

Bram considered, for perhaps two full seconds, turning around and walking back into the rain. Then Dorian looked up from his phone without moving his head, the way a hawk does, and lifted one hand in a small, delighted wave.

*Come in, come in,* the wave said. *We've been waiting so patiently.*

Bram crossed the room. The bar was half-empty at four in the afternoon—a handful of fishermen nursing beers, a woman doing a crossword, the bartender very suddenly fascinated by the taps. Nobody looked at him. That was the thing about the Vanes. They didn't need muscle in the room because everyone in the room already knew who they were, and knowing was enough. Fear traveled faster than fists ever could.

"Okafor." Dorian slid out of the booth just far enough to offer his hand. His grip was dry and precise, a handshake like signing a receipt. "You look terrible. Sit down. You want a drink? Get the man a drink."

"I'm fine."

"He's fine," Dorian told the bartender, who brought a whiskey anyway and set it down in front of Bram without meeting his eyes. "See? He's not fine. Nobody who says they're fine is fine. Drink your whiskey, Bram."

Silas said nothing. Silas rarely did, early on. He watched Bram the way a crane watches shallow water—one eye, unblinking, patient past all reason—and Bram understood that whatever Dorian was, Silas was the part that happened afterward.

Bram sat. He didn't touch the whiskey. "I'm guessing this isn't a social call."

"God, no." Dorian laughed, genuinely delighted, and Bram hated how much he liked the sound. That was the trap of the Vane brothers: they were *fun*. They were the funniest men in any room, the best storytellers, the ones who remembered your mother's name and asked after her hip. You could spend an evening with them and walk away thinking, *those aren't so bad.* And then the interest compounded. "Social calls are for people whose contracts don't have clauses. Yours has clauses, Bram. Beautiful clauses. I wrote some of them myself."

"The storm turned north," Bram said. "Forty miles north. There was nothing to chase. I filed the report—"

"You filed a report," Dorian agreed. "A lovely report. Charts, telemetry, a very sincere paragraph about wind shear. Do you know what we do with reports?" He leaned forward, confiding, as if sharing a family recipe. "We sell them. To insurers, mostly. A storm-chasing contract isn't for chasing storms, Bram. It's for *certainty*. Our clients pay us to know where the weather is going to be before it gets there, so they can move their assets. You sold us a beautiful description of where the weather wasn't." He spread his hands, sorrowful. "Eleven thousand dollars of nowhere."

"The system was forecast to make landfall at Cape Verity—"

"Forecasts." Dorian said the word the way other men said *gossip*. "You're the one who taught me forecasts are stories, Bram. Remember? Two years ago, the Kestrel job. You stood right about there—" he pointed at a spot near the jukebox, as if the memory lived in the floorboards "—and you said, *Dorian, the models are wrong, I can feel it in my teeth.* And you were right. We made forty grand off your teeth." He sighed happily. "I miss those days. Where are your teeth lately, Bram?"

Bram picked up the whiskey and drank it. It was good whiskey, which was its own kind of insult. "What do you want?"

"There he is." Dorian tapped the table twice, pleased. "Business Bram. My favorite Bram." He produced a folder—not a tablet, never a tablet; the Vanes believed paper couldn't be hacked, only burned—and laid it flat between them but didn't open it. "Let's review. Total outstanding principal: sixty-one thousand, four hundred dollars. Accrued interest, equipment liens, and the unfortunate matter of the boat you financed against the *Kestrel's* hull—" he glanced up, eyebrows raised, gentle as a priest "—which brings us to eighty-eight thousand even. Isn't that satisfying? Such a round number. I rounded it down, by the way. Consider it a gift."

Across the table, Silas shifted his weight. It was the smallest movement, six inches maybe, but the whole booth seemed to lean with him, and Bram felt the old animal arithmetic start up in his spine: *exits.* Front door, twelve feet, two fishermen between him and it who would not get out of the way. Back door past the kitchen, probably chained—it always was when the Vanes were in residence. Window over the booth, painted shut since the Carter administration. Bram catalogued all of it and then, deliberately, set it aside, because running from the Vanes wasn't escape, it was just dying somewhere with better scenery.

"Here's what I want," Dorian said. "I want to tell you a story first. Indulge me. Our father—may he rest, wherever he rests—built the salvage business on a simple principle. He used to say: *the sea takes everything eventually. We just decide who it takes it from.* Forty years he ran fleet operations out of Grayhollow and points south, and every year the sea gave up its cargo, and every year we decided. Wrecks, insurance losses, boats that sank too conveniently—we decided." He smiled at Bram, warm as lamplight. "And then the sea got greedy, and the regulators got greedy, and Dad got dead, and here we are, a modern enterprise, diversifying. Weather futures. Data contracts. And lending to ambitious men with excellent instincts and terrible luck."

"Flatterer," Bram said.

"See, that's the thing about you." Dorian pointed at him, delighted. "Everyone else begs. You joke. I respect it enormously. It's going to make this so much easier." He opened the folder at last and turned it around. Inside was a single page, and beneath it, a photograph.

The photograph showed a stretch of coastline Bram knew too well: gray shingle, black headland, the stub of the old lighthouse at Grayhollow Point. The page above it was a contract, dense with clauses, and at the bottom, a line for his signature.

"Sixty days," Dorian said. "You find us something valuable. Salvage rights, recoverable assets, information with resale value—I'm flexible on the definition, Bram, genuinely, I'm a flexible man. In exchange, the debt dissolves. All of it. Eighty-eight thousand, gone, like weather." He laced his fingers together on the tabletop. "Or don't sign. Also fine. Totally fine. In that case Silas takes an inventory."

"An inventory," Bram repeated.

"Of everything you own. Which, granted, won't take long." Dorian's voice stayed light, almost apologetic, and that was the horror of it—the menace arrived sideways, dressed as bookkeeping. "The truck. The instruments. The boat, such as she is. Your father's charts, I imagine, though God knows why you keep those. And then there's the matter of the lien on the *Kestrel*, which means she comes apart for scrap, and scrap prices being what they are, you'll still owe us sixty-two thousand after. So you'd be walking away with the clothes you're wearing and a debt that follows you to any port that has a bank, and most ports that don't." He shrugged, boyish. "We'd hate that. Honestly. We like you, Bram. You're the only client who's ever made Silas laugh."

Silas didn't laugh. Silas looked at Bram and said, in a voice like gravel settling, "Sixty days is generous."

"There it is," Dorian murmured. "The quarterly generosity."

Bram looked at the contract. He read fast—he'd learned to read contracts the way sailors learn to read clouds, hungrily and with a constant expectation of betrayal—and the clauses were clean, which frightened him more than dirty ones would have. Clean meant Dorian wanted him to sign. Clean meant this wasn't a squeeze; it was a *hiring*.

"Why Grayhollow?" Bram asked. "Why now?"

Dorian's smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a shutter adjusting, a lens refocusing. "Because you know that coast. Because you find things. That's your whole tragic gift, Bram. You look at water other people see and you see *what's under it*." He tapped the photograph once. "There's something on that coast worth finding. We're sure of it. We're so sure we're willing to bet eighty-eight thousand dollars that a broke storm chaser can find it before our survey fleet does."

"Your survey fleet."

"Five days out," Dorian said pleasantly. "But fleets are blunt instruments, and you—" he raised his whiskey in a small toast "—you're a scalpel. Find it first. Sign."

The rain hammered the windows. Somewhere behind the bar a radio murmured a marine forecast, low pressure systems stacking up off the shelf like freight cars. Bram thought about the *Kestrel* moored at the public dock with her hull patches and her tired engine, the last true thing his father had left him besides the charts and a talent for being wrong in profitable directions. He thought about eighty-eight thousand dollars, and about sixty days, and about the particular quality of silence that came off Silas Vane like cold rolling off ice.

He thought about running. He ran the routes again anyway—front door, back door, window—out of habit, the way a gambler counts cards in a game he's already lost. Then he laughed. It surprised even him, a real laugh, rough at the edges, and Dorian's face lit up like Bram had given him a present.

"There he is," Dorian said softly. "There's the bravado. God, I love the bravado. It's my favorite part. Right before the signature."

Bram picked up the pen. He signed his name—*Bram Okafor*, the same letters his father had taught him to write on chart margins, *measure twice, cut once*—and slid the page across the table.

"Pleasure doing business," Dorian said, and meant it, which was the worst part. He folded the contract away, stood, dropped a bill on the table that covered the whiskey three times over, and clapped Bram once on the shoulder with real affection. "Sixty days. Starting tomorrow. Oh—and Bram?" He paused, and for half a second the cheerfulness thinned, and Bram saw the ledger underneath the man, cold and endless. "Don't leave Grayhollow. Not for more than a day. Silas worries."

Silas rose too. He didn't clap anyone's shoulder. He looked at Bram for a long moment and said, "The sea takes everything eventually," and followed his brother out into the rain.

* * *

Bram sat in the booth for a long time after they were gone.

The bartender refilled his whiskey without being asked and retreated. The crossword woman finished her puzzle and left. Outside, the storm that had failed to arrive was arriving somewhere else, and Bram felt its absence like a debt of its own.

He took out his notebook—the cheap waterproof one, salt-swollen, held together with tape—and flipped past three years of wind readings and fuel costs to a blank page. At the top he wrote: *88,000 / 60 days.* Underneath: *Grayhollow.*

He knew the village the way you know a scar. Fading fishing town, half the fleet sold, the lighthouse automated and the keeper still living in the cottage below it out of stubbornness or grief. His father had chased wrecks along that coast before the storms took him, and Bram had grown up on the stories: the *Cormorant*, the *Halcyon*, ships that had gone down in the squalls off the point, their cargo dissolved into rumor. If the Vanes were sending a survey fleet, they weren't guessing. Somebody had found something. A chart, a logbook, a deathbed confession—some thread, and the Vanes wanted Bram to pull it for them because Bram pulled threads cheaper than lawyers and drowned quieter than divers.

He wrote *assets* and underlined it twice. He wrote *exits* and stared at it. There were none. That was the elegance of it. Dorian hadn't given him a task; he'd given him a current, and you don't swim out of a current, you swim *across* it, if you're lucky, if you're strong, if the sea permits.

Bram closed the notebook. He drank the whiskey down to the last bitter centimeter and set the glass down with a click that sounded, in the emptying bar, exactly like a lock turning.

Then he went out into the rain to find his truck, and drive north, toward Grayhollow, toward the gray shingle and the black headland and whatever the tide was hiding there—because the tide, he would learn soon enough, was lower than it had been in forty years, and it was about to give something back.
