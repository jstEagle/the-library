## 4. Six Tides

The plane came out of the southeast at half past seven in the morning, when the light was still gray and forgiving and a man could almost believe the day belonged to him.

Bram heard it before he saw it—a wasp's drone swelling over the headland—and his body knew the sound before his mind caught up. Twin-engine, high-wing, flying a lazy grid pattern that no fisherman ever flew and no coast guard ever bothered with. He'd spent six years chasing storms across three oceans, and you learned aircraft the way sailors learn weather: not by studying them but by learning which ones meant trouble.

This one meant trouble with a paint job. Matte gray, no registration markings visible at this distance, and a camera pod slung under its belly like a tumor.

He stood very still on the flats, ankle-deep in water so clear he could see his own shadow printed on the sand below him, and watched it pass. It didn't circle. That was the worst part. A search plane circles when it's looking for something it hasn't found. This one flew a straight line from the headland to the cave mouth and back again, unhurried, the way a man walks through his own kitchen.

Then it banked south toward Grayhollow proper, made one pass over the harbor—counting boats, Bram thought, counting hulls, photographing everything that floated—and climbed away into the cloud deck until the drone of it faded to nothing and the sea took the silence back.

Bram exhaled slowly.

*They know.*

He didn't know how they knew. Maybe the same anomaly that had pulled the tide out farther than any chart promised had shown up on someone else's instruments too. Maybe a dockhand had seen him walking the flats at dawn with a look on his face that men who owed money weren't supposed to wear—the look of a man who has found something. Or maybe the Vanes simply owned enough eyes along this coast that nothing happened on it without arriving, neatly catalogued, on their breakfast table.

It didn't matter how. What mattered was arithmetic.

Bram waded back up the flats toward the cave, moving faster than he wanted to admit, and ducked inside where the light went green and cold and the sound of the sea changed from a roar to a breathing. The compass sat where he'd left it, wedged in a crevice above the tideline, brass gone dark as an old coin. Its needle lay steady now, locked on that bearing out to sea, patient as a dog waiting at a door.

"Okay," he said to it. "Okay. Let's do the math."

Math was the one thing the Vanes couldn't take from him. They could take his boat, his contract money, his name in the salvage registries—they were well on their way to all three—but numbers stayed honest even when everything else learned to lie. He crouched against the wall, pulled the stub of pencil from his jacket, and started figuring on the back of a fuel receipt.

The tide tables were burned into his memory; he'd been reading them since he was small enough to drown in a rain barrel. Today's extreme low had come at 6:12 in the morning. The next comparable low—the next window deep enough and long enough to work the submerged passage beyond the cave—would come roughly twelve hours and twenty-five minutes later, near sunset. Then two lows tomorrow, two the day after, each one a little less extreme as the storm system that had dragged the sea off its foundations moved on and the ocean remembered its manners.

And here was the thing the charts wouldn't tell you, the thing only a storm chaser would know: the Meridian Star's cave wasn't just revealed by big tides. It was *protected* by them. The autumn equinox series—the six great tides that came twice a year when moon and sun pulled together—scoured the entrance channel clear. After the sixth tide passed, sediment would start silting back into the throat of the passage, and within a week the cave would seal itself for the season like a wound closing. He'd seen the mechanism already: the long gravel bar outside, the way the current combed it smooth. The sea had opened a door, and the sea would shut it.

Six tides. Counting tonight, six windows before the door closed until spring.

Against that: the syndicate's fleet. Salvage barges didn't move fast, but they moved *prepared*. If the scout plane had radioed coordinates this morning—and it had, because planes like that never carried film anymore, they carried transmitters—then the Vanes' nearest barge, probably working the wreck-removal contracts down at Corvess Harbor, could be underway by noon and standing off Grayhollow inside five days. Five days, with cranes and compressors and dive crews who got paid whether the water killed them or not.

Five days. Six tides. And somewhere between those two numbers, Bram Okafor's life was going to be decided.

He wrote the figures down and looked at them and felt the fear arrive—not the hot animal panic he'd known in collapsing storm cells and flooded engine rooms, but something colder and more useful. The fear of a man reading a map of a country he is about to cross on foot.

Because here was what the math actually said, if he was honest about it: he couldn't win alone. One man with a dinghy and a borrowed dive mask did not beat a salvage syndicate to a wreck in a sealed cave. One man didn't even *try*. One man cut his losses, took the compass, and ran—which was what Bram Okafor did, always, professionally, the way other men went to church.

But.

He turned the compass over in his hands. On the back, worn nearly smooth, someone had scratched four initials and a date forty years old. *E.M.C.* — and beneath it, smaller: *for whoever needs finding.*

"For whoever needs finding," Bram read aloud, and laughed, once, without much humor. "Well. Found me."

The needle didn't waver. It pointed out through the rock and the water toward the place where the Meridian Star had been dying for four decades, and Bram understood suddenly that running wasn't actually on the menu anymore. The Vanes had given him sixty days to find something valuable or lose everything. But if the Vanes reached that wreck first—if they raised whatever the Meridian Star had carried, or worse, *buried* whatever she'd carried—then the debt wouldn't matter, because there'd be nothing left to bargain with and nowhere left to stand. Men like the Vanes didn't collect debts from witnesses.

So the choice wasn't run or stay. The choice was arrive first or never arrive at all.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what he'd done a thousand times before a chase: preparation as prayer.

The cave gave him what he needed if he paid attention. A dry shelf of rock above the high-tide line, broad enough to stage gear. A freshwater seep threading down the far wall—he tasted it, grimaced at the mineral tang, marked it anyway, because water was water. Two exits: the mouth he'd come in by, and the drowned passage at the rear where the water ran green-black and cold and the compass needle pointed like a finger pressed flat against glass.

He swam the passage mouth twice, just to the edge of his light, feeling the current's pull. Outbound on the ebb, inbound on the flood—it breathed with the tide, the whole cave inhaling and exhaling like something asleep. Which meant every dive window was also a countdown, and every countdown ended with the sea changing its mind.

On his second swim he found the first artifact: a ship's bell, half-buried in silt at eighteen feet, its bronze lip furred with barnacles. He dug around it with his fingers until he could read the casting.

MERIDIAN STAR. 1931.

His heart did something complicated in his chest—triumph and dread in the same beat, two notes struck at once. Not a rumor, then. Not a story the old-timers told over beer. She was real, she was *here*, and she was close enough to touch, which meant she was close enough for anyone to touch.

He surfaced in the green light of the passage and hung there, treading water, listening to the cave breathe.

"Six tides," he said to the dark. "You hear me? I'm coming."

The dark, being the sea, said nothing. It never had. That was why he'd loved storms for so long—they were the only conversation the ocean ever offered, and even then it did most of the talking.

* * *

By late afternoon he'd made his list, and the list was short and ugly.

Air. He had one scuba bottle, half-charged, borrowed against a debt he hadn't told the lender about. He needed compressors, or at minimum a hand pump and spare tanks. Money he didn't have. Lights. Line. A lift bag if anything down there was worth raising. And people—God help him, *people*—because the math didn't work otherwise. Someone to run surface watch while he dove. Someone who knew these waters well enough to predict what the Vanes' pilots would see. Someone who could hold a secret in a village where secrets were currency and everyone was broke.

He knew exactly three people in Grayhollow, and he'd known them for four days.

There was the woman at the harbormaster's office who drew charts that nobody bought, with hands that shook when she thought you were looking at her too closely. There was the dock kid—sixteen, maybe seventeen—who'd beaten every grown man in the village at the breath-holding game off the pier, and who watched Bram with the flat, calculating eyes of someone pricing an exit. And there was the lighthouse keeper, old Maren Cole, who had looked at him yesterday like she was deciding whether to warn him off the flats or off the planet entirely.

None of them owed him anything. That was the problem with building a crew from strangers: you couldn't pay them with trust you hadn't earned, and you couldn't promise them shares in a wreck you might die reaching.

But the compass pointed at what a person *needed*, and Bram was starting to suspect the needle worked both ways—that it didn't just find things for you, it arranged for you to be found. Why else had the tide pulled back farther than it had in a generation, on the one morning he'd walked those flats with nothing left to lose?

He was still turning that thought over when the light changed.

Not sunset—the sun was still a handspan above the horizon—but a shadow crossing it. He stepped out of the cave mouth in time to see the scout plane again, low now, lower than regulations allowed and lower than caution required, coming straight up the channel toward the cave with its camera pod glinting.

It passed directly overhead—close enough that he could see the rivets, close enough that he was certain, absolutely certain, the man in the right seat saw him standing on the rocks with salt drying white on his forearms. The pilot didn't wave. The pilot tipped one wing, slow and courteous, the way you'd tip your hat to a man whose house you intended to buy.

Then it was gone south, and the sound faded, and Bram stood in the last of the light doing the final piece of arithmetic, the one that mattered more than tides or fleets.

The Vanes had smiled at him across a desk eleven days ago and told him he had sixty days. They'd been lying, or rather, they'd been generous, the way cats are generous with mice. The sixty days had never been real. The real deadline was the one written in water: six tides, five days, one wreck, and two parties racing for it with everything they had.

"They're going to be polite about it," he murmured. "That's what scares me. They'll offer to partner. They'll offer to forgive. And the moment I say yes, I'll be cargo."

The tide was turning. He could feel it in the cave behind him—the exhale ending, the inhale beginning, the water rising inch by patient inch toward the marks the sea had set for it. Tomorrow's low would come at 6:40 in the morning. He'd need to be in the water by first light, and he'd need help, and help was going to cost him things he'd sworn never to spend: the truth, mostly, in installments.

Bram looked at the compass one last time. The needle held its bearing, steady as a heartbeat.

"All right," he said. "We race."

He shouldered his gear and started down the flats toward the village lights, walking fast, a man with five days and a list of three names, and behind him the tide came in over the cave mouth like a door quietly closing—though not, not yet, for good.
