## 6. A Diver Without a Past

The docks at Grayhollow smelled of diesel and dying fish, and Tess Ibarra had learned to read both the way other people read weather. Diesel meant money moving. Fish rotting past the point of sale meant money stopping. This morning, the whole harbor smelled like the second kind, which was why she was sitting on the bollard outside the bait shack, shelling mussels into a bucket, when the storm chaser with the debt-shaped face came looking for her.

She'd seen him around for three days now. Bram Okafor. Everyone in Grayhollow knew the name by now—the way small towns know things, through the fish market and the church steps and the woman who ran the chowder stand and missed nothing. He was tall in the way that suggested he'd once tried to make himself smaller and given up. He had a way of standing at the edge of conversations like a man checking exits, and he'd spent the last three days asking the dockhands the same question in different shapes: *Who can dive? Who can hold their breath? Who isn't afraid of dark water?*

The answers kept coming back the same. *Ask the Ibarra girl. If you can find her.*

Tess cracked a mussel shell with her thumbnail and flicked the meat into the bucket. She knew he'd found her the moment his footsteps changed—people's footsteps always changed when they were about to ask you for something. She didn't look up. Looking up was a concession, and concessions were currency, and she was not spending currency on a man who smelled like bad contracts and salt-water desperation.

"You're the one who dives," he said. Not a question. Good. Questions were for people who didn't already know.

"I'm the one who shells mussels," she said. "Diving's a rumor."

"The rumor says four minutes."

"The rumor's a liar. Four and a half." She looked up then, because it was worth it to watch his face do the small, involuntary arithmetic. Most adults tried to hide it. He didn't bother. She liked that about him, provisionally, the way you liked a stray dog that hadn't bitten you yet.

"Four and a half minutes," he repeated. "On one breath."

"Static. Cold water takes a minute off. Moving water takes more." She went back to the mussels. "You want to buy a rumor or a fact? Facts cost more."

"How much more?"

Tess set down the knife and finally gave him her full attention, which she knew from experience was an unsettling thing to receive. She was seventeen and looked fifteen and had learned to use both. "Depends what you're diving for, how deep, and what happens to me if it goes wrong. In that order."

"Can't tell you the first one."

"Then we're done." She picked the knife back up.

He didn't leave. She'd known he wouldn't—desperation had a smell too, and his was coming off him like heat off an engine. He crouched down so they were closer to eye level, which she appreciated and distrusted in equal measure. Adults who got on your level either respected you or were trying to pick your pocket, and the two were not mutually exclusive.

"Can I tell you what I *can* tell you?" he said.

"You can tell me anything you want. Listening's extra."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, maybe, the way a scar is the ghost of a wound. "There's a wreck. Off Grayhollow. Old one. I need someone who can get into it before a certain group of people get into it first. The water's cold, the passage is tight, and I can't pay much up front."

"But?"

"But there's a cut at the end. A real one. If we find what I think is down there, you'll never shell a mussel for money again."

Tess considered the bucket. Fourteen mussels, which at the bait shack rate came to about a dollar sixty. She'd been shelling mussels for a dollar sixty since she got to Grayhollow in the spring, sleeping in the loft above the chandlery in exchange for sweeping it, eating whatever the chowder stand woman decided she'd eaten too slowly to deserve. Before that there had been other towns and other docks and other small jobs that paid in small money, and before *that*—

She put the before-that away. It lived in a room in her head with the door shut, and the door stayed shut, and that was the whole architecture of her survival.

"Here's my price," she said. "Twenty percent of whatever comes up. Not of the profit—of the *haul*. You don't get to tell me it was worth less than you hoped."

"Ten."

"Twenty, and no questions."

His eyebrows went up. "No questions about what?"

"See, you're doing it already." She pointed the mussel knife at him. "No questions about where I came from, who I came from, why a seventeen-year-old girl can hold her breath longer than grown men who get paid to do it, why I don't have a boat, a license, a school, or a last name anyone's heard of. You want my lungs, you get them without the story attached. The story's not for sale."

"And if I need the story? If someone asks who's on my crew?"

"Lie. You seem good at it."

That got the almost-smile again, wider this time. "Fifteen percent, no questions, and I feed you."

"Twenty, no questions, you feed me, and I get to walk away whenever I want. No notice, no explanation. If I'm gone one morning, I'm gone, and you tell anyone who asks that I was never on your crew at all."

He was quiet for a moment. Out on the water, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither of them would ever get. Tess watched him do the math she'd watched a hundred adults do—the math of how much a person was worth against how much a person cost. Most of them got it wrong. Most of them thought people like her were cheap.

"Twenty percent," he said. "No questions. You eat with the crew. And you can walk." He held out his hand. "But if you walk in the middle of a dive, I'm not coming after you."

"Wouldn't want you to." She shook it. His hand was cold and there was a tremor in it he couldn't quite hide, and she thought: *this man is more scared than I am, and he's still doing the thing anyway.* She filed that away. It was either useful or it was a warning, and she'd know which soon enough.

* * *

The chandlery loft was four feet of space between the rafters and the ceiling joists, reachable by ladder, warm in the way that trapped heat under tin roofs is warm. Tess lay there that night listening to the harbor breathe—water slapping pilings, the clank of a loose shackle somewhere, the far-off argument of drunks—and thought about the word *crew*.

She'd been on a crew before. She'd been born onto one.

The Ibarra family dove for salvage out of Corazón Bay, and the Ibarra family had rules, and the rules had a single commandment underneath all of them: *obey*. Her father said it in Spanish and her mother said it in English and her uncles said it with their hands, and by the time Tess was twelve she could free-dive to thirty feet, work a wreck blind in silt, and recite the family's price list from memory. What she couldn't do was ask why they never sold anything at the price it was worth. Why the good finds went into her father's truck and came back empty. Why her cousin Marisol stopped talking after the dive on the *Santa Elena*, and why nobody asked her why, because asking questions was a form of disobedience and disobedience got corrected.

Tess had been a good diver. She'd been a better listener. And the thing she'd heard, the summer she turned sixteen, crouched in the dark behind the engine shed while her father and uncle counted money that didn't match any dive they'd done—the thing she'd heard had made the door in her head open whether she wanted it to or not, and what was behind it wasn't a room. It was a road. South out of Corazón Bay, then north, then wherever the buses went.

She'd left with her mask, her fins, and the clothes she was wearing. She'd left her mother crying in the kitchen and her father saying, in the flat voice he used for weather reports, *she'll come back when she's hungry enough.* Two hundred and eleven days ago. She was still hungry. She was not going back.

The difference, she'd learned since, was this: her family had wanted her obedient. They had called that love, or they had called it family, or they hadn't called it anything at all because naming it would have required looking at it. And Tess had spent two hundred and eleven days learning what the actual thing felt like, and mostly it felt like nothing, like absence, like a chair pulled out from a table. But once or twice—the chowder woman leaving an extra roll without comment; the chandlery owner fixing the loft ladder so it wouldn't wobble and never mentioning it—once or twice she'd felt the edge of the real thing, the way you feel warmth from a fire in the next room. Not aimed at you. Just existing nearby.

She didn't trust it. Trust was for people whose doors stayed shut.

* * *

Bram came back the next morning with a woman in tow—tall, gray-streaked, with the posture of someone who'd spent her life being right in rooms full of people who hated it. "Tess Ibarra," Bram said, "this is Odile Marchand. She's our navigator. Odile, this is our diver."

"Your diver," Odile said, in an accent that put little hooks in the vowels. "How old are you, your diver?"

"Old enough to hold my breath," Tess said. "How old are you, your navigator?"

Bram made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Odile looked at Tess for a long moment, and Tess looked back, and something passed between them that Tess didn't have a name for yet—two people measuring each other with instruments that didn't measure height or weight but something closer to *damage*, and finding the readings compatible.

"The cave passage is narrow," Odile said. "The wreck sits deeper. You'll be diving on a line in poor visibility, possibly in surge. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means I need a good line layer and someone on the surface who doesn't panic."

"It means you could die."

Tess shrugged. "Everyone does. I'd just rather do it after I get paid." She watched Odile's face tighten and thought, *there it is*—the flicker adults got when a kid said the true thing instead of the brave one. Bram, though. Bram didn't flinch. He just nodded, like she'd confirmed something he'd already written down.

"First dive is tomorrow," he said. "Tide window's at dawn. Eat tonight. Sleep if you can."

"Is the food part of the twenty percent or extra?"

"It's extra," Bram said. "God, you're going to be expensive."

"Cheaper than drowning," Tess said, and climbed down off the bollard, and went to find out how many rolls the chowder woman would part with before dawn.

* * *

She tested him that night. She couldn't help it. Testing was how she learned the shape of a person—where they bent, where they cracked, where the hollow spots were.

She found him on the seawall past midnight, sitting with his elbows on his knees, watching the black water. She sat down next to him without asking, which was its own kind of test, and for a while neither of them said anything. The tide was coming in. Somewhere out past the headland, the sea was doing whatever it did over the cave he wouldn't tell her about.

"Four and a half minutes," he said eventually. "Where does somebody learn that?"

"No questions."

"I know. I'm not asking. I'm just—" He exhaled. "I ran numbers for a living. Storms, mostly. You can model a hurricane down to the mile if you have enough data. People are harder. I keep trying to model you and the data doesn't work."

"Because you're asking the wrong questions."

"Which are the right ones?"

Tess pulled her knees up. The harbor light turned the water the color of old pewter. "You want to know if I'll panic down there. You want to know if I'll steal from you. You want to know if I'm running from something that's going to come find me and make your life complicated." She said it flat, like reading off a list. "The right question is whether I'm any good. I'm any good. The rest is weather."

He was quiet for a while. Then he laughed—not much of one, a short exhale through the nose—but it was real, and she felt it land somewhere under her ribs, which annoyed her.

"You know what I was doing three weeks ago?" he said. "Chasing a storm in Kansas for a man who wasn't going to pay me. I knew he wasn't going to pay me. I did it anyway, because the alternative was sitting still, and sitting still meant thinking, and thinking meant—" He stopped. Shook his head. "You said no questions. I guess it goes both ways."

"It always goes both ways," Tess said. "That's the deal. That's why the deal's cheap."

"Twenty percent is cheap?"

"For a person with no past? It's a bargain. People with pasts charge interest."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she held the look the way she held her breath—steady, economical, waiting for the burn and refusing to flinch at it. And what she saw in his face wasn't pity, which she would have walked on, and wasn't suspicion, which she would have walked away from. It was recognition. It was the look of a man seeing his own kind of empty room behind somebody else's eyes.

"Get some sleep, Tess," he said. "Dawn tide."

She slid off the seawall and walked back toward the chandlery, and behind her she heard him stay where he was, watching the water, a man keeping his own kind of vigil over his own kind of door.

* * *

The loft was warm. Tess lay on her back with her arms behind her head and did the thing she did every night: counted the exits. Chandlery window, two feet, drops to the alley. Harbor side, the skiff with the bad oarlock, always unlocked. The north road if she could get past the dog at the net shed. Three exits, memorized, rehearsed, hers.

Except tonight, when she got to the end of the count, she didn't feel the usual click of the lock going home. Tonight there was a loose thread in it, and the thread was a man on a seawall who'd stopped mid-sentence rather than ask a question he'd promised not to ask, and a woman with hooked vowels who'd measured her damage and hadn't flinched, and a twenty percent cut of something at the bottom of cold dark water that she'd sworn she'd walk away from whenever she wanted.

*Whenever I want,* she reminded herself. *That's the deal. That's the whole deal.*

But when she finally slept, she dreamed not of the road south or the engine shed or her father's flat weather-report voice. She dreamed of the dive—black water, a line in her hand, and somewhere below her, a wreck holding its breath, waiting, and the strange and terrible thought rising through her like a bubble: *what if, for once, she didn't want to be the first one to the surface?*

She woke before the alarm, before dawn, before the gulls. Down in the harbor, Bram was already at the seawall, loading tanks into a dory, and beside him stood an old woman Tess had never seen before—small, straight-backed, with a face like weathered rope and eyes that had clearly spent forty years looking at the same stretch of water. The old woman was holding something in her closed fist, and Bram was looking at it the way men look at things that frighten them.

Tess pulled on her boots and went down to find out what it was.
