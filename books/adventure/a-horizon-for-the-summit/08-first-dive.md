## 8. First Dive

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and by half past we were standing at the mouth of the cave with the sea pulling back from us like a held breath.

I had not slept. None of us had, really, but I'd made a show of trying—rolled out my sleeping bag on the shingle above the tideline, stared at the canvas of the cave's ceiling through the entrance until the stars wheeled behind me and gave up their watching. Sleep was a currency I couldn't afford to spend. Every hour I lay unconscious was an hour the Vanes' fleet moved closer, an hour the water climbed higher in its slow seasonal siege of the cave, an hour closer to the moment when this whole mad arithmetic of ours would come due.

So instead I sat up in the dark and checked gear that didn't need checking, and listened to Tess Ibarra breathe.

She slept like something feral curled on warm stone—one arm under her head, the other draped over her dive knife, which she'd refused to surrender even for sleep. Seventeen years old, maybe eighteen if you pressed her, and she could hold her breath for four minutes flat in cold water without so much as a flutter in her pulse. I'd watched her do it twice now, once as demonstration and once, I suspected, as warning. *This is what I am. Don't mistake me for cargo.*

Odile Marchand sat apart from all of us, as she did everything, hunched over her charts by lamplight with a pencil tucked behind her ear like a carpenter's nail. She had redrawn the cave system three times since yesterday, each version more detailed and more apologetic than the last. Falsified charts had ended her career; now she was building a true one, line by careful line, as though accuracy were a penance she could perform in graphite.

And Maren Cole—the keeper, the survivor, the woman who had looked at that brass compass in my palm and gone white as bleached bone—stood at the cave mouth watching the tide fall, her lantern hanging from one hand, saying nothing at all.

"Water's down," she said finally. "Passage'll be clear in twenty minutes. Maybe twenty-five."

"Then we go in twenty," I said.

Tess opened one eye without moving anything else. "You always this charming before dawn?"

"I'm never charming. It saves time."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and was gone, quick as a fish turning. She rolled upright, began checking her rig with hands that moved like they'd been doing this since before she could read. Maybe they had. Runaways learn early that competence is the only inheritance nobody can take from you.

* * *

The submerged passage beyond the cave was exactly what Odile's maps said it would be, which still felt like a small miracle every time her predictions came true—a sensation I imagined she found equal parts gratifying and insulting.

We entered at slack low water, four of us in the gray-green throat of the coast: Tess first, because no argument on earth would have put her second; then me; Odile behind me with a slate and grease pencil strapped to her chest, mapping as she went because she couldn't help herself; Maren last, though she was sixty-three years old and hadn't dived in forty years, and when I'd suggested she stay topside she'd looked at me with an expression that could have stripped barnacles off a hull.

"The Meridian Star is mine to find," she'd said. "Not yours. Not theirs. Mine. I buried everyone on that ship except myself, and I intend to visit the grave."

So she dove. Slowly, carefully, with a regulator she'd tested three times and a calm that was either courage or a kind of grief so old it had fossilized into stillness. I decided it didn't matter which.

The passage ran narrow and black, the walls close enough to touch on both sides, kelp streaming from the cracks like hair underwater. My light cut a cone through water thick with suspended silt, and beyond the edge of the beam there was nothing—not dark exactly, but the *absence* of anything, the particular nothing that lives beneath the sea and has never once cared about human plans.

Tess swam like she'd been born in the wrong element and had finally come home. She moved through the constriction where the passage pinched between two fallen slabs with a economy that made my own progress feel clumsy, and when the tunnel opened—suddenly, enormously—she hung in the new space and turned back toward us, and even through the mask I could see her eyes go wide.

The passage had opened into a cathedral.

That's the only word for it. A flooded chamber the size of a church nave, roofed by stone that arched up beyond the reach of our lights, walled in rock streaked with mineral color—rust-red and copper-green and a deep bruised purple where the light didn't quite reach. The water here was clearer, filtered through some hidden seam, and in the beams of our lamps the silt drifted like snow falling upward.

And there, wedged in a fissure at the far end of the chamber, hull-down against the rock like a ship kneeling in prayer, lay the Meridian Star.

Forty years underwater, and her bones were whole.

I felt it hit me in the sternum—that feeling I'd been running from for a decade, the one I'd trained myself out of the way you train yourself out of any dangerous habit. Wonder. Pure, unmonetized, unclaimed wonder. The wreck rose out of the seafloor silt with her masts snapped and her rails furred white with anemones, but her lines were still beautiful, still proud, the sheer of her sweeping up toward a stern that some long-dead shipwright had carved with scrollwork I could just make out under the growth. She wasn't treasure. She was a *person*. She was a body, and we were swimming through her funeral, and forty years late someone had finally come to pay respects.

Tess reached her first. She ran a gloved hand along the hull where the fissure gripped it—stone teeth closed around oak ribs—and looked back at me with a gesture that needed no words: *wedged tight, going nowhere, ours if we can crack her.*

Maren drifted along the hull like a woman walking a cemetery row, touching the growth here and there with two fingers, light as communion. At the stern she stopped. Her shoulders changed. I swam closer and saw what she was looking at: letters beneath the weed, raised brass or bronze, and she swept her palm across them once, gently, clearing forty years of the sea's handwriting.

MERIDIAN STAR.

Her hand stayed there a long time. In the beam of my lamp I watched her fingers tremble against the metal, watched her press her forehead briefly to the hull, and I looked away, because some grief isn't meant for witnesses.

Then she straightened, and pointed forward, toward the ship's waist, and made the signal we'd agreed on: *cargo hold, amidships.*

* * *

We found the hold on the second pass, and that was where exhilaration curdled.

The Meridian Star had gone down hard—Maren's account, told in fragments over the past two days, said she'd struck the outer shoal in a gale and limped into the coast to die—and somewhere in her dying, part of the superstructure had collapsed inward. The main hatch was a tangle of collapsed decking and a fallen spar, passable with care. But beneath it, blocking the hold itself, a bulkhead had let go—not torn away, which would have left a gap, but *folded*, buckling into the compartment like a door kicked in by a giant, and it had brought tons of shattered planking and ballast stone down with it.

A wall of wreckage, packed tight, sealing whatever lay beyond.

Tess examined it the way a burglar examines a safe—methodically, disrespectfully, with visible contempt. She worked her way along the collapsed bulkhead testing seams, wriggling into gaps and backing out, and finally surfaced from the wreckage cloud and gave me the signal we'd dreaded and expected: crossed arms, flat palms. *Sealed. No way through.*

Odile was already sketching. Of course she was. She drew the collapse in profile on her slate, measured angles by eye with a cartographer's arrogance, and wrote beneath it a single word I could read even mirrored: *explosives.*

Which meant going back to shore, coming back with charges, spending another tide window—and another day closer to the fleet.

I was turning this over, doing the arithmetic I do, the cold little ledger-keeping that has kept me alive and broke for thirty-one years, when I saw it.

At first I thought it was a fish. A pale shape drifting at the edge of my vision, high in the water column near the roof of the chamber, where the fissure in the rock opened upward into darkness. Then it swung, lazy and deliberate, on a tether, and my heart went from steady to hammering in the space of a single breath.

A buoy marker. A small one, a diver's pennant float, wedged up in the upper fissure where the passage narrowed—bright synthetic orange, factory-new, with a stenciled code along its side that I couldn't read at this distance and didn't need to.

I knew that orange. Every salvager on this coast knew that orange. Vane Brothers Marine Recovery marked their claims with it, cheerful as a child's toy, and everywhere it floated, lawyers followed.

They were already here.

Not the fleet—the fleet was days out, Odile had confirmed the harbor reports, the big salvage vessels were still fitting out in Grayhollow's deeper port twenty miles south. But a scout team had come ahead. Had found the upper fissure, maybe from the seaward side, maybe days ago, maybe *weeks*—and had dropped a marker to claim the site the way a dog marks territory, and had left, planning to return with the fleet and the lawyers and the winches.

Or they were still here. Somewhere in the black water above us, in the passages we hadn't mapped, breathing compressed air and waiting.

I got everyone's attention the way you do underwater—banging my tank, lights flashing—and signaled us out. Now. Quietly. No discussion. Tess read my face and understood immediately; she'd grown up around men whose expressions carried sentences. Odile frowned, wanting to know, wanting to map, but she followed. Maren lingered one last moment at the stern, one gloved hand flat against the letters of the name, and then she turned and came with us, and I swear the wreck seemed darker after she left it.

* * *

We surfaced in the cave chamber with the tide beginning its turn, water already creeping back across the flats outside, and I told them what I'd seen while the words still tasted like copper.

"A marker." Odile said it flatly, the way you repeat bad news hoping repetition will change it. "In the upper fissure."

"Vane orange. Fresh. The stencil hadn't even faded."

"They know about the cave." Tess was coiling her line with sharp, violent efficiency. "How long have they known?"

Nobody answered, because nobody knew, and the not-knowing was worse than any answer. Had the scout plane I'd seen on the third day spotted the cave mouth? Had a dockhand sold information? Had the Vanes' father's charts—forty years old—always shown more than anyone guessed?

"It doesn't matter how long," Maren said. She'd pulled her hood back and stood dripping in the lamplight, sixty-three years old and suddenly looking every one of them, and also looking like the most dangerous person in the room. "What matters is what they don't have. They've marked the site. They haven't breached the hold—they couldn't have, not with that bulkhead down, not without heavy equipment and time, and if they'd gotten through, they'd have taken what's inside and burned the rest." Her voice caught slightly on *burned*, and steadied. "The ledgers are still in there. Whatever your compass wanted us to find—it's still in there."

"The bulkhead comes down tomorrow," I said. "Third tide. We bring charges, we set them low along the fold line where Odile says the stress will split it, and we're through before the fleet clears the harbor."

"And if the scouts are still in the caves?" Tess asked. "If they're sitting in some side passage right now listening to us plan?"

"Then we'd better be faster than they are."

It sounded braver than it felt. What it actually felt like was the moment in a storm chase when the sky goes green-black and the pressure drops and you realize the tornado is not ahead of you anymore—you just don't know which direction it's come from. Exhilaration and dread, poured into the same glass, indistinguishable.

Outside, the sea was coming back for the cave, patient as a creditor. We had perhaps an hour before the lower passage drowned, and then the water would own the approach for another full cycle, and we would sit in the dark counting hours we didn't have.

Tess shouldered her gear and paused at the cave mouth, looking out at the rising water, and said, quietly, almost to herself:

"There's silver in that hold. I can feel it. And somebody's already hung a flag on it."

Nobody corrected her. That was the trouble. Nobody corrected her, because she was probably right—and the flag was flying, and the fleet was coming, and we had five tides left to steal a dead ship's secret out from under the living ones who wanted it buried.

The first wave slid across the threshold of the cave, reaching for our footprints in the wet sand, and took them.
