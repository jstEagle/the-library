## 10. The Vane Brothers' Offer

They came for him at the Saltrow Inn, in the hour when the lamps were lit but the fire had burned down to a sullen orange memory, and Bram Okafor understood—before either of them said a word—that the meeting had been arranged down to the minute. The innkeeper had vanished into the kitchen. The two dockhands who'd been nursing ale by the window had paid their tabs and left without finishing their drinks. Even the dog that slept by the hearth had lifted its head, considered the doorway, and slunk out into the rain.

That was the thing about the Vane brothers. They didn't need muscle. The world simply made room for them.

Corvan Vane came in first, shaking rain from his coat like a man returning from church, pleased with the weather and with himself. He was the elder by four years, broad through the shoulders, with his father's heavy jaw and his father's habit of smiling before he said anything that mattered. Dallen followed, thinner, quieter, carrying a leather document case under one arm the way a priest carries scripture. Dallen did the arithmetic. Corvan did the smiling. Between them they had swallowed half the salvage yards on the coast, and Bram's storm-chasing rig along with them—or rather, the debt on it, which amounted to the same thing.

"Bram Okafor." Corvan dropped into the chair across from him uninvited, boots up on the rung, rainwater pooling off his hem onto Bram's floorboards. "You look terrible. Has anyone told you that? You look like a man who hasn't slept in a week."

"I've been busy," Bram said.

"Busy." Dallen took the other chair, set the document case on his knees, and did not open it. That was its own kind of message: *we can wait, we have everything already.* "Chasing storms. Walking beaches at low tide. Sitting in taverns." He smiled thinly. "We keep tabs, Okafor. You know that."

Bram kept his hands loose around his cup of cold tea and let the silence stretch just long enough to seem bored. Inside, he was doing what he always did around these men—counting exits, counting seconds, counting the exact distance between his face and theirs. Old habit. A chaser learns to read pressure drops in the sky; Bram had learned to read them in a room.

"Sixty days," Corvan said. "You remember the terms."

"Day thirty-one," Bram said. "I can count."

"Can you?" Corvan laughed, warm as brandy. "See, this is why I like you, Bram. Most men in your position grovel. You sit there drinking cold tea like it's whiskey and tell me you can count. It's almost admirable." The laugh drained away between one breath and the next, the way it always did, leaving something flat and patient underneath. "Almost."

Dallen opened the case. From it he drew not papers but a chart—Grayhollow and the coast east of it, hand-annotated, and Bram's stomach went cold before his mind caught up, because there, in faded ink, was a circle drawn around a stretch of shoreline, and beside the circle, in someone's careful hand: *anomalous tidal exposure, survey incomplete.*

"We had a plane up last week," Dallen said mildly. "Routine coastal survey. Interesting weather system. Interesting what it strips off the seabed when it goes through." He laid the chart on the table between them, smoothing it flat with both palms, an oddly gentle gesture. "And then interesting reports from Grayhollow. A stranger asking about tide tables. An old woman at the light who hasn't spoken to a living soul in years suddenly receiving visitors. A cartographer nobody's seen since she was run out of Port Meridian. And a dock girl who dives without gear."

"You've been busy too," Bram said.

"We're thorough people. Our father was thorough." Corvan leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the smile was gone entirely and Bram saw the family resemblance to something older and colder than either brother—the shape of the man who had built the syndicate out of wrecks and widows' claims. "Here's what I don't understand, Bram. You owe us eleven thousand crowns. Your rig is collateral. Your future earnings are collateral. By any sane measure, you should be drowning. Instead you're assembling a crew. So either you've found something worth more than eleven thousand crowns—" he spread his hands, inviting Bram to disagree, "—or you've lost your mind. And I've met you. You don't lose your mind. You calculate."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked and went silent.

"Say what you came to say," Bram said.

* * *

What they came to say was simpler than Bram expected, and worse.

"Full forgiveness," Corvan said. "Every crown. The rig back, clear title. A bonus besides—enough to start over anywhere you like. Inland, even. No more storms, no more chasing, no more us." He said it like a gift, like generosity, and that was the poison of it: every word true, every word a hook. "All we want is the compass."

Bram didn't move. Didn't blink. Somewhere behind his ribs his heart was hammering out a diver's rhythm—fast, controlled, conserving air—and he let none of it reach his face.

"What compass?" he said.

Corvan sighed, almost fondly. "Bram. The cave on the flats. The wreck of the Meridian Star. The brass compass found inside her, the one that doesn't point north. Don't insult us. We've known about the Meridian since before you were born. Our father spent forty years and a small fortune trying to find her, and do you know why?" He tilted his head. "Because of what she carried."

"Silver," Bram said. "Every wreck carries silver. That's the whole business."

"Silver," Dallen agreed softly, "and paper."

He said the word lightly, the way a man might say *ash* or *dust*, but Bram heard the weight under it. Paper. Ledgers. Names. And beneath the word, faint as a pressure drop on the horizon, he felt the true shape of the offer assemble itself—not treasure they wanted, not wealth, but *absence*. A hole where evidence used to be.

"Maren Cole," Corvan said, and the name landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. "Sole survivor of the Meridian Star. Forty years keeping our lighthouse, forty years keeping her mouth shut. She was there, Bram. She saw the cargo loaded. She signed nothing, testified to nothing, because there was no one left to testify to. But a sworn statement from Maren Cole—a statement confirming what the ship truly carried, and who put it aboard—" he shrugged, expansive, reasonable, "—that, combined with the compass and whatever documents you've recovered, would settle certain old questions permanently. Quietly. In our favor."

"And if I say no?"

"Then day sixty arrives, and we take the rig, and we take the lien on anything else you own, which after our survey will include whatever you pull out of that cave, and we take it with lawyers instead of smiles, and you'll wish we'd come with smiles." Corvan said it without heat, without threat, the way a man describes tomorrow's weather. "But you won't say no. Because here's the beautiful part, Bram: saying yes costs you nothing. You get your life back. The old woman gets her pension doubled—we'll see to it. Everyone walks away richer. All we're asking is that some paper that's been rotting in salt water for forty years stays rotting."

Bram looked down at his tea. He thought about Odile, who had burned a map once and had been burning ever since. About Tess, who asked no questions because questions got people hurt. About Maren, standing in her lamp room with forty years of silence stacked around her like ballast.

He thought: *they don't want the silver at all.*

It settled over him with a sick, clarifying cold—the same clarity he got in the last hundred feet before a wall cloud, when all the noise stripped away and there was only the storm and himself and the truth of how small he was. The Vanes weren't treasure hunters. They never had been. Their father hadn't scuttled the Meridian Star for profit alone; he'd scuttled it to drown names, claims, partners, and the proof of what he'd done. And now the sons were offering eleven thousand crowns—which was nothing, less than nothing, pocket change against a salvage fleet—to buy back the one thing money couldn't otherwise touch.

And Bram was the tool they'd chosen to hold it. Not a partner. Not even properly an enemy. A debt with legs. A pair of hands they could rent cheap because he was desperate enough to rent them.

"That's a generous offer," Bram said, and heard his own voice come out smooth, easy, regretful—the voice he used on charter clients when the weather turned and he intended to fly anyway. "I'll be honest with you, Corvan. I haven't found the compass. I found the cave, yes—I'm not a fool, I know you know—but the interior's flooded past the second chamber. My diver couldn't make the passage. Whatever's in there is sealed until the spring tides, maybe longer. If the compass exists, it's sitting under forty feet of rock and water, and I've got five days and no equipment worth the name."

It was a good lie. It had the right grain of failure in it, the right smell of a man admitting weakness. Dallen studied him with pale, patient eyes, and Bram held the gaze the way you hold a heading in crosswind—hands steady, eyes forward, trusting the instrument and not the feel.

"The spring tides," Dallen repeated.

"Ask your own divers when they get in there. Ask them what the fissure does to a man's air." Bram shrugged. "You want my advice? Take the forgiveness offer off the table and put a salvage contract on it instead. I'll sign exclusivity. You pay me when I deliver. Everybody's incentives line up."

Silence. Rain. Then Corvan laughed again, and this time it reached his eyes, and that was somehow the most frightening thing yet.

"There he is," Corvan said, rising, buttoning his coat. "There's the calculator. All right, Bram. New terms. You have until the sixth tide. Bring me the compass and the old woman's testimony, and everything's forgiven. Fail, and we stop being generous." At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back with something almost like kindness. "You know what I can't figure out? Why a man like you is protecting a lighthouse keeper he met a week ago. There's no math in that. There's no angle. Men like us, we understand angles." He shook his head, genuinely puzzled, and Bram realized with a strange, distant ache that Corvan Vane was not lying. He truly could not see it. "Sixth tide, Okafor."

The door closed behind them. The innkeeper crept back out. The dog returned and lay down by the dead fire.

Bram sat for a long time with his cold tea, watching the rain crawl down the glass, feeling the shape of the trap close around him with every tide. They had given him a deadline that matched the sea's own clock. They knew about the cave, the crew, the compass. Which meant the fleet was already coming, and the sixth tide wasn't an offer at all.

It was a countdown.

He finished the tea, left coins on the table, and stepped out into the rain, turning toward the headland and the light—because Maren needed to hear every word of this tonight, and because somewhere out past the point, beyond the gray curtain of the squall line building over the water, the first masts of the syndicate fleet were already bending toward Grayhollow.

Three tides, he thought. Maybe fewer.

He started walking faster.
