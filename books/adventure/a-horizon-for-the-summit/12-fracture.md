## 12. Fracture

Tess counted her breaths the way other people counted money.

Four minutes, eleven seconds, dry. That was her record in the old cistern behind the cannery, back when she was still Tess Ibarra and not just Tess, back when holding her breath was a game instead of a skill she'd need to survive. She sat on the overturned dory in Maren Cole's boat shed, lungs full, watching the lamplight waver across the faces of the only people who had never once asked her where she came from.

Bram was drawing on the back of Odile's chart. A rough sketch of the wreck wedged in its fissure, the collapsed bulkhead, the sealed hold beyond. The silver was in there. Tess had seen it through the crack in the bulkhead with her own eyes—chests, dozens of them, their iron bands furred with rust but sound, and through a split lid the dull white gleam of coin bars stacked like bread loaves.

She let the breath out slowly and spoke before she could think better of it.

"The silver's right there."

The lamplight stilled. Bram looked up from the chart, pencil hovering.

"Through the bulkhead," Tess said. "Forty feet of rock and one rotten wall between us and enough money that none of us ever has to look over our shoulder again. That's not a treasure story. That's arithmetic."

Odile, folded into the shadow by the stove with her maps, didn't look up. Maren sat by the lamp with her hands around a mug of tea gone cold hours ago. Bram set the pencil down with the exaggerated care of someone buying time.

"It's not that simple."

"It's exactly that simple. That's what nobody wants to say out loud." Tess slid off the dory, restless, her body always wanting to move when her mouth started. "The Vanes want the ledgers. Fine. The ledgers are paper. Paper burns, paper sinks, paper gets argued about in courtrooms full of lawyers the Vanes have owned for forty years. But silver is silver everywhere. Silver doesn't need a judge. Silver doesn't need anyone to believe anything."

"You've been thinking about this." Bram's voice was careful. Too careful. Tess knew that voice. It was the voice Bram used on the dockhand they needed to lie to, the voice that smiled while it calculated.

"I've been *breathing* about this. It's different."

* * *

The trouble was, Tess had done the numbers so many times they'd worn grooves in her.

A diver's cut of forty chests of coin bars. She'd never been greedy. Greed was for people who believed they'd live long enough to enjoy things. What she wanted was smaller and sharper: a boat with a working engine, a berth in a port where nobody knew the name Ibarra, and enough coin beneath the floorboards that she could stop calculating every single day whether she could afford to be free.

Her family had wanted her obedient. Her father had called it love. *We keep you safe, Tess. We keep you close. Why do you make us keep you close?* The answer, which she'd never said aloud, was that a cage with the door welded shut stops feeling like protection around the time you turn thirteen and realize the world has a horizon.

She'd run with nothing. She'd learned to hold her breath because it was the one thing nobody could take from her—her body's own small sovereignty, four minutes of silence where no one could reach her, not her father's voice, not the dock bosses, not the cold. Underwater was the only place she'd ever been in charge.

And now there was a wreck full of silver, and the one person she'd started to trust was standing in front of it saying *it's not that simple* like a man trying to talk himself out of the obvious.

"Say the plan," she said. "The real one. Not the one for my benefit."

Bram's jaw worked. "We take the ledgers. We take them to a maritime court, or a newspaper, or whoever can use them. The Vanes lose everything—legitimately. The debt dissolves. We walk away clean."

"Clean." Tess laughed, and it came out ugly. "Clean takes how long? A year? Two? While the Vanes' lawyers drown the court in paper and their fleet sits in Grayhollow harbor and their divers are in the water? We have three tides, Bram. Maybe two, the way this storm's moving. You want to win a lawsuit in two tides?"

"Then we take the ledgers and we *keep* them. Buried. Insurance. The Vanes can't touch us if we can end them."

"Insurance." Tess spat the word like a fishbone. "You know what happens to people who hold insurance against the Vanes? Ask Maren."

The silence that followed was total. Maren didn't move, but something in the room shifted, the way water shifts before a wave arrives.

"I didn't mean—" Bram started.

"You did mean. That's the whole point." Tess turned to the old woman by the lamp. "Forty years, Maren. Forty years you've kept that light burning and kept your mouth shut and kept your head down. That's what insurance looks like after forty years. A lighthouse and a locked door and a story you can't tell anybody."

"Tess." Maren's voice was quiet. It was always quiet, and it always landed like a dropped anchor.

"No. I'm tired of being quiet. I've been quiet my whole life and it got me a welded door and a father who called it love." Her hands were shaking. She made fists of them. "I found a family down here in the dark, and now I find out the family's plan is to leave the money in the sea and walk away with *paper*. Paper that gets us killed slower."

* * *

Odile spoke from the shadows, and Tess realized she'd been waiting for it.

"She's not wrong about the arithmetic." The cartographer unfolded herself, came to the lamplight. Her face was unreadable in the way of someone who had practiced unreadable for a decade. "The silver is liquid. The ledgers are leverage, and leverage only works if you survive to spend it. In pure terms—"

"Don't." Bram's voice cracked like a whip. "Don't you *dare* start drafting her argument for her. I know what you've been writing."

Tess watched Odile go very still. "What I've been writing is my own business."

"Two letters," Bram said. "I've seen the drafts. One to the court. One to the Vanes. You've been carrying both in your coat for a week."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, and the shed's boards groaned like a ship's hull. Tess looked from Odile's frozen face to Bram's, and understood, with the sick lurch of a diver whose air line snags on something unseen, that the family had been cracking for days and she'd been the last to feel it.

"Is that true?" she asked Odile. Not accusing. Just wanting to know which floor was solid.

Odile met her eyes. "I haven't sent anything. I've burned nothing either. I'm a mapmaker, Tess. I hold every route open until I know the ground. That's not betrayal. That's how I stay alive."

"It's how *you* stay alive," Tess said. "That's the whole problem. Everyone in this room has a plan for staying alive and not one of them includes the rest of us."

"That's not fair," Bram said.

"Nothing about this is fair. That's why it's called survival and not a picnic." Tess turned back to Bram, and now she said the thing she'd been carrying since the first dive, the thing that had been growing in her chest like pressure in a sealed hold. "You know what I think? I think you don't want the silver because silver is *simple*. Silver is just money, and money is just freedom, and if we took it, there'd be nothing left to be noble about. You'd rather be right than be free. You'd rather sink the truth and stand on the beach and say you did the hard thing. That's not principle, Bram. That's cowardice wearing principle's coat."

The words landed. Tess watched them land. She'd spent her whole life reading people underwater, in the green half-light, reading the flicker of a stranger's eyes before her father's men dragged her back from the cove, and she read Bram now with the same terrible clarity: the flinch, the flush, the moment of pure animal hurt before the composure closed over it like skin over water.

"That's not what this is," Bram said, low.

"Prove it. Tell me the silver means nothing. Tell me we leave forty chests of it in the dark because paper matters more. Say it and mean it."

"I—" Bram stopped. And Tess saw the truth arrive in their face a half-second before the words did: Bram couldn't say it. Couldn't say the silver meant nothing, because it didn't, because it meant freedom, real freedom, and Bram wanted it too, and the wanting was the wound.

The silence went on so long that Maren finally set down her cold tea and stood.

* * *

"I was on that ship," Maren said.

Tess had heard the shape of this story before—the wreck, the scandal, the elder Vane—but not this part, not the part that came out now, in the lamplight, in a voice like a hull taking water.

"My brother Tomás was in the hold when she went down. Not drowned fighting the sea. Drowned because the sea was *sent*. The elder Vane scuttled his own vessel to bury his partners and their claims, and Tomás was a partner's son, and he was nineteen." Maren's hands, which had steered a light through forty years of storms without a tremor, were shaking now. "I survived because I was on deck. I've spent forty years being the only one who could say what happened, and saying nothing, because saying it would have gotten me killed and changed nothing. That's what the Vanes do. They don't just kill the truth. They make the truth so expensive that the people carrying it choose silence. Every year the silence gets heavier and they count on that."

She looked at Tess, and her eyes were not unkind, and that was somehow worse.

"You want the silver because you've never had anything, and I understand that better than you know. I had nothing for forty years too. But that hold isn't full of money, child. It's full of *names*. And the moment we take the silver and leave the names, we become the Vanes. We take the treasure and drown the truth, and forty years from now some runaway kid is sitting in a boat shed explaining why nobody ever came back for *them*."

Tess's throat was tight. "That's not the same."

"It's always the same. That's how it keeps happening."

"It's not the same because I'm not them. I just want out. I just want—" Her voice broke and she hated it, hated the crack in it, the fourteen-year-old underneath the diver, the girl who'd swum out past the breakwater at night because it was the only place her father's voice couldn't follow. "I just want to stop being afraid. Is that so much? Is it cowardice to want to stop being afraid?"

"No," Bram said quietly. "It's the most human thing in this room."

"Then give me something to not be afraid *with*."

"I'm trying to. I'm trying to give you something that lasts."

"Paper doesn't last! Paper is the first thing the sea takes!"

"And silver is the first thing men kill for," Bram said, and for the first time all night their voice rose, cracked open, and Tess saw underneath the composure: the debt, the humiliation, the Vanes' smiles, the whole long grind of being owned. "You think I don't want it? You think I haven't done the arithmetic a hundred times, lying awake, doing what you do—counting the chests, counting the tides, counting the years of my life this would buy back? I want it so bad I can taste the metal. And that's exactly why I can't trust myself with it. None of us can. Not with the Vanes in the harbor. Not with Odile's two letters. Not with—" they gestured at the room, at all of it, "—this much fear in one building."

"So we just leave it. We dive past it. We hold the truth like a candle and pray it doesn't blow out."

"We hold it *together*," Bram said. "That's the only thing I've got. I know it's not coin. I know it doesn't spend. But it's the only thing I've ever had that was worth anything, and I'm not trading it for metal."

Tess stood there, chest heaving, and realized the argument was over and she had lost and it didn't feel like losing, it felt like something tearing.

"Then take your candle," she said. "I'll take the water."

She was at the door before Maren spoke, not loud, just carrying, the way a bell carries across water:

"Tess. The tide turns in an hour. Whatever you decide, decide it on the flats, not in the dark, and come back before it does."

Tess stood with her hand on the door. Behind her: the lamp, the chart, the three people she'd have died for an hour ago and might still, and didn't know anymore, and that not-knowing was the worst feeling she'd ever had, worse than cold water, worse than her father's voice.

She went out into the wind.

* * *

The flats at night were a different country. The tide had pulled back half a mile, exposing the gray ribbons of sand and the black teeth of rock, and the cave mouth yawned in the headland like a held breath. Tess walked out onto the wet sand with her boots in her hand, because she'd always thought better barefoot, skin against the world.

The compass. That was the thing nobody had said tonight, the thing sitting under the whole argument like a stone under sand. The compass that pointed at what you needed. It had pointed seaward for Bram. Tess had held it exactly once, on the first dive, and it had spun and spun and finally settled pointing at the wreck—and she'd told herself it meant the silver, the chests, the gleam through the split lid. Freedom, pointing at freedom.

But lying awake since, she'd wondered. What did it point at, really? What did a girl need who already knew where the money was?

She stood at the waterline, the cold biting her ankles, and looked at the cave, and hated it, and wanted it, and could not make the wanting and the hating fit inside one body.

Behind her, footsteps on wet sand. She didn't turn. She knew the weight of them—Bram walked like someone always about to change direction.

"I'm not apologizing," Tess said.

"I'm not asking you to."

"You should be. Both of us should. That's how families work, isn't it? Everybody apologizing forever?"

Bram came and stood beside her, an arm's length away, the distance of people who weren't sure they were still allowed closer. The wind pushed at them both. Far out, the syndicate's fleet lights sat on the black water like a string of patient, greedy stars.

"Three tides," Bram said. "The bulkhead comes down tomorrow or never. After that, whatever's in that hold, we decide together or not at all. That's my whole position. That's all I've got."

"And if we decide different ways?"

Bram was quiet a moment. "Then I guess we find out what this crew is actually made of."

Tess looked at the cave mouth, at the dark seam of the passage beyond it where a wreck and a fortune and forty years of drowned names waited behind one rotten wall. Her breath came slow and even, the diver's breath, four minutes of sovereignty in a body that had never belonged to anyone but her.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "Third tide."

And out past the fleet lights, past the horizon, the storm that Odile had been mapping all week began, very quietly, to turn toward them.
