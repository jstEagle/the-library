## 13. Third Tide, Rising

The explosives came off a wrecked trawler called the *Petrel*, which had spent eleven years rusting on Grayhollow's northern shoal and had apparently been carrying more than fish. Maren knew about them. Of course she knew about them. She'd known about the cave before we found it, the compass before I held it, and the shape of my own desperation before I'd finished lying to myself about it.

"Flare charges," she said, setting the crate down in the boathouse with a thud that made us all flinch. "Marine salvage grade. My husband's. He was a careful man." A pause, and something moved behind her eyes like weather. "He would have hated what we're doing."

"Then we'll do it carefully," Odile said.

She'd spread her charts across the floor of the boathouse at dawn, weighting the corners with engine parts and coffee mugs, and she'd drawn the interior of the Meridian Star's final resting place from memory—Tess's memory, mostly, corrected twice by Odile's obsessive cross-referencing against the old survey notes she'd burned and then, it turned out, hadn't burned completely. There was always a copy. That was the thing about Odile Marchand: she lied to institutions, never to paper.

"The bulkhead is here." Her finger tapped a section of the hold's aft wall, rendered in her small, precise hand. "Collapsed inward during the scuttling, wedged by the fissure walls on either side. It's not a wall anymore—it's a plug. Behind it is the sealed compartment where the captain kept his private papers."

"And the silver," Tess said from the doorway. She said it every time, like a prayer or an accusation. I'd stopped trying to tell which.

"And whatever else," Odile allowed, without looking up.

Three tides left. I did the arithmetic again even though it hadn't changed since midnight: the syndicate's divers would be in the water within two days, maybe less if the Vanes pushed their fleet through the weather window Odile swore was closing. The storm cells were stacking up offshore like freight cars, and between them lay a gap—a few hours of ugly, workable sea—and after that gap closed, the cave itself would begin its seasonal seal as the winter swells shifted the sandbar at the mouth. After that, nothing human was getting in until spring.

So: three tides. One blast. And a hole in a forty-year-old bulkhead that had already killed a ship.

* * *

We spent the morning placing the charges, and I want to say it went smoothly, but nothing in Grayhollow had gone smoothly since the tide went out too far and showed me a door I should have left shut.

Tess ran the lines. That was non-negotiable; she'd made it so on the first day, when she'd looked at all four of us—me with my debt and my smooth talk, Odile with her burned maps, Maren with her forty years of silence—and said, "None of you can dive worth a damn, so let's be honest about who's doing the work down there." She was seventeen and right, which is a combination that tolerates no argument.

She worked the flooded passage like she'd been born in it, feeding the flare charges into cracks in the bulkhead's weakened seams, packing them with mud and canvas wadding, running detonation line back along the guide rope we'd rigged from the cave mouth. I watched her on the surface interval through the water-glass, that round lens of green-black water, and saw only the shimmer of her fins and the ghost-trace of her bubbles rising in strings, patient as beads on an abacus.

Maren handled the firing box, because she was the only one of us who'd ever touched one that wasn't decorative. "My husband wired the harbor lights for thirty years," she said, checking contacts with a jeweler's screwdriver. "He used to say electricity forgives nothing and forgets everything." She glanced at me over the top of the box. "People could stand to learn from electricity."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means you've been calculating odds since the day I met you, Bram Okafor, and the sea doesn't publish odds." She snapped the last contact home. "It publishes invoices."

Odile stood at the cave mouth with her watch and her tide table and her two letters—I knew about the letters now, had known since the night I found them folded together in her chart case, one addressed to the Vanes' harbormaster and one addressed to no one, and she knew I knew, and neither of us had said a word about it. Some trusts are built out of mutual blackmail. Ours was built out of that and something softer underneath, something I didn't have a name for yet.

"Tide turns in forty minutes," she said. "Window closes ninety minutes after that. If the blast doesn't open the hold, we don't get a second attempt—not safely."

"If the blast opens more than the hold?" Tess asked, surfacing beside the rocks, spitting her regulator out. Water sheeted off her braids. She looked younger with wet hair, and harder, both at once, which I was learning was simply what Tess looked like.

"Then we find out how much of this cave is held up by that bulkhead," Maren said quietly. "And we find out fast."

I looked at the three of them—the mapmaker who'd lied to empires, the keeper who'd lied to herself for four decades, the kid who lied to everyone on principle—and felt the strange, terrifying weight of it. Not the debt. Not the Vanes. This. These people, standing in bad light around a box of stolen explosives, betting their lives on my plan because nobody better had come along.

"On my mark," I said. "Maren?"

Maren put her thumb on the plunger.

"Mark."

* * *

The sea took the explosion and gave back almost nothing—that was the horror of it. No fireball, no roar. Just a deep, felt concussion that traveled up through the rock into our boots and teeth, and a single great bubble of displaced water that rose in the passage mouth and burst with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Gulls lifted off the headland in a ragged white sheet. Somewhere below us, forty years of settled silence got shaken loose.

"Charge one and two fired clean," Maren reported, reading the box. "Three's a dud. Four—" She frowned. "Four shows fired but late. Hangfire delay, maybe. Or a partial."

"Partial means cracked, not opened," Tess said. She was already pulling her gear back on, hands moving fast, no fear in them, which scared me more than fear would have. "Cracked means I go down and finish it by hand. Pry bar and a lifting bag. Twenty minutes' work."

"We wait," I said. "If four's still live—"

"Bram." She looked at me, and behind the salt and the swagger there was that other Tess, the one who'd told me once, in the dark on the dock, that her family had wanted obedience and called it love. "There are three tides left. We don't have time for your math."

She was right. That was the worst part. She was right, and I let her go, and I have thought about that moment every day since—the moment where I ran the numbers instead of trusting my gut, and the numbers said *acceptable risk*, and my gut said nothing at all, because I'd spent so many years drowning it out with calculation that it had forgotten how to shout.

Tess went down at slack water with the pry bar and a spare line. Odile timed her. Maren watched the firing box. I fed the safety line and counted her bubbles, and for eight minutes everything held.

Then charge four finished its argument with the sea.

* * *

It wasn't an explosion, exactly. It was a shrug. The whole passage shrugged—one vast muscular contraction of water and stone—and the guide rope in my hands jumped like a landed fish, and the string of bubbles coming up from below stopped.

Stopped. Not slowed. Stopped.

"Tess, report." Nothing on the line. "Tess." I hauled on the rope and it came, but heavy, snagged, dragging dead weight through a bend in the passage I couldn't see. "Line's fouled. Line's fouled and she's not answering."

"She's got a bailout bottle," Odile said, and her voice was steady the way a bridge is steady, engineered steadiness, load-bearing calm. "Ninety seconds of air if she's calm. Less if she's not."

"How long since the blast?"

"Forty seconds."

I was already in my gear. I don't remember putting it on. I remember Maren's hand on my arm, and I remember shaking it off, and I remember Odile saying something about the collapse zone, about unstable overhead, about the odds—

"No," I said.

It was the first word I'd said in a while that wasn't a calculation. It came out of some floor of me I didn't know was there, older than the debt, older than the chasing, older than the running. My father drowned when I was nine—not dramatically, not at sea, just stupidly, in a reservoir, on a Sunday—and I'd spent my whole life since building a machine out of myself whose entire function was to never be that helpless again. Every horizon I chased was a place where no one could need me. Every contract, every storm, every calculated risk was a way of being needed by no one.

Tess needed me. Right now, in the dark, with ninety seconds of air and a collapsed ceiling between us.

No. There was no version of this where I stayed on the rocks.

I went down into the green black with my torch in my teeth and followed the fouled line into the throat of the cave.

* * *

The passage had changed. That was the first thing. The geometry I'd memorized—the chimney, the elbow, the long low run to the hull—was wrong now, rearranged, the way a room is wrong after a fight. Silt hung in the water like dirty snow that refused to land. My torch beam couldn't reach past six feet of it. Somewhere ahead, the bulkhead had finally given, and it had taken part of the passage's roof with it, and somewhere under that roof was a seventeen-year-old girl who had never once asked anyone to save her.

The line led me true. I followed it hand over hand, feeling for the tension, following the drag toward the snag. My own breathing sounded enormous in my ears, each exhale a thunderclap, each inhale a theft. Thirty meters. Forty. The elbow. Through the elbow the silt began to thin and I saw it:

The debris fan. Half the bulkhead's mass had dropped across the passage floor in a slumped heap of timber and stone, and under the largest slab—under a beam thick as a church pew, pinned by a shoulder of fallen rock—was the line. And past the line, half-buried, one fin. Kicking. Still kicking.

I got to her in four strokes that felt like four miles.

She was wedged in a pocket beneath the slab, on her side, her face turned up toward me, and her eyes found my torch and fixed on it with a ferocity that broke something loose in my chest. Her lips were moving. No sound, of course—just the shapes of words. I read them. *Line's caught. Go around.* Even now. Even pinned under a ton of the Meridian Star's bones, she was telling me the efficient solution. Go around. Cut losses. Calculate.

I shook my head and pointed at the slab.

Her eyes changed. I've seen a lot of things change in people's eyes—fear hardening into greed, greed curdling into panic—but I had never seen what happened in Tess Ibarra's eyes in that green dark, which was the opposite of all of it. Calculation leaving. Leaving all at once, like water out of a pulled plug. What was left underneath was just a kid looking at someone who'd come for her, and not believing it, and believing it.

I got my shoulder under the beam's edge where a broken plank gave me a lip of purchase, planted my fins against the rock, and pushed.

Nothing. The sea pressed back. The beam pressed back. Forty years of ocean pressed back. My lungs started their slow negotiation—*soon, soon, soon*—and I pushed anyway, and the beam groaned, and a crack of light silt shifted, and it came down to physics, and physics said no.

So I stopped being a physicist.

I don't know how to describe what replaced the calculating except that it wasn't courage. Courage implies a choice weighed and won. This was simpler and stupider: I simply declined, entirely, to accept the possibility that the beam would not move. I wrapped both arms around it the way you'd carry a sleeping child, put my whole spine into it, and *refused*. My vision was going gray at the edges. My chest was a fist. The beam screamed against the rock above her—a long metal-on-stone shriek transmitted straight through the water into my skull—and moved. An inch. Two. Enough.

Tess moved like an eel, like the diver she'd sworn she was, sliding free through the gap with her bailout bottle already at her lips, and then her hands were on my harness and her regulator was jammed into my mouth, and we were sharing air in a collapsing cave, rising through our own blizzard of silt, and the last thing I saw before the darkness took the passage behind us was the bulkhead's remains settling into the space where she'd been, closing like a book.

* * *

We broke the surface inside the cave's high chamber, where the air pocket held, and the sound Tess made wasn't crying exactly. It was the sound of someone setting down something they'd carried so long they'd mistaken it for their skeleton.

"You came down," she said. "You didn't do the math. You just—you came *down*."

"I did the math," I said, coughing seawater. "The math was terrible. I ignored it."

She laughed—a raw, cracked, seventeen-year-old laugh with terror still in it—and then she grabbed the front of my exposure suit and pressed her forehead against mine, and we stayed like that, two runaways bobbing in a cathedral of stone, breathing.

Above us, boots on rock. Odile's voice, tight as wire: "Bram! Tess! Report!"

"Both up," I shouted. "Hold's open. But the passage back to the hull is buried—we go in through the breach or not at all."

A pause. Then Maren's voice, older and lower, carrying the weight of a woman who had waited forty years for this cave to give up its dead: "Then we go through the breach. Next tide."

Tess looked at me, salt-streaked and shaking and grinning like the survivor she was. "Three tides left," she said. "Two, really."

"One and a half," Odile called down, and I heard her unfold her charts against the rock. "And Bram—the blast opened more than the bulkhead. The fissure walls shifted. There's a new passage on my survey that wasn't there yesterday, heading deeper under the headland." Her voice dropped, and even shouting across the chamber I caught the edge in it. "It heads toward the old smugglers' galleries. Toward the route the Vanes' divers will use."

The tide turned below us. I felt it first as a pull at my fins, faint and patient, the sea beginning to take back everything it had lent us—including, in a tide and a half, the cave itself.

"Then we'd better be faster than the water," I said, "and faster than the Vanes."

Somewhere far off, beyond the stone and the swell, the first thunder of the arriving storm rolled across Grayhollow like a door being locked behind us.
