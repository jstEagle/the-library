## 14. Breath

The dark down there was not like other dark.

Tess Ibarra had known the dark of a locked closet, the dark of a cellar in a house she'd fled at fifteen with nothing but a knife and a grudge, the dark of harbor water at night when the lamps on the docks went out one by one and a girl could swim until her arms forgot she had a name. She had made a home in the dark. It was the one place nobody could follow, the one country that didn't ask for papers.

But this dark had weight. This dark had teeth.

The blast had gone off wrong—half a heartbeat too late, angled a degree off true—and the bulkhead that was supposed to fold inward had instead shrugged its shoulder and shrugged down, bringing half the fissure wall with it. Tess had felt the pressure change before she heard anything, a fist of displaced water shoving her sideways into the rock, and then the world became a churning gray roar full of grit and broken timber and the taste of her own blood where her lip had caught a ledge.

When it settled, she was wedged.

Her left arm was free. Her right was pinned at the elbow beneath a slab of hull plating the size of a door, and her air line—the good line, the one Odile had spliced herself and sworn on her grandmother's grave was sound—was caught somewhere behind her in the collapse, kinked or crushed or severed, she couldn't tell. The gauge on her back read a number that was falling. The regulator hissed against her teeth in a rhythm that felt less like breathing than like counting down.

*Don't panic.* That was the first rule, the rule she'd learned at eleven in a swimming hole in a town whose name she'd scrubbed from herself, the rule that had kept her alive through four years of harbors and holds and men who thought a girl alone was a thing to be collected. *Panic spends air. Panic spends everything.*

She breathed. Slow. Four counts in, six counts out. The way Bram had drilled into all of them on the first morning, standing on the cave's wet stone with the tide pooling around his boots, saying it like a prayer or a contract: *The water doesn't care how strong you are. It only cares how calm.*

The water did not care. The water was patient. Somewhere above her, forty feet and a collapsed passage away, a storm cell that was supposed to arrive tomorrow was arriving now, and the pressure of it was already making itself known as a low groan in the rock, a sound like the cave clearing its throat.

Tess did the math because math was the only friend she had down here. Her gauge: maybe eleven minutes of air, less if she moved, less if she bled, less if the line was worse than kinked. Her right arm: pinned, and the slab was not something a girl of seventeen with one free hand was going to shift. The passage out: behind her, or where behind her used to be. The collapse had redrawn the map. Odile's map was now a lie, and there was a bitter joke in that which Tess would have laughed at if laughing didn't cost air.

She had always known it would end like this. Not exactly like this—she'd imagined cold, imagined alone, imagined the particular silence of a body giving up in dark water—but the shape of it, the *alone* of it, yes. Everyone left. That was the first law of Tess Ibarra's universe, older than any rule about breathing. Her family had left her long before she'd climbed out their window; they'd left her while she was still sitting at their table, eating their food, being shaped like clay into the daughter they'd ordered. *Obedient, not loved.* She'd understood the difference at nine years old, and she'd been planning her exit ever since—every job, every crew, every port a temporary arrangement, a room rented by the night. Even this one. Even Bram, with his crooked grin and his ledger of debts and his eyes that went soft when he looked at his people like they were a horizon he'd finally stopped chasing. *Even him,* she'd told herself the first night, watching the four of them huddled over Odile's charts by lamplight. *Especially him. The good ones leave last, but they leave.*

The regulator hissed. Nine minutes, maybe.

She tried the arm again anyway, because she was Tess Ibarra and she had never once in her life gone down without working the problem. She braced her boot against the rock floor and pulled, and the slab answered with a grinding groan and half an inch of give, and then a fresh slide of gravel from above that pattered against her shoulder like the cave laughing at her. Fine. Half an inch. She'd take it. She'd take half an inch at a time until the air ran out, because the alternative was to stop, and stopping was a thing she had never learned.

That was when the light came.

A glow first, wavering, green-gray, the color of deep water lit from above—and then a shape inside it, moving through the murk with the unhurried certainty of someone who knew exactly where he was going, which was impossible, because the passage was gone, the passage had ceased to exist, and yet Bram Okafor came around the shoulder of the collapsed wall like a man walking into his own kitchen.

His headlamp found her and held.

She saw his face do something complicated—fear and fury and relief all fighting for the same real estate—and then he was there, hands on the slab, testing it, reading it the way he read weather. He couldn't shout underwater. He didn't need to. His eyes asked the question every diver's eyes ask: *Air?*

She showed him the gauge. His jaw tightened. He showed her his: better, but not good. Not nearly good enough for two.

He tried the slab. He got the same half inch she'd gotten, and the same rain of gravel, and she saw him understand in real time what she'd understood minutes ago—that this was not a problem strength could solve. He stopped pulling. He put his palm flat against the rock instead, and closed his eyes, and *listened*, and Tess thought: *he's crazy, he's actually crazy*—and then she heard it too, under the groan of the storm pressing down from above. Water. Moving. A thin current threading through the rubble behind her, cold against her ankle, coming from somewhere and going somewhere.

Where water went, a way went. That was the oldest law in the cave, older than the lintel, older than the wreck. *The tide forgives.* Maren had said it like scripture. Bram had said it like strategy. Down here, with her arm pinned and her air counting down, Tess heard it like a door.

Bram's hands found the slab again, but differently now—not pulling straight, but levering, rolling, hunting the hinge in the rock the way the current had. He worked with a patience that would have infuriated her an hour ago and now looked like the most beautiful thing she had ever seen. Half an inch. An inch. The slab groaned, shifted, ground against her elbow—and then, with a shudder that went through the whole collapse zone, it rolled free into the gap below and was gone, swallowed by the dark, and her arm came loose in a bloom of pain so bright she nearly blacked out.

She didn't have time for the pain. The gauge said four minutes, and the current was pulling, and Bram had her wrist in his hand.

He pointed. Not back the way he'd come—back was choked, back was a grave—but down and left, along the thread of moving water, into a gap that was barely a gap, a seam in the cave's bones that no chart would ever show because it hadn't existed an hour ago. The cave was making a new way out for them, or the sea was, or the storm was. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that Bram went first, into the unknown dark, so that anything that wanted her would have to go through him.

*Everyone leaves,* said the first law of Tess Ibarra's universe.

Bram did not leave. Bram pulled her into the seam.

* * *

The regulator died at the three-minute mark. It didn't fail dramatically; it just thinned, the way a song thins when the singer runs out of breath, each draw giving less than the last. Tess felt it happen and felt the old cold animal fear rise in her chest like floodwater—and then Bram was there, pressing his own regulator into her hands.

She shook her head. She tried to push it back. The arithmetic was simple and terrible: one mouthpiece, two divers, and the seam still twisting away into the dark with no promise of an opening. One of them was going to run out. That was how the math ended. It had always been how the math ended, for her, in every version of every plan she'd ever made. She'd simply never thought she'd be the one holding the air.

Bram's hand closed over hers on the mouthpiece. He didn't take it back. He held it there, in her grip, and looked at her through the murk with an expression she would spend the rest of her life trying to find words for—not pity, not sacrifice, not the shining martyr look she'd seen on people doing charity. Just certainty. Plain as a chart. *This is not a negotiation.*

Then he tapped his chest. Twice. And she understood: *breathe when you need it, I'll take it when I need it, we do this together or not at all.*

They shared it the way drowning people share anything—badly, desperately, on a rhythm that had no room for pride. Two breaths for her, two for him. The seam narrowed, widened, narrowed. Her shoulder scraped rock and she didn't feel it. His headlamp guttered, steadied, guttered again, painting the walls in stroboscopic flashes: stone, stone, stone, and once, absurdly, a starfish clinging to the ceiling of the world, orange as a struck match, alive and unbothered in all this dying dark.

Between breaths, in the gaps where her lungs burned and her mind went glassy, Tess found herself thinking things she had never let herself think. That Bram Okafor had known her for eleven days. That he knew she stole food from the crew stores and had never said a word. That when she'd told him *no questions* on the dock at Grayhollow, he'd nodded and meant it, and had never once, not in eleven days, looked at her the way people looked at runaways—like a problem, like a debt, like a daughter-shaped hole in someone's life. He looked at her like a diver. Like a person whose hands were good and whose word held. Like someone who belonged on a boat.

*Belonged.* The word surfaced in her mind and she flinched from it, because belonging was a trap with soft walls. Belonging was what her family had called the leash. Belonging was how they'd made her small.

But this—this dark seam, this shared mouthpiece, this man in front of her spending his own air like it was a currency he'd rather lose than hoard—this didn't feel like a leash. It felt like the opposite. It felt like being *carried*, and Tess Ibarra had not been carried since she was small enough to be carried, and she had forgotten—she had genuinely, completely forgotten—what it did to a person. It made you want to put down the knife. It made you want to stop counting exits. It made the dark, for the first time in her life, feel like a place she was passing through instead of a place she lived.

Her vision was going gray at the edges. His too, she could see it, the sluggish drift of his movements, the way his kicks had lost their rhythm. The seam was still going. The current was still pulling. Neither of them had any idea how much farther, and the arithmetic had stopped being math and started being a coin flip with both their lives on it.

She took two breaths. He took two. She took two more, and on the second one she felt his hand tighten on her wrist—not pulling the regulator back, not yet, though he must have needed it, though his body must have been screaming for it—just *holding on*. Making contact. Telling her, in the only language left to them, that she was not alone in the dark, that whatever the next minute held, it would hold them both.

*Everyone leaves,* said the first law.

*Not this one,* said something new, something being born right there in the black water, small and stubborn as a match flame. *Not this one. Not these.*

The seam bent upward.

* * *

They broke the surface into a cavern full of storm-light.

Tess's first breath was the largest thing she had ever felt—a whole sky's worth of air, cold and wet and salt, and she drank it in great racking gulps while her body shook and her arms paddled on pure animal instinct, keeping her head up, keeping her alive, keeping her here. Bram surfaced beside her, coughing, hauling air, and for a long moment neither of them did anything but breathe, the two of them hanging in black water under a ragged ceiling of stone, gasping like landed fish, like miracles, like people who had been given back.

The cavern was small, maybe twenty feet across, its roof pierced by a chimney of gray light that flickered and strobed with every lightning strike somewhere far above. Rain was coming down the chimney in threads. The sea heaved somewhere beyond the cavern's seaward wall, and every few seconds the whole chamber shuddered with the boom of surf against rock, and through a crack in the far wall Tess could see it: the storm, arriving early, arriving huge, the sky over Grayhollow bruised purple and the whitecaps running like a stampede.

The cell that was supposed to hit tomorrow. The window Odile had mapped, the narrow safe channel between two storm fronts—closed. Gone. They'd surfaced into the middle of the thing they'd been racing, and the race had a new shape now, and the new shape was worse.

But Tess found, floating there in the heaving dark with her lungs full and her arm screaming and her whole body one long shudder of spent adrenaline, that she could not make herself care about any of it. Not the storm, not the wreck, not the Vanes, not the six tides, not the ledgers or the silver or the argument that had split the crew three days ago and was still sitting unhealed between her and Bram like a third person in every room.

*You called it cowardice,* she thought, watching him cough and spit and live in the strobing light. *You called it principle dressed up pretty. And then you gave me your air.*

There was a version of her—the one who'd climbed out a window at fifteen, the one who'd swum away from four crews and two harbors and one family—who would have taken the regulator and not given it back. Who would have done the math and let the math decide and slept fine afterward. That version had kept her alive. She owed that version everything.

But that version had never had anyone give her their air.

Bram got a hand on the cavern's rock lip and dragged himself half out of the water, and turned, and reached back for her—reached back without hesitation, without calculation, the way you reach for someone who is simply *yours*—and Tess took his hand and let him pull her up onto the stone, and the two of them lay there side by side on the wet rock, chests heaving, while the storm hammered the world above.

"We're going to have to climb," Bram rasped finally, voice shredded. "Chimney's the only way. And then we're going to have to explain to Maren why we look like drowned rats, and explain to Odile that her window closed, and—" He stopped. Winced. Laughed, a broken, disbelieving sound. "Tess. I thought we were dead. Down there, in the seam. I thought—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

"I know," Tess said.

And then, because the storm had taken everything else and left her nothing to hide behind: "I was always going to leave. You know that. Every crew, every port. I had a bag packed in my head since the day we met. I had the exit planned before I had the job." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I don't have it anymore. I checked, down there. When I thought we were done. I went looking for the exit, the way I always do, and it wasn't there. You'd—" She stopped, furious at the burning in her eyes, helpless against it. "You'd filled it in. With your stupid face and your stupid air and your—" 

Bram said nothing. He just put his hand on the back of her head, the way nobody had since she was nine years old, and let her forehead rest against his shoulder while the storm came down the chimney and the sea tried to break the world.

Tess Ibarra stopped planning her exit on a wet stone floor inside a mountain, in the middle of the worst weather of the season, and she never started again.

It was Bram who lifted his head first, because it was always Bram who heard it first—footsteps, fast and wrong, echoing down the passage from the direction of the main cave. Lamplight swung wild across the water. And then Odile Marchand's voice, cracking, ragged, calling their names into the dark with a desperation that had nothing to do with the storm:

"Bram! Tess! *You have to come now*—the fleet's in the harbor. They're a full tide early. And there's something you need to see—something I have to tell you—before they find it first."
