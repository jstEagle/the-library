## 16. Fourth Tide: The Race

The fourth tide came in gray and mean, the sea already wearing the storm's color like a borrowed coat.

Bram stood at the cave mouth with a rope in his hands and counted bodies the way a gambler counts chips—Tess at the waterline checking her regulator for the third time, Odile crouched over her charts with a pencil clamped in her teeth, Maren up on the headland where the lighthouse stood like an old soldier who hadn't been told the war was over.

Four people. One wreck. A syndicate fleet sitting in the outer roads with its running lights burning through the fog like a string of patient, hungry eyes.

"Say it back to me," Odile said without looking up. "The whole sequence."

"The Vanes' divers enter through the north passage because that's what your map tells them." Bram tested the knot, pulled it apart, tied it again. His hands were steady. He'd stopped being surprised by that somewhere around the third tide. "You've drawn them a beautiful lie—two hundred fathoms of corridor that dead-ends in a collapse from 1911. They'll burn forty minutes finding that out."

"And while they're burning minutes?"

"Tess and I go in through the throat—the low route you swore was suicide two days ago."

"It is suicide," Odile said. She looked up then, and her eyes had the flat shine of someone who has done arithmetic she doesn't like. "That's why they won't take it. The ceiling drops to eighteen inches of air for eleven meters. Tess can do it. You can barely do it. If you panic in there, you die badly, and I will have to write your death into my logbook as *operator error*, which will be the least of anyone's problems."

"You could have just said good luck."

"I don't traffic in fiction." But her mouth twitched, almost, and she went back to her charts.

Tess waded out of the shallows, dripping, her dive knife riding her hip like it had grown there. She was seventeen years old and about to swim into a flooded cave system ahead of professional salvagers, and she was humming. Bram had learned by now that the humming meant she was calm. It was the silence that scared him.

"Beacon's still lit," she said, jerking her chin toward the headland. The lighthouse swept its white arm across the water every nine seconds, painting the fleet's masts in the fog. "Maren's cutting it?"

"On my signal." Bram checked his watch against Odile's against the tide table scratched into slate. Everything agreed, which frightened him more than disagreement would have. When everything agrees, there's nowhere left to hide the mistake. "When the light dies, the fleet loses its approach line. They'll have to anchor blind or wait for dawn. Either way, their divers go in late, tired, and following a map that hates them."

"And us?"

"We go in early, tired, and following a girl who doesn't need a map." He held out his fist. Tess looked at it like he'd offered her something live and biting, then bumped it once, quick, before he could make it strange.

Odile rolled her charts, tucked them into oilskin, and said, quietly, so only he could hear: "I've lied to better crews than this one. Don't make me start practicing on yours."

"That was almost a compliment."

"Don't get used to it. Go."

* * *

The throat of the cave took him the way it always did—a hand closing around his chest, the world narrowing to lamplight and the rasp of his own breath.

Eleven meters of near-submerged crawl. The ceiling pressed down until the water above him was a lens inches thick, trembling with every exhalation, and below him the rock fell away into black that his lamp couldn't argue with. He moved by feel and by faith, faith being Tess's fins churning pale in the light ahead of him, unhurried, certain, a girl swimming through the earth's throat like she'd been born in it.

Then the passage opened like a gasp, and they were in the cathedral.

The main cavern rose above the waterline in ribbed vaults of wet stone, and below, visible through thirty feet of green clarity, the Meridian Star lay wedged in its fissure like a ship that had knelt down to pray and never gotten up. Her hull was black with four decades of the sea. Her name was still legible along the stern, gold gone to ghost, but legible: MERIDIAN STAR. Forty years of tides had polished the letters instead of erasing them, as if the ocean wanted the name remembered too.

"She's holding," Tess said, surfacing beside him, pulling her regulator free. Her voice echoed off the vaults and came back smaller. "Bulkhead's worse than yesterday. The blast opened maybe half a meter. Enough for me. Maybe not enough for you."

"Then you're going first."

"I know." She grinned, and in the lamplight she looked suddenly, brutally young. "Just liked hearing you say it."

They dove together down the green column, past the broken bowsprit, along the hull's wounded flank to the gash where the cargo hold waited behind its collapsed bulkhead—a wall of plating and stone and old, stubborn wood, split now by their explosives into a jagged seam. Tess slid through it sideways, exhaling a ribbon of silver, and disappeared into the dark of the hold.

Bram hung in the water outside and did the thing he was worst at: nothing. He waited. He watched his air gauge. He listened to the sea grind its teeth somewhere far above, where the storm was assembling itself out of pieces the weather service had promised wouldn't arrive until nightfall.

Weather services, Bram thought, were just people who told you comfortable lies with graphs.

Tess backed out through the seam hauling a canvas-wrapped bundle, and even underwater he felt the shape of it, the weight of it, the way it moved wrong for salvage and right for paper. The strongbox's contents. What they'd come four tides to steal from a grave.

They surfaced in the cathedral, and Tess pushed the bundle onto the rock shelf, and for a moment neither of them touched it.

It was just paper. That was the joke of it. Forty years of rumor and menace and debt, and underneath all of it, paper.

"Read one," Tess said. "Out loud. I want to hear if it sounds like freedom."

Bram peeled back the oilcloth. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, lay ledgers bound in rotted leather, bundles of letters tied with string gone soft as cloth, and a manifest whose ink had faded to the brown of old blood but not beyond reading. He lifted the manifest because it was thinnest, because his hands wanted the smallest possible piece of this, and read the first line aloud.

His voice cracked on the second name.

Because the second name was Cole. J. Cole, partner, with a share number and a date of death that predated any wreck by six days. Drowned, the ledger said elsewhere, in the scuttling. Murdered in it. Along with nine others, each name followed by sums—sums the elder Vane had owed them, sums he'd solved with seawater.

"That's her brother," Tess said quietly. Not a question. She'd heard Maren tell the story by lamplight same as the rest of them. "That's Maren's brother's name you're holding."

"I know."

"So we get it out. We get it to a court, and it hangs the Vanes, and—" Tess stopped. Bram watched her stop, watched the thought hit her like cold water. "Except we sink it. That's still the plan. We sink the proof."

"That's still the plan."

"Then why does it feel like stealing from her?" She nodded at the bundle—at Maren's brother, at all of them, the drowned partners with their drowned claims. "We're taking her family's names down there to feed them to the fish."

"No," Bram said. "We're taking them home. There's a difference. Ask me later how I know."

He didn't know yet. He was building the answer as he spoke it, the way you build a bridge while walking across it. But Tess looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw was enough, because she started wrapping the bundle again, careful, the way you wrap something living.

Above them, faint through the stone, came a sound that didn't belong.

Metal on rock. Rhythmic. Distant but getting less distant.

"They're in," Tess breathed.

* * *

On the headland, Maren Cole stood in the lighthouse lamp room with an axe she'd taken from her own woodshed, and Bram heard the whole story of it later in her voice, flat as a chart:

She'd kept that light for thirty-one years. She'd climbed those stairs through gales and grief and the long gray afternoons when keeping a light felt like shouting into a well. And now she stood before the lamp mechanism with salt wind screaming past the glass, and she swung.

The first blow cracked the housing. The second killed the clockwork. On the third, the great lens shuddered, sagged, and went dark mid-sweep—and the beam that had painted the fleet's masts every nine seconds for two nights simply ceased, and the fog rolled in over the water like a lid coming down on a pot.

Maren sat down on the stairs afterward, sixty-eight years old, axe across her knees, and laughed until she cried, and told no one, ever, which part was laughing and which part wasn't.

From the water, Bram saw the darkness fall across the headland and knew the fleet was blind.

* * *

In the false passage, the Vanes' lead diver—Kessler, a broad silent man with forearms like dock cable—swam into Odile Marchand's masterpiece.

She had built it over three sleepless days: a chart of the cave system accurate in every particular except one. The north passage branched, and on her forged copy the branch continued, widening temptingly, promising a direct route to the wreck's stern section. In truth the branch narrowed, dog-legged, and ended in a collapse that had sealed it sometime during the reign of a king nobody aboard the fleet could have named.

Kessler found the collapse forty minutes in, exactly as scheduled. He found something else too: the passage behind him silting out, stirred by his own fins and his team's, the water going from green to tea to milk in the space of ten minutes. Silt-out. Every cave diver's nightmare. The way back dissolved into nothing you could see through.

Two of his team panicked. Kessler got them both to the emergency line Odile had—cruelly, mercifully—left in place, and followed it up himself, and surfaced in the north chamber coughing gray water and fury, with a dead-end map in his pouch and a story forming in his mind about a cartographer named Marchand.

But that was later. For now, in the dark, three professional divers clung to a guide rope in blinding silt, and the sea closed over their mistake like water closing over a stone.

* * *

Bram heard the fleet's boats before he saw them—engines idling off the point, muffled by fog, impatient. The beacon was dead, the false passage was doing its work, and still they came, because men like the Vanes treated obstacles as insults rather than information.

"Tide turns in twenty," Tess said. They crouched on the rock shelf above the waterline, the strongbox contents between them, the entrance passage at their backs. "Twenty minutes to get this out and run the channel before the surge fills the lower galleries."

"And they'll be waiting at the mouth."

"Probably."

"Wonderful. Any ideas?"

"One." Tess's grin came back, and it was not entirely sane. "The throat. Eleven meters of eighteen-inch airspace. I fit. You barely fit. Kessler and his boys—" she jerked her thumb toward the entrance passage, where lamplight was beginning to strobe off the wet walls, growing brighter, closer— "do not fit, and they know it, and they'll assume we wouldn't try it either."

Bram looked at the low route. The black water. The ceiling like a closing fist.

"Odile called it suicide."

"Odile calls everything suicide. She also drew us a map that saved our lives, so maybe her opinions are due for review." Tess was already shouldering the wrapped bundle into its waterproof sack, cinching it tight, moving with the terrible efficiency of someone who had been planning her exits her whole life and had finally found one worth taking. "Come on, Okafor. You wanted a horizon. This one's eighteen inches tall."

Behind them, boots splashed in the entrance gallery. Voices, hard and clipped, echoing off stone. Lamplight bloomed.

Bram took one breath, held it, and followed the girl into the dark.

* * *

The throat was worse the second time. It was always worse the second time, because now you knew.

The ceiling came down and the world became a slot of water and fear, Bram's shoulders scraping rock, the bundle dragging at his harness, his lungs burning through the count—thirty seconds, forty, fifty—and ahead of him Tess's fins beat their slow, maddening, perfect rhythm, and he fixed on that rhythm like a drowning man fixes on a star, and he did not panic, and he did not stop, and somewhere around the seventy-second mark the ceiling let go of him and the passage opened and he broke surface into a pocket of cold night air, gasping, alive, the bundle intact against his chest.

Tess surfaced beside him, spat water, and said, "Seventy-one seconds. You're getting better."

Outside, through the crack of the cave's outer mouth, the fog glowed faintly where the fleet's lights groped through it, blind and furious, hunting a beacon that no longer existed. Somewhere up on the headland an old woman sat in the dark with an axe. Somewhere in the north passage three divers cursed a mapmaker's name. Somewhere behind them, in a flooded cathedral, the Meridian Star kept her dead partners' names safe a little longer.

And the tide was turning. Bram could feel it in the water rising around his waist, patient and enormous, the sea beginning the long work of sealing its cave for the season.

One tide left after this. One.

"Hold on to that," he said, nodding at the bundle. "Tomorrow we decide what the truth costs."

Tess looked at him, and for once she didn't grin.

"Bram," she said. "There are boats in the fog. Lots of them."

The first searchlight swept the water outside the cave mouth, close enough to turn the spray to falling silver.
