## 17. The Ledger Room

Forty years is a long time to keep a door closed. Maren had learned the trick of it the way you learn any trick on the water—by necessity, by repetition, until the motion became part of your hands. You closed the door every morning when you lit the lamp. You closed it again at dusk when you trimmed the wick. You closed it so many times that eventually you forgot there was anything behind it at all.

Now she was swimming toward it with a lantern in a waterproof case and her heart going like a bilge pump with a cracked gasket.

The passage beyond the breached bulkhead was narrow enough that she had to turn her shoulders sideways, the rock scraping the tank on her back, Bram's light bobbing ahead of her like a will-o'-the-wisp with a conscience. The explosion from the third tide had done its work imperfectly—half the bulkhead blown inward, half hanging in twisted plates that Tess had spent two hours cutting away with a torch while the tide crept up their ankles in the air pocket behind them. Now they had perhaps ninety minutes before the fourth tide turned and the lower passages began to drink.

Maren had not told them she'd been here before.

Not exactly here. Not through this breach, not into this hold. But forty years ago she had stood on the deck of the Meridian Star and watched Captain Ellery Vane order his first mate to seal this very compartment, and she had asked why, and he had smiled at her the way his sons smiled now—warmly, generously, like a man handing you a gift—and said, *Some cargo keeps better in the dark, Miss Cole.*

She was nineteen years old. She had believed him.

Bram's hand came back through the murk and found her arm, squeezed once. A question: *Are you all right?* She answered by kicking forward past him, taking the lead, because if she stopped moving she would have to feel what she was feeling, and feeling could wait. Feeling had waited forty years. It could wait another hundred meters.

The passage opened without warning.

One moment the walls were pressing in; the next they fell away and her light spilled out across a cathedral of drowned wood and shadow. The main hold of the Meridian Star lay before them intact—not collapsed, not scattered, but *kept*, wedged as it was in the fissure like a letter slipped under a door by the sea itself. Cargo netting hung in ghostly curtains. A ship's bell swung gently on its bracket, stirred by some slow respiration of current, though no sound reached them down here. The silence was total. It was the silence of a room where something has been waiting a very long time to be said.

Maren's breath caught in her regulator. She made herself breathe. In. Out. The way she'd taught a hundred young divers on this coast, back when teaching was still allowed to her.

*There.* Bram's light found it against the forward bulk: an iron strongbox bolted to the deck frame, the kind captains used for papers too valuable for the safe ashore. Sea growth had claimed it in green and white, barnacles crusting the lock like scar tissue over an old wound. But the box itself—the box was whole.

They crossed the hold together, finning slow, their lights sweeping over crates whose stenciled markings had long since surrendered to the dark. Maren read one anyway, out of habit, out of grief: *VANE & PARTNERS — GRAYHOLLOW — PERISHABLES.* Perishables. That was what the manifest had called them. That was what the elder Vane had called four families' worth of livelihoods, of futures, of names.

Her brother's name among them.

She knelt at the strongbox. Her gloves were old diving gloves, patched twice, and her fingers felt clumsy as a child's inside them. The padlock was fused with corrosion; it would not open by persuasion. Bram produced the pry bar they'd brought for exactly this, worked it into the hasp, and looked at her—a question in the tilt of his head, in the way he always checked before he broke things, which was more than she could say for anyone else she'd ever known.

*Do it,* she signed, because speaking underwater was a waste of breath they might need later.

The iron gave with a groan she felt in her teeth rather than heard. The lid came up trailing a ghost of silt, and her light went into the box, and forty years ended.

Ledgers. Three of them, bound in oilcloth that had done its work faithfully, the leather beneath dark but sound. A bundle of letters tied with string gone soft as cloth. And on top of everything, folded once, a single sheet heavier than the rest—thick paper, formal stock, the kind used for documents meant to survive argument.

Maren lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child.

It was the manifest. The true one. Not the perishables list posted on the harbor office wall, but the real accounting, written in Captain Ellery Vane's own careful hand—the hand that had signed her wages, the hand that had patted her shoulder the day she signed aboard. She read the column of names and her vision blurred, not from the water, not from the salt.

*Aldous Cole.* Her brother. Twenty-six years old, a partner's son, carrying the family's claim papers to the mainland to secure their share of the shipping concern. He had boarded the Meridian Star on a Tuesday in October with a leather satchel full of signatures and promises, and the ship had sailed, and the sea had been calm as glass, and somewhere past the Grayhollow shoals the captain had opened the seacocks in the night and the Meridian Star had gone down with eleven souls aboard and every scrap of paper that proved the Vanes owed anyone anything.

Eleven souls. The official report said twelve. The extra name had been added later, in different ink, to account for a body that washed ashore wearing Aldous's coat—a body that was not Aldous, that Maren had identified and lied about, because the man who paid for the funeral asked her to and she was nineteen and alone and he owned the town she lived in.

She had kept that lie the way you keep a stone in your mouth. Forty years. You got so you didn't taste it anymore.

Bram's glove closed over her shoulder. She hadn't heard him move. His faceplate was fogged at the edges, his eyes steady on hers, and he did not sign *are you all right* this time, because he knew the answer, and he was decent enough not to make her say it.

Instead he pointed at the ledgers. Then at the letters. Then he made the shape they'd agreed on: *take everything.*

But Maren stayed kneeling a moment longer. She pulled off her right glove—it cost her, the cold biting in immediately, the water at this depth near winter temperatures even in summer—and she pressed her bare finger to the page, to the inked letters of her brother's name.

Aldous Cole.

The ink did not run. Forty years underwater and the iron-gall ink held, stubborn as the man himself had been, stubborn as their father raising him to be. She traced each letter slowly, the way you'd trace a face in the dark, learning it again. He'd had a laugh that started in his belly and embarrassed everyone around him. He'd taught her to swim in the harbor shallows by promising her a copper penny for every length. He'd been dead for forty years and she had never once said his name aloud in company, because saying it invited questions, and questions invited the Vanes, and the Vanes invited ruin.

Underwater, in the dark, in the room where he died, she finally let herself weep. No one saw. The sea took her tears the moment they left her, which was, she thought dimly, the only honest thing it had ever done with anything the Vanes put in it.

* * *

They surfaced in the cave's air pocket to gray light and the sound of weather arriving early. Odile stood at the water's edge with her charts pinned under stones, hair whipping, shouting calculations over the wind. Tess crouched at the tunnel mouth with a line gauge in her teeth, watching the water level like a hawk watching a henhouse.

"You've got maybe fifty minutes," Odile called as they hauled out. "Maybe forty. The pressure gradient's wrong—I miscalculated the surge interval, the second cell is moving faster than—" She stopped. She was looking at Maren's face. "What happened down there?"

Maren climbed onto the rock shelf, sat, and began working off her tanks with hands that shook only a little now. "Open the box," she said.

Tess spread the oilcloth bundles on the flattest stone, weighting the corners, and Odile's lamp threw its circle over the ledgers, the letters, the manifest. For a long moment nobody spoke. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"God above," Odile whispered. She was a cartographer; she understood documents the way priests understand scripture. Her fingers hovered over the columns without touching. "These are the partnership accounts. Full entries. Distributions, shares, the lot of it." She flipped pages with a conservator's delicacy. "And here—here's the insurance filing. The Meridian Star was insured for triple her cargo value, filed six weeks before she sailed. Six weeks. He planned it. He planned it and then he wrote it all down."

"Men like that always write it down," Maren said. "They can't help it. They think paper is power."

"It is power," Bram said quietly. He was crouched at the edge of the lamplight, and Maren watched him look at the ledgers the way a starving man looks at bread—with want, yes, but also with fear. She knew about his debts. She knew what the Vane brothers had promised to do to him. This box was his freedom sitting on a wet rock, and he hadn't touched it yet. "This could hang them. All three of them. The courts, the insurers' descendants, the other families—there are other families, aren't there? On this list?"

"Four partners," Maren said. "Four families. Mine was the smallest." She took the manifest from Tess and held it flat on her knees. "Aldous Cole. Josiah Merrick. The Pennhalow brothers, Thomas and Ewan. And a factor named Greaves who wasn't family but had signed surety for the others. Five names. Eleven people went into the water with those five names, counting wives and children who sailed along for the crossing." She looked up. "The Vanes have spent forty years being generous to Grayhollow. New boats. Harbor repairs. Winter coal. You know why? Guilt doesn't look like guilt when it's dressed as charity. It looks like a man buying the whole town's silence one favor at a time."

"And nobody ever asked?" Tess said. She was seventeen and had the particular sharpness of someone who'd learned early that gifts have hooks in them.

"People asked," Maren said. "People who asked stopped asking. Or stopped being able to afford to stay in Grayhollow. My father asked loudest of all. He was dead within the year—bad heart, the doctor said. The doctor drove a boat the Vanes had given him." She smoothed the manifest flat. "I stayed quiet because staying quiet was the only way to keep the lighthouse, and the lighthouse was the only thing left that was his. You don't give up the last thing your father built to make a point. You just don't."

"But now," Odile said softly.

"Now I'm seventy-one years old," Maren said, "and my brother has been lying at the bottom of the sea since before any of you were born, and the men who put him there are coming for this cave with a salvage fleet, and I find I am done keeping doors closed."

The wind chose that moment to drive rain hard against the rocks outside, as if the sky had been waiting for permission.

Bram picked up the top ledger, turned it over in his hands. "We get these out tonight. Dry them, protect them. If the fleet arrives tomorrow—"

"When," Odile corrected. "The dockhand sold my letter. They'll arrive tomorrow or the day after, and they'll come straight for the fissure."

"Then we move now." Bram looked around the circle of lamplight—at Odile, at Tess, and last at Maren, and there was something in his face she recognized because she'd seen it in mirrors for forty years: a person deciding how much truth they could carry. "Maren. Once these leave the water, there's no putting them back. The Vanes will know. There'll be no more hiding in the lighthouse, no more quiet. Are you certain?"

Maren thought about the question properly, because it deserved that. She thought about the lamp she'd lit ten thousand nights. She thought about the coat on the drowned stranger, and the funeral she'd attended for a man who wasn't her brother while her actual brother slept unburied in the dark. She thought about the word *perishables.*

"I buried the wrong body," she said. "I think it's time I dug up the right one."

Nobody laughed. But something passed around the circle anyway—Tess's chin lifting, Odile's shoulders squaring, Bram exhaling like a man setting down weight—and Maren understood that this was what a crew was. Not blood. Not contract. Just people agreeing, all at once, to carry the same heavy thing.

They sealed the documents into the waterproof drums, wrapped the manifest separately against her chest beneath her suit, and prepared to dive the return passage while the tide still permitted it.

It was Tess, hauling the last drum to the water's edge, who went still. She cocked her head the way she did when listening to the sea, and Maren had learned to trust that habit absolutely.

"What is it?" Bram asked.

"Engine noise," Tess said. "More than one. Coming up the coast road." She turned, and in the lamplight her young face was grim as any old sailor's. "That's not tomorrow. That's tonight. They're already here."

Above them, through the cave mouth, the first headlights swept across the headland—and below, far below, the Meridian Star settled deeper into her fissure, holding her dead and her secrets close, waiting to see what the living would do with both.
