## 18. Fifth Tide: The Cave Fights Back

The sea turned against us at half past three in the morning.

I knew it before I heard it—felt it in the stone under my boots, a low shudder like something enormous rolling over in its sleep. The cave had been groaning all night, small complaints of rock and water, but this was different. This was the sound of a door beginning to close.

"Surge," Odile said from the mouth of the passage, her lamp held high. She'd been watching the water line on the lower shelf for an hour, marking it with chalk every ten minutes, and the marks told the story plainly enough: each wave now reached higher than the last, and the retreat between waves grew shorter. "It's coming up through the fissure system. The whole coast is breathing in."

"Tess?" I called down the rope.

Her voice came back thin and echoey from below, from the flooded gallery where we'd rigged the last of the salvage lines. "Still here. Water's at my chest on the low shelf. Give me twenty more minutes for this coil and I'm up."

"You have ten."

"Bram—"

"Ten," I said, and she didn't argue, which frightened me more than the water did. Tess Ibarra argued with gravity when it suited her. If she was taking orders, it was because she'd seen what I'd seen on her last ascent: the way the surge had shoved her sideways into the wall, hard enough to crack the faceplate of her spare mask.

Above us, through the cave's throat, the storm made its opinions known. We could hear it even down here—a vast rubbing pressure, wind dragging the sea by the scruff of its neck and hurling it at Grayhollow's cliffs. Maren was topside at the light, or supposed to be. Odile had charted our window between two storm cells, and the second cell had arrived four hours early, the way weather does when you've bet your life on its schedule.

* * *

We lost the lower gallery at dawn.

Not gradually—the sea doesn't do gradual. One moment Tess was climbing the rope with the last coil of signal line over her shoulder; the next, a brown-green fist of water came up the passage behind her and took the gallery whole, swallowing the shelves, the rigging, the chalk marks, everything we'd built over five tides of work. She came up the rope hand over hand with the surge chasing her heels, and I hauled her over the lip of the upper ledge as the water slapped the stone where she'd been standing.

She lay there gasping, grinning at the ceiling. "Close one."

"Too close."

"That's what makes it fun." But her hands were shaking as she coiled the line, and she didn't meet my eyes, and I understood that too. Fear you can joke about is fear you're still controlling. Hers had gotten loose.

Odile crouched at the edge of the ledge, lamp angled down at the churning black where our dive route used to be. "The false route is holding," she said quietly. "The Vanes' divers went in through the south channel two hours ago. If they're following my map—and their scout plane photographed my buoy placement, so they are—they'll be in the serpentine passage right about now."

"The one that floods first."

"The one that floods first," she agreed. Her voice was level, but I saw her thumb working at the callus on her forefinger, the way it did when she was mapping something in her head she didn't want to look at directly. "In this surge, that passage becomes a washing machine with one exit. And they don't know the exit."

I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. Four syndicate divers, maybe five, in gear worth more than everything I owned, following a chart drawn by a woman who'd built it to lie. Somewhere in that drowning dark was their lead diver—the big quiet man named Corvus who'd stood behind the Vane brothers in every meeting, who'd once caught my elbow at the dock to keep me from slipping on ice, an absent courtesy he probably didn't remember giving.

"They knew the risks," Tess said. She'd read my face; she always did. "They came to take what's ours. Let the cave keep them."

"The cave doesn't keep people, Tess. It kills them and keeps their gear."

"Same thing."

"It isn't." I looked at Odile. "How long until the serpentine goes completely under?"

She checked her watch, then the water line, then some interior table only she could see. "If the surge holds this rate? Forty minutes. Maybe less."

* * *

Maren came down from the light as the sky went the color of wet slate.

She moved slowly these days, her knees paying the toll of forty years on iron stairs, but she came down anyway, lamp in one hand, the other trailing the guide rope we'd strung along the upper passage. She listened to our report without expression, and then she said the thing none of us wanted said aloud.

"There'll be screaming."

Tess frowned. "What?"

"Water in a constricted passage." Maren's voice was flat as a chart legend. "I heard it once before, on the Meridian, the night she went down. Men in air pockets, men in the wrong corridor. It carries through rock like a bell. You'll hear it up here, and you'll know exactly what it is, and you'll have to decide what kind of person you are while you listen." She set her lamp down and looked at me. "So decide now, Bram Okafor, while it's still theoretical."

It stopped being theoretical eleven minutes later.

The sound came up through the fissures first—a thin, warped wail, more animal than human, bouncing off stone until it seemed to come from everywhere. Then, fainter, a second voice answering it. Then the unmistakable metallic clang of a tank striking rock, over and over, someone signaling distress the only way left to them.

Tess was already moving toward the water's edge. I caught her arm.

"No."

"They're dying down there."

"They came to rob us."

"They came because two rich bastards ordered them to, same as you'd have done at nineteen if the money was right." She pulled free of my grip—not violently, just decisively, the way she did everything—and stood at the lip of the flood, judging it. "Someone's got maybe fifteen minutes of air left, and the serpentine's only passable from the far end now. From below. Through the fissure off the wreck gallery."

"That fissure hasn't been surveyed since the blast."

"I know. I mapped it by feel on Tuesday." She was stripping her gear checks as she talked, automatic, hands moving through the ritual: valves, gauges, knife, light. "Bram. Look at me. There is no version of tomorrow morning where I sit up here and listen to that noise and stay the person you want on your crew. Either I go, or I go, or I go. Pick one."

Odile was unfolding her false chart, checking it against the real one in her head. "The far entrance to the serpentine sits at nine meters," she said. "Below the surge layer, mostly. If she enters deep and stays deep, the worst of it passes over her." A pause. "Mostly."

"And the trapped diver?" I asked.

"He'll be in the upper third, in whatever air pocket the geometry allows. Which means she has to go up to get him. Into the washing machine." Odile folded the chart. "I can talk her in by line signals. After that, it's her."

Maren put her hand on my shoulder. Old, strong, heavier than it looked. "This is the decision," she said softly. "Not the ledgers. Not the silver. This one."

* * *

I gave the order I'd sworn I wouldn't give.

"Tess. You go in, you bring him out, and you do not go past the second air pocket looking for anyone else. If the cave offers you a choice between him and yourself, you choose yourself. That's not a request. That's the price of going."

She looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—not gratitude, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone being trusted with a rule instead of protected from consequences.

"Deal," she said, and rolled backward off the ledge into the black.

* * *

What followed was the longest hour of my life, and I include the hour I spent in a Vane warehouse listening to Silas Vane explain, pleasantly, what would happen to my hands.

We ran the line signals from the ledge. Odile called them—two pulls, safe; three pulls, coming back; continuous, emergency—and translated each tug on the rope into plain speech for the rest of us, her voice steady as a metronome while the storm tried to tear the roof off the world above.

"She's through the fissure. Current's bad but manageable." Pause. "Entering the serpentine from below. Visibility near zero—she's going by touch and line."

The screaming from inside the rock had stopped. Somehow that was worse.

Then the line went slack, then taut, then jerked twice—no, three times, hard, and Odile's breath went out of her. "Three pulls. She's got him. She's—" Another series of pulls, frantic, wrong. Odile's face changed. "No. No, no. Continuous. Emergency. Something's pinned."

I was in the water before I finished deciding to be.

The cold hit like a confession. Below the surge layer the water was almost calm, which felt obscene given what was happening thirty feet above my head. I followed the guide line down through the fissure, lamp beam swinging across walls furred with kelp and pocked with old blast scars—our blast, our mess, the bulkhead we'd blown to reach the hold now contributing its rubble to the cave's architecture of traps.

I found them at the second air pocket.

The pocket was a dome of trapped air the size of a chapel, and it was shrinking. The surge drove water up into it in pulses, and each pulse ate another foot of headroom. In the last dry corner, wedged against the rock, was Corvus—Vanes' lead diver, a slab of a man whose regulator hose had fouled in the debris and whose mask had cracked along the temple. Beside him, half-submerged, Tess was hauling at a slab of rock the size of a door, pinning his leg and his line both.

His eyes found my lamp and went wide. He tried to speak, coughed salt water, tried again.

"Don't," I told him, and started digging.

The rock wasn't going to move by strength. I'd learned that much from the bulkhead. So we worked it the way Odile had taught us to work everything in this cave—by geometry, by leverage, by finding the one point where the mountain's own weight could be turned against itself. Tess braced the slab. I drove a salvaged pry bar into the fracture line and leaned my whole life onto it. The rock groaned, shifted a hand's width, and Corvus dragged his leg out from under it with a sound I will hear in my sleep for years.

His tank showed empty. Mine showed a third. Tess's showed less.

One regulator. Three mouths. The math was simple and terrible, and I watched Tess do it faster than I did.

She put the regulator in Corvus's mouth.

He stared at her—at this half-grown dock rat who should have hated him, whose freedom he'd been sent to end—and for a moment he didn't take it. He pushed it back toward her. She pushed it back at him, hard, and pointed upward, and mouthed one word with absolute clarity: *up.*

So we went up. Sharing air in rotation, three of us passing one mouthful of the world between us, through a passage that bucked and slammed as the surge found new angles of attack. Twice the cave tried to separate us. Twice I grabbed whatever was nearest—Corvus's harness, Tess's ankle, bare rock—and refused, with a stubbornness that had nothing to do with courage and everything to do with simply declining to let go.

The last twenty feet were the worst, because the surface was no longer a surface. The upper passage had become a churn, white water hammering in from the sea mouth, and we came up into it like surfacing inside a beating. I got an arm around a formation, got Tess, got Corvus, and then hands—Odile's, Maren's—were pulling us out onto the ledge, and we lay there on the stone like things the sea had decided, for now, not to keep.

* * *

Corvus vomited seawater for five minutes. Then he sat up, dripping, and looked at the four of us arranged around him—me gasping, Tess gray-faced and shaking, Odile wringing out her sleeves, Maren draping a blanket over his shoulders with the brisk tenderness of a woman who'd done it for drowned sailors before.

"Why," he rasped. His voice was gravel and rust. "You could have let me drown. You should have. Your brothers-in-law would have."

"They're not my anything," I said.

"You sank a charge in that passage. Your mapmaker drew a chart designed to kill us." He looked at Odile, who didn't flinch. "And you pulled me out anyway. I need to understand why, because the Vanes won't believe it, and when they don't believe it, they'll assume it's a trick, and they'll act accordingly. You may have just made everything worse by being decent."

"Welcome to Grayhollow," Tess said hoarsely. "Everything here works like that."

But I understood what he was really asking, because I was asking it of myself. Why? Not sentiment—I'd had time to examine the impulse on the long swim up, and it wasn't kindness, not clean kindness. It was something rawer. The knowledge that if I let a man drown in the dark while I stood in the light, I'd become a smaller shape of myself, and no ledger, no silver, no erased debt would ever widen that shape again. The Vanes had spent their whole lives making themselves smaller and calling it power. I'd seen where that road ended: in a cave mouth at the sixth tide, choosing between your soul and your skin.

I hadn't chosen yet. But I'd just learned which choice I was capable of.

"Get him warm," I said. "Get him fed. And when the fleet comes asking, he tells them whatever keeps everyone alive. That's the deal."

Corvus laughed—a broken, disbelieving sound. "You're trusting me?"

"I'm betting on you. There's a difference. Trust you have to earn."

* * *

The cave sealed its first door an hour later.

We heard it rather than saw it—a deep, grinding concussion somewhere below, rock settling into rock, the seasonal seal beginning its slow work. Odile marked it on her chart with a red X and a steady hand, though I saw the tremor arrive after the pen lifted.

"That was the south channel," she said. "Our original entry. Gone until spring."

"Which leaves?"

"One route. The deep fissure past the wreck gallery. Narrow, unlit, and it floods at anything above a moderate surge—which, in case you hadn't noticed, is what we have." She looked around at us: the diver, the keeper, the runaway, the liar, the debtor. "Two tides left. The hold is breached. The strongbox is forty meters into a passage that wants us dead. And the Vanes' fleet is anchored offshore with one fewer diver than they started with, wondering why he's smiling."

I looked at Corvus. He was wrapped in Maren's blanket, drinking soup Maren had conjured from somewhere, and he was, in fact, smiling faintly—the smile of a man who has seen the bottom of the sea and found it less frightening than the surface he'd come from.

"The strongbox," he said quietly. "You haven't opened it."

"No."

"Do you know what's in it?"

"Yes," Maren said, from the shadows by the lamp. Her voice was very calm, the calm of forty years' standing. "Do you?"

Corvus was silent a long moment. The storm hammered overhead. Somewhere below, the sea tested its new door and found it good.

"I've carried the Vanes' gear for eleven years," he said finally. "I've never once asked what it was for. Tonight I nearly died in a passage your friend built to lie, and a girl who owes me nothing gave me the air in her lungs." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and clear. "Ask me again tomorrow. I might answer differently than I would have yesterday."

Odile folded her chart. "Tomorrow," she said, "we find out if the cave leaves us anywhere to stand."

Outside, through the cave's throat, the storm screamed toward the coast like something with a name, and the sixth tide began gathering itself beyond the horizon.
