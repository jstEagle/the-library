## 1. The Mood of the Water

The Sarelle woke before dawn the way it always did, with a long exhale of mist off its brown shoulders, and Marguerite Ashgrove was awake to hear it.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with her hands resting light on the spokes — not gripping, never gripping; a wheel gripped too hard told you nothing — and watched the fog peel back from the water like a curtain drawn by a careful hand. Upstream of her, the river bent east toward the terraced hills where the tea barges waited. Downstream lay Lowport and its debts, and she had no intention of looking that direction before breakfast.

Nine years on this water. Nine years of loading and unloading, of haggling with dockmasters and out-stubborning sandbars, of sleeping four hours and steering six. The *Kestrel* was forty feet of riveted iron and old teak, a stern-wheeler built for a river twice as gentle as this one, and she was — Marguerite allowed herself the word only in the privacy of her own skull, where no one could tax it — beautiful. Her paddle wheel turned with a sound like slow applause. Her boiler, patched three times by an engineer who swore each patch would be his last, grumbled below decks like an old man asked to climb stairs.

"Captain." Odile's voice came up the ladder behind her, rough as rope. "Pirro says the cargo shifted on the last bend."

"Pirro says a lot of things."

"He says it shifted because you took the bend at nine knots."

Marguerite didn't turn around. "The bend is safe at nine knots."

"The bend is safe at seven knots. You take it at nine because you're bored."

That was the trouble with Odile Marchand, who had been her first mate for eight of those nine years: she was usually right, and she never had the decency to be wrong about anything important. Marguerite could feel her standing there in the doorway of the pilothouse, arms crossed, gray-streaked hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of signal flag, waiting. She had a face like a hatchet that had been used kindly for many years.

"I'm not bored," Marguerite said.

"You're humming."

She hadn't realized she'd been humming. It was an old tune from Vesseny, a harvest song her mother used to sing while shelling beans, and she shut her mouth around it so fast she nearly bit her tongue. The mist thinned. Somewhere ahead, a kingfisher stitched blue across the current.

"Check the lashings," she said. "If the crates have walked, walk them back."

"Aye, Captain." Odile paused at the top of the ladder. "And eat something. You've been up since the third bell and you've had coffee and spite."

"That's a balanced meal."

Odile went down the ladder shaking her head, which was how she said *yes, Captain* without saying it.

* * *

The *Kestrel* carried two hundred crates of dried tea leaf, forty bolts of undyed cloth, and one live goat named Duchess who belonged to Pirro and whom nobody else wanted to belong to anybody. They were running her upriver to Ferren's Landing, a day and a half at good speed, where a buyer with soft hands and a ledger full of promises would meet them at the dock and try to shave ten percent off the agreed price. Marguerite had done this run eleven times. She could do it blindfolded, and some mornings, in the fog, she nearly did.

Her crew numbered five besides herself. Odile, first mate, ex-smuggler, keeper of a set of debts she believed were secret and were not. Brannic, the engineer, a mountain of a man from the high country who spoke perhaps forty words a day and gave half of them to the boiler. Pirro, sixteen, deckhand, all elbows and enthusiasm, who had stowed away aboard two years ago fleeing something in Lowport that he'd never explained and Marguerite had never asked about. The twins, Sefa and Rell, rigger and cook respectively, who argued constantly about everything except the rigging and the cooking, at which they were both quietly brilliant.

And that was the whole of it. Six souls and a goat, riding a hundred tons of iron up a river that had drowned better boats than theirs.

"Captain!" Pirro's voice cracked on the second syllable, the way it did when he was excited, which was most of the time. He came pelting along the port rail, pointing upstream. "There's a barge adrift! Just past the willow point — she's swinging!"

Marguerite lifted her glass. Sure enough: a flat-bottomed grain barge, unmanned or near enough, wallowing broadside in the current with her mooring line trailing behind her like a severed tendon. And past her, half-hidden by the willows —

"There's a snag under her," Marguerite said. "See how she won't come around? She's hung up on something and the current's using her as a pivot. Every boat that comes around that point at speed is going to hit her, or hit whatever she's stuck on."

"Should we warn them?" Pirro meant the little packet steamer visible downstream, chugging along fat and happy, its pilot presumably not awake enough to read the water.

"We're going to do better than warn them." Marguerite rang the engine telegraph down to HALF AHEAD, then thought about it and rang FULL. "Brannic! I need everything she has for ninety seconds!"

The *Kestrel* surged. Marguerite felt the wheel come alive in her hands, that faint trembling conversation between wood and water that she had learned to listen to the way other people listened to speech. The river talked. Most pilots knew this and ignored it, the way most people ignore weather. But the Sarelle spoke in currents and pressure and the particular drag of water over submerged things, and if you held the wheel loose and let your palms listen, it would tell you where it was deep and where it lied.

The willow point came up fast. The adrift barge swung lazily across two-thirds of the channel, and beyond it Marguerite could see the pale seam in the water that marked the snag — a drowned tree, probably, torn loose in the spring floods and planted nose-down in the shallows, invisible and patient as a pickpocket.

"Sefa!" she called. "Stand by the starboard fenders!"

"Aye!"

She took the *Kestrel* into the gap.

It was, objectively, a stupid thing to do. There was perhaps thirty feet of open water between the swinging barge and the snag, and the current through that gap ran quick and confused, curling off the obstruction in eddies that could spin a boat like a coin. Any sensible pilot would back off, sound the whistle, wait for the packet steamer to sort itself out.

But the packet steamer wasn't sorting itself out. Its pilot had seen the barge late and was already putting his helm over in exactly the wrong direction, turning toward the gap instead of away from it, and in ninety seconds there were going to be two vessels and one channel and a great deal of shouting.

So Marguerite went through first, to show him it could be done, and to clear the road behind her.

The gap opened. She felt the current grab the *Kestrel*'s stern and try to swing it — there, that pull, that's the snag talking — and she answered it with a quarter-turn of wheel and a burst of the wheel that threw spray across the bow. The swinging barge loomed off the port side close enough that Pirro could have jumped aboard, close enough that Marguerite could see the grain sacks shifting under its tarpaulin like sleeping animals. Then the snag's dead hand reached for her keel and she slid over it with six inches to spare, feeling the *Kestrel* shudder once, a low note through the deck plates, and then they were through, into clean water, the willows falling astern.

Behind them, the packet steamer's pilot, having witnessed the impossible, corrected course and came around the outside with room to spare. His whistle blew twice — thanks, or astonishment, or both.

Pirro was hanging over the rail, watching the barge recede. "How did you know the gap was there? You couldn't see the bottom."

"You can see the bottom," Marguerite said. "You just have to look with your hands."

He stared at her as though she'd said she could see the future with her elbows. Which, she supposed, from the outside, was roughly equivalent.

* * *

They made Ferren's Landing by midafternoon of the second day, and the buyer with soft hands tried to shave twelve percent off the price, and Marguerite got him back up to nine and a half and considered it a fair morning's work, even though it was evening. Odile handled the tally sheets. Pirro and the twins hauled crates. Brannic stayed with the boat, as he always did, and when Marguerite came back down the gangplank after dark she found him sitting on the boiler deck smoking his pipe, listening to the river.

"Anything?" she asked.

"Water's up two inches since noon." He tapped ash over the side. "Rain in the hills somewhere."

Two inches meant nothing yet. Two inches meant the Sarelle was thinking about something. Marguerite filed it away in the part of her mind where she kept such things, alongside the memory of every sandbar's shape and every dockmaster's temper.

Duchess the goat had eaten a corner of a tarpaulin. This was dealt with.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep — which was normal; sleep came to her in fragments, like coins counted out by a reluctant hand — Marguerite climbed to the pilothouse and stood her own private watch. The river ran silver-black under a half moon. Moths battered the lamp glass. From the crew quarters below came the sounds of people genuinely asleep: Rell's snore, steady as an engine; Pirro talking to someone in a dream, low and urgent, words she couldn't make out and didn't try to.

This was the hour when the restlessness found her. In daylight there was always work, and work was a kind of armor. But at night the armor came off, and underneath it Marguerite Ashgrove was just a woman standing in a box of glass and wood, holding a wheel, going somewhere, always going somewhere, and never once in nine years asking herself whether any of the somewheres had been worth going to.

Never stop moving. That was the rule. She'd made it the week she'd taken command of the *Kestrel*, with her sister's name still fresh in her mouth like ash, and she'd kept it ever since. A boat at anchor gathered rot, barnacles, debt collectors, memories. A boat underway outran all of them. It didn't matter where to. That was the secret nobody understood about her: everyone assumed she was running toward something — a fortune, a harbor, a reckoning. She wasn't running toward anything. She was running, full stop. Direction was a courtesy she paid the river.

Below her, the water murmured against the hull. Two inches up, Brannic had said. Now, leaning out over the rail, she judged it closer to four. The current had changed its voice too — a deeper note under the surface chatter, a new weight in the way it pressed against the planking. Rain in the hills. More than rain. Somewhere upcountry the Sarelle was gathering an argument, and in a day or two it intended to make it.

She should wake Odile. She should double the lines, move the boat to the lee side of the landing, take precautions.

Instead she stood at the wheel a while longer, hands loose on the spokes, listening to the river talk about the storm the way it talked about everything — patiently, in a language of pressure and pull, saying what it always said:

*I am coming. Keep moving.*

"All right," Marguerite said softly, to the water, to the dark, to whatever was left of the family whose names she never said aloud. "I heard you."

She was reaching for the telegraph to ring the engine awake when she saw it — downstream, far off, where the moonlight lay on the water like spilled milk. A swell. Not wind-driven; the air was dead calm. A single long rise in the river's surface, traveling against the current, smooth and deliberate as the back of something enormous passing beneath.

It rolled under the *Kestrel*, lifted her gently, and set her down again, rocking.

Brannic's door opened below. He stepped out onto the deck, pipe unlit, and looked at the water, and then up at her in the pilothouse, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing yet to say. But the river had spoken, and they had both heard it.

Whatever the flood was bringing down from the hills, it would reach them by morning.
