## 2. What the Flood Brings Up

The storm came down out of the western hills an hour past dawn, and by noon the Sarelle had forgotten it was a river at all.

Marguerite Ashgrove stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with her boots braced wide and her hands light on the spokes, feeling the water change its mind beneath them. That was the thing about the Sarelle in flood season — it didn't rise so much as it *remembered*, hauling silt and drowned timber and the bones of old landings up out of its bed and flinging them downstream like a man emptying his pockets after a long night. A river in spate was a river with opinions. Her job was to have better ones.

"Log! Starboard bow!" Pirro's voice cracked on the last syllable, the way it always did when he shouted, sixteen years old and still growing into his own lungs.

"I see it." She did — a black spine of driftwood the length of a skiff, rolling end over end where the current braided around a submerged point. She eased the wheel a hand's width to port, no more, letting the flood do most of the work. Overcorrect in water like this and you'd find yourself broadside to something with teeth.

The log went by close enough that she could have spat on it. Behind her she heard Odile exhale through her nose — the first mate's entire vocabulary of approval, compressed into one dismissive sound.

"You could say thank you," Marguerite said without turning.

"I could say you cut it finer than you needed to."

"I could say we'd still be waiting at the fueling dock if I steered the way you'd like."

"And the world would be a calmer place." Odile came forward along the deck rail, moving with the boat rather than against it, the way she'd learned to in twenty years of working rivers that had nothing to do with Vesseny or anyone else's wars. She was a broad woman gone grey early, hair bound back under a kerchief the color of rust, and she had a way of standing beside Marguerite at the wheel that managed to be both companionship and audit. "Cargo's lashed. Pirro's bailing the bilge again because he won't admit the aft seam weeps."

"It doesn't weep," Pirro called from somewhere amidships. "It *breathes*."

"It breathes," Odile agreed dryly, "and one day it'll breathe us all to the bottom."

Marguerite let their voices wash over her and watched the water. This was the part of the work nobody paid for and everything depended on: reading the surface the way other people read letters. The Sarelle told you what it was doing if you knew its grammar — the smooth glassy tongue where the current ran deep and fast, the boiling hump of water over a snag, the long V of a wake that meant something alive and large was swimming against the flood. Nine years she'd been learning this dialect. Some mornings she thought she knew it better than any language people spoke.

Some mornings that felt like an accomplishment. Most mornings it felt like what it was: a woman who had made a home out of motion because staying still meant thinking.

The storm passed east of them by mid-afternoon, trailing ragged skirts of rain across the far bank, and the river kept rising anyway. Flood crests traveled; the water here wouldn't peak until well after dark, fed by all the country upstream that had caught the worst of it. They tied up at a willow-choked bend above the ruins of Marrow Landing — a name left over from some older economy, timber or tar or trouble, Marguerite had never bothered to learn which — and let the *Kestrel* ride out the crest on double lines, nose into the current.

It was there, in the last grey hour before full dark, that the river gave them the chest.

* * *

Pirro saw it first, because Pirro saw everything first and then spent ten minutes explaining how he'd seen it.

"That's a crate," he said, leaning over the rail as they stood on the bank checking the mooring lines. "Out there. In the eddy behind that dead willow. It's been going around and around for — I've been watching it since the rain stopped, it just keeps circling—"

"Everything circles in an eddy," Odile said. "That's what makes it an eddy."

"But it's a *crate*, Odile."

Marguerite looked. He was right. Something box-shaped and pale sat in the slow whirl behind the willow, riding high, turning with the patience of something that had nowhere else in the world to be. Floods brought down all manner of flotsam — fence rails, roof shingles, once, memorably, an entire privy with the seat still attached — but boxes that rode level and didn't ship water were another order of thing. Boxes like that were built to float. Built to be found, maybe. Or built by someone who'd hoped they never would be.

"Grapple," she said.

Odile looked at her. "We're a cargo boat, not a salvage crew."

"We're whatever pays tonight. Get the grapple."

They worked it from the bow with the boat's lanterns turned low, Pirro throwing and Marguerite hauling while Odile kept the lines taut against the flood's pull. The hook took on the third cast, and the weight that came off the bottom was heavier than the box looked — waterlogged, dense, dragging like something reluctant. It took all three of them to swing it over the rail, and when it landed on the deck it landed with a sound too solid for wood alone.

Up close, in the lantern light, it stopped being a crate and became a chest. Iron-banded, corner-strapped, the wood swollen nearly black with nine days or nine years of soaking. And stamped into a brass plate set into the lid, tarnished green but unmistakable, was a seal: a tower between two wheat sheaves, crowned.

Nobody said anything for a moment. The river talked instead, sliding past the hull with its endless low commentary.

"That's—" Pirro started.

"I know what it is," Marguerite said quietly.

She knew it the way you know a face from a country you left. The royal seal of Vesseny. Stamped on grain shipments and garrison orders and, once, on a document her father had burned in the kitchen stove while telling her children shouldn't ask questions. She had been eleven years old. The nation had lasted four more years after that, and then it hadn't lasted at all, and the seal had become the kind of thing that got people searched at borders.

"Well," Odile said, in the tone of a woman deciding not to be impressed. "Flood brought us a present."

"Floods bring up everything eventually," Marguerite said. "Coffins. Cattle. Whole houses, sometimes." She crouched, ran her thumb along the brass plate. Under the verdigris the stamping was crisp, official, done by a press in some ministry that no longer existed. "This came off a boat. Look — the strapping's been cut, here and here. Someone jettisoned it in a hurry, or lost it, or—" She stopped herself. Speculation was a luxury. "Doesn't matter. It's salvage. Salvage is money."

"It's locked," Pirro said, tugging at the hasp. An iron padlock hung there, furred with rust, holding fast.

"Then we sell it locked. There are collectors in Lowport who'd pay a month's wages for the plate alone. Royal Vesseny brass. There are men who collect the *idea* of Vesseny now that there isn't one." She stood, wiped her hands on her trousers. "Chain it in the hold. We'll run it down to Lowport when the crest passes and be rid of it."

"Rid of it," Odile repeated.

"Odile."

"I only heard you say it twice. Wanted to make sure." The first mate folded her arms. She had a face built for disapproval, all planes and weather, and she deployed it now with the ease of long practice. "You know what's in it."

"Documents, probably. Papers keep. Somebody's archive went in the drink."

"Somebody's *royal* archive. With a lock worth picking and a seal worth killing for, sitting on our deck, and your plan is to sell it sight unseen to the highest bidder in the biggest port on this stretch of river." Odile shook her head slowly. "Margo. You want to walk into Lowport carrying this and tell me who you think comes asking about it. Because I can name three kinds of men who would, and none of them pay retail."

"The syndicate doesn't know we have it."

"The syndicate knows *everything* that floats. You taught me that yourself, year two, the gunpowder run." Odile crouched by the chest the way Marguerite had, studying it, but where Marguerite's instinct was to look away, Odile's was to lean closer. It was the difference between them and always had been. Marguerite survived by not looking. Odile survived by looking straight at things until they blinked. "Open it."

"No."

"Margo—"

"We don't open other people's property. That's rule four."

"Rule four exists so customs men can't hang us for what's in the hold," Odile said. "This isn't cargo. This is a question, floating in an eddy, waiting for us. Questions don't get better when you sell them to strangers."

Pirro had gone very quiet, which for Pirro was remarkable, and was watching the two of them the way he watched card games — openly delighted, carefully silent.

Marguerite looked at the chest. The lantern light moved on the wet brass, and the crowned tower seemed to shift under it, alive, patient. She felt the old cold feeling start in her sternum, the one she'd gotten good at ignoring, the one that said *you know whose seal that is, you know what countries mean by crowns, you remember the fire, don't pretend*. Dread, dressed up in curiosity's clothes so she'd agree to let it in.

And underneath the dread — she hated admitting this, even to herself, even alone in her own skull — underneath it was hunger. Nine years of running cargo and outrunning debt and never once opening a door back into the country that had eaten her family. Here was a door, washed up, small enough to fit in her hold.

"One look," she heard herself say, and was surprised to hear it, as though someone else had spoken with her voice. "To know what we're selling. Then it gets chained, and it goes to Lowport, and we take the money and forget it."

"Forget it," Odile echoed, and this time there was no argument in it at all, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The padlock defeated Pirro's pry bar and surrendered to Odile's cold chisel, which was fitting, because Odile believed every problem was ultimately a matter of applied force. The hasp tore free with a shriek of iron that carried out over the floodwater, and all three of them flinched at the sound, absurdly, as if the river might object.

Inside, the chest was lined in oilcloth, and the oilcloth had done its work. Beneath a layer of river silt that smelled of nine years of bottom, they found paper.

Not much of it, as archives went. Two leather document cases, buckled shut, water-stained but intact. A bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone brittle as insect wing. A slim ledger with a broken clasp. And on top of it all, resting as if placed there deliberately, a locket.

It was silver, small enough to hide in a palm, on a chain fine as spider silk. The front bore the same crowned tower, worked in relief no bigger than a thumbnail. Odile lifted it out with two fingers, careful as a surgeon, and turned it over, and frowned.

"There's an inscription," she said. "Wear's rubbed most of it. But there's a name."

"Probably the owner's. Some courtier's sweetheart. Doesn't matter." Marguerite heard her own voice going flat and brisk, the voice she used for negotiating freight rates, and knew she was doing it, and couldn't stop. "Paper's the value. Sealed state documents, provenance intact — there are exiles' societies in the capital cities that would—"

"Ashgrove," Odile read.

The word went through Marguerite like a fishhook through soft flesh.

The deck seemed to tilt, though the *Kestrel* lay steady on her double lines. The lantern hissed. Somewhere out in the dark the river kept on rising, indifferent, hauling the drowned country past them piece by piece.

"Say that again," Marguerite said, and her voice wasn't flat anymore.

"Ashgrove." Odile held the locket out to her. "A-S-H-G-R-O-V-E. Margo, whose name is that?"

*Yours,* said the cold feeling in her sternum. *Yours, yours, yours.*

"It's a common name," she said. "There were Ashgroves all through the river country. Millers. Ferrymen. It means nothing."

Odile looked at her for a long moment — that steady, unblinkable look — and then, mercifully, put the locket into her captain's hand without another word.

Marguerite closed her fingers around it. The silver was cold. Through the worn inscription she could feel the ghost of engraver's strokes, and beneath that, faintly, the shape of something inside the locket — a second compartment, hinged, the kind made to hold a miniature portrait or a curl of hair or a folded scrap of paper no bigger than a stamp.

Her sister had owned a locket like this. Had worn one every day of her life after her daughter was born, had worried it between her fingers when she was frightened, which in the last years had been often. Marguerite had buried that memory under nine years of current, under cargo manifests and fuel costs and the grammar of rivers, and it came up now the way the flood brought everything up — whole, rotting, undeniable.

The documents in the cases bore seals. The letters were in a ministerial hand. The ledger recorded names and dates and payments, and she did not open any of them, because she had seen enough, because the chest had already said what it came to say:

*Vesseny is not finished with you. Nothing is ever finished. The river keeps everything and gives it back.*

"Chain it in the hold," Marguerite said. Her voice came out steady, which she counted as the finest piece of seamanship of her career. "Double-lashed. Nobody touches it. Nobody speaks of it. First light, we make for Lowport, we sell it, and we are done with Vesseny forever."

Odile said nothing. Pirro said nothing. The silence of the two of them together was louder than the flood.

Marguerite stood at the rail long after the others had gone below, the locket clenched in her fist hard enough to print its shape into her palm, watching the black water slide past. She should throw it back. She knew she should throw it back. The river had made a mistake, delivering this to her of all boats, and a wise woman corrected the river's mistakes.

But her thumb had already found the little hinge of the hidden compartment, and it moved — stiff, corroded, but it moved — and inside, folded eight times over into a square the size of her thumbnail, was a slip of paper gone soft as cloth.

She didn't open it. Not yet. She told herself she'd open it in daylight, in the cabin, with the door shut, like a sensible woman conducting business.

She stood there until midnight, telling herself that.

Two hundred miles downstream, at a fueling dock she would reach in three days' time, a thin man in a soaked coat was already running for the reeds with the syndicate's dogs behind him — though of course Marguerite Ashgrove knew nothing of that yet, and slept badly, and dreamed of fire, and woke with the locket still in her hand.
