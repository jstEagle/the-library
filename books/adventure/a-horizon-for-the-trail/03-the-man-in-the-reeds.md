## 3. The Man in the Reeds

Tomas Reyes had learned to measure safety in seconds, and by that arithmetic he had perhaps forty of them left.

He crouched in the reed bank below the fueling dock at Corder's Landing, water seeping through the seat of his trousers, and counted the ways the morning could kill him. The dock itself was simple enough: a timber platform on pilings, a fuel barge moored alongside it, a hand-cranked pump, and a boy of perhaps sixteen dozing in a canvas chair with a broadsheet over his face. Beyond the dock stood the Kestrel.

He had seen her from the ridge road an hour ago and had come down through the marsh on purpose, because a boat that size, moving cargo upriver, was the last kind of vessel the syndicate would bother to search. She was a working boat and nothing more — a hundred and ten feet of blackened hull and sun-faded paint, her wheelhouse the color of old tea, her name painted on the bow in letters that had been white once and were now the color of bone. Steam curled from her stack in a lazy gray rope. Her deck crew were coiling lines with the easy, bored rhythm of people who had done the same thing a thousand mornings running.

Tomas had spent eleven days getting here. He had walked the towpath at night and slept in drainage culverts by day. He had paid a fisherman three days' wages for a ride he was then made to swim the last stretch of, because the fisherman had seen the men behind him and wanted no part of either party. His left shoe had come apart at the sole and he had bound it with reed cord, and now his left foot was wet and cold and beginning to go numb in a way he suspected he should be more worried about than he was.

But it was the satchel that mattered, and the satchel was dry. He had wrapped it in oilcloth and bound it to his chest with straps, and he had slept with it, eaten with it, and once — crossing the flooded fields below Vellmark — nearly drowned with it, and had chosen it over his own breath with a clarity that had frightened him afterward. Everything he had been, everything the archive had been, was in that satchel. The Vesseny royal registry. The succession proofs. Nine years of a dead nation's paperwork, carried out of the burning Hall of Records by a junior archivist who had not been important enough for anyone to think to stop.

He had been important enough now. That was the joke he could not stop telling himself. Eleven days of running, and the men behind him had never once called him by name. They called him *the courier*. As if the papers were the man and the man were merely the wrapping.

* * *

The Kestrel's first mate came down the gangway while he watched — a broad woman with iron-gray hair cut short and a face like a closed ledger. She spoke to the fuel boy, who woke with a start and began cranking the pump, and then she stood with her hands on her hips, surveying the marsh with the expression of someone who found it personally offensive.

Tomas had a speech prepared. He had been refining it for days, in culverts and ditches, and he ran through it now one more time: *I can pay. Not much, but passage is all I ask, and I ask nothing else. I don't want to know your route. I don't want to know your name. I'll work my way — I can read, I can write, I can mend netting, I can cook if it comes to that.* He had never cooked anything in his life. He decided he would learn quickly if it came to it.

He waited until the pump was running and the boy's attention was fully on the fuel line, and then he rose out of the reeds.

The effect was not what he had rehearsed. The first mate did not startle. She turned her head toward him the way a door turns — slowly, heavily, with a sound you could almost hear — and looked at him from his ruined shoe to his three-day beard to the satchel strapped to his chest, and said, in a voice like gravel poured slowly, "Well."

"My name is Tomas Reyes." His voice cracked on the second word and he hated himself for it. "I need passage north. As far as you're going."

"Can't help you."

"I can pay—"

"We're not a ferry." She turned back to the pump.

"Madam. Please." He came up the bank, reeds whispering behind him, and stood on the mud at the foot of the gangway, and the full absurdity of his position arrived all at once: a man of thirty-four, educated at the Collegium of Vesseny, keeper of the second-largest private collection of succession documents in the world, standing barefoot-adjacent in the mud begging a steamboat for a ride. "I'm not asking you to hide me. I'm not asking you to lie for me. Only to carry me a few hundred miles and forget I was ever aboard."

That got her to turn around. She looked at him for a long moment, and something in the look was not unkind, exactly, but was very far from kind. "People who want to be forgotten," she said, "don't usually announce it at the fuel dock."

* * *

The captain came out of the wheelhouse while they were still arguing.

Tomas saw her before she saw him — a woman at the rail above, tall, dark-haired, with her sleeves rolled and her forearms brown from years of sun, watching the river with an expression he would spend a long time trying to find the right word for. It was not watchfulness exactly. It was more like listening. She stood at the rail the way other people stood at the edge of a conversation they were not part of, and Tomas understood, with the small cold click of a lock turning, that this was a woman who read water the way he read documents: fluently, ceaselessly, and with an attention that never entirely rested.

She came down the stairs. "Odile. Problem?"

"Man wants a ride north," Odile said. "Says he'll pay."

"I said I'd work," Tomas said. "I'll do anything. Scrub the deck. Haul line. I can read charts—"

"You're wet," the captain said.

It was not a question, and it was not an accusation. It was an observation delivered with the flat precision of a person cataloging evidence, and Tomas felt it land like a lamp being shone into a corner he had wanted dark. He looked down at himself: the mud to the knees, the ruined shoe, the reed-seed caught in his collar, the satchel.

"I fell," he said. "Crossing the marsh."

"Nobody crosses the marsh," the captain said. "The marsh is where people go to not be seen crossing." She looked at him, and her eyes were a gray-brown the color of the river in November, and they did not blink enough. "You've been in that reed bank since before my mate came down. You watched us fuel up. You picked your moment." She turned to Odile. "He's running from something."

"I'm running *to* something," Tomas said, and heard how it sounded even as he said it.

The captain almost smiled. It was a small movement, quickly arrested, like a bird deciding against flight. "Everybody's running to something. It's what's behind them that buys the ticket." She put one hand on the rail of the gangway. "No. I'm sorry. We don't take passengers without papers, and you don't have papers, and a man without papers who hides in reeds is a man whose trouble comes aboard with him. I've got a crew to feed and a schedule to keep. Ask at Vellmark. There's a packet boat Thursdays."

"The packet boat is syndicate-chartered," Tomas said, before he could stop himself.

Silence. The pump kept thumping. Somewhere in the marsh a rail bird called, a thin descending note, like a wire being plucked and cut.

The captain looked at him differently now. "Is it," she said.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." She came down two more steps, and up close he could see the fine lines at the corners of her eyes and the salt-and-pepper beginning at her temples, and the fact that she was perhaps ten years older than he had first guessed. "Who told you the packet boat was syndicate-chartered?"

"The fisherman who put me in the river."

"And who was chasing you into the river?"

Tomas weighed it. Forty seconds had become four minutes, and four minutes was a fortune, and he was about to spend all of it. "Three men," he said. "Gray coats. One has a scar through his eyebrow. They're not police. They're not ministers. They're Corvane Syndicate, and they've been behind me since Vellmark, and if they reach me they will not take me to any authority, because what I'm carrying is worth more than any authority will pay them, and they know it."

The captain said nothing for a long moment. The pump thumped. The rail bird called again, and again, and stopped.

"Marguerite," Odile said quietly. It was the first time Tomas heard the name, and he would hear it ten thousand times after, and it would come to mean more to him than his own.

"I know," Marguerite said.

"You know what I'm going to say."

"I know what you're going to say." She looked at the river. She looked at it the way Tomas had seen priests look at altars — with a familiarity so complete it had become something like prayer. And then she looked back at him, and her face did the thing he would later understand was the truest thing about her: it closed, like a book being shut, and he knew the answer was no, and he knew the no was final, and he knew that underneath the no there was a current running the other way, because he had spent his whole life reading what people wrote without meaning to.

"Captain," he said. "I'm going to say one thing, and then I'll get out of your gangway and you'll never see me again. Whatever you think I am — courier, smuggler, spy — I'm an archivist. That's all. I kept records for a nation that doesn't exist anymore, and when it burned, I saved what I could, and men have been trying to take it from me ever since, because in these papers—" he touched the satchel, and his hand was shaking, and he let them see it shake, because he had learned that a trembling man is believed where a calm one is suspected, "—in these papers there are names. Living names. Names that certain people have spent nine years and a great deal of money making sure stay dead on paper. And I am the only man alive who knows where they are written."

Marguerite Ashgrove went very still.

It was not dramatic. It was the opposite of dramatic. It was the stillness of a woman standing in a river when the water suddenly goes cold around her ankles, and Tomas, who had spent eleven days learning to read the small signs of the country he fled through, saw it and did not yet understand it, and would not understand it for some time.

"Captain," Odile said again, and this time there was something under the gravel of her voice.

"I heard him," Marguerite said.

And then the morning ended.

* * *

Tomas heard them before Odile did, because he had spent eleven days learning the sound, and the sound was this: boots on a dock, three sets, not hurrying. Men who are hunting do not hurry when they believe the quarry is cornered. Hurrying is for the hunted.

"Down," he said, and it came out as a whisper, and then he said it again, louder: "Down — get down—" and Odile looked at him as if he had lost his mind, and then the three men in gray coats came around the end of the fuel barge with the unhurried certainty of collectors arriving at a debt, and the one in front had a scar through his eyebrow, and the world narrowed to a single bright point.

They saw him. Of course they saw him. There was nowhere to be — a mud bank, a gangway, a steamboat, and open river. The scarred one raised one hand, not a threat, only a greeting, a small courteous wave that was worse than any threat, and said, in the mild voice of a man discussing freight rates, "There you are. We've been looking everywhere."

Tomas did the arithmetic he had been doing for eleven days and arrived, as always, at the same sum: nowhere left. And it was in that moment — with the gray coats twenty feet away and the river at his back and the satchel heavy against his chest — that he looked at Marguerite Ashgrove and saw her make a decision.

He saw it happen. Later, he would try to describe it to her, and she would wave it off, but he saw it: the moment her eyes went from him to the gray coats and back, and something behind them moved like a shadow under water, and her hand found the rail of the gangway, and her weight shifted forward instead of back. Everyone else on that dock — the fuel boy, frozen by his pump; Odile, already calculating; Tomas himself, already surrendering — everyone leaned away from the trouble. She leaned into it. It was the smallest movement, an inch of the body, and it was the whole of her.

"Gentlemen," she said, in a voice pitched to carry across water, pleasant and flat as a lake at dawn. "This is a private dock and a private vessel. State your business or state it to the harbormaster."

The scarred man's smile widened by a degree. "Our business is that man. He's a thief. Stole from our employers."

"Then you'll have a warrant."

"Our employers don't believe in warrants."

"Then you'll have me," Marguerite said, "which is worse."

Tomas would think about that sentence for the rest of his life. He would think about how she said it — not loudly, not bravely, with no performance in it at all, the way you state a fact of engineering: *this bridge holds or it doesn't.* And he watched the scarred man weigh it, the way you weigh a stranger's bridge, and he watched him decide — for now — that it might hold.

"We'll be at Lowport," the man said. "Boats go north through Lowport." He touched two fingers to his brow, a mock salute, and the three of them turned and walked back down the dock with their unhurried boots, and the morning resumed around the place where they had been, the way water resumes over a stone.

Nobody spoke until the fuel boy, with the instinct of the very young, said, "That'll be forty marks for the fuel," and Odile paid him, and the ordinary world reasserted itself like a bone being set.

Marguerite turned to Tomas. Up close, in the aftermath, he could see that her hands were not entirely steady, and that she was angry — not at him, or not only at him, but at the whole arrangement of a world that had put three gray coats on her dock before breakfast.

"You have ten minutes," she said. "Gangway's up in ten. You'll sleep in the cargo hold, you'll eat with the crew, you'll work, and the first time you lie to me about anything that matters, I put you off at the next lock, syndicate or no syndicate. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Tomas said. "Captain. Thank you. I—"

"Don't thank me." She was already turning away, already climbing the stairs, already the captain again and not the woman who had leaned into trouble. "Thank the river. It was going to make me take you either way." She stopped at the top of the wheelhouse stairs and looked back down at him, and for a moment the listening expression returned, the one from the rail, and she said, almost to herself: "Names that stay dead on paper. God help me. I've heard that song before."

Then the wheelhouse door shut, and Odile said, "Well, archivist. You heard her. Ten minutes." She looked him up and down, and something in her closed ledger of a face shifted — not softening, nothing so generous, but a recalculation, a new column being opened. "And Reyes. Whatever you're carrying — if it's going to bring gray coats onto this boat every week between here and the pass, you'll tell me now, or you'll tell her soon, but you *will* tell her, because she'll find out anyway. She always finds out."

"I know," Tomas said, and was surprised to find it was true. He had known her for eleven minutes and he knew it the way he knew the grammar of a dead language: completely, and without being able to say how.

He went up the gangway with the satchel against his chest and his ruined shoe squelching, and behind him the reeds whispered in the wind off the water, and ahead of him the Kestrel's deck smelled of coal smoke and rope and tar, and somewhere below in the hold there was a sealed chest with a dead nation's crest on it that he did not yet know existed, and a name inside it that he did not yet know was his to carry.

The Kestrel cast off at noon. Tomas stood at the stern rail and watched Corder's Landing shrink behind them — the dock, the pump, the reed bank where he had counted his last forty seconds — and he did not see the gray coats again, and he made the mistake of believing that meant they were gone.

The river bent north. The wheelhouse bell rang twice. And in the wheelhouse, Marguerite Ashgrove put her hands on the wheel she had held for nine years and said, to no one, in a voice too low for the mate at the engine telegraph to hear: "Names that stay dead on paper."

She steered into the current, and did not look back.
