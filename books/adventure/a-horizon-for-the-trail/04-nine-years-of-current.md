## 4. Nine Years of Current

The river at night was a different animal, and Marguerite had learned its habits the way other people learned the habits of difficult spouses: patiently, with a certain grim affection, and always with one hand near the wheel.

She stood her watch alone. That was her rule — first watch hers, always, from midnight until the sky went the color of pewter. Odile had argued about it once, years ago, back when arguing with Marguerite was still a thing Odile permitted herself. *You never sleep, Ines. You can't steer a boat on no sleep.* And Marguerite had said, in the flat voice that ended conversations, *I steer fine.* She had been steering fine for nine years, and she would steer fine for nine more, and in the meantime the night watch was hers because the night was when the river told the truth.

By day the Sarelle was commerce — barges and fueling docks, the smell of diesel and cut cane, pilots shouting at each other in the trade tongue. By night it was just water and dark and the occasional eye of a heron lifting off the shallows, and Marguerite could almost pretend she was the only person in the world who had ever learned to read it.

She read it now. The current had a seam to it tonight, a faint puckering on the black surface two hundred meters ahead where the channel shallowed against the old ferry pilings. She eased the wheel a half-point to port without thinking about it, the way a person doesn't think about breathing, and the Kestrel slid through the gap between the piling and the sandbar with a hand's width to spare on either side. Somewhere below, Pirro was asleep in the fo'c'sle with his boots on, the way he always slept, as if he expected at any moment to be ordered to jump.

The lantern in the wheelhouse burned low. She did not trim the wick. She liked the dark.

Behind her, down in the saloon, a light was still burning.

She had told Tomas Reyes to sleep. He had thanked her very politely, in that careful, formal way of his, as if he were a guest at a dinner party rather than a fugitive sleeping on a boat that could be boarded by syndicate men at any of the next four ports — and then he had gone back to work. She could see him through the saloon door's glass: hunched over the chart table, the chest's contents spread around him like the entrails of some drowned animal, papers weighted flat with teacups and the brass ashtray nobody used. His lips moved as he read. Occasionally he wrote something in a small, precise hand on a sheet of his own, cross-referencing, indexing, doing whatever it was archivists did with the bones of dead nations.

Nine days since the flood had given them the chest. Nine days of it sitting in her hold like a stone in a shoe.

She should have sold it. She had wanted to sell it — had said so, loudly, the morning they'd hauled it dripping onto the deck, the royal crest of Vesseny crusted with river mud and something that might have been blood. *There's a collector in Lowport pays good money for Vesseny relics,* she'd told Odile. *Sealed or not. Especially sealed. Mystery sells.* And Odile had looked at her with that maddening, level look of hers and said, *Open it,* and Marguerite had said no, and Odile had said *open it* again in the voice she used on stubborn cargo, and in the end Marguerite had opened it herself, because if she didn't do it, someone else would, and she could not abide other people's hands on her boat.

The locket had been the worst of it. A plain silver locket, river-swollen, the chain snapped, and inside — when she'd finally, three days later, alone, at night, with the wheelhouse door shut — pried it open, a curl of hair gone to rust and a name engraved on the back in a hand she had known since childhood.

She had not told anyone about the name. Not Odile. Not Tomas, though he'd asked, gently, whether the locket meant something to her, and she'd said it was junk, and he'd had the decency to look like he believed her.

The name was *Elodie.*

Her sister's name. Her dead sister's name, engraved in a hand that was her sister's hand, on a locket that had come out of the Sarelle nine years after Elodie Ashgrove went into the fire at Vesseny and did not come out.

Marguerite stood at the wheel and let the river carry the thought away and then, as the river always did, bring it back.

* * *

The night Vesseny fell, it had rained.

That was the detail nobody believed when she told the story, and she rarely told the story. People expected the fall of a nation to happen in sunlight — banners burning against a clean sky, that kind of image, the kind the newsreels loved. But it had rained in Coravelle for three days straight, a thin cold autumn rain, and the fires had hissed and smoked rather than roared, and the smoke had lain along the streets like fog, and the government ministries had emptied not with a crash but with a kind of wet, apologetic whisper, ministers leaving by back doors with their collars up, carrying what they could.

Her father had been Keeper of the Registry. It was not a grand title, though he'd pretended it was. The Registry of Vesseny held the proofs — the genealogies, the succession records, the land grants, the old compacts signed in the days when Vesseny had been two warring river-states learning, badly, to be one. Her father used to say the Registry was the country's memory, and a country that loses its memory is not conquered, it simply stops being, the way a man who forgets his own name stops being the man he was.

Marguerite had been nineteen. Elodie had been twenty-two and already married, already restless, already the brilliant one — Elodie, who read four languages and argued politics with their father at dinner and won, Elodie, who had married a junior clerk in the Royal Household named Dario Fen and moved to the capital full of plans.

When the collapse came — when the banks called in the war debts and the provinces declared themselves free and the King's own guard refused to fire on the crowds — the Registry became suddenly the most dangerous building in Coravelle. Because whoever held the proofs held the future. A legitimate claim to the throne, properly documented, was worth more than an army to the loyalists. It was worth more than that to anyone who wanted to *prevent* a restoration. And her father, poor stubborn man, had refused to evacuate. *The Registry does not move,* he'd said, standing in the doorway with the rain coming in sideways behind him. *The Registry is the country.*

The fire had started at two in the morning. Marguerite had never learned — had stopped letting herself try to learn — whether it was the republicans or the syndicate's men or simply a lamp knocked over in the looting. It didn't matter. Fire is fire. She remembered standing in the street with her mother's arm around her, watching the roof go, and her father running back inside — she could still see the exact motion of it, his coat flapping, his hand raised in a gesture that was either farewell or *stay back* — and she remembered Elodie screaming and going in after him.

Neither of them came out.

Her mother had lived another six years, in exile, in a rented room in a river town not so different from the ports the Kestrel served now, and had died saying the same thing over and over, which was *the proofs, the proofs, someone has the proofs.* Because a week before the fire, her father — cautious, fussy, foresighted in the way of men who are ignored their whole lives — had shipped a portion of the Registry's holdings out of the city. Crates, sealed, sent by river barge under a false manifest to a contact in the northern trade. He had never told anyone where. He had died, probably, in the act of protecting the secret, and the secret had drowned with him, or so the family had believed.

So had Marguerite. For nine years she had built her whole life on the belief that the past was ash — that everything Vesseny had been was burned or scattered or sold, and that the only honest thing left to do was run cargo and pay no debts to anyone, living or dead.

Then the flood had brought up a chest stamped with the royal crest, and Odile had said *open it,* and inside had been documents — and now a man who read dead hands for a living sat in her saloon at two in the morning, moving his lips over her father's world.

* * *

The kettle in the wheelhouse had gone cold an hour ago. Marguerite poured the last of it anyway, drank it bitter, and set the cup down, and when she looked up, Tomas was standing in the wheelhouse doorway.

He moved quietly for a man his size. He had that quality of people who had spent years not wanting to be noticed — a stillness, a way of standing just at the edge of a room. The lantern light caught the grey at his temples and the ink stains on his fingers.

"You should sleep," she said.

"So should you."

"I sleep at anchor."

"You didn't anchor."

"No," she agreed. "I didn't." The Kestrel was running dark through the middle reach, engine at half, because Marguerite had decided without announcing it that they would not stop at Harrow Bend, where the syndicate kept an agent, and would not stop at Tern Lock either, and the extra hours meant running the night instead of sleeping through it. Tomas had noticed. Tomas noticed everything; it was his profession.

He came into the wheelhouse and stood beside her at the chart table, not touching the wheel, not presuming, just standing in the small circle of warmth the little coal stove threw off. For a while neither of them spoke. The river breathed against the hull. Somewhere far upstream, thunder muttered — the monsoon was coming early this year; she could feel it in the way the water ran, heavy and brown and impatient.

"You found something," she said.

He didn't pretend otherwise. She liked that about him. In nine days he had not once lied to her, which in her experience made him either very honest or very dangerous, and she had not yet decided which.

"I've been working on the manifests," he said. "The chest held three layers. The top layer is what anyone would expect — household records, correspondence, nothing sensitive. Someone wanted the chest to look ordinary if it was opened by the wrong people." He laid a sheet of his own cramped notation on the chart table, weighted it with her cold cup. "The second layer is coded. Registry cipher, the old administrative kind — I was trained on it, back when the archive still existed and I still had a job. It's slow going. But it's a shipping cipher, Marguerite. It's not protecting secrets. It's protecting *inventory.*"

"Inventory," she repeated.

"Lists. What was shipped, where, to whom. Dated nine days before the fall of Coravelle." He paused, and she felt him choosing his words the way she chose her channels, feeling for the deep water. "Your father's signature is on the authorization. I recognized it from the surviving examples. He signed himself Keeper of the Registry, and under it, the Ashgrove mark."

The name went through her like cold water through a crack in a hull — not dramatic, not a flood, just a slow finding of every gap.

"My father's been dead nine years," she said.

"I know. I'm sorry." And he was; she could hear it. "But the inventory doesn't end with him. The cipher entries continue. Someone kept updating the lists after the fall. Someone moved the holdings — twice, by the dates. Once upriver to a depot at Saint-Elm. Once further north." He turned the sheet toward her. "And every transfer after the first is authorized by a different hand. Someone who inherited the responsibility."

"Who?"

"I don't know the name yet. It's initialed. *E.A.*"

The wheel was suddenly very far away and very close at the same time. Marguerite kept her hand on it because if she took her hand off it she did not know what her hands would do.

*Elodie Ashgrove.*

Her sister had gone into the fire. Her sister had died in the fire. She had stood in the rain and watched the roof fall and had spent nine years carrying that weight — the weight of the one who stayed outside, the one who lived, the one who owed the dead a debt she could never find a way to pay. Every mile of river she had run, every debt she had outrun, every port she'd left before dawn, had been a payment on a loan whose creditor was a woman in a burned building.

And now a dead man's cipher said *E.A.* had signed for a transfer of the proofs of Vesseny — nine years after the fire.

"There's a mistake," she heard herself say. Her voice was steady. She was proud of how steady it was. "The initials could be anyone. Half the Registry staff were Ashgroves by marriage and coincidence. My father employed cousins."

"Could be," Tomas said mildly. "But there's one more document. The third layer. It wasn't ciphered — it was wrapped in oilcloth inside a hollowed-out ledger, the way you protect the one thing that matters most. It's a letter. Unsent, or never delivered, or delivered and returned — I can't tell. It's in a woman's hand." He drew it from his coat, but he didn't put it on the table. He held it, and looked at her with those careful, patient eyes. "I haven't read it. It's sealed. But the outside of it has a name and an address, and I think — I think it's meant for whoever the chest was trying to reach. And I think that person might be you."

"Give it to me."

"Marguerite—"

"*Give it to me.*"

He gave it to her.

The oilcloth was stiff with age and river-damp. Inside was a letter, folded twice, sealed with a plain drop of wax — no crest, no mark, just a thumbprint pressed into the wax while it was soft, the seal of someone with nothing and no one to vouch for them. The paper was good paper, Registry stock. The hand on the outside was slanted, quick, intelligent.

She knew the hand.

She had known it across dinner tables and through school exercise books and on the flyleaf of every novel Elodie had ever stolen from their father's shelf. She had known it the way you know a voice in a crowd — instantly, completely, before thought has any say in the matter.

*Elodie.* Dated four days before the fire.

Marguerite did not open it. Her thumb rested on the fold and her whole body had gone still, the stillness she used at the wheel when the water ahead looked wrong, and Tomas watched her and did not move and did not speak, because he was a man who understood archives, who understood that some documents must come to rest in their own time.

The river ran. The thunder upstream was closer now, and the first smell of rain came down the valley — wet stone and green rot, the monsoon announcing itself.

"The letter names the Ashgrove line," Tomas said quietly. "I only read the opening lines. I stopped. But Marguerite — the chest didn't survive nine years in the water by accident. Someone packed it to survive. Someone sent it *downriver,* toward the trade ports, toward the places a person with no name and no country might wash up. It wasn't lost in the flood. I think it was *looking* for you."

Outside, the first fat drops hit the wheelhouse roof like fingers tapping. Marguerite looked down at her sister's handwriting, at the thumbprint in the wax — pressed, she understood suddenly, so that the recipient would know the letter was genuine, because a thumbprint was the one seal that couldn't be forged, the seal of the living — and she thought: *you went into the fire, and something of you came out, and it has been in the water all this time, waiting.*

She broke the seal.

The rain came down in earnest then, hammering the river to white noise, and by the light of the low lantern, in her father's old cipher and her sister's unmistakable hand, the past stopped being past — and the first line of the letter told her that Elodie had not died in the fire at all.
