## 5. Passengers and Liars

The trouble with debts, Odile Marchand had learned, was that they did not drown. They learned to swim.

She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* as the boat nosed into Lowport's outer anchorage, watching the town assemble itself out of the morning haze — first the cranes, then the tin roofs catching light like scattered coins, then the long gray smear of the customs house where every debt in the district was written down in ledgers by men with clean fingernails. Somewhere in one of those ledgers was her name. Four hundred and ten crowns, owed to a man named Ferren Brask, who collected interest the way other men collected stamps: patiently, lovingly, and with an eye toward completeness.

Four hundred and ten. It had been three-seventy when they'd last docked here. The arithmetic of it lived in her chest now, a small hot coal she carried everywhere, even up to the wheelhouse, even into sleep. Marguerite thought Odile was saving for a winter coat. Pirro thought she was sending money home to a sick mother, because Odile had told him so, and Pirro believed what people told him the way he believed in the river — completely, without audit. Only the coal knew the truth, and coals tell no tales.

"Lowport," Marguerite said beside her, in the tone she used for ports the way other captains used them for weather. Flat. Watchful. "Two hours. We take on diesel and flour, nothing else. I don't like the look of the water."

Odile glanced down. The Sarelle ran brown-green and sluggish here, carrying more silt than current, the color of old tea left standing. "You never like the look of the water."

"That's why we're still floating." Marguerite's eyes went along the docks, reading them the way she read the river — the idle men at the fueling station who were too well-dressed to be idle, the skiff sitting too far out from the pilings, the way the harbormaster's flag hung limp when every other flag on the waterfront had found a breeze. Something in her jaw tightened, but whatever she concluded, she kept. She always kept. Nine years of running this boat had taught Odile that Marguerite Ashgrove's silences had cargo holds of their own, and you did not get to see what was stowed in them. "I'll handle the fuel. You take the chandlery list. Pirro stays aboard."

"Pirro always stays aboard."

"Pirro is nineteen and would buy us a live goat if someone smiled at him." Marguerite was already moving toward the ladder, then paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back. Her face did something complicated — softened, almost — before it settled again. "And Odile. If anyone comes asking about passengers north, the answer is no. We're not carrying strangers past the gorge. Not this season."

"Not this season," Odile repeated, and heard how easily the lie would come later, and hated herself for hearing it.

* * *

The chandlery smelled of tar, coffee, and the particular sweetness of rope gone soft with age. Odile was haggling over the price of lamp oil — the shopkeeper wanted nine crowns a tin and she'd gotten him to seven and was working on six-fifty — when the door opened and the whole atmosphere of the room changed, the way a room changes when weather comes in.

He was tall, broad through the shoulders without being heavy, dressed in the kind of traveling clothes that pretended to be practical and were actually expensive: good leather boots worn just enough to look honest, a wool coat the color of wet slate. Forty, maybe. Sun-browned. A smile already in place, but not the eager smile of a salesman — something easier, more patient, the smile of a man who had never once in his life entered a room and needed anything from it.

"Lamp oil," he said, reading the situation off the counter between them. "You're getting robbed. He charges river crews eight and tells himself he's doing charity."

"He's getting six-fifty," Odile said.

"He'll get six." The stranger leaned an elbow on the counter, entirely at his ease, and addressed the shopkeeper directly. "Six, Dannel, and I'll take two tins off your hands myself, which means you move four tins instead of two and still make your margin. Everyone eats." And the thing was, the shopkeeper *did* look like a man ready to eat, nodding along, already reaching for the tins, and Odile watched six crowns a tin settle over the transaction like frost and understood she had just been handled — gently, expertly, the way you'd steady a ladder for someone you wanted to like you.

"Clever," she said, when the shopkeeper had gone to wrap the order.

"Honest, mostly. Cleverness is just honesty with better timing." He offered his hand. "Callum Voss. Merchant. Textiles, mostly, some dried goods. I'm told there's a boat called the *Kestrel* running north, and I'm told its captain doesn't take passengers."

"She doesn't."

"Mm." He didn't look discouraged. He looked, if anything, pleased, as though her refusal confirmed something he'd been told and hoped was true. "Then I'll have to be charming at her instead of at you, which will be exhausting for us both." His eyes were pale gray-green, river-colored, and they held hers a moment longer than courtesy required — not flirtation exactly, but assessment. A weighing. Odile had spent enough years around men who bought and sold things to know the feeling of being appraised, and she felt it now, itemized: first mate, forties, competent, tired. What are your pressures? What are your prices?

"I'll pass along that you exist," she said. "That's all."

"That's all anyone can do." He paid for his own tins, overpaid visibly, tipped the boy sweeping the floor, and paused at the door with the morning light behind him. "Miss — forgive me, I didn't catch a name."

"No," Odile said. "You didn't."

He laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, warm as a stove — and touched his hat brim, and was gone into the street. Odile stood among the lamp oil and the rope and discovered that she was smiling, and stopped smiling immediately, because she knew what came next, and knew it the way you know weather in an old scar.

* * *

He came to the dock at midday, and he was everything Marguerite was not, which was precisely the problem.

Where Marguerite met strangers like a closed lock, Voss arrived with his hands full of openness: papers of passage properly drawn, references from two shipping houses Odile actually recognized, a letter of credit from a bank in Meridian that made the harbormaster's clerk suddenly find reasons to be helpful. He wanted passage north — not all the way, he said, just as far as Saint-Elm, where he had business with a mill factor, and wasn't Saint-Elm on their route anyway, and wouldn't it be sensible, splitting his fare across the crew rather than across some syndicate packet line that would charge him triple and feed him worse?

"The answer is no," Marguerite said. She stood on her own deck with her arms crossed, and Odile, coiling line nearby with great attention to the coil, listened with her whole body while pretending to listen with none of it. "We're a working boat. We don't carry passengers."

"A working boat," Voss agreed pleasantly, "with a working budget. Captain, I've seen your hull from the dock. Port side, waterline, about amidships — you've got a patch job there older than my coat, and I'd wager my letter of credit she's weeping a little in heavy water. My fare would cover that patch properly. New plate, proper rivets, done at Saint-Elm where the ironwork is half what it costs downriver." He spread his hands. "I'm not asking you to carry me. I'm offering to pay for your boat's health."

Silence. Odile did not look up from her coil, but she could feel Marguerite looking at the hull, or rather looking *through* it, at the patch, at the truth of it. That patch was Odile's shame too — she'd been the one who'd argued, two months back, that they could stretch the repair another season, that the money was needed elsewhere. Needed where, she hadn't said. Needed for whom, she definitely hadn't said.

"We'll consider it," Marguerite said at last, which from Marguerite was not yes, was barely maybe, was in fact a door held open exactly wide enough to be closed again. "Leave your papers with my first mate. We sail on the evening tide either way."

Voss handed over his documents with a little bow, thanked them both as though the answer had been yes, and went off down the gangway whistling something light and tuneless, and Odile stood holding his folder and understood that she had just become the keeper of a decision she had no right to keep.

* * *

She should have shown Marguerite everything. That was the thought that sat with her through the afternoon, through the fueling, through the loading of the flour sacks, through the long gold hour when the light came slantwise up the river and the *Kestrel* lay against the dock like a tired animal. She should have put the folder in Marguerite's hands and said: here, decide, it's yours to decide.

But under the papers — she checked when no one was looking, alone in the deckhouse with the door shut — under the shipping references and the bank letter, there had been a second document. Thinner. Unofficial. A note of introduction on heavy cream stock, unsigned except by a seal she did not recognize and could not stop recognizing since: a stylized leech curled around a coin.

She knew that mark. Every soul on the lower Sarelle knew it, the way farmers know a particular breed of crow. The Meridian Consolidated — the syndicate, though nobody called it that above a whisper — lent money in flood years and collected in famine ones, and their collectors wore gray coats and their agents wore smiles, and Callum Voss wore both, probably folded in the same trunk. The note said only: *Render all assistance. Subject cooperative. Report at Saint-Elm.*

Report at Saint-Elm. Which meant he wasn't going *to* Saint-Elm. He was going *from* it, or through it, or using it as a mile-marker on some errand that had nothing to do with textiles and everything to do with whatever the syndicate wanted on the upper river this season. And the *Kestrel*, with its sealed chest hidden in the hold and its fugitive archivist pretending to be a deckhand named Tomas who mopped decks badly, was carrying exactly the kind of cargo that made men like Voss get sent upriver.

She could have burned the note. She could have woken Marguerite. She could have done a dozen things, and instead she sat in the deckhouse with the cream-colored paper in her hand and did the arithmetic she always did, the coal glowing in her chest: four hundred and ten crowns, accruing; Ferren Brask's collectors who had begun, lately, to be less patient; the letter of credit upstairs that could clear her debt twice over and leave change for a new coat besides. Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it — Voss would pay handsomely, Voss *had* to pay handsomely, and fares went through the first mate's books, and books could be kept in more than one way.

Not stealing, she told herself. Skimming. There was a difference, and the difference was the width of a lie, and she was going to have to lie it every day he was aboard.

She fed the note to the galley stove and watched the seal curl and go black, the leech burning last, and told herself that destroying evidence was not the same as creating treachery. The stove did not agree. Stoves never do.

* * *

Marguerite decided at dusk, standing at the wheel with the evening tide turning, and she decided no — Odile saw it coming in the set of her shoulders, in the way she'd been quiet through supper, quieter than even her usual quiet. Tomas was below, out of sight, out of sound. The chest was in the hold under the spare line. Everything in Odile screamed that the safest course, the course Marguerite's own rule demanded — *never stop moving, never carry what you can't throw overboard* — was to leave Callum Voss on the dock with his whistling and his letters of credit.

"Captain," Odile said, and heard her own voice do something it almost never did around Marguerite: hesitate. "About the passenger."

"You have something to say, say it."

Say it. Say *he's syndicate, I saw the seal, I burned the proof.* Say *I want his fare because I'm drowning and I didn't tell you I was drowning.* Say any true thing, one true thing, and let the river sort the rest.

What she said was: "His money's good. And the patch on the port side won't survive the gorge. You know it and I know it. Saint-Elm's the last yard before high water, and his fare covers it twice over." All true. Every word true, arranged like stones across a creek so she could cross without wetting her feet. That was the skill of lying, she thought bitterly; it wasn't invention, it was *placement*. "We'd be fools to leave him on the dock."

Marguerite was silent a long moment. The wheel creaked under her hands. Out on the darkening water a fish jumped, once, the slap of it loud as a gunshot.

"Fares go through your books," Marguerite said finally. "You vouch for him?"

Every debt has a moment when it calls itself due early. This was hers, standing at the wheel beside the woman who had pulled her out of a Lowport gutter nine years ago and asked no questions and taken no collateral, and Odile felt the whole weight of the coal in her chest and answered it the way debtors answer everything.

"I vouch for him," she said.

Marguerite nodded once, turned the wheel, and called forward for lines cast off, and the *Kestrel* slid away from Lowport into the dusk with a syndicate agent sleeping in the guest berth and a fugitive hiding below and a chest full of dead kingdom riding in the hold, and Odile went aft to enter the fare in her books, in her real books, and then in the other book, the thin one wrapped in oilcloth beneath her bunk, where she wrote: *C.V., passage to Saint-Elm, forty crowns,* and then, after a moment, in smaller letters, as if ink could be made innocent by shrinking:

*I'm sorry.*

The river carried the boat north. The coal in her chest carried itself. And somewhere behind them, in the lamplit offices of Lowport, she had no doubt at all, a second report was already traveling faster than any boat could — a wire, a whisper, a name moving up the line: *the Kestrel has taken a passenger. The Kestrel is heading for the gorge.*

By morning, Odile told herself, she would find a way to tell Marguerite everything.

She did not believe herself. But she wrote it down, and the ink, at least, was honest.
