## 6. Monsoon Season

The rain came three weeks early, and it came like a debt collector.

Marguerite felt it before she saw it — a change in the river's voice, a low mutter under the usual conversation of current and hull. She had been at the wheel since dawn, running the Kestrel north through the wide green country above Lowport, when the southern sky went the color of wet slate. Not storm-slate. Monsoon-slate. There was a difference, and every boatman on the Sarelle knew it: a storm was an argument, loud and finished in an hour. The monsoon was a sentence passed down from some court that did not hear appeals.

"Odile," she called down the engine hatch. "Get the canvas over the cargo. All of it."

Her first mate's head appeared, gray-streaked hair plastered to her forehead already, though not a drop had fallen. "That bad?"

"That bad."

Odile looked south, squinted, and said nothing else, which was its own confirmation. In nine years Marguerite could count on one hand the times Odile had moved without commentary.

By midday the world had dissolved. The rain did not fall so much as arrive — a solid, warm, relentless presence that turned the riverbanks into smears of green and made the channel markers vanish as though they'd never existed. The Sarelle itself began to change character, swelling with runoff from the eastern hills, carrying whole trees past them like battering rams, brown water climbing the pilings of the fish weirs until the nets drowned.

Marguerite steered by memory and by feel, reading the water the way other people read letters — the boil of a submerged snag here, the smooth glassy tongue that meant deep channel there. The Kestrel rode heavy with grain and salt pork for the upriver settlements, and every pound of it sat lower in her mind than in her hold.

Tomas Reyes came up onto the deck around noon, hunched under a borrowed oilskin two sizes too big, and stood at the rail watching the river eat the world.

"This is normal?" he asked. He had to half-shout to be heard.

"For the season, yes. For the calendar, no." Marguerite kept her eyes forward. "The rains are early. Early means angry."

"You sound like you're describing a person."

"I'm describing something older than most people." She nodded at the brown torrent. "The Sarelle was moving before Vesseny had a name. It'll be moving after everyone aboard is forgotten. Best to stay polite with it."

He was quiet a moment. Then, unexpectedly: "My grandmother used to say something like that. About the mountains, though. She said you don't climb a mountain, you ask permission to pass through."

"Did it work?"

"She died in an avalanche," Tomas said, and there was a dry note in his voice that Marguerite recognized — grief worn smooth by handling, the way river stones go round. "So. Mixed results."

Despite herself, despite everything, Marguerite laughed. It came out of her rough and surprised, like a bird flushed from cover. She hadn't laughed in days — hadn't laughed properly in longer than she cared to count — and the sound seemed to startle them both.

"Well," she managed. "Ask the river anyway. It costs nothing."

* * *

The trouble started at dusk, as trouble preferred to do.

Marguerite heard it first through her boots — a wrongness in the vibration of the deck, a flutter where there should have been steady thrum. Then Pirro came sliding down from the bow, young face pale under his hood.

"Captain. We're shipping water. Forward compartment — it's coming in fast."

They were four hours from the nearest shelter, a fueling dock at a place called Marrow Bend that might or might not still exist above the flood line. Too far to run with a leaking hull and a river full of floating timber. Marguerite made the calculation in the time it took to say the words aloud.

"We ride it out. Odile — drop anchor off the willow bar, stern to the current. Everyone else, get below. If we're going to drown, we're going to drown busy."

The Kestrel took up her station in the lee of a willow island barely taller than the water itself, and the night became a war against the river.

It was worse than Marguerite had let herself believe. The old seam above the forward bilge — patched twice, cursed daily — had opened along its whole length, and the monsoon found it the way water always finds weakness, patiently, absolutely. By full dark they were bailing in shifts, buckets passing hand to hand in the lamplit dimness of the hold, the boat rolling and shuddering around them while the rain hammered the deck overhead like a fist demanding entry.

Odile ran the bucket line with the economy of a woman who had done this before and expected to do it again. Pirro bailed with the frantic energy of youth, soaked to the skin, hair in his eyes, refusing every offer to rest. The engineer, Brammel, stayed wedged in his engine room keeping the pumps alive, shouting reports through the hatch in a language that was only half words.

And Tomas Reyes — archivist, fugitive, a man whose hands were made for paper and ink — stripped off his ruined oilskin, rolled up his sleeves, and took a bucket.

"You don't have to," Marguerite told him, passing him in the line.

"Neither do you." He heaved his bucket up the ladder to Pirro, who dumped it and sent it back down. "But here I am."

"It's my boat."

"And it's my life you're carrying up this river." He said it simply, without drama, and took the next bucket. "Seems fair I help keep it afloat."

Marguerite had no answer for that, so she took her bucket and bailed.

Hours passed. The rhythm became everything — bend, scoop, lift, pass; bend, scoop, lift, pass — until Marguerite's shoulders burned and her palms went soft and raw and her arms moved on their own, thinking nothing, feeling only the weight of the water and the give of the boat beneath her. The lamplight swung shadows across the curved ceiling of the hold. Somewhere above, the rain went on and on, a sound so total it stopped being sound and became weather, became climate, became the inside of the world.

Around what must have been midnight, the water in the bilge stopped rising. They were winning — or rather, they were holding even, which on a night like this was the same thing.

It was Pirro who broke first. The boy had been going on stubbornness and terror for six hours, and somewhere in the seventh hour both gave out at once, and he sat down hard in the sloshing shallows of the bilge and started laughing. High, cracked, hysterical laughter, the kind that has panic underneath it.

"Pirro—" Odile started.

"No, look—" He pointed at Tomas. "Look at him!"

Tomas looked down at himself. The borrowed oilskin was long gone; his shirt was plastered to his chest, his trousers were ruined, his hair stood up in black spikes, and there was a streak of bilge grease across his face from temple to chin that made him look like a man who had lost a fight with the boat itself. Which, in fairness, he had.

"He looks like a drowned clerk," Pirro wheezed.

"I *was* a clerk," Tomas said with dignity, and then the grease streak slid further when he raised an eyebrow, and Pirro went off again, and then Odile snorted — Odile, who Marguerite had once seen watch a man fall overboard without changing expression — and then it was all of them, the whole bucket line laughing in the flooded belly of a dying boat, laughter that had nothing to do with anything funny and everything to do with being alive at three in the morning when the river wanted otherwise.

Marguerite laughed too. She laughed until her ribs ached around the bruise of exhaustion, and when she caught Tomas's eye across the lamplight — grease-streaked, soaked, grinning at her like a fool — something in her chest that had been clenched for nine years loosened by a single degree. Just one. But she felt it move.

"All right," she said, wiping her eyes with a forearm that was already wet. "All right. Shift change. Pirro, sleep. That's an order. Odile, take the line. Reyes—"

"Still here."

"Come topside. Five minutes. You've earned air."

* * *

The rain on deck was a wall you walked into rather than through. They stood under the overhang of the pilot house shoulder to shoulder, watching the willow island dissolve by inches, watching the lightning walk the far hills in silent white strides.

"The documents," Marguerite said, because she had been not-asking for two days and the night had made her honest. "How goes the reading?"

"Slowly. The water got into the satchel before I did." He tilted his head back, letting the rain drum on his face. "But paper is patient. More patient than people. Half of it will recover if I can dry it properly — assuming your stove survives the week."

"And the part you've read?"

Tomas was quiet long enough that she turned to look at him. Lightning lit his profile, there and gone.

"There's a manifest," he said finally. "A private one, sealed separately from the rest. It lists names — people who carried things out of the palace in the last days. Couriers. Custodians." A pause. "One of the names is Ashgrove."

The word landed in Marguerite like a stone dropped down a well. She watched the dark water slide past and waited for the splash that never quite came.

"My family left Vesseny before the end," she said. Her voice came out flat, careful, a deckhand's voice, giving away nothing. "We were nobody. Boat people. Whatever your manifest thinks—"

"I'm not accusing anyone of anything. I'm telling you what the paper says." He turned to face her fully, rain streaming between them. "There's more. I haven't finished. When I have, you'll know everything I know — I swear that on whatever you like."

Marguerite studied him. In the stuttering light he looked younger than his years and older than his face, both at once, the way tired honest people did. She thought about the locket in the strongbox under her bunk. She thought about a name she had not spoken aloud since the fire.

"Ask the river," she said at last, which made no sense and made perfect sense, and he almost smiled.

* * *

Dawn came gray and diminished, the rain slackening to a steady soak, the river running high and fast but no longer furious. They pumped the last of the water out by eight, and by nine Brammel had jury-rigged a patch over the split seam — canvas, pitch, and a brass plate cannibalized from a spare fitting — and pronounced the Kestrel "seaworthy-ish," which from Brammel was a love letter.

They got underway by ten. The drowned country slid past, fields become lakes, roads become canals, farmhouses standing hip-deep with their families waving from upper windows. Pirro waved back at every one of them. Odile conned them through a shortcut she swore existed, and did, though only for another week before the flood erased it again.

At noon, Marguerite found Tomas at the rail amidships, drying pages of salvaged document in the thin sun, weighted with pebbles, laid out along the deck like strange pale flowers.

"Careful," he said, without looking up. "You're dripping on history."

"The river's dripping on history. I'm just contributing." She crouched beside the row of papers, careful of the wet hem of her coat. Most were official-looking — seals, columns of figures, the stiff formal script of a government that had believed itself permanent. One page near the end was different. Smaller. Handwritten, hurried, the ink feathered where water had found it.

She didn't touch it. But she read what she could see.

*...if the chest cannot reach the north, then let it reach no one. Better lost than...*

The rest was eaten by a brown bloom of water damage, except for one word near the bottom, written larger, pressed harder, as if the writer had needed the pen to carry the weight their voice couldn't:

*Ana.*

Marguerite went very still.

"Captain?" Tomas had noticed. Of course he had. "What is it?"

For a moment she considered lying, or deflecting, or doing any of the hundred small evasions that had kept her alive and alone for nine years. Instead she heard herself say, quietly:

"That handwriting. I know that handwriting."

Tomas sat back on his heels. Around them the Kestrel moved steadily north through the drowned world, and the sun came briefly through the torn clouds and lay a stripe of gold across the drying pages, across the small urgent word, across the space between the two of them that was, Marguerite realized, no longer quite so large as it had been.

"There's something you should tell me," Tomas said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes," Marguerite said, and stood, and looked north up the swollen river toward the hills where the sky was still the color of a held breath. "And there's something you should know about what happens when I do. Because the last person who heard that story —"

She stopped. Downriver, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she had been listening for all morning without knowing it: engines. More than one. Running against the current, which meant they were coming from Marrow Bend, which meant they were behind her, and closing.

"Tomas," she said. "Get those papers below."
