## 7. The Minister's Letter

Tomas Reyes had spent eleven years among paper, and he had learned that paper was never quiet. It rustled with the breath of whoever had held it last. It carried fingerprints in ink, hesitations in the pressure of a nib, lies pressed flat between the lines of truth. The documents from the chest were no different — except that these papers whispered in a dead language of seals and succession, and every hour he spent with them made the whisper louder.

He worked at the little table in the Kestrel's aft cabin, a lamp turned low so the light wouldn't bleed through the porthole. Old habit. In Vesseny's archive, before the fall, he had learned to read by slivers of candle because the archivists who read too openly were the archivists who disappeared. Some habits outlive their countries.

The rain had followed them upriver for two days now, a soft grey insistence against the deck above his head. He could hear Marguerite's footsteps through it — she walked her boat the way other people paced a room, heel to toe along the starboard rail, checking, listening, never still. He had begun to know her by sound alone: the particular weight of her boots, the pause she always made outside this cabin door as if deciding whether to knock.

Tonight she knocked.

"Still awake?" Her voice came through the wood.

"The papers are."

The door opened and she came in shaking rain off her oilskin, bringing the smell of the river with her — silt and diesel and something green, the monsoon breathing down their necks even this far north of the delta. She dropped into the chair across from him without ceremony. That was another thing he'd learned about her: she treated his work like weather. Present, occasionally dangerous, not to be argued with.

"Well?" she said. "You've had three days. Odile's started a betting pool."

"On what?"

"Whether it's treasure maps or tax records. Pirro put two days' wages on treasure."

"It's neither." Tomas set down his magnifier and rubbed his eyes. "It's worse. It's a genealogy."

Marguerite went very still, the way she did when the river changed under the hull. "A what?"

"A line of descent. Certified, witnessed, sealed." He spread the sheets carefully — they were fragile as moth wings, water-stained but intact, the ink of the official hand stubbornly black. "Someone assembled this in the last months before the collapse. Someone who knew the end was coming and wanted proof preserved. Look here — the certification chain. Three witnesses, all of them names I recognize from the court rolls. A registrar of the House of Ashes. This isn't forgery, Marguerite. This is the real thing, hidden in a courier's chest, lost in a flood, and fished out of the Sarelle nine years later by a riverboat captain who wanted to sell it for scrap."

"And?" She wasn't looking at the papers. She was looking at him, and her eyes had gone flat and careful, the way they did when he mentioned Vesseny. "Who does it name?"

He could have softened it. He considered softening it. But he had watched her run this river for weeks now, had seen how she handled hard water — straight on, no easing in — and he thought she would rather be told plainly than kindly.

"The old royal line didn't die out at the fall," he said. "There was a daughter. Second-born, sent north as an infant when the regency started to crack. The documents trace her to a family in the northern settlements. They trace *her* child." He slid the final sheet across the table. "There's a living heir, Marguerite. A girl, by the dates. She'd be sixteen now."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and the engine's patient heartbeat beneath the floorboards.

"No," Marguerite said.

"You haven't asked me anything yet."

"I don't have to. The answer's no. We find the nearest magistrate with a locked drawer, we leave the chest, and we're gone before anyone asks our names." She stood, restless, one hand dragging through her wet hair. "Tomas, do you understand what you've just told me you found? You've told me we're carrying a crown in a box. Crowns don't travel quietly. Crowns come with men who ask questions, and men who stop asking questions when they start shooting."

"That's why I'm telling you at night, in a locked cabin, with the lamp turned down."

She stopped pacing. Something moved behind her face — not fear exactly. Recognition. And that frightened him more than fear would have, because recognition meant the papers had touched something in her she hadn't shown him yet.

"There's a name in the witness list," she said quietly. "Read me the third one again."

He read it. *"Ashgrove, M., attesting."*

"My mother's sister signed that document," Marguerite said. "Or someone used her name to make it look true. Either way—" She cut herself off, jaw tightening, and he understood suddenly that he had crossed a border into country she kept fenced. "Either way it's not mine to carry. Goodnight, Tomas."

She left. Through the door he heard her boots on the wet deck, faster than usual, walking off whatever had just moved through her.

* * *

The letter came two days later, at the fueling dock at Corven's Landing, delivered by a boy on a bicycle who pedaled away before Odile finished signing for it.

Tomas was on deck when Marguerite opened it. He saw her face change — saw the color leave it in stages, like a tide going out — and then she folded the letter once, precisely, and said, "Cabin. Now. Bring the documents."

The letter was written on government stock, heavy cream paper with a watermark Tomas recognized from a hundred intercepted dispatches in his former life. The signature belonged to Minister Edouard Lavelle, Undersecretary for National Continuity — a title that had always struck Tomas as the most honest euphemism in the post-Vesseny bureaucracy, since what Lavelle administered, in practice, was everything the new republic preferred to forget.

*To the master of the vessel Kestrel,* it began.

*It has come to the Ministry's attention that certain materials of historical significance, displaced during the flooding of the lower Sarelle, may currently be aboard your vessel. The Ministry does not question your good faith. Accidents of the river are accidents, and no blame attaches to salvage honestly recovered.*

*The Ministry is prepared to be generous. Full amnesty for any irregularities of registry, cargo, or crew associated with your vessel. Compensation sufficient to purchase a new boat of superior class, free of debt and lien. Safe conduct to any port of your choosing.*

*In exchange: delivery of the recovered materials to the Ministry's representative at Lowport within ten days.*

*You will understand that the alternative to this arrangement serves no one's interests, least of all your own. The Ministry hopes it will not be necessary to discuss alternatives.*

*E. Lavelle*

Silence filled the cabin after Marguerite finished reading it aloud. Outside, gulls argued over the fish market. Inside, the documents lay between them on the table like a patient animal.

"'Irregularities of registry, cargo, or crew,'" Odile repeated, from the doorway where she'd appeared without knocking. Her voice was carefully light. "That's us, that's the chest, and that's your man Reyes with a price on his head. He's offering to wipe all of it clean."

"He knows about Tomas," Marguerite said.

"He knows about everything," Odile said. "That's the point of a letter like this. It's not an offer, it's a demonstration. He's showing us he can see the whole board."

Tomas had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the damp. He knew Lavelle's methods from the archive files — the quiet disappearances, the amnesty-that-wasn't, the safe conduct that ended at a dock with the wrong people waiting. But knowing it and watching Marguerite read it were different things. Because Marguerite wasn't reading it like a threat.

She was reading it like a door.

"A new boat," she said. Not to either of them. To the letter. Her thumb moved over the words *free of debt and lien*, and Tomas watched her mouth shape the word *debt* silently, the way a man tests a sore tooth.

"Marguerite." He kept his voice level. "Lavelle's amnesties are famous. There are forty families in the river towns who took one. Ask any of them where they are now."

"I know what Lavelle is." She looked up, and her eyes were tired in a way he hadn't seen before — tired down at the bottom, where nine years lived. "I also know what my books look like. We're carrying a cracked hull patch, a fuel bill we haven't paid, and a fugitive whose face is on syndicate circulars in every port from here to the delta. One letter buys all of that back. One letter, and the Kestrel stops running."

"The Kestrel doesn't stop," Odile said quietly. It wasn't a slogan; it was a fact, stated the way she'd state the draft of a channel. "That's the rule. Nine years, that's been the rule."

"The rule was mine to make." Marguerite folded the letter closed. "Maybe the rule was just fear wearing a captain's coat."

Nobody answered that. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere forward, Pirro laughed at something, young and unguarded, and the sound passed through the bulkhead like light through water.

Marguerite stood at the porthole a long time, looking out at the grey river. When she spoke again her voice had changed — flatter, more careful, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"If I give Lavelle the chest, he stops hunting. If he stops hunting, you can get off this boat somewhere quiet and disappear properly, instead of hiding in my aft cabin eating my salt pork. If the chest goes to the Ministry, it goes into a vault, and vaults keep things safe better than riverboats do." She turned. "Tell me I'm wrong, Tomas. Tell me the heir is safer with us than in a government vault."

And there it was — the trap inside the temptation, the thing that made the offer clever. Because she wasn't wrong about the danger. A sixteen-year-old girl in the northern settlements, named in those papers, was safer as an unproven rumor than as a documented claim. Handing the documents to Lavelle might protect the girl. It might also bury her claim forever under ministry seal, which was perhaps the same thing, depending on what you believed a person owed to the dead.

"You're not wrong about the danger," Tomas said slowly. "But listen to what the letter doesn't say. Lavelle doesn't say *destroy* the documents. He says *deliver*. A man who wanted them destroyed would say destroy. He wants them because they're valuable — because someone will pay for them, or trade with them, or use them. Which means the heir stays a piece on the board, Marguerite. She just changes hands. And the next hand might be kinder, or might be the syndicate's, and either way she never gets to choose for herself."

"She's sixteen. She doesn't get to choose for herself anyway."

"She gets to choose more than a vault allows."

Marguerite held his gaze. For a moment — a long, suspended moment, the kind of silence a river makes between rapids — he thought she was going to say yes. He saw it happen behind her eyes: the whole easy future assembling itself, a new hull, paid debts, Tomas vanished safely inland, the horizon finally quiet. He saw how much it cost her to want it, and how much it would cost her to take it, and he understood that the offer was working precisely because it wasn't corrupt. It was *kind*. That was what made it lethal.

Then her gaze dropped to the table, to the top sheet of the genealogy, to the witness line she'd asked him to read twice.

*"Ashgrove, M., attesting."*

Her jaw set. Whatever balance had been tipping tipped the other way, and he watched it go, helpless, like watching a boat answer her helm.

"No," she said. "Not to Lavelle. Not to anybody." She picked up the letter, and for a wild moment Tomas thought she was going to tear it, but instead she folded it small and tucked it inside her coat, against her ribs, where a person keeps the things they can't afford to lose and can't bear to look at. "We don't answer. We don't deliver. We don't stop moving."

"Ten days," Odile said. "After ten days he discusses alternatives."

"Then we won't be anywhere he expects in ten days." Marguerite was already moving, captain again, the tiredness shuttered away somewhere deep. "Odile, we take on fuel tonight and run on the morning tide, ahead of schedule. Tomas — the documents sleep in the strongbox from now on, and the strongbox sleeps with you. And nobody—" she paused at the door, and her voice dropped half a tone, "—nobody mentions this letter to the crew. Fear spreads faster than floodwater, and I need my people thinking about the river, not about ministers."

She left. Odile lingered a moment, looking at Tomas with an expression he couldn't quite read — guilt, maybe, or its close cousin — and then followed.

Tomas sat alone with the lamp and the papers. He should have felt relieved. Instead he felt the way he'd felt in the archive, the day he found the first discrepancy in the official history: the cold certainty that a much larger machinery had just become aware of them.

Because he had read the letter four times now, and on the fourth reading he had noticed what he should have noticed first. The letter was dated five days ago. Five days ago, the chest had been aboard the Kestrel for less than a week, sealed, unopened, known only to six people on a riverboat and the floodwater that delivered it.

Which meant Lavelle hadn't learned about the chest from the river.

He had learned about it from someone aboard.

Tomas blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the rain begin again on the deck above, counting the crew in his head like a man counting lifeboats — and finding one too many shadows on the passenger list.
