## 8. Sabotage

Odile heard the engine die before she understood it had died.

She was in the galley at dawn, coaxing a fire out of damp coal, when the rhythm beneath her feet — that deep, patient heartbeat she had slept against for nine years — stuttered once, twice, and stopped. The silence that followed was worse than any storm. A riverboat without its engine is just a coffin with pretensions.

She set down the coffee pot and stood very still, listening the way Marguerite had taught her to listen in the first year: not with the ears but with the soles of the feet, the palms, the small animal brain that knows when a thing is wrong before the mind can say why.

The Kestrel drifted. The current took her gently by the shoulders and began to turn her, slow as a hand turning a page.

"Marguerite," Odile said to the empty galley, and then, louder, up the companionway, "Captain."

* * *

By full light they were all on the engine deck, and the smell told the story before anyone opened the housing. Not the clean iron-and-oil stink of a tired engine but something sour, organic, the smell of the river bottom itself.

Marguerite crouched over the open housing with a lantern held low. Her face, in that yellow light, was the face Odile had learned to read across nine years of hard water — and what she saw there made her stomach drop.

"Silt," Marguerite said. "Packed into the intake screens. Packed, not washed in." She lifted out a handful of grey mud, dense as bread dough, and let it fall back with a wet slap. "The river didn't do this. The river doesn't pack mud like a mason lays brick."

Nobody spoke. Pirro stood at the top of the ladder, half in and half out of the engine deck, his young face going through all the stages of a man realizing he is standing in the wrong place at the wrong time. He was nineteen, maybe twenty — Odile had never been sure; he'd come aboard at Lowport three seasons ago with rope-burned hands and no history, the way most of them came aboard — and he had the particular bad luck of being the newest soul on a boat where the newest soul is always the first suspect.

"It wasn't me," Pirro said.

"I haven't asked you anything yet," Marguerite said, without looking up.

"You were going to."

That was true, and everyone knew it was true, and the knowing sat between them like a fourth person on the deck.

Tomas Reyes came down the ladder last, moving carefully, still not entirely sure of his footing on a working boat after two weeks aboard. He carried one of the Vesseny documents wrapped in oilcloth out of habit now, Odile noticed — the archivist's instinct, guarding papers even when the danger was elsewhere. He looked at the silt, at the packed intake screens, and said quietly: "This would take an hour. Maybe more. Someone would have to work alone, below, while the rest of us slept or stood watch above."

"Thank you for the arithmetic," Odile said, sharper than she meant to.

Tomas glanced at her. He had sharp eyes, the kind that missed nothing, and Odile felt them pass over her face the way a searchlight passes over a hull — briefly, thoroughly, leaving her feeling exposed in a way she could not afford. She turned away and busied herself with the tool locker, which did not need busying.

* * *

They drifted all morning, anchored in a slack backwater off the main channel while the engine came apart piece by piece. It was filthy, slow work. Marguerite directed it with a flatness in her voice that Odile recognized and feared: the captain gone quiet was far more dangerous than the captain shouting.

It was Pirro who worked hardest. That was the thing nobody wanted to see clearly. He stripped to the waist and went into the bilge where the water was cold enough to make a man gasp, and he scraped silt from places no saboteur would bother cleaning, and he did it without complaint for six hours straight, and every hour of it made him look either more guilty or more desperate, depending on how you chose to see him.

Rennick chose to see guilt.

The bosun was an old river man, thick through the shoulders, with a face like a knot in hardwood. He'd served on cargo barges before the Kestrel, and he trusted the river and distrusted everything that came off it, including people. By midday he had planted himself at the foot of the ladder with his arms crossed, watching Pirro work, and by early afternoon he had begun to say things.

"Funny," Rennick said, not loudly, "how the new man finds all the fouled spots first. Like he knows where to look."

"He found them because I sent him to find them," Marguerite said from the housing.

"Funny how the new man volunteers for the cold work too. Eager to be seen scrubbing it out."

"Rennick." Marguerite's voice didn't rise. It never needed to. "Hold your tongue or go sit on the roof until it thaws."

But Rennick had gotten a taste of the suspicion and found it sweet, the way some men do. "We're carrying a fugitive and a stranger who chartered passage north, and the engine dies the same week. And we're meant to believe it's the river's doing?" He spat over the rail. "I say we ask the boy some real questions. Where he slept last night. Who paid him."

"Paid him," Pirro repeated, coming up out of the bilge dripping, his lips blue with cold, and there was something in his voice Odile hadn't heard before — not fear exactly, but the specific fury of a young man who has been poor long enough to know exactly what an accusation costs. "Who paid me. You want to know who pays me, Rennick? You do. This boat does. Three shares a season and my keep, same as you agreed to at Lowport when you shook my hand and called me shipmate."

"That was before—"

"Before what? Before someone filled our engine with mud and you needed somebody to bleed for it?"

"Enough." Marguerite stood. She was not a tall woman, but on her own deck she had a way of seeming to occupy more space than her body allowed. "Here is what we know. We know the engine was fouled deliberately. We know it was done between midnight and dawn. We know that whoever did it knew this boat well enough to find the intakes blind, in the dark, without a lantern." She paused, and let the pause do its work. "Which means it wasn't done by a stranger. And it wasn't done by a boy who's been aboard three seasons and still asks permission to use the good lamp. So the next word out of anyone's mouth about Pirro will be an apology, or it will be their last word aboard the Kestrel. Am I understood?"

Silence. Rennick's jaw worked. Odile watched him decide — watched the moment when the old loyalty to the boat outweighed the sweeter poison of suspicion, barely — and he gave a short nod and turned away to coiling rope that didn't need coiling.

"And I'll say this once more for the whole crew," Marguerite went on, quieter now, which somehow carried further. "Nobody on this boat is guilty because they're convenient. If there's a traitor among us, we'll find them by evidence, not by appetite. Pirro — get dry. You've earned a hot meal and I'll bring it to you myself."

Pirro blinked. Odile saw the boy try to hold onto his anger and fail, saw it crumble into something younger and rawer underneath. "I didn't do it, Captain," he said, and his voice cracked on *captain*, and Marguerite put one hand briefly on his wet shoulder and left it there a moment longer than she needed to.

"I know," she said. "Now get dry before you die of pride and pneumonia both."

* * *

Odile brought the meal herself, actually, because Marguerite got pulled back to the engine and Odile volunteered before she thought about why.

She found Pirro in the bow, wrapped in a blanket, watching the backwater. She handed him the bowl — rice and salt pork, the good pork, not the ration stuff — and sat down beside him without asking, the way you do on a small boat where privacy is a fiction anyway.

"Eat," she said.

He ate. For a while neither of them spoke. The backwater was quiet in the way that only river backwaters are quiet — not silent, but murmurous, alive with small sounds that add up to peace. Somewhere a bird Odile couldn't name was making an argument with another bird about something.

"You didn't deserve that," Odile said finally. "What Rennick said. What all of us were thinking."

"Were you thinking it?" Pirro asked. He didn't sound accusing. He sounded genuinely curious, which was worse.

"Yes," Odile said. "For about an hour. Then I remembered what it's like to be suspected of something you didn't do, and I stopped."

Pirro looked at her sideways. "You been accused of something?"

"Once. Long time ago. Different life." She picked a splinter of wood off the deck planking and flicked it into the water. "A woman I worked for lost a strongbox. I was the only one who'd been near her office. Nobody asked questions. They just decided, the way people do, because deciding is easier than finding out. I lost the position. I nearly lost worse." She had, in fact, lost considerably worse than that, though the details belonged to debts and names she kept locked below her own waterline, and she was not going to open that hatch today. "So. I'm sorry. For the hour."

Pirro shrugged, but she saw the shrug cost him something to perform. "It's not you I mind. It's—" He stopped. Started again. "I came aboard because I heard the Kestrel takes people as they are. No papers, no past required. Just work and loyalty. That's worth more than wages, you know? And now there's a traitor on board, and everybody's looking around, and I keep thinking — if they decide it's me, I don't just lose a job. I lose the only place that ever took me as I am."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Nineteen years old and already he understood the exact shape of the thing. A boat isn't a boat. A boat is the last place that will have you.

"They won't decide it's you," she said. "The captain's already decided otherwise, and the captain's decisions are load-bearing on this vessel. You could take that to the bank."

He almost smiled. "Is that a river expression?"

"That's an Odile expression. They don't travel well."

* * *

But Odile did not sleep well that night, and not because of Pirro.

She lay in her bunk listening to the repaired engine turn over — Marguerite had run it up to half power by dusk, testing, and the heartbeat was back, steady under the hull — and she thought about who *had* done it. Because someone had. Someone aboard, or someone who had come aboard recently enough to learn the boat's dark anatomy.

Voss. The thought arrived without ceremony, the way unwelcome guests do. Callum Voss, the charming merchant with the soft hands and the charter north, who laughed at the right moments and asked careful questions disguised as idle curiosity. *How fast does she run light? How deep her draft? Does the captain ever take the gorge route?* Odile had signed him aboard herself. Had taken his coin herself. Had not mentioned it to Marguerite, because mentioning it would have meant explaining the debt that made his coin necessary, and the debt was a door Odile had nailed shut years ago.

Had Voss fouled the engine? It didn't quite fit. What would a syndicate agent gain from stranding them in a backwater? Slowing them down, maybe. Keeping them on the river, in reach, instead of losing them in the northern hills. Or — colder thought — making the crew tear itself apart looking for a traitor, so that when the real move came they'd be too busy suspecting each other to suspect him.

Or maybe it wasn't Voss at all. Maybe it was someone using Voss's arrival as cover. Maybe it was—

No. She was not going to stand in that particular courtroom and start trying defendants. She had spent nine years learning that guilt is a country you can't navigate from inside your own hull.

Still, sleep did not come easily, and when it came it brought the old dream: the counting house, the missing ledger, the woman's voice saying *we both know it was you, Odile* — except in the dream the voice was Marguerite's, and Odile woke with her heart slamming and lay in the dark listening to the engine beat, beat, beat, and told herself the lie she had gotten good at telling: *the past stays past if you row fast enough.*

Except the flood had already proved otherwise. The river had reached down into its own drowned past and handed up a chest stamped with a dead nation's seal, and now the past was sitting in their hold in oilcloth, and the engine was full of silt, and somewhere ahead a gorge waited with hired boats in it, though she didn't know that yet — she only knew the feeling of it, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth.

* * *

Near dawn she went up on deck because sleep had given her up entirely, and found Marguerite at the wheel, though they were anchored and there was nothing to steer.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Odile asked.

"The engine's running too clean," Marguerite said.

Odile waited. With Marguerite you learned to wait; the second sentence always came, and it was always the important one.

"When I rebuilt the intake this afternoon, I checked the packing on the shaft seal. Someone had loosened it. Not enough to leak — not yet. Enough to fail later, under strain, somewhere it would matter." Marguerite's hands rested on the wheel spokes lightly, the way a mother rests a hand on a sleeping child. "Two acts of sabotage, Odile. One loud, to be discovered. One quiet, to be survived until it kills us."

Odile felt the cold come up through the deck plates. "In the gorge."

"In the gorge. Under strain, at speed, with walls on both sides." Marguerite turned her head, and in the pre-dawn grey her eyes were the color of deep water. "So whoever did this isn't trying to strand us. They're trying to sink us, and they want us to think we fixed the problem first." She looked forward, upriver, toward the haze where the channel bent north. "Get some sleep, Odile. Tomorrow we run, and tomorrow night I want eyes on everyone — including our charming passenger. Especially him."

Odile nodded and turned to go, and stopped, because there was a third figure at the rail that neither of them had marked — Callum Voss, in his dressing gown, apparently out for the same sleepless air, smiling his easy smile at the waking river.

"Lovely hour," Voss said pleasantly. "The birds are extraordinary up here."

"Extraordinary," Marguerite agreed, and her hand did not leave the wheel, and Odile walked below with the cold still in her bones, wondering which of them the smile was for — and hating herself, a little, for the coin in her pocket that made the question hers to answer.
