## 9. Callum's Ledger

Tomas Reyes had spent eleven years among paper, and paper had taught him one lesson above all others: people lie with their mouths and their faces, but their hands betray them in ink.

He was thinking about this on the third night out of Lowport, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the Kestrel's aft hold with the Vesseny documents spread around him like the petals of some enormous, poisonous flower. The lamp was turned low — Marguerite's rule, to keep the light from showing through the seams of the hull at night — and by its amber glow he worked his way through the cipher that had defeated him for two weeks and then, tonight, surrendered all at once, the way ciphers do: not gradually but catastrophically, a single misjudged assumption collapsing and the whole structure of nonsense resolving into plain language as if it had never been anything else.

The key had been liturgical. Of course it had been. The archivists of Vesseny had encrypted their most dangerous records in the calendar of saints' days observed by the old royal chapel, and Tomas had spent years of his exile memorizing that calendar because there had been nothing else to do in the long winters of the northern settlements except memorize things. Saint Aldric's feast. The octave of the Dedication. The fast of the Nine Sorrows. He had thought the knowledge useless, a dead man's trivia rattling in a living skull, and now it lay open before him like a door.

He should have felt triumphant. Instead he felt cold, because the first fully decoded page was not about succession at all.

It was a manifest.

*Item: sealed chest, royal provenance, recovered from flood debris near Kiln Bend. Item: bearer of same, female, riverine, known associate of the Reyes archive. Item: disposition — transfer at the Gorge of Sarelle-Vashti to syndicate custody, force authorized if persuasion fails.*

Force authorized. He read the phrase three times, hoping it would mean something gentler on the third pass. It did not. And beneath it, in a different hand — a hand he recognized from the syndicate correspondence he had catalogued during his last desperate months in the capital, before the burning and the running — a note appended like a signature to a death warrant:

*Voss confirms vessel will transit gorge no later than the twelfth. Passengers unaware. Payment on delivery.*

Tomas sat very still. Somewhere above him, through two decks of old pine, he could hear the Kestrel's nightly music: Odile's boots crossing the wheelhouse, Pirro laughing at something down in the engine room, the soft percussion of the river against the hull. Callum Voss was on watch. Voss, who charmed Pirro with card tricks and brought Odile coffee and told Marguerite stories about the northern trade routes with such easy, generous fluency. Voss, whose handwriting Tomas had just spent an hour matching against the ledger, letter by letter, the way you match a face to a wanted notice.

*Passengers unaware.*

He gathered the pages together with hands that were not quite steady, squared them into their oilcloth wrapping, and went to find Marguerite Ashgrove.

* * *

She was not in the wheelhouse. He found her, instead, in the strangest place he had yet discovered aboard her boat: a narrow strip of open deck behind the funnel where she kept three clay pots of herbs and a folding chair, her single concession to anything resembling a life lived in place. She was sitting in the chair with a mug of chicory going cold beside her, watching the black water slide past, and when he came through the hatch she looked up without surprise, as if she had been waiting for someone to bring her bad news and had simply been placing bets on which messenger.

"You've cracked it," she said. Not a question.

"The cipher, yes." He lowered himself onto the deck across from her, knees complaining. "Marguerite, I need you to listen to all of it before you do anything."

Her eyes narrowed slightly. "That's how you know it's bad, when a man asks me to promise my behavior in advance."

"Please."

A long moment. The river said what rivers say, which is nothing and everything. Then she nodded once, and he told her.

He told her about the liturgical key, quickly, because she didn't care about that. He told her about the manifest, slowly, because she did. He watched her face while he spoke, and it was remarkable mostly for how little it moved — a stillness like deep water, like the pools below a weir where the current goes quiet and something large waits underneath. When he reached *force authorized*, her jaw tightened, a small tectonic event. When he reached Voss's name, nothing moved at all.

"Say the last part again," she said.

"*Voss confirms vessel will transit gorge no later than the twelfth.*"

"The twelfth." She looked out at the water. "That's four days. We'd make the gorge in five, six if the rain holds." A pause, and then, with terrible mildness: "Unless someone aboard is planning to slow us down. Or speed us up. Or steer us somewhere we weren't asked to go."

"Odile doesn't know," Tomas said. "I don't think Odile knows. Voss wrote *passengers unaware* — plural, general. If Odile were his, he wouldn't need to write it down."

"If Odile were his, he'd be a fool to write it down, and fools don't run ledgers for the syndicate." Marguerite picked up the cold chicory, considered it, and drank it anyway. "No. This reads like a man working alone inside our walls. Which means either he's very good or we're very lucky, and I stopped believing in luck nine years ago."

"What do we do?" It came out smaller than he intended. He was an archivist; he dealt in truths already fallen, in histories that could no longer be changed. Decisions that still had living consequences were another country, and he spoke its language badly.

Marguerite set down the mug. For a while she didn't answer, and Tomas understood that she wasn't deciding — she was already decided, and the silence was her taking the measure of how much to tell even him.

"We do nothing," she said.

"Nothing?"

"We smile at him. We feed him. Pirro keeps showing him his card tricks, and Voss keeps pretending to lose." Her voice was level, almost pleasant, and that pleasantness frightened Tomas more than shouting would have. "We let him believe his ledger is a secret, because the moment he believes otherwise, he sends word ahead — and there are places along this stretch where a man can get a message to a fast skiff, and I can't watch every dock. Right now the syndicate thinks they have us blind. That's worth more than throwing one clerk overboard."

"He's not a clerk. He's the man who's going to hand us to them at the gorge."

"Yes." She said it without heat, and that absence of heat was its own kind of terrifying. "And the gorge is narrow, and the water this season runs mean, and any boats they bring will have hired pilots who don't know the Sarelle the way I do. Let them come to the gorge expecting to find a fat, unsuspecting cargo boat. Let them plan around the boat we appear to be." She finally turned to look at him directly, and in the low lamplight spilling from the wheelhouse her eyes were the color of wet slate. "I'm not angry, Tomas. Anger is expensive and I can't afford it right now. What I am is *interested*. Because a man like Voss doesn't write *passengers unaware* unless part of him needs to believe it. And men who need to believe comfortable lies are men who can be made to believe truer ones."

"That's a gamble."

"Everything is a gamble. The only choice is which odds you take." She rose, joints cracking, and stood at the rail looking north, toward the dark line of hills where the river narrowed and waited. "We say nothing to the crew. Not yet. Odile has enough ghosts; I won't hand her another until I know whether it's hers. Pirro's too young to carry it. You and I hold this between us, and we watch, and we wait for the gorge."

"And if Voss moves first? If he signals them early?"

"Then I'll deal with him at whatever speed the situation requires." Something flickered under the stillness then — not anger exactly, but its ancestor, banked and breathing. "But hear me, Tomas, because this is the part that matters. I've thrown people off this boat before. Men who stole, men who lied about worse than ledgers. Every time, I told myself it was necessity, and every time, the river remembered it longer than I wanted it to. So Voss gets one thing first: he gets the chance to be better than his orders. People surprise you, if you give them room to fall in the right direction." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Nine years on the water teaches you that much, if nothing else."

Tomas looked at her — this woman who had lost a nation and a family and a name, who slept with her boots by the door and her heart behind triple glass — and he realized that what frightened him was not her coldness. It was that underneath the strategy, patient as sediment, there was hope. She genuinely believed a traitor might turn. She had built her whole life on the wager that people could be carried rather than condemned.

"And if he doesn't turn?" Tomas asked quietly. "If he's exactly what his ledger says he is?"

Marguerite Ashgrove watched the water for a long time before she answered.

"Then at the gorge," she said, "the Sarelle and I will have a conversation about him. And the river always wins those."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the watch had changed and the boat had gone quiet except for the engine's heartbeat below — Tomas climbed to the wheelhouse roof with the decoded pages wrapped against his chest, meaning to check the stars and clear his head. He found the hatch to the cargo deck ajar, and through the gap, lamplight.

Callum Voss sat alone amid the freight, a traveling desk across his knees, writing. His pen moved quickly, confidently, a merchant recording figures. And as Tomas watched, frozen in the dark of the companionway, Voss finished the page, blotted it, folded it with practiced economy into a shape no bigger than a coin, and slipped it into the lining of his coat — glancing once at the closed hatch, once at the ceiling, once at the river sliding past the open gunwale, with the careful, habitual thoroughness of a man who had been doing this his whole life and had never once been caught.

Tomas drew back into the shadow and did not breathe until Voss doused the lamp.

Four days to the gorge. One letter already sewn into a coat, bound for whoever the syndicate kept on this stretch of water. And somewhere below, asleep in her cabin, Marguerite Ashgrove dreaming her restless dreams, believing she had bought herself time.

The gorge, Tomas thought, was going to come sooner than any of them planned.
