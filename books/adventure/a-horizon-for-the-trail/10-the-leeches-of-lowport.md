## 10. The Leeches of Lowport

Lowport announced itself the way a sick man announces himself: by smell. Sulfur from the tanneries upstream, tar and rot from the fish yards, and beneath it all the sweetish stink of standing water that had nowhere to go. Marguerite smelled it a full mile before the harbor came into view, and she stood at the wheel a long moment, one hand on the spokes, weighing it.

"We could push through," Pirro said beside her. He'd taken to standing watch at her shoulder since the sabotage, as if proximity to the wheel could keep the engine turning. "Patch her at the next landing. There's a yard at Corren's Bend, another two days up."

"Two days with a cracked strake and a bilge pump running every hour?" She shook her head. "She takes water faster than we take her out. Lowport it is."

Odile came up from below, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last season. "Lowport," she repeated, and there was something in her voice Marguerite couldn't place. Not reluctance exactly. Something closer to recognition.

"You know it?"

"I grew up forty miles from it. My brother ran cargo out of there before the collapse." Odile looked at the town as it resolved out of the haze — a low sprawl of tin roofs and drying nets, the harbor a brown crescent cupped between two stone jetties. "It's changed hands since. Everything has."

The Kestrel came in on the morning tide, sliding past the breakwater with her usual grace, and Marguerite felt the old familiar easing in her chest that any harbor gave her — the sense, always brief, always a lie, of having arrived somewhere. She knew better. Harbors were just the river holding its breath.

The harbormaster met them at the customs shed, a heavy man in a coat too fine for the climate, silver chains across his vest and a smile that arrived a half-second before his eyes did. He introduced himself as Master Ferren, and he shook Marguerite's hand with both of his, warm and damp.

"The famous Kestrel. We don't see many boats your size willing to run this stretch in monsoon season." His gaze slid past her to the deck, cataloguing — the patched rail, the tarp over the engine housing, the faces of her crew. It lingered, just a moment, on the hatch where the chest was stowed, and Marguerite watched him do it, and said nothing.

"Business is business," she said. "We need a slip, a yard crew, and new oakum. Three days, no more."

"Three days." Ferren smiled again. "For you, Captain, the best slip on the north wall. Deep water, easy access." He said *easy access* the way another man might have said *no witnesses*, and Marguerite filed it away with all the other small wrongnesses: the customs men who didn't ask for her papers, the yard boss who quoted her a price without haggling, the two men drinking at the Anchorline who stopped talking when she walked in and started again when she walked out.

The syndicate had a long reach. She'd known that since the man in the reeds. She'd simply hoped Lowport was beyond it.

Hope, she had learned nine years ago, was a cargo that never paid its freight.

* * *

The work went fast, which was its own kind of warning. By the second afternoon the yard crew had the cracked strake off and a new one steamed and fitted, and the Kestrel sat in her slip looking better than she had in two years. Marguerite should have been pleased. Instead she stood in the shadow of the customs shed and counted the things that didn't fit.

The harbormaster's men had taken a sudden interest in the north wall. Not the whole harbor — just the slip where the Kestrel lay. Two of them at the jetty's foot, day and night, playing cards by lantern light. A third who "inspected" the harbor chain twice a shift. And at the Anchorline, a table of men who drank from noon to midnight and never seemed to get drunk, whose boots were river boots and whose hands were not workingmen's hands.

She was counting them for the fourth time when Odile found her.

"You've seen it too," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"I've seen something. I don't know what yet."

"I know what." Odile leaned against the shed wall, arms crossed, and for once she didn't look away. "The harbormaster. Ferren. He's syndicate now, or syndicate-adjacent — bought or leased, I couldn't tell you which. I heard it from a woman who runs fish off the north quay. She said Ferren's been telling people a boat was coming. A riverboat with a fugitive aboard, and that there'd be money in it for whoever helped hold her at the wall."

Marguerite felt the words settle over her like cold water. "When did you hear this?"

"Last night. I've been sitting on it, trying to work out if the woman was selling me a story or selling me true." Odile's jaw tightened. "I should have come to you straight off. I know that. I just — I wanted to be sure before I put a knife in your hand."

"Odile." Marguerite turned to face her fully. "You could have come to me with a rumor about my hat and I'd have wanted it straight off. That's what first mate means."

Something in Odile's shoulders loosened, and Marguerite realized she'd been watching her first mate carry the weight of it alone for a full day, and that the weight had been heavier for the carrying alone. "All right," Odile said. "Straight off, then. We're boxed in. The harbor chain comes up at night — Ferren's orders, he says it's against smugglers — and there are six of them at the Anchorline and four more he can call. We've got Pirro, a boy who fights like a boy, and Reyes, who fights like a book. And you and me."

"And the river," Marguerite said.

Odile almost smiled. "And the river."

* * *

They planned it in the engine room, where the hammering of the yard crew finishing the last seams would cover their voices. Tomas sat on an upturned crate with the ship's charts spread across his knees, and Marguerite watched his finger move along the channel below the north wall and felt the plan take shape the way a river takes shape — not designed but discovered.

"The chain," Tomas said. "It comes up at night?"

"So Ferren says," Marguerite said. "Which means it comes up slow, or it comes up partway, because chains like that are old and the winches are older. Odile?"

"Older," Odile confirmed. "My brother used to slip boats past it. There's a gap on the east side, where the chain sags between the third and fourth float. At low water you can clear it with a shallow draft and a steady hand. The Kestrel draws four feet. It's tight."

"Tight how?" Pirro asked. He was coiling rope with the excessive care of someone trying to look busy and useful at once.

"Tight like if you sneeze we lose the rudder," Odile said. "And past the chain it gets worse. The channel below the wall is a maze at night — sandbars move with every flood, and there's a stretch the locals call the Needle's Eye where the water funnels between two shoals. My brother ran it by memory and by ear. You listen for the water. Where it hisses, there's a bar. Where it's silent, it's deep."

"By ear," Marguerite said. "In the dark. In monsoon rain."

"I can do it," Odile said. "I grew up on this water. I haven't run it in nine years, but the river doesn't forget, and neither do I."

Marguerite looked at her — really looked, at the woman who had been at her side through sandbars and storms and nine years of never stopping, who had hidden a passenger from her and carried a secret alone and was now offering to steer them through a channel that had drowned better pilots than either of them. She thought about what it cost a person to offer that. She thought about how much of her own life had been built on people offering exactly that and meaning it.

"Then we run tonight," she said. "Before Ferren's friends stop drinking and start working."

* * *

They went out on the ebb, two hours past midnight, in a rain that fell with the patience of something that intended to go on forever. Marguerite had the wheel. Odile stood at the bow rail with a lantern shuttered to a slit, and Pirro was at the engine telegraph, and Tomas crouched by the hawse hole watching the black water slide past, and the Kestrel moved out of her slip without a single light showing, a ghost in her own harbor.

The rain helped. It kept the men at the Anchorline under their awning, kept the customs men huddled in the shed, kept every eye in Lowport aimed down instead of out. But it also filled the channel with noise — rain on water, water on water, the hiss and churn of a river in flood — and Marguerite knew that Odile's ear was going to have to be very good indeed.

"Slow ahead," Marguerite said, and Pirro rang it down, and the engine dropped to a murmur.

The north wall came up on their left, a black mass wet with rain. The chain was where Ferren's men had said it would be, a low line of floats just visible in the dark, and Marguerite held the Kestrel against the ebb and waited while Odile leaned out over the bow rail, reading the water.

"East side," Odile said quietly. "Come left. Easy. *Easy.*"

Marguerite came left. The floats slid past on the starboard side, close enough that she could have reached out and touched one, and then the bow dipped and the hull shuddered — a long, grinding kiss against the chain's sagging span, oak on iron, a sound like a held breath — and then they were through, and the Kestrel was in open water, and Marguerite let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the customs shed.

"Nicely done," she said.

"Don't praise me yet," Odile said. "The Needle's Eye is ahead."

The channel below the wall was everything Odile had promised and less that was good. The rain came harder. The current, running strong on the ebb, pushed them toward shoals that showed themselves only as a change in the sound of the water — a hiss where the river dragged over sand, a silence where it ran deep. Odile called them by ear, her voice low and even, and Marguerite steered to her voice the way she'd once steered to the stars.

"Starboard. Hard starboard — there's a bar off the point. Now ease. *Ease.* Come back left. Listen — you hear it? The hiss, off the port bow? That's the shoal. Give it a boat's length. More. *More.*"

Marguerite gave it more. The Kestrel threaded the gap with inches to spare on either side, and in the wheelhouse the only sounds were the rain, the engine, and Odile's voice, and Marguerite thought, with a clarity that felt like grief and gratitude at once: *this is what we are. This is what we've been all along. Not a captain and a crew. A single animal with many hands.*

Then the lantern flared at the harbor mouth behind them.

It was a signal light — three flashes, red, then white — and Marguerite saw it reflected in the wet glass of the wheelhouse windows, and she knew what it meant. Ferren had woken up. Or one of his men had, and the harbor was waking with him, and somewhere back there a boat was being launched or a chain was being winched or a gun was being loaded, and none of it mattered, because none of it could catch them now.

"Full ahead," she said.

Pirro rang it down. The Kestrel surged, and the Needle's Eye opened before them like a door.

* * *

They ran dark for six miles, until the rain thinned and the channel widened and the lights of Lowport were a smudge of orange behind them, and then Marguerite brought her to a stop in the lee of a willow island and let the engine idle, and the crew came up on deck and stood in the wet and looked at each other and started, helplessly, to laugh.

It was Pirro who started it — a high, cracked sound of pure relief — and then Odile, who laughed the way she did everything, thoroughly and without apology, and then Tomas, whose laugh was quiet and surprised, as if it had been stored away somewhere and he'd forgotten the key. Marguerite held out the longest, because she was the captain and captains don't laugh until the boat is safe, but the boat was safe, and she laughed too, and the rain fell on all of them, and it was the finest sound she'd heard in nine years.

"We did it," Pirro said. "We actually — did anyone else hear the chain? We *hit* the chain, and we—"

"We kissed it," Odile said. "The Kestrel kissed the chain and apologized after. That's how it's done."

Tomas had gone quiet. Marguerite noticed it the way she noticed everything about him now — a half-second behind the noticing, a half-second too late to pretend she hadn't. He was looking back at the smudge of Lowport's lights, and his face had the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"What is it?" she asked.

"Three flashes," he said. "Red, then white. I've seen that signal before. In the syndicate correspondence I decoded — the Voss letters. It's their harbor signal. It means *the package is moving, intercept downstream.*" He turned to her. "Marguerite. They weren't trying to keep us in Lowport. They were trying to keep us *there* until someone could come up from below. We didn't escape the trap. We just left it early."

Marguerite looked back at the smudge of lights. Somewhere downriver, in the dark, there would be a boat — or boats — with syndicate men aboard, running up to meet them, and between here and the gorge there were a hundred miles of channel and a dozen landings, and any one of them could hold a net.

"Then we don't stop," she said. "We don't stop at Corren's Bend, we don't stop at any landing, we don't stop to take on water or wood or anything else. We run straight through to the gorge, and we run it in daylight, and we run it fast." She turned to Pirro. "Can the engine take it?"

Pirro's chin came up. "She can take it if I can."

"Odile — the channel from here to the gorge. You know it?"

"I know it well enough to know it's mean," Odile said. "But mean I can handle. It's patient I can't."

"Then we won't be patient." Marguerite put her hand on the wheel, and the rain came down, and somewhere behind them the lights of Lowport were going out one by one as the town gave up on the night, and ahead of them the river ran black and wide and fast toward the gorge, and she felt the old restlessness in her chest change its shape at last — not the itch of a woman running from something, but the lean of a woman running toward.

"Full ahead," she said again, and the Kestrel answered, and the dark water took them.
