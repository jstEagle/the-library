## 11. What Odile Owes

The Kestrel lay at anchor two miles below Lowport, in a backwater where the willows grew thick enough to hide her silhouette from the channel. Odile had chosen the spot herself — she knew every bend of the Sarelle between here and the delta, knew which cut-offs held deep water and which held nothing but mud and mosquitoes. She had grown up on this river, before the collapse, before Vesseny became a word people said quietly, the way you said the name of a dead man who'd died badly.

Now she stood at the rail of the aft deck and watched Marguerite come toward her across the boards, and every one of those years sat in her chest like a stone swallowed whole.

Marguerite had not shouted. That was the worst of it. When Pirro had come running up from the engine room with the story — silt packed into the intake strainer, packed by a hand, not by the river — Marguerite had gone very still, the way she did when the water did something wrong, and then she had looked at each of them in turn. At Pirro, whose eyes were red-rimmed with the injustice of being suspected. At Tomas, who had been aboard barely three weeks. At Callum Voss, who had smiled his merchant's smile and offered to help clear the strainer himself. And last at Odile.

She had looked at Odile longest. Not accusing. Reading. The way she read the river — the way a line of foam told her where the sandbar slept, the way a certain green in the current meant floodwater coming down from the high country. She had read Odile like that, and whatever she'd seen had made her jaw tighten once, and then she'd said only: *Odile. With me. After we anchor.*

*After we anchor.* Which meant now.

"You wanted the willow bank," Marguerite said, stopping beside her at the rail. "Good holding ground?"

"Clay bottom. Holds all season." Odile kept her eyes on the water. A kingfisher went down the slough like a thrown blue dart. "I fished this stretch with my father. Before."

"I know."

Of course she knew. Nine years together, and Marguerite Ashgrove forgot nothing anyone ever told her; she just filed it away in that quiet ledger of hers and never let on what page it was written on. Odile had always found that comforting. Tonight it felt like standing in front of an open strongbox.

"Say it," Marguerite said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been carrying since Voss came aboard. Whatever made your voice do that thing it does when you're lying about something small — the mooring fee at Tarnholt, whether there was coffee left. I know that voice, Odile. I've heard it maybe six times in nine years, and I have never once liked what was under it."

The river moved past below them, brown-green and patient. Somewhere upstream a woodpecker knocked against a dead trunk, three beats, pause, three beats. Odile discovered that her hands had closed around the rail hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and she made herself let go.

"There's a man in Lowport," she said. "Name of Ferren Brac. He holds paper on me."

She felt Marguerite go still beside her. Not moving away. Not moving closer. Just still, the way the Kestrel went still when Marguerite killed the engines to listen for trouble under the sound of them.

"Gambling?" Marguerite asked.

"Cargo." Odile laughed, and it came out ugly. "That's the joke of it. Everyone assumes cards or dice or a woman. No. It was cargo. Three years ago, winter run, I took a charter south with a hold full of dyewood, and the buyer in Port Sarelle offered me double to sell it to him instead of my charter client. Double, cash, no questions. And I was — " She stopped. Made herself start again. "My sister's boy was sick that year. Bad lungs. The doctors in the delta wanted money I didn't have, and the money I *did* have wasn't going to be enough no matter how many runs I made, so when a man offers you double, cash, no questions, you don't think about the client. You think about the boy coughing blood into a rag."

"And you took it."

"I took it. And the client — a grain factor out of Tarnholt, decent man, never did anything to me — he lost his shipment and nearly lost his business, and he talked, because that's what factors do, and the syndicate's people heard the talk. Brac bought the debt off the dyewood buyer. He's been holding it ever since. Interest like a leech. Every year it grows. Every year he finds me in some port and smiles at me and says, *Miss Marchand, how is the river treating you.*" She could hear Brac's voice even now, soft and unhurried, a man who had never once needed to raise it because he owned time itself. "He doesn't want the money, Marguerite. He wants the hook in me. A first mate on the Kestrel who owes the syndicate — do you understand what that's worth? More than the debt. Ten times more."

Marguerite said nothing. Below them, the anchor chain creaked as the current leaned on the hull.

"So when Voss came to me at the Tarnholt dock," Odile went on, because if she stopped now she would never start again, "and said he'd pay off Brac — all of it, principal and interest, a clean slate, paper signed and burned — all I had to do was make sure he got passage north without you asking too many questions — I said yes." Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "I forged your signature on the passenger manifest. I told you he'd been referred by the grain factor's office, which was true in the sense that Brac's people arranged the referral. And I have been watching him watch you for three weeks, telling myself that whatever he's after, it can't be that bad, telling myself I'd warn you if it got serious, and then Pirro pulled that strainer full of silt and I watched your face while you looked at us, and I thought — she thinks it's one of us. She thinks someone on this boat is against her. And she has no idea how right she is."

The words ran out. There was silence after them, wide as the slough, and Odile stood in it and waited for the blow.

It didn't come. That was somehow worse.

* * *

Marguerite turned away from the rail and walked to the stern bench and sat down on it, slowly, the way a woman sits when her legs have decided independently that they're done holding her. Odile stayed where she was. She deserved to stand.

"Three weeks," Marguerite said at last. Her voice was level. Too level. Odile had heard that voice once before, seven years ago, when a lockkeeper had tried to cheat them on fuel and Marguerite had negotiated him down to half price with a smile fixed on her face like paint. "Three weeks I've had a syndicate agent sleeping twelve feet from my cabin. Eating at my table. Helping bail water in the monsoon. And my own first mate put him there."

"Yes."

"I trusted you with the boat, Odile. Not just the cargo, not just the crew. The *boat*. Do you know what that means to me? You're the only person alive I've ever let steer her alone."

"I know." It came out as a whisper.

"Do you?" Marguerite looked up at her then, and her eyes were terrible — not hot, not furious, but cold and clear and hurt all the way down, the way deep water is cold and clear. "Because I don't think you do. I think you think this is about money. About a debt. About a bad decision three years ago. But it isn't. It's about the fact that when I close my eyes at night, the only reason I can sleep is that I know exactly who is on this boat and exactly where they stand. Every one of them. Pirro, who'd die before he lied to me and probably would lie badly anyway. Renna, who complains about everything and hides nothing. Even Voss — well. Now I know about Voss. But you, Odile. You were the keel. You were the thing I didn't check, because checking you never occurred to me. And you let a stranger buy that."

Each sentence landed like a stone dropped from a height. Odile took them. She did not defend herself, because there was nothing to defend. She had run the arithmetic herself a hundred times in the dark watches and it always came out the same: she had sold the safest thing on the river for the price of her own fear.

"He showed me the papers," she said quietly. "Brac's debt, and Voss's draft against the syndicate's Lowport house. It was all real. That's the devil of it, Marguerite — none of it was a trick. He really did buy my freedom. He just also bought me."

"And what does he think he bought? What did he tell you he wanted?"

"Passage north. Nothing else, he said. He said the syndicate has commercial interests in the northern settlements and needed a discreet boat." Odile shook her head. "I didn't believe it even when I agreed to it. Men like Voss don't spend that kind of money on discretion. But I told myself it wasn't my problem. I told myself I'd been solving other people's problems with my honesty for nine years and it was someone else's turn." She heard how it sounded the moment it left her mouth. "That's not even true. I just wanted the weight off. You spend long enough under a debt and you stop caring what lifts it."

For a long moment Marguerite didn't answer. The woodpecker upstream had gone silent. The light was failing in the willows, the day draining down to that grey-green hour when the river seemed to hold its breath.

Then Marguerite laughed. One short, hard note, with nothing in it of humor.

"You know what I was doing nine years ago tonight? Roughly. This season." She didn't wait for an answer. "I was in Vesseny, in the last week. My family's estate was already burning — I've told you that part, everyone gets the fire, everyone gets the smoke. What I don't tell anyone is what I took when I ran. There was a strongbox in my father's study. Papers. Deeds, letters, things that belonged to the crown, things my family had been keeping safe for generations because that's what the Ashgroves *did*, they were keepers of paper, boring honorable people who died for their filing habits. And I could have taken it. I could have carried it north and done my duty and maybe — maybe — saved something of what was falling. Instead I sold the box's iron to a scrap dealer in the border towns and used the money to buy a share in a leaky riverboat, because I wanted to be anywhere but where I owed anything to anyone." Her hands were flat on her knees, pressing down, as if the memory were trying to lift her off the bench. "I have spent nine years telling myself that was survival. Some nights I almost believe it."

"Marguerite — "

"No. Let me finish, because I'm going to say this once and then never again." She stood. In the dying light her face was half shadow, and Odile thought she had never looked less like a captain and more like what she actually was underneath: a woman who had outrun a country and never once caught her breath. "You asked the river for forgiveness by drowning yourself in work and never saying a word, and I asked it by never stopping moving, and neither of us got it, because that's not how debts work. They don't drown. They swim alongside. Waiting."

* * *

Odile found she was crying. Not sobbing — she hadn't sobbed since she was a child — but the tears came anyway, steady and silent, and she let them, because hiding anything else from this woman tonight would have been one lie too many.

"I'll go to Brac," she said. "Tomorrow, at Lowport. I'll give myself to him if that's what clears it. He'd take the trade — a first mate's testimony about this boat, about the chest, about Reyes — "

"Stop." Marguerite crossed the deck and took Odile's face in both hands, the way she had once, years ago, steadied a young deckhand having a seizure on the cargo deck — firm, ungentle, entirely present. "Look at me. You are not giving that man anything. Not your testimony, not your guilt, and certainly not yourself. Do you hear me? If Brac owns a piece of you, then we're going to take that piece back, and we're going to do it my way, which means nobody walks into Lowport and surrenders themselves to a leech out of some idea of penance. Penance is cheap. Staying is expensive. Stay."

"Why?" It tore out of her raw. "Why, after what I did? You should put me off at Lowport. You should — "

"Because I know exactly what you owe," Marguerite said, "and I know what it makes a person, because I am what it made me. And because when the flood brought that chest out of the water, the first thing I wanted to do was sell it. The second thing I wanted to do was throw it overboard. It took me a week to want the right thing, Odile. You get one night."

Odile laughed through the tears — a broken, disbelieving sound — and Marguerite's hands dropped away, and for a moment the two women stood in the willow-shadowed dark and neither of them was a captain or a first mate, just two debtors on a boat, owing the river everything.

"The strainer," Odile said finally, wiping her face with the heel of her hand. "Voss did it. It has to be him. Who else?"

"Pirro's hands were clean, yours just confessed, and Tomas was asleep in the chartroom — I checked. So yes. Voss." Marguerite's voice cooled again, but differently now, the cold of purpose rather than hurt. "Here's what happens next, and you're going to hate it. We say nothing. We change nothing. Voss believes his purchase is still secret, and that belief is worth more to us than his arrest. You're going to go on setting his place at dinner and smiling at his stories, and inside you're going to be the best liar on this river for exactly as long as I need you to be. Can you do that?"

Every instinct in Odile screamed against it — walk the plank, take the shame, be done. But she had spent three years learning what debts cost when you ran from them, and Marguerite had just spent five minutes teaching her what they cost when you faced them, and only one of those prices bought anything.

"For you," she said. "For the boat. Yes."

"Not for me." Marguerite looked past her, down the black line of the slough, toward Lowport and the north beyond it. "For the girl in the documents. For Ana. Everything from here runs through her now, and I won't have syndicate hooks in my crew when we reach her." She paused, and something shifted in her face — a tightening Odile had learned to read like weather. "And there's one more thing. Tomas decoded another page tonight. The syndicate's northern timetable. Voss's real orders. They're not planning to buy the chest at the gorge, Odile."

"What, then?"

"They're planning to take the *Kestrel*. Boat, crew, chest, all of it — at the narrows, where no one could hear us." Marguerite turned back, and in the last grey light her eyes were the color of deep water over a hidden bar. "So tomorrow, when we make port at Lowport to patch the hull, you're going to show me every back channel your father ever taught you. Because we're going to need a way out of that harbor that the harbormaster doesn't know exists — and I have a feeling we're going to need it in the dark."
