## 12. The Gorge

Marguerite felt the gorge before she saw it.

It came up through the deck plates first — a change in the river's voice, the low conversational murmur of the Sarelle tightening into something harder, more insistent, the way a crowd goes quiet before a knife is drawn. She stood at the wheel with her palm flat against the brass rail and let the vibration climb her arm, reading it the way other people read letters. The water was telling her what waited ahead: stone on both sides, a throat of it, and the whole weight of a monsoon-fed river being pushed through that throat with nowhere else to go.

"Half a mile," she said. "Maybe less."

Odile came up from the engine well wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being gray years ago. "Soundings?"

"Deep enough in the channel. It's the edges I don't trust." Marguerite tilted her chin toward the banks, where the jungle had begun to lean inward, vines trailing into the current like fingers testing the water. The trees grew right down to the rock there, no shingle, no beach, no mercy of a landing if anything went wrong. "You remember what the pilot charts say about this stretch."

"I remember what the charts *don't* say." Odile leaned on the rail beside her. "Half the soundings in the gorge are forty years old. The flood two springs back rearranged everything above the Narrows. Any hired pilot would charge you double and then refuse."

"That's why we're not hiring one."

Behind them, at the aft rail, Callum Voss stood with his hands folded neatly behind his back, watching the closing walls of the gorge with an expression Marguerite had spent nine days learning to read and still didn't entirely trust. He was very good at looking like a man admiring scenery. He was almost as good at looking like a man who wasn't counting something.

She had known for three days now. Tomas had come to her cabin with the decoded pages spread across her chart table like a confession, his voice low and steady even as his hands shook. Voss's correspondence, hidden in the false bottom of his own trunk, written in the syndicate's merchant cipher — dates, signals, a rendezvous. *The gorge,* the last message said. *Take her at the gorge, where she cannot turn.*

She had not thrown him off. She had not confronted him. She had done something harder: she had kept serving him dinner, kept letting him stand at her rail, kept smiling at his stories about the wine country south of Lowport, all while quietly moving the chest from the hold to the space beneath her bunk and shifting their fuel stops, changing nothing he could predict and everything he'd planned around. Pirro now slept across the doorway to the cargo deck with a boat hook within reach. Odile knew. Tomas knew. Voss did not know that they knew, and that asymmetry was the only weapon Marguerite had ever needed.

The gorge walls rose ahead like the pages of a book closing.

* * *

The Sarelle's Gorge ran four miles between cliffs of black granite, and in ordinary seasons it was merely dangerous — a fast, deep channel studded with drowned rock, runnable by any competent captain with good light and a steady hand. This was not an ordinary season. The monsoon had come early and stayed late, and the river ran six feet above its summer mark, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees in its current like splinters. Where the channel narrowed at the Narrows, the water piled up into standing waves and the air itself seemed to vibrate.

Marguerite took her time approaching. That was the secret nobody believed: the slower you entered fast water, the more of it you got to see. She brought the Kestrel down to bare steerage way and studied the entrance while the crew gathered without being ordered to — Odile at the engine telegraph, Pirro forward with the sounding pole, old Sefa at the stern lines, and Tomas standing beside her at the wheel because he had earned that place somewhere in the long nights of bailing and had never asked for it and so could never be sent away from it.

"There," she said, pointing with her chin. "See how the current bends left before the first cliff? That's a shoal under the surface, pushing the flow off it. The channel's on the far side of that boil line, even though it looks calmer over right."

Tomas squinted. "The calm part is the trap."

"The calm part is always the trap. Rivers are liars. So are merchants." She let that sit in the air between them and heard, rather than saw, the small shift in Voss's posture at the aft rail. Good. Let him wonder how much she knew. A man who suspects he's suspected makes mistakes; a man who's certain does not.

She rang for slow ahead and took the Kestrel in.

For the first mile it was work, but honest work — the kind of piloting she could do half-asleep, reading the boils and slicks, nudging the boat through the seams between currents where the water ran smoothest. The cliffs rose on either side until they cut off the sky, leaving only a ribbon of gray light overhead and the rain-forest dripping down the rock faces in green curtains. The engine beat steadily beneath her feet. Pirro called soundings in a clear young voice. For a little while, Marguerite allowed herself the rarest luxury of her life, which was to believe that the day might go exactly as planned.

Then the mirror signal flashed from the cliffs above the second bend.

Three flashes. Pause. Three again. Then a fourth, longer — and answered, fainter, from somewhere downstream behind them.

Odile saw it too. "That's not fishermen," she said. "Fishermen don't code."

"No," Marguerite said. "They don't." She did not look at Voss. She made herself not look at Voss, and it was the hardest steering she did all day. "Pirro! Get below. Sefa, break out the canvas covers for the windows. Odile — I want everything that engine has, but not yet. On my mark."

Voss's voice came smooth across the deck. "Trouble, Captain?"

"Could be nothing." She turned then, and gave him the full weight of her gaze, and watched the charming merchant mask hold for exactly two seconds before something flickered underneath it — calculation, or fear, or possibly regret. "Could be the syndicate, waiting at the Narrows with two boats and a chain across the channel. Could be a coincidence. What do you think, Mr. Voss? You've traveled this river. You'd know better than I whether men with signal mirrors like to fish here."

The color left his face by degrees, like a tide going out. "Captain Ashgrove—"

"Hold that thought." She spun back to the wheel. "We'll have it out later, you and I, assuming there is a later. Right now every hand on this boat works for me or swims. Understood?"

He understood. Whatever else Callum Voss was — syndicate agent, liar, man with debts of his own — he was not stupid, and stupid men do not survive long enough to become anyone's agent. He moved to the stern lines without another word.

Around the third bend, the Narrows opened before them, and Marguerite's stomach dropped the way it always did when the river showed her its true face.

The channel choked to less than sixty feet between walls that leaned toward each other overhead. The water hit that pinch and stood up — two great rolling waves with a trough between them, white-capped even in the gray light, and beyond them the current tore downstream in a series of diagonal creases that would flip a boat broadside in a heartbeat. And moored along the right-hand wall, just past the worst of it, sat two flat-bottomed syndicate boats, engines idling, men visible on their decks with rifles slung casual as fishing rods. Across the channel, barely breaking the surface, floated a line of pale buoys — and Marguerite's eye traced the cable sagging between them, a steel chain set just high enough to catch a hull below the waterline.

They'd been expected. Of course they had. Voss's messages hadn't only named the gorge; they'd named her draft, her speed, probably her favorite brand of tobacco.

"Mark," she said quietly, and pushed the telegraph to full ahead.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, each one complete and separate, the way disaster and triumph both arrive — not as a blur but as a sequence of moments each demanding everything.

First: speed. The Kestrel surged, her old engine roaring up through its registers, and the bow lifted as the river took hold of them. Marguerite aimed for the standing waves themselves — not around them, not the seam of smoother water to the left that every sane pilot would choose, because the sane water was where the chain waited. The waves were the only unguarded door.

"You can't be serious," Odile said, reading her heading.

"The chain's set for the channel. Nobody chains the waves — you can't anchor cable in that turbulence." Marguerite's hands were steady on the wheel. Her voice was steady. Somewhere underneath both, a girl who had once run burning out of a collapsing house was screaming, and Marguerite told that girl to wait her turn. "Hold on to something. All of you!"

Second: the impact. The Kestrel hit the first wave bow-on and the world went white — spray sheeting over the wheelhouse, the hull slamming down into the trough with a crack of timber that Marguerite felt in her teeth, a sound no captain ever wants to hear and every captain eventually does. Something structural, down low, aft of midships. Not fatal. Not yet. The boat staggered, wallowed — and came back, because the Kestrel had been built by a shipwright in Vesseny who had loved her planking the way Marguerite loved her wheel, and love holds.

Third: the second wave, bigger, and this time she met it at a slight angle, riding up its face with the engine screaming, and for one suspended moment the Kestrel hung at the crest with the whole gorge laid out below — the tearing current, the syndicate boats turning in confusion, the chain glinting under the foam — and then they were through, down the back side, into the fast water beyond.

"Hard to starboard!" Pirro shouted from forward, pointing. A deadfall tree, whole and massive, spun in an eddy directly across their path.

Marguerite saw it, saw the seam of current running just left of it, and threaded the needle — close enough that the pine boughs raked the starboard rail, close enough that Pirro flattened himself against the deck as the branches swept over him. Then the channel widened, then bent, then narrowed again, and she flew it. There was no other word. She flew the Kestrel through four miles of water that no hired pilot would dare, reading each crease and boil a half-second before it formed, her hands translating the river's rage into small precise corrections, Odile matching her on the telegraph call-and-answer they'd perfected over nine years — *half ahead, half ahead, full, ease — full — ease to standard* — until the two of them were not two people operating a boat but one creature thinking with a single mind.

Behind them, the syndicate boats tried to follow through the waves.

The first one made it, badly, shipping water, its pilot visibly fighting for control in the same current Marguerite was dancing through — but dancing requires practice, and these were harbor men, boarding men, used to catching slow barges in open water. They gained for a quarter mile, their rifle fire cracking off the granite walls, one round punching through the wheelhouse window and burying itself in the chart rack an inch from Tomas's shoulder. He didn't flinch. Later, Marguerite would think about that — how the archivist who had never crewed a boat in his life stood in the spray and the gunfire and held the wheelhouse frame steady with both hands as if he could lend the boat his strength.

The second syndicate boat hit the deadfall.

It came around the bend too fast, too wide, and the spinning pine caught it amidships with a crunch audible even over the engine. By the time Marguerite glanced back, it was pinned against the left wall, swinging slowly in the current, its crew scrambling along its rails like ants on a stick.

The first boat pressed on alone. For another mile it stayed with them, gaining in the straight stretches, falling back in the turns, its riflemen shooting at a target that refused to move the way targets were supposed to move. Then the channel split around a midstream crag — the Devil's Tooth, the old charts called it — and Marguerite took the left passage, the narrow one, the one with the undercut ledge where the current sucked backward along the rock face. She took it because she had taken it twice before, years ago, in lower water, and remembered the exact feel of the eddy that would either destroy them or save them.

She put the Kestrel's bow into the eddy's edge and let the river grab them sideways, swinging the boat behind the crag like a door closing. The syndicate boat, committed to the main channel, shot past the opening at full speed. By the time its pilot realized, by the time it fought back upstream against six feet of monsoon current, the Kestrel was a mile gone in the maze beyond.

* * *

The gorge released them in late afternoon, spilling the Kestrel onto a broad stretch of braided shallows where the river spread out and caught the light like hammered tin. Marguerite held course for another ten minutes — past the first side canyon, past the second — before she finally eased the telegraph back and let the boat breathe.

Only then did her hands start shaking.

She hid them in her coat pockets and gave the wheel to Odile and walked the length of the deck on legs that felt borrowed, checking. Pirro, soaked and grinning, coiling lines. Sefa, methodically reloading nothing, just wanting his hands busy. Voss at the stern, gray-faced, staring back downriver at the empty water as if expecting the gorge to give them back.

And the hull. She went down into the hold with a lantern and found it the way she'd known she would: a seam sprung aft of the engine bed, planks cracked along their grain, water seeping through in a thin silver thread that the bilge pump could handle — today, in calm water, with luck. Tomorrow, in the open lake above the gorge, with weather, maybe not.

Not a wound. A promise of one.

She climbed back into the gray evening light and found them all waiting for her — Odile, Pirro, Sefa, Tomas, even Voss standing apart at the rail like a man at a funeral who isn't sure he's welcome. The Kestrel rocked gently on the settling water. Somewhere upstream, a bird cried out once and went silent.

"Well," Marguerite said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Nobody died, the chest is dry, and the syndicate is eating our wake. I'd call that a good day."

"A good day," Odile repeated flatly. "We took rifle fire, Marguerite. We've a cracked hull and a passenger who sold us to the men shooting at us, and you're calling it a good day."

"I'm calling it a good day *so far*." Marguerite looked at Voss then, really looked, and the humor drained out of her voice like water out of a cracked hull. "Because tomorrow we reach Saint-Elm, and there's a shipyard there, and while my hull is being mended, you and I are going to have a long conversation about mirrors, and messages, and exactly how much your employers paid for the lives of my crew. And Mr. Voss — I want you to spend the night thinking very carefully about which side of that conversation you'd rather be standing on. Because I've seen what happens to men the syndicate is finished using, and I promise you it's worse than anything I'd do to you."

Voss opened his mouth. Closed it. Nodded, once, like a man signing something.

Marguerite turned away and looked north, where the river ran on toward the high country, toward the pass, toward whatever truth the chest carried and whatever price it meant to pay. The first stars were coming out over the ridge. Behind her, Tomas moved to stand at her shoulder, saying nothing, needing to say nothing.

"We cracked a plank today," she said quietly, to him, to the river, to herself. "Boat and captain both. Question is which of us the shipyard can fix."

Ahead, dark against the last light, the water ran on — and somewhere in it, faint but unmistakable, Marguerite could already hear the next thing coming for them.
