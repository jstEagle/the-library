## 13. Stranded at Saint-Elm

The Kestrel came out of the gorge on her last breath of luck and died a mile downstream, in the shallows off a shingle bank that some drowned cartographer had named Saint-Elm. Tomas stood at the rail as they grounded her, watching the water slide away from her hull like something letting go, and thought: *this is what it looks like when a river finishes with you.*

She wasn't finished. That was the strange thing. The crew was already moving before Marguerite gave the order — Odile shouting for lines, Pirro wading into the brown water with a rope coiled over his shoulder, Callum Voss, of all people, hauling on the capstan with his fine merchant's coat stripped off and slung over a snag. They beached her stern-first on the gravel with the practiced violence of people who had done this before, and when she settled, listing gently against the stones, someone — Pirro, probably — let out a whoop that echoed off the gorge walls behind them.

Tomas did not whoop. He sat down on the rail and shook, quietly, where no one could see.

They had come through the gorge. He kept turning the fact over like a coin he suspected might be counterfeit. He had watched Marguerite Ashgrove take a boat through water that no hired pilot would touch, threading rapids that had swallowed syndicate boats whole, and he had seen the hull crack beneath them like an egg, and still they were here. Behind them, somewhere back in the white noise of the gorge, three boats full of men who wanted the chest had learned what the Sarelle did to strangers.

Ahead of them lay nothing but rain.

* * *

Saint-Elm was not a town. It was a gravel bar half a mile long, fringed with alder scrub, with a fisherman's hut leaning at one end and a shrine to some river saint — the plaque long since washed blank — at the other. It appeared on charts because rivers needed places to put their orphans. The monsoon had arrived early and arrived to stay; the rain came down in gray sheets that turned the world beyond the bank into rumor.

They hauled the Kestrel up on rollers salvaged from the hold, all fourteen of them working in shifts, until she sat above the flood line with her cracked belly exposed to the sky. The damage was worse than Marguerite had hoped and better than she'd feared, which by her arithmetic made it a good day. A seam had opened along the port strake below the waterline, and two planks were sprung where the gorge had squeezed her. The engine had taken water. The chest, Tomas noted with a kind of grim humor, was perfectly dry, sealed in its oilcloth cocoon in the strongest room of the boat, while the boat itself nearly drowned around it.

"You find that funny?" Marguerite asked. She had come up beside him without a sound, the way she always did, and he'd stopped being startled by it sometime in the last hundred miles.

"I find it characteristic," he said. "Of rivers. Of crowns. Of most things worth fearing."

Her mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a small shift at one corner, quickly corrected. Nine weeks on the river and he had catalogued her expressions the way he'd once catalogued manuscripts: obsessively, helplessly, with the growing suspicion that the collection would never be complete.

"Rations say we're here ten days," she said. "Odile says twelve. Pirro says eight, but Pirro says everything's eight days from being done." She looked out at the rain hammering the shingle. "The calving season opens when the meltwater drops. We have maybe four weeks after that to make the pass. So we work."

"We work," he agreed.

But she didn't move. She stood there in the rain with her hands jammed in her coat pockets, watching her broken boat the way other people watched sick children, and Tomas understood suddenly that she was afraid — not of the gorge, which she had already beaten, but of the stopping. Of nine days on a gravel bar with nothing between her and her own thoughts but weather.

"Captain," he said carefully, "when did you last sleep a full night?"

"When I'm dead," she said, and walked away to argue with Odile about caulking.

* * *

The days found their shape. Mornings were for the hull: heating pitch over driftwood fires, driving oakum into seams, wrestling new planks from the alder stands upriver — green wood, not ideal, but the Kestrel had never been a purist's vessel. Afternoons, the rain drove everyone under the awning they rigged from the cargo tarpaulin, and there the work turned inward. Odile re-rigged the winch. Pirro taught Voss to play cards and lost spectacularly, then accused him of cheating, then forgave him, then lost again. And Tomas decoded.

He worked on the documents in the driest corner, spreading the water-stained pages under oilcloth and piecing together the cipher that the archivists of Vesseny had used for state papers — a cipher he had spent six years learning and one terrible year fleeing. Each day the pages gave up a little more. Names. Dates. A genealogy written in the careful hand of a court scribe, tracing a bloodline through exile and concealment to its living end.

And each day, the name Ashgrove surfaced in it again, and each day Marguerite found a reason to be elsewhere when it did.

He noticed. He was an archivist; noticing was his trade. He noticed that she could stand in the gorge with the boat breaking apart beneath her without blinking, but she could not stand within earshot of him reading aloud. He noticed that she took the midnight watch alone, every night, walking the length of the stranded hull with a lantern, checking lines that did not need checking. He noticed that she slept in the wheelhouse, never in the cabin aft, though the cabin was drier.

On the fifth day, the rain slackened to a drizzle and he climbed to the wheelhouse at dusk and found her already there, sitting on the deck plates with her back against the wheel pedestal, a tin cup of tea going cold in her hands.

"This seat's taken," she said, without looking up.

"The boat's aground, Captain. I'll risk it." He lowered himself to the plates beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and did not quite let them. Outside, the drizzle silvered the air. Down on the bank, Pirro was singing something obscene and tuneless, and Odile was telling him to stop, and neither of them meant it.

For a while they said nothing. It was, he had learned, the language she spoke most fluently.

"My sister used to sit like that," Marguerite said finally, to the rain rather than to him. "Back against something solid. Watching water. She said it was the only honest mirror."

"What happened to her?"

"You know what happened to her. You've been reading it in those papers for five days." Her voice was flat, but not cruel. "You just haven't told me yet."

Tomas turned his cup slowly in his hands. This was the conversation he had rehearsed forty times and abandoned forty times, because every version ended with her taking the chest and leaving, and every version left him standing on a bank somewhere, watching a horizon empty of her.

"The succession proof names a living heir," he said. "Direct line. Legitimate under the old law, documented under the old seal. If it were presented, it would unmake the minister's government and the syndicate's charter claims in a single stroke. Everything they've done since the collapse rests on the throne staying empty."

"And the heir's name?"

He hesitated.

"Tomas." She turned her head and looked at him, and her eyes in the dusk were the exact color of deep water. "I have run this river for nine years. I have outrun debt collectors and press gangs and one memorable tax assessor. Do not make me outrun you."

"Ashgrove," he said. "The heir's line is Ashgrove."

She went very still. Not the stillness of surprise — he realized, watching her, that she had known, had perhaps known since the locket, had carried the knowledge the way the boat carried the chest, sealed and dry and deadly. The stillness was of a woman setting down a weight she had forgotten she was holding.

"Then it's true," she said quietly. "All of it. What my sister did. What she hid. What she died keeping."

"Lena," he said — because the documents had given him the name, and saying it felt like laying a flower on a grave. "Lena Ashgrove. She didn't die running from Vesseny. She died carrying it."

Marguerite laughed — one short, hard note with nothing in it of laughter. "Carrying it. Yes. That was Lena. She'd carry the whole country if it asked nicely." She drank the cold tea anyway, grimacing. "So now you know why I don't sleep. Every night I lie in the wheelhouse doing arithmetic. How many days to the pass. How many miles. How many men want what's in my hold. And underneath it, the real sum: whether I sail north because my niece needs me, or because a dead country does."

"Is there a difference?"

"That," she said, "is the question, isn't it."

* * *

The fire on the bank burned low that night. The crew had gone below or into the hut, and the rain had stopped entirely for the first time in six days, and the sky over Saint-Elm opened up like a wound healing — stars in ragged ranks, the river running quiet and black past the shingle. Tomas found Marguerite at the shrine at the end of the bank, sitting on its worn step, and this time he sat down without asking.

"I owe you honesty," he said. "You've earned more of it than I've given."

"You've given me more than anyone has in nine years," she said. "That should frighten us both."

"It does." He watched the current slide past, patient as time. "So let me say the plain thing, and then you can decide what to do with it. I've spent my life among dead papers, Marguerite. Dead kings, dead laws, dead words in dead languages. I told myself it mattered — that truth preserved was a kind of rescue. Then Vesseny fell, and the men who burned the archives burned my colleagues, and I ran, and I told myself that too was preservation. And then a flood dropped a chest into your hold, and for the first time the papers in my hands were alive. They could change things. They could hurt people. They could heal one."

"And?"

"And I know what they cost. I've watched what they cost. Your sister paid in full." He turned to face her. "So here is the choice, stated plainly, because you deserve plainness and I am tired of cipher. We are aground for nine more days. The pass opens in four weeks. There is a southern road, a bad one, but a road — we could take the chest south, sell it to the loyalists, buy a new boat, buy new names, and be done. Or you could take this boat apart plank by plank and burn it for warmth, and we could walk away, and no one would ever find us, and Vesseny could stay dead and its heir could stay a girl in the northern settlements who never learns what she is. No minister. No syndicate. No gorge. Just the river, and us, and whatever horizon we point at."

Marguerite was silent for a long moment. The firelight moved on her face.

"You're offering me desertion," she said.

"I'm offering you a life. There's a difference, and I think you're the only person on this river who can tell me what it is."

She looked at him then, fully, and everything she had kept behind competence and command rose to the surface — the grief, the exhaustion, and underneath both, unmistakable, the thing she had been not-saying since the monsoon night when they bailed water side by side and laughed like fools in the flood.

"If I were a different woman," she said softly, "I'd take your hand right now and we'd walk south and I'd never think of Vesseny again. If I were that woman, Tomas, I think I'd be happy."

"But you're not that woman."

"No." She exhaled. "I'm the woman who read the name in the locket and didn't throw the chest in the river, God help me. There's a girl up there — Ana, the papers say, Ana Ashgrove — who is going to spend her whole life hunted if nobody tells her what she is. Hunted by the minister, hunted by the syndicate, hunted by every man with a claim and a gun. My sister died so that girl could choose. Not reign — choose. And I am the only person alive who can carry that choice to her." She shook her head. "That's not duty. Duty is what other people call it when they want you to do the hard thing for free. This is just — family. The kind you don't get to pick."

Tomas nodded slowly. He had offered her the exit and she had declined it, and he found — this was the part he hadn't predicted — that he was relieved. Because he had known, even as he offered it, that the woman who took the exit would not be the woman he —

"There's something else plain I ought to say," he said, "while we're being reckless."

"Don't."

"All right." He smiled. "I won't say it. But I'll say that when this is over — when the chest is delivered or destroyed or fed to a glacier — I intend to be standing wherever you're standing. North, south, aground, afloat. I've run from one country already. I find I've lost the taste for running."

Marguerite reached over and took his cold hand in hers, briefly, firmly, like a captain sealing a bargain.

"Nine more days," she said. "Then we go north."

* * *

It was Odile who brought the news, at dawn, at a run across the wet shingle, and the look on her face told Tomas before she spoke that the nine days had just been taken from them.

"Smoke," she said, breathless, pointing downriver. "South bank, past the bend. Cooking fires. A lot of them." She caught her breath. "And Captain — there's a boat coming up the channel flying government colors. White ensign. It's the minister's launch."

Marguerite was already moving, already awake, already giving orders — and Tomas stood a moment longer at the edge of the bank, looking south through the morning rain toward the smoke, and understood that the choice he had offered her had never really been on the table.

The river had simply been waiting to see what she'd do.
