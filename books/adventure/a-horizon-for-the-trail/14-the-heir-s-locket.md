## 14. The Heir's Locket

The locket had been sitting in Marguerite's cabin for eleven days, and she had walked past it every one of those days the way a woman walks past a door she has decided not to open.

It sat in the shallow drawer beneath her berth, wrapped in an oilcloth that did nothing to disguise its shape — small, oval, heavier than it had any right to be. She had told herself it was brass, river-tarnished, worthless. She had told herself the documents in the chest were someone else's history, some loyalist family's grief pressed flat between sheets of vellum. She had told herself so many things that the telling had begun to sound like the engine: constant, rhythmic, easy to stop hearing.

But tonight the Kestrel lay hauled against the shingle at Saint-Elm, patched and propped, waiting out the last of the monsoon, and there was no engine noise to hide behind. Only rain on the deck above, softening now after weeks of fury, and the creak of the hull settling into the bank like an old dog lying down, and the locket in the drawer saying nothing at all, which was worse than anything it could have said.

Marguerite lit the lamp, turned the wick low, and took it out.

* * *

She had known whose it was from the moment Odile pried the chest open and the smell of drowned paper rose up like something exhaling. Nine years, and she had known. The crest meant nothing to her — crests were for people who believed nations could be trusted with their names. But the chain, fine as spider silk, knotted twice where it had always knotted because Elise could never be bothered to work the clasp and simply pulled the thing over her head until the links learned her impatience — that, Marguerite had known the way you know your own heartbeat.

Elise. Her sister. Dead nine years this autumn, if dead was what you called what happened in Vesseny when the capital burned and the ministries scattered and the Ashgrove name went from being spoken in drawing rooms to being scratched off manifests.

Marguerite turned the locket over in her palm. The engraving on the back had been worn nearly smooth by a thumb — Elise's thumb, or perhaps the child's. *A.V.* Two letters. Ana Vesseny-Ashgrove, though the second name would have been struck from any record the new government kept. A girl of four when the world ended. A girl of thirteen now, if she lived.

*If she lived.* Marguerite said it to herself the way she checked the depth-sounding: routinely, without letting it mean too much.

The hinge was swollen with river water. She worked it with the tip of a knife, gently, the way she'd free a fouled valve, and hated herself a little for knowing how to do it, for having hands that only understood machines. The locket resisted. Then, with a small salt-crusted click, it gave.

Inside, folded into quarters no larger than a thumbnail, a letter. Paper gone amber, ink faded to the brown of old blood but legible, written in a hand she knew better than her own — the leftward slant, the way the *e*s leaned like women listening at doors.

Marguerite set the letter on the desk without unfolding it.

She poured herself two fingers of the rough plum brandy Odile traded for at Lowport and did not drink it. She looked at the lamp flame. Outside, Pirro laughed at something down on the shingle, high and young, and the sound went through her like a fishhook.

Then she unfolded the letter.

* * *

*M.,*

*If you are reading this, then the river found you before I could, which is fitting, since I always said the river loved you best.*

*I don't have long. They are coming for the household records and I am sending what matters north with Marta. You will want to burn this. Burn everything else if you must, but read this first.*

*The documents name Ana. Not me — they never wanted me, I was only the archivist's wife, useful and invisible, which is how we survived this long. The succession runs through Father's line to Ana. She is the heir they will kill for. I have hidden the proofs where only a riverwoman would think to look, and I have paid the men who will see the chest delivered to the water, because I know you will find it. You always find what the river gives up. It is the one prayer I have left.*

*Do not come looking for me. By the time this reaches you I will be whatever they make of me, and I need you to be something else. I need you to be the thing I cannot be — alive, moving, impossible to corner. Take Ana north if you can reach her. Marta has her in the settlements past the glacier pass. If you cannot reach her, then forget us, and live, and know that forgetting was the kindest thing I ever asked of anyone.*

*You won't do it. You never could let a thing go once it had your name on it. That is why I am writing instead of praying.*

*Tell her the locket was mine. Tell her I kept her first tooth in it until the letter needed the space more.*

*All my love, longer than the river,*
*E.*

Marguerite read it twice. Then she sat very still for a long time, the way the river sits under ice — everything still on top, everything moving underneath.

* * *

The niece was alive.

Not a name in a dead nation's paperwork. Not an abstraction Tomas could decode by lamplight, not a claim three factions would bleed over in the abstract. A girl. Thirteen years old. In the northern settlements, past the glacier pass, in the exact direction the Kestrel had been crawling all monsoon with a hold full of other people's destiny.

Marguerite stood, and her knees informed her she'd been sitting far longer than she'd thought. She crossed to the porthole. Rain, thinning. The black line of the far bank. Somewhere out there, upstream and north and impossibly far, a girl with Elise's leftward slant in her handwriting was growing up without a country, without a mother, without knowing that an aunt existed who had spent nine years running from the very name that would save her.

Nine years. Marguerite had built a life on the Sarelle out of the wreckage of that name — a boat, a crew, a rule (*never stop moving*) that was really just grief wearing practical clothes. And Elise, dying or worse, had spent her last hours not cursing her sister for surviving but trusting her. Trusting her to find the chest. Trusting her to carry it north. Trusting her with the one thing Elise loved more than her own life.

And Marguerite had wanted to sell it.

The memory arrived with physical force — the dock at Corvane, the salvage broker's offer, her own voice saying *unopened royal seal, there's collectors who'd pay*. She had held her sister's last prayer in her hands and priced it by weight.

She braced both hands on the desk and breathed until the shaking stopped.

* * *

A knock. Two soft raps, the way Tomas knocked — always asking permission even when the door was open, as if he'd spent his whole life being told archives belonged to other people.

"Come in."

He came in wet to the elbows, smelling of pitch and rain, a coil of caulking cotton over one shoulder like a strange scarf. He saw the locket on the desk, the letter, the untouched brandy, and stopped as if he'd walked into glass.

"You opened it," he said.

"My sister wrote it." Marguerite heard her own voice and was surprised by how level it was. "Elise. She was married to the keeper of the household archives — your archives, Reyes, the ones you've been spending my fuel money decoding." She almost smiled. "Small river."

Tomas set down the caulking cotton with great care, as though the room had become a place where things might break. "The documents name a successor. We established that. A living heir, the direct line—"

"Ana. My niece. Thirteen years old. Alive, in the northern settlements, if my sister's word is worth anything." Marguerite picked up the locket and closed her fist around it until she felt the hinge bite her palm. "Which it always was. That was the infuriating thing about her."

Tomas was quiet a moment. She watched him do what he always did — take the fact, turn it, test its grain. "Then it was never chance," he said softly. "The flood. The chest. Your boat, of all the boats on the Sarelle."

"The river doesn't deal in chances. My sister knew that better than anyone." Marguerite finally drank the brandy. It burned going down and she welcomed it. "She steered it to me. Drowned and nine years dead, and she still out-navigated every pilot on this water."

"She must have loved you very much."

That undid her more than the letter had. She turned to the porthole again because she could not let him see her face do what it was about to do. "She asked me to forget them. Can you imagine? *Forget us, and live.* As if living were a thing you could do with your back turned." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I tried, Tomas. Nine years I tried. And the whole time I was steering away from Vesseny, I was steering toward that girl and didn't know it. Every mile north. Every run up this godforsaken river. Like a compass needle I mistook for habit."

When she turned back, Tomas had moved closer without seeming to cross any distance, the way he did. He didn't touch her. He simply stood within reach, which from Tomas was its own kind of language.

"What will you do?" he asked.

And there it was — the question she'd been outrunning for nine years, finally standing in her cabin with rain on its sleeves.

Because here was the truth she couldn't say aloud yet: the duty was no longer abstract, and that changed everything. A document could be surrendered, sold, buried, argued over by ministers and syndicate men who deserved each other. But a girl — a girl with Elise's handwriting waiting somewhere inside her, a girl who had been hidden by a dying woman's last cleverness — a girl could not be sold. Could not be handed to a minister in exchange for amnesty and a new hull. Could not be left in a drawer while Marguerite ran south forever.

Every faction on this river wanted the papers. None of them, Marguerite realized, had ever once mentioned the child. To the loyalists she was a banner. To the syndicate, an asset. To the minister, a problem to be dissolved. Only Elise had written about her as a person — *tell her the locket was mine* — and only Marguerite, now, knew the difference.

"I'm going to finish the repairs," Marguerite said slowly, "and then I'm going to take my boat up a river nobody pilots in calving season, through a pass that opens for a few weeks if it opens at all, past a minister who's promised to be waiting for us." She looked at the locket in her fist. "And I'm going to put this in a girl's hands and let her decide what it's worth. Not the crown. Not the syndicate. Her."

"That isn't what the loyalists will want. Or the minister. Or—"

"I know what they'll want. They'll want what they've always wanted — a symbol they can carry and a woman they can blame." She met his eyes. "They can stand in line behind my niece."

Tomas smiled then, tired and real, and Marguerite felt something in her chest shift its moorings — not the wild warmth of the bail-night laughter, something quieter and more frightening. Recognition. He had lost a country too. He had been carrying other people's truths in sealed boxes too. Of everyone on this river, he was the only one who understood that what she felt wasn't courage. It was arithmetic. One girl against everything, and the girl outweighed.

"Get some sleep," she said, gentler than she'd meant to. "We sail when the hull's sound and not before. But we sail north, Tomas. All the way."

He gathered his caulking cotton, paused at the door. "For what it's worth — your sister's handwriting. The *e*s leaning like that." He hesitated. "My mother wrote like that. I noticed it in the documents months ago and never said, because it wasn't mine to say."

After he was gone, Marguerite sat back down and read the letter a third time, slower, hunting it for anything she'd missed. Near the bottom, half-hidden in the fold, a postscript in smaller script:

*P.S. — If a man named Reyes ever helps you, trust him. I vetted his father myself.*

Marguerite laughed — a short, disbelieving bark at the empty cabin — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. Elise. Dead nine years and still arranging the crew.

Above her, boots crossed the deck; Odile's voice murmured to Pirro about the morning's caulking; the Kestrel groaned against the shingle, impatient, a creature made of movement forced to lie still. Marguerite folded the letter along its old creases, returned it to the locket, and hung the chain around her own neck — knotting it twice, the way Elise always had, because some habits skip generations and land on sisters.

Then she blew out the lamp and lay in the dark, listening to the rain die, and did not sleep at all. Because through the hull, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she'd been waiting nine years to hear without dreading it: the river, dropping. The monsoon ending. The pass beginning to open.

By dawn, she was already on deck, watching the northern hills show their shoulders through the clearing mist — and watching, far downstream, a smudge of smoke that had no business being there, moving against the current.

Something was coming upriver. And for the first time in nine years, Marguerite Ashgrove was not thinking about how to run.
