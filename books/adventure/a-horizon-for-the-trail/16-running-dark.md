## 16. Running Dark

Odile had learned to read a river by its silences. Not the water itself — anyone with a season's wages and a strong back could learn the water — but the quiet around it. The hush of birds gone still before a storm. The absence of frogs where there should have been frogs. And now, standing at the rail of the *Kestrel* an hour past dusk, she listened to the silence of Saint-Elm Reach and heard what it was hiding: engines. Two of them, maybe three, idling low somewhere in the dark ahead, the way wolves breathe slow when they've already decided on the kill.

The minister's gunboats had come upriver while they patched the hull at Saint-Elm. Pirro had seen their lights from the shingle bank that afternoon — two white points moving against the current, too steady to be fishing boats, too patient to be anything but a net being drawn closed. By evening the whole crew knew. The minister would be waiting at the narrows below the calving fields, and he had all the time in the world, because the glacier route didn't open for another nine days and he knew it as well as they did.

Marguerite had stood at the wheelhouse window for a long while after that, saying nothing, her thumb working at the scarred wood of the rail the way she did when she was solving a problem with her hands instead of her mouth. Then she'd said, "We go tonight."

"Tonight," Odile had repeated. "Through the narrows. In the dark. With a hull we glued back together with pitch and prayer."

"With engines muffled," Marguerite said. "And Pirro steering."

That had been the part that made even Tomas look up. Pirro was nineteen years old and had been steering the *Kestrel* through open water for exactly one season. But Marguerite had watched him take the night watches, had watched him find his way by stars the way other boys found their way by streetlamps, and when she made a decision like this she wore the particular calm of a woman who had already grieved every alternative and found them worse.

So now Odile stood at the rail in the dark, listening to the silence, and did the thing she was best at besides reading rivers: she worried, thoroughly and professionally, about everything that could go wrong.

* * *

They'd spent four hours preparing, and the preparations had the strange intimacy of people dressing a body. The engine room went first — Rennik and Tomas wrapping the exhaust pipes in wet canvas and old blankets until the *Kestrel*'s heartbeat became something closer to a pulse felt through the deck than a sound heard across the water. Every loose fitting was padded. Every creaking board was wedged. They took down the running lamps, coiled the wiring, and covered the wheelhouse windows with tarps so not one candle's worth of light could escape.

Odile's own job was ballast. She moved cargo forward by hand, crate by crate, shifting the boat's weight toward the bow so she'd sit lower at the front and higher at the stern — riding shallower over the sandbars at the narrows' mouth, taking less freeboard exposed above the waterline. It was the kind of trick you learned running contraband in your twenties and never quite stopped being ashamed of, or grateful for.

Voss had tried to help. That was the thing she couldn't stop noticing about him lately. He'd appeared in the hold with his fine merchant's hands and started hauling crates without being asked, and when she'd snapped at him — some reflex left over from the weeks when she'd known what he was and hated herself for letting him aboard — he hadn't snapped back. He'd just kept hauling.

"You don't have to do this," she'd told him finally. "When we run the blockade, if they catch us, they'll hang the captain and the archivist. You they'll promote."

"I know," Voss had said, setting down a crate of salt pork with exaggerated care, as though it were something breakable. "I've been thinking about that."

It wasn't an answer. But it wasn't nothing either.

Now, at the rail, Odile watched Marguerite come down from the wheelhouse and gather them on the aft deck: Rennik still black with grease, Tomas with his sleeves rolled and his forearms scratched from engine work, Pirro pale and trying not to show it, Voss standing slightly apart the way he always did, as if unsure whether the circle included him.

"Here's how it goes," Marguerite said, and her voice was low and even, the voice she used when the river was high. "The gunboats are anchored at the narrows, probably bow upstream, probably watching the channel markers. So we don't use the channel. We go east of the second marker, through the flooded willows, where the water's shallow enough that no pilot with sense would try it." A pause. "Pirro has no sense. That's why he's steering."

A couple of them laughed — thin, nervous laughter, but laughter. Pirro grinned like a boy handed a live grenade.

"Engines stay muffled. We run at half speed, no more. Nobody speaks above a whisper once we're inside the reach. If we're spotted—" Marguerite looked at each of them in turn, and Odile saw her decide not to lie to them. "If we're spotted, we run for the far bank and ground her, and we take our chances in the reeds. Better wet and alive than dry and boarded. Questions?"

"Stars," Pirro said. "Cloud cover's breaking up around midnight. I'll want the Big Dipper low on the horizon for the first leg, then Cassiopeia after the bend."

Marguerite nodded slowly. "You'll have them."

"And if the clouds don't break?" Odile asked, because someone had to.

"Then we wait," Marguerite said. "And we hate waiting together."

* * *

The clouds broke at eleven minutes past midnight, which Odile knew because she was watching the clock in the wheelhouse and pretending she wasn't holding her breath.

They cast off without lights, without horn, without the usual ritual of shouted commands. Marguerite took the telegraph; Pirro took the wheel; Odile stood at the starboard wing with a fender pole, feeling absurd, feeling terrified, feeling more alive than she had since she was a girl sneaking her father's skiff out past the harbor chain at Lowport.

The *Kestrel* moved into the current like a held breath released.

Without her lamps, the river became a different animal. Odile had spent nine years learning this water by daylight and lamplight, and now it showed her its night face: banks that seemed to lean closer, reflections doubled and trebled where the stars came through the torn clouds, the water so black in places it looked solid, as if the boat were traveling across polished obsidian and the only proof of motion was the cold wind in her teeth.

Pirro steered by those stars. Odile watched him do it and felt something turn over in her chest. He stood at the wheel with his chin lifted, eyes going from horizon to compass to horizon again, calling the course changes in a whisper — "coming starboard two points... ease her... now hard over" — and Marguerite answered the telegraph, and the *Kestrel* leaned and swung and straightened, and the flooded willows slid past on both sides close enough to touch, their drowned branches trailing in the water like hair.

There was a moment — Odile would remember it all her life — when the boat passed between two great willow trunks with barely an arm's width to spare on either side, in full darkness, at speed, steered by a nineteen-year-old reading the sky. Nobody spoke. Even the river seemed to hold its tongue. And then they were through, into the wider water beyond, and Odile let out a breath she'd been rationing for twenty minutes.

That was when the searchlight came on.

* * *

It hit them from astern — a white column of light that turned the night to noon in a single stroke, and Odile's heart went from zero to hammering before her mind caught up. Behind them, maybe six hundred meters back, a gunboat's silhouette resolved against the stars: the minister's ship, not anchored at the narrows at all but patrolling, patient, lucky.

Or not lucky. Odile thought of the harbormaster at Lowport, bought and paid for. Thought of how easily word traveled upriver ahead of a boat. *He knew our schedule,* she thought. *He's been waiting for us to move.*

"Pirro," Marguerite said, and her voice didn't rise at all, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Take us off the marked channel. North bank. Now."

The wheel spun. The *Kestrel* heeled. The searchlight swung after them, dragging its white finger across the water, and a voice came booming over the water through a speaking trumpet, distorted but unmistakable in its grammar of authority: *"Vessel Kestrel. Heave to. This is a government interdiction. Heave to or be fired upon."*

"They won't fire," Tomas said from the shadows near the wheelhouse. "Not at night, not on a civilian boat with witnesses downstream. They need us intact. They need—"

A shot cracked across the water anyway. High, deliberate, a warning — but warnings have a way of becoming something else, and Odile saw Marguerite's jaw set in the reflected glare.

"He needs us intact," she said. "But he'd rather have us scared. Pirro — the old logging cut, north side. Do you see it?"

Pirro leaned into the dark. Ahead, where the north bank bulged around a point of gravel, there was a gap in the trees — a straight-edged gap, man-made, a century-old canal cut where loggers had once floated timber down to the main channel. Half-silted. Unmarked on any chart less than forty years old. Odile knew it because her father had run whiskey through it during the embargo years, and she had sworn never to tell a living soul, and here she was about to spend the secret to save them all.

"The entrance shoals to eighteen inches at the lip," she said quickly. "Come in shallow and fast, bow-first, and don't touch the wheel for the first hundred meters — the current in there sets hard to port. After that it deepens. It comes out above the narrows. Above the *narrows*, Marguerite."

Marguerite looked at her for one long second. Whatever she saw in Odile's face — the guilt, the gratitude, the whole tangled history of a woman who'd spent nine years paying down debts she never named — she didn't ask about it. She just nodded.

"Odile calls the line. Pirro steers. Everyone else — hold on to something and pray quietly."

* * *

The logging cut took them the way a throat takes a pill: reluctantly, narrowly, with the banks scraping close on both sides.

They came in fast and shallow exactly as Odile had said, and the *Kestrel* kissed the gravel bar at the lip with a grinding shudder that Odile felt in her back teeth, and then the current took hold and swung the bow to port exactly as hard as she'd promised, and Pirro — bless him, brave stupid loyal Pirro — kept his hands off the wheel and let the river drive them, and for ninety seconds the boat was not theirs at all but the water's, rushing down a corridor of black trees with the searchlight slashing uselessly overhead, blocked by the canopy, the gunboat's voice fading behind them into fury and static.

Then the cut widened, deepened, slowed, and the *Kestrel* exhaled.

Odile realized she was laughing. Quietly, helplessly, bent over the fender pole, laughing the way you laugh when you've walked away from something that should have killed you. Around her the others were doing versions of the same thing — Rennik thumping the deck plates, Tomas pressing both hands flat against the wheelhouse wall as if steadying himself against the world, Pirro grinning so wide his face might split.

Voss was not laughing. Voss was looking over the port rail with an expression Odile couldn't read in the dark, and she was about to ask him what was wrong when the river answered for him.

* * *

The snag came out of nowhere, because snags always do.

A deadfall — a whole tree, rootball and all, lodged in the cut where the flood had driven it — lay just under the surface, invisible in the dark, its trunk angled across two-thirds of the channel. Pirro saw it a half-second too late. Marguerite rang the telegraph full astern, and the muffled engines groaned against their wrappings, and the *Kestrel* began to swing, but water doesn't negotiate and neither does a ton of waterlogged oak.

The impact was less a crash than a terrible, intimate crunch — the sound of a hull meeting something older and harder than itself. The boat lurched. Odile went to her knees on the deck. Somewhere aft, cargo shifted with a groan of rope. And then the water began to come aboard, fast and cold, and she understood with a clarity that felt almost peaceful: they were sinking.

"Not sinking," Marguerite snapped, as if Odile had said it aloud. "Taking water. There's a difference, and the difference is we're going to fix it. Rennik — engine room, tell me where she's holed. Tomas, get the pump turning. Pirro, keep her pointed at the bank — ground her, gently, before she goes down in deep water."

The crew scattered into motion, and Odile ran aft to secure the cargo lines, and that was when she heard the splash.

Small. Human. The particular splash of a body going into cold water, not a crate.

She turned. The aft rail was empty where Voss had been standing. Below, in the black churn behind the settling boat, something was struggling — arms slapping the surface, a gasp, a curse swallowed by water.

Later, Odile would try to reconstruct why he'd gone over. The most likely answer was the simplest: the impact had thrown him. He'd been standing at the rail, unbraced, a man who'd spent his life on ferries and office floors and had never learned what a river does to a boat that hits a snag. Or perhaps — and this was the version she preferred, the version she'd eventually tell him over brandy in some northern tavern — he'd jumped. Jumped because he'd seen the water pouring in through the hull breach and assumed the worst, because men who sell betrayals for a living assume everyone else is also calculating an exit.

Either way, he was drowning, and the *Kestrel* was settling, and every rational part of Odile Zhou's mind said: *let him.*

This was the man whose forged papers had nearly gotten them boarded at Lowport. The man whose coded ledger Tomas had found stitched into a coat lining. The man whose syndicate was waiting at the gorge with grappling hooks and guns. She owed him nothing. Less than nothing. Every hour she'd spent keeping his secret was an hour she'd spent owing her captain a debt she hadn't yet confessed, and here was the universe offering her a clean ledger — one drowned traitor, no questions asked.

She kicked off her boots and went over the rail after him.

* * *

The water was colder than sin. It closed over her head and pressed on her eardrums and stole the breath out of her chest, and when she surfaced, gasping, the world had become a chaos of black water and starlight and the looming dark mass of the settling boat. She heard Marguerite's voice cutting through it all — "Line! Throw her a line!" — and saw, absurdly, a coil of rope arcing out of the darkness toward her, thrown with the flat, deadly accuracy of a woman who had once won money throwing knives in river towns.

Voss had gone under again. She dove, caught a fistful of his coat — good wool, expensive, the coat of a man who dressed for the life he wanted instead of the life he had — and hauled. He came up fighting her, which was the way of drowning men, thrashing and clutching, and she took the elbow strike he gave her across the cheekbone and wrapped her arm around his throat from behind in the towman's carry her father had drilled into her before she was twelve. *Don't fight the drowning man. Carry him. Let him exhaust himself against your grip, not your lungs.*

"Stop," she said into his ear, through chattering teeth. "Stop fighting. I have you."

And he did stop. That was the thing she would remember. In the middle of the black water, with his lungs full and his fine coat dragging him down, Callum Voss stopped fighting — stopped struggling against her grip, against the river, against whatever war had been running inside him since the day he'd stepped aboard with his charming smile and his hidden orders — and went limp in her arms, trusting her, a man with no reason on earth to trust her, and let her carry him.

The rope found them. Hands found them. Rennik and Tomas hauled them up over the stern onto the deck, where they lay streaming and coughing in a puddle of river water while the *Kestrel* sat grounded on the soft north bank mud, listing, holed, alive.

Odile rolled onto her back and stared at the stars and laughed again, that same helpless laugh, until her ribs hurt.

Above her, Marguerite Ashgrove's face appeared, upside down, framed by the sky. Her expression was unreadable — fury and relief and something else, something that might have been awe, braided together tight.

"You went in after him," Marguerite said.

"He was drowning," Odile said.

"Yes," Marguerite said. "I can see why that would confuse you."

* * *

They worked until dawn. Rennik found the breach — a seam opened along the third strake, two planks sprung, the snag's signature written in splintered oak — and they patched it by lantern-light with canvas, pitch, and a backing plate Tomas improvised from a salvaged hatch cover. The pump ran all night, its rhythmic clank the heartbeat of a boat refusing to die. Pirro fell asleep sitting upright at the wheel, hand still resting on the spokes, and nobody woke him, because he'd earned it.

Toward morning, Odile sat on the cargo hatch wrapped in two blankets, her cheekbone bruised purple, her hands still shaking in the delayed way they always did after the danger was over. Voss came and sat beside her. Not too close. Just beside.

"You saved my life," he said.

"Don't make it strange," she said.

"It was already strange." He was quiet a moment. The eastern sky was going grey, then gold, and the mist was rising off the cut in long silver banners. "I've been carrying something," he said. "In my cabin. Under the floorboard by the bunk. I've been telling myself I'd deliver it, because a man has to be something, even if what he is, is bought. But tonight—" His voice caught, and he covered it with a short, unhappy laugh. "Tonight a woman I lied to for six weeks carried me out of a river. I keep thinking about that. About what she owed me. Which was nothing. Which was absolutely nothing."

Odile looked at him. His face in the dawn light was stripped of everything charming, and what remained underneath was younger than she'd expected, and tired, and — she searched for the word and found it with a small internal shock — honest. For the first time since he'd come aboard, actually honest.

"Burn it," she said.

He looked at her sharply.

"Whatever it is. The orders, the timetable, the promises. Burn it." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I'm not telling you as a friend. I'm not sure I know how to be your friend yet. I'm telling you as a woman who owes debts she can't pay, and I'm telling you the only way out of that life is through the fire. You burn it, or it burns you. Those are the options. The river taught me that. Tonight it taught you."

Voss was silent for a long time. Down the bank, Marguerite stood at the bow checking the patch, and Tomas was asleep against the wheelhouse door, and the mist burned away gold thread by gold thread.

"All right," Voss said finally, and stood, and Odile watched him walk toward his cabin with the stride of a man going to do the hardest easy thing of his life.

She should have felt lighter. Instead she felt the weight of her own unfinished confession settle back onto her shoulders — the charter she'd accepted, the debts she'd hidden, the secret she'd kept from the woman who had just watched her jump into a freezing river for a traitor. Grace, she thought, was a debt that compounded. Every kindness you received made the truth harder to tell.

By midmorning the pump had won, the patch was holding, and the *Kestrel* backed off the mud into the deep water of the cut, pointing north, above the narrows, above the gunboats, above everything the minister had planned.

Marguerite rang the telegraph ahead slow, and the muffled engines took up their wrapped heartbeat, and Odile took her place at the rail as the boat slid out of the logging cut into open water — where, far off on the southern horizon, a smudge of smoke was rising that no campfire made, and Pirro, squinting at it through the glass, went very still and said, "Captain? You're going to want to see this."
