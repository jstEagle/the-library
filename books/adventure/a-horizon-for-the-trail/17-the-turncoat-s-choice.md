## 17. The Turncoat's Choice

Callum Voss had spent his whole life learning the exact weight of a lie, and so he knew, with a merchant's precision, that he was drowning in one.

Not literally — though the river had tried that too, two nights ago, when the Kestrel ran the minister's blockade and he'd gone over the side into black water full of debris and cold. He could still feel it: the moment the deck tilted, the moment his hand slipped on wet rope, the moment he understood that no one aboard owed him anything at all. He had been their passenger. Their guest. He had smiled at Odile over cards and complimented Marguerite's piloting and all the while carried a ledger in his breast pocket with their deaths written out in neat columns — *Kestrel, seized at the gorge; crew, disposed of quietly; cargo, transferred to Syndicate custody.*

And Odile had jumped in after him.

He kept coming back to that. He turned it over in the dark of the fo'c'sle like a coin he couldn't stop biting. He had watched her do it — seen her strip off her coat without a word, seen Pirro scream something he didn't catch over the engine noise, seen the water take him and then give him back because a woman who owed money to half the ports on the Sarelle had decided his life was worth the risk of her own.

*They don't know,* he told himself, in the first hours. *If they knew what I am, they'd have let me drown.*

But that was the coward's arithmetic, and Voss had never been a coward. A coward would have stayed quiet, ridden the Kestrel north, waited for the syndicate's boats at the calving fields, and stepped aside when the guns came out. A coward would have told himself the crew had brought it on themselves by carrying the chest at all.

Voss lay in his bunk and listened to the boat around him: the tick of cooling metal, the groan of the patched hull, Marguerite Ashgrove's voice somewhere aft giving quiet orders for the morning run. The monsoon had broken. Through the porthole the sky was doing that thing it did after weeks of rain — rinsing itself clean, going from slate to silver to a blue so hard it looked cut. Somewhere beyond that blue were the glacier fields, and beyond them the northern settlements, and somewhere in the settlements a girl named Ana who didn't know she was the last living argument for a dead country's throne.

Voss knew exactly how much she was worth. He had the figures memorized. That was the job he'd been given: know the price of everything, and believe in none of it.

He sat up.

The orders were still where he'd hidden them, sewn into the lining of his spare coat — because a man who trusted a mattress on a riverboat deserved to lose everything. He took the coat down and worked the stitches loose with his thumbnail, slowly, the way he did everything now, as if his hands had learned caution from watching people die. The oilskin packet came free. Inside: the syndicate's seal pressed into grey wax, three pages of coded timetable, and a letter of credit against his own life — *payment rendered upon completion; failure voids all protection.*

*Failure voids all protection.* He'd read that line a hundred times and never once felt it. Now it read like a sentence passed.

He climbed out onto the deck.

Dawn on the Sarelle above the gorge was a country unto itself. Mist stood in the shallows like congregation, and the mountains came up out of it range by range, white-shouldered, indifferent. The Kestrel lay at anchor in a backwater Odile had chosen — "a place the maps forgot and the tax men never found," she'd said — and the crew was already moving. Pirro coiling line with the exaggerated care of a boy performing innocence. Odile at the rail with tea, her knuckles split from the night's work, watching the current read like scripture. And Marguerite Ashgrove at the wheel even at anchor, hands resting on the spokes as if the boat might bolt if she let go.

She saw him come up and didn't smile. She hadn't smiled at him since Lowport. But she didn't look away either, and there was a difference, and Voss had spent nine years reading faces across bargaining tables to know that the difference mattered.

"Captain," he said. "A word."

"Passengers get words," she said. "Crew gets work. Which are you?"

It wasn't cruelty. It was an open door dressed as a wall, and he recognized the style because it was his own.

"Neither," he said. "Something else."

He crossed the deck and set the oilskin packet on the binnacle, between her hands and the compass. Then he took out his matches.

"Before I burn this," he said, "you should know what you're refusing to take. There are four syndicate boats working the upper river. Two are fast — shallow draft, hired pilots from Corvane, good ones. They're staging at the mouth of the Meltwater Channel. The calving season opens the ice route in—" he glanced at the sky, counting days the way other men counted coin, "—eleven days, maybe twelve. The syndicate means to be through the pass before the minister's gunboats can follow. They want the chest, and they want the girl, and they've paid the harbormaster at Saint-Elm to signal them when you move." He paused. "That's the part I'm ashamed of least, which tells you something about the rest."

Marguerite hadn't touched the packet. Her eyes went over his face the way her eyes went over water — reading the surface for what moved beneath.

"Why?" she said. Just that. One syllable, and it landed heavier than any accusation.

Voss looked at Odile. She was watching him over the rim of her cup, and her face gave away nothing except that she was tired in a way sleep wouldn't fix.

"Because you pulled me out of the river," he said. "All of you. You had every reason not to. I've been lying to you since the day I chartered passage, and when I went over the rail, Odile didn't hesitate. Pirro threw me a line before he even thought about it. Tomas—" he laughed, short and joyless, "—Tomas sat with me half the night after, talking about archives, of all things, as if I were a man worth knowing." He spread his hands. "I have been bought and sold my whole career, Captain. I know what loyalty looks like when it's purchased. This wasn't that. I don't know what this was. But I found myself on the bottom of that river thinking that if I drowned, the last honest thing anyone ever did for me would be wasted."

"So this is repayment," Marguerite said.

"No," Voss said. "Repayment is a debt, and debts end. This is—" he searched for the word and hated that he had to search, "—this is me deciding which side of the ledger I want to die on. Because the syndicate will kill me for this. You understand that. The letter of credit dies the moment these pages burn. After today I'm not an asset. I'm a liability with a name."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because you deserve to make the choice with your eyes open," he said. "Throw me off at the next landing. Tie me up and leave me for the syndicate to find. Or keep me, and use what I know. Those are the options. I just won't be the one who picks for you. I've done enough picking for other people."

For a long moment the only sound was the river moving past the hull, patient as time.

Then Marguerite picked up the packet, weighed it once in her hand, and held it out to him.

"You burn it," she said. "Your orders. Your fire. If you're going to turn, turn all the way, where I can watch you do it."

Voss struck the match.

The wax caught first, then the paper, and the syndicate's timetable curled and blackened in his fingers — eleven days, four boats, the harbormaster's bribe, the letter of credit, all of it going up in a small fierce heat that smelled of resin and endings. He held it until the flame licked close and dropped the last of it over the rail, where it rode the current a moment, glowing, then went dark and was gone downstream toward everything he'd been.

"There," he said, and his voice cracked on the single syllable, which embarrassed him more than the tears would have.

"Now," Marguerite said, and something in her tone shifted — not warmth, exactly, but the absence of frost, which on this boat was nearly the same thing. "You'll tell Tomas everything. Every code, every name, every arrangement, down to what the syndicate pays its pilots. He'll write it down, and then we'll decide what's true and what's bait. And Callum—" she stepped closer, and up close her eyes were the exact color of the river in flood, brown and gold and utterly unsentimental, "—if there's a second ledger anywhere on my boat, I will put you off at the middle of the gorge and sleep fine afterward. Do we understand each other?"

"Perfectly," Voss said. "It's the first honest contract I've ever signed."

"It's not signed yet," she said. "Work the deck till Saint-Elm. We'll see what your hands say about you."

* * *

His hands said plenty.

They said he'd spent years signing documents instead of hauling rope, because by midmorning his palms were blistered raw and Pirro was laughing at him — laughing, not cruelly, but with the bright uncomplicated delight of a boy discovering that the elegant merchant couldn't tie a bowline under way. "Left over right and through, señor, left over *right*—" and Voss, forty-one years old, a man who had negotiated shipping contracts on three rivers, fumbled the knot a fourth time and heard himself laugh too, a rusty sound, like a door long unopened.

Odile appeared beside him without comment and retied the knot once, slowly, so he could see. She didn't offer sympathy. She offered instruction, which from Odile was the same thing.

"You could hate me," Voss said quietly, keeping his eyes on the line. "I forged the charter. I reported our position twice from Lowport. The trap at the harbor — that was mine."

"I know," Odile said.

"You *know*?"

"Tomas figured the correspondence three days ago. Told the captain. Captain told me." She shrugged, a small economical motion. "We've been waiting to see what you'd do."

Three days. Three days he'd eaten at their table, slept under their deck, walked among them carrying his secret like a knife — and they had known, and they had waited, and they had pulled him out of the river anyway. Voss gripped the rail and looked at the water and understood, finally, the shape of the thing that had undone him. It wasn't gratitude. Gratitude he could have paid off and been rid of. It was the discovery that these people had extended him trust he had done nothing to earn, and that the trust had sat in him like a seed in wet ground, doing what seeds do whether or not the gardener approves.

"I owe you money," Odile said abruptly. "Debts in six ports. I let you aboard to clear them. So before you go making yourself into some grand penitent — remember that everyone on this boat is running from something, and the only difference between us is what it cost."

"What did yours cost?"

"Nine years of looking sideways," she said, and went forward to yell at Pirro about the jib, conversation closed.

* * *

That evening Tomas spread the reconstruction across the chart table: the syndicate's northern plan, decoded from Voss's memory and cross-checked against the burned timetable's fragments, which Tomas had fished from the bilge with the tenderness of a scholar rescuing a manuscript.

"They'll stage here," Tomas said, tapping the Meltwater Channel's mouth. "Two fast boats forward, two holding the lower channel as reserve. They expect the Kestrel to come through the pass in the main window — day eight to day twelve. Before that, they'll sweep the approach. After that, they assume the ice closes and nobody comes at all."

"Which means," Marguerite said, studying the chart the way she studied water, "we don't come through the window. We come through early, ugly, and where they aren't looking."

"The eastern shelf," Odile said slowly. "It opens first — meltwater gets there ahead of the main channel. My father ran timber through it, years back. It's not a route, it's a rumor with rocks in it."

"A rumor with rocks," Marguerite repeated, and Voss saw it happen — saw the whole crew lean toward the idea at once, the way a crew does when a captain has already decided. "Then that's our door. Callum — the syndicate's pilots. How good?"

"Corvane-trained. The best money hires."

"Good," Marguerite said. "Then they'll be sensible. Sensible men don't run the shelf."

Voss felt the words land somewhere below his ribs. *Sensible men.* He had been a sensible man his entire life, and it had bought him a burning packet and blistered hands and a seat at a table where a boy was grinning at him over cold rice because he'd finally tied his knot right.

Later, when the others had turned in, Marguerite found him at the rail.

"One more thing," she said. "The girl. Ana. When we find her — the syndicate wants her delivered alive. Why?"

Voss hesitated. This was the last coin he had, and he'd known since dawn he'd spend it.

"Because a dead heir makes a martyr," he said. "A living one makes a puppet. The syndicate doesn't want the crown restored, Captain. They want it *owned*. A girl on a throne who owes them everything is worth more than any cargo that ever floated." He met her eyes. "Whatever you're planning to do with those documents — do it fast, and do it in front of witnesses. The moment she's alone with them, she's merchandise."

Marguerite was quiet a long time, watching the dark water slide past.

"Thank you," she said at last, and it sounded like it cost her.

"Don't thank me yet," Voss said. "I'm still deciding what I am."

"No," Marguerite said, turning back toward the wheelhouse. "You decided this morning, at the binnacle. The rest is just learning to live in it."

She left him there, and Voss stood his watch alone under a sky rinsed clean of rain, the mountains white and enormous ahead of them, and for the first time in his life the horizon ahead looked less like a place he was fleeing toward than a direction he had chosen — and far upstream, unseen, the first lights of the syndicate's boats were kindling at the mouth of the Meltwater Channel, eleven days early.
