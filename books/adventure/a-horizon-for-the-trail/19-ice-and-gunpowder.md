## 19. Ice and Gunpowder

The ice spoke first.

Marguerite heard it before dawn, before the light, before anything on the Kestrel was awake but her — a long groaning sigh that rolled down the channel from the north, the sound of a glacier shifting its weight in its sleep. She had grown up with rivers that murmured and gossiped, rivers that lied about their depth and their temper, but she had never heard water talk like this. It was the voice of something vast and indifferent, and it made the hair on her arms stand up inside her coat.

She stood at the wheel with her hands bare on the wood, the brass rim cold enough to bite, and watched the calving fields open before her in the gray half-light. The charts called it the Verre Strait, though no chart she had ever seen had done it justice. The glacier's tongue reached down from the mountains on either side like two great pale hands, and between them the meltwater had carved a channel — a shifting, treacherous lane of open water threaded with bergs the size of chapels. It was open now. It would be open for weeks, perhaps a month, before the calving season choked it with ice. The only road north. And somewhere behind them, closing fast, the minister's gunboat.

*The Kestrel* moved through the channel at a crawl, her engine muffled since the blockade, her hull groaning in sympathy with the glacier. Every few minutes a berg calved somewhere out of sight with a report like artillery, and the water shivered, and the ice in the channel — the pans and floes that drifted like sleeping animals — rearranged themselves. A route that had been open at midnight might be sealed by morning. A channel wide enough for two boats might be wide enough for one, or none.

"You haven't slept." Odile came up the companionway with two mugs of coffee, steam trailing from them like signal smoke. She set one on the ledge by the wheel without ceremony. "And don't tell me you did, because I heard you pacing."

"I was listening."

"To the glacier."

"To the glacier." Marguerite took the coffee. It was terrible, scorched, the way Pirro made it, and she drank it anyway. "She's calving faster than she should. The meltwater's running high. That means the channel's going to start closing from the north end first, and it means—" She stopped.

"It means the minister knows it too," Odile said. "He's been shadowing us since the blockade. He doesn't have to beat us. He just has to wait for the ice to do his work."

"Yes." Marguerite turned the wheel a spoke to port, easing the Kestrel's bow around a floe that had drifted into the channel overnight, white and blue-shadowed, big as a barn. "But he doesn't know this water. He doesn't know *ice*. His pilot is following our wake, Odile. He's been following our wake since Lowport."

Odile was quiet a moment. "You're thinking something," she said. "I can hear it. It sounds expensive."

Marguerite didn't answer. She was thinking about the Kestrel. She was thinking about nine years — nine years of diesel and river mud and debt and dawn watches, of Odile's coffee and Pirro's terrible singing and the wheel worn smooth under her hands. She was thinking about the sound the hull made at night when the current held her just right, a sound Marguerite had never been able to describe to anyone who didn't live aboard. And she was thinking about the calving face she'd marked on the chart two days ago, the sheer blue wall of the eastern glacier where it met the water, and the way the ice there had been booming all night like a drum.

Below her, in the saloon, Tomas was awake with the documents spread out under lamplight, and somewhere ahead of them a girl named Ana was waiting in the northern settlements, not knowing what was coming down the river toward her. Marguerite had made her choice on that shingle bank at Saint-Elm, and she had made it again at the blockade, and she would make it as many times as it needed making.

But the river was asking a different price now. The river always asked.

* * *

The gunboat showed herself an hour after sunrise, when the light came up hard and white off the ice and the whole channel blazed like a furnace.

She was called the *Vigilant*, and she was a government boat in the way a fist is a government hand — gray-painted, armored at the bow, with a deck gun forward that the minister had requisitioned for "customs enforcement." She came up the channel at half speed, conservatively, her hired pilot hugging the wake the Kestrel had cut through the floes, and the minister stood on her bridge in a coat too fine for the weather, and even at four hundred meters Marguerite could feel him looking at her.

She had the speaking trumpet in her hand before she'd decided to take it.

"Minister Hale!" Her voice went flat and hard across the water, the way it did when she shouted at barges in fog. "This channel is closing. You can see the ice moving. If you want the chest, come and take it — but you'll have to come alongside, because I'll not heave to in moving ice for any government in the world."

A pause. Then the trumpet on the *Vigilant*, a smoother voice, a voice that had never shouted anything. "Captain Ashgrove. You are transporting stolen state property and a fugitive of the Republic. Surrender the cargo and the man Reyes, and you and your crew will be released without charge. This is the last time I will offer."

"The last time," Marguerite repeated to Odile, quietly. "He's said that three times now."

"He means it this time."

"He means it every time. That's what makes him dangerous." She lifted the trumpet again. "Minister! The documents name a living heir to the throne of Vesseny. You know it. Your masters in the Republic know it. That's why you're here with a gunboat instead of a warrant. Come take them, if you dare the ice. Otherwise, get out of my channel."

She saw him turn and speak to someone behind him — the pilot, she guessed — and saw the *Vigilant* begin, carefully, to close the distance. Four hundred meters. Three hundred. The gunboat's bow wave spread out across the channel and set the floes rocking, and somewhere up-channel the glacier answered with a boom that rolled down the water like thunder looking for a place to land.

Tomas came up on deck then, pale, his coat buttoned wrong, the locket in his fist. "Marguerite. The channel ahead — Pirro says the north end is narrowing. Fast. He says we have maybe two hours before it seals."

"Then we have two hours to make him regret following us." She handed Odile the trumpet. "Get everyone into life vests. No arguments. And get the documents and the locket into the waterproof satchel — the one we rigged at Saint-Elm. If the Kestrel goes down, the truth doesn't go down with her."

Tomas had gone very still. "*If* she goes down."

Marguerite looked at him. She found, to her surprise, that she could smile. "Tomas. Look at this boat. Look at her." The Kestrel sat in the blazing channel with her patched hull and her scarred wheel and her nine years of river written on her in rust and paint and dents, and Marguerite loved her the way you love a thing that has carried you when nothing else would. "I've run her through a monsoon and a gorge and a government blockade. But I've known since the gorge that this channel would be where it ends, one way or another. The ice doesn't negotiate. Neither does he. One of us is going to give."

"Marguerite—"

"Get the satchel." She turned back to the wheel. "That's an order, archivist."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, she explained to Odile and Pirro in the pilothouse in under a minute, because a plan that takes longer than a minute to explain is a plan that dies in the telling.

The eastern calving face. The chart called it the Verre Wall — a kilometer of blue ice standing thirty meters out of the water, sheer as a cathedral, and all night it had been booming and shedding. The glacier calved in cycles; Marguerite had watched it through her glass since midnight, and she had the rhythm of it the way she had the rhythm of every river she'd ever run. Every eleven, twelve minutes, a section let go. Sometimes small. Sometimes the size of a chapel. When a big one went, the wave it threw across the channel could swamp a boat, roll a boat, drive a boat onto the ice like a toy.

The *Vigilant* was following their wake. The *Vigilant* did not know the calving cycle. The *Vigilant* had an armored bow and a deck gun and a minister who believed that the world was a document he could requisition.

"She'll follow us straight down the wall," Marguerite said, tapping the chart. "We run the channel close in — closer than any sane pilot would — and when the next big calving comes, we turn hard north into the floe field where she can't follow, and the wave takes her." She looked up. "Or it takes us. I won't lie to you. It could take us."

Pirro, seventeen years old and green as June, swallowed. "And the Kestrel?"

Marguerite's hand rested on the wheel. "The Kestrel will do what I tell her to do," she said. "That's all any of us can promise."

Odile studied her for a long moment — the look of a woman who had shared a boat with Marguerite Ashgrove for nine years and knew every weather her face could make. "You've already decided something," Odile said slowly. "Something you're not saying."

"Get to your stations," Marguerite said. "Both of you. And Odile—" she caught her first mate's sleeve, "—if it goes wrong, you take the satchel and the crew and you don't come back for me. You swear it."

"Marguerite—"

"*Swear it.*"

Odile's jaw worked. Then, quietly, in the voice she used for the truest things: "I swear. But you swear the other thing. You swear you're not planning to go down with her."

Marguerite let go of her sleeve. Outside, the glacier boomed, and the channel shivered, and the *Vigilant* came on at three hundred meters, patient as debt.

"I don't know what I'm planning," Marguerite said, which was the closest to a lie she had ever told her first mate, and they both knew it, and Odile went below without another word.

* * *

The run down the Verre Wall was the finest piece of boat-handling of Marguerite Ashgrove's life, and there was no one to see it but the ice.

She took the Kestrel in close — fifty meters off the wall, then forty, threading water so full of brash ice it ground and hissed along the hull like sand. Above them the glacier rose in blue and white strata, a thousand years of winters pressed into stone, and the cold coming off it was so fierce it made the breath ache in the chest. Behind them, exactly as she'd known he would, the minister held his course — because the safe water was the water the Kestrel had already broken, and because he believed, as powerful men always believe, that the world would make an exception for him.

"Eleven minutes since the last one," Pirro called from the engine room, his voice thin through the speaking tube. "Captain, the ice is cracking up there. I can hear it through the hull."

"Good," Marguerite said. "Keep her at full. Odile, watch the floe line to the north — I need a gap, and I need it when I call for it."

She watched the wall. That was all there was now — the wall, and the water, and the wheel in her hands. She had learned the river as a girl the way other children learned prayers, and she had learned the ice in nine hard northern years, and everything she knew was in her hands now, reading the glacier the way she read the Sarelle: the way the meltwater welled at the base in a dirty gray band, the way a hairline crack had opened near the waterline and was weeping, the way the whole face had begun, very slightly, to *lean*. The glacier was not a wall. It was a wave that hadn't fallen yet. And it wanted, with the patience of geology, to fall.

Two hundred meters back, the *Vigilant* matched her course. She could see the minister at the rail, his fine coat snapping in the wind, his trumpet raised: "Captain Ashgrove! Heave to or we will fire!"

"Then fire!" she called back, and heard the madness of it, and did not care. "Fire on the heir's documents, Minister! Explain that to the Republic!"

The deck gun did not fire. She hadn't thought it would. A man who wanted the chest intact did not put a shell through it, and a man who wanted the chest *secret* could not risk the story a sunken government gunboat would tell. He had come all this way on the assumption that she would eventually do the reasonable thing, and every meter down the wall she was teaching him, meter by meter, that she had stopped being reasonable somewhere back at Lowport.

"Captain!" Pirro again. "Cracking — it's loud, it's—"

The wall spoke.

It did not boom. It *spoke* — a sound like the sky tearing, like the first day of the world, and thirty meters above the waterline a section of the face the size of a church let go all at once and came down in a single white thunderclap that Marguerite felt in her teeth, in her ribs, in the wheel.

"*Hard to port!*" she roared, and threw the wheel herself, and the Kestrel heeled as she turned her bow north into the floe field, into the gap Odile had found, into the jumble of drifting ice where a gunboat with an armored bow could not follow.

The wave came a heartbeat later.

It came across the channel like the hand of God — a gray wall of water five meters high, crowned with spray and spinning ice, and it caught the Kestrel on the quarter and lifted her and threw her. Marguerite went to her knees on the pilothouse deck. Somewhere below, something enormous struck the hull — a floe, driven by the wave, striking with the weight of a house — and she heard the sound no captain ever forgets, the deep wooden *crack* of a hull being opened.

The wave went on. It crossed the channel and took the *Vigilant* full on her beam.

Marguerite, dragging herself up the wheel, got one look back. The gunboat was rolling — rolling past thirty degrees, her deck gun dipping into the water, men sliding along her rail, the minister's fine coat a dark speck against the gray. The wave drove her not onto the ice but *against* it, her armored bow grinding up a floe with a shriek of steel, and then the floe — unstable, wave-lifted — tilted, and the *Vigilant* slid, and the channel's current took her stern and swung her broadside into the path of the next floe coming down.

The Kestrel was dying. Marguerite could feel it through her boots — water in the forward hold, the engine laboring, the hull working and grinding with every floe they touched. But the *Vigilant* was dying faster, holed on her beam, listing hard, her pilot's voice cracking across the water on the wireless band, calling mayday, calling the minister's name.

"Marguerite!" Odile burst into the pilothouse, soaked, alive, her eyes wild. "We're holed forward — two meters of water in the bow, Pirro's bailing but it's coming faster than the pump. We have to beach her. Now. There's a shingle bar a kilometer north—"

"Did you see her?" Marguerite was still looking back. "Odile. Did you see the *Vigilant*?"

"I saw. There are men in the water, Marguerite. There are men in the water and the ice is closing and the minister is—" Odile stopped. She looked at her captain, and whatever she saw in Marguerite's face made her go quiet.

Because Marguerite had already turned the wheel.

Not north. Not toward the shingle bar and safety. Back — back into the channel, back toward the listing gunboat and the men in the water, the Kestrel driving through the floes with her broken hull taking water every meter of the way, toward the minister who had hunted them across nine hundred kilometers of river and ice.

"What are you doing," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"What nobody did for Vesseny," Marguerite said. "What nobody did for my family. We go back for them, Odile. All of them. Even him." Her hands were steady on the wheel. Her voice was not. "*Especially* him."

The Kestrel groaned beneath her, taking the river's cold into her bones, and Marguerite Ashgrove — who had spent nine years running, who had built a whole life on the principle of never stopping, never turning back, never paying what the water asked — turned her dying boat into the ice and the wreckage and did not look away.

Ahead, through the spray, she could see men clinging to a floe. And on the tilting deck of the *Vigilant*, one figure still standing, still upright, watching her come — Minister Hale, with the water at his knees and his face turned toward the boat he had tried to break, wearing an expression Marguerite would remember for the rest of her life.

It was not hatred.

It was the look of a man watching something arrive that he had never once, in all his calculations, believed possible.
