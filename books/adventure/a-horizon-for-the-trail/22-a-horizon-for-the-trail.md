## 22. A Horizon for the Trail

The northern rivers did not run brown. That was the first thing Marguerite had learned about them, a year ago now, standing on the shingle at Saint-Elm with river silt still in her boots and the Kestrel's bones behind her in the ice. The water here came off the glaciers, ground fine as flour between stone and stone, and it ran the color of milk and jade, cold enough to ache through the hull. It had taken her a full season to stop reading it like the Sarelle — to stop expecting the treacherous brown murk that hid its sandbars and kept its secrets. The northern rivers told you everything. They showed you every rock, every shallow, every drowned log lying pale on the bottom like a sleeping animal.

And still, she thought, watching the morning light come up over the water from the wheelhouse of the Kestrel II, they could kill you just as dead.

"Sandbar ahead, port side," Pirro called down from the bow, where he stood with a sounding pole even though he didn't need one. He was seventeen now, or near enough — he'd stopped counting his birthdays somewhere in the monsoon of last year, and none of them had thought to correct him. He'd grown three inches and learned to read water the way she did, not with charts but with the back of his neck. "Shoaling fast. Maybe two feet under."

"I see it," Marguerite said, though she'd seen it before he had. She eased the wheel a spoke to starboard, and beneath her hands the new boat answered the way the old one never quite had — quick, eager, a little too willing. Odile said it handled like a young horse. Odile meant it as a complaint.

Through the wheelhouse glass she watched the shoal slide past on the left, a long pale tongue of gravel reaching out into the current, and beyond it the valley opened the way it always did here: sudden and enormous, birch forest climbing the slopes, snow still lying in the high folds of the mountains though it was nearly midsummer. Somewhere ahead, past the bend, the river widened into the lake country, and past the lake country lay the settlements, and past the settlements —

Well. Past the settlements there was no more map. That was rather the point.

"You're smiling," Odile said from the doorway, coffee mug in hand, her gray-streaked hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of signal flag. "I don't trust it. Last time you smiled like that we lost an engine and half our cargo."

"That was Voss's smile," Marguerite said. "Mine is entirely different."

"Yours is worse. His you could see coming."

Odile leaned against the doorframe and drank her coffee, and Marguerite let the boat run a moment without watching her, because this too had become part of the year: the ease of it. It had not come quickly. Forgiveness, she had learned, was not a door you walked through once; it was a room you kept having to re-enter, some mornings barefoot and cold, until one day you noticed you'd stopped flinching at the threshold. Odile had told her the truth about Callum Voss in a low voice on the aft deck with the smoke of Lowport still rising behind them, and Marguerite had been furious — properly furious, the kind that lives in the hands — and then she had looked at this woman who had steered them dark through a bought harbor, who had pulled a drowning man out of the gorge water with no obligation whatsoever except the one she'd chosen, and she had understood that they were both running from debts, all those years, and that neither of them was running anymore.

"Ana's coming aboard at Tarnholt," Odile said. "Message came down on the mail skiff yesterday. She wants the whole summer route, end to end."

"The whole route." Marguerite turned that over. Ana Ashgrove — Ana Vesseny, if you asked the loyalists, whom nobody did anymore — did not do things casually. "She's supposed to be sitting in sessions until the thaw."

"She says the sessions can sit themselves. Her words." Odile grinned around her mug. "She also says to tell you she's bringing the good tea, so don't serve her whatever swill Tomas calls coffee."

Marguerite laughed, and the sound surprised her a little, the way it still did sometimes — how easily it came now, how little it cost. For nine years on the Sarelle she had kept herself the way you keep a boat in flood season: sealed tight, bailing constantly, trusting nothing to stay dry. Grief had been her cargo, ballast she never unloaded because she feared the boat would ride wrong without it. She had not known, until the Kestrel went into the ice with the gunboat chasing her onto the calving face, that she could lose the thing she loved most in the world and find herself still floating. Still steering. That the ballast had never been holding her steady at all. It had only been holding her down.

* * *

Tomas found her at midmorning in the hold, going through the cargo manifest with a pencil stub, and he did not announce himself, because he never did; he simply sat down on the crate beside her the way he'd been doing for a year, close enough that their shoulders touched, and waited for her to finish the line she was reading.

"You're going the wrong direction," he said.

"We're going north."

"Exactly. Everyone sensible goes south in summer. Warm rivers, warm ports." He took the manifest out of her hands, gently, and set it aside. "You have ink on your face."

"It's pencil."

"You have pencil on your face, then." He rubbed at her cheekbone with his thumb and did not entirely succeed, and did not seem to be trying very hard. He was thinner than when she'd fished him out of the reeds at the fueling dock two years ago — they were all thinner, river people are — but the hunted look had gone out of him entirely. The archivist who had begged passage with nothing but a stolen satchel and terror in his eyes now wore the same canvas jacket as everyone else aboard, patched at the elbows by Odile, and carried the documents — the proof, the succession, the truth that had toppled a minister and scattered a syndicate — in a plain oilcloth packet that lived in the chart drawer, between the soundings of the Tarnholt reach and Pirro's collection of river-toll receipts, because Tomas had insisted it should be somewhere ordinary. "It spent two years being the most dangerous paper in the world," he'd said. "It's earned the right to be boring."

"They'll want you at the anniversary," Marguerite said. "In the capital. The restoration assembly. Ana will ask you to stand up there with her, you know. You're the reason any of it happened."

"I'm the reason a boat sank."

"The boat was my decision." She said it evenly, without the old flinch. For a long time after the ice, she had dreamed about it — the groan of the hull, the white wall of the calving face leaning over them, the moment she'd put the Kestrel's nose at the gunboat and opened the throttle and known, with a clarity she had never felt about anything, that she was trading her boat for her people and would make the trade again every time, forever. Some nights the dream ended with the water closing over the wheelhouse glass. But more and more often now, the dream went further: the crew in the shallows, counting heads, all of them there, all of them breathing, and the silence where the engine noise used to be, and Ana waiting on the shore. "Every part of it was my decision. I'd make it again."

"I know," Tomas said. "That's why I'm here instead of there."

He said things like that. He had been saying them, more or less plainly, since Saint-Elm, since the night on the shingle bank when he'd offered her the choice to walk away from all of it and she hadn't been able to answer him yet. A year of almosts. A year of shoulders touching in the wheelhouse at midnight watch, of his handwriting appearing in the margins of her charts, of the crew exchanging looks over their heads with the patience of people watching a slow lock fill. Pirro had a wager going. Odile held the stakes.

"Tomas." She turned on the crate to face him. In the dim hold light his eyes were the color of the glacier water, and she thought, not for the first time, that she had spent nine years learning to read a river and had never once managed to read this man in advance, and that she no longer wanted to. "Ask me again. What you asked me at Saint-Elm."

He went very still. "You know what I asked you."

"Ask me anyway. I have an answer now. I've had it for months. I was waiting for the season to turn."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to say it in motion," she said. "Not stranded. Not hiding. Not choosing between one thing and another. I wanted to say it the way I mean it — underway."

So he asked her again, there among the crates and the smell of pine pitch and rope, in the same words he'd used on the shingle bank while the Kestrel's ribs dried in the sun: *stay with me — not ashore, not settled, just — with me, wherever the water goes.* And Marguerite Ashgrove, who had refused passage to a fugitive because stopping was death, who had built a whole life on the principle of never mooring her heart anywhere it could be burned, heard herself say yes so simply that it sounded like it had always been true and they had merely been waiting for the river to carry them to the place where it could be said aloud.

Above them, through the deck planks, they heard Pirro whoop — he'd seen them come down the hold ladder together and had apparently abandoned all pretense of subtlety — and then Odile's voice, dry as August: "Pay up. I told you it'd be before Tarnholt."

* * *

They took Ana aboard at Tarnholt on a bright afternoon with a wind out of the west, and she came down the landing steps carrying a single bag and a wrapped parcel of tea and looking, Marguerite thought, less like a claimant to an extinct throne than like any young woman escaping a stuffy room, which was precisely what she was.

"Aunt Marguerite." Ana said it easily now. The first time she'd tried the word, a year ago on the shore at Saint-Elm, it had come out careful as glassware. Now it was just a word, worn smooth by use. She hugged Marguerite hard, then stepped back and looked up at the wheelhouse with open delight. "She's beautiful. I'm sorry — I know it's strange, me admiring the replacement—"

"She's not a replacement," Marguerite said. "There's no such thing. She's the next one."

The Kestrel II had been built that winter in the settlement yards, on credit Odile had arranged and half the northern river towns had quietly helped pay down, because word travels on water faster than any syndicate's money, and the story of the boat that opened the pass and gave the truth to the public square had become the kind of story people wanted to put their names on. She was smaller than the first Kestrel, shallower of draft, built for these braided northern channels. Marguerite had salvaged the old wheel herself, hauled it out of the wreck line at the calving fields with her own hands, and the shipwrights had fitted it to the new helm, scarred and salt-darkened, and it fit her palms the way an old glove fits. The nameplate hung beside it: KESTREL II, and underneath, in letters Tomas had painted himself, small enough that passengers had to lean in to read them:

*A horizon is not a place.*

Ana claimed the bow rail within the hour and did not relinquish it for three days. She had inherited the family gift, Marguerite discovered — the river-reading, the neck-prickle for shifting water — and Pirro took it upon himself to teach her the sounding pole, and the two of them stood at the forepeak calling depths to each other in a private shorthand while Odile pretended not to be moved and failed completely.

On the fourth evening, running the wide reach below the lake country with the sun going down gold behind the western ridges, Ana came up to the wheelhouse. Marguerite was alone at the wheel; Tomas was below arguing cheerfully with Odile about navigation lights. Ana stood beside her a while without speaking, watching the jade water unspool ahead.

"There's a delegation coming to the autumn assembly," Ana said at last. "From across the mountains. From the old eastern provinces. They want to talk about — everything. Borders, restitution, the archives. Whether Vesseny should be a crown at all, or something else." She glanced sideways. "Tomas will be needed. And Odile knows those provinces better than anyone alive. And Pirro speaks three of their dialects, which nobody expected."

Marguerite kept her eyes on the channel, on the long glittering line where the current set toward the far bank. "You're asking me to take the Kestrel east."

"I'm asking whether you'd want to." Ana smiled — and it was her mother's smile, and for once the sight of it did not hurt; it had become, over the year, simply a thing the world contained, like the mountains, like the water. "But I know the answer already. I've watched you for four days. You don't steer away from anything anymore. You just point the bow at whatever's farthest off and go."

Marguerite laughed and put the wheel over, easy, two spokes, setting the Kestrel II's nose toward the last of the light where the river bent and vanished between the hills — toward the lake country, the passes, the provinces beyond, the whole unmapped width of the world. Behind her the crew's voices rose and tangled in the warm air, and the new engine beat steady as a heart, and the old wheel turned smooth under her hands.

"Tell the delegation," she said, "we'll be there by the autumn rains. Tell them we always are."

The river ran on ahead of them, silvering into dusk, going everywhere at once, and Marguerite Ashgrove followed it — not fleeing, not chasing, not paying any debt at all — simply moving, the way water moves, toward the horizon, which was not a place, and had never been a place, and which opened, the closer you came to it, into more river than one life could ever run.
