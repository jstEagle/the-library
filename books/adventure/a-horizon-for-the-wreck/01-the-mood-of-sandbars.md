## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The Verrazano Reach had a mood the way old men had moods, and Sable Villalobos had learned to read both from a distance, without ever getting close enough to be blamed for what they did next.

That morning the river was in one of its gray, thoughtful tempers—flat water the color of unpolished pewter, mist sitting on it like a held breath. The *Kestrel's Debt* moved through it at her usual unhurried pace, her engine ticking over at half-throttle because there was no reason to hurry and every reason not to be seen hurrying anywhere in particular. Fifteen years on the Reach had taught Sable that speed attracted attention, and attention was the one cargo they never carried.

They measured their days in miles now. It had been years since they'd measured them in anything else. A mile was an honest unit: it asked nothing, promised nothing, and always came due exactly when expected. Sixty miles from Harrowgate down to Saltmarsh Landing, another forty if the charter wanted to go as far as the weir, and then back up again before dark. That was today. That was most days.

Sable stood at the wheel with one hand resting on the spokes and the other holding a tin cup of coffee gone bitter from sitting too long, and watched the sandbars make their slow argument with the current. There was a bar off Gullwrack Point that shifted every spring flood, wandering like a drunk trying to find his own street, and every year Sable relearned its temper by feel—the way the water wrinkled over it, the particular brown-green stain where the bottom came up to meet the light. Charts said one thing. The river said another. Sable had long ago decided to believe the river.

"Captain."

The voice came from behind, from the passenger bench under the canopy where the day's sole charter sat wrapped in a wool coat too fine for river travel. Sable didn't turn. "Mister Quill."

"You said we'd pass something worth seeing around this bend."

"I said there'd be water worth seeing. Whether you see anything in it is your business." Sable took a sip of the dead coffee. It tasted like the inside of a kettle, which was to say like home.

Quill was a mapmaker's agent out of Coldharbour, or so he'd claimed at the dock, paying his fare in crisp notes and asking polite questions about channels and depths. He had the kind of face that was easy to forget five minutes after looking away, which Sable noted professionally and distrusted accordingly. Men with forgettable faces were usually paid to have them.

But he'd paid double for the run and asked nothing about Sable's business, which put him ahead of most passengers by a comfortable margin.

"There," Quill said, leaning forward. "Those birds. What are they standing on?"

Sable glanced. Off the starboard bow, a scatter of gulls stood ankle-deep on a bar that shouldn't have been showing until midsummer. The water around it lay thin as spilled tea.

"That's the Gullwrack shoal," Sable said. "And it's showing early."

"Early how?"

"Early enough that I don't like it." Sable eased the wheel a spoke to port, giving the bar a wider berth than the chart demanded. Old habit. The river was full of things that showed early and cost late.

* * *

The debts were quiet these days, which was how Sable liked them.

There were three that mattered. The first belonged to a woman named Odile Ferrant who ran the coal yard at Harrowgate and who never once mentioned, in eleven years of monthly payments, the favor she'd done a frightened young deckhand with a stolen boat and a warrant with their name misspelled on it. She just held out her hand, counted the notes, said *"Same time next month, Villalobos,"* and went back to weighing coal. The debt would take four more years to die. Sable intended to outlive it.

The second debt was owed to nobody living, and could never be paid, and was the reason the boat was named what it was named. Sable did not think about it on days ending in even numbers.

The third was small and practical: a hundred and forty crowns still owed to the engine-wright at Weir Lock for a rebuilt fuel pump, plus interest that the wright pretended didn't exist and Sable pretended not to notice him pretending. Between the three of them, the debts formed a kind of architecture. They gave the miles a shape. Without them, Sable sometimes suspected, the days would simply pour away like water through an open bilge.

Port gossip said otherwise, of course. Port gossip said a lot of things. At Saltmarsh Landing, where Sable tied up for an hour to take on fresh water and let Quill stretch his legs, the gossip was waiting the way gossip always was, perched on the dock bollards like the gulls.

"Heard Marchetti's people bought up the salvage concession for the whole lower reach," said the harbormaster's clerk, a round man named Petch whose conversation arrived pre-chewed. "Whole reach, Villalobos. Forty years of wrecks, all of it theirs now. You believe that?"

"I believe it's none of mine," Sable said, coiling the water hose.

"They say the Condesa's in there somewhere. The Condesa del Mar. You remember the Condesa?"

Everyone remembered the Condesa. Forty years gone with all hands and a sealed manifest, lost somewhere between the point and the deep channel, and forty years of salvagers, dreamers, and liars claiming to have found her. The Marchettis had built half their fortune on shipping and the other half on being the kind of family whose name opened doors that stayed shut for everyone else. If they'd bought the concession, it was because they knew something, or thought they did, or wanted everyone else to think they did.

"Wrecks keep," Sable said. "That's the whole virtue of them." They signed the water chit, tipped Petch nothing because tips were remembered longer than faces, and walked back up the gangplank without looking at anyone directly. It was a skill, not looking at people directly. It had taken years to learn and had kept them free for all fifteen of them.

Quill was already back aboard, standing at the rail with his hands clasped behind him, watching the far shore with the fixed attention of a man memorizing it.

"Find what you were looking for?" Sable asked, taking the wheel.

"Geology, mostly." Quill smiled. He had a good smile, warm as a stove, and Sable trusted it exactly as far as the coffee. "You've run this river a long time, Captain."

"Longer than some rivers have run themselves."

"Then you'll know better than I whether the tide tables are lying." He produced a folded sheet from his coat—not printed, but hand-copied, the ink still sharp—and held it out. "My office compiles these. There's a spring tide forecast for the end of the month that the almanac doesn't account for. Lunar conjunction, pressure trough, the sort of thing that happens once in a generation. They're calling for the lowest water on record along the lower reach. Low enough, my colleagues suggest, to change the shape of the coastline for a few hours."

Sable looked at the paper without taking it. "Tides come, tides go."

"These tides," Quill said mildly, "come further out than any charted sounding in the archive. There are features on the riverbed, Captain, that no living person has seen. Perhaps some that no person has seen at all." He set the sheet on the chart table, weighted it with his brass compass, and stepped back as though the matter were settled. "For your professional interest. Consider it a tip, against the day someone offers you a charter to look at a cave."

"I don't do cave charters."

"No," Quill agreed pleasantly. "I imagine you don't do much of anything twice."

He spent the rest of the run in silence, which Sable appreciated, and disembarked at Harrowgate with a nod and a generous tip, which Sable appreciated less, because generosity was also remembered.

* * *

Night came down on the Reach the way it always did, all at once, like a lid.

Sable tied up at a private mooring three miles below town—a rotting piling arrangement rented from a farmer who asked no questions because he was paid not to—and ate supper standing at the galley counter: bread, hard cheese, an apple going soft. The cabin smelled of diesel and old varnish and, faintly, of the river itself, which got into everything the way grief gets into a house.

Fifteen years. Sable turned the number over the way you'd turn a coin you found in the street, checking both faces. Fifteen years since the mutiny, since the night everything burned and broke and scattered, since Juno—

They stopped the thought at the dock, as usual, and made it wait outside.

It wasn't loneliness. Sable had decided that long ago, and the decision held the way a well-caulked seam holds. Loneliness was wanting company. What Sable had was solitude, which was different, which was chosen, which fit like an old coat worn soft at the elbows—too familiar to be comfortable anymore, perhaps, but too well-shaped to replace. A crew meant names on a manifest. Names meant records. Records meant questions, and questions, on the Verrazano Reach, meant warrants, and warrants meant the whole careful architecture of miles and silent debts coming down at once like a badly loaded hold.

One person aboard. One name, half of it false. One boat with a debt for a figurehead. That was the whole inventory, and it balanced, and balance was survival.

So it was not loneliness when Sable caught themselves talking aloud to the engine room at midnight, running through the maintenance litany in a low voice just to hear words in the air. It was procedure. It was not loneliness when they paused on the aft deck with a mug of tea and listened to the far-off bells of Harrowgate counting ten, imagining—for no reason, for no purpose—who else might be listening. It was vigilance. Knowing who else was awake on the river was a security measure.

The coat fit. It had always fit. That was the trick of it, and the trap.

* * *

The tide table sat on the chart table where Quill had left it, brass compass pinning it flat, and Sable managed to ignore it for almost two hours.

Then, somewhere past one in the morning, with the lamp turned low and the river making its small patient sounds against the hull, Sable gave in and picked it up.

Hand-copied figures, columns of them, dates running through the end of the month. Most matched the almanac within inches. But the last week of the month climbed steadily, each tide lower than the last, the numbers dropping off the page like a man descending stairs into a cellar—and on the final date, circled twice in someone's tidy hand, a figure so far below low-water ordinary that Sable read it three times to be sure the copyist hadn't slipped.

Minus nine feet on the Gullwrack gauge. Nine feet below anything the charts recorded. Nine feet of riverbed laid bare along the whole lower reach, for however long the conjunction held—an hour, maybe two.

Sable knew the lower reach the way you know the back of your own hand, including the parts you try not to look at. Nine feet down off Gullwrack Point would expose the shoal entire, and the channel floor beyond it, and whatever the river had swallowed and kept. Wreck timbers, certainly. Old anchors, mooring rings, the bones of a century of small disasters.

And caves. The cliffs below Gullwrack Point were hollowed with sea caves, every child on the Reach knew that, though the water never left them enough to enter. Every child also knew the story attached to them—that the Condesa del Mar had been seen running for the Point in the storm that took her, close inshore where no sane pilot would steer, and that she'd vanished between one lightning flash and the next. Swallowed, the old men said. Taken into the hill.

Forty years of nonsense. Forty years of divers drowning on rumor.

Sable set the table down, picked it up again, and stood at the aft rail in the cold with it, reading by the mooring lantern while the river breathed beneath the hull. The sensible move was to leave it on the chart table for the next passenger to find. Better: drop it over the side. A paper like this was a magnet. It drew surveyors, brokers, men with forgettable faces—and it drew the people who hired them, and the people those people answered to. The Marchettis had just bought the whole lower reach. Whatever the tide gave back at the end of this month would belong to them by law, and men who owned the law did not share what it recovered.

Stay invisible. That was the whole doctrine, fifteen years deep. See nothing, keep nothing, owe nothing but the debts already owed.

Sable folded the tide table along its creases, once, twice, and stood holding it over the black water for a long moment.

Then, without deciding to—in the same way you swallow, in the same way you flinch—they slid it inside their coat, flat against the ribs, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

Below the rail, the river ran on in the dark, keeping its secrets the way it always had.

For twenty-six more days, anyway.
