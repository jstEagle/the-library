## 3. Fifteen Years of Miles

The ink froze twice before Juno Reyes finished the first line.

That was Harrowgate in late autumn for you—dockside clerk's office, a room the size of a coffin standing on end, one window facing the gray water of the Verrazano Reach and one iron stove that gave heat to whoever stood closest and no one else. Juno had learned to write in gloves with the fingertips cut off. She had learned to write standing up, because sitting meant the cold crept up through the chair. She had learned, over six years of tallying cargo manifests in this room, to keep her head down and her handwriting neat, because neat handwriting was invisible, and invisibility was the only pension a woman like her would ever collect.

She dipped the pen again. The ink had gone to slush in the well. She held the well in both hands like a small wounded bird and waited.

*Sable,* she wrote.

Then she stopped, because there were fifteen years behind that name and she didn't know which ones to put on paper.

* * *

The mutiny had happened on a night so clear the stars looked close enough to steal.

Juno had been twenty-two, second mate aboard the *Prosperity Rose*, a grain hauler running the middle Reach, and the captain—Captain Aldous Marchetti the elder's man, a soft-bellied tyrant named Wren—had ordered the flogging of a deckhand named Ivo for the crime of dropping a coil of rope. Not even dropping it. Letting it slip. Juno could still see the coil sliding off the pin, could still see Ivo's face as it went, that half-second of *oh*, the way a man's whole future can hang on a rope's weight.

They strung Ivo up at the mainmast. And the crew—hungry, unpaid for three months, worked past reason—looked at each other the way people do when they realize, all at once and without a word, that they have already decided.

Juno had said the first word. She'd never told anyone that. In every version of the story she'd let settle over the years, the mutiny had been a tide, a thing that rose without authorship. But it had a first word, and the first word was hers: *"No."*

Sable had said the second. Or maybe the second word was just Sable's hand on Wren's shoulder, turning him around, and the third word was the whole crew coming off the rail like weather.

It had gone wrong the way these things go wrong—fast, then slow, then fast again. Wren's mates had knives. Ivo, half-flogged and furious, had gotten hold of a belaying pin and done damage that couldn't be undone by any court's mercy. By dawn the *Prosperity Rose* rode at anchor off a nameless shingle beach, her officers rowed ashore with their boots tied around their necks, and eleven people who had been crew were now, by every law on the Reach, criminals.

They'd scattered. That was the plan—scatter, go dark, go quiet, let the warrants cool. Juno had gone upriver, changed her name twice, learned bookkeeping because nobody suspects a clerk. Sable had gone sideways, into the in-between water, onto the riverboats where a person with no past could buy a berth with competence and silence.

The promise had come last, at the shingle beach, in the gray hour when the boats were pulling away. Juno had gripped Sable's forearm hard enough to leave marks.

"Whatever happens," she'd said, "we don't forget each other. Fifteen years, fifty years. You come find me. Or I come find you. We don't forget."

Sable had looked at her with that flat river-gray gaze and said, "I don't forget anything."

It wasn't a lie when they said it. That was the worst part. It had only become a lie the way a river becomes a delta—slowly, by deposit, one missed year at a time. Fifteen years. Juno had sent three letters. She'd received one, eight years ago, a single line on riverboat stationery: *Still afloat. Don't look for me.* She'd obeyed, because obeying was what Juno did, because obedience was how you survived a man like Wren and then a world full of men like Wren.

But she'd never stopped writing the letters in her head.

* * *

The ink had thawed. Juno bent over the page.

*I'm not going to waste your time with the part where I ask how you are, because I know you'd lie, and I know you'd lie in a way that was technically true, which is worse. So: here is what I have to say, and then you can burn this letter and go on being a rumor, and I will never trouble you again. I mean that. I've had fifteen years to get good at not troubling people.*

*Three days ago a survey barge came up the Reach and anchored off the Gullwrack headland. I signed its mooring fees myself, which is how I know the name on the charter: Marchetti. Four surveyors, a lead-line man, and a fellow in a good coat who did no work at all and watched everything. They were charting the approach to the sea caves below the headland. The deep ones. The ones that only open when the tide goes out like it's holding its breath.*

*I know what you're going to say. Caves are caves. Surveys are surveys. But Sable—I clerk the harbor ledgers. I've seen the Marchetti salvage concession filings. The Gullwrack approach isn't in any of them. They are looking for something they haven't told the registry they're looking for, which means whatever it is, they want it before the paperwork can catch them.*

*And there's a second thing, and I've written this paragraph four times and thrown it in the stove four times, so I'll write it plain and let it stand: a dockworker came through last week, drunk, talking about a riverboat captain who'd been asking after tide tables. Asking about the freak lows. Asking the kind of questions a person asks when they've already found something and want to know how long they have before someone else finds it too.*

*I told myself it wasn't you. I've told myself that about a lot of things for fifteen years.*

*Here is the part I swore I wouldn't put in the letter, so naturally here it is. Ivo's sister lives in Harrowgate now. Did you know that? She runs a laundry off Cordwainer Lane. Her name is Maren. She has a son. She has never once, in fifteen years, said a word against you—not to the magistrates, not to the Marchetti men who came asking after the *Prosperity Rose* crew two years back, not to anyone. When they showed her a paper and asked if she knew where the mutineers had gone, she looked them in the eye and said, "The river takes people. That's what rivers do."*

*She covered for you, Sable. She covered for both of us. And now the Marchettis are charting caves under a headland forty miles from her door, and I am the only person in Harrowgate who knows enough to be afraid of it, and I am writing to a ghost because the ghost is the only one who ever knew how to run water like this.*

*Come to Harrowgate. Don't come to Harrowgate. Burn this letter. Frame it. I don't care. I just couldn't carry it alone anymore, and you were the only other person who was there.*

*The tide tables are wrong this year. Everyone says so. The lows are lower than they've been in forty years.*

*Forty years. Think about what was on the water forty years ago, and then think about why a man in a good coat is paying mooring fees to look at a hole in a cliff.*

*—J. Reyes, Clerk, Harrowgate Harbor Office*

* * *

She read it through once by stove-light. It was too long. It was too honest. It was, she thought, exactly the kind of letter that got a dockside clerk with a forged second name noticed by the wrong people, and she found, to her own mild surprise, that she didn't care.

Six years in this office. Six years of mooring fees and cargo tallies and the particular smell of wet rope and ledger ink. Six years of being *Mistress Reyes, Clerk* to men who tipped their hats because she held the pen that recorded their fees, not because they respected her. She had built a life the way you build a shelter in a flood—low, tight, nothing that catches the current. And it had worked. It had worked for six years, which was longer than anything had worked since the *Prosperity Rose*.

But the survey barge had come, and the man in the good coat had stood at the rail with his hands clasped behind him like a man at a wedding, and when Juno had brought the mooring chit up for signature he'd smiled at her and said, "Fine stretch of water, this. Deep in places, I'm told. Uncharted places." And he'd watched her face while he said it. The way Wren used to watch the crew eat, checking for hunger.

His name was Corliss. She'd copied it onto the fee ledger and felt the letters go down like a fishhook.

She folded the letter, sealed it with a stub of wax she kept for official correspondence, and addressed it to the only dead-drop she still trusted: a chandler's in Millbrook, two hundred miles downriver, run by a woman who owed Juno a favor from a life neither of them mentioned. The letter would sit in a drawer there until a riverboat captain with no fixed name came through, as one eventually did. Or it wouldn't. Or the captain would burn it unread.

Juno held the sealed letter for a long moment, feeling its small weight, its enormous weight.

"Your move," she said to nobody. To the river. To fifteen years of miles.

* * *

The flashback came to her that night, as it always did, uninvited, in the hour before sleep when the mind stops guarding its doors.

Not the mutiny itself—she'd made her peace with the mutiny, or as much peace as a person makes with the best and worst night of their life, which are often the same night. No. What came was the morning after. The shingle beach. Eleven mutineers standing in the gray light with their belongings in bundles, strangers now to each other and to their own names.

Sable had stood apart, as Sable always stood apart, at the waterline where the stones gave way to sand, and Juno had gone to them.

"You could stay with me," Juno had said. "Upriver. We could—"

"No." Not cruel. Just final, the way a rudder is final. "Warrants travel upriver faster than people do. I'll take the water. You take the land. We don't write, we don't ask, we don't—"

"We don't forget," Juno had finished. "I know. You said."

Sable had looked at her then, and for one unguarded second—Juno had kept that second for fifteen years, turned it over like a coin, worn its edges smooth—something in that flat gray face had cracked open. Something that looked, in the gray light, like grief.

"I don't forget anything," Sable had said again. And then, quieter, almost drowned by the water: "That's the problem with me, Juno."

And then Sable had waded out to the skiff, and the skiff had gone east into the fog, and Juno had stood on the shingle until the fog closed like a door.

She had spent fifteen years deciding what that sentence meant. *That's the problem with me.* At first she'd thought it meant the remembering hurt too much, that Sable was warning her off, protecting her the way you protect someone by pushing them away. Later she'd decided it meant something harder: that Sable remembered everything—the flogging, Ivo's face, the sound the belaying pin made—and carried it all, and could not put any of it down, and could not stand to be near anyone who might add to the load.

Now, older, she suspected it meant none of those things. She suspected it meant: *I will remember you, and I will never come back, and I need you to know those are the same decision.*

* * *

Three days later, the harbor office bell rang at an hour when the bell had no business ringing.

Juno went to the door with the ledger under her arm out of pure habit and found a harbor runner, a boy of maybe fourteen, soaked to the thighs and out of breath.

"Message for the office," he gasped. "Off a downbound packet. Chandler's at Millbrook says there's—says there's a reply come back. For the clerk. Personal."

Juno's hands went cold in a way the stove could not fix.

The runner held out a single sheet, riverboat stationery, water-spotted, the handwriting slanted and hard as channel markers. She read it standing in the doorway with the cold coming in off the Reach and the boy waiting for a coin.

It said, in its entirety:

*Juno. Stay away from the headland. Stay away from Corliss. If the survey barge is still anchored when you get this, count its lights every night and write down the number. I'm coming upriver. Don't ask me how soon. The answer will only be wrong.*

*—S.*

*P.S. Tell Maren Ivo's sister the river doesn't forget either.*

Juno read it three times. Then she folded it, put it inside her glove, against her palm, where she could feel it, and paid the boy double, and went back to her desk, and opened the mooring ledger, and began, with a steady hand, to record the day's fees.

That night, from her room above the chandlery where she lodged, she counted the lights on the survey barge off the Gullwrack headland.

There were nine of them.

There had been seven, the night before.

Someone had come aboard.
