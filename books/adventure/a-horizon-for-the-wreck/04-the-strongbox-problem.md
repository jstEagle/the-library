## 4. The Strongbox Problem

Sable did not sleep that night. They told themselves it was the current—the Reach running high with snowmelt from the Kestrels, pushing against the *Marrow's* hull in long, muscular shoves that made the mooring lines groan like a ship in mourning. That was a lie. The lines were fine. The river was fine. It was the strongbox sitting on the deck beneath an oilcloth tarp, six feet from where Sable lay in the wheelhouse bunk, that would not let them rest.

It weighed nothing. That was the strange part. Forty pounds of brass and iron, and it weighed nothing at all, because weight was a thing you could measure, and this—whatever this was—refused to be measured. Sable had carried crates of contraband brandy heavier than it. Had carried a stolen dinghy over their shoulder through a flooded cane field at nineteen. Weight was familiar. Weight could be paid off, mile by mile, the way debts could.

This was not weight. This was a question.

*Take it back,* said the voice of fifteen years' discipline, calm as a harbor pilot. *Wait for the next low tide. Row out to Gullwrack. Put it exactly where you found it. Walk away. Nobody knows. Nobody ever has to know.*

It was good advice. It was the best advice Sable had ever given themselves, and they had given themselves excellent advice for a decade and a half: don't stop in Harrowgate, don't answer letters, don't learn the names of dockhands because names become attachments and attachments become anchors. Don't be anyone. Be a wake on the water, nothing more.

Sable rolled out of the bunk, lit the lamp, and went to look at the box again, because apparently fifteen years of discipline had drowned somewhere between the cave mouth and the boat.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and thin over the Reach, and Sable sat cross-legged on the deck with the strongbox between their knees, working at the lock with a marlinspike and a growing contempt for Marchetti craftsmanship.

The seal had been stamped into the lid—a crest, a wreath of wheat around a tower, pressed deep enough to survive forty years underwater without losing its edges. Sable had seen that crest before. Every lock station on the upper Reach flew it on a painted board. Every toll collector wore it on a brass button. The Marchettis didn't own the river; they owned the idea of the river, which was worth more.

The lock itself was a simple lever tumbler, salted shut with corrosion. Sable worked the spike into the seam above the hasp, braced their boots against the deck ring, and leaned.

Nothing. Then a shriek of metal, a sound like a gull being murdered, and the hasp tore free in a spray of green-crusted rivets. The lid came up on hinges stiff as old men's knees.

Inside, wrapped in oilskin gone the color of tea, lay two things.

The first was a ledger. Not a logbook—a manifest, bound in cracked leather, the pages furred with damp but legible, written in a clerk's careful copperplate. *Condesa del Mar. Outbound manifest, final voyage.* Sable turned the pages with hands they kept deliberately steady. Cargo listed in neat columns: baled cotton, salted pork, machine parts, specie. And then, on the third page, an entry that stopped the breath in Sable's chest:

*One sealed case, legal documents, consigned to the office of Aldous Marchetti Sr., Harrowgate. To be opened only upon safe arrival.*

Legal documents. On a ship that sank within sight of home. Documents so important they'd been locked in brass and stamped with the family seal—and then, when the ship went down, never mentioned again. Never salvaged. Never mourned. Just erased, along with everyone who might have remembered them.

The second item was smaller. A single sheet, folded twice, protected inside a leather sleeve that had done its work well. Sable unfolded it under the lamp and read it three times, because the first two times the words refused to arrange themselves into sense.

It was a bill of sale. Dated eleven years ago. Conveyance of salvage rights to the wreck of the *Condesa del Mar*, all claims, from one **Emeric Voss** to **Aldous Marchetti Jr.**, for consideration of four hundred crowns.

Sable read the name again. Emeric Voss. Their father. Dead nine years, and still finding ways to complicate Sable's life from beyond whatever quiet grave he'd been lowered into.

But that wasn't the problem. The problem was the date—or rather, what sat beside the date. Because Sable knew, the way a pilot knows a channel, that Emeric Voss had been in the Verrazano Penitentiary on that date. Sable had visited him there. Had sat across a scarred table from him while he complained about the food and asked, for the hundredth time, whether Sable had found honest work yet. A man does not sign bills of sale from a cell block. A man cannot sell what he is legally forbidden to hold.

And beneath his signature, notarized and stamped, the clerk's certification: witnessed and entered at the Harrowgate registry, *by order of Magistrate's warrant.*

The concession the Marchettis held—the paper that gave them exclusive right to the cave, the wreck, everything sleeping in Gullwrack—rested on a signature forged by a man in prison, certified by a magistrate's office that had either been fooled or bought, and Sable would have bet the *Marrow* on bought.

They sat very still for a long time. The river slapped the hull. Somewhere upstream a lock horn sounded, flat and patient.

*Throw it in the river,* said the voice of self-preservation, and this time it wasn't calm. This time it had an edge of panic underneath, because the arithmetic had changed. Before, the box was a mystery. Now it was evidence. Evidence was not property. Evidence was a debt owed to people Sable had never met—to a crew dead forty years, to whoever had sealed those depositions aboard expecting them to reach daylight. And debts to the dead were the only kind Sable had never figured out how to run from.

*Throw it in. Let the river keep its secrets. You've kept yours this long.*

Sable stood up with the bill of sale in one hand and walked to the rail.

The water below was brown-green, moving fast, hungry. It would take the paper and the ledger and the box and grind them into silt, and by tomorrow there would be nothing anywhere in the world except what lived behind Sable's eyes—and Sable's eyes, they knew, were not a reliable archive. Fifteen years of forgetting was a practiced skill, but it was not perfect, and some things leaked through at night.

They held the paper over the water.

And thought about Juno Reyes.

Not deliberately. Juno simply arrived, the way she always did, uninvited and grinning, sixteen years old and elbow-deep in an engine they were both too young to be trusted with, saying, *You know what your trouble is, Voss? You think disappearing is the same as being free.* She'd said it once, on the worst night of both their lives, and Sable had spent fifteen years proving her wrong, or trying to, and had never once managed to forget she'd said it.

If Sable dropped this paper into the Reach, the Marchettis' claim stayed clean. The forgery stayed buried. And if anyone else ever got close to the truth—if a dockside clerk in Harrowgate noticed surveyors charting a cave approach, say, and started asking inconvenient questions—then the people asking questions would be dealt with the way such people were always dealt with. Quietly. Procedurally. With warrants and cells and sentences set for weeks no lawyer could reach.

Sable lowered their hand.

They stood at the rail until their arm ached, holding the bill of sale over open water, and understood—with the clarity that comes only at moments of genuine cowardice—that they were not strong enough to do the smart thing. That the smart thing required a kind of emptiness they no longer owned. Somewhere between the cave and this rail, without noticing the transaction, Sable had stopped being a person who could let this drown.

"Damn it," they said aloud, to the river, to the gulls, to nobody. "Damn it, Juno."

Then they folded the paper back into its sleeve, wrapped the manifest in its oilskin, closed the ruined box, and carried everything aft.

* * *

The engine room of the *Marrow* was Sable's cathedral, and like most cathedrals it was built on secrets. Beneath the floor plates, beneath the bilge stringers, there was a void between the fuel tank and the hull plating—a gap wide enough for a strongbox, dry in all but the worst weather, accessible only by lying flat on your back in grease and reaching into a space no inspector had ever thought to check. Sable had found it the first year and used it for things that needed to not exist: papers, coin, once a whole crate of untaxed tobacco that had funded a winter in Port Calloway.

They laid the box in the void, wedged it with a strip of rubber belting so it wouldn't shift in rough water, and slid the floor plate back over it. Then they sat down on the engine housing, wiped their hands on a rag that was already blacker than their hands, and looked at the spot on the deck where the box now invisibly lived.

It should have felt like safety. It felt like burial.

Because here was the thing Sable could not stop seeing: hiding it was not the same as throwing it away. Throwing it away would have ended the question. Hiding it just moved the question below decks, where it would ride along through every lock and every port, humming under the engine noise, present in every conversation with every charter passenger who mentioned tides or caves or the Marchetti name. Sable had hidden things before and forgotten them completely. They already knew, with sick certainty, that they would not forget this one.

Self-preservation, Sable reflected, had always been simple. It was a compass with one bearing: *away*. Run from the mutiny. Run from the warrants. Run from the letters signed *Juno* that sat unopened in a drawer for two years until the drawer got flooded and the ink ran and Sable told themselves that settled the matter. One bearing, held for fifteen years, and it had worked—it had kept them alive, uncaught, unattached, free in the narrowest possible meaning of the word.

But a compass with one bearing doesn't get you anywhere. It gets you everywhere, which amounts to the same thing. Miles and miles and miles of everywhere, and not one mile of it home.

The box under the floor plates was the first thing in fifteen years Sable had chosen to carry *toward* something instead of away. They hated it. They caught themselves, twice in the next hour, going aft to lift the plate and confirm it was still there, as if the river might change its mind and come take it, as if some part of them hoped it would.

By afternoon the light had turned gold and the wind had swung around to the northwest, and Sable did what Sable always did with unbearable knowledge: they worked. Greased the winch. Repainted the nameboard, touching up the *M*'s flaking gilt. Cooked rice with too much pepper and ate it standing at the galley counter, watching the Reach slide past, brown and broad and indifferent.

Indifferent. That was the word that finally undid them, standing there with the spoon halfway to their mouth. The river didn't care what slept in Gullwrack Cave. The magistrate's clerks wouldn't care. The whole vast machinery of the Reach—locks and tolls and registries and concessions—was built to not care, to process paper and move barges and never once ask whether the paper was true. Forty years ago someone had sealed testimony into a strongbox and trusted it to a ship, and the ship had failed, and the machinery had shrugged, and the Marchettis had bought the shrug.

Nobody was coming to care about this. If Sable didn't, no one would.

"That's not a burden," Sable said to the empty galley, testing the words. "That's just a fact."

It didn't help. Facts rarely did.

* * *

Toward evening, making way downriver toward the Delft Locks, Sable passed a survey barge anchored off Marker Nine. Two men in good coats stood at its rail with a theodolite, sighting something inland—toward the headland, toward the bluffs above Gullwrack. A third man sat on the barge roof doing nothing at all, which was how hired watchers sat, and he watched the *Marrow* pass with the unhurried attention of a man counting boats.

Sable kept their face bored. Fifteen years of practice made boredom a costume they could put on in half a second. They raised two fingers off the wheel in lazy salute, and the watcher touched his cap, and the moment passed, and Sable's heart hammered for a quarter mile afterward.

They weren't looking for the box. Couldn't be—they had no reason to think it existed outside the cave. But they were looking at the cave, and the cave was looking at Sable now, and the distance between those two facts was shrinking with every mile.

At dusk Sable tied up at a willow landing ten miles short of the locks, a mud bank with a fire ring and no name, and climbed to the wheelhouse roof with the last of the coffee. The stars came out hard and cold. Upstream, invisible in the dark, the Kestrels were already wearing their first white, and the weather-glass on the bulkhead had begun its slow, stubborn fall.

Sable thought about the tide table folded in their coat pocket. Thought about the next spring low, eight days out, when the cave mouth would open again and the box could go home to the dark where it belonged. Eight days. Plenty of time to row back up, return it, and put the whole affair behind them like a bad charter.

They took out the tide table, smoothed it on their knee, and read it by lamplight.

Then they took out a pencil, and instead of marking the return date, they wrote something else in the margin, small, in handwriting they barely recognized as their own: *Harrowgate. Juno.*

The pencil hovered. The river ran. Under the floor plates, forty pounds of brass rode in the dark, weighing nothing at all.

Sable underlined the name once, blew out the lamp, and went below—telling themselves, in the last honest corner of the mind, that they hadn't decided anything yet.

They were lying, and the river knew it, and by morning so would the whole Reach.
