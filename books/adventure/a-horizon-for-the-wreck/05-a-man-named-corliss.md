## 5. A Man Named Corliss

Dane Corliss had a theory about rivers: they were the only honest ledgers left in the world. Every debt flowed downstream eventually. Every secret, given enough rain, surfaced. He had built a career on being the man standing at the outflow with a net.

He stood now on the dock at Tallow Bend, watching a riverboat come down the Reach with the particular unhurried arrogance of a vessel that owed nothing to anyone. She was an old boat—forty years if she was a day, probably older in her bones—with a patched wheelhouse and a name that had been painted over so many times it read as a smudge: *Vesper*, or *Vespera*, or possibly nothing at all. The pilot at the wheel was a lean figure in a salt-stiffened coat, face half-shadowed under the brim of a hat that had been rained on and dried and rained on again until it had the texture of old paper.

Sable Villalobos.

Corliss smiled at the dock, at the gulls, at the gray morning generally—a smile he kept polished the way other men kept their boots. Fifteen years, the file said. Fifteen years of running quiet water, paying cash, refusing charters longer than three days, never tying up twice in the same port within a season. A person could live like that for a long time. But a person who lived like that also developed habits, and habits were the handles by which you lifted them.

The Marchettis wanted the cave. The Marchettis wanted silence around the cave. And somewhere between those two wants sat this boat, this pilot, this morning—and Corliss intended to find out which side of the ledger Sable Villalobos stood on before the tide turned.

The *Vesper* came alongside without ceremony. No deckhand threw a line; there was no deckhand. The pilot stepped off, made fast, and looked up with eyes the color of river slate—assessing him, Corliss noted with pleasure, exactly as thoroughly as he was assessing them.

"Captain Villalobos." He extended a hand. "Dane Corliss. Salvage broker, out of Harrowgate."

A pause, precisely long enough to be rude without quite being an insult. Then the hand took his—dry, strong, calloused across the palm in the pattern of someone who'd spent decades hauling line.

"I don't take salvage work," Sable said.

"Good thing I'm not offering any." Corliss let the handshake end naturally, no squeeze, no games. Intimidation was for men who hadn't learned charm. "I need a pilot. Upriver run, Harrowgate to Coldharbour and back. My own boat's in drydock with a cracked shaft, and every licensed pilot on this stretch is either booked, drunk, or dead. You come recommended."

"By whom?"

"A woman at the fuel dock in Pell's Landing. Tall, opinionated, charges too much." He spread his hands. "You know how it is. Reputation travels faster than boats."

Something moved behind those slate eyes—not warmth, but calculation. Good. Stupid people were exhausting.

"Rate's high," Sable said.

"Rate's whatever you say it is. I'm not a man who haggles over the difference between expensive and very expensive." He gestured up the gangplank. "May I? I'll confess I've never been aboard one of the old reach-runners, and my curiosity is genuine. It's my worst quality."

*It is almost certainly not,* Sable did not say, but the silence said something adjacent to it, and then—because refusing would be more interesting than accepting, and interesting was dangerous—the captain jerked their chin toward the boarding plank and walked away, leaving Corliss to follow.

Which was, of course, exactly what Corliss had hoped for.

* * *

The cabin below decks smelled of engine oil, coffee, and lamp smoke—the honest smell of a working boat. Corliss catalogued it the way he catalogued everything: the chart table with its charts rolled tight and tied, the single bunk made with military corners, the shelf of tinned food arranged by size rather than type. One cup on the hook by the stove. One spoon in the jar. One plate.

Fifteen years of solitude, laid out like evidence.

"You travel light," he said, settling onto the bench across the table uninvited and finding it solid, well-kept. Everything about this boat was well-kept. That was interesting too. People who planned to disappear didn't maintain their vessels like this. People who maintained their vessels like this were planning to keep running—but running toward something, someday, or running until they could stop.

"I travel alone," Sable corrected, pouring coffee into two tin cups anyway, because hospitality was a habit even fugitives couldn't kill. "That's different."

"Is it?" Corliss wrapped his hands around the cup and did not drink yet. First sips were for after the opening moves. "My mother used to say the same thing. She meant she trusted no one. Turned out she just hadn't met the right accountant." He grinned. "I became the accountant. Family business."

"What family business is that?"

"Moving things from where they are to where they're worth more. Cargo, mostly. Sometimes information. Occasionally regrets." He produced a folded paper from his breast pocket and set it on the table between them, squared its edges with one finger. "Charter agreement. Harrowgate to Coldharbour, four days, your boat, my cargo—which is paperwork, I promise you, the most boring cargo on the river. I'll pay double your going rate, and I'll pay half up front, in coin, because I know you don't touch bank drafts."

He watched the word *coin* land. Watched the small involuntary stillness of a person doing arithmetic against debts they didn't name aloud. The file said Villalobos carried obligations—old ones, quiet ones, the kind that got paid through intermediaries in ports downriver. Everyone had a leash. Most people just didn't know where theirs was held.

"And why me?" Sable asked. "There are pilots in Harrowgate."

"There are pilots everywhere. But I asked around, and your name kept coming up attached to a particular quality." Corliss leaned back, letting the pause stretch like a mooring line taking strain. "You know water nobody else knows. Sandbars that aren't on the charts. Channels that open and close with the seasons. A man told me—you'll forgive me, I've forgotten which man—that Captain Villalobos once ran the Narrows at night during flood stage when every other boat was tied up praying. Is that true?"

"It's true I'm still here."

"Which is better than true." He laughed, warm and easy, and drank his coffee at last. It was good coffee, which annoyed him slightly; he'd have preferred it bad, something else to hold against them. "Here's the honest version, Captain, since honesty disarms people faster than lies. I'm working a salvage assessment for a client. Old wreck, forty years down, possibly exposed by this freak tide cycle we've been having. My client needs a survey done of the approach channels before they commit equipment. I need someone who can read this river the way a priest reads scripture. Your name came up. Here I am."

He said the word *tide* lightly. He said *wreck* lighter still. And he watched.

What he saw was small—so small a lesser observer would have missed it entirely. A fractional tightening at the corner of the jaw. A blink held a half-beat too long. The cup set down with a shade more care than it was picked up. Sable Villalobos heard the words *wreck* and *tide* and something behind those slate eyes went very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when someone inside hears a footstep on the porch.

Corliss's heart performed a small, private flourish.

"Well?" Sable said. Their voice was level. Their voice was very nearly perfect. "Where's this wreck?"

"Gullwrack Point," Corliss said, and watched again. "Some sea cave or other, according to the old charts. Probably nothing. Probably a rumor with barnacles on it. But my client pays me to check rumors, and I pay you to get me there. Four days' work. Coin up front."

The silence that followed had texture. Corliss sat inside it comfortably, the way he sat inside all silences, counting what grew in them. Outside, water slapped the hull in a slow rhythm, patient as bookkeeping.

"I don't do salvage," Sable said finally. "I told you."

"This isn't salvage. This is piloting. There's a difference, and the difference is that only one of us touches the wreck." He tapped the charter agreement. "And I notice you haven't said no. You've said *I don't do salvage*, which is a sentence about principle, and principles are wonderful things—they keep a person's hands clean while their boat does the work."

"You talk a lot for a man who hasn't been told yes."

"I talk a lot for a man who's usually told yes eventually. It saves time later." Corliss smiled and let it fade slowly, deliberately, into something more candid—he'd practiced that transition in mirrors until it stopped looking practiced. "Captain. Let me be plain, because I suspect we're alike in one respect: we both hate wasted mornings. Whatever you think I am—and you clearly think I'm something—I'm not your problem. I'm a man with a contract and a schedule. If you take it, you'll be paid handsomely and never see me again. If you don't take it, I'll find another pilot, and you'll never see me again anyway, and one of those outcomes leaves you richer. I'd hate to see you choose poverty out of stubbornness. Stubbornness is a luxury good. Very few can afford it."

Sable looked at him for a long moment. Looking back, Corliss thought, was the most honest thing the captain had done all morning. Those slate eyes went through him the way a sounding line goes through dark water—down, down, looking for bottom, and finding none, and noting that too.

"No," Sable said.

Corliss blinked. Genuinely, for the first time in years, he was surprised.

"No?"

"I don't take charters past three days. I told the woman at Pell's Landing to tell people that. Apparently she tells people I come recommended instead." Sable stood, collected his cup, and nodded toward the companionway with the flat courtesy of a hotelier ending a visit. "Four days is two days too many. Find another pilot, Mr. Corliss. I hear Harrowgate's full of them."

For a heartbeat—for one unguarded, unforgivable heartbeat—Corliss felt the smile slip on its hinges. Then he caught it, reset it, and rose, extending his hand across the little table one final time.

"Can't blame a man for asking," he said warmly. "And can't blame the river for bringing us alongside. These waters are smaller than they look, Captain. I imagine we'll meet again."

"Rivers only flow one way," Sable said, shaking his hand briefly. "Whatever meets twice was pushed."

*Pushed by whom, though.* Corliss climbed the companionway steps feeling pleasantly bruised, the way he always felt after encountering a mind worth the effort. On deck, the morning had brightened by a single grudging degree. He paused at the gangplank and turned back, delivering his parting shot with the casualness of a man discussing weather:

"Oh—one more thing, since you know these waters. The tide tables this month are remarkable, aren't they? Lowest ebb in forty years, they're saying. Enough to drain places that haven't seen air since before either of us was born." He tipped an imaginary hat. "If you happen to be out on the water when it bottoms out—do give my regards to anything you find. I'm told the sea gives back what it owes, eventually. I've built a whole profession on collecting."

He saw it then, unmistakably, even at six feet's distance: the flinch. Small as a fish turning under the surface. There and gone, and the slate eyes gone flat and blank above it, giving him nothing, giving him absolutely nothing—

Except everything. Except confirmation.

"Safe travels, Mr. Corliss," Sable said, and there was nothing safe anywhere in the words.

* * *

Corliss walked the length of the Tallow Bend dock with his hands clasped behind his back, whistling something old and off-key, and did not allow his expression to change until he reached the warehouse at the end of it, where his launch waited and his assistant, a nervous young man named Pell—no relation to the landing—stood holding a clipboard like a shield.

"Well?" Pell said.

"Well," Corliss said, "our captain has it."

"Has— you mean the box? They found it? Already?"

"The tide hasn't even bottomed out yet, Pell, do try to keep up. They found the *cave*. Or they knew about it before I ever opened my mouth, which frankly I'd bet on—Villalobos doesn't flinch at the word *Gullwrack* unless they've already been there, or already dreamed about it. Either way." He stepped down into the launch and settled himself on the thwart, pulling on his gloves finger by finger, enjoying the small theater of it. "Either way, they refused a four-day charter that would have taken them a hundred miles in the wrong direction, from a stranger paying double rate in coin. Nobody refuses that kind of money out of caution. They refused it because they cannot leave this stretch of river right now. Because they're waiting for something. Or hiding something. Or both, which is my favorite kind of person."

"So what do we do?"

Corliss looked out across the gray water, where the *Vesper* was already making way, sliding north upriver with that same unhurried arrogance, carrying whatever it carried in its hold or its head.

"We do what one does with a locked box," he said. "We don't break it open. We find out what the owner loves, and we put that on the table next to it, and we wait." He smiled at the water, at the gulls, at the whole dishonest shining world. "Everyone trades, Captain. Some of us just haven't been shown the price tag yet."

He untied the launch himself. "Pell. Get me everything on Villalobos—every port, every debt, every name they've so much as shared a meal with in fifteen years. Start with Harrowgate. Start with anyone they might run *to*."

The launch pulled away from the dock, heading north, following the riverboat's wake as it widened and faded among the sandbars.

Behind them, far up the Reach, the tide was still falling.
