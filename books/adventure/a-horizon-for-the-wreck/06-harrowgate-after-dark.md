## 6. Harrowgate, After Dark

The letter had said *come before the equinox*, and the equinox was four days gone.

Sable ran the *Kestrel* upriver through the last of the light, engine pushed to a pitch that made the old hull shudder like a dog shaking off water. The Verrazano narrowed as it climbed toward Harrowgate, its banks closing in from marsh to timber to the first gray shoulders of the Kestrel Range, and every mile Sable covered felt like a mile of cover being stripped away. Fifteen years of practice told them exactly what they were doing: they were making themselves visible. A boat running hard after dark, no cargo manifest filed, no port fees paid at the intermediate locks—anyone watching the river would see it, and Corliss was watching the river. Sable knew that the way you know weather coming in your bad knee.

They went anyway.

That was the thing about guilt, Sable thought, hands steady on the wheel while everything under the hands shook. It didn't wait for understanding. It arrived ahead of it, like floodwater coming down a dry gulch—you heard it before you could say what it was, and by the time you understood, you were already wet. Sable had read Juno's letter a dozen times since Gullwrack Cave. *Surveyors on the cliff road. Marchetti colors on their gear. They're charting the approach, Sab. Somebody sold somebody something, or somebody's looking for something.* And Sable had sat in the cabin with a brass strongbox stamped with the Marchetti seal hidden under their bunk and written back nothing at all, because writing back meant admitting the box existed, and not writing back meant Juno was standing alone on a dock full of men who broke people for a living.

Fifteen years ago Sable had chosen invisibility over Juno once already. The mutiny aboard the *Pale Meridian*, the night everything burned, the promise shouted across black water—*I'll come back for you*—and then the long coward's arithmetic afterward: too many warrants, too many witnesses, too much to lose. Sable had told themselves for fifteen years that going back would have gotten them both hanged. It was mostly true. It had never once been comforting.

Now the tide table from the charter passenger sat folded in Sable's breast pocket like a confession, and the strongbox rode under the engine plates, and the river carried them north toward a promise they'd already broken once, hoping the second time might count for something.

Harrowgate showed itself at dusk: a smear of lamplight along the left bank where the river bent around a shoulder of rock, sawmills shuttered for the season, the great timber flumes silent, and above it all the county courthouse with its cupola lit yellow against the early dark—the one building in town that never went fully dark, because justice, like a fever, kept its own hours.

Sable brought the *Kestrel* in past the mooring buoys with her lights doused, easing up to a rotting private dock two hundred yards downstream of the public landing, behind a screen of dead reeds. Old habit. You didn't tie up at a lit landing when you were carrying something worth stealing and wearing a name worth arresting.

The dock boards groaned but held. Sable looped the lines, stood a moment listening to the town breathe—dogs, a millrace somewhere, the flat horn of the evening packet boat far downstream—and then walked up into Harrowgate with their collar up and their hat low, which fooled nobody, because Sable Villalobos had never once succeeded at looking inconspicuous. Tall, broad-shouldered, moving like someone perpetually stepping over a rope only they could see. Invisibility had always been less a disguise than a discipline: don't stop, don't ask, don't be remembered.

Juno's lodging house was on Cordwainer Street, three blocks up from the water, a narrow gray building squeezed between a chandler's and a shuttered assay office. Sable had the address in the same pocket as the tide table, worn soft from handling. They climbed the outside stairs, knocked on the door at the top, and waited.

No answer.

Knocked again, harder. Down in the street a woman paused with a market basket, looked up, looked away. Sable tried the handle. Locked.

"Looking for Reyes?" The voice came from below—an old man on the bottom step, pipe glowing, face a map of veins. "Third floor's been empty two days."

"Two days." Sable came down the stairs slowly. "She work days? Dock office?"

The pipe smoker gave Sable the look locals give strangers who ask questions they shouldn't need to ask. "Dock office don't keep her neither. Sheriff's men came for her Tuesday night. Something about equipment. Stolen goods." He drew on the pipe. "She seemed a decent sort. Quiet. Kept to herself." A pause, and then, with the mild cruelty of small towns everywhere: "You family?"

"No," Sable said, and heard how the word came out wrong, hollow, like a door opening onto nothing. "A friend."

"Mm," said the old man, who did not believe it, and went back to his pipe.

* * *

The Harrowgate lockup was attached to the sheriff's office on Foundry Row, a squat stone building with iron-barred windows set high enough that even a tall prisoner couldn't see out of them. Sable circled it twice, learning it: single entrance, lamp over the door, a side yard with a woodpile and a privy and a window at ground level barred with iron that was newer than the rest of the wall. Someone had escaped—or tried to—from this place before, and someone had spent money making sure it wouldn't happen again.

There was a tavern across the street called the Anvil & Tide, and Sable hated themselves for how easily they walked into it, because taverns were where information lived, and information was the one currency Sable had never been able to refuse. They took a corner table, ordered whiskey they didn't drink, and listened.

The talk was ordinary—timber prices, a wedding, someone's mule—but the current underneath it wasn't. Sable caught it in fragments, the way you catch fish under ice.

"—Marchetti survey crew, up from Coldharbour, been at the Grand Hotel a fortnight—"

"—clerk woman took the transit instrument and the brass tripod both, right off their barge, they're saying—"

"—Oyelaran himself signed the warrant, that's how you know it's serious—"

"Serious," muttered a sawyer near the stove, "is when a dock clerk suddenly needs stealing instruments worth more than she'd earn in ten years. That's not serious, that's theater."

"Keep your voice down, Aldous's cousin drinks here."

Sable sat very still and let the pieces assemble themselves, and the picture they made was so clean it was almost beautiful. A theft with no witnesses. Equipment belonging to the survey crew—the survey crew Juno had written about, the ones charting the cave approach. A warrant signed fast, by a magistrate whose name Sable had heard whispered at a dozen landings the way sailors whisper about reefs. And the timing: Juno writes a letter saying *they're looking at the cave*, and within the week she's in irons for a crime shaped exactly like the crime of knowing too much.

Guilt arrived ahead of understanding, and now understanding arrived ahead of anything useful. They hadn't come to warn Juno. They had come to watch her get buried, four days late, because Sable Villalobos had spent a week sitting on a strongbox instead of sending word, weighing their own neck against hers, and the scale had tipped the way it always tipped, toward silence, toward miles, toward the comfortable coat of being no one.

The whiskey stayed untouched. Sable paid for it anyway—generously, because generous strangers got forgotten slower than stingy ones—and stepped back out into the dark.

* * *

The side window of the lockup was twenty feet from the woodpile, and the woodpile was shadowed by the courthouse wall, and Sable reached it in the gap between the lamplighter's rounds and the changing of the jailer's watch—a gap they'd counted from the tavern doorway, forty minutes, give or take the jailer's fondness for his supper.

The bars were new iron, but the mortar around them was 1840s stone and lime, and Sable had spent fifteen years learning the weaknesses of things built by other people. A short pry bar from inside their coat, wrapped in cloth to kill the sound, working the lowest bar's footing in slow quarter-turns, pausing whenever a cart passed on Foundry Row. Frost was coming out of the ground with the night cold; the stones sweated; the mortar crumbled like stale bread. Ten minutes. Twelve. The bar came free with a sound no louder than a knuckle cracking, and Sable slid it aside and put an eye to the hole.

The cell beyond was small, lit by a single candle stub, and Juno Reyes was sitting on the cot with her back to the wall, knees drawn up, staring at the floor the way people stare at floors when they've run out of anywhere else to look.

She was thinner than the last time Sable had seen her—seven years ago, a chance passing at Tallow Bend, two boats tied overnight, one bottle shared, all the big things carefully not said. Her hair was shorter. There was a bruise along her jaw going green-yellow at the edges. But she was sitting the way she'd always sat, coiled and patient, a person conserving herself, and seeing it hit Sable somewhere beneath the ribs that armor doesn't cover.

"Juno," Sable said, low, through the bars.

Her head came up fast—too fast for surprise; more like a trap springing in reverse. She crossed the cell in two strides and dropped to her knees at the window, and her eyes in the candlelight were furious and bright and, worst of all, unsurprised.

"Sable Villalobos." Her voice was a whisper with a blade in it. "You have some nerve showing your face at my jail window."

"I came to warn you. I got your letter—I came as fast as—"

"You came four days late." The whisper didn't rise but it sharpened, the way wire does. "I wrote you that they were sniffing at the cave. I wrote you that if anyone came asking, we'd never spoken in years, that you should stay gone, because I knew—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "I knew you wouldn't come. I wrote it so I wouldn't have to watch you not come. And then they arrested me anyway, and I thought, well. At least I was right about one thing."

"I'm here now."

"You're here now." Juno laughed, one dry note, no humor in it anywhere. "Do you know what they charged me with, Sab? Stealing a surveyor's transit. A transit! Worth eight hundred crowns, they say, and I'm supposed to have carried it off a guarded barge and vanished it into thin air, and Magistrate Oyelaran set the hearing for the third week of Frostmonth." She let that sit. Sable felt it land like a stone dropped down a well, taking a long time to hit bottom. "You know what happens the second week of Frostmonth?"

"The pass closes."

"The pass closes. The registry at Coldharbour shuts until spring. Any claim, any filing, any paper that isn't entered by then is worth nothing until thaw—and by thaw, whatever's in that cave will have been picked cleaner than a Sunday chicken." Juno pressed her forehead briefly against the bars, and when she spoke again the fury had cracked and something rawer showed through. "They're not punishing me for stealing, Sab. They're parking me here until it stops mattering. I'm not a defendant. I'm a clock."

Sable's hand found the bar they'd loosened and gripped it, because the alternative was gripping something else. "What did they actually take you for? Did they search your rooms?"

"They searched everything. Took my letters." A pause, deliberate, and her eyes came up and fixed on Sable through the grate with terrible clarity. "All my letters. Every one I ever kept. From anybody."

The frost on the stones seemed to climb Sable's spine. If Juno had kept the old letters—if she'd kept anything from the *Pale Meridian* years, anything with a name, a date, a port—

"They'll have read them," Sable said.

"Some of them. The old ones are water-stained. Half-legible." Juno's mouth twisted. "But the new one wasn't. The one from three weeks ago. The one where I wrote about surveyors and a cave mouth and asked you to come." She leaned closer, and her whisper dropped to almost nothing. "The jailer's clerk reads everything before it goes in the file, Sab. And yesterday a man came from Coldharbour to look at the file. Well-dressed. Charming. Bought the whole office drinks at the Anvil & Tide and laughed at everybody's jokes. Name of Corliss."

The name went through Sable like cold water through a hull. Dane Corliss, who had stood in the *Kestrel*'s cabin a week ago with his hat in his hands and his eyes doing arithmetic, mentioning Gullwrack Cave the way a man mentions the weather, watching Sable's face for the flicker he was paid to find. He'd found it. Of course he'd found it. Sable had spent fifteen years practicing invisibility and had undone it in a single unguarded breath in front of a professional.

"He knows," Sable said. Not a question.

"He knows there's a cave. He knows I know. He knows whoever I wrote to exists, even if your name's smudged off the page." Juno's fingers curled around the bars from her side, so close to Sable's that the iron between them was the only thing they touched. "So here's what I can't figure out, and I've had two days in a cell to figure it. If he knows there's a cave, why hasn't his salvage crew moved on it? Why arrest me, why play games with hearings and clocks—why not just go dig?"

And Sable, who had been carrying the answer under the engine plates of a boat tied in the reeds two hundred yards downstream, felt it become suddenly, enormously heavy. Because the concession was built on forged papers. Because the Marchettis' own seal was stamped on a strongbox that proved it. Because Corliss could chart the approach all he liked, but the moment his crew touched the wreck without clean title, he handed the whole fraud to anyone holding better paper—and he suspected, he must suspect, that someone was holding better paper, or he wouldn't be buying jailers drinks.

"Because he's missing something," Sable said slowly. "Something he needs before he can touch the wreck. And he thinks I have it."

Juno stared. Then, in the manner of a woman who had known Sable for thirty years and been lied to by her for fifteen: "Sab. What did you find?"

Down the street, the lockup door opened. Lamplight spilled across Foundry Row, and voices—two of them, unhurried, the jailer and someone else, someone whose laugh carried even at this distance, warm and easy and wrong. Footsteps on gravel, coming around the side of the building, toward the woodpile, toward the window, toward the two of them kneeling in the dark with their fingers hooked in the same iron.

Juno's eyes went wide. "Go," she breathed. "Sab—go, now—"

Sable didn't move. Fifteen years of running had taught every muscle in their body to move, and for the first time in fifteen years, every one of those muscles refused.

The footsteps stopped. A match flared, and in its light Sable saw the glow settle into a cigar, and above the cigar a familiar face turned toward the woodpile shadows, smiling the smile of a man who has just found exactly what he came out to check for.

"Well," said Dane Corliss pleasantly, into the dark. "Captain. We really have to stop meeting like this."
