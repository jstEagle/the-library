## 7. The Magistrate's Arithmetic

Magistrate Adunni Oyelaran kept a ledger that appeared in no official index. It was a slim black book, bound in calfskin, and it lived in the second drawer of his desk beneath a false bottom of yellowed case reports, and in it he recorded—not bribes, he was careful never to record bribes, because bribes were a word for other men's sins—but *considerations*. Debts of gratitude. Obligations incurred. The arithmetic of a career.

He opened it now, in the blue hour before the courthouse clerks arrived, and ran his thumb down the columns while his tea went cold beside him.

*Marchetti, A. — 14th year. Seat confirmed. Consideration: full.*

Fourteen years. He had been a younger man then, hungry and precise, the kind of magistrate who could recite statute from memory and did, at parties, to people who hadn't asked. Aldous Marchetti had come to him with a problem—a land dispute, nothing dramatic—and Oyelaran had resolved it with a ruling so clean that even the losing party's counsel had shaken his hand afterward. Marchetti had sent a note of thanks. Then another favor had needed doing. Then another. Somewhere in there, without any single transaction he could point to, without any envelope of cash or whispered threat, Oyelaran had become the Marchetti family's magistrate, the way a river becomes a canal: gradually, and then permanently, and always in service of someone else's cargo.

He told himself, as he told himself every morning, that he had never once ruled against the law. That was true, in its way. The law was a wide country with many roads through it, and Oyelaran had simply become expert at choosing which roads to walk, and when, and how slowly. Delay was not corruption. Continuance was not corruption. A docket was a living thing, and a magistrate who claimed he couldn't shape it was either lying or incompetent, and Oyelaran was neither.

The file on his desk this morning was thin. That was the first thing wrong with it. *The Crown v. Reyes, J.* — theft of survey equipment from a Marchetti charter operation, value assessed at some four hundred crowns, evidence consisting of two statements from Marchetti employees and a set of boot prints photographed outside a locked shed. No recovered goods. No witness who had seen her take anything. The accused was a dockside clerk with eleven years of unblemished service and no prior record beyond a public disturbance charge from her river days, long expunged.

Oyelaran read it twice, the way he read everything, hunting for what wasn't there.

What wasn't there was a reason. Four hundred crowns was a petty theft, the kind that came before a junior assessor and ended in a fine. But this file had been routed directly to his chamber, flagged *priority*, with a covering note from the prosecutor's office that used the phrase *matters touching upon ongoing commercial interests of regional significance*. He knew what that phrase meant. He had helped refine it, years ago, in a quiet conversation over brandy with a man whose name he no longer permitted himself to remember.

It meant: *the Marchettis want this woman buried, and they want it done in your courtroom, where it will be done properly.*

He set the file down and looked out the window at Harrowgate waking up—the fish carts, the gulls arguing over the morning's offal, the river running brown and patient under the bridge. Somewhere down on those docks was a clerk named Juno Reyes who had, by every indication, touched nothing but ledgers and ink for a decade. And somewhere upstream, if the harbor gossip that reached even his chambers was to be believed, there was a cave, and a wreck, and a concession worth more than the town it overlooked.

Oyelaran poured out his cold tea and did not drink the fresh cup his clerk brought him. His stomach had been unreliable lately. He suspected it was not the food.

* * *

The prosecutor, a damp young man named Vetch, came at ten o'clock with his collar too tight and his briefcase held like a shield.

"Your Honor. The Reyes matter. We'd like to request an expedited—"

"You'd like to request a delay," Oyelaran said.

Vetch blinked. "Sir?"

"The file says priority. Priority files usually mean someone wants speed." Oyelaran folded his hands on the thin file. "But I've read it, Mr. Vetch, and I'll tell you what I see. I see a case that will fall apart under cross-examination. I see two employees of the aggrieved party as your only witnesses. I see no property recovered. If I try this next week, you lose, and the loss is *loud*, because someone has made it priority. So we're going to be deliberate instead. Deliberate is defensible."

Vetch's throat moved. "And if the—the interested party prefers expedited?"

"Then the interested party should have built a better case." Oyelaran let the silence sit for exactly three seconds, long enough to establish that he was not a man to be managed, and short enough that Vetch would understand he was, entirely, a man to be managed—just not by Vetch. "Set it for the first week of Frostmonth."

Something flickered across the young man's face—relief, quickly smothered. "Frostmonth. Yes, Your Honor. Of course."

Of course. Because everyone in the room understood what Frostmonth meant, even if the court record never would. The Kestrel Pass closed with the first real snows, and the pass closed the road to Coldharbour, and Coldharbour held the land registry, and the registry held the calendar of concessions. The Marchetti salvage concession on the Reach expired at the end of the season. Any challenge to it—any document, any claim, any proof—had to be filed before the pass closed or it died in the snow until spring, and by spring the Marchettis would have stripped the cave to its bones and buried the bones.

A trial in the first week of Frostmonth was not just a trial. It was a vault. Whatever truth existed about Juno Reyes, whatever paper might have cleared her, would be legally worthless by the time the verdict was read. The evidence wouldn't be suppressed. It would simply arrive *late*, and lateness, in law as in tides, was indistinguishable from absence.

Oyelaran wrote the date into the docket himself, in his small precise hand, and felt the familiar sensation—that faint nausea, that pressure behind the eyes—that he had learned, over fourteen years, to classify as *nothing*.

* * *

At midday he walked the long corridor to the records annex, ostensibly to inspect a filing backlog, actually to stand for a few minutes among the shelves where the Reach's history slept in boxes. He liked it here. The air was dry and smelled of dust and glue, and nothing in the room ever asked him for anything.

His own earliest rulings were here. He found the box without looking—fourteenth shelf, third row—and drew out the folder from his confirmation year. *In re: Marchetti Land Holdings.* The ruling that had started the ledger. He read his own younger voice and marveled at it: so certain, so clean, every citation exact. There had been no rot in that document. The rot had come later, drop by drop, the way rot always came—never as a decision, only as a series of non-decisions. The favor granted. The inconvenient witness reassigned. The hearing scheduled for a day when the key witness would already be at sea.

He had a wife once who had left him over a smaller version of this. She hadn't accused him of anything. She had simply said, one evening, folding napkins at their dinner table, *"You've stopped being able to tell which thoughts are yours,"* and she had been right, and he had hated her for eleven years for saying it, and missed her for twelve.

Now the thoughts were all somebody's. He could no longer locate the ones that belonged to him.

A clerk passed in the aisle and wished him good morning. Oyelaran returned it gravely, replaced the folder, and stood a moment longer with his hand flat on the box lid, as though taking a pulse.

* * *

That evening, a card arrived by courier. Heavy stock, embossed, no message beyond a single line in an elegant hand:

*The family appreciates Your Honor's continued discretion. — C.*

Corliss. The broker. Oyelaran had met him twice, and both meetings had felt like being measured for a coffin by a cheerful tailor. The man worked for the Marchettis the way a knife works for a hand—efficiently, and without any opinion about what it cuts.

Oyelaran sat with the card for a long time. Discretion. As if he were a physician keeping a confidence, rather than a judge scheduling a burial.

He took out the black ledger, opened it to the current page, and beneath *Marchetti, A. — 14th year* he wrote, in the same small precise hand he used for dockets: *Reyes, J. — Frostmonth. Consideration pending.*

Consideration pending. Not yet paid. Perhaps never paid in any way he could hold in his hand. But the column existed now, and columns, once begun, wanted filling.

He thought—briefly, the way a man thinks of jumping, from a great height, just to feel the wind—of writing something else instead. *Charges insufficient. Refer to assessor.* Two lines. The whole machine jammed. He imagined the relief of it, the clean cold air of it, himself walking out of the courthouse at dusk with nothing owed to anyone.

Then he imagined the morning after. The letter from the judicial council, prompted by an anonymous complaint. The audit of his last decade of rulings, each one reexamined under the light of his sudden scruples. The seat gone, the pension gone, the name gone from the courthouse wall. And the Reyes woman tried anyway, in another courtroom, by another magistrate, because the Marchettis did not require *him* specifically—they required only a magistrate, and magistrates were replaceable in a way that consciences, unfortunately, were not.

His ruin would buy her nothing. That was the sentence he built the cage with, and it was even true.

Oyelaran closed the ledger, blew out the lamp, and sat in the dark of his chambers listening to the river he could not see, running its brown patient miles toward a sea he had never visited. Somewhere out there, he supposed, the tide was doing whatever tides did—giving things back, taking things away, keeping its own books without any need of a ledger.

The trial was set. First week of Frostmonth. By then the pass would be white and shut, the registry sealed, the concession safe, and whatever evidence might have saved a dockside clerk would be sleeping under snow, legally weightless, perfectly preserved, and utterly useless.

He fell asleep in his chair and dreamed, as he often did lately, of a door he was locking from the inside, and in the dream he could not remember whether he was keeping something out—

or keeping himself in.
