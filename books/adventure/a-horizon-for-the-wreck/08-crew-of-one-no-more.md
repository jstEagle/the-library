## 8. Crew of One No More

Sable counted the ways to fail on the walk back from the Harrowgate jail, and by the time they reached the docks the list had grown to eleven.

One: they could not get into the jail again without a warrant for their own arrest. Two: even if they could, Juno would still stand trial, and Magistrate Oyelaran set calendars the way other men set traps. Three: the evidence that could break the case open sat under the engine plates of a riverboat with a forged registration and a captain with no legal name, which meant the evidence was worth exactly nothing until somebody with credentials said otherwise. Four, five, six—

They stopped at the rail of the *Marrow's Grace*, their own boat, their own rusted kingdom, and watched the water carry the town's lamplight downstream in long yellow smears.

The truth of it was simple and it sat in Sable's chest like ballast: fifteen years of traveling alone had been a strategy, and the strategy had worked, and the strategy was now the thing most likely to get Juno Reyes hanged. One person could run. One person could hide a strongbox, dodge a broker, vanish upriver at the first smell of trouble. One person could not authenticate a forty-year-old manifest, file it past a hostile clerk, outclimb Corliss's hired men, and keep a dying engine alive through mountain water—all before the snows closed the Kestrel Pass.

That took a crew.

Sable had spent fifteen years explaining, politely and then less politely, that they did not have a crew. Crews were liabilities with appetites. Crews talked, drank, remembered faces, sold stories when the money ran out. Every name aboard a boat was a door someone could kick in.

And yet.

They went below, lifted the engine plates, and looked at the strongbox a long time. The Marchetti seal caught the lantern light and threw it back, dull gold, like something blinking in its sleep.

"All right," Sable said to the empty hold, because saying it aloud made it harder to take back. "All right. Three. No more than three."

* * *

The archivist came first, because the archivist was the one Sable dreaded most.

Tamsin Okonkwo kept a stall in the Harrowgate night market, between a man selling eels and a woman selling broken clocks, and what she sold was provenance. For a fee she would date a paper, match a watermark, tell you whether the seal on your bill of sale had been pressed by the hand it claimed or by a clever forger two towns over. She had been, fifteen years ago, the youngest senior authenticator in the Verrazano provincial archive, and then she had been nothing at all, dismissed in a scandal whose details everyone knew and no one could prove. Sable had heard the story in a dozen river towns. Sable had also heard that she was the best, and that being the best was half of why she'd fallen.

Sable found her closing up, blowing out the little oil lamps one by one.

"I need a document authenticated," Sable said. "Off the books."

Tamsin didn't look up. "Everything I do is off the books. That's why there's a stall and not an office." She blew out another lamp. "Come back tomorrow."

"It's a ship's manifest. Forty years old. Sealed manifest, from the *Condesa del Mar*."

Her hand stopped over the last lamp. The flame wobbled in the draft and steadied.

"Sit down," she said.

Sable didn't sit. "Not here. Not tonight. Tomorrow, on my boat, at the coal dock. And Tamsin—" they waited until she looked at them, really looked, her eyes going over their face the way she'd go over a watermark, "—whatever you think you know about what this is, you're wrong, and I'd like to keep it that way as long as possible."

"You're the second person this month to say that word to me," Tamsin said. "*Condesa.* The first was a man with very good boots and a broker's smile, asking whether I could authenticate a bill of sale if one happened to surface." She snuffed the last lamp and the stall went dark. "I told him the truth. That it depends who's asking. He didn't like that answer."

"What did you tell him you'd charge?"

"Everything," Tamsin said. "I always quote everything. It keeps the amateurs away." She pulled a tarp over her goods and knotted it with quick, practiced hands. "Coal dock. Dawn. If I'm not there by mid-morning, assume I was never coming, and don't come looking."

"And if you are there?"

"Then you'll owe me everything," she said, "and we'll both find out what that means."

* * *

The stowaway Sable found by accident, which was the only way anyone ever found Bo.

Sable discovered her at the coal dock an hour before dawn, when a coil of mooring line moved, sighed, and resolved into a skinny teenager with rope burns on her palms and the unbothered expression of someone who had been caught before and intended to be caught again. She was curled in the line locker like she'd been born to it.

"You're stealing my boat," Sable said.

"I'm borrowing your boat's shadow." The girl unfolded herself and stood. She was maybe fifteen, all elbows and certainty, hair cropped short with what looked like a fish knife. "You're going upriver. Everyone at the fish market says so. You need a climber."

"I need a climber."

"You're going through the Kestrel Range, or you're going to try." She said it the way other people said *the sun will rise*. "The weirs are chained this time of year. The lock gates ice up. Somebody has to go up the gate towers and drop the counterweights by hand, and that's a climber's work, and you don't have a climber, because you don't have anybody, because you're the captain who travels alone." She said the last part with a contempt that Sable felt in the ribs. "I've watched your boat for three days. You check your lines twice and your wake once. You're careful. But you're one person."

Sable looked at her a long moment. "What's your name?"

"Bo."

"That's not a name, that's a fitting."

"It's the one I'm keeping." Bo crossed her arms. "I'll work for passage. I don't eat much. I don't talk."

"You're talking now."

"I'm negotiating." For the first time something moved under the bravado—quick, quickly buried, the way a fish moves under ice. "I'm useful. And I'm not going back to the mill town, whatever you decide, so you can either have me aboard where I'm useful or have me hanging off your stern where I'm a problem."

Sable thought: *this is how it starts.* This is how the arithmetic begins, one small number at a time, each one easy to justify, until the sum is a crew and the crew is a weakness and the weakness is a noose. Fifteen years of discipline, and it was unraveling at a coal dock before dawn because a child with a fish knife had correctly counted their crew at one.

"Forward hold," Sable said. "You touch the engine plates, you're over the side. You touch anything with a seal on it, you're over the side. You get caught, you never heard my name, because I'll tell you right now I'll deny you faster than you can fall."

Bo's grin was sudden and total, like weather. "Aye, Captain."

"Don't call me that."

* * *

Old Ferro was the hardest, because Old Ferro was the one Sable hadn't planned on.

They found him where the plan said he'd be—the plan being a half-remembered story Juno had told once, years ago, laughing: *there's a mechanic in Harrowgate who claims he served on the Condesa, and nobody believes him, and he's stopped claiming it, and that's how you know it's true.* He worked the repair slip at the far end of the harbor, a broad old man with forearms like mooring posts and hands that trembled only when they were still. His name, according to the slip's ledger, was Ferro. Just Ferro. The ledger had stopped asking questions years ago.

Sable watched him work for an hour before approaching. He was rebuilding a water pump that three other mechanics had pronounced dead, and he did it without hurry, without notes, his fingers reading the metal like a blind man reading a face. When the pump's impeller turned freely for the first time in a decade, he grunted—not triumph, just acknowledgment, the way you'd nod to an old acquaintance.

"You're the one from the *Marrow's Grace*," he said, without turning around. "Your stern bearing's singing. Flat note, port side. Been singing since you came in."

"It's on my list."

"It's been on your list since before Harrowgate." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been white in some previous geological era. "You want something. Everyone who stands there watching instead of talking wants something."

"Juno Reyes sent me."

That stopped him. Not dramatically—he didn't drop the rag or go pale—but something in the set of his shoulders reorganized, the way a boat shifts when a wave comes from a direction it wasn't braced for.

"She in trouble," he said. It wasn't a question.

"She's in a cell. Charged with theft she didn't commit. The charge is a lever, and the people holding the lever are the Marchettis, and what they actually want is a cave on the lower reach and everything that's inside it." Sable kept their voice flat, giving him nothing to hold onto, nothing to refuse. "I'm putting together a crew to go upriver before the pass closes. I need a mechanic. I'm told there was one aboard the *Condesa del Mar*."

For a long moment Ferro looked at Sable, and then past them, at the gray water of the harbor, at something much farther away than the harbor.

"Forty years," he said. "Forty years, and it comes looking for me at a repair slip in Harrowgate." He folded the rag with great care, corner to corner, as if the folding were the last of some long task. "I was an oiler. Third assistant in the engine room. I was twenty-two years old, and when she went down I swam, and I didn't look back, and I told myself for forty years that not looking back was just what swimming is." He looked up. "You don't want me for your crew. You want me for your penance. They're different jobs, and I've only ever been qualified for one."

"I need both," Sable said. "But I'll settle for the mechanic."

Ferro almost smiled. It was a ruined thing, mostly scar tissue, but it moved. "The manifest," he said. "You've got something off her. I can smell it on you. Forty years of salvage men and Marchetti surveyors sniffing around that coast, and now a stranger with a singing stern bearing asks me about the *Condesa* in the same week." He shook his head slowly. "Whatever you've got, it's the reason Juno's in a cell. And it's the reason you're standing here instead of running, which means it's bigger than you, which means—" he exhaled through his nose, a sound like a valve releasing, "—which means I'm coming, aren't I. God help the river."

* * *

They cast off from the coal dock two hours after dawn, and Sable stood at the wheel with the strangest sensation of their adult life: the boat was heavier.

Not literally. The *Marrow's Grace* sat no deeper in the water with a stowaway, an archivist, and an old oiler aboard than she had with one. But Sable felt the weight the way you feel a change in the wind—not in the instruments, in the bones. Fifteen years of being answerable to no one, and now every decision had three sets of consequences attached, three people who could be arrested, drowned, bought, or broken because Sable Voss had chosen a heading.

Tamsin sat in the cabin with the strongbox open in front of her and a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, and she had not spoken in four hours. Bo had claimed the roof of the pilot house and sat there cross-legged like a figurehead with opinions, watching the banks slide past. Ferro was below, on his back under the engine, and every so often the singing note from the port stern bearing would falter, change, and die, and Ferro would say something short and profane and satisfied.

It was near dusk when Tamsin finally came up the companionway. Her face was the face of someone who had spent the day carrying something heavy up a long stair.

"Sit down," she said to Sable. "You'll want to be sitting."

"I'm steering."

"Then steer and listen." She gripped the rail. "The manifest is genuine. Paper, ink, seal, hand—all of it. The Marchetti concession papers are forged; the forgery's good, but it's a forgery, and I can prove it, and more than that, I can prove *how* it was done, and who had access to do it. That's the part that will hang somebody." She paused. "But that's not what I came up here to say."

Sable waited. The river ran. Somewhere below, Ferro's wrench rang against the hull like a slow bell.

"The *Condesa* wasn't carrying treasure," Tamsin said. "Everyone's hunted her for forty years because of the sealed manifest, because sealed means valuable, because that's what sealed means." She shook her head. "It's not cargo. It's not coin. The hold manifest lists crates of depositions. Sworn testimony. Dozens of them, from the founding years—farmers, dockworkers, a provincial treasurer's own clerk. Testimony against the Marchettis. Against what they did to build everything they own." She looked at Sable, and her voice dropped, not for drama but because the words were heavy. "They didn't lose a fortune in that cave, Captain. They lost a trial. And they've spent forty years making sure nobody found the verdict."

Sable's hands were steady on the wheel, which surprised them. Below, the wrench rang. Above, Bo shifted on the pilot house roof, and Sable realized the girl had gone very quiet, and was listening.

Three people. Sable counted them again, the way you'd count heads in a lifeboat, and felt the arithmetic settle into place with all its terrible weight: three strangers who now knew, three lives now moored to the same wreck, three more names on the list of people Corliss could reach.

"Get below," Sable said quietly. "All of you. We run through the night, and we don't stop."

Tamsin frowned. "Why? What's changed?"

Sable watched the dark water ahead, where the river bent north toward the locks and the mountains and the deadline, and answered the only question that mattered.

"Because as of tonight," they said, "we're not the only ones who know what we're carrying."

Behind them, far downstream, a lantern was gaining on the dark.
