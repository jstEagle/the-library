## 9. Charts and Confessions

The lamp above the chart table had a habit of buzzing when the river ran high, and tonight the Verrazano was running high enough that Tamsin felt the sound in her teeth. She didn't mind. Noise meant water, and water meant they were moving, and movement—away from Harrowgate, away from the magistrate's arithmetic, away from the particular silence of a woman sitting in a cell for a theft she hadn't committed—was the only currency Tamsin Quill still trusted.

She had cleared the table herself. Wiped it down with a rag that smelled of diesel and old tea, laid out her tools in the order she'd used them for eleven years at the Coldharbour Provincial Archive: magnifier, calipers, the little brass balance with its chipped pans, a bottle of distilled spirits she'd bought under pretense of cleaning wounds. The boat's cabin was too small for proper work. Her elbows knocked the bulkhead every time she reached for the lamp. The engine plates beneath the floorboards ticked like something sleeping.

Sable stood in the doorway with their arms folded, watching her arrange the room the way other people arranged their faces.

"You don't have to stay," Tamsin said. "This part's tedious."

"I know what you're going to find."

"Then you know why I'd rather you stayed."

Sable held her gaze a moment longer—those flat grey eyes that gave away nothing except, lately, everything—and then sat down on the bench by the door, as far from the table as the cabin allowed. A compromise. Tamsin recognized compromises; she'd built a career out of them until the career collapsed and took her name with it.

She lifted the manifest out of its oilcloth wrapping with both hands, the way you lift a bird you intend to release.

It was smaller than legend. Forty years of dockside stories had made the Condesa del Mar's lost papers into something the size of a Bible, bound in leather, glowing faintly. In truth it was a ledger-bound folio, salt-stained, its spine cracked into three soft segments, the paper gone the color of weak tea. Water had gotten to it sometime in four decades—the lower corner of every page furred and swollen—but whoever had sealed the strongbox had done it well. The wax had kept the worst out. The Marchetti seal stamped in red across the closure flap stared up at her like an eye that had been shut a long time and resented opening.

"Forty years," Ferro said from his corner. He'd wedged himself onto the engine housing where the warmth was, hands around a tin mug he wasn't drinking from. "And it looks like it died last week."

"Wax is honest work," Tamsin said. "Nobody forges wax this carefully. It costs more effort than the forgery would earn."

She began.

The work itself was the only prayer she knew anymore. Fiber first: she teased a thread loose from the margin and held it to the flame of a match, watching it burn brown and slow, smelling of rag pulp, not wood. Rag paper. Expensive. Consistent with a shipping line's official registers circa the loss year. The ink next—a drop of spirit on a cotton swab, touched to the letter *C* in *Condesa*, and the swab came away iron-grey without bleeding. Iron gall, properly made, properly aged. No modern shortcuts. The watermark, when she tilted page one against the lamp, showed the millmark of the Harrowgate Paperworks, discontinued nineteen years ago—which proved nothing except that the paper predated any conceivable fraud, because nobody counterfeits a watermark by using paper older than their crime.

"Page one is genuine," she said, and heard Sable exhale behind her.

She worked outward from there. The folio was the Condesa's master manifest, kept by the purser, initialed at each port of call down the Reach and along the coast road to Vell. Cargo entries in a neat commercial hand: baled cotton, machine parts, salt pork, crates of wine marked for a consignee in Coldharbour. Ordinary. Disappointingly ordinary. Tamsin felt the old professional disappointment rise in her—the archivist's melancholy that history so often turns out to be invoices.

Then she reached the third signature.

Every purser initials his pages. This signature appeared on pages nine through fourteen, in the margins, small and slanted and pressed hard enough to emboss the paper: *A.M.V.* And beside each one, in different ink—fresher ink, though still forty years old—a date.

"Ferro," she said. "You said you served aboard her. Deck crew, two seasons."

"Aye."

"What does A.M.V. stand for?"

The mug stopped halfway to his mouth. In the lamplight Tamsin watched an old man decide, for the second time in his life, whether to be brave—and watched him take longer about it this time.

"Aldous Marchetti Vane," he said finally. "He signed her manifests himself. Came aboard at Vell, unannounced, with two men who never touched cargo. Rode her the whole coastal leg. Signed the margins himself, which no owner does, because owners trust their pursers." He looked at the mug. "We talked about it below decks. Crew talk. Nothing more."

Tamsin turned to page twelve and stopped breathing.

The cargo entry there read, in the same neat hand: *One (1) sealed strongbox, documents, per instruction of A.M.V., hold safe, not for sale, not for inventory.* And beneath it, squeezed against the margin in a hand that was not the purser's—smaller, hurried, the letters leaning as if written braced against a rolling deck:

*Depositions. Seven names. If we do not arrive, they are the truth.*

She read it aloud. The cabin was so quiet that the words seemed to land on the wood and stay there.

"Seven names," Bo said from the ladder hatch, upside down, where teenagers live. "Names of what?"

"Not cargo," Tamsin said. Her voice sounded strange to her, distant, the voice she used in reading rooms. "Testimony. Depositions sworn against someone. Against—" She looked up at Sable. "Against the Marchettis. Sworn forty years ago, sealed aboard their own ship, carried under their own seal. Why would Aldous Marchetti seal depositions against himself inside a box with his own crest on it?"

"Because a box with your crest on it is the last place anyone looks," Sable said quietly. "I've been hiding in plain sight for fifteen years. It works."

"Or," Ferro said, and now he set the mug down, and his hands were not steady, "because he meant to destroy it and never got the chance. Because the ship went down before he could."

The lamp buzzed. The river ran high.

Tamsin turned the remaining pages with the care she reserved for things that could ruin lives—including, twice now, her own. Pages thirteen and fourteen were not cargo entries at all. They were a list, seven lines long, each line a name, a town, and a sum. Not payment. She'd seen payment ledgers; sums paid out marched in columns, tidy. These figures were written *against* the names, debits, and beside each debit a word she knew intimately from her archive years, because she had once, for money, misfiled a folder full of them:

*Settled.*

Seven people who had been prepared to swear out depositions against the founding fraud of the Marchetti fortune—whoever they were, whatever that fraud had been—and every one of them marked settled. Paid off. Or paid *for*. The Condesa del Mar had not been carrying treasure. It had been carrying the evidence that a great family's money was built on silenced mouths, and the silencer himself had sealed it aboard his own vessel, and the river had swallowed the whole arrangement before anyone could open it.

"The wreck isn't a treasure," Tamsin said. "It's a witness."

* * *

Bo wanted to celebrate, which was how Bo processed everything: loudly, hanging upside down, proposing they toast with the cleaning spirits. Sable overruled the toast. Ferro said nothing at all, which Tamsin, watching him, understood was its own kind of statement.

She repacked the manifest at midnight and found she couldn't make herself close the oilcloth. Her fingers kept returning to page fourteen, to the seventh name on the list, the last one, the one whose *settled* was written in a shakier hand than the rest, as if the writer's conscience had deteriorated down the page.

"You knew," she said to Ferro.

The engine ticked beneath him. He looked smaller than he had when they'd pulled him off the dock at Weir's Landing, smaller than his hands suggested.

"I knew the box existed," he said. "Not what was in it. I've known since the night she went down, and I've told no one, and I'll tell you why, and you won't like me after."

"Tell me anyway. I'm professionally acquainted with not liking myself."

He laughed—one dry syllable, no humor in it—and then he told her, while Sable sat motionless by the door and Bo, uncharacteristically silent, sat on the stairs with their chin on their arms.

He'd been twenty-three. Second season aboard, able seaman, good with rigging, better at keeping his head down. The night of the storm, the Condesa had been running light and riding wrong, and the captain had ordered her into the narrows against Ferro's own private judgment, because there was a passenger aboard who insisted—insisted, and paid, and the captain's debts were the kind that answered to insistence. They'd taken the rocks at Gullwrack in the dark. Ferro had gone over the side with a line, had hauled three men and one woman through the surf to the cave's throat, and then the sea had come back in and taken the boat's stern down and most of the rest with it.

"We sheltered in the cave till dawn. Six of us. The woman was hurt—bad, ribs, maybe worse. She kept saying one thing over and over: *the box, the box, tell them about the box.* We thought she was raving." His jaw worked. "By morning a Marchetti salvage skiff found us. First on scene, faster than any rescue has ever moved on this river. And a man aboard—he never gave a name—asked us each, separately, the same question: did we see a strongbox. Six times he asked. Five of us said no."

"And the sixth?"

"The woman died on the skiff. Before she could say anything to anyone but us." He met Tamsin's eyes. "They paid our passage home. Each of us. Enough to start over. Enough to never ask again. I was twenty-three and I had nothing, and I took it, and I told myself the box was lost, the sea keeps what it takes, no harm done by silence." He turned the empty mug in his hands. "Forty years I've told myself that. Then your captain fishes the thing out of the tide like picking up a dropped coin, and here I am on this boat, and I understand now that I didn't take the money to survive. I took it because staying silent was easier than being the man who knew."

The cabin held his confession the way the wax had held the pages: imperfectly, but holding.

Tamsin thought about a folder in the Coldharbour Provincial Archive, misfiled—not lost, misfiled, into a drawer where no honest researcher would ever look—in exchange for a sum that had covered her mother's last winter and destroyed everything else. She had told herself a version of the same sentence. Everyone who survives by silence tells some version of it.

"I buried evidence once," she heard herself say. "For money. Real evidence, real case, real people who never got their day because a clerk with my face looked the other way." She said it plainly, because she had learned that shame spoken plainly loses half its weight. "They struck me from the rolls. Best thing that ever happened to me, though it took me six years to believe it."

Bo, from the stairs, very quietly: "I stole a purse off a magistrate's bench once. Didn't even want what was in it. Just wanted to prove I could take something back from somebody like that." A pause. "I still have the purse."

And then the strangest thing happened, the thing Tamsin would remember afterward as the moment the crew became a crew: nobody laughed, and nobody comforted anybody, and nobody looked away. Four people in a cramped cabin, each holding their own small treachery up to the lamp, and the lamp—buzzing, cheap, river-stained—showed all of it and broke none of them.

"So the Marchettis' whole concession," Bo said slowly, working it through, "rests on forged papers. And the proof of the forgery is in a box that also proves their fortune started as a crime. And the magistrate who's trying Juno owes them his seat."

"That's the shape of it," Sable said.

"Then Juno isn't just framed to scare people away from the cave." Bo's voice had gone hard, older than the kid's years. "She's framed so that if anyone *does* get to the registry, the evidence gets there already poisoned—found by fugitives, carried by thieves. Everything we touch turns criminal. That's the design."

Tamsin felt the cold of that settle over the room. It was elegant, in the way rot is elegant. You didn't need to stop the evidence. You only needed to make sure that whoever carried it arrived discredited. Fifteen years of Sable's invisibility, her own disgrace, Ferro's silence—all of it, every scar on this boat, was ammunition for the other side.

"Then we change what arrives," Sable said.

Everyone looked at them.

"They expect a smuggler's box, found by a smuggler, defended with lies. So we don't defend it with lies." Sable's voice was level, but Tamsin saw their hands, laced tight together on the table's edge, knuckles pale. "Tamsin authenticates it—on the record, under her own struck-off name, however much that costs. Ferro testifies to what happened at Gullwrack, however much that costs. I put my own warrant on the table alongside the manifest, however much *that* costs. We arrive filthy and we say so. Let Oyelaran explain why a forged concession needs a frame job to protect it."

"It might not work," Tamsin said. "Courts are machines, and machines favor whoever oils them."

"It might not," Sable agreed. "But I've spent fifteen years learning exactly one skill: reading what a river will do before it does it. And this river"—they nodded at the manifest, at the box, at the sleeping engine—"is turning. I can feel it in the hull."

Ferro stood, joints cracking like the boat's timbers, and did something Tamsin had not seen him do: he crossed the cabin and laid his palm flat on the oilcloth-wrapped manifest, the way you'd lay a hand on a coffin lid or a friend's shoulder.

"Seven names," he said. "I only ever knew the woman's. Marta Ellery, of Vell. She deserved better than dying between my silence and their money." He looked around the cabin, at each of them in turn. "If we're doing this, we do it for the seven. All right? That's my price for being brave this late in life. Somebody remembers the names."

"Somebody will," Tamsin said, and found she meant it, and found that meaning it felt like standing upright after a long time bent.

* * *

She worked until nearly dawn, because authentication done honestly means documentation, and documentation means copies, and copies mean the original can be lost, stolen, burned, or confiscated without killing the truth. She copied the seven names onto a separate sheet in her own hand and tucked it into her shirt, against her skin, where she had once carried nothing but the weight of what she'd buried.

Near the end, sorting the folio's final leaf, she found one more thing—a slip of paper laid between the last page and the cover, unsigned, undated, in the hurried marginal hand from page twelve:

*If the box reaches shore, give it to the courts, not the family. A.M.V. feared his own heirs more than his judges.*

She read it three times. Then she carried it to the doorway where Sable stood the night watch alone, coat collar up, watching the black water slide past.

"There's a postscript," she said, and handed it over.

Sable read it by the light of the compass binnacle. For a long moment they said nothing, and the river said everything, hurrying them north toward locks, toward winter, toward a pass that would close like a mouth.

"He was afraid of his own children," Sable said at last.

"People usually are, when they know what they've left them."

Sable folded the slip with great care and put it inside their coat, over the heart, where a man keeps the things he intends to use. When they turned, their face in the binnacle light was the calmest Tamsin had seen it since Harrowgate, and that calm frightened her more than any storm, because she recognized it. It was the calm of someone who has stopped calculating escape routes.

"Get some sleep," Sable said. "Tomorrow we run the Kestrel locks, and Corliss will be waiting at the lock station, wearing that smile of his."

"How do you know?"

"Because I'd be." Sable looked back at the water. "And because the lock keeper owes me a favor, and favors are how you learn who else has been asking questions. Someone asked about us today, Tamsin. Asked the right questions. Down to the boat's draft and the color of my coat."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere ahead, unseen in the dark, the first lock gates of the Kestrel flight stood closed against the current, and behind them, Tamsin thought, closing like the gates themselves, came everything the Marchetti name could buy.

She went below to sleep among the charts, one hand resting on the copy of seven names, and dreamed, for the first time in six years, of drawers that opened.
