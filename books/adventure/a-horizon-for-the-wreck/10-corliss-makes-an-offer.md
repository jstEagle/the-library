## 10. Corliss Makes an Offer

The Vell's Landing lock station sat at the throat of the river like a fist half-closed. Two stone piers reached toward each other across the current, and between them the gates rose and fell on a schedule older than anyone working them, lifting boats twelve feet from the low water of the southern reach to the high water of the north. Everything going upriver had to pass through it. Everything going downriver too. Which was why Sable had spent fifteen years treating locks the way other people treated strangers with questions: politely, briefly, and always ready to leave.

They came through at dusk, running the *Magpie* in on the last commercial window before the station closed for the night. The light was doing that thing autumn light did on the Reach—turning the water to hammered pewter, setting every bare tree along the banks alight for about four minutes before the dark took it all back. Tamsin was below decks with the manifest spread across the chart table, her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, muttering to herself in the shorthand of archivists. Bo was aloft, ostensibly checking the rigging, actually seeing how far out along the bowsprit she could get before Sable yelled at her. Ferro had the engine room, keeping the old girl ticking with the tenderness of a man who'd found, late in life, something worth being gentle to.

And Juno was in a cell in Harrowgate, forty miles upstream, waiting for a trial scheduled for the week after the pass closed.

Sable stood at the wheel and watched the lock gates swing open ahead of them, black mouths yawning against the last of the light, and thought: *this is where I would do it.* If Corliss wanted to corner someone, this was the place. No side channels. No shoals to dodge behind. Just stone walls rising on either side as the *Magpie* slid into the chamber, and the stern gate groaning shut behind them, and the water beginning to rise.

The lock keeper waved from his hut—a bored man with a lantern and a ledger—and called down that they'd have a ten-minute wait; there was a barge coming down on the next cycle and he liked to share the lift. Ten minutes. Sable tied off fore and aft, checked the fenders, and was reaching for the kettle when the knock came at the gunwale.

Three knocks. Even, unhurried, almost friendly.

Dane Corliss stood on the lock wall above them, dressed like a man who had never once been cold outdoors: a wool coat the color of good coffee, a scarf knotted with the kind of care that suggested a mirror had been involved, leather gloves tucked under one arm though the evening had a bite to it. He smiled down at Sable the way a man smiles at a hand of cards he's already seen.

"Captain Villalobos," he said. "Mind if I come aboard? The keeper says we've got ten minutes, and I hate wasting other people's time almost as much as I hate wasting my own."

Sable looked past him. The lock keeper's hut glowed warm and yellow, but the keeper himself was nowhere in sight. The barge due on the next cycle was, as far as Sable could see, a fiction. "How did you know we'd be here?"

"Because there's exactly one way north from here, and you're on it." Corliss stepped down onto the deck without waiting for permission, moving with an easy grace that didn't match his build, and helped himself to the spot by the rail where he could see both the cabin door and the engine hatch. A man who'd done this before. "Also because your mechanic bought pipe fittings at Vell's Landing this morning, and the chandler there is a talkative man. Ferro, isn't it? Old fellow. Hands like anchor chain."

"He minds his own business."

"Everyone does, right up until it pays better not to." Corliss said it lightly, the way another man might comment on the weather, and Sable felt the temperature of the conversation drop several degrees without either of them raising their voice. "I'll be quick, Captain. I know you're quick. It's one of the things I admire about you."

"You don't know anything about me."

"I know you run a forty-foot riverboat single-handed—or you did, until recently, which is itself interesting. I know you pay cash everywhere between here and Harrowgate and never sleep at the same dock twice. I know you turned down three lucrative charter contracts last spring for no reason anyone could name." He ticked these off on gloved fingers, gently, like a man counting blessings. "And I know that two weeks ago you were seen at Gullwrack Point at dawn, on the lowest tide in nine years, and that you came back with your boat riding heavy."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere below, Ferro's engine idled, patient as a heartbeat. Above them the stars were coming out, indifferent and sharp.

"Riding heavy," Sable repeated.

"Water in the bilge, I assumed. At first." Corliss unwound his scarf a half-turn, a small gesture of settling in, of a man prepared to be comfortable. "But then my employers' surveyors went out to Gullwrack on the next low tide and found their markers moved. Found boot prints inside the cave mouth. Found—" he paused, savoring it, "—a brass strongbox-shaped absence in the sediment, with a clean rectangle where something square had rested for forty years."

"Could've been anything."

"It could have been anything," Corliss agreed warmly. "It could have been a fisherman's cache. It could have been rum runners' stock from the old days. It could have been nothing at all. But then I started asking around about you, Captain, and I learned that you don't do anything for nothing. You're the most transactional soul on this river—which is a compliment, by the way. I mean it as one. You weigh everything. So I asked myself: what could a woman like that find in a sealed wreck that she'd risk her invisibility to carry?" His eyes were pale gray, almost colorless in the dusk, and they did not blink enough. "And then I stopped asking myself, because I realized I could simply ask you."

The water in the lock was rising. Sable could feel it—the slow lift of the hull, the lines creaking, the stone walls sliding downward past the rail. Twelve feet between the reaches. Between the low country where the Marchettis' word was law and the high country where the registry sat, and the pass, and the snows.

"Say what you came to say," Sable said.

Corliss smiled wider. "There she is. I knew we'd get along."

* * *

He laid it out the way a merchant lays out cloth—smoothly, in folds, each piece shown to its best advantage.

"The Marchetti family is not unreasonable," he began. "People say otherwise, but people say a lot of things. What the Marchettis are is practical. They hold a salvage concession on the Condesa del Mar. That concession cost them money—surveyors, lawyers, gifts to various officials whose names I won't insult you by listing. They expect a return on that investment. Now. If certain documents were to surface suggesting the concession rests on imperfect paperwork—" he shrugged elegantly, "—that return is threatened. And threatened men stop being practical. I'd rather we all stayed practical, Captain. Wouldn't you?"

"What are you offering?"

"A hundred thousand crowns." He let the number sit in the air between them like a coin spinning. "For the box, unopened or opened, I don't care which, delivered to me personally. Plus passage papers—clean ones, any name you like, any port on the coast. Plus my personal assurance, which has value, whatever else you think of me, that the Marchettis will never look at you, your boat, or your crew again."

"And Juno?"

Something flickered behind Corliss's pleasant mask—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. He'd expected Sable to haggle over the money first, maybe. To pretend, for one more round, that the box was about the box.

"Ah," he said softly. "Juno Reyes."

"You know the charges are invented."

"I know the charges are *manageable*. There's a difference, and the difference is worth money to somebody." He turned to face the water, hands clasped behind him, giving Sable the courtesy of his profile like a portrait sitter. "Here is what I can do. The magistrate in Harrowgate is a reasonable man in most respects. If the box comes to me, I can arrange for the survey equipment to be found—really found, this time, planted somewhere convenient—and for Miss Reyes's part in the matter to be recharacterized. A fine. Time served. Perhaps even dismissal, if the season goes well and everyone remains agreeable." He glanced back. "Her sentence, Captain, can be managed. That is literally the service I provide. I manage sentences. Some go up. Some go down. It depends entirely on the cooperation of the interested parties."

The lock was nearly full now. The upper gates stood black against the starlight, still closed, holding back the whole cold weight of the northern reach. Sable became aware that they were, at this moment, in a stone box full of rising water with a Marchetti man standing on the deck, and that the only thing keeping the situation civil was that Corliss wanted something.

A hundred thousand crowns. Sable had spent fifteen years measuring life in miles and in debts, most of them silent, some of them dangerous, all of them unpaid. A hundred thousand crowns wasn't money. It was erasure. It was a new name on clean papers and a saltwater port where nobody knew the shape of your past. It was the thing Sable had told themselves they didn't want, every night for fifteen years, in the voice people use when they're repeating something so often they can forget whether they believe it.

And Juno, walking out of a Harrowgate courtroom with a fine and a sneer instead of a sentence, free to spit at Sable for the rest of her life for taking the deal.

"You want me to sell you the proof that your employers are frauds," Sable said, "and buy my oldest friend's freedom with it."

"I want you to be *practical*." Corliss spread his hands. "Look at the arithmetic, Captain. Truly look at it. You keep the box, what happens? You carry it to Coldharbour, if the weather lets you, if my colleagues don't take it off you first—and they will try, and they are less polite than I am. You file it, if the clerk accepts it, and clerks are such unpredictable creatures. And then what? A hearing. An inquiry. Years of it. Lawyers. And somewhere in those years, a magistrate who owes my employers his seat finds a reason to look closely at a riverboat captain with no registered name, no fixed address, and—" his voice dropped, gentle as a knife going in, "—a history. Because that's the trouble with evidence, Captain. Evidence doesn't float free. It drags its owners into the light with it. You've spent fifteen years staying invisible. Are you really going to trade all of that—for a box?"

The lines creaked. The water stilled—they were at the top of the lift now, level with the northern reach, and somewhere below the lower gates were being winched open to let the fictional barge through, or perhaps just to give the keeper an excuse to be elsewhere.

Sable thought about the cave. About the ribcage of the ship rising out of the drained dark like the skeleton of the world's largest animal, barnacled and patient. About the brass box stamped with the Marchetti seal, sitting under the engine plates six feet from where they stood, warm from the machinery, holding the depositions of dead people who had trusted a ship to carry their testimony and a river to keep their secret.

About Juno's letter. *You were the only one who ever made me feel like the river was ours.*

"No," Sable said.

Corliss sighed, and for just a moment the charm dropped away—not into anger, but into something worse: professional disappointment, the look of a craftsman watching good material ruin itself. "No," he echoed. "Just like that. No counteroffer? Everyone counters, Captain. It's how I know I've started the bidding in the right neighborhood."

"There's no bid. There's no market. You can't buy this."

"Everything has a price. I've never once met the exception, and I've been looking for twenty years." He picked up his gloves from the rail and tugged them on, finger by finger, unhurried. "But let's say I believe you. Let's say the box isn't for sale at any figure. Then understand me clearly, because I'm going to say this once and I'd hate for you to mistake it for a threat—it's merely a forecast." He settled the scarf back into place. "My employers believe the box exists. Whether it exists or not is now almost beside the point. Belief is cheaper than proof and travels faster. So they will act as though it exists. They will watch your boat. They will watch your crew—the archivist, the boy, the old man. Oh yes, Captain, I know about the old man. Ferro. Forty years ago he signed aboard the Condesa del Mar as an assistant engineer, and forty years ago he walked away from her wreck with his silence in his pocket, and I promise you the Marchetti family remembers him far better than he'd like." Corliss stepped toward the rail, toward the gangway the keeper had finally lowered, and paused there with one hand on the line. "Sell me the box, and Juno Reyes walks. Keep it, and she drowns in paperwork while you drown in snow. Those are the forecasts. I do hope you'll check them against the sky."

He went up the gangway without hurrying, nodded to the lock keeper as though they were old friends, and was gone into the dark of the towpath, his footsteps fading on the stone.

* * *

Sable stood at the rail for a long time after the upper gates opened and the northern reach lay waiting, black water running fast and cold toward Harrowgate, toward the pass, toward the snows.

The trap hadn't been the offer. Sable had understood that much even while Corliss was talking—the offer was theater, a formality performed so that later, when things got ugly, the Marchettis could say they'd tried to settle fairly. No, the trap was smaller and sharper than that, and Sable only felt its teeth now, alone at the rail with the engine humming below:

Corliss knew.

Not suspected. Knew. Knew about the box, knew about the manifest, knew about Tamsin and Bo and Ferro—knew Ferro's name and his history on the Condesa, which meant the Marchettis' memory ran long and deep, which meant there was no version of the future where they simply let the *Magpie* chug north unmolested. Every mile from here to Coldharbour would be watched. Every dock, every lock, every lonely stretch of channel. And Juno—

Juno's sentence could be *managed*. Up or down, at someone's pleasure. Which meant that from tonight, every day Sable carried the box north was a day someone in Harrowgate might decide to make Juno's cell colder, her charges heavier, her trial quicker and crueler. The girl Sable had broken a promise to fifteen years ago was now, formally and precisely, a hostage.

Sable went below. Tamsin looked up from the chart table, glasses in her hand, and read Sable's face the way she read documents—with a scholar's eye for what had been altered.

"He knows," Sable said. "All of it. The box, the manifest, Ferro. He offered a hundred thousand crowns and Juno's freedom, and when I refused he wished me luck with the weather."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then she laid the manifest flat on the table, smoothing its edges with both palms, an archivist's reflex, ordering the world one page at a time. "Then we don't take the river," she said. "Not the main channel. Not past any more locks."

"Ferro," Sable called down the engine hatch, and heard the old man's boots on the ladder before she'd finished the word.

He came up wiping his hands on a rag, saw the two faces bent over the chart table, and understood immediately, the way old crew always understood. "Locks are watched," he said. "Stations are watched. You want the old markers." His voice went strange on the words, as if he were handling something fragile. "The Condesa channel. Nobody's run it since her."

"Can you find it in the dark?"

Ferro looked at the chart, then out the cabin windows at the black water rushing north, and Sable watched forty years of cowardice and grief fight it out on his face before something older and harder won.

"I don't need to find it," he said. "I *am* it. Get us past the weir lights, Captain. After that, steer where I tell you and trust me past all sense." He rolled the rag in his fists. "It's time somebody did."

Above them, far up the valley, the first wind of the front came down off the Kestrel Range and touched the water, and it smelled, faintly and impossibly early, like snow.
