## 11. Sabotage at the Weir

Bo had learned to sleep in the engine room because it was the warmest place on the boat and the only place where nobody asked questions. The *Kestrel's Mercy* — Sable never explained the name, and Bo never asked — had a bilge space behind the boiler that held body heat like a cupped hand. Bo wedged in there with a coil of rope for a pillow and listened to the engine tick itself cool, and thought about how strange it was to trust a floor.

Three weeks ago a floor was just something you stole across. Three weeks ago Bo had been a stowaway under tarpaulin on the Harrowgate coal barges, small enough to fold into a crate, quick enough with fingers to eat. Now there was a bunk of sorts, a job of sorts — grease, gauges, the midnight watch over a temperamental engine that Old Ferro treated like a sick animal — and four people who knew Bo's name and hadn't yet used it against anyone.

It was the strangest thing Bo had ever stolen: belonging. Taken without permission, from people who kept leaving it out where anyone could reach it.

The weir came up around midnight.

Bo felt it before hearing it — a change in the boat's voice, the hull shivering as the current found the sill of the night weir and dragged at them sideways. They'd been warned about it at the last lock station: the Verrazano dropped through a stone throat here, and after dark the lock keeper let boats shoot the spill channel rather than work the gates by lamplight. Fast water, he'd said, but honest. Keep your bow straight and your hands off the tiller unless she swings.

Bo climbed out of the bilge and up into the cold, bare feet finding the iron rungs by memory. Through the engine room's aft grate they could see the river running silver-black under a half moon, and ahead — a white seam of foam stitched across the whole width of the channel, glowing faintly like a scar.

Sable stood at the wheel, coat collar up, hat low. Tamsin was braced in the doorway of the cabin with a lantern, its light swinging wild. Ferro crouched by the boiler, one hand flat on it like he was taking its pulse.

"Spill channel," Sable said, mostly to themself. "Bow straight. Nobody touches anything."

"I don't touch anything even when you want me to," Bo said.

"Then we agree."

The boat went into the foam.

It was like being shaken inside a drum. Water came over the rails in sheets; the lantern died; Tamsin swore in three languages, two of which Bo wrote down mentally for later. The hull groaned, lifted, slammed down again, and Bo grabbed the boiler bracket and rode it out, grinning despite everything, because this — this violence, this speed, this total commitment to the next ten seconds — was the only kind of fear Bo had ever liked.

Then the grin died.

Because the engine didn't come back.

A boat shooting a weir is supposed to land in the slack pool below with her heart still beating, wheel answering, current carrying her gently toward the far bank. Instead the *Kestrel's Mercy* landed dead — no thrust, no steerage — and the slack pool turned out not to be slack at all. The current took them by the stern and swung them broadside, and Bo heard the sound every river kid knows and dreads: the long tearing kiss of rocks under the hull.

"We're drifting!" Tamsin shouted.

"I can see that I'm drifting," Sable snapped, hauling the wheel uselessly side to side. "Ferro! Engine!"

Ferro was already down in the dark, lamp between his teeth, and what he said when he got the lamp lit made Bo's stomach drop through the deck plates.

"She's flooded. Intake's open — someone's left the sea cock cracked and jammed it. Water's over the flywheel." He looked up, and his face in the lamplight was older than old. "This wasn't wear. This was done."

*Done.* The word went through Bo colder than the river air. Done while they all slept. Done by hands that had been aboard.

"Can you fix it?" Sable's voice was flat, working-flat, the voice of someone putting feelings in a drawer and sitting on it.

"Not from in here. The intake gate's outside, under the counter, below the waterline. Someone's wedged it open with a chock — I can see the shape of it through the housing. It has to be pulled from outside."

"How deep?"

Ferro measured with his eyes, then his hands. "Four feet to the gate. Maybe five."

The boat ground over another rock. Somewhere aft, planks complained in a voice that sounded permanent.

"Then somebody goes in," Sable said.

"I'll go," said Bo.

Everyone looked at them. Bo stood there dripping weir-water and engine grease, five foot nothing of stowaway, and met their eyes one at a time because looking away would cost more than the cold would.

"I dive. I've been diving since I could walk — off the pilings at Harrowgate, off anything. Four feet is nothing. Ferro can't do it, his chest is bad, and Tamsin—" Bo glanced at the archivist, who was clutching the document satchel like a life vest. "Tamsin has to keep the papers dry. That leaves me or you, Captain, and if you drown, who steers us off these rocks?"

"That's not how—"

"It's exactly how," Bo said. "Give me a knife and a line."

There was a silence in which Sable looked at Bo the way Bo imagined a person looks at a door they're afraid to open. Then Sable moved — fast, no wasted motion — pulling the coil of rope from the bilge hatch, tying one end around Bo's waist with a bowline their fingers tied without being told, checking it twice.

"Three pulls means pull me up. Two means hold. One means give slack. You feel the line go heavy and dead, you haul, no thinking. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Bo." Sable crouched to eye level, and for one second the captain's face wasn't a wall. "The boat is wood and iron. There are more boats. There is only one of you. If it comes to choosing, choose wrong. Choose yourself."

Bo nodded, because arguing took breath, and breath was going to be money tonight.

* * *

The river in November was a lie told by the moon. It looked soft. It was not soft.

Bo stripped to shirt and trousers on the aft deck, teeth already chattering, and lowered themself over the transom into water that hit like a fist made of knives. The cold was total, immediate, a white noise that erased everything except the body's screaming. Bo had been cold before — hungry-cold, gutter-cold, winter-in-a-doorway cold — but this was different. This cold wanted them specifically.

Down.

The line paid out above. The moon came through the surface in broken coins. Bo swam along the hull by touch, counting ribs, until they reached the counter where the intake housing jutted like a broken tooth. Ferro had been right: the sea cock's gate was wedged wide, and the wedge was a hardwood chock driven in tight, deliberate, by someone who knew engines. Someone who'd known exactly which boat and exactly which night.

Bo got fingers around the chock and pulled. It didn't move. Pulled again, bracing boots against the housing, and the world narrowed to the ache in the hands and the fire spreading outward from the core of them, the cold eating inward toward the place where decisions were made.

*Think,* Bo told themself. *You've picked harder locks than this.*

They couldn't get leverage pulling, so they pushed instead — turned, planted both feet on the chock, and kicked with everything the river hadn't taken yet. The chock groaned. Kicked again. On the third kick it shot free and spun away into the black, and Bo's boot slipped off the housing, and the current — done waiting — took them.

The line snapped taut and yanked Bo sideways, tumbling, and for one merciful second that was fine, that was the plan, Sable hauling them home. But the rope had fouled. Bo felt it drag across something below — the weir's drowned timberwork, a snagged spar, some old bone of the river — and then the line went rigid and Bo stopped moving entirely, suspended in the freezing dark, four feet of rope between them and the surface, and the surface suddenly seemed very far away and very bright, like a country they'd emigrated from.

Panic arrived all at once, the way it does, huge and stupid. Bo thrashed. Thrashing spent air. Air was running out — how long had it been? Thirty seconds? A minute? The cold had gone past pain into a strange warm drowsiness, and some quiet traitor part of Bo's brain suggested that this was actually quite comfortable, that letting go would be easy, that nobody would blame—

*No.*

Bo had survived fifteen years by being too stubborn to die politely. They stopped thrashing. Went still. Felt along the rope with numb fingers to the snag point, found the loop caught over a jagged bolt-head on the old timber, and worked it — small motions, precise motions, the motions of a girl who'd once picked a padlock in the dark with a fishhook because her hands were too frozen for anything bigger. The loop jumped free.

And the line went slack, and then it went alive, and the river tore upward past Bo's face, and then there was air — enormous, impossible, sweet-tasting air — and hands on their collar hauling them over the transom onto the deck boards.

Bo vomited river water and lay there shaking so hard their bones rattled.

* * *

Afterward, wrapped in every blanket the boat owned, wedged in front of the re-lit boiler while Ferro coaxed the engine back to life — the intake cleared, the flywheel turning, the old man muttering to it like an apology — Bo watched Sable stand at the rail and not look at them.

That was the thing about Sable Villalobos. Everyone else had fussed — Tamsin had rubbed Bo's arms raw, Ferro had forced hot tea down their throat and called them, in his gravel way, "a fool with good hands" — but Sable just stood at the rail with their back turned, shoulders set like a closed door, watching the black water slide past.

Bo got up, blankets and all, and shuffled over.

"You hauled too late," Bo said. "I got myself loose first."

"I know." Sable didn't turn. "I felt the line go slack before I felt you. I thought—" The captain's jaw worked. "I thought I was hauling in a rope with nothing on the end of it. Fifteen seconds. Longest fifteen seconds of my life, and I've been shot at twice."

"You counted?"

"I count everything. It's a disease." Sable finally turned, and in the low light their face did something complicated and then put itself away again. "Whoever wedged that gate knew our boat. Knew our watch rotation. That chock was cut to fit — that's hours of work, done ashore, planned. Corliss doesn't want to catch us anymore, Bo. He wants to stop us. And he's willing to let the river do the killing so his hands stay clean."

Bo pulled the blanket tighter. Downriver, the rocks they'd scraped over fell astern, and ahead the channel bent north toward the Kestrel Range, whose peaks had appeared that afternoon — impossibly close, impossibly white, wearing snow like a warning delivered early.

"Ferro says the engine'll hold," Bo said. "But we lost an hour on the rocks, and the pump ran dry twice. He says we can't push her faster than half speed until he repacks a bearing, and he needs a day's lay-up to do it right."

"A day." Sable said it the way other people said a curse word.

"There's more." Bo nodded north, at the mountains. "Look at them. Lock keeper at the last station said the snows usually hold off till midwinter. Those aren't midwinter clouds. That's early snow coming down early, and if it closes the pass before we cross—"

"I know what it closes." Sable's voice was quiet. Quiet like ice forming.

The engine coughed, caught, settled into a rough but steady rhythm, and the *Kestrel's Mercy* leaned into the current and began, at half her heart, to make way. Behind them lay the weir, the wrecked schedule, the wedge cut by patient hostile hands. Ahead lay a day lost and a mountain filling up with weather.

Bo stood beside Sable at the rail, two small figures on a wounded boat, and realized they weren't cold anymore — or rather, that the cold had moved somewhere else, somewhere deeper, the kind that blankets didn't touch. Because Bo had finally gotten something worth losing, and now every mile north was a mile spent from a purse that was emptying fast.

Far upstream, where the sky met the Kestrel Range, the first flakes came down out of the dark — fat, slow, unhurried, falling on the water like the river itself was being erased, one flake at a time.

Sable watched them fall and said, softly, to no one Bo was meant to hear:

"They're early. Everything's early. We're the only ones running late."
