## 12. The River Turns Against Us

The engine had a voice, and Ferro knew it the way a man knows his own heartbeat—by listening for what was wrong.

It was wrong now. Not fatally, not yet, but the starboard cylinder was running rough since Bo's dive at the weir, water in places water should never go, and every revolution carried a faint metallic complaint like a coin rattling in a tin cup. Ferro lay on his back in the engine bay with a rag under his head and his hands inside the guts of the *Marrow*, coaxing, adjusting, lying to himself about how long she'd hold.

Above him, through the deck plates, he could hear Sable at the wheel, and the particular quality of silence that meant they were watching the sky.

The sky was the problem. The sky had been the problem for two days.

Fifteen years Ferro had worked engines on boats that never went anywhere—dredges, ferries, a floating cannery that smelled of fish and despair—and fifteen years he had told himself it was because he liked solid moorings. It wasn't. It was because a boat that never left the dock couldn't carry anyone upriver into trouble, and Ferro had sworn off carrying people into trouble the night he walked away from the Condesa del Mar's survivors on the shingle below Gullwrack and let them believe there was no help coming.

He tightened a fitting. The coin-rattle dimmed but didn't die.

*You'll pay for it eventually,* he told the engine, or himself, or the dead of the Condesa, who had been patient creditors for four decades and showed no sign of forgiving the debt.

* * *

They made the narrows above Tallow Bend by midafternoon, and that was when Ferro climbed to the pilothouse, because what came next required eyes that remembered.

Sable stood at the wheel with their collar up, watching the water with the flat attention Ferro had learned to read as vigilance rather than calm. Tamsin was below with the manifest spread across the chart table like a patient on an operating slab. Bo sat on the roof hatch, one arm splinted and bound from the waystation skirmish, refusing with teenage dignity to acknowledge it hurt. Juno was still in a cell in Harrowgate, three hundred miles downstream, and every mile north was a mile bought with fuel they were burning too fast and daylight that was dying early.

"Water's dropping," Sable said without turning. "Fast."

"I know." Ferro came to stand beside them at the glass. "That's not snowmelt season ending. That's cold locking the high country down. River's being fed less every hour." He nodded at the banks, where the willows had gone the color of old rope and the first scum of ice laced the slack water behind the snags. "We're racing something we can't see yet."

"We're racing a registry clerk," Sable said. "I've raced worse."

"You haven't raced winter." Ferro said it gently, which cost him. Gentleness had never been his tool; competence had been, and competence was how he'd justified everything else. "But I can give you back some hours. If you'll take them from me."

Now Sable looked at him. Their eyes were the gray of the river in November, and they held nothing away. "What do you mean?"

"The maps are wrong." He said it plainly, because there was no other way to say it after forty years. "Every chart of this reach above Tallow Bend is built on surveys done after the flood of '61, when the channel shifted. The registry maps, the pilot guides, all of it—they follow the old survey lines. But the deep water moved. There's a channel through the Kestrel Range that runs eleven miles shorter than any marked route, and it's only on paper in one place." He tapped his own temple. "Condesa crew knowledge. We ran her through it twice a year, smuggling season, before she ever made her last voyage. The markers are still there if you know what you're looking for."

"What kind of markers?"

"Not buoys. Buoys get moved, stolen, sunk. Ours were stone." Ferro watched the water slide past and felt the old shame rise like silt disturbed from the bottom. "There's a split rock on the west bank shaped like a kneeling woman—we called her the Widow. Where her shadow falls at midday, that's your line. Two miles up, there's a leaning pine with a lightning scar; you pass it so close you could shake its hand, because the safe water hugs that bank even though every instinct says otherwise. Then the Three Brothers—boulders in midstream, looks like certain death, and it would be, except between the second and third there's a slot wide enough for a boat our size and deep as a church. The charts show the Brothers as a hazard line. They're a doorway."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. The engine knocked its coin-rattle below, and the wind worried at the pilothouse windows with fingers of grit.

"Why tell me now?" they asked finally. "Fifteen years you've been aboard boats I've crewed on. You never mentioned any of this."

"Because mentioning it means saying why I know it." Ferro kept his eyes on the river. "And why I know it is that I was one of nine men who brought the Condesa up this reach the last time, and one of the two who walked away from the wreck alive, and the only one who walked away clean. The other man stayed with the wounded. I told him I was going for help." His throat closed around the lie he'd been swallowing for forty years, and he made himself finish it. "I went for help the way a man goes for help who has already decided the help will arrive too late. I took the skiff. I rowed downstream. And when I reached the fishing camp at Harrowgate, I sat by their fire until morning, and then I kept walking, and I never said her name again out loud until the day you pulled that strongbox out of Gullwrack Cave and I saw the Marchetti seal and understood that my silence hadn't just been cowardice. It had been useful. To them."

He heard Sable breathe. Below them, Bo shifted on the hatch, pretending not to listen and failing completely, and Ferro found he didn't mind. Let the boy hear. Let someone carry it besides him.

"The Marchettis paid for silence," Sable said. It wasn't a question.

"They paid for a great many silences. Mine was cheap. A job reference, a letter to the harbor board, and no questions asked about a survivor who wouldn't testify." He laughed, and it came out like the engine's rattle. "Forty years I thought I'd gotten away with something. Then you opened that box and I learned the depositions were aboard—the testimony against them, sealed in the manifest—and every year I kept my mouth shut was another year they got to keep their fraud. I wasn't neutral, Captain. I was ballast."

* * *

They passed the Widow at noon the next day, and Ferro stood at the rail and watched her shadow fall across the water exactly as it had fallen in another life, thin and black and pointing the way home.

"Line us on the edge of it," he called. "Not the middle. The middle's where the snag lies hidden."

Sable adjusted without argument. That was the thing about them that Ferro had come, over these strange weeks, to trust: they took knowledge wherever it lived and never asked it to apologize for its address. Fifteen years of dodging debts and warrants had taught Sable Villalobos that everyone was carrying something, and the trick was not to care what it was until it mattered.

The channel narrowed. The banks rose into the first shoulders of the Kestrel Range, dark with spruce, and the temperature fell the way a stone falls—steadily, and then all at once. Ice feathers formed along the hull seams. Bo came down from the hatch and wedged himself beside Ferro at the rail, splinted arm cradled, watching the leaning pine grow out of the mist ahead.

"That's really it?" the boy said. "You weren't making it up?"

"Boy, I have lied about many things in my life. Channel markers were never among them. Channels don't care about your feelings, so there's no profit in flattering them."

The pine came and went close enough to touch, exactly as promised, and beyond it the Three Brothers rose out of the current like the backs of drowned cattle. Sable slowed the engine to a crawl—the poor wounded thing knocking its complaint—and Ferro talked them in, second by second: *ease left, ease left, now hold, hold, trust me, NOW.* The slot opened. The boat slid through with a hand's width on either side, and the water between the boulders was black and smooth and deep as a church, just as he'd said, and on the far side the river widened and ran quick and clean toward the range's heart.

Bo whooped, once, and then pretended he hadn't.

Sable killed the throttle and let her drift, and looked at Ferro across the pilothouse with something that wasn't quite gratitude and wasn't quite grief.

"How many times did you run that?"

"Twenty-six. Twenty-seven counting today." He flexed his hands, which ached in the cold the way old guilt aches in the dark. "My father ran cargo through here before me. His father before him. It's in the family blood, reading water." He paused. "The Condesa's master used to say I had the best eye for channels he'd ever hired. He said it the same week I deserted his survivors. People are generous to cowards, Captain. It's one of the ways you know you're one."

"Don't do that," Sable said quietly.

"Do what?"

"Hand me your verdict like I'm supposed to file it. I spent fifteen years doing that to myself—reading the sentence, signing it, serving it daily. You know what I learned?" They turned back to the wheel and put their hands on it. "The sentence doesn't free anybody. It just keeps you in port. What frees you is pointing the boat at the thing you ran from and going anyway." They glanced at him. "You're pointing the boat, Ferro. That's all I'm asking of anyone aboard. Including me."

Ferro turned away and busied himself with the engine telegraph, because his eyes had gone hot and he was too old to pretend it was the wind.

* * *

That evening they tied up in a fold of the bank below the range's first real wall, where the spruce broke the wind and the water ran too fast to freeze. Tamsin cooked something from tinned goods that tasted better than it had any right to, and Bo fell asleep sitting up with his chin on his chest, and Sable stood the first watch alone because they always did, and Ferro sat by the little stove and did what he had not done in forty years.

He wrote it down.

Not a confession—that word was too clean, too finished, too much like something a man signs before a priest. Just names. The names of the Condesa's crew, all nine, and what he remembered of each: Mateo who sang off-key, the twins from the coast who finished each other's sentences, the master with his ruined hand and his generous heart. And the wounded men on the shingle, whose faces he had made himself forget and now made himself remember, one by one, in the stove light, while the river argued with itself outside and the temperature fell past the point where the puddles on the deck went silent with ice.

Tamsin appeared beside him with two cups of tea and set one down without asking. She looked at the page upside down, which was a very Tamsin thing to do, and said nothing for a long time.

"Archivists believe in documents," she said at last. "Even ugly ones. Especially ugly ones. The pretty ones lie; the ugly ones are usually true." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "When we get to Coldharbour, if it comes to it—I can enter that in the record alongside the manifest. Witness corroboration. It matters, legally. It matters more than legally."

"It matters to the dead," Ferro said. "Whether it matters to a magistrate, we'll see. Magistrates are their own weather."

"Oyelaran's weather is changing," Tamsin said. "Corliss's employers will see to that, if Corliss doesn't beat them to it. Men like that eat their own when the wind shifts." She sipped her tea. "Get some sleep, old man. Tomorrow we climb."

Tomorrow they climbed. Ferro looked at the page of names, folded it once, and put it in his breast pocket over his heart, where a man carries the things he intends to deliver.

* * *

He woke before dawn to the sound of Sable's boots on the deck overhead, quick and wrong, and climbed into the freezing pilothouse to find them standing rigid at the glass.

Upstream, where the river bent into the range, the water had stopped moving.

Not slowed. Stopped. A shelf of gray-white stretched across the channel from bank to bank, young ice thickening by the minute in the killing cold, and beyond it the river vanished into a world gone the color of ash. Snow was falling in the high country—heavy, silent, deliberate, the kind of snow that closes passes and ends seasons and turns deadlines into gravestones.

"The river's done," Sable said. Their voice was level, but Ferro had spent enough years around captains to hear the keel beneath it. "She won't carry us another mile. We portage from here—gear on our backs, over the shoulder of the Kestrel, down to Coldharbour on foot." They turned, and in the gray pre-dawn light their face was all resolve and no comfort. "Registry closes when the pass does. Corliss knows that better than we do. Which means his men are already on the mountain."

Ferro looked at the frozen reach, at the boat that had carried them this far on a wounded engine and borrowed time, and felt something settle in his chest that was not fear and not peace but the strange alloy of both that men call purpose.

"Then we'd best teach Bo the Widow's shadow," he said, "before the snow takes her shape. Because from here on, Captain, there are no charts at all."

Behind them, downstream, far off through the falling white, came the sound Ferro had been dreading for two days and would have known anywhere among a thousand sounds: an engine, running rough, coming upriver through the ice.
