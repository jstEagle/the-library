## 13. Portage of Ghosts

The river told us before the sky did.

We'd been running north for two days, Ferro at my shoulder reading water like an old man reading scripture, when we came around the bend below Marrow Bend—Marrow Bend, where the current slows and widens—and found it wearing a skin of ice. Thin, gray, laced with open leads that steamed in the cold, but a skin nonetheless. The Verrazano Reach does not freeze lightly. It freezes once, and then it keeps what it takes until spring.

"Three days," Ferro said. "Maybe four. After that, nothing moves on this river but the wind."

I stood at the wheel and did arithmetic I hated. Coldharbour and the land registry lay over the Kestrel Range, two days' hard walk from the nearest landing if you carried nothing. We were carrying everything: the strongbox under its oilcloth wrap, Tamsin's document case, food, rope, the tools Bo refused to leave behind even though half of them belonged to a boat we might never see again.

The *Vela*—I'd finally let Juno's old name for her stick, the *Vela*, small stubborn sail of a boat—could go no farther. Not through ice. Not with her hull still seeping where the weir had bruised her.

"We beach her at Stone Landing," I said. "Chain her to something permanent. And we walk."

Nobody argued. That was the first sign of how bad it was. Bo always argued, on principle, the way other people breathed.

* * *

Stone Landing was less a landing than a memory of one—a rotted dock, a coal shed leaning into the wind, a waystation hut meant for lock tenders who hadn't been stationed there in twenty years. We beached the *Vela* in the shallows with her bow wedged in gravel, and Ferro chained her to a boulder older than any deed, and I stood looking at her for longer than was useful.

Fifteen years I'd kept that boat moving because a moving boat is hard to find, hard to hold, hard to owe anything to. Now she sat still in freezing water, and every mile between us and her felt like a debt accruing.

"You'll see her again," Tamsin said behind me. She said it the way she filed documents—precisely, as if saying it made it so.

"I know," I lied, and shouldered my pack.

We divided the load by weight and by what each of us could afford to lose. Ferro took the tools and the engine spares, which was absurd—he was sixty-eight and built like a bundle of kindling—but he took them before anyone could stop him, and I understood: this was his penance portage. He'd walked away from the *Condesa del Mar* forty years ago with nothing but his life and his silence. He would carry iron over a mountain now, or die trying, and either way the ledger balanced a little.

Bo carried the climbing rope and the food, because Bo could carry twice their weight and complain about it in three languages they'd picked up from canal crews. Tamsin carried the documents against her chest, strapped down, and checked the straps every hour like a woman guarding a heartbeat.

And I carried the strongbox.

It wasn't heavy. Brass and paper, that's all it was—the manifest, the bill of sale, forty years of dead men's testimony sealed under a Marchetti stamp. It weighed maybe nine pounds. By the second switchback of the first day it weighed nine hundred.

* * *

The Kestrel Range doesn't look like much from the river—a blue shoulder of hills shrugging up out of the valley. From the river. Up close it's a different animal: scree fields that slide under your boots, pine forest gone black and silent with cold, and above the treeline a long bare spine of rock where the wind comes off the high snowfields with teeth. There was no storm yet. But the light had that flattened, yellowish quality that old mountain people call *the held breath*, and Ferro watched the western sky all day without liking what he saw.

"There's weather queued up behind those clouds," he said at our noon halt, hunched against a boulder out of the wind. "Two days. Maybe less. When it comes, it won't come as snow first. It'll come as wind, and the snow will come sideways, and after that nobody finds anybody."

"Then we don't stop," I said.

Bo grinned at me through a face chapped raw. "Captain's decisive today."

"Captain's counting hours."

We made good distance the first day—twelve miles, most of it climbing, Bo ranging ahead like a dog too young to know fear. It was Bo who spotted them, near dusk, from a shelf of rock overlooking the valley we'd climbed out of.

"Smoke," they said, flat. "Back along our line. Two fires, maybe three. Somebody's following our exact track and not bothering to hide it."

Corliss's men. Of course. Corliss didn't need to ambush us on the mountain; he just needed to follow us to the registry and take the box off us at the door, or better, let Oyelaran's clerk do his work and buy the papers legally out from under us afterward. But men hired to shadow are men hired to be ready for more than shadowing, and everyone on that ridge knew it.

"They're a day back," Tamsin said, squinting. "Tired men move slow."

"Tired men with money move however they want," Ferro said.

We walked another hour into the dark, until the cold made stumbling more dangerous than stopping, and sheltered in a hollow beneath an overhang with a fire the size of a cupped hand. Nobody slept well. I lay listening to the wind test the trees and thinking about the waystation marked on Ferro's chart six miles ahead—Kestrel Waystation, an old toll house on the pack trail, stone walls, a stove, a door that locked. A fortress, by mountain standards.

"If we reach it first," I said to the dark, "we hold it."

"And if they reach it first?" Bo asked.

I didn't answer. Neither did anyone else. Some questions you answer by walking faster.

* * *

We reached Kestrel Waystation at midmorning of the second day, and for one hour—one bright, frozen, stupid hour—I believed we'd won the race.

The hut sat in a saddle between two ridges, squat and gray, its slate roof furred with frost. The door was swollen shut and the lock was rusted, but Bo went up the wall like water running uphill, dropped through the broken roof vent, and opened the door from inside grinning like they'd personally conquered the range. Inside: a stone floor, an iron stove, a stack of split wood some tender had left a decade ago, two bunks, and windows small enough to defend.

"We rest here till dark," I said. "Then we cross the saddle at night, hit the descent by dawn, and they wake up to our dust. Ferro, get the stove going. Tamsin—"

That was when Bo, checking the woodpile in the lean-to at the back, said, "Captain. You want to see this."

Boot prints. Fresh ones, pressed into the frost of the lean-to floor, coming up the trail from the south—the direction we'd come—and stopping. Turning around. Heading back down.

They'd been here. They'd seen the hut, measured it, and gone back down the trail to wait. Not ahead of us. Behind us. Herding.

"They want us in the open," Ferro said quietly. "On the saddle, in the wind, where four armed men can walk up to us and have a polite conversation about the strongbox."

"Or they're waiting for dark to come up and take the hut while we sleep," Tamsin said.

"Then we don't sleep," I said. "Bo, bar the door. Ferro, stove. We hold this place until the weather breaks or the deadline forces them to move, whichever—"

The first stone came through the window at midafternoon.

Not thrown—a shot. A rifle report cracked off the ridgeline and the window above the stove exploded inward, glass and splinters and a chip of stone that buried itself in the wall a hand's width from Tamsin's head. She didn't scream. She dropped flat and pulled the document case under her body, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that still shames me: *good instincts.*

"Down! All of you down!" I was already moving, dragging Ferro behind the stone chimney breast, and Bo—Bo was at the back window, and Bo was *looking out of it*, because seventeen-year-olds believe themselves to be made of the same stuff as heroes.

"Bo, *down*—"

Second shot. The frame of the back window disintegrated, and Bo went over backward with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—not a scream, worse, a short punched-out grunt of pure surprise, like someone had knocked the wind out of the world.

I got to them across the floor on my belly, through glass, through the smoke-smell of black powder hanging in the cold air. Blood, a lot of it, pumping bright and shocking onto the gray stone from a furrow torn across the outside of Bo's left arm—graze, graze, not deep, *not deep*, I told myself, pressing both hands on it, telling myself the way you tell yourself anything at the top of your voice inside your own skull.

"Rifle ball took the window ledge apart," Ferro said, calm as a man describing weather, kneeling beside me with a strip of shirt already torn. "Stone fragments did this. Pressure, both sides, keep it tight." His hands were steadier than mine. Forty years ago he'd probably done this exact thing on the deck of the *Condesa*, and the thought arrived and left again without permission.

Outside, a voice carried up the slope, pleasant as a shopkeeper's. "Captain Villalobos! No need for anyone else to bleed today. Just send out the box and walk on south. You have my word as a broker."

Corliss himself. He'd come up the trail with his dogs. Of course he had—men like Corliss never trust the dirty work entirely to the hired.

"You have my word as a captain," I called back through the broken window, "that you can go to hell and I'll draw you a chart."

A laugh, genuinely amused, drifting up through the cold. "Worth trying. We'll talk again at dark, Captain. Or the weather and I will."

* * *

We worked by the light of the stove and the last of the afternoon, and the waystation stopped being a shelter and became a siege position, and I stopped being a person making a plan and became something older and simpler: a defender.

Bo sat propped against the chimney breast, white-faced, arm bound and slung, refusing with tremendous profanity to lie down. The wound was ugly but clean—stone-cut, not ball-torn, Ferro confirmed it twice—and Bo treated the whole business as a personal affront. "I got grazed by a *window*," they kept saying. "Do you understand how embarrassing that is? I've free-climbed the Harrowgate cranes. A *window*."

"Be embarrassed quietly," I said, "and stay behind stone."

Tamsin barricaded the door with the bunk frames and the woodpile, working with a methodical fury I recognized—she was frightened, and she answered fear the only way she knew, which was procedure. Ferro loaded the one thing in the waystation we hadn't expected to find: a lock tender's old fowling piece, rusted but oiled, with a paper sack of cartridges on the shelf beside it as if left there for us specifically by whatever god looks after besieged people. He sat by the shuttered front window with it across his knees and did not look old anymore.

And I stood at the gap in the shutters watching the light die on the saddle, doing the new arithmetic.

Here is what the plan had been: walk fast, beat the snow, file the papers, save Juno. Clean lines. Here is what the plan was now: four of us, one wounded, one gun, a hut full of glass, and somewhere between eight and fourteen hours until either Corliss's men came up the hill in the dark or the storm came down it, and possibly both at once, and beyond all of it a deadline ticking in Coldharbour that cared nothing for bullets or weather or blood.

The journey had stopped being a plan somewhere on the trail below. Plans are things you make about the future. This was a siege, and sieges are only ever about the next hour.

"Captain." Ferro, low, not turning from the window. "When they come up tonight—they'll come from two sides. They'll want the door and the roof vent both. Bo's the only one of us who fits that vent."

"Bo's not going anywhere near that vent."

"Then we lose the roof," Ferro said simply, "or we trust the kid."

Behind me, Bo lifted their head, gray-faced and grinning, and said, "Trust the kid."

And out past the saddle, past the ridgeline, past the whole darkening shoulder of the Kestrel Range, the western sky had begun to turn the color of wet slate, and the held breath of the mountains finally, softly, began to let go.
