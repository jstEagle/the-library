## 15. The Pass Before the Snows

The sky broke open at dawn like a debt being paid in full.

Sable stood at the mouth of the shepherd's hut with a tin cup of snowmelt going cold in their hands and watched the clouds peel back off the Kestrel Range, ridge by ridge, the way a hand peels cloth from a wound. The peaks came out white and hard-edged and impossibly close, as if the storm had carried them all night just to set them down at the hut's doorstep. Above the saddle of the pass, the air was rinsed clean, blue as kiln glass.

"A window," Tamsin said behind them, already packing. She worked fast, folding charts into oilcloth with an archivist's economy, nothing wasted. "Six hours, maybe eight if we're lucky. After that—" She nodded east, where a gray shelf of cloud was building over the far ranges, patient as a creditor.

"After that it closes for the season," Sable said. "And everything in this pack becomes paper."

Bo was already outside, testing the crust on the drifts, light on her feet the way only half-starved teenagers can be. Ferro sat by the dead stove, working his boots on, moving like a man twice his seventy years. He'd barely slept. None of them had. But there was something else in him this morning, something Sable had been trying not to name since the waystation skirmish two days ago—the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

They ate standing up. Hard bread, rendered fat, a strip of dried fish each. Then Sable shouldered the strongbox's contents—manifest, bill of sale, Tamsin's authentication notes sealed in wax and wrapped in three layers of oilskin—and cinched the pack down until the straps creaked.

"Rules," Sable said, and they all looked at them because Sable almost never gave rules. "We move at my pace. Nobody's a hero above the treeline. If anyone goes down, Bo anchors, Tamsin signals, I cut loose the rope. We do not stop for gear." A breath. "We do not stop for anything."

Tamsin met their eyes and understood exactly what they were not saying. *Not even each other.*

The climb took them out of the pines within the hour, into scrub, then into nothing but rock and wind-scoured snow. The world simplified. There was the slope, the rope between them, the sound of four sets of lungs working. Sable counted steps without meaning to—fifteen years of measuring days in miles, and here were days measured in switchbacks instead, each one a stone stair built by shepherds who'd been gone so long their walls had become part of the mountain.

Halfway up the shoulder, Bo slipped.

It was small—a boot punching through a wind slab, a skid of six feet—but the rope went taut and Sable dropped to one knee and held, ice burning through their glove, until Bo found her line again and stood, grinning shakily, blood on her lip where the snow had kissed her.

"Sorry, Captain."

"Don't be sorry. Be careful." But Sable's heart was hammering against their ribs like something trying to get out, and they thought: *this is what it costs, caring about people. Every step is a step you can lose them.*

They crested the summit ridge at midday.

For a moment nobody spoke. The Reach lay below them to the west, a silver thread stitched through brown country, running north toward Coldharbour and the registry and Juno. To the east, the storm they'd outrun was regrouping along the horizon, a wall of slate with lightning buried somewhere inside it like a knife in a mattress. And between those two truths, the descent fell away in a long spine of switchbacks, steep and exposed, the kind of ground where a single wrong step became a very long fall.

"Down and through before dark," Tamsin said, checking the sun. "If the window holds."

"The window won't hold," Ferro said quietly.

Everyone turned. The old man was looking east, at the storm, with the particular stillness of someone reading water. Fifteen years Sable had known him—or thought they had—and only now, after the confessions in the hut, did they understand how much of that stillness was guilt wearing patience as a disguise.

"Those aren't following us," Ferro said. "They're herding us. Corliss knows this window too. He's been sitting under it waiting for us to come out, same as we have." He spat into the wind. "There'll be men on the switchbacks. Mark me."

* * *

There were men on the switchbacks.

Sable saw them from the third turn down—a glint of steel where no steel should be, then movement, deliberate, unhurried. Four figures spreading across the lower zigzags like ink dropped in water, cutting the line of descent. Behind them, higher up, two more had appeared on the ridge they'd just left, closing the door.

"Corliss," Tamsin breathed.

"He doesn't want a fight on this ground any more than we do," Sable said, thinking aloud, thinking fast. "He wants the papers. He'll take us alive if he can. That's his weakness."

"And ours?" Bo asked.

"Our numbers. Our speed. And the fact that he thinks we're four frightened people with a deadline." Sable studied the ground below, the geometry of the switchbacks, the way the lower turns funneled toward a narrow shelf above a scree field. There. Where the path kinked against a buttress of black rock, there was a choke point—three meters wide, cliff on one side, drop on the other. One person could hold it. Not forever. But long enough.

The arithmetic assembled itself in Sable's head with terrible clarity, and they hated every number in it.

"Ferro—"

"No." The old man said it before Sable could shape the rest. He was already unbuckling his pack, already handing the weight of it to Bo, already pulling the heavy wrench from his tool roll—the one he'd carried since Harrowgate, since the boat, since a lifetime of engines. "You're the fastest and you carry the papers. The girl climbs like a spider and the archivist has the seals memorized. I'm the slowest thing on this mountain, and I'm tired, Captain. Been tired fifteen years."

"You don't have to buy it back today," Sable said. "Not like this."

Ferro smiled, and it was the saddest thing Sable had seen in a year of sad things. "Boy," he said—because that was what he called Sable when the truth got too big for titles—"I ran from that ship when people needed me to stand. I kept my mouth shut while good folks drowned in paperwork instead of water. You're offering me the one thing I never earned." He gripped Sable's shoulder, his hand rough and warm as an engine block. "A chance to stand. Don't take it from me. It's the last salvage I've got."

Below, the first of Corliss's men started up the switchbacks, calling out something cheerful and horrible about how nobody needed to get hurt.

"Go," Ferro said.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do.

They ran the upper switchbacks, Bo leading, Tamsin's breath tearing in ragged counts, Sable driving them from behind with the pack straps biting into their shoulders. Below and behind, Ferro planted himself in the choke point at the buttress like a fence post driven into bedrock, wrench in both hands, and Sable heard—not saw, heard—the collision. A shout. A clang of steel on stone. Another shout, angrier.

Then a voice, smooth even across half a mile of thin mountain air: Corliss himself, or near enough, saying something reasonable-sounding that Sable couldn't make out and didn't need to. Offers. Corliss always led with offers.

"Hold the pace," Sable gritted. "Don't look back."

Bo looked back. Everyone looked back. It was human.

What they saw, framed for one instant between turns of the trail, was Ferro swinging the wrench in a wide, practiced arc—the arc of a man who'd spent forty years breaking rusted bolts free—and two of Corliss's men giving ground, and the old man roaring something wordless and enormous, and then the trail bent and he was gone.

They made the shelf above the scree field before the sounds stopped.

That was the worst part, Sable would think later. Not the fight. The silence after it. The way the mountain simply went on being beautiful, the light gilding the snowfields, the storm wall crawling closer, as if none of it had ever contained an old man's voice.

"They've taken him," Tamsin said. She'd stopped at the edge of the scree, chest heaving, face gray. "Sable. They've taken him and they'll—"

"I know what they'll do." Sable's voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. Their eyes were already moving, measuring. Back up the switchbacks: five men, maybe six, converging on the buttress, Ferro a small dark figure among them, upright, alive—that was something, that was everything, that was nothing. Ahead: the descent, the valley floor maybe two hours down, then the river road, then Coldharbour, then the registry, which closed its books for the season when the pass closed, which closed—

When? Sable looked east. The storm wall had crossed half the sky while they fought and ran. Two hours. Three, if the mountain was merciful, and the mountain had never once been merciful in Sable's experience.

Back for Ferro meant fighting uphill, outnumbered, with the papers—the only evidence that could free Juno, the only reason any of this had cost anything—risked in a melee on frozen rock. Forward meant walking away. Walking away from a man who had stayed behind on purpose, who had chosen it, who had *asked*, whose whole redemption rested on Sable honoring the choice rather than undoing it.

Or that was the story Sable could tell themselves. They knew better. Ferro had asked. But asking wasn't the same as wanting to be left, and leaving wasn't the same as honoring, and no sentence clean enough to sleep behind could be built out of what Sable felt right now, standing on a shelf of stone with the wind pulling at them like hands.

"Captain." Bo's voice, small. "What do we do?"

Sable looked at the girl—at the split lip, the frost-burned cheeks, the absolute trust—and understood that whatever they chose next would become the load-bearing wall of everyone's life for years. That was the thing nobody told you about command. It wasn't deciding. It was deciding *in front of witnesses who would carry your decision inside them forever.*

"We go down," Sable said.

The words tasted like engine oil and ash.

"We make the registry. We file the manifest. And then—" Sable's jaw tightened until it ached. "And then we burn Corliss's whole world down around his ears, and we take Ferro back, or we die trying. But we do it with the law in our hands instead of a wrench. Because Ferro didn't stay behind so we could throw his sacrifice away on a hillside. He stayed so the papers would arrive. So we *arrive.*"

Tamsin wiped her face with the back of her glove—whether wind-tears or real ones, the mountain didn't care and neither did she. "He'd say the same."

"He'd say it kinder," Sable said. "Move."

* * *

They descended through the dying light like souls with a debt to settle.

The storm ate the eastern sky and then the southern and then began, patiently, to eat the world. Snow came sideways, then came everywhere, erasing the trail, the switchbacks, the valley, until Sable navigated by slope and compass and the memory of Ferro's voice teaching them the old channel markers—*the Condesa crew cut a blaze on the third pine past the spring, see, boy, trees remember even when men don't*—and found, impossibly, a blazed pine at the tree line, and another below it, the dead ship's crew guiding them home across a mountain they'd never walked.

By full dark they reached the river road, and by midnight, half-frozen, they saw Coldharbour's lights strung along the valley floor like a handful of coins spilled on black cloth.

Sable counted heads twice. Three. Always three. The fourth was a hole in the world shaped like an old man, and Sable carried it in their chest the way they carried the papers—wrapped, sealed, and heavier than iron.

At the town gate, Bo caught Sable's sleeve. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and young.

"When we go back for him," she said. Not *if*. Sable loved her for that and hated the mountain for making it necessary.

"When," Sable agreed. "But first—" They touched the pack, felt the oilskin corner of the manifest through the canvas, solid as a heartbeat. "First we make his sacrifice worth something. Dawn's in four hours. The registry opens at seven."

Above the pass, the snows came down like a verdict, sealing the Kestrel Range for the winter—with Ferro on the wrong side of it, and Dane Corliss holding the key.
