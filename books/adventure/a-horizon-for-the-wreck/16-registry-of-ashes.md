## 16. Registry of Ashes

The land registry at Coldharbour sat on a granite bluff above the river like a tomb that had learned to do paperwork. Tamsin Vey had seen a hundred government buildings in her career as an archivist—some grander, some older, none more deliberately intimidating. The Marchetti money was everywhere here, worked into the very stone: the brass fittings gleamed with private patronage, the steps were swept clean by hired men, and the coat of arms over the door bore the subtle addition of a shipping anchor that hadn't been part of the original provincial design. Someone had paid to be woven into the architecture of the law itself.

She climbed those steps with the document case strapped across her chest like a bandolier, and she counted her heartbeats because counting kept panic from metastasizing.

Behind her, Sable Villalobos stood at the bottom of the stairs with the riverboat's lines still in hand, tied off at the public dock below—a violation of docking protocol they couldn't afford to care about. Bo crouched near the mooring post, pretending to adjust a cleat, eyes flicking constantly to the road. Old Ferro was supposed to be watching the boat itself, but his gaze kept climbing to the registry doors as if he expected them to open and disgorge soldiers.

They had made it. That was the miracle Tamsin kept refusing to believe. Four days over the Kestrel Range with frostbitten fingers and Bo's bandaged arm, one ambush survived, Ferro left behind on a switchback with three of Corliss's hired men bearing down on him—and still, impossibly, the case against her chest held the original manifest of the Condesa del Mar, authenticated in her own hand, witnessed twice, sealed against weather and tampering.

And the concession expired at sundown.

"Time," Sable called up quietly.

Tamsin checked her pocket watch. Eleven minutes past four. The registry closed at five. The clerk's office would stop accepting filings at half past, per the notice posted in the vestibule—the only notice in the building not printed on Marchetti paper stock, though Tamsin had already noted that even it used the same typeface. Small things. She collected small things the way other people collected grievances.

"I'll be out before the quarter hour," she said.

"If you're not—"

"You'll come in after me and make a scene, and we'll both lose." She allowed herself a thin smile. "I've been thrown out of better archives than this one, Captain. Go be anxious where I can't see you."

Sable's face did something complicated—gratitude and fear and the particular helplessness of a person who has finally learned to trust others and hates how it feels—and then turned away toward the dock. Tamsin watched for exactly two seconds, because sentiment was a luxury billed by the minute, and went inside.

* * *

The vestibule smelled of floor wax and cold stone. A single clerk's window glowed at the end of the entry hall, behind frosted glass, and beneath it a wooden bench worn smooth by generations of people waiting to have their lives entered into record or erased from it.

The man at the window was young—younger than Tamsin had expected. Oyelaran's senior clerk, the one whose signature appeared on every certified filing from this district: Pellam Wraye. She knew his work. She had studied it, on the riverboat, by lamplight, while the rest of the crew slept. His certifications were meticulous. His margins were immaculate. And in eleven years of records, Wraye had never once rejected a filing from a Marchetti interest, and had rejected forty-one filings from parties contesting Marchetti interests—on procedural grounds, every time, each rejection citing a different subsection of the registry code, as if he were rotating through them to avoid the appearance of a pattern.

A pattern was exactly what Tamsin did for a living.

Wraye looked up when her shadow crossed his window. He had a narrow face, ink-stained fingers, and the weary competence of a man who was very good at a job he no longer believed in. She recognized that too. She saw it in mirrors some mornings.

"Filing?" he asked.

"Entry of claim. Salvage and provenance documentation, under the Reach Concession Act, section nine." She set the document case on the counter and opened it with the unhurried precision of someone who had rehearsed this moment for six hundred miles. "Original manifest of the Condesa del Mar, lost forty-one years ago, recovered from the wreck site within the concession period. Authentication certificate enclosed, my seal, my credentials—I was senior archivist at the Harrowgate Provincial Records Office before my dismissal. Two witnesses available outside."

Wraye didn't reach for the documents. He looked at them the way a customs officer looks at luggage he's been told contains something.

"The Condesa," he said.

"You know the name."

"Everyone knows the name." He glanced past her shoulder—at the clock on the wall, at the empty vestibule, at something that wasn't there but might have been. "You're the party that came over the range. There was a telegram this morning."

"There was a telegram yesterday, too, I'd wager. And one the day before." Tamsin kept her voice level, almost pleasant. "From whom, I wonder? Because I'd hate to think the Coldharbour registry took instructions on filings from interested private parties. That would be an irregularity."

Something moved behind Wraye's eyes—not guilt, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man meeting someone who has read his file.

"Ma'am," he said, "it is now twenty-two minutes past four. If you'll slide the documents through, I'll review them for completeness."

"Review," Tamsin repeated. "Not enter."

"Everything gets reviewed."

"Everything gets reviewed," she agreed, "and Marchetti filings get entered the same day, sometimes the same hour. I've read your daybook, Mr. Wraye. It's public record. I read everything that's public record; it's a professional deformity." She slid the manifest through the tray, square by square, letting each page land like a card dealt face-up. "So let's save ourselves the theater. Enter the claim. Certify the time of receipt. You don't have to like me, and you don't have to believe me—that's what authentication certificates are for. Mine will survive any examination your office can mount. I guarantee it professionally, and my reputation is the only thing I have left, which means I defend it the way other people defend their children."

For a long moment Wraye said nothing. His pen hovered. The clock ticked with the patience of stone.

Then he drew the manifest toward him, and Tamsin felt hope rise in her chest like water in a lock chamber—careful, controlled, terrified of its own weight.

He unrolled the first page. His eyes moved down the columns. She watched him read the cargo listing, the deposition schedule, the seal of the ship's notary—and she watched the exact moment he reached the bill of sale folded behind it, the one proving the Marchetti concession rested on a forgery, the one that would unravel forty years of the family's claims along the entire Reach.

Wraye went very still.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "This is a significant document."

"It is."

"And you're filing it under section nine. Provenance recovery." He looked up. "Not under the criminal referral statute."

"The criminal courts can subpoena it once it's entered into record. My concern is the concession. The concession lapses at sundown. Once it lapses without challenge on file, the wreck site reverts to provincial custody—and the Marchettis' survey crews are the only ones who know where it lies, and the provincial custodians will take their word for everything, forever. Entering this today is the only way anyone's grandchildren ever see this document mean anything." She leaned closer to the frosted glass. "You know all of this. You've known it for years, Mr. Wraye. Forty-one rejections. I counted."

"Thirty-eight," he said quietly. "Three were legitimate."

The honesty of it nearly undid her. Here it was, then—the thing she had traveled over a mountain to find: not courage, exactly, but a man standing at the exact midpoint between the life he had and the one he could no longer pretend to deserve.

"Thirty-eight," Tamsin conceded. "Enter this one right, and it's thirty-seven."

Wraye picked up his pen. Dipped it. Held it over the entry book—

And the vestibule door opened behind her.

* * *

Tamsin did not turn around immediately. That was discipline; her hands wanted to shake and her spine wanted to whirl, but she had spent fifteen years learning that the second before you turn around is the last moment in which you still control the room. She listened instead. One set of footsteps, unhurried, well-shod, stopping a precise and practiced distance behind her—close enough to be heard, far enough to deny intent.

"Madam Vey." The voice was smooth as river ice. "What a long way you've come."

Dane Corliss. Of course. She had never met him, but she had read his filings too—dozens of them, elegant, airtight, each one a small masterpiece of plausible deniability. A man who filed paperwork the way assassins fired shots: rarely, precisely, always from a distance.

"Mr. Corliss." She turned. He was exactly as Sable had described: silver at the temples, charm worn like good tailoring, eyes that calculated while the mouth smiled. "I'd say this is a surprise, but you did send the telegrams."

"I sent inquiries." He spread his hands. "Routine due diligence. The Marchetti interests have a concession expiring this evening, and when large assets are in play, we notify the relevant offices to expect... irregular activity. Forgers, mostly. People with grudges. It's remarkable what surfaces in the final hours of a concession—everyone with a grievance and a facsimile press comes crawling out of the hills." His gaze dropped to the counter, to the manifest lying open under Wraye's hovering pen, and his smile deepened by a degree. "Ah. Case in point."

"That document is authenticated," Tamsin said.

"Documents usually are. That's rather the point of forgery—to produce documents that authenticate beautifully." Corliss strolled forward, boots ringing on the stone, and rested one gloved hand on the counter's edge, beside the manifest but not touching it. A lawyer's discipline. "Here's my difficulty, Madam Vey. This filing arrives minutes before expiry, carried over the mountains by a fugitive riverboat captain, a dismissed archivist, and—forgive me—a child, accompanied by no chain-of-custody documentation whatsoever. No provincial escort. No salvage license in the filer's name. Recovered, we're told, from a wreck site that has been conclusively surveyed and found empty on three separate occasions." He clicked his tongue softly. "If I were the clerk, I would have questions."

"If you were the clerk," Tamsin said, "you'd be the forty-second rejection."

Corliss's smile didn't waver, but something behind it adjusted—she had scored, and he noted it, and he banked it. "Procedural integrity isn't persecution, madam. It's the foundation of the registry system. Surely an archivist understands that better than anyone. Rules exist so that moments like this—dramatic, emotional, urgent—don't override the careful order of things." He turned his attention to Wraye, and his voice warmed into something almost paternal. "Pellam. You have a difficult call here. Nobody envies you. But the code is the code. Take whatever time the review requires."

The clock read nineteen minutes to five.

Tamsin understood then, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was the whole design. Not bribery—Corliss was too careful, and Wraye perhaps too honest for a bribe to be necessary. Just procedure. Time. The infinite, patient, weaponized slowness of a system built by the powerful to be navigated only by the powerful. The Marchettis didn't need to steal the truth. They only needed the truth to arrive late, incomplete, improperly witnessed—one subsection at a time, one clerk's caution at a time—until it expired on a shelf somewhere, technically preserved and practically dead.

Fury rose in her like floodwater. Fifteen years ago she had buried evidence for money, and she had told herself it was survival, and she had hated herself in the specific, granular way that only archivists can hate themselves—because she knew exactly what each destroyed page had cost, line by line, name by name. She had come over a mountain to buy that debt back, and now a man in good gloves proposed to bury it again with a shrug and a citation.

"No," she said.

Corliss raised an eyebrow.

"No review," Tamsin said. Her voice came out low and steady, and she was distantly proud of it. "Mr. Wraye, I am invoking my right under the Registry Appeals Act to demand immediate entry or immediate written refusal, signed, dated, timed, and citing the specific statutory grounds. Not verbal. Written. On the record. Because a written refusal becomes a document, Mr. Wraye, and documents are my religion. I will appeal it. I will publish it. I will read it aloud at Juno Reyes's trial in Harrowgate, and at every hearing thereafter, and I will spend the rest of my considerably stubborn life making sure that everyone who ever asks about the Condesa del Mar learns the name of the clerk who refused her manifest eleven minutes before the deadline—on the written instruction, duly telegraphed, of a Marchetti broker standing at his window."

The vestibule was silent except for the clock.

Corliss's charm finally cracked—not into anger, but into something more dangerous: recalculation. "Madam Vey—"

"Mr. Corliss." She turned to him fully now, and let him see all of it, every year of shame and every mile of the range. "You think this ends at sundown. It doesn't. Sundown ends a concession. It doesn't end testimony. It doesn't end copies. It doesn't end me."

It was, she would reflect later, the truest sentence she had spoken in fifteen years, and also the most reckless—because she had just told Dane Corliss, in front of a witness, that the evidence existed beyond the document in front of him. That there were copies. That killing the filing wouldn't kill the story.

His eyes flickered with it. Filed it. Banked it.

Wraye's pen touched paper.

* * *

He wrote for a long time. Long enough that the clock passed five o'clock and neither Corliss nor Tamsin remarked on it, because the entry—or the refusal—had been requested before the hour, and both of them knew the fight was now over which words would fill the page.

When Wraye finished, he blotted the sheet, folded it once, and slid it through the tray with two fingers, the way a man returns something he cannot bring himself to hand over.

Tamsin unfolded it.

**REFUSAL OF ENTRY — FILING NO. 44-C.** Grounds: *incomplete chain of custody; absence of licensed salvager of record; documentary provenance unverifiable within filing period; referral to district magistrate for adjudication of authenticity prior to acceptance.* Signed, dated, timed: 5:04 in the afternoon, seven minutes after the concession had lapsed, four minutes after her demand.

Adjudication by the district magistrate. By Oyelaran. By the man who had scheduled Juno Reyes's trial for the week after the pass closed, after the evidence became legally worthless—who owed the Marchettis his seat and his salary and probably his soul.

Referral to Oyelaran was not a delay. It was a burial with honors.

"Well," Corliss said softly, behind her. "The system works."

Tamsin read the refusal twice, committing it to memory the way she committed everything—line by line, margin note by margin note—and then she did the only thing left to her. She folded it into her document case alongside the manifest, where the refusal and the evidence it refused now lay together, each making the other more dangerous.

"Thank you for the document, Mr. Wraye," she said. "Signed and timed. You have no idea what you've just given me."

Wraye looked at her, and then—quickly, before Corliss could see, or perhaps knowing full well that he would see and unable to stop himself—he tapped one ink-stained finger twice against the counter. Twice. The old clerks' signal, she remembered suddenly, from her years in the records office. *Check the annex. Check what's already filed.*

Her breath caught.

Forty-one rejections. Thirty-eight, he'd corrected. Three legitimate. And a meticulous man, an honest man drowning in dishonest work, who had spent eleven years certifying Marchetti filings and hating every signature—what does such a man do with his guilt? Where does an archivist's soul go to hide?

Into the files. Always into the files.

Tamsin walked out of the registry with her spine straight and her mind racing, past Corliss's pleasant "Good evening, madam," down the granite steps into the blue dusk where Sable stood waiting at the rail of the battered riverboat, snow beginning to fall in the first fat, indifferent flakes of the season.

"We lost," Sable said. Tamsin could hear it in the flatness—not a question. Word had traveled, or the expression on her face had.

"The filing was refused. Referred to Oyelaran." She reached the gangplank and stopped, gripping the rail, letting herself say the next part only to Sable, quietly, while the snow thickened around them. "But the clerk signaled me, Captain. There's something already in the annex—something Wraye filed himself, sometime in the last eleven years, hidden in plain sight in his own registry. He's been keeping receipts." She looked back up at the glowing window, where the narrow silhouette of Pellam Wraye still stood watching them, one hand raised—not waving. Tapping. Twice. Against the glass.

"And Sable—somewhere between that man's guilt and our mountain, we may have just found the second copy of everything. Because I don't think Ferro was the only coward on this river who mailed the truth somewhere safe."

Below them, at the dark edge of the dock, a lantern came around the warehouse corner. Bo's whistle sounded—two short notes, the danger signal—and a figure detached itself from the shadows, moving fast, wearing the unmistakable silhouette of one of Corliss's hired men, walking straight for the boat.
