## 18. The Trial of Juno Reyes

Harrowgate smelled the same as it had fifteen years ago: tar and frying oil and the particular sourness of a river town that had learned to look away from its own docks. Sable stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Mercy*—their boat, their patched and salt-scoured boat—and watched the courthouse clock across the square tick toward nine. The building was granite and self-importance, three stories of it, with a copper weathervane bent from some storm nobody remembered. Justice in Harrowgate kept regular hours. It just didn't keep them for everyone.

Juno had been in the magistrate's lockup for nineteen days.

Sable knew the number the way they knew channel depths—by heart, by ache. Nineteen days since they'd come upriver through the fog with the strongbox rattling under the engine plates like a guilty conscience. Nineteen days since they'd stood outside the jail window and watched Juno Reyes press her palm flat against the glass, mouthing something Sable couldn't read and hadn't needed to. *You came. You finally came.*

The evidence that could free her sat forty miles south, sealed in a registry clerk's refusal, and the snows had already closed the pass behind them. Tamsin had filed the manifest. Tamsin had done everything right, at the last possible hour, and Oyelaran's clerk had looked at her over his spectacles and said the words *procedural irregularity* as if he were describing weather. The document existed now only as a rejected filing, a ghost in a ledger, legally worth less than the paper it was stamped on.

Which left the courtroom. Which left testimony. Which left Sable.

"You're doing the thing where you stand very still and pretend you're a post," Bo said, coming up beside them with two tin cups of dock coffee that tasted like boiled rope. The kid pressed one into Sable's hands. Their arm was still bound from the waystation skirmish, sling knotted from a shirt hem, and they held their cup awkwardly against their chest rather than admit any weakness. Fifteen years old and already fluent in stubbornness. "Drink it. You'll need your hands steady."

"My hands are always steady."

"Sure." Bo sipped. "That's why you've been staring at that clock like it owes you money."

Below on the dock, Ferro was checking the engine housing one more time—the old man's ritual, his penance, his prayer. He'd barely spoken since Coldharbour. Since Corliss's men had taken him off the switchback while Sable ran north with the documents burning a hole in their coat. He'd come back to them four days ago, released without charge, dropped at a lock station like returned freight, thinner and grayer and with a new hesitance in his step. He wouldn't say what they'd asked him. He wouldn't say what he'd told them. But every night since, he'd sat up past midnight in the cabin, writing letters Sable pretended not to see.

Tamsin emerged from belowdecks with her hair pinned back and her archivist's posture on like armor. She'd spent the morning copying the manifest by hand—three copies, hidden in three places, because she trusted no institution anymore, including the one she'd once served. "The gallery will be full," she said. "Corliss made sure of it. Half those people are paid to be there."

"And the other half?"

"Are paid to believe whatever the first half says."

Sable drank the terrible coffee. Somewhere in the town a bell began to ring the hour, and the sound moved through Sable's chest like water through a hull finding its leak.

*Here is what invisibility costs,* they thought. *It costs exactly this. It costs nineteen days of an innocent woman in a cell while you rehearse silence.*

* * *

The courtroom was oak-paneled and overheated, built for a hundred and holding perhaps sixty: dockworkers who owed Juno favors, Marchetti men in good coats sitting too casually near the doors, a scattering of townsfolk hungry for spectacle, and one thin man in the back row with ink-stained fingers who took notes no one had hired him to take. Sable noticed him anyway. Fifteen years of reading rooms had made noticing a reflex.

Magistrate Oyelaran presided from beneath a portrait of some dead benefactor whose face had gone dark with varnish. He was smaller than Sable remembered from the one glimpse outside the jail—a compact man with careful hands and eyes that never quite landed on anything he was looking at. He read the charges in a voice like a man counting someone else's coins: theft of survey equipment belonging to the Marchetti Salvage Concession, value assessed at a figure designed to elevate the crime from petty to serious. Value assessed, Sable noted, by the party claiming the loss.

Juno sat at the defendant's table in a borrowed jacket, her wrists chafed pink where the irons had been. When she caught sight of Sable in the gallery she didn't smile. That was the thing about Juno—she'd never wasted a gesture on comfort. She just looked, and held the look, and let it say what it said: *I know why you're here. I know what it costs. Decide fast.*

The prosecution called its witnesses, and they were a liturgy of the bought. A Marchetti foreman who identified Juno from "across the yard, at night, but I'd know her anywhere." A constable who'd found nothing in her lodging but testified to her "general manner of guilt" until even Oyelaran's mouth thinned. A warehouse clerk whose testimony contradicted itself twice before lunch, and whose corrections were entered into the record with the gentle patience of a man being paid by the hour.

Through all of it, Sable sat with their hands flat on their knees and did the arithmetic they'd been doing since Coldharbour.

Option one: stay silent. Let the machinery grind. Juno would take a sentence—two years, maybe three, managed downward if Corliss felt generous, which he might, because a cowed Sable Villalobos was worth more to him than a martyred dock clerk. Sable would keep the boat, keep the river, keep the nameless freedom they'd built out of fifteen years of miles. And they would spend the rest of their life measuring days in the distance between themselves and this courtroom.

Option two.

There was no option two yet. There was only a shape moving under the ice of option one, the way the Condesa had moved under the tide, waiting for the water to drop.

Tamsin leaned close during the recess. "The defense advocate is competent," she murmured. "Oyelaran appointed him himself, which means either mercy or theater. He'll get the contradictions on record. It won't be enough. Not without corroboration."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her eyes were sharp and kind at once, the expression of a woman who had buried evidence once and spent every year since digging. "Because I've watched you sit through six hours of perjury wearing the face of a woman deciding whether to buy a hat. Whatever you're going to do, do it before the closing arguments. After that, the record closes, and records—" she almost smiled, "—records are my religion. They don't reopen for anyone."

* * *

They came back from the recess and the afternoon light fell through the high windows in long dusty bars, and the prosecution rested, and the defense began calling character witnesses—dockhands, a landlady, an old pilot who swore Juno Reyes wouldn't steal so much as a mooring line. And then the advocate turned toward the gallery, scanning it with the deliberate aimlessness of a man following instructions, and said:

"The defense calls—"

And Sable stood up before he could finish, because if she let him say the name she'd have another breath to lose her nerve in, and she'd spent fifteen years learning that nerve doesn't survive a second breath.

"Sable Villalobos," they said, loud enough for the clerk to hear, loud enough for the Marchetti men by the doors to go still. "I'll testify. Voluntarily. Under oath."

The room made a sound like water pulling back from a shore. Oyelaran's gavel came down once—not for order, Sable thought, but because his hand needed something to do. In the back row, the ink-stained man stopped writing and looked up.

"Sheriff," Oyelaran said carefully, "do we know this person?"

"We know the name," said a voice from the doorway, smooth as poured oil, and Dane Corliss stepped into the courtroom as if he owned the air in it. Of course he'd come. Of course he'd want a front-row seat. He wore gray today, funeral-gray, and his smile was for everyone and no one. "A riverboat captain of... considerable local reputation. Though I confess I'm curious what testimony Captain Villalobos imagines is relevant to a simple matter of theft."

"That's for the court to determine," the defense advocate said, and there was something new in his voice—something that suggested he'd been handed a live grenade and found, to his surprise, that he wanted to throw it.

Oyelaran looked at Sable for a long moment. Looking, this time. Actually looking. "Captain Villalobos. You understand that sworn testimony binds you. Everything you say becomes part of the permanent record. You may be charged for anything you admit under oath."

"I understand," Sable said. "That's rather the point."

They walked to the stand. The floorboards creaked under their boots, the same sound the deck of the *Mercy* made in a swell, and Sable fixed on that—*this is just a deck, this is just weather, you have run worse water than this*—and put their hand on the book and swore.

The advocate approached. Up close he looked tired and slightly amazed, a man watching his own luck turn. "State your name and occupation for the record."

"Sable Villalobos. Riverboat captain, licensed on the Verrazano Reach, fifteen years running cargo and charter between Harrowgate and the sea locks."

"Fifteen years. And in that time, have you known the defendant?"

"Known her? I've known her since we were both seventeen years old and stupid enough to sign aboard the *Marrow's Pride*." Sable let the name land. Watched the Marchetti men exchange glances. The *Marrow's Pride* meant nothing to most of this room. It meant everything to the ones it needed to. "She was boatswain. I was helmsman. We served together three years."

"And your relationship since?"

"Distant," Sable said, and heard the truth of it hurt going out of their mouth. "Deliberately distant. Because fifteen years ago, on the *Marrow's Pride*, there was a mutiny. The crew rose against a captain who was running contraband under a legitimate charter and beating the men who questioned it. The mutiny failed. Most of us scattered. Some were taken." They paused. "I ran. I've been running since. I've used other names. I've avoided ports where warrants might find me. I have lived fifteen years as a fugitive, and I am telling this court so now, freely, because the alternative is letting an innocent woman hang her future on my silence."

The room had gone absolutely quiet. Even Corliss wasn't smiling anymore; he was calculating, visibly, furiously, and Sable understood that for the first time since Gullwrack Cave, the advantage had changed hands.

Oyelaran's voice came down dry as August grass. "Captain. This court is trying a theft. What does a fifteen-year-old mutiny have to do with survey equipment?"

"Nothing, Your Honor. Yet." Sable turned to face the advocate. "Ask me about the cave."

"Cave?"

"Gullwrack Cave, on the southern reach. Forty years ago a ship called the *Condesa del Mar* went down in a storm with all hands and a sealed manifest, and the wreck was never found, and the salvage concession to search for it was eventually granted—to whom, Your Honor?"

The advocate glanced at his notes, though Sable suspected he didn't need them. "To the Marchetti family. By grant of the regional registry."

"Granted on the strength of a bill of sale," Sable said, "purporting to show that the *Condesa*'s surviving owner sold all claim to the wreck in the year after her sinking. I have seen that bill of sale. I have also seen the original manifest, recovered from the wreck itself, which I can lead this court to. The signatures do not match. The ink is wrong by a decade. The bill of sale is a forgery, Your Honor, and the entire Marchetti concession rests on it—and the reason Miss Reyes sits in the dock is that she works the Harrowgate docks, she saw the surveyors charting the approach to that cave, and she knew too much about what they were looking for. The theft is a frame. It isn't a case. It's a lid."

"Objection!" One of the Marchetti lawyers was on his feet, silk and outrage. "This is speculation! This is—where is this alleged manifest? Where is this evidence?"

"It was filed at the Coldharbour land registry," Tamsin's voice rang out from the gallery before Sable could answer, clear and furious, "and your magistrate's clerk refused to enter it. Ask him why. Ask him who instructed him."

"Order," Oyelaran said, and then, terribly quietly, "order," and Sable watched the man's careful hands go still on the bench, watched him look at the dark-faced portrait above him—at the benefactor whose fortune had built this courthouse, whose name Sable had read on the registry's founding stone in Coldharbour, a Marchetti name—and saw the arithmetic happen behind his eyes. Saw him realize that the lid was off, that the ink-stained man in the back row was writing every word, that the story was already loose in the room and could not be stuffed back into any ledger.

"Captain Villalobos," Oyelaran said slowly. "You state that you can produce this manifest. That you can locate the wreck."

"I can. I will. Under the court's protection, and on the record."

"And you understand that in giving this testimony you have admitted to years of false names, evaded warrants—"

"I have, Your Honor. Every word. I'm not asking this court to forget who I've been." Sable looked down at Juno, who was watching them with an expression Sable had waited fifteen years to earn and would have traded every mile of those years to have earned it sooner. "I'm asking it to decide who I am now. And who she has been all along. She never stole anything except, once, a loaf of bread off my galley table, and I let her have it because she made better coffee than I did."

Someone in the gallery laughed—a real laugh, startled out of a stranger—and the spell of fear in the room cracked down the middle like river ice in spring.

Corliss rose. "Your Honor, if I may—the defense produces a self-confessed fugitive, a document of unknown provenance allegedly refused at registry, and a fairy tale about a legendary wreck. Is this a courtroom or a tavern?"

"It's a courtroom, Mr. Corliss," Oyelaran said, and something had shifted in his voice, something that sounded almost like a man stepping off a ledge and discovering wings. "Which is why we proceed by evidence and not by interruption. The witness will stand down—for now. The court is adjourned until tomorrow morning, at which time the question of the manifest's admissibility will be heard, and Mr. Corliss—you may inform your clients that this court will entertain a formal motion to compel the registry to produce the refused filing." He paused, and the pause had teeth. "In writing. Today."

The gavel came down. The room erupted into motion and noise, and through it Sable walked down from the stand on legs that felt borrowed, and Juno met them at the bar of the court, escorted, ironed, grinning like the seventeen-year-old who'd stolen bread off their table.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"I had a detour," Sable said. "Forty years long. Somebody else's."

Juno's grin softened into something older and truer. "Worth the trip?"

Sable thought of the ribcage of the ship rising out of the drained dark, of the brass box stamped with a seal that should have stayed shut, of Ferro's letters, of Bo's sling, of Tamsin's three hidden copies, of the snow falling somewhere north on a pass they'd crossed at the cost of a friend's freedom.

"Yeah," Sable said. "Ask me again tomorrow."

Behind them, at the courtroom door, Corliss paused. He didn't threaten. He didn't sneer. He simply looked at Sable the way a man looks at a chart when the river has shifted overnight—recalculating, redrawing, and finding, for the first time, that the safe channel had closed.

Then he was gone into the crowd, and Sable understood, with the cold certainty of a captain reading weather, that whatever he did next would come fast, and it would come tonight.
