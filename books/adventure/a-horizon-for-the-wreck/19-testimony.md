## 19. Testimony

**Sable**

The courtroom in Harrowgate had been a granary once, and it still smelled faintly of wheat and mouse droppings, as if justice here were something stored against winter and rationed out by the bushel. Sable stood at the back wall through the morning session, hood up, watching the proceedings the way they'd watched for sandbars for fifteen years—reading the surface, guessing at what lay beneath.

Juno sat at the defendant's table in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, her hands folded like a woman waiting for a ferry she suspected would never come. Magistrate Oyelaran presided from the bench with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. The Marchetti lawyers were three in number, gray-suited, gray-voiced, and they moved through the evidence of Juno's guilt with the smoothness of men reading from a script they'd written themselves.

Because they had. Sable knew it now the way you know a channel marker is lying—by the way everything else bent around it.

The plan had been Tamsin's: wait, watch, let the prosecution hang itself on its own thin rope. The depositions were published that morning—the *Coldharbour Courier* had run them across four pages under a headline Sable hadn't been able to finish reading. Ferro's copies, mailed before his capture, authenticated by Tamsin's careful annotations. The founding fraud of the Marchetti fortune, laid out in forty-year-old ink: the drowned testimony of the *Condesa del Mar*'s officers, sworn statements about falsified cargo manifests, bribed inspectors, a ship scuttled for insurance with a skeleton crew told to swim for it.

The town had read it over breakfast. The courtroom was full to the doors.

But the charges against Juno were not about the *Condesa*. They were about survey equipment, a broken lock, a night in March. Small charges. Safe charges. Charges designed to be tried quietly before anyone connected them to anything larger.

The prosecutor—a young man named Vess who kept glancing toward the Marchetti table like a helmsman checking his owner's hand on the wheel—was calling his final witness when the doors opened.

Sable didn't remember deciding to move. That was the thing about fifteen years of running: the body learned to make decisions before the mind caught up, and usually the decision was *away*. This time the body walked forward, down the center aisle, past every face turning like weathervanes, and stopped at the rail.

"I have testimony," Sable said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "For the defense."

Vess was on his feet. "Your Honor, this person isn't—"

"Captain Sable Villalobos." Sable set their riverboat license on the rail, the one they'd bought under a name that wasn't quite theirs, in a port that didn't ask questions. "Master of the vessel *Kestrel's Wake*. And I am also," they said, and here was the door, the one they'd been standing outside for fifteen years, "the person who has spent those years avoiding warrants issued under my given name. Which I will now provide, if the court will hear me."

The silence had weight. Oyelaran looked down at them for a long moment, and Sable watched him do the arithmetic—watched him understand that whatever he decided next, the room full of people holding newspapers was going to remember it.

"The court will hear the witness," Oyelaran said.

* * *

**Oyelaran**

Magistrate Oyelaran had sentenced a hundred people he believed innocent, and he could account for every one of them the way a bookkeeper accounts for losses: necessary write-offs against the cost of keeping his seat, his chambers, his daughter's schooling, his mother's care in the Coldharbour sanatorium. He had never enjoyed it. He had never once imagined he'd be given a chance to stop.

Then Villalobos walked up the aisle and offered their real name like a man laying a knife on the table, and Oyelaran felt the whole ledger tilt.

He knew what the Marchettis expected of this trial. A conviction by Thursday, quiet, procedural, done before the pass closed and the story died in the snow. He knew Corliss was somewhere in the gallery—he'd seen the man's gray gloves, heard the soft click of his watch cover—and he knew what a wrong ruling would cost him.

He also knew what the newspapers on the spectators' laps cost the Marchettis more.

"Proceed," he said, and heard his own voice sound older than it had yesterday.

Villalobos testified the way water moves—without hurry, around obstacles, wearing things down. The cave at Gullwrack. The wreck. The strongbox with the Marchetti seal. The manifest. The bill of sale whose signature predated the death of the man who supposedly signed it. Vess objected eleven times; Oyelaran sustained four and let the rest stand because refusing to hear them aloud would be its own kind of verdict, and everyone in the granary knew it.

Then Villalobos said the thing Oyelaran had been dreading since the witness sat down.

"On the fifteenth of October, I met Dane Corliss aboard my vessel. He offered me money for the strongbox and promised that Juno Reyes's sentence would be 'managed.' I refused. He then arranged the sabotage of my boat at the weir, the ambush at Kestrel Pass, and the capture of Old Ferro, who is at this hour either dead or in Marchetti custody. I say this on the record knowing it implicates me in nothing and him in everything, and knowing that Your Honor must weigh it however Your Honor must."

The old formula. *However Your Honor must.* It was a courtesy. It was also a hook, baited and dropped into the water directly beneath the bench.

Oyelaran looked at the Marchetti table. The senior counsel gave him a small nod—*dismiss it, and all is forgiven*—and Oyelaran felt fifteen years of write-offs rise in his throat like floodwater.

He thought of his mother, who had taught him the law from a primer so old it named crimes no one committed anymore. He thought of the girl at the defendant's table, who had looked at him exactly once all day, without hope, without hatred, just taking his measure the way sailors take the weather.

"The court finds the testimony credible," Oyelaran said. "The court further notes that the documents published this morning bear the registry seal of Coldharbour and are therefore matters of public record, which this court cannot un-see. The charges against the defendant Reyes are continued for thirty days pending investigation—" and here he paused, and did the last piece of arithmetic, the first honest sum in years, "—pending investigation by prosecutors who do not report to any party with interest in the outcome. Bail is granted. The defendant is released."

The granary erupted. Gavel, shouting, gavel again—but Oyelaran found he didn't much mind the noise. It sounded, for the first time in his career, like weather breaking.

**Tamsin**

Tamsin saw Corliss leave before anyone else did—not running, never running; Corliss simply stood, adjusted his coat, and moved toward the doors with the unhurried gait of a man catching a train he was confident was late.

She was already moving too. Bo was faster, slipping out the side door while the crowd surged toward Juno, and Tamsin went the other way, out onto the steps, where the cold hit like a slap and the light off the snow-dusted rooftops made her squint.

Corliss reached the bottom of the courthouse steps and stopped. Two constables were waiting there, and behind them, unmistakably, stood the editor of the *Courier*, arms folded, looking pleased with himself in the way of men about to sell a great many papers.

"Magistrate Oyelaran's warrant," Tamsin said, coming down the steps beside him, matching his pace. "Filed an hour ago. Abduction, conspiracy, tampering. There's a second warrant with your employers' names on it being drafted as we speak—I know because I helped draft the summary. Archivist's habit. We document everything."

Corliss looked at her, and for a moment she saw past the charm to the machinery underneath—cold, efficient, already calculating exits. Then the constables took his arms, gently, professionally, and the machinery stalled against the simple fact of hands stronger than his.

"You should know," Corliss said to her, conversational, almost kind, "that men like the Marchettis don't lose. They regroup."

"I know," Tamsin said. "I used to work for people like that. That's how I know the regrouping takes money, and their money is currently evidence." She smiled, and it felt strange on her face, like a key fitting a lock she'd thought rusted shut. "You should also know that Old Ferro is alive. He walked into the Coldharbour constabulary this morning on his own two feet. Whatever else you've cost us, you didn't get to write his ending."

Something crossed Corliss's face then that Tamsin would remember for years—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man discovering, at the very end, that the current had turned and he had never once learned to read water.

They took him away. The crowd on the steps cheered, and Tamsin stood in the noise feeling none of it land, thinking instead of a shepherd's hut above the treeline, and a confession traded for a confession, and how strange it was that the worst thing she had ever done had turned out to be the qualification that saved them all.

* * *

**Sable**

Juno came out of the courthouse into the snow-light and stopped when she saw Sable waiting at the rail, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Fifteen years, one broken promise, one mutiny, one cave, one trial—and all of it suddenly too large for the space between two people on courthouse steps.

"You gave them your name," Juno said finally.

"It seemed to be the going rate."

"They could still press the old warrants."

"They could." Sable looked out at the harbor, where the *Kestrel's Wake* rode at anchor, patched and scarred and floating. "I'm told there's a magistrate working on that. Apparently he owes somebody a favor he can't repay in money anymore."

Juno laughed—a short, cracked sound, half sob—and then she was gripping Sable's arm hard enough to bruise, forehead nearly touching shoulder, saying nothing, which between the two of them had always been the fullest sentence either knew how to speak.

Bo came bounding down the steps with Tamsin behind, and Ferro—Ferro, alive, thinner, walking with a stick but walking—was helped down by two constables who seemed unsure whether they were escorting him or honoring him. The five of them stood there in the falling quiet while Harrowgate emptied its courthouses and filled its taverns with the story.

It wasn't clean. Sable knew that. Somewhere the Marchettis were already burning paper and moving money, and Oyelaran's thirty days might stretch to thirty years, and the *Condesa*'s dead were still dead, and no verdict would warm the water where they lay. Justice hadn't arrived. It had stumbled in sideways, drunk on newspaper, carried by a coward's mailed copies and a magistrate's sudden conscience and a fugitive's willingness to stand up and be counted.

But Juno was free. Ferro was free. The record held their names, and the truth was public property now, too widely printed to burn.

That evening, at the dock, a courier found Sable with a letter bearing the Marchetti crest. Sable weighed it in one hand for a long moment, watching the snow begin—real snow, the season's first, settling on the deck of the boat that had carried them through everything.

Then they broke the seal, and read, and understood that the fight was not over—it had only changed shape.
