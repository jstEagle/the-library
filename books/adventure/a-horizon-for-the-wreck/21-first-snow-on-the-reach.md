## 21. First Snow on the Reach

The riverboat had a name now.

Juno stood on the Harrowgate dock in the grey half-light of morning and read it twice, because the first time she'd assumed her eyes were playing tricks—fifteen years of clerical work would do that to a person, make them distrust anything not stamped and countersigned. But there it was, painted fresh along the bow in letters the color of old brass: *Condesa's Daughter*.

"You're staring," Sable said from the gangplank, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat Juno had never seen before. New. Bought for someone who expected to be seen in it.

"I'm reading," Juno said. "There's a difference. One of us learned it working for the Marchetti survey office."

"Careful." Sable came down the plank to stand beside her. "You were acquitted. You don't get to be bitter anymore. It's in the record."

"It's in the record," Juno agreed, and felt the words land somewhere deeper than she'd expected. Acquitted. It had been eleven days since Magistrate Oyelaran—his voice cracking like river ice in front of a packed courtroom—had struck the charges and named the frame for what it was. Eleven days of people stopping her on the street to shake her hand. Eleven days of sleeping without counting the hours until the cell door opened. She still woke at dawn out of habit, heart hammering, before remembering that no one was coming to take anything from her anymore.

She looked at the boat again. Sable had bought her off a bankruptcy auction two weeks ago—a stubby, deep-bellied freighter with good bones and an engine Ferro had spent every waking hour coaxing back to life—and renamed her in front of witnesses, which was its own kind of testimony. The *Condesa* had no daughter. The *Condesa* had forty dead and a sealed manifest and a cave full of cold water. But she had this now: a boat carrying her name upriver, crewed by people who refused to let the dead stay convenient.

"When do we cast off?" Juno asked.

"When you sign." Sable produced a folded paper from inside the coat with the exaggerated care of someone handling something explosive. "First mate. Wages negotiable, quarters aft, one share of whatever cargo we carry that isn't contraband."

"And the other share?"

"Sunk costs," Sable said, and Juno laughed—the first real laugh since the arrest, and it hurt her ribs in a way she decided to keep.

* * *

Signing took place in the cabin, because Sable insisted everything happen properly now, in daylight, with paper. Tamsin sat at the little table with her spectacles down her nose, acting as witness, which was either a joke or a sacrament depending on how you looked at it. Bo hovered by the stove pretending not to watch, radiating the specific boredom of a teenager forced to attend something meaningful.

Juno read the article twice. Fifteen years as a dockside clerk had taught her that documents were where people hid their true intentions, so she read for the traps: no exclusivity clause, no penalty for leaving, no language about debts owed or silence purchased. Just terms. Plain ones.

"This says I can quit whenever I want," she said.

"Yes."

"That's terrible business practice."

"I've never once been accused of good business practice," Sable said. "It's why I'm alive."

Juno signed. Her hand shook slightly—not from fear, she realized, but from the strangeness of writing her own name on something she wanted. For fifteen years her signature had been a thing she gave away: manifests for other people's cargo, receipts for other people's profits, statements to magistrates. This was the first paper in longer than she could remember that existed entirely because she'd chosen to put her name on it.

Tamsin witnessed it with a flourish. Bo demanded to see the signature and pronounced it "crooked," and Ferro, coming down the companionway with grease to his elbows, said that crooked signatures lasted longer than straight ones, and then everyone was talking at once about engine hours and channel markers and whether the galley stove would light in wet weather, and Juno stood in the middle of it with ink drying on her fingers and thought: *oh.* This is what it is. This is what I broke my life open for, without knowing that's what I was doing.

* * *

They cast off at midmorning, and the snow began an hour later.

Juno was at the wheel—Sable had handed it over without ceremony an hour out of Harrowgate, saying only "you know the Reach better than your own handwriting"—when the first flakes came down. They arrived sideways off the western hills, sparse and deliberate, like scouts sent ahead of an army. Juno watched one land on the back of her hand and hold its shape for a moment before melting.

Behind her, on deck, Bo whooped. She heard the kid scrambling up onto the cargo hatch with the reckless ease that had gotten them all through the Kestrel Range portage, arms spread to catch snowflakes, yelling something about being able to taste the sky. Tamsin's voice followed, dry as ledger paper, warning about hypothermia and slippery decking, and Bo's answering laugh carried across the water.

Ferro came up to the pilothouse and stood beside Juno at the window, saying nothing for a long while. He did that. In the three weeks since his release—Corliss's men had abandoned him at a waystation when the news broke, deciding he wasn't worth guarding—he'd spoken maybe four hundred words total, most of them technical. But he watched the snow come down across the water with an expression Juno recognized from the trial, when the journalist's depositions had been read aloud: the look of a man watching weather he'd been told was coming for forty years finally arrive.

"The old crew used to say," Ferro said at last, "that the first snow on the Reach was the river closing its books."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe," Ferro said, "that some years the river forgives, and some years it collects, and you can't tell which until spring." He turned toward the stern, where the wake unspooled behind them, white water folding into grey. "This year feels like forgiving. But I'm not a reliable judge. I called it wrong once before."

He went below before Juno could answer. She didn't try to stop him. Some accounts, she understood, took more than one winter to close.

* * *

At noon they passed Gullwrack Point.

Juno hadn't asked to see the cave. She'd spent enough nights in a cell imagining it—the black mouth of it, the ribcage of the ship inside, the strongbox that had cost her freedom and nearly cost Ferro his life. But the channel ran close under the point, closer than the charts allowed, because Sable knew a cut through the shoals that no surveyor had ever bothered to map, and so the cave simply presented itself: a low dark seam in the cliff face, already drinking the tide back in, the storm surge of the past weeks having collapsed part of its mouth into rubble.

Sable slowed the boat as they passed. Didn't stop. Just eased the throttle back until they were barely making way, and came and stood beside Juno at the window, and together they watched the water slide into the dark.

"They sealed it," Sable said. "Registry order. Memorial covenant. No salvage, no entry, in perpetuity." A pause. "Tamsin wrote the covenant herself. Made sure it would hold against any future Marchetti lawyer, any magistrate, any government. She said it was the best document she ever filed."

"She buried evidence for money once," Juno said. "Now she buries evidence for the dead."

"Now she files it where everyone can find it. Same skill. Better use."

The current took them past the point. Juno kept her eyes on the cave until the cliff turned and it was gone, and she found that what she felt wasn't dread or grief exactly, though both were in there somewhere. It was more like the feeling of setting down a load she'd forgotten she was carrying. Forty people had died in that cave, and their names were carved now on a stone marker above the tideline where any traveler could read them, and the fraud that had killed them was public record, and the family that built itself on the lie was finished—broken up by scandal, its assets frozen, Aldous Marchetti himself summoned before a commission that Oyelaran, testifying with the desperate thoroughness of a man buying back his soul, had helped convene.

None of it brought the forty back. But the water over them belonged to everyone now, and no one, and that was the only monument Juno knew how to believe in.

* * *

The strongbox had gone to the Coldharbour archive the week after the trial.

Juno had gone along for the delivery—had insisted on it, actually, over Sable's mild objection that she should rest. They'd taken the morning coach through country already whitening, and Juno had carried the box herself up the archive steps, feeling its weight shift in her arms, heavier than brass and paper had any right to be. Inside it: the original manifest of the *Condesa del Mar*, the forged bill of sale, and the depositions—forty pages of sworn testimony from passengers and crew who had known, in 1861, exactly what the Marchetti founding fortune was built on, and had trusted the sea to keep their secret better than the courts would have.

The archivist, a small ferocious woman named Pell who had known Tamsin before the disgrace and greeted her return to honest work with a single raised eyebrow, catalogued the contents in silence. Then she closed the box, set it on a shelf between a land grant and a shipping ledger, and said:

"Open access. Any citizen may request to view it."

"That's the point," Tamsin said.

"Mm." Pell had looked at Juno then—at the bruise still fading on her wrist from the irons, at the way she stood too straight, the way people stand when they've been broken and reassembled and are still learning the new seams. "And you? What will you do now?"

Juno had opened her mouth to say *go back to clerking*, which was the answer fifteen years of habit would have given. What came out instead was: "I've been offered a position. First mate."

Pell nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something. "Good. Paper keeps the truth. But paper never goes out to meet it." She'd shaken each of their hands in turn, and that had been the whole ceremony: a box on a shelf, a door left unlocked, the past signed and closed.

Riding back down through the pass, watching the snow line climb down the mountainside toward them, Juno had slept against the coach window for the first time in months without dreaming of cells.

* * *

Evening found them moored at a private anchorage ten miles upriver, a shelf of calm water behind a gravel bar where Sable had evidently anchored a hundred times before and never once told anyone about. The snow had thickened after dark, falling straight and silent now, windless, accumulating on the deck in a soft inch of white that muffled everything. Ferro ran the anchor light. Tamsin cooked—badly, with great confidence—and Bo ate three servings and declared the meal "structurally unsound but delicious."

Afterward they gathered in the cabin, because it was warm there and because none of them, Juno suspected, had quite learned yet how to be alone again. Sable dealt cards. Bo cheated. Tamsin caught Bo cheating and levied a fine of dishwashing duties that Bo appealed and lost. Ferro won three hands in a row through the impenetrable strategy of having played cards on a riverboat forty years ago and remembering nothing and forgetting nothing.

Juno sat with her back to the stove and watched them, and let herself feel it all at once, the way she'd been refusing to for weeks: the grief and the gratitude in the same breath, inseparable, like heat and light from a single flame. She grieved for the fifteen years—the mutiny she'd fled and the promise Sable had broken and the long silence between them, the small gray life in Harrowgate measuring other people's cargo, the cell, the fear. She grieved for Ferro's forty years of shame and Tamsin's ruined career and Bo's childhood, wherever that had gone. She grieved for forty strangers in a flooded cave whose names she'd memorized from the marker because it seemed like the least she could do.

And she was grateful for all of it anyway—or not *for* it, precisely, but grateful that it had ended here, on this boat, with these people, in this exact room where a crooked signature on a plain piece of paper had made her free. Gratitude, she was learning, didn't require the suffering to have been worth it. It only required that you notice what you had now.

"Your deal," Sable said, sliding the cards across to her.

Juno gathered them up. Outside, the snow fell on the river and did not melt, and the season turned its long page, and somewhere south of them the sea waited, patient as an archive, holding everything and giving back only what the tide chose to return.

"Deal me in," Bo said.

"You're already in," Juno said, and shuffled, and dealt.
