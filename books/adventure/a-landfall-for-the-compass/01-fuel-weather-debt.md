## 1. Fuel, Weather, Debt

The rain came in off the river at dawn the way it always did, like something owed and collected without argument.

Wren Nakamura stood under the corrugated awning of the Puerto Cielo airstrip shed with a mug of black coffee going cold in her hand and watched the water hammer the red clay into submission. Six years she'd been waking to this sound. Long enough that she no longer heard it as rain but as weather—a thing with moods, a thing that could be read, a thing that could be bargained with if you knew its language. The river country spoke a dozen dialects of water: the fine gray drizzle that never lifted, the afternoon thunderheads that stacked up over the divide like furniture being moved by giants, the horizontal sheets that came ahead of a front and turned the whole world the color of gunmetal. This morning's was the polite kind. A courtesy. It would be gone by eight.

She drank the coffee anyway, cold, because hot coffee was a luxury you saved for days when the sky wasn't trying to kill you, and this was not one of those days. This was a medicine run day. Forty minutes upriver to San Esteban de las Nubes, a strip so short the villagers had cut it twice into the same hillside before they got it right, a crate of antibiotics and insulin and one sealed box of vaccine that had to stay between four and eight degrees Celsius or it was worth exactly nothing. Routine. She could fly it asleep. Some mornings she suspected she did.

Her airplane sat at the end of the apron where she'd chained it two nights ago, nose into the wind, looking like what it was: a De Havilland Canada Beaver that had been old when her father was young and had been loved badly by at least three owners before her. She'd bought it—no. Not bought. That was the wrong verb for what she'd done, which was more like adopted it out of a bankruptcy auction in a river town whose name she still avoided saying, paying in cash she'd earned flying cargo nobody asked questions about, and had spent the years since keeping it alive with baling wire, prayer, and parts cannibalized from two sister airframes slowly dissolving into the jungle behind the hangar. The paint had once been company blue. Now it was mostly bare aluminum with patches of primer the color of old bone, and on the tail, where a registration number should have been crisp and legal, the letters were hand-painted and slightly crooked because Wren had done them herself with a brush and a steady hand and a deep, professional contempt for paperwork.

She crossed the apron through the last of the rain and ran her palm along the cowling the way other people touched the shoulders of people they loved. Warm work, checking an aircraft. Honest work. You opened the oil cap and looked, you drained the fuel strainer and watched for water, you kicked the tires—not superstition, actually, though everyone called it that; you kicked them because a flat spot or a soft casing told you things a glance wouldn't—and you pulled the prop through by hand and listened to the engine complain in its sleep. The Beaver's Continental grumbled at her, six cylinders full of grievances, and she patted the spinner and said, "I know. I know."

Talking to airplanes was normal here. Talking to anything else was optional.

Puerto Cielo was not a town so much as an argument between the river and the forest, with a gravel strip laid down the middle of it by some mining concern forty years dead. There was the mission on the hill with its tin roof and its shortwave mast. There were the cantina and the fuel depot and the row of stilt houses leaning along the waterfront like tired men against a bar. And there was the company office—the *Compañía*, everyone said, in a tone that made the capital letter audible—two stories of poured concrete with a satellite dish on the roof and a flagpole out front flying nothing, because the company flag had been stolen twice and nobody had bothered a third time. The Compañía held the timber concessions, the mineral rights, the fuel contract, the radio licenses, and, in every way that mattered, the law. The nearest magistrate was a nine-hour boat ride downstream and came twice a year, in the dry season, when the mosquitoes permitted.

Wren had learned all this in her first month and had then spent the next five years learning how to be unremarkable inside it. It was a discipline, smallness. She practiced it the way a musician practices scales. She flew the routes nobody wanted, at hours nobody kept, for fees nobody haggled over. She paid cash. She drank one beer at the cantina, never two, and left before the card games got loud. When settlers on the middle river needed a coffin flown down or a bride flown up, she did it and didn't put it on any manifest. Her name was Wren Nakamura and it was true and it was also a wall she'd built brick by brick around whatever had come before, and she had gotten so good at living behind it that most mornings she could almost forget there had ever been another name, another life, another woman who had—

The thought stopped where it always stopped, at the edge of the pit, and she stepped back from it the way you step back from a well in the dark.

*Small,* she reminded herself, zipping her flight jacket to the chin. *Stay small. Small keeps you breathing.*

* * *

The ledger lived in a green canvas satchel behind the counter of the fuel depot, and Reyes kept it the way medieval kings kept relics: visible, untouchable, occasionally displayed to remind the faithful of their station.

Emiliano Reyes was fifty-some years old and built like a filing cabinet, with a face so smooth and patient it seemed less grown than manufactured. He wore guayaberas in colors the jungle had never produced—pale pink, mint, a yellow like old butter—and he spoke to everyone in the same unhurried voice, whether he was selling them kerosene or ruining their lives, which meant that in Puerto Cielo his voice preceded him the way thunder precedes lightning, and people flinched at the sound of it on the radio before they even knew what he wanted. He was not cruel. Cruelty required heat, and Reyes had none. He was arithmetic. He was the sum of everything anyone within two hundred miles owed the Compañía, carried forward monthly, compounded quietly, and he genuinely, sincerely believed he was the fairest man on the river, because he never charged interest he hadn't announced first.

"Señora Nakamura." He was already at the depot when she came in off the apron, sitting behind the counter with the ledger open and a glass of tamarind water sweating beside his elbow. He never arrived early. He simply was always there, as though the building secreted him overnight. "You'll want the good drum today. The pump on number two is putting water in the fuel again."

"I'll strain it myself," Wren said. "Give me the good drum."

"Of course." He didn't move. That was the ritual. The pause was the point. "San Esteban?"

"Medicine run. Vaccines."

"Mm. The mission's crate." He turned a page in the ledger with one finger, though she was certain he hadn't read anything on it. "You know, I've been thinking about your account."

Her stomach did the thing it always did, a slow cold roll, like ballast shifting. "It's current."

"It's current," he agreed pleasantly. "Current is not the same as settled." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were brown and mild and bottomless, wells with no rope. "Fourteen hundred and sixty dollars, señorita. Plus the engine mount I advanced you in March. Plus the alternator. You are, forgive me, a woman of considerable obligations."

"I pay every month."

"You do. Reliably. Like rain." He smiled, and it reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not complaining. I'm observing. A man in my position observes." He closed the ledger—not slammed, never slammed, just closed it, softly, the way a door closes on a room you're not supposed to enter yet—and folded his hands on top of it. "Which brings me to a matter unrelated. Purely professional curiosity."

Here it comes, Wren thought. She kept her face doing nothing at all. She'd gotten very good at that too; it was the second discipline, after smallness. "What matter?"

"The Halvorsen survey party. Up past the falls, on the blackwater branch." He said it lightly, conversationally, a man discussing the price of diesel. "Contracted through us. Geological assessment, boundary verification, the usual. They've missed their check-in window. Three windows, in fact. One hundred twenty-six point seven megahertz, scheduled at—" he consulted a card he clearly didn't need to consult, "—oh-eight-hundred, fourteen-hundred, and twenty-hundred, daily. Nothing since Tuesday."

"Radios die up there," Wren said. "Batteries, moisture, antennas in the trees. Half the expeditions on this river go quiet for a week and turn up fat and sunburned."

"Half," Reyes agreed. "And the other half." He let it sit. Outside, the rain had stopped and the light was turning the particular hard silver that meant the morning would build heat by noon, and somewhere down the waterfront a generator coughed itself awake. "The Compañía would prefer not to send a formal search. Formal searches involve officials, and officials involve reports, and reports involve questions about our operations upstream that are frankly nobody's business. What we would prefer is a discreet flight. One aircraft. One pilot. Land at their camp, look around, report back. If they've broken a radio, bring them a new one. If they've gone home early, note it. If—" the mildest possible hesitation, "—if anything has happened, you assess and return. No heroics. You are a supply pilot, señorita, not a rescue service."

"And my part in this hypothetical errand?"

"Two hundred dollars off your balance. Per day, up to five days." He named the figure the way a fisherman names the weight of a lure, knowing exactly what it would do to the water. Two hundred a day. Ten days' flying income, erased, just like that, for perhaps three days' work. It would take her—she did the math without wanting to, hated herself for doing it—nearly half a year of medicine runs and mail hops and coffin flights to close a gap that size. The debt was a stone in her pack and she had carried it so long she'd forgotten what her shoulders felt like without it.

"And if I say no?"

Reyes spread his hands, wounded, reasonable. "Then nothing. Absolutely nothing. You say no, I thank you for your time, and I ask someone else. Perhaps the charter man from Bella Vista. Perhaps I wait for the magistrate's next visit and we make it formal after all, with officials and reports and questions." He picked up his tamarind water and drank, unhurried. "No is a perfectly respectable answer, Señora Nakamura. I only mention the account because I know you keep your books honestly. A woman who pays what she owes—I respect that enormously. It's rare."

There it was. Not a threat. Reyes didn't make threats; he made observations, and let you threaten yourself. She could feel the hook settling in, barb by barb, dressed up as courtesy: her debt, her honesty, her pride in her own reliability, all of it braided into a rope and offered to her hand like a gift. Say yes and she bought back a piece of herself. Say no and the stone stayed on her back, and maybe grew heavier in ways she couldn't predict, because men like Reyes remembered refusals the way the ledger remembered balances.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the debt, older than the fear: *upriver.* Past the falls, on the blackwater branch. The direction she had sworn, six years ago, never to fly again except when paid to, and even then only with her eyes on the horizon and her mind shut like a hatch.

"Three days," she said. "Two hundred a day, credited against principal, not interest. In writing, in the ledger, initialed."

Something moved behind Reyes's mild eyes—satisfaction, probably, though with him it was impossible to swear to—and he opened the ledger again and wrote in it with a fountain pen, in a small precise hand, and turned it toward her, and she signed, and the ink was barely dry before she was already telling herself it was just a flight. Just a look-see. Land, count heads, take off. In and out before the weather built.

"Sensible," Reyes said warmly, capping the pen. "I knew you would be. The camp coordinates are on the flight board. Their last position fix was—" he glanced at the card again, "—upstream of the confluence. You'll want to be on the ground before the afternoon cells come over the divide. This time of year they build fast up there."

"I know how the weather works, Mr. Reyes."

"I'm sure you do." He gathered his satchel, tucked the ledger into it with the tenderness of a man handling scripture, and paused at the door of the depot, silhouetted against the steaming bright morning. "One more thing, and then I'll leave you to your vaccines." His voice stayed pleasant, stayed mild, stayed absolutely level. "If you happen to find papers up there—survey notes, maps, anything of that nature—they're Compañía property. Contractual. Bring them back with the people. It's in everyone's interest that nothing gets... lost."

He tipped an imaginary hat and was gone, and Wren stood alone in the fuel-smelling dimness with her heart going harder than it should have over a routine request, telling herself the cold feeling in her gut was just the coffee.

* * *

By ten o'clock the Beaver was loaded, fueled from the good drum, strained twice through chamois into a clear jar until she could see no water line in the gasoline, and ready. The vaccine box rode wedged in a foam cooler behind the copilot's seat, a thermometer taped inside the lid. Tam Okafor's replacement battery for the San Esteban radio sat in the cargo bay wrapped in sacking—no. Not Tam. She didn't know a Tam. The battery belonged to the mission, that was all, a favor for Sister Anselma, who repaid favors in bread and silence, both of which Wren valued.

She walked the plane one last time. Control surfaces free and true. Fuel caps secure. Pitot clear. Tires—she kicked them, because you kick them. The river lay beyond the strip, wide and brown and muscular, carrying the whole watershed toward the sea without hurry, without mercy, without memory. Somewhere up that river, past the falls, on the blackwater branch, six people had stopped answering their radio, and the man who owned her debt wanted her to find out why, and wanted whatever they'd written down brought home in their place.

She climbed in, ran the checklist by touch, and started the engine. The Continental caught on the third blade, caught fire in the metaphorical sense, settled into its ragged idle, and she taxied out with the morning sun full in her eyes and the runway long and empty ahead.

At the run-up area she stopped, ran the magneto checks, watched the gauges behave, and sat for a moment with her hands light on the wheel, listening to the airplane breathe.

*Just a look-see,* she told herself. *Land, count heads, leave.*

She lined up, pushed the throttle forward, and let the old Beaver gather itself off the clay and into the silver air, turning north up the river, toward the falls, while behind her Puerto Cielo shrank to a smudge of tin roofs and the ledger stayed open on Reyes's desk like a mouth.

The weather over the divide was already building.
