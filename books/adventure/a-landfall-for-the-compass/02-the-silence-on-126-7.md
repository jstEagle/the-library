## 2. The Silence on 126.7

The radio at Puerto Cielo was a scarred old Hallicrafters bolted to a shelf in Reyes's office, and it had been silent for three days where it should have spoken.

Wren learned this the way she learned most things in Puerto Cielo—sideways, from someone who wanted something. In her case it was Dominga, who ran the cantina and traded gossip like currency, pressing a cold bottle of ginger beer into Wren's hand before Wren's boots had fully left the mud of the airstrip.

"Your friend the company man has been up all night," Dominga said, fanning herself with a folded newspaper that hadn't been current since the last government fell. "Three nights. Talking into that radio like a priest saying rosary. *Halvorsen party, Halvorsen party, come in.* Nothing comes back but the river hiss." She leaned closer, lowering her voice as though the humidity itself might carry it downriver. "They missed their check-in Sunday. And Monday. And today."

Wren turned the bottle in her hands, condensation running over her knuckles. "Not my expedition."

"No," Dominga agreed pleasantly. "But everything on this river is somebody's problem eventually."

That was the whole philosophy of Puerto Cielo in one sentence, and Wren had spent six years trying to prove it wrong by being no one's anything.

She finished the ginger beer on the walk back to the strip, where the *Kestrel* sat tied down against the afternoon wind—a 1942 De Havilland Canada bush plane with a fabric-and-fuselage history longer than most marriages, doped linen patched so many times the tail looked like a quilt. She'd named her the *Kestrel* because kestrels hover. They hold still in moving air. It seemed like a thing worth aspiring to.

She spent two hours doing what she always did when the world got quiet enough to hear itself: maintenance she could have put off. Draining water from the fuel strainer. Checking the tension on the landing gear bungees. Running her thumb along the propeller's leading edge where a stone chip had been dressed and dressed again until the aluminum there was thinner than an apology. The work steadied her hands. Her hands needed steadying more than the plane did.

At four o'clock, when the light went amber and the river mist began its slow climb out of the valley, Reyes appeared at the edge of the strip.

He always walked like a man arriving rather than approaching—shoulders square, hat brim low, as if the entire country were a room he'd been shown into and found adequate. He wore the same gray guayabera he wore every day, spotless in a way that defied the mud, and he stopped a polite six feet away, which was how you knew he considered himself a civilized man.

"Señora Nakamura."

"Wren is fine."

"Señora Nakamura," he repeated, because using her name was a small exercise of power and Reyes never let a small exercise go unperformed. "You look well. Flying agrees with you."

"Flying pays me. Agreement's optional." She set down the cowling panel and wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being white sometime in the previous decade. "You've got that look, Reyes. The one where you're about to offer me something I don't want."

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You never do. That's the trick of it."

Something moved behind his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if he kept one on the premises. He took off his hat, which he did only for business, and held it against his chest like a man at a funeral. Maybe it was one.

"The Halvorsen survey party," he said. "Nine souls. Contracted through Meridian Land and Resource to complete boundary verification along the upper Río Blanco watershed. They've missed three scheduled check-ins on frequency 126.7. Three." He let the number sit in the air between them like a stone dropped in still water. "Company protocol requires a forty-eight-hour silence before escalation. We are past protocol now."

"So call the military. They've got helicopters."

"The military," Reyes said delicately, "has a way of turning a missing survey into a missing survey *and* a provincial incident, with journalists, and inquiries, and men in clean uniforms asking questions about land claims nobody wants asked yet." He put the hat back on. "What the situation requires is discretion. A single aircraft. A single pilot. Someone who can be up there tomorrow morning, back within the week, and whose report consists of what she saw and nothing else."

"And if what I saw is nothing? Nine people just decided to stop answering their radio?"

"Then you file a report saying so, and Meridian sends a proper search, and everyone looks diligent and nothing embarrassing happens." He paused. "And your account with us is adjusted."

There it was. Wren felt it settle over her the way the evening mist settled over the strip—cool, soft, and impossible to see through.

Her account with Meridian. Six years old now, and compounding. She'd stopped calculating the balance years ago because the arithmetic was obscene. A fuel advance here, a parts order there, an engine overhaul after the season the alternator died over the Serranía—all of it signed for, all of it carried forward, all of it owned by a company that had never once mentioned a due date, because a debt without a due date isn't a loan. It's a leash.

"How much?" she asked, and hated herself for the speed of it.

Reyes produced a folded paper from inside the guayabera, unhurried, as though he'd written it days ago. Perhaps he had. Perhaps the moment the radio went quiet, some clerk in a city office had already penciled her name into the solution column. "Forty percent," he said. "Forgiven. Cleared entirely upon your return with a full report."

"Forty." She laughed, short and ugly. "Sixty percent of a leash is still a leash, Reyes."

"A hundred percent of nothing is also nothing," he said mildly. "I'm told the alternative to flying for us, these days, is not flying at all. Fuel allocations being what they are." He didn't say *we control the fuel*, because he didn't have to; he'd arranged the world so that the sentence was unnecessary. "Consider it, Señora Nakamura. Consider it tonight. But consider also that every day the party stays silent is a day something worse than silence may be happening to them, and you are the only pilot on this river who knows the upper watershed well enough to find a camp that doesn't want finding."

He tipped his hat then, actually tipped it, an antique gesture he must have learned from a film, and walked back toward the office with his spine straight and his shadow long across the grass.

Wren stood by the *Kestrel* a long time after he'd gone, watching the mist eat the far hills.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep, so she did what she did instead of sleeping: she read the river.

Her room above the cantina had one window, and from it she could see the black line of the airstrip and, beyond it, the dark mass of the country going north—the Río Blanco country, the wet green labyrinth where the maps thinned out and the law became a rumor people told each other to feel brave. Somewhere up there, nine people had gone quiet. A geologist named Halvorsen whom she'd met exactly once, at a fuel stop, a big sunburned man who'd shaken her hand like he meant it and called her *pilot* instead of *señora*, which she'd appreciated more than she'd admitted. His daughter was on the expedition too—Annelise, twenty-something, fresh from a university somewhere cold. And a security chief named Mercer, according to the manifest Reyes had once made her sign as a witness, though why a supply run needed a security chief was a question she'd been smart enough not to ask out loud.

And a cartographer. Ilsa Voss. Wren had loaded her trunk onto the plane herself, back in March. It had weighed almost nothing. Just clothes and instruments and a wooden case of drawing pens, and the woman had carried a leather roll of maps under her arm like a mother carrying an infant, refusing to let it be stowed.

*Documents,* Wren thought now, staring at the ceiling fan turning its slow, useless circles. *Maps. Paper.*

People went missing on the Río Blanco for ordinary reasons. Engines failed. Boats capsized. Fevers burned through camps faster than any medicine run could outrun them. The river ate people the way it ate banks—patiently, continuously, without malice. That was the merciful explanation, and she made herself hold it, turn it over, examine it from every side like a coin she hoped was counterfeit.

But three check-ins. On a schedule. With a functioning transmitter—she knew, because she'd tested it for them herself in March, keying the mic while Tam, the kid, the sixteen-year-old radio tech with the sunburned neck and the father's compass hanging off his belt loop, had listened on the other end and given her a thumbs-up through the window of the comms tent.

Tam. She'd given him half her chocolate ration at the fuel stop because he'd reminded her of no one, which was the safest thing a person could remind her of.

She rolled over and pressed her face into the pillow that smelled of mildew and kerosene.

Here was the trap, laid out plain, and the worst part was that it wasn't even hidden. Reyes hadn't disguised it because he didn't think she deserved the courtesy of disguise—or because he knew disguise was unnecessary. Fly alone, off the books, into country where the company's fingerprints were all over the map. Come back with a report, meaning come back with whatever story Meridian needed told. Forty percent forgiven for obedience. And if she found something up there that the company didn't want found—

Well. People went missing on the Río Blanco all the time.

She lay in the dark and ran the alternatives the way she'd run them a thousand nights before, the same tired circuit. She could refuse. Then the fuel allocation dried up, and the medicine runs dried up, and the mission at San Rafael lost its insulin deliveries, and the settlements upriver lost their mail and their vaccines and the thin thread of connection that kept nine hundred scattered lives stitched to the world. She could fly for someone else—but there was no one else; Meridian owned the fuel, the strip lease, the customs concessions, the weather reports, such as they were. She could run. She'd run once already, six years ago, all the way from a name to a callsign, and it had bought her this: a room over a cantina, a plane held together with prayer and dope-and-fabric, and a ledger that grew in the dark like a root crop.

Debt always wins. That was the first law of the river country, older than any boundary line. You could argue with it, resent it, fly circles around it, but sooner or later every compass needle swung back to it and pointed home.

Except.

Except that nine people were quiet, and one of them was a boy who liked chocolate, and one of them was a woman who carried maps like infants, and Wren had spent six years perfecting the art of not knowing things, and she could feel, tonight, that particular art failing her—cracking along its seams like old dope on fabric, letting the damp truth seep through.

If she flew up there and found graves, she could report graves. That was clean. Graves required nothing of her but grief.

But if she flew up there and found paper—if she found whatever it was that had made a company man lose sleep for three nights over a radio—then she would be standing in the exact place where her whole architecture of smallness either held or didn't. And she suspected, with the flat certainty of a woman who had bet wrong before, that it wouldn't hold. That Reyes hadn't sent her upriver to find the Halvorsen party at all. He'd sent her upriver to find out what they'd found, and to bring it back, or to leave it—and maybe herself—where the river keeps things.

The fan turned. The mist pressed against the window. Down at the strip, the *Kestrel*'s tie-down ropes creaked in the night wind, patient as a dog waiting at a door.

Wren got up before dawn, which surprised no one, least of all herself.

* * *

Reyes was already in his office when she came in at first light, sitting behind his desk with two cups of coffee poured, which told her he'd known her answer before she had. The office smelled of paper and insecticide. On the wall behind him hung the framed map of the concession—Meridian's map, the official one, the boundaries drawn in confident red ink—and Wren looked at it a beat too long, the way you look at a face you're sure you'll remember.

"Señora Nakamura."

"I'll fly it," she said. "Alone, off the books, per your terms. Upper Río Blanco, the Halvorsen camp coordinates, visual search of the watershed, report on my return." She held out her hand for the paper. "But I want it in writing that the forty percent is forgiveness, not deferral. No new interest accruing while I'm gone. And I want a fuel allocation for the round trip plus reserve, drawn tonight, before I take off. Not promised. Drawn."

For a moment something flickered in Reyes's eyes—she'd have called it respect if she thought him capable of it, or annoyance if she thought herself lucky. Probably it was just accounting.

"Naturally," he said, and wrote it out in his neat clerk's hand while she drank coffee that tasted of chicory and rust. He signed, stamped it, and slid both copies across the desk. "One condition of mine, since we're negotiating. You fly the direct route. You do not deviate to the west of the Blanco main channel." He said it lightly, a travel tip, nothing more. "The weather's been foul in the western valleys. Storm cells. Not worth your life."

The western valleys. Where the old survey vault was, if the rumors that reached even Puerto Cielo's cantina had any sediment of truth in them. Where the original boundary surveys had been filed, decades ago, by a government that no longer existed, in a concrete structure built to outlast governments.

"Weather's weather," Wren said. "I go where the search takes me."

"Of course." Reyes smiled his small, dry smile and stood, and offered his hand, and she shook it because refusing would tell him something true. "Bring our people home, Señora Nakamura. Or bring me certainty. Either will do."

Outside, the dawn was coming up the color of brass, and the mist was burning off the strip in long rags, and the *Kestrel* stood ready in the wet grass with her tanks full and her engine cowl still beaded with the night's condensation. Wren walked out to her slowly, papers folded against her chest like a letter she already regretted opening, and ran through the preflight with hands that knew every bolt by touch.

She climbed in. Pumped the throttle twice, cleared her, hit the starter, and the radial caught on the third blade with its familiar coughing roar, shaking the dew off the grass in a silver ring. The compass on the dash swung lazily, found north, and settled.

North. Upstream. Into the silence on 126.7.

She keyed the radio once out of habit—"Puerto Cielo traffic, De Havilland Kestrel, departing runway one-seven"—and heard nothing back but the river hiss, and lifted off into the brightening air with sixty percent of her life still owed and a hundred percent of her instincts screaming that she had just flown into the open end of a net.

Behind her, small and gray against the morning, Reyes stood at the edge of the strip and watched her go.
