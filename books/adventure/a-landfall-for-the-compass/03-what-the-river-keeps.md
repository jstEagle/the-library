## 3. What the River Keeps

The storm cells began at the Serrado bend, exactly where they always did, and Wren threaded the Otter between them the way a woman threads a needle she has threaded ten thousand times—without looking directly at it, without thinking about what her hands knew.

Rain came sideways across the windscreen in gray sheets. The river below had gone the color of gunmetal, swollen and muscular, braiding itself around sandbars that would not exist by August. She flew at nine hundred feet because eight hundred was inside the cloud deck and a thousand put her in the shear layer where the air turned against you like a bad debt.

*Debt.* Her hands tightened on the yoke before she could stop them.

She had been flying for forty minutes since Puerto Cielo, and for forty minutes she had managed not to think about Reyes's smile when he'd slid the amended ledger across his desk—the one where her number shrank by half if she brought back word of the Halvorsen party, by three-quarters if she brought back the party itself. *Off the books,* he'd said, as if the books were a country and she could simply step over its border. As if she hadn't spent six years learning that there was no border to step over. There was only the next favor, and the one after that, and the slow arithmetic of a life measured in what she owed.

The Otter bucked. She corrected without thought, trim tab, rudder pressure, a quarter-turn of the wheel, and the old girl settled again into the wet air like a dog shaking off water.

That was the thing nobody understood about flying in this country: it wasn't courage. Courage was for people who had choices. This was muscle memory laid down over fear until the fear became furniture—you stopped seeing it, but you still had to walk around it every day of your life.

Lightning flickered off her left wing, silent at this distance, a white vein pulsing inside a cloud the size of a cathedral. She banked right, gave it room. Somewhere under that cloud was a village called San Esteban where she'd once sat out three days of weather on a dirt floor, eating plantains with a woman who'd lost two sons to the fever and asked Wren nothing about herself, which was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for her.

The radio crackled static. 126.7 stayed dead. Three missed check-ins from Halvorsen's expedition, and now the silence had weight, had texture, like the humidity pressing against the fuselage.

*They're fine,* she told herself. *Radios die upriver. Batteries sour, antennas snap in the wind, people get lazy or sick or stubborn.* All true. All reasonable. And none of it explained why her stomach had been clenched since takeoff like a fist around a stone.

Because she knew this stretch of river. That was the real problem. Not the geography—geography was just geography—but the fact that six years ago she had flown this same corridor north with a hold full of crates she had never opened and a name she would never use again.

* * *

Her name then had been different. That was the whole point of names; they were costumes, and hers had fit too well for too long.

Six years ago she had been twenty-six years old and certain of exactly three things: that she was the best bush pilot on this coast, that the company paid late but always paid, and that questions were a luxury for people whose ledgers were clean. She'd been running cargo for Corriente Holdings even then—the same company, the same man Reyes, though he'd been younger and his smile had been less practiced—and the work had been simple. Fly north. Land where marked. Don't open the crates. Don't ask.

The night it happened there had been no storm. That was the detail that still caught in her throat sometimes, the unfairness of it. A clear evening, the river running gold with sunset, and her Otter heavy in the air with crates stenciled MACHINERY PARTS that clinked wrong when she taxied—too light, too loose, shifting with a sound like bottles.

She had opened one. Just one. At the strip above the confluence, alone, with a pry bar she kept for changing tires.

Inside: not machinery. Antiquities. Ceramics wrapped in newspaper, gold work dull with river mud, and beneath all of it, sealed in plastic, documents—old ones, brittle, stamped with survey marks and dates going back decades. She hadn't understood them then. She'd understood enough. You didn't ship pottery as machinery parts unless someone was erasing where it came from, and you didn't erase where things came from unless someone had taken them badly.

And she had closed the crate. That was the part that lived in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had looked at it, and she had closed the lid, and she had nailed it shut, because the payment was due Friday and her own debts—smaller then, ordinary, the debts of a young woman who liked cards and fast planes—were pressing on her.

She flew them north anyway. Delivered them to a man named Ostrander at a camp that appeared on no map she owned. Took her money. Told herself it wasn't her business what moved through the sky so long as her hands stayed clean.

Three weeks later, the village at the confluence burned. The one whose land those ceramics had come out of. Officially: an accident, a cooking fire, dry season. Unofficially—unofficially there were men in town who drank too much and talked, and what they talked about was a company that wanted a valley empty and got it empty, and a pilot who'd carried something north the same week.

Wren never learned if anyone connected her name to it. That was the torture of it—not knowing whether she was hunted or merely haunted. But she had read the magistrate's notice posted in Puerto Cielo, *witnesses sought, information regarding transport of cultural materials*, and she had understood that the law in this country was a rumor except when it wanted something, and what it wanted was a body to hang the story on. Hers would do. Bodies always did.

So she had gone to Reyes—not because she trusted him, God, no, but because he was the only door in the room—and made a bargain that she told herself was temporary. He paid her fines quietly, buried the inquiry in paperwork, bought the silence of two officials. In exchange: exclusivity. Her plane, her hours, her discretion, indefinitely. A leash made of gratitude, which is the strongest kind, because the animal holds it in its own teeth.

And she had changed her name the way you change a tire on the roadside—quickly, efficiently, with grease to the elbow. Nakamura, her mother's name, unused since her mother died, dug up and worn like armor. Wren, because wrens were small and brown and nobody looked twice at them.

Six years. Two hundred thousand miles of river sky. And the whole time, the stone in her chest, growing.

* * *

The present reached for her as a downdraft slammed the Otter two hundred feet toward the treetops. Wren hauled back on the yoke, full power, the engines howling, and clawed back into cleaner air with her heart going like a rivet gun.

"Easy," she said aloud, to the plane, to herself. "Easy, old girl."

Ahead, the storm line broke around a gap—a corridor of bruised light maybe four miles wide, sliding slowly east. She could go around and lose twenty minutes, or thread it and arrive before the light failed. She checked her fuel. She checked her watch. She thought about Tam—no, she didn't know his name yet, wouldn't for another day—but she thought about the youngest voice on the expedition's check-ins, the kid who ran their radio, sixteen maybe, whose transmissions always ended with a cheerful *out* delivered half a beat too early, like a boy slamming a door on a conversation he wasn't sure he'd survived.

If the radios were dead, someone had to be the radio.

She threaded the gap.

Inside the corridor the light went strange and green, storm-lit from both sides, rain falling in ropes. The river below vanished and reappeared, vanished and reappeared, and each time it surfaced it looked less like water and more like something alive that had decided, recently, to stop pretending otherwise. Flood stage. The tributaries feeding it from the west ran brown and thick as batter.

This was the country the maps lied about. She knew, because she'd helped make some of the lies—had drawn in false oxbows and omitted sandbars at Reyes's request, early on, before she understood that a map was never neutral, that whoever held the pen held the land. Ilsa Voss had signed the Halvorsen expedition's charts; Wren had seen the name on the manifest. A cartographer. Someone whose whole profession was telling the truth about ground. What did a person like that find, up here, that was worth three missed check-ins?

The question arrived uninvited and stayed, circling, like a bird riding her wingtip.

Her father had owned a compass—brass-cased, Russian-made, the kind that flooded every market stall on the coast for a dollar fifty. He'd used it to navigate nothing more ambitious than the walk home from the cantina, but he'd held it like a relic, tilting it to watch the needle tremble and settle, tremble and settle, and he'd told her once, drunk and tender: *The needle doesn't point north, mija. It points at all the places you're not. That's why men follow it.*

She'd laughed at him then. She was not laughing now, alone in a storm with a debt for a co-pilot, following a needle toward all the places she'd sworn never to return to.

* * *

The rain thickened until the world outside the windscreen was a single gray thought. Wren dropped to five hundred feet, instruments and instinct split evenly between them, and the Otter bucked and wallowed through turbulence that felt personal. Hail ticked against the cowling, small and hard, like knuckles.

And underneath the engine noise, underneath the hail, her memory kept its own time.

The night she'd left Puerto Cielo the first time—really left, with a duffel bag and a plan to fly south until the fuel ran out—she had looked down at the town's lights shrinking under her and felt nothing. That was what she remembered most clearly, what she returned to in the long hours over open water: not grief, not relief, just a vast administrative emptiness, as if she were a file being transferred between offices. She had rebuilt herself in the years after, in a dozen river towns, under the company's thumb but at least under its protection, and she had gotten very good at the emptiness. Smallness as strategy. Make yourself a rumor. A rumor can't testify.

But the river country had a way of keeping things. It kept the bones of boats in its shallows and the names of the drowned in its villages' mouths. It kept the smell of woodsmoke and diesel and wet rot in the grain of everything built along its banks. And apparently, despite six years of careful flight paths and careful silences, it had kept her—kept the shape of her guilt the way floodwater keeps the shape of the valley, invisible most of the year, absolute when it rises.

Because that was the truth she'd been outrunning, and the storm wrung it out of her now, plain as the hail on the glass: she hadn't closed that crate because she was afraid. She'd closed it because she was *convenient*. Because the money was easy and the questions were hard and the village was far away and poor and would never fly north to knock on her door. She had weighed other people's lives on the scale of her own comfort and found them lighter, and she had done it smiling, and the company had loved her for it, and that was the debt no ledger recorded and no favor forgave.

Reyes thought he was sending her upriver to collect the Halvorsen party. He was actually sending her back to the scene of the crime. The river had been waiting six years with excellent patience, the way rivers do.

The hail stopped. The green light ahead softened into ordinary failing daylight, and the storm line fell away behind her like a curtain drawn by a tired hand. Below, the terrain changed—the forest crowding closer to the banks, hills shouldering up through the canopy, and the river narrowing, quickening, turning from a highway into a argument.

Twenty minutes to the Halvorsen camp, if the coordinates on the manifest were honest.

Wren keyed the radio one more time, mostly for the ritual of it. "Expedition Halvorsen, Expedition Halvorsen, aircraft November Charlie Kilo, inbound from the south, do you copy?"

Static answered her, vast and indifferent, the sound of a country keeping its mouth shut.

She flew on. The needle pointed north. The stone rode in her chest. And far ahead, past the last ridge, the river bent toward a clearing she could not yet see, where a kettle might be warm and a camp might be empty, and whatever waited there had already begun, quietly, to change the shape of her life.

She felt it before she saw it—a wrongness in the air, the particular hush a place gets when it has recently been abandoned by people in a hurry. Wren eased the throttle back and began her descent, eyes moving across the treeline, reading the river the way she'd taught herself to read faces: for what it was hiding.

There. A notch in the canopy. A strip of beaten earth beside the water, and the glint of tarps.

The Halvorsen camp. Silent. Waiting.

Wren Nakamura—whoever that was—put the wheels down.
