## 4. Camp Empty, Kettle Warm

The river bent north below her like a snake deciding whether to strike, and Wren followed it the way she'd followed it a hundred times before—low enough to read the water, high enough to clear the deadfall that storms left stacked along the banks like kindling nobody had gotten around to burning.

The Halvorsen camp sat on a bluff above a slow oxbow, a place the survey crews had used for thirty years because the ground was firm and the mosquitoes were merely biblical rather than apocalyptic. She'd dropped supplies there twice in the spring. She remembered the layout: two canvas wall tents and a dome of orange nylon, a cook shelter open on three sides, fuel drums lashed under a tarpaulin, the radio mast guyed with wire that caught the morning sun like a spider's ambition.

She saw the mast first. It was down.

Not leaning. Down—snapped at the second section, the top half hanging in its own guy wires like a broken wing. Her stomach did something slow and cold that had nothing to do with turbulence.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the plane, to herself, to the version of her that wanted to bank south right now and tell Reyes the weather turned her back. "Okay."

She circled once. The clearing looked intact from the air—the tents up, the fire pit dark, no smoke, no movement, nothing torn loose. That was almost worse than wreckage. Wreckage told you a story. This told you nothing, which in Wren's experience was what people arranged when they wanted the story to stay theirs.

She picked her line through a gap in the canopy where the expedition had cut an approach lane, sideslipped over the tall trees at the river's edge, and put the Beaver down on the grass strip with the particular tenderness she reserved for landings made alone, where a bent gear leg meant walking out or not walking out at all.

The wheels kissed earth. The tail came down. The propeller spun down into silence, and then there was only the river sound and the insects and her own breathing, loud inside the headset until she pulled it off.

* * *

She sat for a full minute with her hands on the yoke, doing what she always did before stepping into something unknown: inventorying herself. Pistol in the map case under the seat—Reyes didn't know about it, and would never learn. Machete on the fuselage rack. Water for two days if she stretched it. A flare gun whose cartridges were older than some of the trees. A satellite phone that worked when it felt like it, which today, judging by the three bars of signal and the storm cells stacking up over the northern ridgeline, meant it mostly wouldn't.

Six years ago she'd have gone in fast, all nerve and noise. Six years ago she'd been someone else, with a name that fit her like a good coat, and she'd lost both the name and the coat in a single bad night over the coast, and everything since had been an exercise in becoming small. Small debts. Small routes. Small talk with men like Reyes, who collected smallness the way other men collected stamps.

But smallness, she'd learned, had one advantage: nobody expected anything from it. And nobody expecting anything meant she could walk into an empty camp and look properly, without anyone asking why a supply pilot knew how to clear a building.

She got out. The heat took her immediately, wrapped around her like wet wool, and the smell of the place arrived next—woodsmoke gone stale, canvas mildew, kerosene, and underneath it all the green rot sweetness of the forest breathing.

The camp was wrong in ways that accumulated slowly, each detail small on its own.

The cook shelter's table still held plates. Not dirty ones—used ones, scraped clean but not washed, sitting where people had stood up from a meal mid-motion. A tin of coffee, half full, lid set loosely on top instead of screwed down. Someone's paperback, spine cracked open face-down on the bench as though the reader intended to come back after just a moment, just a minute, just—

The kettle was warm.

Wren touched it before she understood why she was touching it, fingertips to the dented aluminum, and felt the ghost of heat still living in the metal. Not hot. But not cold either. Hours, maybe less. The fire pit held ash and the gray skeletons of sticks, and when she crouched and held her palm above it, the ash breathed warmth up at her like something sleeping.

Her mouth had gone dry. She made herself stand slowly, made herself turn in a circle and take the whole camp in with the flat, methodical attention she'd once been trained for, back when she'd had a different life and a different name and a license to do things with aircraft that supply pilots weren't supposed to know about.

Tents: the orange dome unzipped halfway, sleeping bags inside rumpled but present, a boot by the door standing upright as if set there carefully. The wall tents the same—clothes folded, gear stowed, a chess game abandoned mid-board on a crate, black clearly winning. Nobody packs in a hurry here. Nobody flees. Everybody just... stops.

The radio shack was a lean-to against the biggest tent, and the radio was smashed.

Not damaged. Smashed. The transceiver had been pulled off its shelf and driven down heel-first into the mud, the chassis bent double, the front panel caved in so the tuning dial looked like a collapsed eye. The backup handset lay beside it in pieces, and someone had taken the time to find every piece—the cord, the microphone cap, the little glass fuse—and scatter them wide, into the grass, into the brush, the way you'd do if you wanted to make certain that no one, ever, could reassemble them.

Wren knelt over the wreck for a long moment. Mud had splashed up the side of the tent wall. Boot prints, deep ones, in the churned earth—a man's weight, driving down hard, more than once. Rage, then. Or thoroughness dressed up as rage.

Three missed check-ins on 126.7. She'd assumed equipment failure, weather, the ordinary attrition of the bush. Now she was looking at the alternative, and the alternative had fingerprints.

* * *

She found the map in the fire pit.

It had been burned deliberately but incompletely—thrust into the coals and then, apparently, pulled out again, or rained on, or interrupted. Half of it was char gone soft as moth wings; the other half was a corner of a survey chart, the kind the company printed on heavy stock with their crest in the margin and contour lines fine as hair. What survived showed a stretch of the upper river and, stamped faintly through the scorch-shadow, a symbol she recognized from old company charts: a square with a diagonal, the mark they used for decommissioned infrastructure.

Underneath the symbol, in pencil, someone had written coordinates. The graphite had survived the fire better than the paper around it. Four figures easting, four northing, and beneath them a single word in a small, cramped, very controlled hand:

*VAULT.*

Wren sat back on her heels in the ashes and let the fear arrive properly, because she'd earned it and delaying it would only make it worse.

She knew about the vault. Everyone on the river knew about the old survey vault, the way everyone knew about the ruined mission downstream and the graveyards of dredges rusting in the shallows—half history, half ghost story. The company had built it decades ago, back when they surveyed this country with armed escorts and grand plans, and then the plans died and the escorts went home and the vault stayed, sealed, a concrete tooth in the jaw of the hills. Maps said it was there. Nobody she knew had ever seen it open.

And now somebody had written its coordinates on a chart and tried to burn the chart, and failed, and the failure was sitting in her hands.

She looked at the handwriting again. Small, cramped, controlled. The hand of someone who drew lines for a living. Halvorsen ran his team like a benevolent dictator—she'd met him twice, a big bearded geologist who laughed too loudly—but he wrote like a man signing bar tabs. The junior techs scribbled. The security chief, Mercer, she'd seen him sign a delivery chit once, block letters, no personality at all.

But the cartographer—Ilsa Voss—Wren had watched her annotate a chart at Puerto Cielo while waiting out weather, and the woman's letters had been tiny and exact, drawn rather than written, each one placed on the page like a surveyor placing a benchmark.

Ilsa Voss had written this word. Ilsa Voss had tried to burn this map and hadn't finished the job—or hadn't wanted to.

Wren closed her hand around the charred corner, careful, careful, and put it inside her jacket, against the flannel, where the sweat of her body couldn't get at the pencil marks any faster than it already had.

* * *

The tracks started at the edge of the clearing and went north.

She found them by accident, nearly—stepping toward the tree line to relieve the pressure of the whole morning, and then stopping, because the ground in front of her was printed with passage. Multiple sets. Boots of at least three sizes, pressed deep, heading away from the camp in a loose column—not running, not scattered, but moving with purpose. Here and there a drag mark, a long shallow furrow in the leaf mold, as though someone had carried something heavy and low. A stretcher? A crate?

Beside the main trail, offset a few meters, a single set of smaller prints broke away toward the rocks at the base of the bluff, then vanished among them. Lighter. Shorter stride. A kid's weight, maybe, or a small adult moving carefully. They didn't rejoin the column.

Wren stood at the tree line a long time, letting the forest look back at her.

Everything in her argued for the plane. Get in, lift off, fly south, tell Reyes the camp was deserted, collect whatever crumb of debt forgiveness he dangled, keep being small. The storm cells to the north were building like accusations. The satellite phone had two bars now instead of three. The smart move, the survival move, the six-years-carefully-constructed move, was to leave.

But the kettle was warm. The coffee was half-full. The chess game waited for a player who'd meant to come back. And somewhere north, past the ridge, past the old dredge channels, a woman with a cartographer's precise hand had written *vault* on a map and tried to destroy it, and a boy-sized set of footprints had slipped away alone into the rocks.

Nobody packs in a hurry. Which meant whatever happened here hadn't been an attack from outside. It had been a decision. The expedition had found something, and split, and gone quiet on purpose—or been made quiet—and either way, the people who walked north had walked there carrying something heavy.

Wren thought about debt. She thought about the ledger Reyes kept, the columns of favors with interest compounding quietly in the margins, six years of smallness purchased on credit. She thought about how debt always won, eventually, and how she'd told herself that flying north for Reyes was just another payment.

But this—this wasn't Reyes's errand anymore. Reyes wanted silence brought back in a bag. Whatever had made these people smash their own radio and burn their own maps was the opposite of silence.

She went back to the plane. Not to leave—to prepare. She shrugged into her pack, checked the pistol, filled her water bottle from the drum, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the Beaver's cowling, the metal ticking as it cooled, the way it always sounded like the plane was talking in its sleep.

"Hold on," she told it. "I'm coming back."

Then she walked to the tree line, found the column of tracks where it entered the green shade, and followed them north, the machete loose in her right hand, the burned map pressing its pencil ghost against her ribs.

Behind her the camp kept its vigil: plates unwashed, kettle cooling by degrees, the broken radio staring blind-eyed at a sky that had stopped answering.

Ahead, deeper in the folds of the hills, the tracks climbed toward the old survey road, and Wren noticed—without wanting to, the way you notice a change in engine note—that the smaller prints had rejoined the trail after all. Faint, hours old, keeping to the edges, following the others north.

Someone small was out there ahead of her, alone, walking toward the same concrete tooth in the hills.

She tightened her grip on the machete and walked faster.
