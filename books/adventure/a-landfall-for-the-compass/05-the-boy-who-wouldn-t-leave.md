## 5. The Boy Who Wouldn't Leave

Tam had counted the days by the drip.

There was a seam in the limestone above his sleeping roll where the river's breath came through the rock, and every few minutes—longer when the heat pressed down, faster when the storms rolled over the ridge—a bead of water gathered there and fell into the tin cup he'd wedged beneath it. *Plip.* One day. *Plip-plip*, when the rain outside swelled the seep. He had marked eleven days on the wall with a stub of graphite from Halvorsen's own pencil case, and then he'd stopped marking, because eleven was a number that made the hunger worse and the silence worse, and after that he'd just listened to the water fall and tried to be a machine about it. Machines didn't cry. Machines didn't count their father's compasses over and over with cold fingers. Machines waited for a plane that might never come.

The cave mouth opened onto the north face of a bluff above the river bend, screened by a curtain of hanging roots and a shelf of fern gone brown at the edges. From inside, lying flat on his stomach in the dark with his cheek against stone that smelled of bat guano and wet clay, Tam could see a slice of the sky and, when the light was right, a sliver of the river itself—brown-green, muscular, sliding past the drowned trees with that patient, indifferent strength the river had. Sixteen years old and he'd already learned what the older men on the expedition knew without saying it: the river did not care. The river would carry you or take you, and it would not feel one way or the other about which.

He'd been a radio tech for nine months. Before that he'd been a kid in Puerto Cielo who could fix anything with a soldering iron and a prayer, which was how Halvorsen had found him—squatting behind the cantina repairing a fisherman's shortwave set for two dollars and a plate of rice. *You've got good hands, boy,* Halvorsen had said, in that Norwegian-flat voice of his, and Tam had thought: *this is it, this is how I stop being nobody's son.* His father had flown supply runs too, before the storm took the plane and the river took the wreckage and the company took three weeks to even send someone looking. The compass had come back in a box of effects, its glass cracked, its needle still swinging true whenever Tam held it level. He carried it everywhere now. It was the only inheritance he had, and he checked it the way other boys checked their hearts were beating.

Eleven days ago, the expedition had come back down from the vault, and everything had broken.

* * *

They'd been up there four days, Mercer and Dr. Okafor and the two survey men and Ilsa Voss with her map cases, and Tam had stayed at camp with the radio because somebody always stayed with the radio—that was the rule, that was *his* rule, the one thing on this expedition that belonged to him. On the third night the set had crackled and Halvorsen's voice had come through, thin and strange, full of static: *Tammy, we found something. Tell no one. Not Reyes, not anyone on the band. We'll explain when we're down.*

When they'd come down, they hadn't explained. They'd argued.

Tam had sat outside his tent with a headset around his neck, pretending to log frequencies, listening to the voices rise and tangle in Halvorsen's tent like birds fighting in a sack. Okafor's voice, high and hard: *—obligation, Erik, we have an obligation, this goes to the magistrate, to the press, to anyone who will print it—* And Mercer, low and flat as ever: *—goes nowhere until we know what it buys us. You don't walk into Puerto Cielo with that and expect to fly out again.* And Ilsa, quiet in a way that cut through both of them like wire through cheese: *I've copied everything. Three sets. Where they go is not your decision, Mr. Mercer, nor yours, Doctor. It belongs to the people whose land this is.*

And Halvorsen, tired, older-sounding than he had any right to be: *Nobody does anything tonight. Nobody transmits. Tam—*

But Tam had already keyed the mic, because that was his job, because the schedule said check-in, because rules were the only solid ground he'd ever stood on. *Camp to base, camp to base, routine position report, over.*

The argument stopped. Then Halvorsen was out of the tent, and his face—

Tam had never seen a man look like that. Like a house with the foundation pulled out. Halvorsen had crouched down to his level, put one big weathered hand on Tam's shoulder, and said, very gently, *You didn't hear anything on that set tonight, boy. Wipe the log. Please.*

Then, two mornings later, the strangers had come.

Tam had heard the boat first—the wrong kind of engine, not the expedition's outboard but something heavier, muffled, running quiet the way men run engines when they don't want to be heard. He'd been up the bluff trail checking the rain gauge, and he'd flattened himself against the rock and watched six men in company khaki come up the bank with rifles slung casual across their backs, the way Reyes's contractors wore them, all business-as-usual and dead-eyed underneath. They'd searched the camp. They'd taken the radio apart with a knife, cutting the wiring harness out in one piece, professional, like a surgeon removing an organ. They'd burned papers in the fire pit. And they'd asked each other, in Spanish, in the bored tone of men doing a chore: *¿Y el muchacho? ¿Dónde está el muchacho?*

*Where is the boy.*

Tam had climbed. Higher than he'd ever climbed, up past the fern shelf, up to where the bluff went sheer, and he'd found the cave by accident—by grace, maybe, though he wasn't sure anymore what he believed—and he'd crawled into it and pulled the roots closed behind him and not made a sound while the searchers' voices drifted up through the green. He'd stayed there while they left. He'd stayed there while the expedition scattered—Ilsa going downstream alone in the canoe with her map case wrapped in oilcloth, Okafor and the survey men walking out overland toward the mission, Halvorsen and Mercer heading upriver toward the vault again, arguing even in departure, splitting like a dropped plate.

He'd stayed there while the days dripped past, eating the last of the cassava cakes he'd stuffed in his jacket, then the boiled sweets from his pocket, then nothing, drinking from the seep, watching the river slice go silver at dawn and copper at dusk and black at night. Waiting for a plane. Any plane. Because his radio was dead, his radio was *murdered*, but a plane—someone would come looking eventually. The schedule said so. Schedules were real. Schedules were the bones of the world, and if you held onto them hard enough, the world couldn't shake you off.

On the twelfth day, he heard the engine.

* * *

It was the wrong sound first, and his heart fell before his brain caught up. Not the heavy thrum of a charter twin. Something lighter, looser, an engine with a rasp in it, an engine that sounded like it had opinions. A single-engine taildragger, laboring upriver against the wind, and Tam lay in the cave mouth with his fingers digging into the dirt and thought: *company, company, they've come back to finish it,* and then the plane swung wide over the bend and he saw the livery—or the absence of it, the sun-bleached fuselage, the patched cowling, the way it flew low and slightly crooked, the way no company pilot alive would ever fly—and the sound that came out of him wasn't a word at all.

He scrambled down the bluff trail on legs that shook and lied to him about their strength, slipping twice, catching himself on roots, shouting before he could think about whether shouting was safe. The plane flared over the old landing meadow, wheels kissing the grass in a long hissing roll, and Tam burst from the tree line waving both arms like a man signaling a ship, and the plane slewed around and stopped and the door banged open and a woman stepped down onto the grass.

She was smaller than he'd imagined anyone brave being. Brown-skinned, black hair cut short and practical, a canvas jacket with the cuffs worn to fuzz, eyes that swept the treeline before they ever landed on him—checked the shadows first, the way Mercer did, and something in Tam's chest clenched at that, because good people weren't supposed to have learned that habit.

"You're one of Halvorsen's," she said. Not a question. Her voice was rough, tired, the voice of someone who'd been flying through weather since before dawn.

"Tam." His voice cracked on it. "Tamás Ferreira. Radio tech. Are you—" and then the sentence collapsed under its own weight, all the questions he'd hoarded for twelve days shoving for the door at once. "Are you from the company? Did Reyes send you? Because if Reyes sent you, I'm not here, I was never—"

"Reyes didn't send me." She said it flat, and he couldn't tell if it was true, and he hated that he couldn't tell, and he hated more that it mattered, that trust had become a currency he couldn't afford. She looked past him at the empty camp, at the smashed radio he'd dragged out and laid on the table like a body at a wake, at the fire pit with its half-burned map corner still visible among the ash. Her jaw tightened. "Where's the rest of them?"

"Gone. Split up. The company men came and—" He was shaking now, the delayed shaking, twelve days of it arriving all at once. "They cut out my radio. They asked where the boy was. They were going to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I hid."

Something moved across the woman's face, quick as a fish under water. She crouched, suddenly, bringing herself down to his level the way Halvorsen used to, and up close he could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the dehydration cracks on her lips, and also—clearly, unmistakably—the moment she noticed the compass clipped to his jacket strap. Her gaze snagged on it. Held. Something flickered there that he couldn't read, some recognition that had nothing to do with him.

"You're half-starved," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're swaying."

"I'm *fine*." But the meadow tilted gently to the left as he said it, and she caught his arm—not grabbing, just steadying, the way you'd steady a mast—and the touch undid something in him that twelve days of dark and dripping water hadn't managed to touch. He sat down hard in the grass. To his horror, his eyes stung.

"I can't—" He dragged his sleeve across his face, furious at himself. "You can't take me back yet. You have to listen. There are documents. From the vault. Survey records—Halvorsen found them, the original boundary lines, the company *moved the lines*, they falsified the whole survey, they've been taking land that isn't theirs for thirty years. Ilsa copied everything. Three sets. And the company men came for it, and they'll come again, and everyone split up and nobody agrees and Halvorsen went back *up* there, toward the vault, with Mercer, and Mercer—" Tam's voice dropped, went small. "Mercer keeps looking at the money side of things. I heard him. *What it buys us.*"

The woman had gone very still. It was a particular kind of stillness, Tam realized—the stillness of someone recalculating, fast, behind a face that gave away nothing. He'd seen pilots do it at the airstrip when the weather turned. Reading the sky behind the sky.

"What's your name?" he asked. "Your name, I mean. Your real one. Everyone on the river says you don't—"

"Flying Wren works fine." She stood, cutting it off clean, and offered him a hand up. Her palm was dry and callused. "Can you walk to the vault?"

Tam blinked. "To the—Halvorsen went *up* there, the company already hit it once, there could be—"

"There could be a lot of things. Is Halvorsen armed?"

"Mercer is. Halvorsen has a machete and a bad knee."

"And Ilsa went downstream alone with copies of the evidence, and Okafor's walking to the mission, and the company knows all of it except where Ilsa went." Wren looked upriver, at the green wall of the gorge, and Tam watched her do the arithmetic he couldn't see—the fuel, the daylight, the storm cells stacked on the horizon like dirty plates. When she spoke again, it was quieter, almost to herself. "Debt always wins."

"Please." Tam grabbed her sleeve before he could stop himself, and she stiffened, and he let go, but he didn't take the words back. "Everyone thinks I'm a kid they left behind. I'm the radio tech. I know the frequencies, I know the vault grid reference, I know which channel Ilsa listens on when she thinks nobody knows. You need me. And I'm not—" His voice broke and he pushed through it, jaw set, sixteen years old and standing as straight as hunger would let him. "I'm not staying in another hole waiting for somebody to come. I did my waiting. I'm coming with you."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. He watched her armor do something he'd only ever read about—it didn't come off, exactly, but a seam of it showed, a hairline crack where the light got in. He saw her decide not to send him back to the cave. He saw her hate herself a little for deciding it. He recognized that too; he was becoming an expert on people deciding things they hated.

"Eat first," she said, jerking her thumb at the plane. "There's crackers and tinned fish in the cargo box. Then you show me the grid reference, and we fly up before the weather closes." She was already moving toward the De Havilland, already talking like the decision was settled, and then she stopped, halfway there, and glanced back at him over her shoulder. "And Tam. If anyone asks—you fixed my radio. That's why you're aboard. Nothing else."

"Why? What happens if someone asks?"

She looked upriver, at the gorge, at whatever she was seeing that he couldn't.

"Then it means we're out of time," she said, "and we're already being followed."

Far up the bend, where the river narrowed to green glass between the bluffs, a bird rose from the canopy in a clatter of wings—for no reason Tam could see, no reason at all—and did not come back down.
