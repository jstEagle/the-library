## 6. Compass Points

The compass sat in Tam's palm like a small confession.

Wren had seen it when she pulled him out of the cave—had registered it the way a pilot registers a flicker on an instrument panel, noted and filed, nothing more. But now, in the gray light leaking through the canopy as they ate cold beans from a tin, she looked at it properly and felt the flicker become something else. A warning light. The kind you ignore at your own peril.

It was a Meridian Mark III. Brass-cased, hinged lid, the mother-of-pearl inlay worn to a soft glow on the north point where a thumb had worried it for years. Her own sat on the dash of the *Albatross* at that moment, bolted into a bracket she'd machined herself, the same pearl worn the same way by a different thumb.

"Your father's?" she asked, keeping her voice level.

Tam nodded, mouth full. He swallowed. "He flew the river for eleven years. Medevac mostly, some mail. He said a pilot who trusts GPS more than a compass deserves to be lost." He turned it over, and she saw the engraving on the back before he covered it with his hand—an old habit, protective, though he didn't seem to know he was doing it. *To J., who always comes about. —M.*

Wren looked at the fire pit instead. The coals had gone gray overnight. Somewhere upslope, a toucan was making its idiot racket, and the river talked to itself below the bank, patient, indifferent, keeping everything it took.

"Where'd your father buy it?" she asked. Casual. Too casual, and she heard it, and Tam heard it too, because the boy was sharp in the way that hungry, frightened, clever people get sharp.

"He didn't. It was a gift. From a woman he flew with, before I was born." Tam's eyes came up, wary and curious at once. "Why?"

"Same model as mine," Wren said. "That's all."

It wasn't all. It was a thread, and she could feel it running back through six years of darkness to a night she did not let herself think about, and she was not going to pull it. Not here. Not with a half-starved boy watching her with eyes like his father's—she was almost sure of that now, almost sure in the way you're sure of a heading before you've confirmed it on two instruments—and she was not going to pull it.

She stood, brushed off her trousers, and became, deliberately, the pilot again. The small woman. The competent one. The one with no history larger than the next waypoint.

"We move in ten minutes," she said. "Eat while you can. Water now, because there won't be a good chance later."

* * *

The burned map had told them more than fire intended to leave. Wren had pieced the fragments together twice by lantern light, turning them, fitting torn edges, reading what the flames had spared. The Halvorsen expedition's cartographer—Ilsa Voss, according to the neat block letters—had drawn the route to the survey vault in her own hand, with elevation contours so fine they looked like woodgrain. Half the sheet was gone. What remained showed a ridgeline, a waterfall marked with a surveyor's symbol, and a dashed line climbing a spur that the fire had chewed away at its middle.

So they would walk the spur and trust the middle.

The forest took them the way the river took things: completely. Within an hour of leaving the cave, Wren lost all sense of the camp behind them. The light came down green and dim, filtered through layers of leaves, and the air was a wet wool blanket she had to push through with every step. Tam moved well for a boy who'd spent three days in a cave on scraps—he knew the forest, she realized, knew it the way river kids did, reading game trails, stepping over lianas without looking. But he kept stopping. Turning. Listening.

"You hear something?" she asked the fourth time.

"No," he said. "That's what I don't hear. The birds went quiet back there." He pointed with his chin, back along the way they'd come. "Something's walking behind us."

Wren stopped and made herself listen the way she'd been taught to listen to engines—not for sounds, but for the absence of sounds, the wrongness in the pattern. The forest had been talking since dawn: insects, birds, the drip and creak of the canopy. And underneath it all, faint as a misfire, a rhythm that didn't belong. Not an animal's rhythm. A man's rhythm. Steady, economical, unhurried.

Someone was following them, and whoever it was wasn't trying very hard to hide it.

"Keep walking," she said quietly. "Don't look back. Just walk."

"Who is it?"

"Could be one of the expedition. Could be the company. Could be a prospector who likes our trail." She started moving again, setting a pace that was fast without looking like flight. "Could be nobody. But we plan for somebody."

"Should we run?"

"Running tells them we're worth chasing." She glanced back at him—just once, quick. "We walk like we're going somewhere ordinary, and we let the ground do the work. There's a stream crossing ahead, if the map's right. Water kills a trail."

"And after the stream?"

"After the stream," she said, "we climb."

* * *

The stream was there, cold and quick over red gravel, and they crossed it twice in opposite directions and walked a stretch in the shallows where the current ran fast enough to blur their prints. Tam watched her do it without being told, and she saw him file the trick away, and something in her chest that had been locked for six years turned over in its sleep.

Stop it, she told herself. He's cargo. Get him out. That's the job.

But he wasn't cargo. Cargo didn't ask questions. Cargo didn't hold a compass that matched hers.

They climbed through the afternoon, up the spur the burned map had promised, through a world that grew older and stranger as they rose. The trees thinned. Moss hung from the branches like old laundry. Fog came up the valley in slow white rivers, and they walked inside the fog, and the fog was a mercy because it blinded their follower too—or she hoped it did.

It was Tam who found the second set of tracks.

He'd gone ahead to scout a fallen tree they had to cross, and when Wren caught up he was crouched at its base, not moving, his hand flat on the moss beside a boot print pressed deep into the soft ground. Fresh. Hours old at most. The print pointed up the spur—the same direction they were going—and beside it, half-hidden in the moss, a cigarette butt, still faintly fragrant, the paper unbleached.

"Company issue," Tam said. "The expedition's security chief smoked those. Mercer. I used to buy them for him in Puerto Cielo." His voice had gone flat, careful. "He's not behind us anymore, is he. He's ahead."

Wren crouched and studied the print. Size eleven, heel worn on the outside edge—a man who walked slopes a lot, who favored one leg slightly. She'd read ten thousand tire tracks and a few thousand footprints in her years on the river, and this one told her what she needed: someone patient, someone competent, someone moving with a destination, not a search pattern.

He wasn't hunting them. He was going to the vault, and they were going to the vault, and sooner or later the map's dashed line and his boot prints were going to arrive at the same door.

"He might not be hostile," she said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"You didn't see the camp." Tam's jaw was tight. "The radio was smashed. Not broken—smashed, with a machete, over and over. Halvorsen's rifle was gone but his ammo box was left. That's not looting. That's a message." He looked up at her, and the fear in his face had hardened into something older. "Whoever did that wanted them silent. And now we're walking to the same place they went silent."

Wren stood. The fog moved around them, and somewhere below, the follower's rhythm had gone quiet—either lost in the stream crossing, or no longer bothering to hide.

"Then we change the math," she said. "We don't arrive tired at dusk at a place we've never seen, with him already there. We stop here. Cold camp, no fire, above this fog line where nothing burns wet. We sleep in shifts. At first light we're moving while he's still making coffee."

"You think he sleeps?"

"Everybody sleeps." She said it like she believed it. "Even the company's dogs."

* * *

The cold camp was a ledge beneath an overhang of granite, dry, defensible, one way in. Wren took first watch because she knew she wouldn't sleep anyway, and Tam curled against the rock with the compass in his hand even as his eyes closed, like a child with a talisman, and she watched him and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing all day.

*To J., who always comes about.*

She'd owned her Mark III for eleven years. She'd bought it—no. That wasn't true. That was the story she told customs officers and curious passengers. The truth was that it had been given to her, on a night with rain on a tin roof, by a man who said she flew like the weather owed her money and laughed when she didn't deny it, and the engraving on hers read the same words, and she had never once told anyone where it came from, because the man's name was a debt and the debt was the whole architecture of her life.

J. Jonas. Jonas Halvorsen. The expedition leader whose camp she'd found empty, whose rifle was gone, whose signature was on the contract Reyes dangled over her like bait.

The coincidence was not a coincidence. The company hadn't picked her at random. Reyes had known exactly which ghost to send upriver, because the company kept ledgers of everything—fuel, favors, names, sins—and somewhere in a file in Puerto Cielo was a page with her old name on it and Halvorsen's beside it, and that was why her, that was why alone, that was why off the books.

She sat with that in the fog-dark and felt the trap's shape at last, all of it, the whole elegant machine: fly up, find the camp, find the documents, burn the documents, come home free. And if she died up here, or disappeared up here, well—the river kept what the river kept, and the company had lost nothing but a debt.

"Hey," Tam whispered. He was awake, watching her from under the overhang. "You figured something out just now. I saw your face."

"Pilots make calculations," she said. "It looks like that."

"About the vault?"

"About the compass." She turned toward him. In the dark she could just make out his shape, thin and stubborn, his father's son. "Your father and I knew each other. A long time ago. Before you."

Tam was silent for a long moment. The fog dripped. Somewhere far below, a howler monkey started its engine and ran it down.

"Is he alive?" Tam asked finally. "My dad. Do you know?"

"I don't know," Wren said, and it was the truth, and it cost her. "But I know he'd want you off this river, and I know he'd want those documents to matter. So tomorrow we do both. In that order."

Tam nodded slowly. Then he held up the compass, opened, its needle catching what little light there was.

"He said a compass doesn't point north," the boy said. "Everyone thinks it does. It points at the magnetic pole, which moves. You have to correct for the error, year by year, or you end up somewhere you didn't mean to go." He closed the lid. "He said that's true about people too."

Wren looked out at the fog, at the invisible valley, at the invisible man somewhere in it with his unbleached cigarettes and his patient boots, and she thought about error, and correction, and the long slow drift of a woman who'd been navigating by a false heading for six years.

"Get some sleep," she said. "First light, we move."

She took the watch, and the night came down, and near dawn—she would remember this precisely, later, when everything that happened had happened—she heard it. Not behind them. Ahead. Up the spur, in the direction of the vault, a single sound carrying through the fog with terrible clarity:

A door. Heavy, iron-ringed, opening.

And then, fainter, a voice—male, low, and not alone.
