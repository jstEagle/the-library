## 7. Reyes on the Wire

The office above the fuel depot had once been a shipping clerk's quarters, and it still smelled like it—canvas, diesel, and the ghost of someone else's cigarettes. Reyes kept the window open anyway. In Puerto Cielo, air was the only luxury that came free, and even that was rationed by the rain.

He sat with his boots off the desk for once, because tonight he wanted to feel the floor under him. Grounded. Solid. The shortwave crackled on the shelf beside a tin of mints he never ate and a photograph of a woman in Cartagena who no longer answered his letters. He tuned through the static the way other men prayed—mechanically, without expectation—and found the frequency he wasn't supposed to have.

*126.7.* The expedition band. Dead for nine days now, except for the sounds nobody official had heard: a burst of carrier wave two nights ago, half a second of open mic, breathing. And this morning, at 0410, something else. A voice, young, male, ragged with distance, saying a word that might have been *hello* or might have been a name before the signal folded into static like a bird into brush.

Reyes wrote the time down in his neat accountant's hand. He wrote down everything. It was the one discipline they couldn't take from him when the company took everything else, which they did slowly, politely, the way a river takes a bank.

The satellite phone sat in its padded case like a sleeping animal. He'd called it twice already today. Twice was careless. Three times was a pattern, and patterns got men like him replaced, and men like him who got replaced down here didn't get reassigned—they got audited. He poured himself two fingers of the cheap rum he kept for guests and drank it straight, because there were no guests.

Then he picked up the phone.

* * *

"Status." The voice on the other end belonged to a woman named Okafor whom Reyes had met exactly once, in a conference room in a city with air conditioning, where she'd explained to him over forty minutes that the Halvorsen contract represented a material exposure and that material exposures required resolution. She had not used the words *make them disappear*, but she had used the word *resolution* four times, and Reyes had been in this country long enough to hear what lived underneath a word like that.

"She's airborne," Reyes said. "Nakamura. Wheels up at first light, filed a medicine run to San Esteban, which is not where she's going."

"You're certain."

"I know her flight habits. She banks left out of the strip to stay out of the mission's sightline, then corrects north. She did it today." He turned the rum glass a quarter-turn on the blotter. "She also took the survival kit I keep locked in the depot. Picked the padlock. Didn't even try to hide it."

"That concerns you."

"It tells me she expects trouble and plans to be alone in it. That's useful. A woman flying alone doesn't file reports. Doesn't have witnesses." He paused. "It also means if she finds what we think she'll find, she'll come back with it, because she has nowhere else to put it. No family. No friends worth the name. Six years in this country and she's built herself a life with no load-bearing walls."

Okafor was quiet long enough that he could hear the hum of whatever climate-controlled elsewhere she occupied. "Mr. Reyes. Let me be precise about the outcome we require. The survey materials recovered from the vault are proprietary company property. If Nakamura locates them—or any member of the Halvorsen party in possession of them—those materials do not leave the region. Do you understand the distinction I'm drawing?"

"I understand that you want the paper to burn and the people holding it to be somewhere the paper isn't."

"I want," Okafor said, with the patience of a woman explaining arithmetic to a child, "a clean resolution. The expedition was lost to weather. That is already the story. Weather is very thorough down there, I'm told. Flash floods. River rise. Aircraft go down in cells that don't show on any forecast." Another pause, softer now, almost kind. "You've done difficult things for us before, Mr. Reyes. This is the last difficult thing. After this, your account is settled. All of it. You'd be a free man."

Free. Reyes looked at the photograph of the woman in Cartagena. He thought about the word the way Wren Nakamura must think about her own name—a door he'd sealed shut so long ago he'd forgotten whether anything was still behind it.

"The debt," he said. "All of it?"

"Every entry. You'd fly out of Puerto Cielo owing nothing to anyone."

"And Nakamura? Her ledger too?"

"That depends entirely on how useful she proves to be."

He hung up and sat for a while with the receiver warm against his ear, listening to the dial tone like it was surf. Then he opened the bottom drawer, took out the second ledger—the real one, the one that lived behind the false spines of agricultural reports—and turned to the page with her name on it.

*W. Nakamura. Outstanding: 11,400. Interest accruing.*

Below it, in pencil, in his own hand, a note he'd written three years ago and never erased: *She pays early. Always early. Watch that one.*

He sharpened his pencil and, beneath it, added a new line. Then he crossed it out immediately, because even alone in his own office, some sentences should never exist on paper.

* * *

Two hundred miles north, above a green country gone gray with rain, Wren flew with her jaw set and her hands light on the yoke, doing the thing she did best, which was being exactly where no one expected her to be.

The storm cell ahead looked like a bruise the size of a province. She'd been threading between the cells since midmorning, riding the corridors of calmer air the way she'd learned to read them years ago—not from instruments, which lied, but from the color of the water in the rivers below. Brown meant floodwater coming down from the high country. Tea-colored meant the swamp creeks were backing up. Clear meant nothing at all, because clear water up here meant limestone caves, and limestone meant sinkholes, and sinkholes meant the ground itself had opinions about where you landed.

She checked the compass on the dash. Her father's, brass-cased, the needle trembling toward north like a small nervous animal. Beside it, taped to the panel, the burned fragment of map she'd taken from the fire pit at the empty camp, its edges charred soft as moth wings. Half the contour lines were gone. What remained showed a bend in the river and, penciled in a hand she didn't recognize, tight and precise, a set of bearings converging on a blank space where the old survey grid should have been.

Someone had erased that section deliberately. Someone had wanted the vault unfindable—which meant someone had also wanted it findable, or why burn the map incompletely? Why leave it in the fire pit where a pilot with good eyes would see it?

Because whoever left it hadn't known who would come. Only that someone would eventually. Hope, Wren thought, was just a message sent without knowing the frequency.

The radio hissed. She'd been monitoring 126.7 since wheels-up, telling herself it was procedure. Every few minutes she keyed the mic and gave her call sign into the static, and every few minutes the static declined to answer, and every time the silence came back she felt it settle onto her chest a little heavier, like ballast she couldn't jettison.

*Nakamura to anyone on this band. Nakamura. If you can hear me, key twice.*

Nothing. Just the long gray breath of the atmosphere.

She climbed to clear a ridge line and the plane shuddered through turbulence that felt like the whole sky shrugging. Below, the river appeared through a tear in the cloud deck—wide, brown, muscular, hauling the storm's leavings toward the coast. Somewhere along that river was a camp with a smashed radio and a cold kettle. Somewhere past it, a vault in the limestone, and whatever the Halvorsen people had pulled out of the ground that made grown men stop answering their radios.

And somewhere behind her, she knew without knowing how, was Reyes.

She'd known him six years. Long enough to read him the way she read rivers. He'd given her the job too easily. He'd mentioned the debt forgiveness before she'd asked. He'd watched her pick the padlock on the survival kit and said nothing, and Reyes always said something—that was his nature, a man who narrated the world into smaller pieces so it couldn't swallow him whole. His silence had followed her up the strip like a dog that knows the way home.

Which meant this wasn't a rescue errand. It had never been a rescue errand. She was the errand. She was the instrument someone else was using to locate what they couldn't find themselves, and when she found it—

Her hands tightened on the yoke, then deliberately loosened. Fear was fuel; her father had said that. Waste it and you crash. Burn it slow and it carries you.

"All right," she said aloud, to the compass, to the dead radio, to the rain. "Then we change the flight plan."

She banked south first—wrong direction, deliberate, a feint for any eyes on her route—held it for ninety seconds until she was sure she'd look like a woman doubling back for weather, then swung hard north into the low cloud, dropping to treetop height where radar went to die and the world became a green tunnel rushing at her windshield.

* * *

In Puerto Cielo, the rain arrived at dusk, and with it a man named Delgado who did not officially work for the company and whose boat, officially, was a fishing boat, though it had never in its life held a fish.

Reyes met him under the depot awning, out of the rain, and handed him a canvas roll. Inside: a satellite phone, a rifle, and a hand-drawn copy of the burned map fragment—Reyes had photographed it the day Wren reported the camp empty, before she'd taken it, because Reyes photographed everything, because photographs were the only currency in this country that never devalued.

"She'll make the vault by tomorrow afternoon if the weather holds," Reyes said. "If it doesn't hold, she'll make it the day after, angry, low on fuel, and stupid. Either way, you'll be downstream of her position by dawn. You do not approach her. You do not engage her. You let her do the work."

Delgado, a broad quiet man with a scar through one eyebrow, looked at the map. "And after she does the work?"

"After she does the work, you wait for my call." Reyes held his gaze. "That part is not negotiable. Whatever happens at that vault, it happens on my word. Not before. Not after. There are people upriver—Halvorsen's people, maybe survivors. Survivors complicate things. We handle complications in the correct order."

"The correct order," Delgado repeated, without inflection.

"The documents first. Everything else is weather." Reyes zipped the canvas roll closed and pushed it across. "One more thing. The boy."

"What boy?"

"The radio tech. Sixteen, maybe. Tam Osei. If he's alive—if any of them are alive—" Reyes stopped. Rain drummed on the tin awning, filling the silence for him, and he was grateful for it. "Company policy on survivors is case-by-case. My instruction is: report, don't decide. You're not paid to decide."

Delgado took the roll and slung it over his shoulder. At the edge of the awning he paused, rain silvering around him, and looked back with an expression Reyes couldn't quite parse—pity, maybe, or its close cousin, contempt.

"And you, señor? Are you paid to decide?"

Reyes didn't answer. He stood under the awning a long time after the boat's running lights dissolved into the dark river, watching the water carry the rain away toward the coast, toward the sea, toward everywhere he had told himself for twenty years he would eventually go.

Then he went back upstairs, keyed the shortwave to 126.7 one more time, and listened to the static where a young voice had begged hello at four in the morning.

He told himself he felt nothing. He was very good at telling himself things. It was, in fact, the only skill the company had ever truly paid him for.

* * *

Night caught Wren over the tributary country, and she put the plane down on a settler's strip she'd used maybe four times in six years—a mud-and-grass clearing beside a mission outpost where an old woman named Beatriz traded her engine oil for tinned coffee and never asked questions, which was the highest form of hospitality the river country offered.

Beatriz brought her beans and plantains and sat with her under the wing while the rain worked the fuselage like a patient drummer.

"Heard anything on the band?" Wren asked, casual as she could make it, which was not very.

Beatriz chewed slowly. "Mission shortwave catches fragments. Last night, near dawn—someone transmitting weak. Young voice. Kept repeating coordinates in the old survey format, the pre-war grid. Nobody uses that format anymore except maps older than me." She looked at Wren with eyes that had watched sixty years of this river take and give back. "You going up there."

Not a question. Wren looked at her beans.

"There's a kid on that frequency," she said finally. "Maybe. Maybe it's nothing."

"Mm." Beatriz rose, joints cracking like kindling, and gathered the bowl. "My husband used to say the river keeps what it's owed and gives back what it chooses." She paused at the hangar door, silhouetted against the lamplight. "Fly careful, hija. And listen close on that band tonight. Signals travel farthest just before dawn—cold air carries them. If someone's calling, that's when they'll call."

Wren lay that night in the hammock Beatriz strung under the wing, listening to rain on fabric, the compass in her jacket pocket pressing against her ribs like a second heartbeat. At 0400 she woke without deciding to, crawled out into the wet dark, and keyed the radio inside the hangar, volume low, scanning 126.7 through the hiss.

Static. Static. Static.

Then—at 0412, almost exactly twenty-four hours after the last transmission—a sound. Faint, broken, riding the edge of the noise floor. Not a voice this time. A pattern. Two clicks. Pause. Two clicks. Pause. Then three.

She knew it instantly, the way you know a knock on your own door. Morse. Slow, clumsy, someone remembering letters learned long ago and half-forgotten. She grabbed a pencil and wrote it on her palm:

*T-A-M. V-A-U-L-T. C-O-M-I-N-G. H-E-L-P.*

Then, after a long gap, one final group, transmitted slower, as if the fingers sending it were shaking:

*R-U-N-N-I-N-G. N-O-T. A-L-O-N-E.*

Wren stared at the words on her palm while the rain came down and the compass needle trembled in her pocket, pointing north, pointing at a sixteen-year-old boy on a limestone hill with something following him through the dark.

Not alone. Neither of them, anymore.

She was rolling the tie-downs before the sky went gray.
