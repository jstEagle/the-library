## 8. The Vault Door

The rain stopped an hour before dawn, and the forest began to breathe.

Wren had learned to read that breathing over six years of flying into places maps forgot—the way the canopy exhaled its stored heat into the cooling air, the way the river fog rose off the water in slow gray ribbons, the way every sound seemed to arrive from two directions at once. This morning the breathing felt wrong. Too loud. As if the forest knew they were coming and was talking about it.

She crouched at the edge of a ravine, binoculars raised, watching the far slope where the old survey vault was supposed to be. Tam knelt beside her, close enough that she could hear his stomach growling—a thin, insistent sound, like something small trying to get out of a cage. He'd eaten half her emergency ration bar three hours ago and refused the other half, and she hadn't pushed. She remembered being sixteen and proud and hungry all at once.

"There." He pointed, not quite touching her arm. "That notch in the ridge. The map said the door faces southeast."

She found it. A slab of poured concrete set into the hillside like a headstone, half-swallowed by vines, with a rusted steel door maybe four feet wide. It should have been sealed. It should have been the kind of forgotten thing that stayed forgotten for fifty years.

It stood open. Not forced—open, swung inward on hinges someone had oiled recently enough that the metal still gleamed wetly in the early light.

"Breached from inside," Tam whispered. "Like I told you."

"You told me the door was open. You didn't tell me what was inside."

"Because I don't know. I ran before—" His voice caught, and he swallowed it down. "I ran."

Wren lowered the binoculars. In the gray light his face looked older than sixteen, hollowed under the cheekbones, eyes red-rimmed from days of hiding in a cave eating nothing but river snails and fear. She'd pulled him out of that cave yesterday morning because she couldn't do anything else, and now here they were, following a half-burned map toward a hole in a hillside that had already swallowed seven people.

Eight, counting Ilsa Voss, wherever she was.

"We can still go around," she said, though they both knew it wasn't true. "Circle downstream, pick up the river, come back with—"

"With what? Reyes's men? The company's fixers?" Tam's jaw tightened. "You met Reyes. You know what he'd do with whatever's in there."

She did know. That was the whole problem. She'd spent six years learning exactly what men like Reyes did with things that were inconvenient, because once upon a time she had been the inconvenient thing, and she'd run instead of standing, and running had cost her everything except her life, and some mornings she wasn't sure it had even kept that.

"The Halvorsen team went in there five days ago," she said quietly. "Seven people. Radios smashed, camp burned from the inside, tracks heading this way. Whatever they found, somebody didn't want them reporting it."

"And you're going in anyway."

"I'm going in anyway."

He nodded, and there was something in the nod that frightened her more than the open vault door—not bravado, not a boy's hunger for adventure, but the flat steadiness of someone who had already decided that if this killed him, at least he'd die moving toward his father's killers rather than away from them. His father, Dr. Emil Tam, expedition geologist, missing presumed scattered somewhere between the vault and the river. The boy carried the man's brass compass in his shirt pocket, and every few minutes his hand drifted up to touch it, the way you'd touch a wound to make sure it was still there.

* * *

They came down through the ravine single file, Wren leading, boots placed soft on wet stone. Her machete cleared a path through the vine curtain, each cut loud as a gunshot to her straining ears. The air near the vault smelled wrong—concrete dust and machine oil and, underneath it, something organic and sweetish that she chose not to name.

Twenty meters out, she stopped and held up a fist. Tam froze behind her, good kid, better than she deserved.

The ground around the door told a story if you knew how to read it. Boot prints, several sets, going in. One set coming out, heavy, deliberate, spaced like a man who wasn't hurrying. Drag marks near the threshold—something wide and rectangular hauled out on a tarp or a sled. And pressed into the mud by the hinge side, crisp and recent: the tread of a military boot, size eleven or better, no more than twelve hours old.

Someone was inside. Or had been, very recently, and might come back.

She weighed it the way she weighed weather, the way she weighed fuel loads and crosswinds and the thousand small calculations that kept a pilot alive in country like this. Going in was bad. Waiting outside was worse—you couldn't defend a position you didn't have, and whoever owned those boot prints owned the slope above them.

"In fast," she breathed back to Tam. "We find cover just inside, we look, we leave. Two minutes. If anyone comes, we don't fight—we run for the ravine and split up. You go left, I go right, we meet at the big kapok tree downstream. You remember the tree?"

"The one shaped like a fist."

"That's the one."

He touched his father's compass through his shirt, once, like a prayer, and nodded.

Wren crossed the last twenty meters in a low sprint, machete flat against her forearm, and slipped through the doorway into darkness that smelled of cold stone and old paper.

* * *

Her eyes needed thirty seconds to adjust. When they did, she wished they hadn't.

The vault was bigger than the door suggested—a gallery cut back into the hill, maybe fifteen meters deep, ceiling lost in shadow. Steel shelving lined both walls, and most of it had been emptied. Not looted—emptied, methodically, boxes stacked and removed, drawers pulled and their contents sorted into piles that were themselves gone. Whoever had been here hadn't ransacked the place. They'd *harvested* it.

But not completely. Against the far wall, half-hidden behind a fallen shelf, sat a single steel document case, lid open, contents spilled across the floor like the guts of something slaughtered. Papers fanned everywhere. She could see survey sheets, hand-inked boundary lines, typewritten memos with carbon copies still attached—and across several of them, in red grease pencil, a single word repeated like a signature:

**FALSIFIED.**

Someone had gone through these documents and marked them. Someone who wanted the lie to be legible to whoever came next.

"Tam," she whispered. "Light. Low."

He clicked his penlight on behind a cupped palm, and the beam found the papers, and Wren's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

She knew survey documents. She'd flown enough of them into enough remote corners to recognize the format, the agency stamps, the grid references. These were original boundary surveys for the company's land claims—the same claims that let them log, mine, and bulldoze a third of the river country. And layered beneath them, older, on different paper entirely, were the *actual* surveys. Government ones. Indigenous territory demarcations, signed and dated decades ago, showing lines that bore no resemblance whatsoever to the company's versions.

The company hadn't disputed the old boundaries. They'd simply drawn new ones and paid someone to make the new ones official.

"Oh," Tam said softly, beside her. "Oh, this is—my dad was talking about this, the night before. He said they'd found something in the deep files, something about the eastern parcels, and Halvorsen got quiet, real quiet, and then Mercer started sleeping with his rifle—"

The penlight beam trembled. Wren put her hand over it, dimming it to a glow.

"How many people knew?"

"All of them, I think. That's why they split. Halvorsen wanted to take it to the magistrate in Puerto Cielo. Dr. Osei wanted to broadcast it first, get it out to every settlement so the company couldn't just—make it disappear. And Mercer said—" Tam's voice went flat. "Mercer said they were all idiots, and that the smart play was to sell it back to the company for passage out. That's when the shouting started. That's when I took the radio batteries and ran."

Wren stared at the spilled documents, at the red grease-pencil word marching across them, and understood that she was looking at the reason eight people had vanished. Not the jungle. Not an accident. Arithmetic. Seven witnesses plus one truth equaled a liability, and somewhere in Puerto Cielo a man with a ledger had done the math.

"We're taking these," she heard herself say. "All of them. Now."

She was stuffing papers into her jacket when the light changed.

Not the penlight. Something else—a faint green wash from deeper in the gallery, from a side chamber past the fallen shelf, where the concrete floor dropped away into a lower room. A chemical glow stick, burning steady and cold.

And a voice, low and level, coming out of that darkness like a blade laid flat against her neck:

"Hands where I can see them. Both of you. Slow."

* * *

Wren went still with her fingers full of evidence and her heart slamming against her ribs. Beside her, Tam's breath went ragged.

"Mercer?" he said. "Mr. Mercer, it's me, it's Tam, it's—"

"I know who you are, kid. Hands. Now."

The green light rose. A shape detached itself from the shadows of the lower chamber—big, deliberate, rifle held at the low ready, muzzle tracking between them with the unhurried economy of a man who had done this enough times that it no longer required thought. Mercer stopped six meters away, where the glow stick's light caught his face: heavy-jawed, gray-stubbled, a fresh scar of duct tape residue across one cheekbone where something had been held together recently and badly.

His eyes went to the papers in Wren's hands. Something moved behind them—too quick to name, gone before it finished arriving.

"Pilot," he said. "Nakamura. The supply run. Reyes sent you."

"He offered," Wren said carefully. "I came because your people went silent."

"My people went silent because silence was the only thing left that they could choose." Mercer's voice didn't change register at all, and that was the frightening part—no anger, no threat, just inventory. "Where's the rest of you? Reyes always sends a second man on retrieval work."

"There is no second man. There's me and a sixteen-year-old radio tech who's been eating river snails for four days. Look at us, Mercer. Does this look like a retrieval team?"

He looked. She watched him actually do it—watch his gaze move over her mud-caked flight jacket, the borrowed machete, the boy shaking beside her—and she watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes and come up wrong for him. Because if Reyes had sent her alone, off the books, then Reyes intended for whatever she found never to be reported. And if Reyes intended that, then the pilot standing in front of him was not a colleague. She was cargo. Or she was cleanup. Either way, she was disposable, and so, by extension, was everyone standing in this room.

Including him.

"You've been here how long?" Wren asked, filling the silence before it hardened. "Since the team split?"

"Some of us didn't split." Mercer shifted his weight, and the rifle barrel dipped two degrees—toward the floor, not quite at rest, but no longer a sentence waiting to be pronounced. "Halvorsen took four people downstream toward the mission, carrying copies. Osei and your friend Voss went separate ways with the originals. And two of my own contractors decided the company's severance package came in lead, so I handled it, and then I handled my own exit, and I've been sitting in this hole for three days deciding which direction to run."

"Which way did you pick?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "You want to know something funny, pilot? I've worked for that company nine years. Nine years of escorting surveyors and evicting squatters and making problems evaporate, and every year the pay went up and the work got dirtier, and I told myself the same story every time—that I was the professional, that I kept things clean, that clean was a kind of honorable. Then Halvorsen opens a steel box in this room and finds out the entire legal foundation of everything I've been guarding is a forgery, and my employers' response is to burn seven people off the map." His thumb moved along the rifle's stock, slow. "So now I'm standing here pointing a gun at a woman and a kid, wondering whose side pays better. And the honest answer is that nobody's paying anymore. The account's empty. It's been empty for years. I just kept writing checks."

Tam made a small sound. When Wren glanced at him, the boy was staring at Mercer with an expression she recognized—she'd worn it herself, once, at sixteen, looking at an adult who had finally said the true thing out loud.

"You were there," Tam said. "When they took my dad. Were you there?"

Mercer's jaw worked. For a long moment the only sound was the green light humming its cold hum and, far above, the forest breathing through the open door.

"No," he said finally. "I was upstream marking a helipad. But I know who was. And I know where they were ordered to take him." He looked at Wren, and for the first time his eyes weren't doing arithmetic—they were asking. "I can't give you the boy's father back. Probably. I won't promise what I can't deliver. But I can give you the names of the men who took him, and I can get you downstream to Voss before Reyes's real cleaners get there, because they *will* be there, pilot, within a day, maybe less. Reyes reported your tail number to the charter desk yesterday afternoon. You've been flying blind since Puerto Cielo."

The words landed like ice water. Wren thought of the long storm cells she'd threaded on the way north, the empty channels, the hours with no one listening—and realized the silence hadn't been the jungle's. It had been engineered. Every minute of this flight, someone in an office with air conditioning had known exactly where she was and exactly what she was walking into, and had let her walk anyway.

"If I lower this rifle," Mercer said, "and you two are company assets playing a long game, then I'm a dead man, and I've made peace with the odds on that. But if you're what you look like—" his gaze flicked to Tam, to the compass outline visible through the boy's soaked shirt, and something almost human crossed his face— "then we've got maybe eighteen hours, one boat, and a woman downstream holding the only original documents left in this country. So. Pilot. Whose side are you on? Because I need to hear you say it."

Wren looked at the papers clutched against her chest—decades of stolen land, inked and stamped and marked **FALSIFIED** in a dead-sure hand. She looked at Tam, who had lifted his chin the way his father must have lifted his, daring the world to be smaller than it was.

She thought about smallness. About six years of flying under a borrowed name, keeping her wake narrow, her debts paid, her history folded up and locked in a box she never opened. Small had kept her alive. Small had also brought her here, alone, into a hole in a hillside, because small was exactly what men like Reyes counted on.

"My side," she said, "is the one with the truth in it."

Mercer held her eyes for three full seconds. Then he thumbed the rifle's safety on, slung it across his back, and crouched to gather the scattered documents into the steel case with hands that moved fast and gentle, like a man handling something that could either save him or convict him, knowing it would probably be both.

"Then listen close," he said, "because we're about to do something stupid, and stupid goes better with a plan."

Outside, far downstream, faint as a memory, Wren heard the sound she'd been dreading since Puerto Cielo: an engine, high and thin, climbing out of the south.

Reyes's charter. Hours ahead of schedule.

They had until nightfall.
