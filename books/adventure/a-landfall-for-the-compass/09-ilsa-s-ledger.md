## 9. Ilsa's Ledger

The vault had been built by men who expected the jungle to lose.

That was the first thing Wren understood as she stood in the doorway, Mercer's flashlight beam cutting a pale wedge through the dark ahead of her. The structure sat back into the hillside like a tooth in a jaw—poured concrete, steel door, a lintel stamped with a survey company's name that had been dead for forty years. The vines had climbed it, tested it, and given up. Whatever the river took, it had never managed to take this.

The door hung open. Not broken—open. Swung wide on oiled hinges, as if someone had left in a hurry but had not been in a hurry to hide the fact.

"Mercer said it was breached from inside," Tam whispered at her shoulder. His voice had a raw edge to it; he'd been talking more since the cave, as if words were something he'd been rationing and could finally spend. "That's what he said. Breached from inside."

"I heard him." Wren stepped over the threshold.

The air inside was cold and mineral, the smell of stone that had never seen sun. Her flashlight found the walls first: steel shelving, rows of document cases, the gray-green rot of old paper. Then the floor, and her stomach dropped.

The Halvorsen expedition's gear was everywhere. A theodolite on its tripod, knocked askew. A rucksack split open, its contents spilled like entrails. A sleeping bag wadded in the corner, still holding the shape of the body that had last slept in it. And in the center of the room, a folding table with a camp lantern on it—unlit now, but a coil of fresh fuel line beside it said someone had worked here for days.

"They lived here," Tam said. He'd moved past her without her noticing, drawn to the table the way he'd been drawn to the radio shack in the camp. Kids and machines. "Ilsa and the others. After they found whatever they found."

"Careful."

"I'm being careful." He wasn't, quite. He was sixteen and half-starved and his father was somewhere in this country either dead or running, and Wren decided she could not in good conscience ask him for more careful than he had.

Behind them, out in the green light under the trees, Mercer stood with his rifle slung and his back to the vault, watching the trail they'd come up. He hadn't asked to come inside. He'd said, "I'll hold the door," in the flat voice of a man who had decided something overnight and hadn't announced it, and Wren still didn't know whether what he'd decided was loyalty or arithmetic.

She turned her light on the shelving and began to read the labels.

* * *

The old survey cases were catalogued in a hand that had died with the company that employed it. *Sector 14 — Hydrographic. Sector 15 — Boundary Verification, 1963.* She pulled one at random and the cardboard came apart in her grip, exhaling a breath of air that had been sealed since before she was born. Inside: mylar sheets, linen-backed, the drafting so fine and so confident it looked like it had grown there rather than been drawn.

Rivers. The river, actually—the same brown artery she'd been following since dawn, rendered in blue ink with soundings penciled along its length in a surveyor's cramped hand.

"Wren." Tam's voice had changed. "Wren, come look at this."

He was standing at the folding table, and the lantern was lit now—he'd found the matches, of course he'd found the matches—and under its warm circle lay a spread of documents that were not forty years old. These were new. These were the expedition's own work, printed on the portable printer they'd packed upriver, and someone—Ilsa, it had to be Ilsa—had laid them out in an order that was meant to be read.

Wren leaned over the table.

The first sheet was the company's current land claim filing. She knew the format; she'd carried enough of them in her cargo hold to recognize the letterhead, the boundary coordinates, the certification block at the bottom with its stamp of the regional registrar. The company's claim to the upper valley ran along a line drawn on this map in confident red, enclosing something like forty thousand hectares of what the filing called *unencumbered, unoccupied, and unclaimed terrain.*

The second sheet was the original 1963 survey. Same valley. Same river. And along the same stretch of the boundary, in the old surveyor's hand, a notation that made the hair rise on Wren's arms:

*Boundary per treaty line, surveyed and marked this season. Indigenous reserve lands begin at this monument — see plat 14-B. NOT subject to grant.*

The red line on the company's filing did not follow the 1963 boundary. It followed a line two kilometers east of it. Two kilometers that swallowed the reserve whole—two kilometers of riverfront, of the mission, of the settlements where people bought her medicine and waved at her plane and had never once asked her name.

"Tam," she said quietly. "Go through the rest of the table. Touch nothing, read everything. Tell me if there's anything dated after the filing."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know if they knew what they were looking at before they sent us up here." She was already pulling the third sheet free. "Because if the company knew this survey existed—if they knew their own claim was built on a moved line—then this wasn't a rescue mission from the start. It was cleanup."

* * *

It took her an hour to understand the full shape of it, and understanding did not make it smaller.

The 1963 survey was not the only one. There were three verification surveys over the decades, each one confirming the treaty line, each one filed with the regional registrar, each one—here was the part that turned her mouth dry—each one *signed off* by the same corporate ancestor of the company that now owned Wren's debt, her contract, and, on paper, the air she breathed in Puerto Cielo. The company had known. The company had always known. And when the Halvorsen expedition was hired to do a modern resurvey—hired, Wren realized, by the very same people—they had found the old monuments still standing in the forest, moss-grown brass caps on concrete posts, exactly where the 1963 map said they'd be, exactly two kilometers west of the line on the company's filing.

The expedition's own field notes were there, in Halvorsen's blocky capitals. *Monument 7 located as per plat. Monument 8 located as per plat. Discrepancy with current filing: approx. 2.1 km. Recommend immediate legal review. This is not a survey problem. This is a fraud problem.*

And then, in a different hand—smaller, precise, unhurried, a hand that drew lines for a living—Ilsa Voss's annotation in the margin:

*Not a discrepancy. A decision. Someone moved the line and paid for the ink. We should decide what we're going to be about it.*

Wren stood in the cold vault light and felt the ground shift under a life she'd spent six years nailing down.

She had told herself a story on the flight up. She was good at stories; she'd been telling herself them since the night that had unmade her name. *You're going upriver to find missing people. You'll find them, or you won't, and either way you'll come back and Reyes will shave a number off the ledger and you'll go on being small.* It had been a clean story. It had required nothing of her except the flying.

But this—this was not missing people. This was the whole rotten foundation of the country she lived in, pulled up into the light and laid out on a folding table under a camp lantern. Every settlement downriver, every family that had been pushed off the riverfront, every "unoccupied" hectare the company had logged and fenced and sold—the theft had a paper trail, and the paper trail was in her hands.

She thought of Reyes in his air-conditioned office with his soft voice and his ledger of favors. She thought of the way he'd said *quietly*. She had assumed *quietly* meant *without panic*. Now she understood it had meant *without witnesses*.

"Wren." Tam's voice again, and this time it was strange in a new way—hushed, almost reverent. "There's more. There's a note. It's—I think it's for us. For whoever came."

She crossed to him. He was holding a single sheet of paper, and at first glance it looked like nothing: a column of figures, coordinates and numbers, the kind of thing a cartographer produced by the ream. But the figures were grouped in fives, and the groupings didn't correspond to anything on the maps, and at the bottom, in that same precise hand, was a line that was not numbers at all:

*For the pilot. If you can read a chart, you can read this. — I.V.*

"She knew," Tam said. "She knew someone would come. She knew it would be you."

"Everyone on that river knows my plane," Wren said, but her voice wasn't steady, because the note was addressed to *the pilot*, singular, definite, as if Ilsa Voss had sat in this concrete room with the company's fraud spread out around her and known exactly who the company would send, and had decided to trust her anyway.

* * *

The code took her most of the afternoon, and she was glad of it. Glad of the work, glad of the numbers, glad of anything that kept her hands busy while her mind kept circling back to the documents on the table like a moth at glass.

It was a surveyor's cipher—Ilsa had built it out of the trade itself. The numbers were coordinates, but not coordinates of places; they were coordinates of *points on the expedition's own published maps*, the preliminary charts Halvorsen had radioed down to Puerto Cielo in the expedition's first month, back when the check-ins were still coming and the silence hadn't started. Wren had carried copies of those charts in her own chart case. She'd flown over them. She'd looked down at the terrain they described a hundred times without knowing she was looking at a message.

Each coordinate resolved to a feature. A bend. An island. A tributary mouth. And the features, read in sequence, spelled a route.

*Downstream,* the cipher said, when she finally had it whole. *Past the falls. White church on the left bank, two days' travel by water. Ask for the woman who mends nets. Bring nothing written. Trust the boy. Trust no one with a company radio.*

"Trust the boy," Tam read over her shoulder, and then he read it again, and Wren watched something move across his face—grief and pride tangled so tightly she couldn't have said which was winning. "She knew I was alive. She knew I'd make it to you."

"She built the note so only someone with the expedition's charts could read it," Wren said. "And she wrote *trust the boy* into the route itself." She looked at him. "She wasn't sure whoever came would believe you. So she made believing you the only way to find her."

Tam looked down at the paper for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was thick. "My dad said she was the stubborn one. He said she'd redraw a map ten times before she'd let it be wrong. He said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "He said the company picked her because she was the best, and she said yes because she wanted to map the river honest. One honest map. That's all she wanted."

The lantern hissed. Out beyond the vault door, the forest made its endless green noise, and somewhere in it Mercer shifted his weight, rifle stock tapping stone, a small sound that Wren's ears had already learned to sort from the river's voice.

"Tam," she said. "I need you to hear me on this. What's on that table—it's not a rescue anymore. You understand that? If we carry this out, we're not carrying people out. We're carrying a case. The company will do anything—*anything*—to keep those sheets from reaching a magistrate. They didn't send Reyes after the Halvorsen team because they were worried about them. They sent him because the team found the moved line, and the moved line is worth more than all of our lives put together. Mine included."

"I know."

"I don't think you do. I think you're sixteen and you want your father and you want Ilsa and you want this to be the kind of story where the good people win because they're good. But this is the kind where they win because they're careful, and because people like me decide the truth is worth more than staying invisible." She heard her own voice and was surprised by it—not by the hardness but by the certainty underneath it. When had that happened? Somewhere between the cave and the vault door, apparently. Somewhere between a half-starved boy refusing to let her turn back and a dead company's brass monuments standing in the moss like gravestones for a lie.

Tam set the cipher note down on the table, next to Halvorsen's blocky capitals, next to Ilsa's margin note. *We should decide what we're going to be about it.*

"Then decide," he said. "You're the pilot. It's your plane."

* * *

She spent the last hour of light photographing everything with the battered camera she kept for documenting cargo damage—every sheet, every monument photo, every page of field notes, the cipher note last of all. The originals she repacked into a waterproof document case, and the case she did not put in her rucksack. She strapped it flat against her chest under her jacket, where a knife would have gone, where a heart was.

Mercer came to the vault door as the light went amber. He stood in the frame with the forest behind him, and he looked at the table, at the lantern, at the two of them, and Wren watched him do the arithmetic she'd seen him doing since the ambush—the same arithmetic, running again with new numbers.

"So," he said. "Now you know what they found."

"Now we know."

"And you're going to take it out." It wasn't quite a question. He rubbed his jaw with the back of his rifle hand, a habit she'd already catalogued. "You should know what that means. I've worked for people like this my whole life. They don't send lawyers first. They send men like me, and when men like me stop being useful, they send men like me again, only hungrier."

"Is that a warning or a recruitment pitch?"

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, tired at the root. "Both. I haven't decided yet. Give me till morning." He turned to go back to his watch, then stopped. "The woman. Voss. She was the one who argued with Halvorsen about coming down the river in the open. She said the company would own the water before they'd own the truth. Halvorsen wanted to run for the mission anyway." His voice went flat. "I was the one who told Halvorsen the downstream route was clear. That was four days ago. I haven't heard from the downstream end since."

Wren's blood went cold. "You told them it was clear. On whose information?"

"Company information. Relay company." He met her eyes, and for the first time since she'd known him—which was all of two days, which felt like a season—Mercer looked like a man reading his own ledger and not liking the balance. "If I was played, then the company's had eyes on the downstream route since before your plane left Puerto Cielo. And if they've had eyes on the route, they know where Voss would run."

The forest had gone quiet around them, or maybe it was only Wren's ears. Two days' travel by water. A white church on the left bank. A woman who mends nets, who was maybe already a woman being watched.

"We leave at first light," Wren said. "And Mercer—whatever you decide by morning, decide it before we're on the water. Because I'm not stopping to renegotiate in the rapids."

He nodded once and walked back into the dark, and Wren stood in the vault door with the fraud strapped to her chest and the cipher burning in her pocket, and listened to the river running downstream toward everything that was about to happen.
