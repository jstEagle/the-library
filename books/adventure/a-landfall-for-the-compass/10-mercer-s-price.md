## 10. Mercer's Price

The gun was still in his hand when he understood he wasn't going to use it.

That was the thing about holding a weapon on someone for long enough — the weight changed. It started as authority and ended as an argument you were having with yourself, a cold iron voice saying *now, do it now* while some older, quieter part of you kept asking what exactly it would buy. Mercer had been good at not listening to that quieter part for most of his life. He'd built a career on it. But standing in the vault corridor with a boy's terrified breathing echoing off the concrete and a pilot looking at him like she was already calculating how many ways she could kill him with her bare hands, the arithmetic had gone strange on him.

"Lower it," Wren Nakamura said. Not begged. Said, the way you'd tell a man his fly was down.

He lowered it. Partway. Enough.

* * *

His name was Daniel Mercer once, before it got shortened and sanded down to something that fit on a contract. His father had been a surveyor too — that was the joke nobody in this river country would ever get — a government man who walked boundary lines through country that looked a lot like this one, three thousand miles north and forty years earlier, back when lines were drawn with transit and chain and a handshake that meant something because both men knew the other one's grandfather. His father used to say a line was a promise made permanent. That you should never draw one you wouldn't stand behind in twenty years, because the land would outlast every argument about it, and the people who lived along your line would inherit whatever honesty or dishonesty you'd folded into it.

Daniel had believed that at fifteen. At nineteen he'd believed it harder, studying geomatics on a scholarship he was proud of, planning to be the kind of surveyor whose maps were quoted in courtrooms.

Then his father's lungs went, and the medical bills came, and the scholarship didn't stretch, and a mining concern offered a young man with clean credentials four months of work at triple the going rate to walk some lines in a place where nobody would check his math. He told himself it was temporary. One job. He'd pay off the house, finish the degree, get back to honest lines.

That was the first installment.

The second came two years later, when the same concern needed a witness statement adjusted — nothing dramatic, just a matter of emphasis, of which measurements got mentioned first — and Daniel Mercer discovered that a man who has sold once is worth more to buyers than a man who hasn't, because he comes pre-broken-in. The third installment was a signature. The fourth was silence about a village relocation that went wrong in the way relocations go wrong when the people being moved aren't consulted. By the tenth installment he'd stopped counting them as installments at all. A debt paid in pieces stops feeling like a debt. It starts feeling like a salary.

Somewhere in there he'd picked up a sidearm, then training, then contracts where the deliverable wasn't a map anymore but a guarantee. Security chief for expeditions into places where the law was a rumor. He was good at it. That was the worst part — he was genuinely, verifiably good at keeping people alive in bad country, and the skill didn't care whether the expedition deserved protecting. Skill was amoral. It just did its work, like water finding low ground.

He'd told himself for years that he was different from the men who pulled triggers for pleasure. He only pulled them for necessity. He kept people alive; that was the service. What the people he kept alive went on to do — that was above his pay grade, none of his business, not his weight to carry.

It was Halvorsen who'd cracked that ledger open.

Old man Halvorsen had hired him straight, no company middleman, paid from an account Mercer could see was mostly the family's own money. And Halvorsen had sat across from him the night before they flew upriver, drinking terrible instant coffee in a Puerto Cielo hotel, and said: *You've worked for the land company before. I know it. Everyone knows it. I'm hiring you anyway because I want a man on this trip who knows what they're capable of.*

And Mercer had said yes, and meant it as a professional courtesy, and then six weeks later he'd stood in a concrete vault under a hill while Halvorsen's people unrolled survey documents sixty years old and watched the old man's face go gray as wet ash, and understood that he was standing inside the largest installment sale of his career. The company hadn't just moved a boundary line here. They'd moved a whole history. Falsified the baseline surveys, forged the commission signatures, redrawn a river's course on paper so that land belonging to people who'd never signed anything became land belonging to paper. Sixty years of it. Whole generations dispossessed by ink.

Ilsa Voss had looked up from the documents at him — the cartographer, sharp-eyed, sunburned, hands stained with graphite — and said nothing. She didn't have to. She'd done the same math he had: *this man is the company's eyes. Whatever happens next depends on what he reports.*

What happened next was the radio getting smashed. What happened next was the camp splitting — Halvorsen wanting to go public immediately, half the team wanting to run, Mercer standing in the middle with his hand on his holster doing the calculation he'd been doing his whole life, which was: *what does the buyer pay, and what does refusal cost?*

Except this time the numbers wouldn't resolve. This time, when he imagined the report he'd file — *expedition compromised, documents destroyed, witnesses handled* — he saw his father's face, and his father was saying nothing at all, which was worse than any accusation.

So he'd hesitated. For the first time in twenty years, Daniel Mercer had let a decision sit open overnight, and in the morning the camp was empty and the documents were gone and a sixteen-year-old kid was missing in jungle full of things that eat careless boys, and Mercer had spent three days telling himself he was tracking the survivors to protect them from the company's cleanup crew, which was coming, he knew the crew, he'd trained two of them.

Three days of following tracks and telling himself stories. Then the pilot showed up.

* * *

"You're Reyes's man," Wren said now. They'd retreated to the vault antechamber, where the light came gray through a ventilation slit and Tam sat against the wall hugging his father's compass like a talisman, watching Mercer with the flat hatred only teenagers can produce without effort. "Everyone upriver knows it. So either you're here to clean us up, or something's broken."

"Something's broken," Mercer said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only true one I've got." He set the pistol on a crate between them, grip toward himself but visible, and watched her register the gesture. She didn't relax. Smart woman. "Halvorsen found what was in this vault. You know that much?"

"I know enough."

"Then you know the company can't let it exist. There are men downstream — good ones, professionals — whose entire job is making sure those papers burn and everyone who's read them develops a sudden case of drowning." He rubbed his jaw. Three days of stubble, three days of bad sleep. "I'm supposed to be one of them. I trained half their roster."

"And instead you're sitting in a hole in the ground telling me this."

"Instead I'm sitting in a hole in the ground having a conversation I can't take back." He laughed, short and ugly. "You want the rest? Fine. Ilsa Voss left here with copies. Halvorsen took the originals downstream toward the mission at Santa Rosa — slow, because the old man's heart isn't what it was. Voss went to ground somewhere along the lower channel. She's the one who organized all of it, splitting the evidence so no single fire kills it. Clever woman. Company thinks she's the dangerous one."

"Is she?"

"She's the one who matters." He met Wren's eyes. "Here's my price, Nakamura. I'll guide you to her. I know this channel better than any chart — I helped survey half of it, back when I still pretended my work meant something. I can get you past the company's checkpoints, and when Reyes's crew comes, I know their playbook because I wrote half of it."

Tam spoke from the wall, voice cracking on the edges. "Why? Why would you help us?"

Mercer looked at the boy. Sixteen. Same age he'd been when his father died, when the first easy money had appeared like a door opening in a wall he'd thought was solid. He remembered exactly how the world looked at that age when you learned that everything had a price and most people were quietly for sale. He hated putting that look on another kid's face.

"Because I sold my conscience in installments," he said. "Small ones, over a long time, each one small enough to swallow. And I told myself the total didn't count because I never sold it all at once." He tapped the crate beside the pistol. "Those papers in that vault are the balance due. All of it, itemized. Sixty years of lines drawn by men like me who knew better." He exhaled. "I want out. Not forgiveness — I'm too old to believe in that. Out. And the only currency that buys exit from a company like this is leverage, and the only leverage in this river is whatever Voss is sitting on."

Wren studied him for a long moment. He could see her running the same audit he'd run on himself for three days in the bush: *is this real, is this a trap, what does he gain.* He let her look. It was the first honest audit anyone had performed on him in years and he found he didn't mind the exposure.

"So you're not helping us," she said finally. "You're buying in."

"I'm buying in. Don't confuse it with virtue. If it goes wrong, I'll save my own skin first — I'm telling you that now so you can plan around it. But if it goes right, the documents come out, the company's claims collapse, and a man named Mercer becomes very expensive to disappear." He shrugged. "A cut. That's my price. A cut of whatever protection the truth buys, and passage on whatever plane gets out."

"A cut of the truth," Wren repeated. Something moved behind her eyes that might have been disgust, might have been recognition. "You want to own a piece of it so it can't be taken from you."

"I want to own a piece of it so I'm worth more alive than dead. Yes." He picked the pistol back up and holstered it, deliberately, letting them watch. "Virtue doesn't survive contact with Reyes. Leverage does. I'm offering you leverage with legs."

Tam stood up, compass still clenched. "My dad trusted you."

"I know," Mercer said, and it landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. "Your dad hired me knowing what I was, same as I'm asking her to. Turns out that was his whole method. Give a man a chance to be better than his invoice and see if he takes it." He swallowed. "I intend to take it. Whether I deserve to is a question for later."

* * *

They moved out an hour before dusk, Mercer leading, because the light would die fast under the canopy and he wanted the river reached before dark. He set a pace the boy could hold and pretended not to notice the boy matching it out of pride.

The forest did what it always did to him — pressed in close and indifferent, cicadas sawing the air, the smell of wet rot and green growth so thick it felt like walking through something alive. He'd spent years in country like this and it had never once felt like home. It felt like a creditor. Like everything here was waiting, patient, for accounts to settle.

Behind him, Wren moved quiet as spilled water, and Tam stumbled and recovered and stumbled again, and Mercer thought about the last time he'd guided people through this terrain — the company's retrieval crew, eight months ago, after a rival survey outfit got curious about the eastern concession. He'd gotten them in and out clean. Nobody had asked what happened to the rival surveyors afterward. He hadn't asked either. That was the craft of it, the whole craft: never ask the question whose answer changes your cut.

He was asking it now. Every hour of it, all at once, late.

At the river they found the boat Halvorsen's team had cached — a battered aluminum skiff with a pull-start outboard, hidden under palm fronds exactly where Mercer would have hidden it, because he'd taught them to. He swept the fronds aside and felt something loosen in his chest that he refused to name.

"Downstream to the third oxbow," he said, checking the fuel. "Then we beach and walk the last stretch. Voss won't have stayed anywhere findable from the water." He glanced at Wren. "One condition I didn't say back in the vault. When this ends — however it ends — I give testimony too. On the record. My name attached."

Wren paused, one foot on the gunwale. "That's not a price. That costs you."

"It's the only payment I'll accept." He heard his own voice come out rougher than intended. "Twenty years of installments, Nakamura. You don't cancel a debt that size. You settle it. In public."

She looked at him a moment longer, and whatever she saw seemed to decide something, because she nodded once and climbed aboard and started the engine before he could make the moment heavier than it needed to be.

The skiff swung out into the brown current, and the current took them, and Mercer crouched at the bow watching the banks slide past — banks his own instruments had measured, years ago, for men who meant to steal them — and thought about how strange it was that a man could navigate perfectly by every chart he'd ever falsified, and call that, at last, a kind of heading home.

Then, around the first bend, he saw it: a thread of woodsmoke rising thin and deliberate from the far bank, three short puffs, spaced evenly.

Signal smoke. Someone downstream knew they were coming.

And from the timing of it — from the fact that it rose the moment their engine noise carried — Mercer understood that whoever was signaling wasn't signaling to them.

Someone else was already on the river.
