## 11. Rapids and Reckoning

The boat was a crime in itself—sixteen feet of dented aluminum with a forty-horse Evinrude that someone had loved badly and often, tied up at a slough below the vault trail where Mercer said the Halvorsen expedition's own launch had been moored before it wasn't. Mercer called it requisitioning. Wren called it what it was and pushed off anyway, because the river didn't care about vocabulary and neither did the men who would be coming behind them.

She took the stern. It wasn't negotiable. Mercer could sulk about it or he could sit in the bow with his rifle across his knees like a man pretending to be furniture, and Tam could take the middle seat and hold on.

"Current's going to run four knots through the narrows," she said, reading the water the way other people read faces. "Faster after the bend. There's a chute past the second island where the channel pinches down to maybe thirty meters. We run it wide open or we don't run it at all."

"And if we don't?" Tam asked.

"Then we walk two days through country that eats boots and optimism in equal measure." She tilted her head at the sky, at the bruise-colored cloud stacked over the western ridge. "And I'd rather not be walking when that breaks."

Mercer grunted. He'd been grunting since the vault, since Wren had put the ledger pages in the waterproof map case and strapped it inside her jacket against her spine, since he'd named his price—a cut of whatever leverage those papers bought—and she'd said *fine* too quickly for him to believe she hadn't already decided to cheat him. Maybe she had. Maybe she'd decide later. The river was generous that way; it let you defer your decisions until they were made for you.

They went downstream with the engine at half throttle, the banks unspooling green and indifferent, ferns hanging over the water like curtains in an abandoned house. Tam talked. That was the thing about him—sixteen years old, three days hiding in a cave eating river mussels and stolen cassava, and he still talked. About his father's compass riding in his shirt pocket. About how Ilsa Voss had taught him to read contour lines by drawing them in the dirt with a stick. About the sound the vault door made when it opened, a groan like something waking up embarrassed.

Wren let him talk. She'd learned long ago that silence invited questions, and questions were a currency she couldn't afford. But there was something about the boy's voice under the engine noise, something stubborn and bright, that made the silence feel less like armor and more like a wall she'd built around an empty room.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You're ferrying him. That's all. Ferry him downstream, hand him to whatever comes next, disappear again.*

The first set of rapids came at them the way trouble always did—announced, underestimated, then suddenly everywhere. The river narrowed between granite ribs and stood up in gray ridges, and Wren opened the throttle and threaded the boat through the standing waves with her wrists, feeling each one slam the hull like a fist on a door. Spray came over the bow in sheets. Tam whooped—actually whooped, a raw teenage sound of pure terror-joy—and Mercer braced his boots and said nothing, which from Mercer was practically applause.

Then the bend. Then the chute.

She saw it a half-second too late: the deadfall.

A tree had come down somewhere upstream in the last storm, whole and enormous, and it lay across the channel just below the pinch point, its trunk submerged except for one black elbow of branch combing the current. The safe line—the line she would have taken—was gone. What remained was a slot on the right side barely wider than the boat, water piling into it fast and ugly, and no time to do anything but choose.

"Right side," she snapped. "Tam, get low."

She put the nose into the slot. The world became white water and engine scream. The hull slewed, kissed rock with a shriek of aluminum, corrected—and then the current did the thing currents do, reached under the boat with its whole cold hand and shoved, and the stern swung wide, and the black elbow of the deadfall swung into view exactly where she did not want it.

"Down!"

The branch caught the gunwale above Tam's head with a crack like a rifle shot. The boat spun. And Tam—who had been ducking, who had almost been low enough—was simply gone, plucked off the thwart by the water as if the river had reached in and picked him up.

* * *

Later, Wren would remember it in fragments, the way the mind files catastrophe: Mercer lunging for the throttle and missing. The map case strap cutting her shoulder as she twisted. The boat yawing broadside into the chute while she scanned the foam for a jacket, a hand, a boy—*there*, a flash of yellow sleeve two meters off the starboard quarter, already sliding backward, already being eaten by the recirculating water below the falls where the river folds back on itself and holds what it catches.

She killed the engine so the prop wouldn't find him. She grabbed the throw rope coiled under her seat—twenty meters of polypropylene she'd carried for six years and never once thrown—and got a turn of it around her waist, and she looked at Mercer and said, "Me," because there was no time to say more, and went over the side.

The water hit her like a verdict. Cold enough to stop thought, fast enough to make the river feel alive and personal. She swam the angle she'd have flown—an intercept course, leading the target—and the current fought her every stroke, and the roar of the chute filled her ears until it was the only sound in the world. Tam surfaced ten meters ahead, arms windmilling, mouth open on a scream the water stole before it was born.

"Tam! Flat! Float flat!" She screamed it twice before he heard, and then—God bless Ilsa Voss and whoever else had drilled him—he flattened out on his back, feet up, riding the boil instead of fighting it. But he was drifting toward the fold, the place where the current curled back under, and she could see it happening the way you can see a stall developing in a plane: the moment when recovery stops being possible.

She caught his wrist at the edge of it. His skin was ice, his pulse hammering like a bird in a box, and for one full second their eyes met—his wide and unbelieving, hers narrowed against the spray—and then the rope went taut and the river tried to keep them both.

Mercer hauled. She felt him through the rope, steady and strong, a man doing the one uncomplicated thing left in his complicated life. She kicked for the eddy line, dragging Tam, and the current released them grudgingly, in stages, like a landlord returning a deposit, and then they were in slack water against a gravel bar and Mercer was pulling them in hand over hand and Tam was coughing up half the watershed.

Wren knelt in the shallows with the boy's head cradled against her shoulder and felt her own heart trying to exit through her ribs. He was shaking. So was she. The difference was that she'd had practice hiding it.

"You're okay," she said. "You're okay. Breathe."

"I'm—" A cough. "I'm okay."

"Liar." But she was smiling, or something adjacent to smiling, wet and shaky. "Good float. Who taught you?"

"Dad." Another cough. "He said rivers don't hate you. They just don't know you're there."

*Your father taught you to float and gave you his compass and died owing money to people like me,* Wren thought, and the thought arrived with such unexpected weight that she had to look away, out at the water, at the chute that had nearly kept him.

Mercer stood over them, dripping, breathing hard, and for once he didn't grunt. He looked at Wren with an expression she couldn't file—something between reassessment and unease, the way a man looks at a dog he assumed was a pet and has just seen kill a snake.

"The boat?" she asked.

"Beached below the bar. Took some water. She'll run." He wrung out his sleeve. "So will you, apparently."

"Don't sound disappointed."

"I'm never disappointed," Mercer said. "That's my whole problem."

* * *

They bailed the boat, ran the engine dry, and made camp on the gravel bar because the light was dying and running the narrows blind was suicide even by Wren's standards. Mercer built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter. Tam sat close to it wrapped in the emergency blanket from Wren's kit, shivering in waves, holding his father's compass in both hands like it might need comforting too.

Wren dried the map case by the fire, checked the ledger pages, repacked them. Ritual work. Hands-busy work. The kind of work you do so you don't have to think.

But the fire was small and the night was loud with river and eventually the hands ran out of tasks, and Tam looked across the flames at her with the directness only teenagers and drowning survivors can manage.

"You went in after me," he said. "You didn't even tie the rope right. You just went."

"It's called a rescue. People write songs about it."

"Why?"

"Because most people wouldn't."

"No." He shook his head, patient, infuriating. "Why are you like this? You fly alone, you pay a man you hate, you won't tell anyone your name—Ilsa said your name isn't even your name. Why does someone like that jump into a river for a kid she met yesterday?"

The fire popped. Downstream, the chute kept up its low continuous argument with the rocks. Mercer, at the edge of the light, very carefully became interested in his rifle's bolt mechanism.

Wren could deflect. Deflection was her native language; she was fluent, she was elegant, she had conjugated deflection in three countries. She opened her mouth to do it.

And then she looked at the boy—blue-lipped, blanket-wrapped, alive because she'd happened to be in the water at the right angle at the right second, alive by luck dressed up as skill—and heard herself say, "Because six years ago I didn't."

Tam went still.

"There was a girl," Wren said, to the fire more than to him. "Nineteen. Flying charter out of a strip not so different from Puerto Cielo, except the law there wasn't a rumor, it was a punchline. She owed money—not company money, worse, the kind that comes with kneecaps attached—and a man offered to clear the debt if she flew one load south and didn't ask what was in the crates. She knew. That's the part nobody believes. She knew what was in them. Medicine meant for a village upriver, diverted, sold. Kids went without. Two of them died—one of them a boy about your age, a boy who used to hang around the strip and ask her about the plane, ask her about the radio, ask her everything the way you ask questions." Her voice caught on something and she let it, because stopping now would cost more than continuing. "She told herself it was one flight. One debt. One crate. And then it was done and the man owned her, because that's how it works—you don't sell the cargo, you sell yourself, one installment at a time."

The river filled the pause.

"She ran. Changed her name, burned her license history, bought a wreck of a De Havilland and rebuilt it with her own hands so that no record, no registry, no man with a ledger could ever find her again. She got small. Small is safe. Small doesn't jump into rivers, because jumping in means mattering, and mattering means somebody can find you." She finally looked at Tam. "Six years ago, that girl watched a boy drown rather than go in after him, because going in meant being seen, and being seen meant the whole fragile architecture of her new life coming down. She told herself the current was too fast. It wasn't. She just calculated the cost and chose herself."

Tam's eyes were wet. Whether from the smoke or the story, he didn't say, and she was grateful.

"So when I saw you go under," Wren said, "there wasn't a calculation. For the first time in six years, there wasn't a calculation. My body went in before my brain could vote." She fed a stick to the fire. "I'm not telling you this so you'll forgive me. I'm telling you because you deserve to know who's flying you out. Not a hero. Just a woman paying a different kind of debt, the kind that doesn't show up in any ledger."

"What was her name?" Tam asked softly. "The girl."

Wren held the question the way she'd once held the rope—feeling the weight of it, the pull, the choice of whether to hold on or let go.

"Wren," she said. "Her name was Wren. It's the only true thing I kept."

Across the fire, Mercer's hands had stopped moving on the bolt. He was watching her with the expression of a man recalculating a contract he'd thought he understood, and Wren found, to her surprise, that she didn't much care what number he'd arrived at.

Tam reached into his shirt and brought out the compass, held it out to her across the flames. "My dad said a compass doesn't point north," he said. "It points home. North's just where home happens to be, for most people." He turned it in his fingers. "For you, I think it points wherever you decide to stop running."

"That's a lot of wisdom for a kid who floated face-first into a rapid an hour ago."

"He also said wisdom keeps better dry." Tam grinned—chattering teeth and all—and tucked the compass away. "Get some sleep, Wren. I'll take first watch. I owe you a river."

"You owe me nothing."

"Then I'll take watch because I want to." He settled against a log with the blanket around his shoulders, sixteen years old and suddenly older than that, and Wren lay back on the cold gravel with the map case under her head and the fire painting the underside of the trees orange, and slept deeper than she had in six years.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. They were on the water by first light, running the remaining narrows in a river that had spent the night deciding to be angry—the rain upstream swelling the current, the banks sliding past faster now, the whole watershed leaning downhill with intent. Wren drove them hard. Every hour mattered and she couldn't have said why, only that the itch between her shoulder blades had returned, the old pilot's instinct that said *someone is behind you and gaining.*

They cleared the last rapids near midday, where the river widened and gentled and the mission's tin roof showed through the trees a bend ahead—white walls, red cross faded to pink, smoke rising from a cookfire. Civilization, or the nearest thing this country offered.

Tam stood up in the bow, pointing. "There! That's where Ilsa—"

The shortwave in Wren's jacket crackled. It had been silent for two days. Now it spat static, then a voice—thin, distorted, but unmistakably a woman's, urgent, repeating:

*"—any station, any station, this is Mission San Lorenzo. Company aircraft on the strip, armed men, they are asking for a cartographer by name. Repeat: they are asking for Ilsa Voss by name. Any station—"*

Wren's hands went cold on the throttle.

Ahead, the mission waited in the morning light. Behind them, somewhere upriver, the sound of an engine that wasn't theirs.

She opened it up.
