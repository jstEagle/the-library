## 12. The Company Man Arrives

The river gave them a morning of mercy and then took it back.

Wren felt the change before she saw it—the way the water ahead of them flattened into that glassy, expectant stillness that always preceded weather in this country, as if the whole basin were holding its breath. The sky over the eastern ridge had gone the color of old pewter, and the air had thickened until every breath tasted like wet copper and green rot.

"Storm's building," Tam said from the bow of the stolen boat, his father's compass open on his knees even though they weren't navigating by it anymore. Mercer had taken the tiller an hour ago when the current turned contrary, and he steered with the economy of a man who'd run rivers for pay in another life. "Radio said—well. Radio didn't say anything. Radio's been dead since the camp."

"The mission's shortwave will be alive," Wren said. "Father Anselmo runs the most stubborn transmitter on the river. Half the settlements between here and Puerto Cielo set their clocks by his evening broadcast."

"And the other half?"

"The other half pretend they don't listen."

Mercer grunted from the stern. It was the first sound he'd made in twenty minutes, and Wren had learned to read his silences the way she read cloud formations—by what they threatened. He was doing arithmetic again. She could see it behind his eyes: the value of three fugitives and a satchel of documents, weighed against whatever Reyes was paying him, recalculated hourly like exchange rates.

She kept the documents dry in a waterproof roll lashed inside her jacket, against her ribs, where she could feel them with every heartbeat. Forty pages of survey records. Boundary lines drawn through villages that had stood for generations, inked over by men who had never walked the ground. Ilsa Voss's careful annotations in the margins—dates, discrepancies, the quiet fury of a professional who had caught her employers lying and couldn't unsee it.

*Documents don't testify,* Ilsa had written in her coded note. *People do. Come downstream. Come alone if you can. Come fast if you can't.*

They were coming downstream, all right. Three of them, in a boat that smelled of fish guts and gasoline, running the current toward a mission that might already have company.

* * *

They heard the plane before they saw it.

It came out of the south, low over the tree line—a twin-engine charter, white-bodied, no markings except the registration numbers, which told its own story. Legitimate aircraft wore their owners' names proudly. This one flew anonymous, the way Wren herself had flown for six years, and something about seeing her own strategy used against her made her stomach clench.

"That's not a bush plane," Tam said, shading his eyes. "That's corporate."

"It's Reyes," Wren said flatly.

"How do you know?"

"Because nobody else on this river can afford to burn fuel like that." She watched the charter bank wide over the bend, circling once—surveying, choosing—and then drop toward the far bank where the mission's clearing opened up among the kapok trees. Father Anselmo had cut that strip himself, thirty years back, mowing the grass with a machete and stubbornness until it could take a light aircraft in dry weather. In wet weather it was a mud soup that swallowed wheels to the axle.

The charter's pilot put it down anyway. Good pilot. That was bad news. Company men who hired good pilots weren't planning to leave quickly.

"They're landing at the mission." Tam's voice cracked on the last word. "That's where—Ilsa's there. She's *there*, Wren."

"I know where she is." Wren was already hauling on the painter rope, pulling their boat toward the near bank, away from the mission's side of the river. "And so does Reyes, apparently, which means somebody talked, or somebody followed, or—" She stopped herself. There was no point finishing that sentence with Mercer sitting six feet away, listening with the stillness of a man cataloguing everything.

Mercer met her look without flinching. "I haven't radioed anyone," he said. "You smashed my radio. Remember? Back at the vault, when you decided I was more useful disarmed."

"I remember deciding you were more useful *alive*," Wren said. "The disarmament was a bonus."

"Charming." He killed the engine as they slid into the reeds along the northern bank, and the sudden silence pressed down like a hand. Through the trees, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the charter's engines winding down, and then voices—several voices, carrying across the water with the strange clarity that open rivers lent to human speech. A man's voice giving orders. Not shouting. Never shouting. Reyes didn't shout; shouting was for people who hadn't learned that quiet carried further.

Tam grabbed Wren's sleeve. His fingers were thin—they'd been thin since the cave, since the days of half-rations and hiding—but they gripped like a drowning man's. "We have to go around. Through the forest. We can reach the mission from the land side before—"

"Before what?" Mercer asked mildly. "Before we walk into eight hired guns because a sixteen-year-old wants to play cavalry?"

"Before they put Ilsa on that plane," Tam shot back, "or put her in the ground. Those are the options, and I'm not standing in the reeds debating while—"

"Both of you shut up." Wren said it quietly, which was how she said the things that mattered. The argument died. She scanned the far bank, counting shapes in the mission clearing: four men visible near the aircraft, two more moving toward the mission buildings, and Reyes himself standing apart from all of them, hands clasped behind his back, looking up at the chapel cross with an expression she couldn't read at this distance and didn't want to.

Seven, maybe eight. Against three, one of whom was a boy and one of whom was for sale.

"We're not going anywhere yet," she said. "We're going to listen. Anselmo's shortwave is on a schedule—if he can transmit, he'll transmit, and if he transmits, every ear on this river hears it, including ours, because Tam here is going to build us a receiver out of the boat's spare coil and whatever's in my emergency kit, and he's going to do it in the next hour."

Tam blinked. "I—yeah. Yeah, I can do that. If I had—"

"You'll have what's in the kit." She was already digging it out: wire, pliers, a crystal earpiece, the scavenged variable capacitor she'd been meaning to replace for two years and had never gotten around to, because smallness meant traveling light and traveling light meant surviving. For the first time in six years, the philosophy of smallness felt less like wisdom and more like cowardice wearing work clothes.

Mercer watched her unpack with an unreadable face. "And while the boy builds his radio," he said, "what exactly are you proposing we do about the fact that our destination now has a welcoming committee?"

"We wait for dark," Wren said. "And then we find out whether Father Anselmo's God protects the ones who come in peace or just the ones who arrive quietly."

* * *

The receiver took Tam ninety minutes, hunched in the bottom of the boat under a tarp, soldering by the light of a shielded candle stub while rain began to fall in the soft, persistent way of the afternoon tropics—not a storm yet, just the sky warming up. Wren kept watch through the reeds, and Mercer sat at the stern cleaning a pistol he had no ammunition for, which Wren found either pathetic or honest, depending on her mood, and couldn't decide which.

At 1700 hours exactly, the speaker crackled.

Not Anselmo first. Static, long washes of it, the river band alive with distance and weather. Then, threading through the noise, a voice Wren knew—Anselmo's reading voice, the one he used for the evening notices, measured and unhurried, as though the world were a parish and everyone in it merely late for supper.

"—repeat for those joining late. This afternoon a chartered aircraft arrived at the mission of Santa Rosa del Río bearing seven passengers who did not identify themselves and who have requested the use of our facilities overnight. They have also requested that we surrender to them a guest of the mission, a woman recovering from injuries sustained upriver. I have explained to these gentlemen that the mission does not surrender guests. They have explained to me that they are patient. I have explained that patience is a virtue I am pleased to share with them. Evening prayers will be held at the usual hour. All are welcome. Especially," and here the old man's voice acquired, very briefly, a blade's edge, "*especially* those listening on frequencies they should not be listening to. Out."

Static swallowed him.

Tam lowered the earpiece slowly, his face lit candle-yellow and fierce. "He knows. He knows people are listening—he's telling us—"

"He's telling us they're staying the night," Wren said. Her mind was moving fast now, the way it moved in weather, stripping everything down to vectors and margins. Seven men. Overnight. Which meant Reyes expected to fly out in the morning—which meant he had orders not to create a corpse on church grounds if he could avoid it, or else he'd have finished the job by now. Patience. Leverage. He wanted Ilsa to walk to the plane herself, or he wanted whoever came for her to walk into his hands.

Or both. Reyes had always been efficient that way.

"He'll move at dawn," Mercer said. He'd stopped pretending to clean the pistol. "Company protocol. Extract before first light, deny everything by noon. I've run that playbook, Nakamura. I wrote half of it."

"Then we go in tonight."

"Through a perimeter manned by professionals, in the dark, to extract a wounded woman from a building full of civilians, and then what? Walk her forty miles downriver?" He shook his head. "You need a plan better than courage."

"Courage is the plan. The rest is improvisation." Wren looked at the documents against her ribs, then at the grey river sliding past, then at the two faces watching her—one young and burning, one middle-aged and calculating, both waiting on her word as though she were someone whose word mattered. Six years of flying supply runs and keeping her head down, and somehow she'd arrived here: responsible for a boy, a traitor, and the truth.

"There's a settler net," she said slowly, working it out as she spoke. "Anselmo mentioned it once, years ago—families up and down the river who keep radios on the same frequency, pass word when the company's boats come through. If Ilsa's been hiding at the mission, she's been talking to them. And if she's been talking to them..."

"Then the whole river already knows the company's coming," Tam breathed. "They know *everything*. The broadcast—she could have sent copies—"

"Documents don't testify," Wren murmured. "People do." She stood up in the rocking boat, rain streaming off her jaw. "Here's how it goes. We wait for full dark. Then Tam and I cross the river wide, come at the mission from the cemetery side where the fence is down—Anselmo never fixed it because he says the dead should be able to leave too. Mercer—"

Mercer raised an eyebrow.

"—you're going to make noise on the north road. Enough noise to pull two guns off the perimeter. You wanted a price for guiding us. Consider this the down payment."

"And if I'd rather sell you to Reyes instead?"

"Then you'd have done it already, and we'd be zip-tied in the chapel." She held his gaze until he looked away first. "You're tired of being bought, Mercer. I can tell. It leaks out of you like oil. Tonight you get to spend yourself on something that isn't for sale."

He was silent a long moment. Rain drummed the tarp. Somewhere across the river, faintly, the mission bell rang the hour, and beneath it, if Wren strained, she thought she could hear the murmur of many voices—prayers, or an argument, or a congregation deciding what it believed.

"Dawn extraction," Mercer said finally. "Which gives us maybe nine hours. I'll want my pistol back. And ammunition."

"You'll get both when Ilsa's on this boat."

"Half now."

"*When she's on this boat.*"

Something that was almost a smile moved across his scarred face and died there. "You know," he said, "Reyes described you as a glorified courier. I'm starting to think he undersold."

"Everyone does." Wren turned to Tam. "Get some sleep. Both of you. We move at 0200."

But sleep didn't come for her. She sat in the bow with the rain on her shoulders and watched the mission lights across the water—warm squares in the dark, seven strangers' shadows moving behind them—and she thought about Ilsa Voss, whom she'd never met, lying wounded somewhere in those rooms, holding a fortress of paper against men with guns. About names. About the one she'd buried and the one she was wearing and the difference between them, which was shrinking daily, like ice in the sun.

Across the river, one of the warm windows went dark. Then another.

The clock had started. Hours now, not days.

Wren checked her watch, checked the river, and began, silently, to count.
