## 13. Ilsa Voss, Alive and Stubborn

The mission announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and wet thatch and the green rot of the riverbank—and then by sound: a bell, cracked down one side, tolling a note like a coin dropped in an empty well. San Rafael de las Aguas sat on a bluff above a slow brown bend of the river, six buildings huddled around a church whose white plaster had gone the color of old teeth, and Wren brought the boat in against the current with her heart hammering somewhere up under her jaw.

They had come the last three hours in silence. Not comfortable silence—Mercer's kind, the kind with weight in it, everyone listening past everyone else for the sound of an outboard that wasn't theirs. Tam sat in the bow with his father's compass open on his knee even though the river made navigation unnecessary; he just needed to look at something steady. Mercer polsed the motor through the shallows with the economy of a man who had done ugly things quietly for a long time.

"Church first," Wren said as they grounded the boat in the reeds. "Ilsa's note said the mission. She'll be inside or she'll be watching us from cover, and either way we don't want to look like company men."

"We don't look like company men," Mercer said. "We look like a pilot, a kid, and a security chief who hasn't slept in four days."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

The church door was heavy cedar, swollen with humidity, and it opened onto dimness and candle-smell and forty wooden pews worn smooth by generations of river people. At the far end, past the altar, a woman was sitting on the floor with her back against the wall and a rifle across her knees, and she did not stand up when they came in. She just lifted the barrel a few degrees and said, in a voice dry as survey paper:

"That's far enough. State your business and your names, in that order."

"Ilsa." Wren kept her hands wide and empty. "Wren Nakamura. Reyes sent me upriver to find you—or to find out what happened to you. I've decided those aren't the same errand anymore."

Something moved behind Ilsa Voss's eyes—calculation, quick and cold, the arithmetic of a woman who had been alone with a rifle and a secret for eleven days. Her left leg was propped on a rolled tarp, splinted below the knee with a slat of pew wood and strips of altar cloth, and there was a fever-shine on her face that Wren recognized from pilots who'd flown too long on adrenaline and not enough on food.

"Nakamura." Ilsa tested the name like a coin bitten for gold. "That's not the name on your flight plan. Your flight plan says *J. Marlowe*."

"My flight plan lies."

"So does every document Halvorsen ever filed." But the rifle came down an inch. "And the boy?"

"Tam." He stepped out from behind Wren, and Ilsa's face changed—the first honest thing on it—cracking open with relief so raw it looked almost like pain.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. "I told you to run south and I told you not to stop and I told you—"

"You told me a lot of things." Tam's voice wobbled and steadied. "I hid in the cave instead. Like you taught me. Never be where they expect the evidence to be."

For a moment the cartographer and the radio tech looked at each other across the ruined nave of that little church, and Wren understood that whatever these two had built between them out on the river, it was load-bearing. Then Ilsa's eyes went past them both to Mercer, and the rifle came back up with a speed that didn't match her ruined leg.

"Halvorsen's security chief walks into my church," she said. "You want to tell me why I shouldn't put a bullet in him and thank God for the target practice?"

* * *

It took twenty minutes and all of Wren's remaining credibility, and even then the truce was a thin thing, a rope bridge over a long drop. Mercer sat three pews away where Ilsa could see his hands, and he told her about the vault breached from inside, about the ambush he hadn't carried out, about the men Reyes would bring if the documents surfaced, and about the price he wanted—a cut of the leverage, passage out, a life that wasn't measured in installments. Ilsa listened the way she must have listened to river soundings, marking everything, trusting nothing, her thumb hooked in the trigger guard like a woman holding a conversation on a leash.

"You sold us once already," she said finally. "In camp. You told Reyes where Halvorsen planned to move the records. That's why the raiders hit us two days early. Two of my people are dead because of a phone call you made from a satellite handset on a Tuesday."

Mercer took that without flinching, which was either courage or exhaustion. "Yes."

"And now you're here because?"

"Because I've been paid in money my whole career and it turns out it doesn't spend where I'm standing." He rubbed his face. "Because the kid pulled me out of a rapids when he could have let me drown. Because I want to see how this ends with me on the right side of it, once, before I'm too old to care."

Ilsa looked at Wren. "And you? Reyes bought your debt. Why aren't you reporting back to him right now?"

"Because I read what was in the vault," Wren said. "And because I've spent six years being nobody, and I know exactly what it costs, and I'm starting to think it costs more than the alternative."

Ilsa held her gaze for a long moment. Whatever she found there seemed to satisfy some instrument of hers, some internal compass. The rifle went down across her knees—not holstered, never holstered, but down.

"All right," she said. "Then let's talk about what happens next, because I assume you've all flown or paddled a long way to tell me to run."

* * *

She had the copies hidden the way only a cartographer would hide anything: in plain geographic sight. Behind the third station of the cross in the side chapel, inside a cavity that had been hollowed and re-plastered years ago, there were oilcloth-wrapped bundles—photographs of every page, negatives sealed in film canisters, and two full duplicate sets of the boundary surveys, the falsified transect reports, the internal memos with the company letterhead that proved the northern line had been moved nine kilometers into indigenous territory and then redrawn, dated backward, witnessed by men who had never set foot on the land.

"They think there's one original," Ilsa said, laying the bundles out on the altar cloth like a surgeon laying out instruments. "Halvorsen thought it. Reyes thinks it. That's why they burned the camp—to destroy the original and call it an accident. But I've been making maps for nineteen years, and the first thing a mapmaker learns is redundancy. You never trust a single copy of anything. Not a chart, not a river, not a person."

Tam turned a photograph over in his hands with a delicacy that made Wren's chest ache. "So we take them and go. Wren has a plane. We fly out tonight, get ahead of Reyes—"

"No."

The word landed flat and final. Ilsa said it without raising her voice, which somehow made it worse.

"Ilsa—" Wren started.

"No." She gathered the photographs back toward herself, an instinctive motion, a mother hen with paper chicks. "You want to know what I've been doing for eleven days on a busted leg in a mission infirmary, besides learning to hate morphine? I've been listening to the shortwave. Father Anselmo lets me sit with it. And do you know what I've heard? Nothing. Static. Three settlements' worth of river people going about their lives on land the company claims it owns, waiting for the day the company decides to enforce its claim with the paperwork it forged. If we run—if the documents disappear into some magistrate's filing cabinet in the capital, or worse, into a safe-deposit box that gets bought and paid for—they win anyway. Slowly. Quietly. The way they always win."

"So what's your plan?" Mercer asked. "Stand on the altar and read the memos aloud until somebody important flies over?"

"Somebody doesn't need to fly over." Ilsa's fever-bright eyes swept the three of them. "Somebody needs to *listen*. Documents don't testify. People do. A photograph of a memo is ink. It can be called forged, dismissed, lost. But a living witness—several living witnesses—who will stand in a room and say *I surveyed that line, and I was ordered to move it, and here is my field book*—that's different. That's a body in the machine. They can't buy that off as easily, and they can't make it disappear as cleanly, because disappearing people makes noise, and noise is the one thing the company can't afford."

"And you're willing to be that body?" Wren asked. "You're wounded. You can barely walk. Reyes is coming—I'd bet the plane on it—and when he comes he won't be alone, and he won't be negotiating. Running isn't cowardice, Ilsa. Sometimes running is the only way the truth survives."

"Is that what you believe?" Ilsa asked, and there was no cruelty in it, only a terrible precision. "Or is that what you've had to believe, to live with the running?"

The question went into Wren like a fishhook. She stood there in the candle-smell and the dust motes and felt six years of smallness rearrange itself around the point of it.

"I ran because staying meant dying," Wren said. "Or worse. I ran because the man I testified against had friends in places I couldn't see, and the only currency I had left was anonymity. I built a whole life out of being hard to find. It worked. It worked so well that when people needed finding, there was nobody there—because I'd made sure there was no *me* there." She shook her head slowly. "I'm not telling you running is wrong. I'm telling you I know its price better than anyone in this room, and I'd pay it again. But I also know this: everything I ran from caught up with me anyway. Debt always does. Maybe the only choice we ever really get is whether we meet it standing up."

Ilsa studied her for a long moment. Outside, the cracked bell tolled vespers, and rain began on the tin roof, soft at first, then drumming.

"You sound like a woman rehearsing," Ilsa said at last. "Like someone trying a speech on for size before she has to give it for real."

"Maybe I am."

"Good." Ilsa leaned her head back against the wall and closed her eyes, and for a moment she looked simply exhausted, a hurt woman in a wet church at the end of the world. "Then here's my position, and I won't move from it. The copies travel—all of them, duplicated again, scattered, because redundancy. Tam goes with you, because he's sixteen and he's suffered enough and I will not spend his life to make my point. But I stay. I broadcast. Father Anselmo's transmitter reaches every settlement on the band, and tomorrow morning I am going to read the boundary numbers on the air, in Spanish and English, and tell every family on this river what was done to their land and where the proof lives. Let the company chase a hundred rumors instead of one woman. Let the settlers hear it from a voice, not a filing cabinet. And when the magistrate comes—because eventually someone official will have to come—I testify. In person. Under my own name, which I have never once been ashamed of."

"It'll paint a target on this mission," Mercer said quietly. "On the priest. On everyone who shelters you."

"Everything paints a target on someone," Ilsa said. "Silence just paints it slower, and wider, and on people who never got a vote." She opened her eyes and looked at each of them in turn. "You three have until dawn. Rest, eat, argue with me more if it helps you sleep. But at first light I go on the air, and after that, none of us gets to pretend this is a rescue anymore. It's a reckoning. Choose your part in it."

* * *

They ate beans and dried fish in the rectory kitchen by lamplight while the rain worked the roof. Tam fell asleep sitting up halfway through his plate, his head tipping against Wren's shoulder, the compass still clutched in one hand, and she sat very still so as not to wake him, looking at the lamplight on his sleeping face and feeling something in her chest she had spent six years successfully not feeling.

"He trusts you," Ilsa said from the doorway. She moved with a crutch now, badly, refusing help. "The boy. That's a debt heavier than any you owe Reyes."

"I know."

"Do you?" She lowered herself carefully into the chair opposite, laid the crutch aside, and for a moment the defiance drained out of her and left just a tired woman with a splinted leg. "When I was twenty-three I mapped a flood zone in another country, and the government's numbers were wrong, and I knew they were wrong, and I signed the report anyway because I had a mother to support and a career to protect. Forty-one people drowned the following spring. I've spent my whole life since making maps that couldn't lie. It doesn't balance. Nothing balances. You just decide which direction you're going to lean." She looked at Tam, asleep against Wren's shoulder. "Lean right, Nakamura. Whatever else you do."

Wren was still turning that over when Father Anselmo appeared in the doorway, an old man shaped like a question mark, holding the shortwave handset with both hands as though it were a communion cup.

"Señora Voss," he said gently. "There is traffic on the band. A charter flight, filed for the missionary strip at Boca Chica—twenty minutes north of us. It requests weather." His old eyes were troubled. "The voice on the radio. I have heard it before. It belongs to the man who visits the company office in Puerto Cielo."

The rain hammered. Tam stirred against Wren's shoulder. Ilsa reached for her rifle.

"Well," she said. "It seems the reckoning is coming to us after all."
