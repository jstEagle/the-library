## 14. Betrayal at the Mission

Mercer had made a hundred deals in his life and regretted maybe ninety of them, but he had a system for the other ten: never think about them. Thinking was the tax you paid on conscience, and he'd stopped paying taxes years ago.

He told himself this one was different. This one wasn't even a betrayal, not really. All he did was key the company frequency at dusk, from the treeline behind the mission's drying shed, where the shortwave antenna Ilsa had rigged wouldn't pick up his transmission—she'd built it to broadcast out, not to listen in. He gave Reyes three things: the mission's coordinates, the number of people inside, and the fact that Voss was alive.

That was all. A location. A headcount. A name.

The voice on the other end was smooth as river mud. *Understood, Mr. Mercer. Stand by. No action required of you.* And then, because the company man always wanted to remind you what you were: *Your account will be settled.*

Settled. He turned the word over as he walked back through the wet grass, mosquitoes stitching whines around his ears. Settled meant money. Settled meant the debt ledger in some office in Puerto Cielo with his name on it finally balanced, closed, forgotten. Six years of contracts and quiet jobs and looking the other way, all of it converging on this one clean transaction, and then he could go somewhere the rain didn't fall sideways and buy a life that belonged to him.

Inside the courtyard, lamplight spilled from the refectory window. Tam sat on the mission steps with the compass open in his palm, showing Wren something about the needle, and Wren was laughing—actually laughing, a short rough sound like she'd forgotten the mechanism and it had surprised her by working. Ilsa stood in the doorway with her arm still bound against her chest, arguing cheerfully with the old priest about whether the radio mast violated some ecclesiastical principle or other.

Mercer stopped at the edge of the light and looked at them for a long moment.

*No action required of you.*

He went and sat alone by the well and cleaned a gun that didn't need cleaning until the lamp in the refectory went out.

* * *

Wren slept badly, which was normal, but she woke wrong, which was not.

It wasn't a sound exactly. It was the absence of one—the frogs along the mission's fishpond had gone silent, all of them at once, the way they did when something large moved through the dark with intent. She lay on the cot in the cell the priest had given her and listened with her whole body, the way you listened to an engine when the gauges said fine but your hands knew better.

Then she heard it: an engine. High, thin, descending. Not hers.

She was up and moving before the thought finished forming, bare feet on cool stone, down the corridor to the room where Tam slept. The boy was already awake, sitting up, eyes huge in the dark. "I heard it," he whispered.

"I know." She put a hand on his shoulder—just briefly, just enough—and kept moving. "Get Ilsa. Get the documents. Don't turn on any lights."

The courtyard was silver-gray with pre-dawn. The mission had been built two centuries ago by men who expected sieges, and its walls were thick and its gate was iron-banded, but nobody had expected the siege to come from the sky. The engine sound circled once, north of the compound, and cut out over the old logging road—the flat stretch there was barely long enough, which told her the pilot was either very good or very careless, and company charters didn't hire careless.

Fourteen minutes, she estimated. Maybe less if they jogged.

She found Mercer already at the gate, rifle slung, standing in the gray light like a man waiting for a bus. That was her first warning. Her second was that he wouldn't look at her.

"You knew," she said.

His jaw worked. "Wren—"

"How long?"

"Last night." He said it fast, the way you pull a bandage. "I called it in. Coordinates, headcount, that Voss was alive. That's all I gave them. They said no action—I was supposed to stand aside while they negotiated. That was the deal. Nobody was supposed to get hurt."

"The deal," Wren repeated. The word tasted like bilge water. "You've been sitting at our fire for three days."

"And eating your food, yeah." His voice cracked into something rawer than cynicism. "You think I don't know what I am? I've been the company's dog so long I forgot there was anything else to be. This was my way out. One call, one payment, and I'm done with them forever." He finally looked at her, and what was in his face wasn't defiance. It was worse. It was hope, ugly and dying. "They were supposed to talk, Wren. Reyes doesn't shoot people. He buys them. That's the whole point of Reyes."

Somewhere beyond the wall, a branch snapped under a boot.

Wren's blood went cold and then colder. "How many did they send?"

"He didn't say. But he didn't charter a plane for a negotiation team of two."

* * *

The first shot came through the gate's iron bands at 5:12 by the chapel clock, and it took the top hinge off the door and killed a hundred years of silence in the same instant.

What followed was chaos in the specific flavor Wren knew too well: shouting in the refectory, the priest's voice rising in protest, Ilsa's answering bark, the crack-crack-crack of rifles from the treeline firing not at targets but at the building itself—suppression, her mind supplied usefully, they want us pinned while they breach. She grabbed Tam by the collar and hauled him off the steps as a round sparked off the stone where his head had been.

"Courtyard wall!" she shouted over the noise. "There's a drainage grate behind the kitchen—"

"There isn't time." It was Mercer, sliding down beside them behind the well, rifle up. Blood ran from a graze along his scalp; he didn't seem to notice it. "They'll have the back covered. Listen—" A burst chewed the well's lip above them, raining grit. "Listen to me. There are six of them, maybe seven. Reyes stayed with the plane. They'll rush the gate on my signal—they think I'm calling them in."

Wren stared at him. "You're going to walk out there."

"I'm going to walk out there and tell them the deal changed." He checked his magazine with a motion so practiced it was almost gentle. "And when they decide the deal changed for them instead, I'm going to make them regret being right about me for six years." He looked at Tam then, and something in his face did the thing Wren had spent her whole adult life refusing to look at in mirrors. "Kid. You keep your head down and you keep breathing. That's an order from the security chief of the Halvorsen expedition, and it outranks everything."

"You're coming back," Tam said. It wasn't a question. Sixteen-year-olds always said it like it wasn't a question.

"Yeah," Mercer said, and stood up into the gunfire.

* * *

Later, Wren would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory broke them into shards.

Mercer walking toward the gate with his rifle slung and both hands visible, shouting *Hold your fire, hold your fire, it's Mercer, the deal stands*—and the shooting stuttering, uncertain, because the company men knew his voice the way dogs know their handler's whistle.

Ilsa dragging herself across the courtyard with the document case strapped to her chest like a breastplate, screaming at the priest to get the children who boarded here down into the crypt.

Tam, breaking from cover—not toward the drainage grate, toward the gate, because Mercer had taught him how to clear a jammed weapon two nights ago and the boy had decided, with the magnificent stupidity of sixteen, that he owed the man a loaded rifle.

And Wren running after him through the smoke and the ringing air, knowing she couldn't reach him, knowing it the way you know a storm cell is between you and home.

The company men opened fire instead of negotiating.

It happened almost politely. Mercer reached the gate, said something—Wren never learned what, some last version of the truth—and the answer came as a full-auto burst from two rifles at once, the kind of response you give not to a threat but to an inconvenience. He turned, not away from the bullets but toward the courtyard, toward Tam frozen in the open twenty meters behind him, and his body made the decision his conscience had been circling for six years. He took the rounds across the shoulders and back, a human wall in a doorway built of nothing, and folded down onto the flagstones with a sound Wren felt in her teeth.

Tam screamed. Wren got an arm around the boy's chest and dragged him backward as rounds chased them across the courtyard, chipping stone, splintering the well's wooden roof. Ilsa appeared out of the smoke like vengeance in a sling, hauling the gate bar down with her good arm, and the priest's deep voice rolled out over the compound—words in the river tongue, then Spanish, then plain furious English: *This is a house of God and you are shooting at it,* and absurdly, horribly, the firing slackened, because even hired men hesitate at churches.

They got Mercer behind the refectory wall. He was conscious, which Wren hadn't dared hope, and gray, which she had. Two rounds high in the back, one through the meat of the shoulder. The priest knelt over him doing things with cloth and prayer in equal measure.

"Plane," Mercer rasped, catching Wren's wrist. His grip was still strong. Everything else about him was leaking away. "Reyes is at the plane. He won't leave without the documents—he can't, it's his neck too now. Which means—" a wet cough, "—which means he needs you to stay put. Don't stay put."

"We're not leaving you."

"You're not leaving *him*," Mercer said, jerking his chin at Tam, whose face was wrecked and set at the same time. "Both of you. Voss. The papers. Get to the strip before they regroup. I bought you the gate, I'll buy you the—" His eyes lost focus, came back. "Tell the kid... tell him the compass thing. About true north. Tell him it was..."

He passed out mid-sentence, which was probably mercy, because Wren suspected the end of that sentence was going to be sentimental.

* * *

Outside the wall, the company men regrouped in the tree shadows, and Wren stood at the refectory window with Ilsa and watched the smoke drift and did arithmetic.

"They'll wait for full light," Ilsa said quietly. Her bound arm made her voice tight. "They lost the initiative and they know the priest will have radioed every settlement within a hundred klicks by noon. Reyes has hours, not days. He'll come at the front gate hard, and meanwhile someone's circling around to the airstrip."

"To my plane."

"To your plane." Ilsa turned and looked at her, and in the lamplight her face was terrible and calm. "So we have a choice, pilot. We run for the swamp and hide, and the documents die with us eventually, or we run for the strip and race them to it."

Wren looked down at the courtyard—at the priest kneeling over Mercer, at Tam crouched beside them pressing a fresh bandage down with both hands because somebody had to and he had decided it was him. Three days ago she'd have been calculating wind loading and fuel burn. Now she was calculating something else entirely, something with no gauge on the panel.

"The strip," she said.

Ilsa smiled with half her mouth. "I hoped you'd say that."

From the infirmary, faint but clear, came Tam's voice—reading aloud, steady as a metronome, the way you talked to keep a man anchored: *"True north, kid. The needle lies a little everywhere on earth, but the compass knows where home is if you correct for it..."*

Wren picked up the document case and headed for the gate.

Behind them, at the edge of the trees, a second engine started up—lighter, faster, turning toward the airstrip.
