## 15. Grounded

Wren heard the plane before she saw it—the wrong engine note, too high and thin for a Beaver, and her stomach dropped through the mission's stone floor before her mind caught up.

She was in the courtyard, hauling water, when the sound crossed the ridge to the east. Not Reyes's charter. Something smaller. A Cessna 206, maybe, running rough on purpose the way hired men run things when they want you to hear them coming.

"Inside," she said, and didn't wait to see if Tam obeyed.

He did. That was the thing about the boy—sixteen years old, half-starved three weeks in a cave, and he moved like someone who had already learned that obedience was sometimes love. He grabbed Ilsa under the arm and steered her toward the chapel while Wren stood in the open with water slopping over her boots, watching the sky.

The 206 came over the treeline low, gear down, and made one slow pass along the grass strip where the Otter sat tied down at the edge of the mission compound. It didn't land. It just looked. Then it climbed away south, trailing its ragged song, and was gone into the cloud deck.

"They're marking us," Mercer said from the doorway of the dormitory. His shoulder was still bound from the bullet at the courtyard, his arm in a sling made from a bedsheet, but his eyes were doing their cold arithmetic. "Recon pass. They'll be back with more."

"Then we move now." Wren dropped the bucket. "Ilsa can't walk far. We take the boat—"

"Wren." Mercer's voice was flat as a machete laid on a table. "Look at your plane."

She turned.

Two men were standing beside the Otter. They must have come up the river trail while everyone watched the sky—a feint, clean and professional—and they weren't hurrying. One of them poured something from a jerrican over the cowling. The other swung a machete, once, twice, three times, with the unhurried rhythm of a man chopping firewood, and even from across the courtyard Wren could hear the sound it made going into an engine: not metal-on-metal but metal-into-metal, a wet heavy thunk like an axe finding heartwood.

"No," she said. The word came out small. She was already running.

They heard her coming and didn't run. That was the worst part. They finished—one of them emptied the rest of the jerrican into the open cowl, and fuel fumes rolled out sweet and poisonous—and then they walked away up the strip, unhurried, and lifted two fingers off their brows in something that wasn't quite a salute. A message, delivered by hand: *we could have taken you too. We didn't need to.*

By the time she reached the plane they were gone into the trees.

The cowling hung open. The fuel was everywhere, soaking the tie-down ropes, pooling under the fuselage in rainbow skins. And inside the cowl, where her engine had lived for eleven years—through storms and overload runs and one dead-stick landing on the Esmeralda bar that still visited her dreams—the block was ruined. The machete had gone through the crankcase twice. She could see daylight through her own engine.

Her hands were shaking. She put them flat on the warm aluminum of the cowl and let them shake there, alone, because no one else was close enough to see.

Eleven years. The Otter had been the last honest thing she owned, the one object that had never asked her name.

* * *

They gathered in the chapel because it had walls and a roof and because Father Anselmo, the mission's one ancient priest, had retreated to his cell with his rosary and the wisdom of a man who had survived forty years on this river by knowing which arguments were not his.

Ilsa lay on a cot near the altar, her leg splinted, her face gray but her eyes lit with the particular fury Wren was beginning to understand was simply how the woman metabolized fear. Tam sat on the floor at her feet, knees drawn up. Mercer stood by the door, watching the courtyard through the gap.

"So," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Options."

"None," said Mercer.

"Thank you. Next."

"The boat," Tam offered. "We got here by boat."

"The river's watched. They marked the strip because they know we can't fly, which means they know we'll try the water, which means the water has men on it." Wren dragged a hand through her hair. "And even if we beat them downstream, then what? Every radio in Puerto Cielo is company property or company-bought. Reyes owns the air. We'd arrive with documents nobody's allowed to believe."

"Then we don't arrive," Ilsa said.

Everyone looked at her.

"We make the river come to *us*." She pushed herself up on one elbow, wincing, and pointed at the shortwave set on the table beneath the window—an old Collins rig, brass-faced, older than any of them, the mission's lifeline to the outside world. "Anselmo broadcasts scripture readings every Sunday. Half the settlements up and down this band keep their sets tuned because it's the only voice that isn't selling them something. I've been listening to that schedule since I was a girl at my father's station." Her mouth curved, sharp. "Sunday is tomorrow."

"You're going to read survey coordinates instead of scripture," Wren said slowly.

"I'm going to read everything." Ilsa's voice cracked across the chapel like a whip. "Every falsified boundary line. Every date. Every signature. The whole ledger of what that company stole and who signed off on stealing it. Coordinates mean nothing to most people—but names mean something. The names of the men who took the bribes. The name of the magistrate who rubber-stamped the first claim. People will listen to that whether they understand the maps or not, because people always listen when you tell them who robbed them."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a bird screamed and was answered.

"And then what?" Mercer asked, and for once his voice wasn't mocking. It was almost gentle, which was worse. "You broadcast the truth to forty fishing camps and a mission. Reyes flies in with twelve armed men and burns this place down around your ears. Documents don't testify, Ilsa. You told me that yourself."

"I told you documents don't testify," she agreed. "*People* do. But people testify better when a thousand ears have already heard the truth. When every settler on this river knows what's coming before Reyes can shape the story. When the magistrate in Puerto Cielo wakes up tomorrow knowing that if he buries this, four hundred people heard the evidence first and will remember he buried it." She looked around the room, and her gaze snagged on Wren's. "You want to disappear. I understand disappearing. But disappearing only works while nobody's looking. Tomorrow, everybody looks."

Wren stood very still. Outside the window, her dead plane sat in the sun like a beached whale, and the thought that had been circling her all morning finally landed.

"It doesn't matter what we broadcast," she said, "if we're still here when they come back. Which means the plane matters. The plane is the only way any of us live past Sunday afternoon."

Mercer laughed once, without humor. "The plane is scrap. I watched them kill it."

"You watched them kill the engine." Wren walked to the window. The Otter sat fifty meters away, and behind it, half-hidden under a tarp against the mission's workshop wall, sat Father Anselmo's other inheritance: a 1940s-era Noorduyn Norseman, bought surplus decades ago, cannibalized over the years for parts, sitting on flat tires under bird droppings and thirty years of rain. She'd noticed it her first day here and dismissed it the way you dismiss a grave marker.

But a Norseman and an Otter were both radial engines. Different makes, different years, but radials shared a grammar. And a man at the mission in San Tomás—old Mateo, who owed her for three years of medicine runs—had a warehouse of war-surplus parts and no questions.

"Anselmo's Norseman," she said. "It's a wreck, but wrecks are just airplanes waiting for donors. The Otter's mags might be salvageable. The prop is probably fine. Tires, hoses, the battery out of the mission truck. If Mateo will sell me a cylinder set and a carburetor—" She stopped, recalculating, hating the number she arrived at. "Even then it's three days' work. Maybe four. With two people who know engines."

"I know engines," Tam said.

Everyone turned. The boy flushed but didn't drop his gaze. "Radio tech means power plants, generators, compressors. My dad rebuilt a Ford trimotor when I was twelve. I held every wrench he handed me." His jaw set. "And I'm not staying here again. Last time I hid, everyone I knew walked into the forest and didn't come back. I'm done hiding."

Wren looked at him—at the hollows under his eyes, the compass hanging at his throat, the stubborn line of his mouth that was so much like his father's—and felt something in her chest rearrange itself, painfully, into a shape she recognized. It was the shape of having something to lose.

"All right," she said. "Tam, you're on the engine with me. Mercer—you can hold a wrench one-handed, and more importantly, you know how Reyes's people think. You're our lookout and our liar-in-residence; if anyone comes up the river, you buy us minutes however you have to." She met his eyes. "Can you do that? After the courtyard?"

Something moved behind Mercer's face—the installment-plan conscience, another payment coming due. "After the courtyard," he said quietly, "I owe them nothing and you everything. Yes. I can do that."

"Ilsa." Wren turned to the cot. "Tomorrow, noon. You get one hour of airtime before Reyes's operator in Puerto Cielo starts jamming or comes upriver. Can you say what needs saying in an hour?"

Ilsa smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent six weeks in a vault memorizing other people's crimes. "I've been rehearsing my whole life," she said. "Get me a script and a pencil."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the blue dark, and the work was a kind of prayer.

Wren pulled the tarp off the Norseman and coughed through the dust of decades. The fuselage was sound—that was the miracle of old bush planes, built in an era when aluminum was religion—but the engine was a tomb. Two cylinders corroded to lace. The carburetor full of dried mud where a bird had nested and died. The tires rotted soft as overripe fruit. She inventoried it aloud, Tam scribbling the list on the back of a hymnal page, and the list was long enough to be a eulogy.

But the Otter gave and gave. Its magneto came off clean. Its propeller, unscarred by the machete, matched within an inch. Its battery, its oil lines, its spare spark plugs, the coil of safety wire in her maintenance kit—all of it crossed the courtyard in the arms of a boy who ran the trips like he'd been born to ferrying, which, Wren supposed, in a way he had.

By full dark they had the Norseman's cowl off and the bad cylinders exposed to the moonlight, and Wren sat on the wheel chock with grease to her elbows, eating cold rice, and let herself feel the despair fully for exactly one minute. She timed it on her watch. Sixty seconds to look at the impossible distance between here and flying, between here and alive. Then she closed the watch and stood up.

"How much is left?" Tam asked.

"Everything," she said. "That's the good news. Everything left means nothing's been wasted yet."

He grinned—first grin she'd seen on him since the cave—and went back to cleaning spark plugs with gasoline and a toothbrush.

Above them, in the chapel window, a lamp burned late. Through the glass Wren could see Ilsa bent over the table, writing, crossing out, writing again, Anselmo's shortwave humming softly beside her like a sleeping animal. Once, the cartographer looked up and caught Wren looking, and raised her pencil in a small salute.

*We are going to tell everyone,* that gesture said. *Everything. Are you ready?*

Wren turned back to the engine. No. She was not ready. She would never be ready. But the cylinders needed honing and the night was short, and somewhere south, Reyes was counting his men and his hours, certain that a grounded woman was a caught woman.

Let him count. By Sunday noon, the whole river would be listening—and by whatever dawn mercy granted them, Wren Nakamura intended to be in the air, carrying the truth home on wings assembled from scraps, prayers, and parts.

She picked up the hone and went back to work.
