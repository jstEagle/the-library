## 16. Night Work

The mission's generator coughed itself out at nine o'clock, and after that the only light in the world was the one Wren made herself.

She'd rigged it from a truck battery and a headlamp lamp with the strap cut off, wired to a toggle switch she'd salvaged from the De Havilland's dead cabin panel years ago. It threw a hard white circle on the hangar floor, and inside that circle sat the airplane's belly, opened like a patient: cowling off, access panels down, the punctured fuel line lifted out of its channel and clamped in a vise she'd bolted to a workbench that had been old when the missionaries were young.

The hole was small. A rifle round had gone through the line where it ran along the spar root—through and through, clean entry, ugly exit—and the company men who'd done it had been thorough about everything else. Tires slashed. Magneto leads pulled. The battery terminals wrapped in a rag soaked in something that had eaten the copper green. But they hadn't known airplanes, not really. They'd shot the plane like they'd shoot a man, center mass, and a man would have died. The Otter just bled.

Wren fed solder to the joint and watched it flow, silver-bright, into the seam. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the one inheritance from her father that had never once betrayed her.

Outside, the night was enormous. Rain had come through at dusk and moved off downstream, leaving the air scrubbed and cool for the first time in days, and now the mission compound lay under a sky so crowded with stars it looked less like night than like some vast interior of a cathedral seen from underneath. The river spoke to itself beyond the tree line. Somewhere a dog turned over and went back to sleep. Somewhere closer, in the mission's main room, Ilsa Voss was sitting up with a shortwave set and a list of frequencies, because tomorrow they would need every friend the river had ever made.

Tonight there was only this: a woman, a lamp, a wound in a machine, and the work of closing it.

* * *

She heard Ilsa before she saw her—the uneven footfall, left leg stiff, the cane she refused to use replaced by a machete handle cut to length. Then the lamplight found her at the edge of its circle, and Wren tipped her chin toward an oil drum turned on end across the bench.

"Sit if you want. Fair warning, the conversation's mostly me swearing at brass fittings."

"I've heard worse conversations." Ilsa lowered herself onto the drum with the care of someone whose body had become a ledger of debts owed to it. She was fifty-something and built like the maps she drew—all long lines and hard corners—and even sitting still she had the quality of a person who was paying attention to everything. Her wounded thigh was bound fresh; Tam had changed the dressing an hour ago with the grave tenderness of a boy who had learned too young what wounds meant. "How bad is the line?"

"Bad enough that they thought it would ground me. Not bad enough that they were right." Wren held the joint over the flame again, watched the solder take. "Whoever trained them in sabotage should be embarrassed."

"They weren't trained to stop you permanently." Ilsa said it without drama, the way she said everything, as though she were reading coordinates off a chart. "They were trained to slow you down until Reyes arrived with more men. There's a difference, and the difference is that they expect you to fail tonight."

"Then let's disappoint them." Wren set the torch down, flexed her fingers. The joint cooled, dulling from silver to gray. She ran her thumb along it, feeling for the flaw that wasn't there. Good work. Work she could bet three lives on.

Ilsa watched her do it. "You trust your hands," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"My hands have never lied to me. Can't say that about much else."

"No," Ilsa said. "Neither can I."

* * *

The silence between them was comfortable in the way silences between working people are comfortable—two people occupied with the same night, the same small light, the same ticking clock. Wren cleaned the line with emery cloth, fitted it back toward its channel, began the slow business of re-seating the clamps. Her mind worked ahead of her hands the way it always did: fuel flow, pressure, the burn rate at gross weight with three aboard, the strip length here versus the strip length at Puerto Cielo, the wind that came off the water at dawn and which way it would push or punish her.

"You're calculating," Ilsa observed.

"Always calculating."

"So am I. It's why we're going to get out of here." Ilsa leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied the open belly of the plane with a cartographer's eye—as if she were surveying it, as if she might draw it later, true to scale. "Can I ask you something? You can refuse. Most pilots I've met treat questions like weather—they acknowledge them and fly around."

Wren snorted despite herself. "Ask."

"The name. *Zephyr.* Everyone downriver calls you Zephyr. The manifest at Puerto Cielo says Zephyr Vasquez. But Tam told me—" she paused, choosing the words with the precision she chose all words, "—Tam told me that when he asked you once, years ago apparently, whether Zephyr was your given name, you said, 'It's the name I gave myself when the other one got heavy.'"

The emery cloth stopped moving.

"He remembers everything," Wren said. "That's going to make him either a great radio man or a terrible houseguest."

"It made him a survivor," Ilsa said quietly. "He hid in that cave for eleven days because he remembered everything—every schedule, every frequency, every promise anyone ever made him. He remembered that you promised to come back for the expedition, and so he stayed near the camp where you'd look first." She let that sit. "So. Was it heavy, the other name?"

Wren picked up the torch, lit it, didn't use it. Just held the small blue flame and looked at it, because looking at fire was easier than looking at people when the answer mattered.

"There was a girl," she said finally, "who grew up in a town you won't find on any map you've drawn, because it stopped existing in every way except paperwork. Her father taught her engines. Her mother taught her that you pay your debts or the debts start paying you, and they collect in interest you don't want to imagine. And when she was twenty-four, she did something—" Wren turned the flame down, smaller, tighter. "There was a charter flight. A man who owned half the fishing rights on the coast wanted a rival's cargo delayed, and he paid well, and the girl needed money for her mother's hospital bills, and she told herself it was just a delay. Just paperwork. Just a radio call that never got made."

"What happened?"

"The cargo was insulin. Cold-chain medicine for a village clinic upriver from here, actually—different river, same country, same kind of country. The delay was six days. Three people died who wouldn't have." Wren said it flatly, the way you say things you have said to yourself so many times the words have worn smooth as river stone. "Nobody ever traced it to her. That was the worst part. She got away with it completely. And she discovered that getting away with something is not the same as being free of it. So she buried the girl and became a wind—a thing with no weight, no history, no debts. Zephyr. Fast, directionless, touching nothing."

Ilsa was silent for a long moment. In the dark beyond the lamp circle, the river kept talking.

"And the debt to Reyes?" she asked. "Is that the same debt?"

"No. That one's simpler. Six years ago I flew a load I shouldn't have for a man I shouldn't have trusted, got caught in a storm, put down hard on his employer's strip and tore up their fence and their pride. He bought the paper on my repairs and has been collecting ever since. Money debt. Ugly but honest." She shook her head. "The other debt doesn't have a number on it. That's the problem with it. You can't pay off a debt with no principal. You just keep paying interest forever."

"Until you do something else with it," Ilsa said.

Wren looked up. The lamplight caught the older woman's face—deep lines, a healing cut along the jaw from the vault collapse, eyes that had spent thirty years drawing boundaries and had finally drawn one she was willing to die on.

"Say what you mean, Ilsa."

"I mean this." Ilsa gestured—not at the plane, not at the mission, but at the whole dark continent of the night around them, the river, the settlements, the scattered fires of people who by morning would know what the company had done. "Documents don't testify. People do. I've been telling you that since the cave, and you keep nodding like you agree while planning your exit. And I understand the plan. Believe me—I lived invisible for two years in Puerto Cielo before Halvorsen hired me. I know exactly how sweet it is, being no one. No one can hurt no one. No one owes anything." Her voice roughened. "But no one also can't stand up in a room and say *I saw the boundary stakes moved. I flew the falsified surveys. I know.* Only a person can do that. A named person."

"A named person gets destroyed," Wren said. "Reyes will bury me. The company will bury me. My past gives them everything they need—criminal record, false identity, a woman with no legal existence flying their skies. The moment I say my real name out loud, I hand them the knife."

"Yes," Ilsa agreed placidly. "You do."

"That's your argument? *Yes, you do?*"

"My argument is that you're already holding the knife." Ilsa leaned forward, and the lamplight moved with her, and for a moment her shadow fell across the airplane's open belly like a wing. "Wren. May I use that name? It's the one you wear, so it seems polite to ask." She didn't wait for permission. "For six years you have been paying interest on a debt with no principal. Every favor for Reyes, every run you hated, every year of being small—that was interest. Tomorrow, if we get out of here, you have a chance to pay the principal. All of it. One payment. It costs you your name, your anonymity, maybe your license, maybe your freedom for a while." She held up a hand before Wren could speak. "And in exchange you get the only thing that has ever actually been for sale in this whole rotten country at a fair price: you get to be *real*. On the record. In the light. With witnesses."

"The witnesses are farmers and fishermen with hunting rifles."

"The witnesses," Ilsa said, "are everyone whose land the company stole, which is to say most of the river, which is to say more people than the company can buy, intimidate, or shoot. I broadcast to forty-one settlements last night. Forty-one. Do you know what I heard back? Static, mostly. And then, one by one, voices. A schoolteacher in San Fermin. A fisherman's cooperative at the delta. Two elders from the villages whose boundary lines the company moved—villages that have been waiting thirty years for someone to draw the map *honestly*." She smiled then, and it was not a gentle smile; it was the smile of a woman who had found the flaw in the terrain and intended to exploit it. "The company owns the law, Wren. They own Reyes and the magistrate's cousin and probably the magistrate. What they do not own, and cannot own, is the truth in enough mouths at once. That's what testimony is. Not one voice. A chorus with a paper trail."

Wren turned back to the engine. Picked up the clamp wrench. Set it down again. Her hands, for the first time in six years, were not entirely steady, and she found she didn't hate it.

"If I testify," she said slowly, "I testify under my real name. All of it. The insulin, the false papers, everything. Ilsa, I'm not a clean witness. I'm a criminal testifying against worse criminals, and they'll make the courtroom smell that."

"Good," said Ilsa.

"*Good?*"

"Good. Because a clean witness is a story, and stories can be dismissed. But a woman who stands up in front of God and the magistrate and says *here is what I did wrong, and here is what they did worse, and I am willing to be punished for mine if they are punished for theirs*—" Ilsa shook her head. "No lawyer in the hemisphere knows what to do with that. It's the one thing money can't cross-examine."

Wren laughed—a short, startled sound, half disbelief. It echoed off the hangar tin roof and died in the dark.

"You know what my mother used to say?" she said. "'Debts pay you back in interest you don't want.' She meant it as a warning. Stay small, stay square, stay safe." She looked down at her hands, at the solder scar on her thumb she'd carried since she was nine years old, since the first engine she'd rebuilt with her father standing behind her saying *again, slower, do it until your hands believe it.* "But I've been thinking, these last few days—maybe she had it backwards. Maybe the only way to stop paying interest is to walk into the bank and put everything on the table. Even the ugly parts. *Especially* the ugly parts."

"Now you sound like a woman making a decision," Ilsa said.

"I am." Wren took a breath, and it went deep, deeper than breath usually went, all the way down to some sealed compartment she hadn't opened in six years. "My name is Marisol Elena Nakamura-Vasquez. My father was Kenji Nakamura, an engine mechanic from Callao, and my mother was Rosa Vasquez, and they named me after my grandmother, and my grandmother grew tomatoes and argued with priests, and none of those people ever did a single thing they needed to hide from." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the last part. "Marisol. Sea and sun. She named me for open water and clear sky, and I spent six years trying to be fog instead."

Ilsa reached over and laid her hand—dry, strong, map-callused—over Wren's forearm. She didn't say congratulations, or good, or welcome back. She just held on for a moment, the way you hold a compass needle steady while it settles, and then let go.

"Marisol," she said, testing it, giving it back. "It suits you better than the wind did."

"Don't spread it around until Puerto Cielo," Wren said, and her voice was thick and she didn't care. "I want to say it myself. Out loud. In the room. First."

"Understood." Ilsa rose from the drum, joints cracking, and stood a moment looking at the opened airplane with something close to affection. "Finish your line. I'll finish my broadcasts. And Marisol—" she paused at the edge of the lamplight, half in, half out, "—when this is over, I'd like to draw a proper chart of the coast where you grew up. The town that isn't on the maps. Everything should be on the maps. That's rather the point of the whole affair."

Then she was gone, machete-handle cane tapping, toward the shortwave's blue glow in the mission window, and Wren was alone with the lamp and the plane and the name sitting in her chest like a warm coal.

* * *

She worked until nearly three.

Fuel line seated and safetied. Clamps torqued. New battery cables spliced from wire she stripped out of the mission's ancient well pump, terminals scraped bright and coated in petroleum jelly against the damp. The magneto leads reconnected, the tires—there was nothing to be done about the tires except fill the slash-cuts with plugs and rubber cement and pray, which she did, both literally and mechanically, pressing patch after patch into the gashes while the river murmured and the stars wheeled and somewhere in the mission Ilsa's voice went out over the bands in three languages, calm as a lighthouse, calling the river to witness.

At some point Tam appeared in the doorway barefoot, hair wild, drawn by the light. He didn't ask to help. He just picked up the second wrench and held the far end of the cowl brace while she worked, and when she glanced at him he shrugged and said, "Couldn't sleep. Radio's keeping Dad's frequency open, in case." His father's compass hung around his neck on its lanyard, catching the lamplight. The same model as hers. The same stubborn needle.

"Hey, Zeph," he said, as they tightened the last fitting together. "When we get to Puerto Cielo. When you talk to the magistrate." He looked down, scuffed his toe. "You'll tell them about the cave, right? How I hid? Not the scared parts. Just—so it's in the record that I stayed."

Wren put her hand on the back of his neck, briefly, the way her father used to.

"In the record," she said. "Every word. Starting with my real name."

His head came up. She watched him understand, watched sixteen years of intelligence assemble it, and the grin that broke across his face was the first unguarded thing she'd seen him do since the cave.

"Marisol," he tried, and then again, delighted, like a new frequency he'd just tuned. "Marisol. Okay. Yeah. That's—okay."

"Go to sleep, Tam."

He went. She stayed.

* * *

By four-thirty the Otter was whole again—or as whole as plug-filled tires and a patched fuel line and borrowed wiring could make her—and Wren sat on the oil drum in the dark with the lamp off, letting her eyes adjust, letting the night come all the way in. The pre-dawn had that particular quality it got in river country: the black going gray at the eastern edge, the birds gathering their arguments, the air lying flat and cool on the grass strip like a held breath.

In an hour, maybe two, Reyes would come. Ilsa's last broadcast had been heard in Puerto Cielo—she was certain of it, certain the company man was already moving, already angry, already past the point where negotiation lived. Whatever happened next would happen fast, and loud, and at the edge of this quiet strip of grass.

Wren turned her father's compass over in her hands. The needle swung, wavered, settled—pointing not away, not along any escape vector, but north-northeast, toward Puerto Cielo, toward the magistrate's courtroom, toward the room where a woman named Marisol Elena Nakamura-Vasquez intended to stand up and say her own name out loud for the first time in six years.

Down the strip, in the gray beginning of morning, a vehicle's headlights came around the bend of the mission road and started toward them, low beams off, running dark.

Wren stood up, closed the cowling, and latched it.
