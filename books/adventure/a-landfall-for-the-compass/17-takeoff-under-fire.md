## 17. Takeoff Under Fire

The fuel line held.

Wren had checked it four times through the night, running her thumb along the solder seam like a rosary, and now in the first gray light it sat in its coupling without weeping a single drop. The mission's mechanic—a Guatemalan brother named Esteban who smelled of incense and carburetor cleaner—had given her a length of copper tubing, a fistful of clamps, and the only soldering iron within a hundred miles of the river. What he hadn't given her was sleep, and neither had the work, and neither had the knowledge that somewhere down the road, Reyes's men were regrouping around the two trucks whose headlights she'd watched crawl away at midnight like patient predators deciding to be patient a little longer.

She stood at the wing of the De Havilland and watched the mist come off the grass strip in slow ropes. The Beaver sat where she'd left it, nose toward the open field, fuel tanks topped from the mission's ancient drum stock. Ilsa was already aboard, strapped into the jump seat with the document case locked across her lap like a sleeping child, her wounded shoulder bound tight enough that Wren could see the pain in the set of her jaw and the refusal in everything else. Tam crouched by the tail wheel, running the pre-flight with the checklist in his shaking hands, doing it right, doing it twice, because his father had taught him that and because doing things right was the only prayer a sixteen-year-old radio tech had left.

Mercer stood at the edge of the strip where the grass gave way to the red clay of the mission road, and he was looking at the road the way other men looked at their own graves.

"You're not coming," Wren said. She hadn't asked. She'd known since two in the morning, when he'd taken the mission's hunting rifle down from above the chapel door and checked the action with the tenderness of a man greeting an old enemy. Some decisions you could smell before you could hear them spoken.

"Road comes up from the south through the banana fields," Mercer said, not turning. "Single approach. If I'm sitting at the bend with this rifle and the mission's other gun, nobody drives that road fast. Nobody drives it at all, if they're smart. Company men are many things, Nakamura, but they didn't hire me for being stupid." He shrugged, a small motion in the gray light. "They hired me for being exactly this."

"There's a seat."

"There's a seat full of documents that matter more than I do." He turned then, and in the half-dark his face was just a suggestion—a jaw, a scar, eyes that had learned to calculate and were, for once, tired of the answer. "I did the math a long time ago. Sold it by installments, remember? This is the last payment. It clears the account."

"Mercer—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't make it a speech. I've sat through enough speeches at enough campfires to know they're just people talking so they don't have to say goodbye properly. Say it properly. Say *we'll send someone back for you*, and mean it, and then get in the plane."

The mist was burning off. That was the thing about dawn in the river country—it didn't give you time. The light came up like a tide, and whatever you were going to do, you did it inside of twenty minutes or you did it in front of witnesses.

Wren said, "We'll send someone back for you. The whole river, if we have to. You heard Ilsa's broadcast. Half the settlements on the band are awake and angry, and angry river people travel."

"Then I'll try to leave some of them alive to greet you." Almost a smile. It didn't survive. "Go. Now. Before the light finishes coming up and they finish deciding."

* * *

Tam wouldn't stop looking at him.

Wren watched it from the cockpit as she ran the engine checks—mixture rich, prop full forward, magnetos hot—the boy twisted around in the second seat, his face pressed against the window frame, his father's compass clutched in one fist so hard the brass had to be leaving marks in his palm. Mercer stood at the road bend now, a hundred meters off, a shape in the mist with a rifle, and Tam was looking at him the way you look at a door closing.

"He's coming back," Tam said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't a statement. It was a boy demanding the universe sign a contract.

"Get your harness tight," Wren said.

"He's coming *back*."

"Tam." She reached over and put her hand on the back of his neck, the way she'd seen mothers do at the airstrip in Puerto Cielo, the gesture that meant *look at me*. He did. His eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "He held a gun on us two days ago and now he's holding the road for us. Whatever else is true about that man, he's good at staying alive. It's the one thing he's practiced." She squeezed once and let go. "Harness. Now."

He snapped it home. Behind them, Ilsa leaned forward between the seats, her voice rough from the night's broadcasting, from the wound, from everything. "Mercer," she called out the window, loud enough to carry. "You owe me a map. You burned half of mine."

The shape at the road bend raised one hand—not a wave, an acknowledgment, the way soldiers acknowledge—and then turned back to the road, and that was the last ceremony anyone got, because Mercer was right about speeches and Wren was right about the light, and the light was coming up.

She ran the engine to full and the old Beaver shook itself awake, the propeller blurring into a disc, the whole airframe groaning the way old airframes do, like a man standing up from a chair he's been sitting in too long. The grass strip was six hundred meters of mission-mowed savanna, damp, soft at the south end where the drainage failed. Short. Heavy. Three souls, full fuel, a tailwind that was supposed to be a headwind and wasn't.

"Radio check," Tam said, and his voice came through her headset steady, and she could have kissed him. "Mission tower—that's you, Ilsa—Wren, you're five-by."

"Five-by," Ilsa said.

Wren looked down the strip one last time. Mist. Grass. The road bend, empty. The banana fields beyond, green and silent and lying.

"Pray if you've got it," she said, and pushed the throttle to the stop.

The Beaver surged. The tail came light at forty knots and the nose followed at fifty-five and the strip poured away beneath them, the grass hissing against the tires, the engine hauling them down that short green runway like a mule hauling a wagon out of the mud—slow, then less slow, then the sick arithmetic of a pilot watching her airspeed and her remaining runway disagree about the future. Fifty knots. Fifty-five. Two hundred meters left. Sixty. One hundred meters. Sixty-five, and the wheels touched the grass one last time and left it, and the Beaver sank for one long horrible half-second into air that didn't want it, and then the wing found the morning and bit, and they were climbing.

They were climbing.

"Gear's fixed, airspeed's alive, we're—" Tam's voice cracked and reassembled itself. "We're up."

Wren banked left, away from the road, away from the fields, climbing in a slow spiral over the mission so she could see it—the chapel roof, the courtyard where a gunfight had happened two nights ago and where blood still darkened the flagstones, the road bending south into the trees.

And the road was not empty.

They came out of the banana fields in a line, three trucks this time, running without headlights because they no longer needed the dark, and the lead truck was fast, too fast, swinging wide at the bend, and Wren saw the shape at the bend drop to one knee and saw the muzzle flash—small, obscene, a spark struck in daylight—and the lead truck swerved and stopped and men poured out of it like something spilled.

"Mercer's engaged," Tam said. His voice had gone flat, radio-operator flat, the voice you used when the emotion would drown you otherwise. "Three trucks. Maybe eight, ten men. He's got cover at the culvert."

"He's got a rifle and a culvert," Wren said. "That's not cover, that's a delay."

She put the nose down and turned back.

"*Wren.*" Ilsa's voice, sharp as a scalpel. "What are you doing."

"Buying him minutes. He bought us ours."

"You turn this plane around and you turn this whole mission into a battlefield. You put ten armed men in a courtyard with wounded people in it—"

"I'm not landing." She was already low, two hundred feet, coming down the road axis with the sun behind her, and she understood in her bones what a Beaver could do that a rifle couldn't: it could be *loud*. She keyed the transmit switch on her stick so the engine roar went out over the road, and she dove at the trucks.

The effect was everything she'd hoped and nothing she was proud of. Men who have been shot at from the air do not stop to reason about whether the aircraft is armed. The second truck went off the road into the ditch. The third braked hard enough to stand on its nose. The men at the culvert—Mercer's problem a moment ago—scattered for the trees, and Mercer, bless his calculating soul, used the ten seconds she'd purchased to fall back off the bend toward the mission, moving fast, moving hurt, she could see it, a hitch in his stride like a skipped stitch.

"Pass one," Tam said into her headset, and then, quieter, "They're shooting at us. They're actually shooting at us." Tracers, or just sunlight off rifle barrels—she couldn't tell, didn't care, hauled the Beaver up and around in a climbing turn that made the old girl groan on every rivet.

"Second pass or run?" she asked the cabin.

"Run," Ilsa said.

"Run," Tam said, and then, wrecked, "He's almost to the chapel, Wren, he's *almost there*—"

She looked. He was. Fifty meters of open ground between Mercer and the mission wall, and the men in the road regrouping, and one of them—the tall one, the one with the scoped rifle who'd been in Reyes's truck at the mission two nights ago—going to one knee in the calm, professional way of a man who had done this before and been paid for it.

Wren did the math she'd sworn off doing. Altitude, angle, distance. The Beaver's prop wash against a man's aim. It wasn't much. It was what she had.

She rolled in.

The dive was steep and short and the engine screamed and the whole world narrowed to the road and the running shape and the kneeling shape, and she pulled up at the last possible second, low enough that the prop wash flattened the grass along the road like a thumb smoothing a bedsheet, and the tall man's shot went wide—she'd never know how wide, a foot, a mile—and Mercer hit the chapel wall and was gone behind it, and men in the road were firing upward at a plane that had never fired at them, and Wren hauled the Beaver into the climb with her hands shaking so hard she had to grip the yoke in both fists.

"Run," she said to no one, to everyone. "Now we run."

* * *

They ran.

South and east, down the river valley, the mission falling away behind them, and for four minutes—Tam counted, of course he counted, he counted everything now—there was nothing in the sky and nothing on the radio but the wind and the engine and the river unspooling below like a brown vein.

Then Tam sat up so fast his harness clicked.

"Contact," he said. "Aircraft, high, north-northeast, closing. It's the charter. It's *him*, it's the company charter, the twin-engine, the one that landed at the mission strip yesterday—I've got him on the band, he's talking to Puerto Cielo, they're—" His voice did the flat thing again, harder this time, the flatness of a boy holding a door shut with his whole body. "They're telling him to force us down. *Force her down*, those are the words, they said *do not let her reach the coast*."

Wren looked at the fuel gauge, which had begun its long slow lie of optimism. She looked at the sky to the northeast, where the morning was stacking thunderheads over the cordillera like a man stacking furniture against a door—gray at the base, bruise-purple at the crown, the kind of weather that ate aircraft and asked for seconds.

The charter was faster. The charter was newer. The charter had a pilot who'd been paid to be exactly where he was.

But Wren Nakamura had spent six years learning what the river country's weather could do, and the man chasing her had spent six years flying scheduled routes between paved strips, and there was a canyon of cloud ahead with her name on it.

"Strap in," she said. "Both of you. Tight."

Tam's hand found her shoulder, one squeeze, and let go. Behind her, Ilsa pulled the document case closer and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the sky hadn't already said.

The first cloud wall came up to meet them, and Wren flew into it.
