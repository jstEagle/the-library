## 18. The Long Way Home

The storm had chewed on the Otter all night and spat her out at dawn with a limp.

Wren felt it in the controls before she saw it anywhere—the left aileron sluggish by a half-beat, as if the airplane were thinking twice about every command. Bullet damage from the strip's edge, Mercer's covering fire still ringing in her ears like a bell that wouldn't finish fading. She'd taped the punctured cowling panel with fabric and dope in the dark, working by flashlight held in her teeth while Ilsa kept watch and Tam fed her rivets, and now, eleven hundred meters over the green flood of the river country, she was flying an aircraft that had been shot and patched and prayed over, carrying two people who could not afford to be caught, toward a town where a company man was almost certainly waiting.

"Fuel state?" Ilsa asked from the right seat. She'd claimed it without asking, the way she claimed everything—quietly, absolutely, like a woman who had spent three weeks alone in the jungle deciding she was done deferring to anyone.

"Two hours ten if I lean her out." Wren eased the throttle back a fraction, listening to the engine's voice change. "Puerto Cielo's ninety minutes if the wind cooperates."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we swim the last part."

Tam laughed from behind them—a short, startled bark—and then stopped laughing when he realized she might not be joking. He was wedged between the document case and the survival kit, the sealed aluminum box holding Ilsa's copies of the survey records strapped down like a passenger with a ticket. His father's compass sat open in his palm, needle trembling with every correction Wren made. She had one just like it on the dash. Two compasses, same make, same brass housing gone dark with handling. She hadn't asked him about it yet. There hadn't been time for anything but fuel lines and gunfire and goodbye.

The river unspooled below them, brown and swollen with last night's rain, braiding itself around islands that hadn't been islands a week ago. Somewhere under that water was the rapids where Tam had nearly drowned, where Wren had hauled him out by his collar and told him—only a piece, only the edge of it—about the night six years ago that had cost her her name. He hadn't pressed. He'd just wrapped both hands around a thermos of coffee and said, *So you've been paying it back ever since*, and she had flown the rest of the day with her throat tight.

"Puerto Cielo traffic, Puerto Cielo traffic," Tam said suddenly. He had the handheld radio up, scanning. "I'm hearing—wait. That's not local."

Wren's hand went to the yoke without her permission. "Company charter?"

"Can't tell. Signal's weak. Someone calling a tail number." He turned the volume up. Static, then a voice, clipped and bored: *"—confirm visual contact, report position. Reyes wants—"*

The static swallowed the rest. It didn't need to give more.

"There," Ilsa said.

Wren followed her finger. Behind them and below, maybe eight kilometers back, a shape hung in the haze off the river's eastern bend—white fuselage catching the sun, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that knew exactly where its prey had to go. A Cessna Caravan, or something like it. Faster than the Otter in a straight line. Newer. Cleaner.

"They can't force us down," Tam said. "They can't just—they're not police."

"No," Wren said. "But they can crowd us. They can get ahead of us and cut across our path until we turn back. They can follow us into Puerto Cielo and be standing on the strip when we land, with men, with paper that says whatever Reyes paid a magistrate to write on it. Or—" she watched the white shape grow, "—they can do something stupider, if they think no one's watching. Out here, who's counting airplanes?"

"The mission," Ilsa said. "Father Anselmo has the shortwave. If we go down, he'll know roughly where."

"If we go down, knowing roughly where is a comfort to the people who bury us."

She banked left, gentle, testing the damaged aileron. It answered late and soft, like a tired animal. Fine. She could fly late and soft. She'd learned to fly this way years ago, out of strips that weren't strips, in weather that wasn't weather, carrying loads that weren't legal. The Otter forgave. That was why she flew a De Havilland and not something faster and prettier—because forgiveness mattered more than speed, most days.

Today, speed might matter more.

* * *

The cloud buildups rose ahead of them like a wall, towering cumulus stacked over the high ground north of the Serranía, their bases dark as wet slate, their tops burning white. To a passenger it would have looked like a reason to turn around. To Wren it looked like a door.

"You're not thinking what I think you're thinking," Ilsa said.

"I'm thinking the charter pilot has a company flight plan and a company schedule and a boss on the radio telling him what to do. And I'm thinking I have none of those things."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be." Wren checked the engine gauges—oil pressure steady, temperatures climbing a little, nothing catastrophic yet. "That man flying that airplane has never been inside a cloud he didn't file a plan for. I'm going to show him one."

She pushed the nose toward the wall of weather and pulled the throttle up to climb power. The old radial roared, shuddered, settled into its work. Behind her, Tam braced himself against the cargo net and said nothing, which she appreciated. Six weeks ago he'd been a radio tech who'd never been airborne. Now he read her hands on the controls the way he read Morse—fluently, without needing the letters spelled out.

The first cloud took them at four hundred meters. One moment the world was green river and white airplane; the next it was gray, total, the visibility collapsing to the length of the cowling, rain hammering the windshield in sheets. Wren went to instruments without thinking—attitude, heading, altitude, the little world of needles and glass that didn't care how loud the thunder was. Turbulence hit in long rolling swells, the kind that came off the ridgelines, and the damaged aileron lagged through each correction so that the wings wallowed a half-second behind her intent.

"Talk to me, Tam," she said. "Anything on the band."

"He's calling Puerto Cielo. Asking them to confirm—we're not squawking anything, so he's lost us in the return." A pause. Static crackle. "He sounds nervous."

"Good. Nervous pilots make wide turns."

She brought them out the far side of the cell into a corridor of broken cloud, sunlight strobing through the gaps, and there—two kilometers off the right wing, lower, paralleling—the white shape again. He'd guessed her line. Not bad. But he'd come out of the weather ragged, visibly rocking in the turbulence, and Wren felt something cold and professional settle over her, the old competence rising like ballast.

"He sees us," Ilsa said.

"I know." She rolled into a descending turn, tight, using the terrain. "Now he gets to decide whether he follows me toward rising ground in deteriorating air, or goes around and loses us. Company man. Salary pilot. Watch what he chooses."

For thirty seconds the Caravan held its line. Then, grudgingly, it began to widen its turn, giving the ridgeline the respect its flight manual demanded.

"Going around," Tam reported, half-triumphant.

"For now." Wren leveled low over the trees, close enough that she could see the river glinting through the canopy gaps, close enough that a flock of macaws scattered ahead of them like thrown confetti. Flying this low was a gamble—no room for an engine failure, no margin for surprise—but it put them in the clutter, in the noise, where a radar return dissolved into ground return and a pair of eyes in a clean white cockpit couldn't pick a battered yellow Otter out of a landscape full of light and shadow. "We stay down. We stay in the broken stuff. We don't cross open sky unless we have to."

"How far?" Ilsa asked.

"Ninety minutes became two hours. Maybe more, threading it like this." She glanced at the fuel gauge and did the arithmetic she didn't like. "And we're burning the reserve to buy the hiding."

"Then spend it," Ilsa said simply. "There's no version of this where we land with extra fuel and also land free."

* * *

They played the game for an hour.

It was, Wren thought, the strangest hour of her flying life—stranger even than the night six years ago, because that night had been terror and this was chess. The Caravan would swing wide, climb, try to look down the river corridors ahead of them, predicting. Wren would duck into a cloud fragment, reverse course along a bend, drop behind a ridge, and come out somewhere the other pilot's mental map said she couldn't be. Twice they spotted him again and dove away. Once he got close enough that Tam, watching through the window, read the registration off the tail and called it out in a whisper, as if the pilot might hear.

*"Tango-Bravo-Three-One-One,"* Tam repeated. "That's a company tail. I logged paperwork for that airplane once, back at base camp. Pilot's name is Okafor."

"You know him?"

"Everyone knows everyone out here. He taught my ground school module on weather." Tam's voice went strange. "He's—he's good, actually. He's really good."

"Then he'll be harder to lose and less likely to do something reckless," Wren said. "Both of which I'll take."

But the arithmetic kept getting worse. The fuel gauge crept toward numbers she'd promised herself she'd never see outside of training scenarios. The patched cowling panel started to buzz at certain power settings, a thin metallic whine like a mosquito in the cockpit, and every time it changed pitch her stomach tightened. And the clouds ahead were thickening—not building, thickening, the whole sky over the Serranía foothills congealing into a low, featureless gray deck that offered neither corridors nor cover.

"We can't keep playing," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question.

"No." Wren pulled the chart from beside her seat, spread it against the yoke. "Options. One: Puerto Cielo direct, final forty minutes over open swamp, nowhere to hide, and if Okafor's waiting for us at the field, we land into whatever he's built there. Two: the missionary strip at San Rafael—grass, short, uphill, but it's neutral ground and it's twenty minutes closer. We land, we take stock, we finish the trip by road or wait him out."

"Three," Tam said quietly from the back.

Wren looked at him in the mirror.

"San Rafael's where Father Anselmo broadcast Ilsa's evidence. Half the settlements on the band heard it. You said it yourself—river people came armed with radios and solidarity." He swallowed. "If we land there, we won't be landing alone."

Wren held his eyes in the mirror for a long moment. Sixteen years old, half-starved three days ago, face still marked where the jungle had worked him over, and he was doing navigation-by-community, plotting a course through people instead of terrain. She thought: *the river raised this one better than any school could.*

"San Rafael," she said, and turned the wheel.

* * *

Okafor found them twelve minutes out from the strip.

Of course he did. There was only one sensible approach, one gap in the ridge, one thread of open sky leading to San Rafael's grass runway, and a good pilot guards the door. The white Caravan slid out of the haze a kilometer ahead and five hundred feet up, positioned exactly where Wren would have positioned herself, and she felt a flash of pure professional admiration before the fear arrived.

"He's going to cut us off at the field," Ilsa said. "Land first. Put himself between us and the ground."

"Or he's going to call ahead." Wren's mind was running fast and cold now, the way it did when the options ran out and only execution remained. "Tam. Radio. Anything from San Rafael?"

"Anselmo's on frequency. He's saying—hold on." Tam's head tilted, listening, and then his face changed. "He's saying there are trucks on the road. Settlers. A lot of them. They heard the broadcast, they've been coming since yesterday. He says the strip is clear and—" a pause, disbelief creeping into his voice, "—and he says to tell the pilot that the river remembers her, and the river is watching."

Wren laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, a sound she barely recognized as her own.

"What does that mean?" Ilsa asked.

"It means Okafor is about to land at a grass strip with forty settlers and a priest watching, and whatever Reyes told him to do, he's going to have to decide how much of it he's willing to do in front of witnesses." She throttled back, trimmed for the approach, and felt the Otter settle onto the glide path like a bird coming home to a branch it knew. "Companies are brave in empty places. This place isn't empty anymore."

The Caravan overshot the field once—slow, deliberate, a message written in traffic patterns—and then climbed away to the northeast, back toward Puerto Cielo, without landing at all.

"He's leaving," Tam said.

"He's calling Reyes," Wren corrected. "Which means we have until he gets an answer. Maybe an hour. Maybe less." The grass strip rose to meet them, gold-green in the afternoon light, and beyond it she could see them already: figures along the fence line, a pickup truck, a woman waving a cloth the color of flame. "Flaps. Everyone hold on. This strip is shorter than my temper."

The Otter touched down hard, bounced once, and rolled out through the tall grass with the patched cowling buzzing its mosquito song, and when she finally stood still at the end of the run, engine ticking, Wren's hands were shaking so badly she had to grip the yoke to make them stop.

Ilsa put her hand over hers. Neither of them said anything.

Then the truck reached them, and the doors opened, and the river poured in.
