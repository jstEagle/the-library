## 19. Landing on a Borrowed Strip

The engine had been talking to her for an hour.

Not the healthy conversation of pistons and fuel and fire doing what they were built to do—Wren knew that voice like she knew her own breathing. This was something else. A hitch in the rhythm every fourth or fifth revolution, a metallic hesitation, like a man deciding whether to keep walking or sit down in the road. She'd nursed it through the storm front, through the cloud canyons where the company charter had hunted them with all the patience of a shark following blood, and now, with Puerto Cielo still forty minutes south and the sky finally opening into hard blue morning, the engine was telling her its decision.

It was going to sit down whether she liked it or not.

"Talk to me," Tam said from the radio seat behind her. He'd been saying it for twenty minutes, one hand braced against the fuselage frame, his father's compass open on his knee as if it could do anything but point north while they flew south. His face was gray under the tan, and not just from the near-drowning two days ago. He'd heard the engine too. Sixteen-year-olds who grew up on river strips learned to hear engines the way other children learned lullabies.

"It's running lean," Wren said. "Or it's running out of things to burn. Could be the line I soldered at the mission didn't hold. Could be sediment in the carb." She eased the throttle back a quarter inch and listened. The hitch softened, then came back, deeper. "Could be a lot of things."

Behind Tam, in the second row of seats they'd stripped to save weight, Ilsa Voss sat with the document case locked across her lap and both hands flat on top of it, as if the papers might bolt. She hadn't spoken since the charter gave up the chase and turned back toward Puerto Cielo. Wren had caught her reflection in the canopy glass once or twice—Ilsa's eyes moving over the river below, reading it the way she read everything, as territory, as claim, as contested ground.

"The strip," Ilsa said now. Her voice was hoarse from the broadcast, from forty-eight hours of talking settlements onto the band until her throat gave out. "There's one ahead. San Rafael. Missionary grass. It's on my old surveys."

"I know it." Wren did know it—a green slash on the east bank, half a kilometer of mowed field between the mission compound and the river bluff, maintained by brothers who flew their own mail run in a Cub older than any of them. It wasn't Puerto Cielo. It wasn't a magistrate, a courthouse, a room full of witnesses. But Puerto Cielo belonged to the company the way the moon belonged to whoever paid for the telescope, and Reyes would have men on that airstrip by now, standing in the shade of the fuel shed with their hands folded, waiting for her to come down to them.

The engine coughed. A real cough this time, a full skip, and the De Havilland sagged fifty feet before Wren caught it with the nose.

"That's decided, then," Tam said.

"San Rafael," Wren said, and put the plane down toward the river.

* * *

She brought it in the way you bring in anything when the machine beneath you has stopped being trustworthy: slow, flat, and committed. The grass strip rose up out of the morning haze, longer than she remembered, the grass yellowed at the edges where the dry season was starting to bite. No windsock—or there was one, hanging dead. No movement on the field at all.

That was the part that made her hands cold on the yoke. A missionary strip at seven in the morning should have had somebody on it. The brothers rose before dawn; there should have been a figure by the hangar, laundry lines, smoke from the cookhouse chimney. Instead the compound sat quiet as a held breath, and Wren thought: *they got here first.* Reyes had flown out in the dark. Reyes had bought the brothers or frightened them or simply arrived with enough men to make the question unnecessary, and now the whole field was a closed hand waiting for her to land inside it.

"There's nobody," Tam said. "Why is there nobody?"

"Because either we're early," Wren said, "or we're late."

She flared over the grass, felt the wheels touch soft and even, and rolled out with the engine dying in stuttering increments, each cough shorter than the last, until the prop wound down to a stop just short of the far fence line and the silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere in it, a bird called twice and thought better of it.

For a long moment none of them moved. Then Ilsa unclipped the case from her lap, set it on the seat, and said, quietly, "Well. We're down."

"We're down," Wren agreed. Her heart was going like a sewing machine. She looked out at the empty compound—the whitewashed chapel, the low dormitory, the hangar with its doors shut—and made herself say the rest of it. "And I don't know whose ground this is."

Tam was already moving, pulling the radio headset off its hook, and Wren watched him do it and understood that he'd stopped being a kid somewhere between the rapids and the gunfire and the night work, and that she couldn't give that back to him even if she wanted to. He keyed the set, ran the band in a fast sweep, and stopped.

"—repeat, all stations, this is San Rafael, we are receiving, we are receiving—" The voice was young, breathless, a boy's voice cracking on the second word. "Aircraft on the field, aircraft on the field, if you can hear this, stay in your plane, do not—no. Wait. *Come out.* Come out, they said come out with your hands visible, it's—it's fine, it's fine—"

The transmission collapsed into static and shouting, voices overlapping, and Wren sat with her hand on the door latch and her whole body telling her the oldest story she knew: *small, invisible, gone.* Six years of that story. Six years of landing nowhere, registering nothing, letting the company ledger hold her name so the world didn't have to.

Ilsa leaned forward between the seats. "Wren."

"They said hands visible," Wren said. "That's either very good or very rehearsed."

"Wren." Ilsa's voice had the flat patience of a woman who had spent three days broadcasting truth into a country that mostly preferred rumor. "Look at the tree line. Look at the *river*."

Wren looked.

They were coming out of the trees along the east fence—not soldiers, not company men in matching canvas, but people. A dozen of them, then more, spilling from the trail that ran down from the bluff and along the riverbank where the settler boats tied up. Men in rubber boots and work shirts. Women with machetes still stuck through their belts from the morning's clearing. An old man walking with a stick and a boy carrying, of all things, a battery lantern in full daylight, because the boy had been told to bring it and had brought it. They spread along the fence line without hurry and without menace, and they did not carry guns pointed anywhere—they carried radios. Handheld sets, ship sets, a car battery wired to a transceiver lashed to a wheelbarrow. One woman held hers up over her head like a signal lamp.

And above the chapel, running up the pole where a flag should have been, someone had hoisted a bedsheet with letters painted in tar:

**WE HEARD HER.**

*Her.* Not *them*. Not *the aircraft*. Wren felt something move through her chest that she didn't have a name for, something that started below the ribs and went up into her throat and stayed there.

"The broadcast," Tam whispered. "The last relay. Ilsa, your last relay reached here before the power cut."

"I hoped," Ilsa said. "Hoping isn't knowing." But her hands, flat on the document case, had begun to shake, and Wren understood that the cartographer who had hidden alone in a flooded vault and a mission infirmary for weeks, who had refused to run because documents don't testify, was shaking now—not from fear. From the arithmetic of it. All those nights speaking into the dark, never sure if the dark was listening. And the dark had heard. The dark had painted a bedsheet.

Wren opened the door and stepped down onto the grass with her hands visible, and the field erupted—not in violence, in noise. Shouting, calling, the old man banging his stick on the fence rail. The woman with the handheld radio pressed it to her mouth and her voice came out of every set on the fence line at once, doubled and trebled across the field like a congregation answering:

*"San Rafael to all stations—she's here! The pilot is here! The plane is down safe!"*

And from somewhere downriver, faint through the morning air, another radio answered, and another, voices relayed voice to voice up and down the water, settlement to settlement, until it seemed to Wren that the whole river was talking, that the country Reyes had taught to be silent had found its frequency overnight and would not shut up again.

* * *

They came through the gate the way water comes through a broken weir.

The old man with the stick reached her first—he moved faster than he had any right to—and took her hand in both of his, which were cracked and brown and strong as root wood. "Nakamura," he said. "You're Nakamura. The broadcast said the pilot, Nakamura, flying the blue De Havilland." He peered up at her, eyes milky at the rims but sharp underneath. "I'm Esteban. I fished the upper channel thirty years before the company drew its line through it and told me my own boat was trespassing." He said it without heat, the way you state a fact that has had decades to cure. "Thirty years, señora. And in one night, one voice on the band says the maps are lies, and every boat on this river remembers exactly where it always went."

"You heard Ilsa's broadcast," Wren said.

"We heard *everything*." He turned, and the crowd parted around him with the ease of long practice, and Wren saw that they weren't looking at her anymore. They were looking past her, at the woman climbing stiffly down from the plane with the document case hugged to her chest, and the sound that went through the settlers then was not cheering. It was quieter than that and heavier—the sound of a thing confirmed. Ilsa Voss stood on the grass of San Rafael holding the proof of everything they'd lost, and the settlers looked at her the way starving men look at bread they'd stopped believing existed.

Then Tam slid out of the radio seat, and the sound changed. It broke open.

"Tam." A woman came through the gate at a dead run—gray-streaked hair, a fisherman's oilskin over her dress, and Wren saw the resemblance hit her a half-second before the name did. Marta Okonkwo, the expedition's cook, listed missing with the others. She crossed the grass like the ground owed her money and caught the boy around the shoulders and went down to her knees with him, and Tam, who had not cried at the rapids or the ambush or the gunfight, buried his face in her neck and shook like a sapling in wind. Around them the settlers were calling names—Halvorsen, Prieto, the surveyor twins—asking, checking, and Wren stood in the middle of it with her hands still half-raised and let the grief and the relief wash around her like current around a piling.

"He's alive," Marta was saying into Tam's hair, over and over. "You're alive, you're alive, God is stubborn and so are you."

Esteban's hand settled on Wren's arm. "The others? The ones who walked out?"

"At Puerto Cielo," Wren said. "Halvorsen and four others surrendered to the magistrate's office two days ago rather than to the company. They're under protective custody—such as it is. Reyes couldn't reach them without the magistrate watching." She hesitated. "Mercer stayed behind at the mission strip. Holding the road."

The old man nodded slowly. "The one who took the bullet."

"The one who took the bullet." She still didn't know if he'd lived through the night. She'd left him propped against a fuel drum with a tourniquet and the mission doctor's opinion split down the middle, and Mercer had grinned at her through the blood on his teeth and said, *Fly, Nakamura. I've been holding ground my whole life. First time it's been the right ground.* She hadn't let herself think about it since. Thinking about it was a luxury the sky hadn't allowed.

Esteban studied her face, and whatever he saw there, he had the grace not to name. "Then we pray for him and we work," he said. "Come. There is food, there is coffee, and there is a radio room full of people who have not slept because they would not leave the band in case you needed them." He paused, and his grip tightened on her arm, gentle and immovable. "And señorita—there is a magistrate coming. From Puerto Cielo. By boat, with two constables and a court clerk, because when the whole river starts demanding a hearing, even a bought town has to send somebody. He will arrive tonight."

Wren went still. "Reyes knows?"

"Reyes knows the river knows." Esteban smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a man who had waited thirty years for a tide. "Last night, after the broadcast, forty boats went down to Puerto Cielo and anchored in the harbor with their lamps lit. Nobody said a word. Nobody had to. The company man looked out his window at a harbor full of lights and understood that the country had changed shape around him while he slept." He turned her gently toward the compound, toward the chapel and the coffee and the noise of the living. "Tonight there will be a hearing on this grass, under that sheet, with every settlement on the band listening. The magistrate will take testimony. The clerk will write it down. And the woman who holds those papers—" he nodded at Ilsa, who was surrounded now, weeping and laughing and being fed by three grandmothers at once, "—will read them aloud."

"And me?" Wren asked. It came out smaller than she meant it to.

Esteban looked at her for a long moment, and behind the milkiness of his eyes she saw a shrewdness that had survived everything the river and the company could do to it.

"You," he said, "are the pilot who brought them home. That is already testimony, señora. Everything else is just your name."

* * *

They put her at a table in the mission refectory with sweet coffee she couldn't taste and cassava bread she ate without noticing, and around her the room filled and emptied and filled again—settlers coming to look, to touch the wing of the blue plane through the window, to ask Tam questions about the vault that he answered in a voice still raw from crying. Ilsa sat across from her with the case unlocked at last, papers fanned out on the scrubbed wood, and the two of them worked in the old rhythm now, the cartographer pointing, the pilot memorizing: boundary line here, falsified 1947; purchase record here, signature witnessed by a man dead eleven years before the date; the same hand, the same ink, the same lie repeated down forty years of filings.

By midday the sun had burned the haze off the river, and Wren stood at the window looking south toward Puerto Cielo and let herself feel, for the first time in six years, something that was not fear wearing the mask of calm.

Tam came and stood beside her. He'd cleaned up, borrowed a shirt that fit him badly, and his father's compass hung around his neck on its lanyard now instead of living in his pocket. He followed her gaze south.

"You're thinking about tonight," he said.

"I'm thinking about how easy it would be to fly out right now," Wren said. "Fuel's still good in the aux tank. I could be over the coast by dark. New name by next month." She said it evenly, testing the words, and found they weighed nothing. They were a costume that no longer fit. "Six years ago I would have done it. Two weeks ago I would have done it."

"What changed?"

Wren looked at the field. At the settlers repairing the fence rail the storm had cracked, at the women rigging loudspeakers on the chapel roof, at the boy with the battery lantern—still carrying it, though the sun was high—standing guard over the blue plane as if it were a church. At the bedsheet snapping softly in the breeze: **WE HEARD HER.**

"I used to think disappearing was safety," she said. "Turns out it's just loneliness with better logistics."

Tam laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and bumped her shoulder with his, and outside, down at the river landing, the first boats of the evening were already coming up the channel with their lamps unlit and their decks full, settlers from the far camps coming to stand on a mowed strip of grass and watch a woman tell the truth.

Then the radio room door banged open and a girl leaned out, breathless, handheld set in her fist.

"San Rafael to the pilot—message from Puerto Cielo, relayed twice." The girl swallowed. "The magistrate's boat launched an hour ago. But so did another one, right behind it. Company colors. Eight men aboard, armed, and Reyes himself standing at the bow." She looked at Wren, and her voice dropped. "They'll reach the mouth of our channel by sundown. Same time as the magistrate."

Wren set down her cup. On the table, Ilsa had gone very still, one hand flat on the oldest forgery, and Tam's hand had found the compass at his throat.

"Well," Wren said, and heard her own voice come out level, steady, the voice of a woman with a name. "Then we'd better get the testimony started before they arrive. Justice shouldn't keep a gentleman waiting—but it doesn't wait for Reyes, either."

She picked up the document case and walked out onto the grass, where the whole river was gathering to listen.
