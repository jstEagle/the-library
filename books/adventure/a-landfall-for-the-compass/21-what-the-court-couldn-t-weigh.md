## 21. What the Court Couldn't Weigh

The magistrate's court in Puerto Cielo had been a granary once, and it still smelled like one—dust and old maize and the ghost of rats in the rafters. Someone had hung a ceiling fan that turned with the optimism of a dying thing, and beneath it they'd set out folding chairs that had been borrowed from three different churches, judging by the mismatched hymn numbers stenciled on their backs.

Wren sat at the front, at a table too small for what was happening on top of it, and watched the room fill.

It filled the way water fills a dry creek bed—not all at once but finding every channel, seeping into every low place. Settlers from the middle river who'd come down on barges flying hand-painted banners. The mission sisters in their gray habits, Ilsa among them with her arm still in a sling, refusing the chair they kept trying to give her. Tam, scrubbed and solemn in a shirt his mother would have been proud of, sitting where she could see him because neither of them had pretended otherwise since the rapids. Even men she knew only as faces at the fuel dock, faces that had never once met her eyes in six years of buying avgas, now looking at her directly, deliberately, as if to say *we see you, and we came anyway*.

The regional magistrate was a small woman named Ochoa with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the unhurried manner of someone who had spent thirty years watching powerful people discover that rivers do not sign contracts. She let the room settle. She let the fan turn. Then she opened a folder thick enough to stop a bullet and said, "We will begin with the company's claims, and we will end with Ms. Nakamura's."

Wren's stomach did what it always did when her name was spoken aloud—that lurch, that old reflex of a woman hearing a door open that she'd bricked over years ago.

The company sent lawyers. Of course it did. Three of them, flown in on a charter that had cost more than most settlers earned in a decade, wearing linen suits already wilting in the heat, spreading documents across their table like a card sharp laying out a winning hand. The senior one, a pale man named Corbett whose voice had the smoothness of something machine-polished, spoke for forty minutes about boundary surveys, about title chains, about the *bona fides* of acquisition going back two generations.

Ilsa listened with her good hand folded in her lap and her bad one resting on the table, and Wren watched her face and understood that Ilsa was not listening to the argument. She was listening for the seams. And finding them. A cartographer learns to hear a false coordinate the way a musician hears a flat note—it isn't a decision, it's a flinch.

When Corbett finished, Magistrate Ochoa turned to Ilsa and said, "You have prepared a response."

"I have prepared a map," Ilsa said, and stood.

What followed took two hours and should have taken two days, except that Ilsa Voss had apparently decided that mercy was not among her obligations. She walked the court through the original survey records page by page—the ones from the vault, the ones she'd copied twice and hidden in three places, the ones Wren had carried out through gunfire sewn into the lining of her flight jacket. She showed where the company's filed boundaries diverged from the surveyed ones. Not by meters. By kilometers. By entire watersheds, shifted south and east until villages that had stood for a century found themselves inside land the company owned on paper and had never set foot on.

"The error is consistent," Ilsa said, "in one direction only. Errors drift both ways. Intentions don't." She tapped the map. "Every line bends toward profit. That is not surveying. That is theft with a ruler."

Corbett objected. Ochoa overruled him without looking up. Somewhere in the back of the room a settler woman began, very quietly, to cry, and no one asked her to stop.

By late afternoon the suspension came down—the company's claims frozen pending a full tribunal, Reyes's arrest warrant signed before the ink on the first order dried. They took him at the airstrip hotel, Wren heard later, mid-drink, and he didn't run. Maybe he understood, better than anyone, that there was nowhere on this river his employer's reach would matter anymore.

Ochoa adjourned until morning. As the room emptied, Tam caught Wren's sleeve.

"They're going to do you tomorrow," he said. Not a question.

"Tomorrow," she agreed.

"Will you—" He stopped. Started again. "I'll be there. Whatever happens."

She looked at this boy who'd hidden in a cave eating river snails for eleven days rather than abandon his father's compass, and felt the thing in her chest that had been loose ever since the rapids shift around again, painful and warm. "I know," she said. "That's the part that scares me."

* * *

They held her overnight.

Not in a cell, exactly—the Puerto Cielo jail was a single room behind the police post with a cot, a bucket, and a window barred more against jaguars than prisoners—but detained was detained, and the constable who locked the door apologized twice and brought her coffee that tasted like boiled tires and regret.

"You could have run," he said through the bars, awkwardly. "After the landing. Before we got word. Nobody would've blamed—"

"I'm done running," Wren said, and was surprised at how little performance it required.

The night was long in the way nights are long when you've finally stopped outrunning them. She lay on the cot and listened to the river, invisible beyond the wall but audible everywhere in Puerto Cielo after dark—a vast slow breathing, patient as anything that has outlasted empires. Six years ago she had crossed that water in the bottom of a canoe with a false name in her mouth and a dead man's debt on her books, and every day since she had made herself smaller. Quieter flights. Fewer questions. A name shortened, an accent flattened, a history amputated at the knee so it wouldn't leave tracks.

And it hadn't worked. That was the joke of it, the bitter punchline six years in the telling. Smallness hadn't saved her; it had just made her easier to squeeze. Reyes had known exactly how much pressure to apply because a woman with no name, no people, no past on record is a woman with nothing between herself and the screw. Debt always wins, she'd told herself on the flight north. But debt only wins against people standing alone.

She thought about Mercer, somewhere across town bleeding slowly in a mission infirmary because he'd finally picked a side and the side had shot him for it. She thought about Ilsa saying *documents don't testify, people do*, with a bullet graze on her scalp and her voice steady as a level line. She thought about her own testimony that afternoon—still to come—and the crime she would have to confess along with everything else, the smuggling run six years ago, the man who hadn't survived it, the company that had buried the paperwork and sold her the silence instead of the cell.

Sleep came eventually, shallow and dreamless, and dawn came behind it the way dawn comes on the river—all at once, gold and merciless.

* * *

The hearing resumed at nine. This time the company's lawyers stayed away, which told Wren everything about how the wind had shifted. Corbett had flown out on the dawn charter, someone said, carrying a briefcase and an expression like a man transporting his own funeral.

Magistrate Ochoa read the charges into the record in a voice pitched for precision rather than drama: unlicensed transport of contraband, six years past; falsification of identity documents; failure to report a death under circumstances then under investigation. Each one landed like a stone dropped in still water, and Wren watched the ripples move through the settlers' faces—surprise, yes, but not the surprise she'd feared. Not disgust. More like recognition. Everyone on this river was running from something, or paying for something, or both.

"Do you contest these charges?" Ochoa asked.

"No, ma'am."

"Do you wish counsel?"

"I'll speak for myself."

Ochoa nodded, unsurprised, and Wren stood, and her legs held, and she gave her name.

Not Wren. Not Nakamura-the-surname-only, the half-name she'd worn like a scarf pulled up over her face. All of it—the name her mother had given her, the name on the birth certificate in a country she'd fled, spoken clearly into a room full of strangers while Tam sat forward in his chair with his hands knotted together and Ilsa closed her eyes as if in prayer or satisfaction, possibly both.

Then she told it. All of it. The smuggling run, the cargo she'd been promised was medicine and wasn't, the storm, the crash on the far bank, the man—her partner, her friend, the one who'd talked her into it—who'd died in the wreckage while she crawled free. The company fixer who'd appeared within a day, who'd offered her a choice between a cell and a ledger, and who had kept her on that ledger ever since, tightening it run by run until she was flying errands into the interior for a man who smiled like a closing door. She told it plainly, without ornament, because she had learned from Ilsa that the truth needs no decoration, only a witness.

When she finished, the fan turned. The river breathed. Ochoa looked at her over the beaded glasses for a long moment.

"The court finds the facts as stated to be credible," she said. "Fines are assessed"—she named a figure that would take Wren a year of supply runs to pay, and Wren nodded, because it was fair, because debts should cost something real—"and a term of detention, suspended given time already served and cooperation rendered. However." Ochoa removed her glasses entirely, which Wren would learn later was unprecedented. "This court sits in territory where formal law arrives late and leaves early, and where, in its absence, another jurisdiction has long operated. I refer to the communities of the river themselves. I am permitted—I am obliged—to hear them."

She turned to the room. "Who speaks for the river?"

There was a silence, and then, to Wren's astonishment, nearly everyone stood.

Not dramatically. Not all at once, even. But row by row, family by family, the settlers rose—the barge families, the mission sisters, the fuel-dock men, the old woman who cried yesterday now upright and gripping the back of the chair in front of her. Tam stood so fast he knocked his chair over and left it lying there. Ilsa rose last, slowly, using the table, and remained standing though her face went white with the effort, because some things must be done on your feet.

An elder from the middle river, a man Wren knew only as a face at a hundred landings, spoke for all of them in few words. He said that the woman called Wren had flown medicine through storms no charter would touch, for prices the poor could pay. He said that when the company's men came burning, it was her plane and her stubbornness that carried the proof out. He said that the river keeps its own accounts, and hers was paid.

Ochoa wrote something in the margin of her file. Then she looked up.

"The fines stand. Justice that costs nothing reforms nothing. But the suspension is confirmed, and the record will note that the accused was vouched for by"—she paused, and Wren saw the ghost of something almost like a smile—"a great many people. Court is adjourned."

* * *

Afterward, the courtyard outside the granary-court was chaos of the best kind—handshakes, questions, a settler grandmother pressing a bag of smoked fish into Wren's arms as if it were a medal. She bore it the way she supposed one bears any weather: head down, moving forward, letting it happen to her.

Mercer was propped up in the infirmary when she got there that evening, gray as ash but alive, one shoulder wrapped in bandages and a chessboard nobody was playing balanced on his knees. He looked up as she came in and produced something that on another face might have been a smile.

"Heard you got fined," he rasped.

"A year of flying."

"Cheaper than mine cost." He shifted, winced. "Bullet's still in there. Sister says it can stay if it behaves." He studied her a moment. "You used your name today. All of it."

"All of it."

"Huh." He moved a pawn with his good hand, pointlessly, an opponentless gesture. "Six years I figured names were just handles. Things you grab people by." He set the pawn down. "Maybe they're handles you grab yourself by, too."

She didn't answer. Some conversions you don't need to narrate.

At the door she paused. "Recovery's going to take months. You'll be here when the rains start?"

"Where else?" He gestured at the bandages, the cot, the whole improbable fact of himself alive in a mission infirmary instead of a company grave. "Turns out I'm a man of the river now. God help us all."

Walking back through the dusk, past the mission garden and the smell of woodsmoke and frying plantains, Wren found Tam waiting on the airstrip fence, silhouetted against the last light, and beside him—of course—her airplane, the old De Havilland sitting in the grass with its cowling open and its wounds bandaged in fresh sheet metal, and Tam radiating the particular pride of a boy who has fixed something he wasn't supposed to touch.

"She'll fly tomorrow," he said. "Fuel line's good. New magneto off the mission's pump engine—don't tell the sisters." He hesitated, then added, quieter: "Ilsa says she's staying through the tribunal. Mapping. Properly, this time. And she says—" he stumbled over the relay of it, suddenly shy— "she says if you're flying mail runs north next month, she knows the coordinates now. The true ones."

Wren put her hand on the warm cowling, on metal that had carried her out of the dark and home again, and looked down the strip to where the river glowed copper in the last of the light, running north toward everything they'd survived and everything still waiting.

"Tell her," she said, "I'll want a copilot who knows the true coordinates."

Tam grinned. And downriver, faint but unmistakable, a shortwave crackled to life at the mission—someone, somewhere, already calling her new call sign, the one with her whole name in it, asking if she was taking passengers.
