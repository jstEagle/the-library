## 22. A Landfall for the Compass

The morning fog had not yet burned off the river when Wren Nakamura walked out to the strip with two thermoses of coffee and a list of errands longer than her arm. The De Havilland sat where she'd left it the night before, dew silvering her wings, and beyond her the water ran brown and patient toward the bend where Puerto Cielo's tin roofs caught the first light like coins at the bottom of a well.

Six months. Long enough for the grass on the strip to have been cut twice, for the magistrate's temporary office to have acquired a permanent sign, for the word *testimony* to have stopped tasting like a wound in her mouth. Long enough for the ledger of favors she owed the company to have been torn up, burned, and scattered—Reyes's neat columns of debt gone the way of Reyes himself, who now occupied a cell in the regional capital and wrote letters nobody answered.

She set the thermoses on the wing root and checked the plane the way she always did, out of habit rather than doubt: fuel, control surfaces, tires, the new fuel line she and Ilsa had soldered by lantern light on the worst night of their lives. That line still carried a faint scar of silver solder beneath its wrapping, and Wren touched it every morning the way some pilots touched a saint's medal. It had held. They all had.

"Told you I'd beat you out here."

Tam came down the path from the settlement with the radio batteries cradled in his arms like a sleeping animal, sixteen going on seventeen, taller than he'd been in the cave, his voice finally settling into the lower register it had been promising all year. He'd filled out on mission cooking and Wren's terrible flying rations. There was color in his face now, and a new callus of confidence where the fear used to live.

"You didn't beat me. You saw me walking and ran the last hundred meters."

"That's called tactics, Captain. You'd know that if you'd ever been a scout." He loaded the batteries into the bay behind the second seat with the economy of a boy who had learned radio work in a place where every gram mattered, then climbed into the right-hand seat—the operator's seat, *his* seat now, the headset already hanging on its hook—and began his pre-flight checks without being told. He'd made up a little litany for it, half prayer and half inventory, murmured under his breath: *antenna secure, spare fuses, spare fuse-holders because someone keeps blowing them, logbook, pencil, pencil backup—*

"I can hear you, Tam."

"I know. It's for the logbook. Future historians will want to know about your fuse problem."

Wren laughed and let it go. Six months ago this boy had been half-starved in a limestone cave with a smashed radio and a dead man's compass, and now he corrected her Morse timing and argued about antenna theory and called her Captain with a straight face because the river camp had voted on it and the river camp's votes were law.

* * *

They were airborne by seven, climbing through the fog layer into a morning so clean it looked rinsed. The rainforest unrolled beneath them, green to every horizon, cut by the river's brown cursive. Wren flew with the window cracked because she liked the smell of it—the wet green breath of the country, woodsmoke from a settlement she couldn't see, the mineral tang of the water. For six years she had flown this landscape as a stranger, keeping her eyes on instruments and her name in a locked box, moving like a woman wading a river who never let the current get above her knees.

She hadn't realized how much of herself she'd been holding above the waterline until the day she let go of it.

"San Marcos first," Tam said, reading the manifest. "Medicine for the clinic—amoxicillin, the malaria kits, and the new spectacles for Doña Rosa, tell her they're the strong ones she asked for. Then the mail drop at the mission, then Ilsa's survey markers at the north bend, then home by four because the Vasquez girl's piano recital is at five and you promised."

"I promised nothing. I was *informed*."

"You were informed in a tone that was a promise." He grinned at her sideways. "Also there's a package from the capital. Court clerk's office. It came on the steamer yesterday and I signed for it because you were at the magistrate's."

Wren felt the old reflex tighten in her chest—the flinch she'd spent six years training into herself, the smallness she'd built plank by plank. Paper from the capital had only ever meant one thing, in her former life.

"What is it?"

"Settlement of the land claims. Final. The claims are dissolved—permanently, not just suspended. The boundary survey is struck from the registry. And there's—" he paused, and his voice changed, softened. "There's a letter in there with the seal of the regional court. For you. I didn't open it."

She flew in silence for a while. Below them the river bent around a bluff where the old survey vault stood with its door hanging open, a dark tooth in the green. She'd flown over it a dozen times since. It didn't pull at her anymore. It was just a place, and places only had the power you lent them.

"Open it," she said.

Tam tore the envelope with his thumbnail. Unfolded two pages. Read. She watched his face in the corner of her vision—the frown, then the slow spreading of something that wasn't quite a smile, more like the expression of a boy watching a door he'd assumed was locked swing open on its own.

"It's a citation," he said. "An official commendation. For 'courage in the service of truth and the river communities.'" He looked up. "It's got your name on it. Your *real* name."

"Read it out loud."

He hesitated—because he understood, this boy who'd lost a father and found a family, who understood better than anyone what names cost and what they were worth—and then he read it, every word, and the sound of her own name in his voice over the engine's drone was the strangest and most ordinary thing Wren had ever heard. Like hearing your name called from another room in a house you'd forgotten you owned.

"Frame it," she said. "Put it up in the radio shack. Next to the fuse-holders."

* * *

The north bend was where Ilsa was working this week, re-surveying the boundary country with instruments that told the truth, at her own expense and the settlers', mapping honestly the land the company had mapped as a lie. They found her orange marker tarp pegged to a knoll above the water, and beside it Ilsa herself, sunburned and cheerful, waving them down with the whole-arm sweeps of a woman who had spent a year signaling aircraft and had never quite stopped.

They landed on the water at the gravel bar, and Ilsa waded out to the floats with her trousers rolled to the knee and her survey notebook held above her head like an offering.

"Captain," she said, hauling herself onto the float. "Radio operator. Tell me you brought the coffee and not the tea. If it's the tea I'm defecting to the company."

"Coffee," Wren said, and handed down a thermos, and the three of them sat on the floats in the sun with their boots dangling in the current, drinking coffee and looking at the country Ilsa was giving back to itself one honest line at a time.

"Finished the east section," Ilsa said. "Every marker verified, every line witnessed. The settlers' council will have the full map by dry season. When they print it—" she tapped the notebook, "—it goes in every schoolhouse on the river. The children will learn the shape of their own land. That's how you kill a lie permanently. Not with a trial. With a map in a schoolroom."

"You sound like you," Tam said.

"I am like me. It took me fifty years to get here, so I intend to enjoy it." She looked at Wren then, and her eyes did the thing they always did—the thing Wren had stopped flinching from sometime around the night they'd soldered the fuel line together, when Ilsa had said *names are tools, use the one that fits the work* and Wren had said *mine never fit* and Ilsa had said *then make one that does, and mean it*. "The capital letter came?"

"Came. Commendation. My name on it."

"Good." Ilsa drank her coffee. "Frame it."

"Tam said the same thing. You two are a conspiracy."

"We're a *crew*," Tam said. "It's different. A conspiracy has secrets. We don't have those anymore."

Wren looked at him—at the compass hanging on its lanyard around his neck, his father's, the same model as the one on her own dashboard, twin needles that had pointed two men toward the same river country from two different lives—and she thought about what he'd said, and found it was true. There were no secrets left in her. There was the story of the incident six years ago, told in a courtroom in her own voice, and the fine she'd paid, and the ninety days she'd served, and the river camp that had stood up in the gallery when the magistrate asked if anyone would speak for her, and spoken, one after another, until the gallery sounded like the river itself in flood. There was nothing hidden. It turned out that was what freedom felt like: nothing hidden, nothing held above the waterline, the whole weight of yourself resting on the current and the current holding.

"Ilsa," she said. "The north section. The stretch above the falls. I want to fly the line with you when you survey it."

Ilsa raised an eyebrow. "That's a week in the bush. Mosquitoes, rapids, my cooking."

"I know your cooking. I'm still asking."

"Then it's a date, Captain. The compass will want to come along, I suppose." She nodded at the dashboard through the windshield, at the little brass instrument sitting in its bracket where it had sat for six years, pointing—Wren had only recently understood this—where it had always pointed. She'd thought it pointed away. It had pointed home the whole time. She'd just been flying the other way.

* * *

They dropped Ilsa at her camp and flew the mail to the mission, where Sister Anunciación made them stay for lunch because Sister Anunciación had never once in recorded history let an aircraft leave before lunch, and the courtyard where the gunfight had happened was now a garden, tomatoes climbing the bullet-scarred walls, Mercer's handiwork—he'd taken to gardening during his recovery the way some men took to religion, with total and slightly alarming devotion.

Mercy himself was there, one arm in a sling that was mostly ceremonial at this point, arguing with the sisters about whether his tomato stakes were straight. He'd stayed on at the mission after the trial. Nobody had asked him to. He'd simply shown up with his tools and his conscience, which he was still paying off in installments, he said, but at least now the payments were going to the right account.

"Captain," he said, and touched two fingers to his brow, the old security-chief salute gone soft. "Heard you got your commendation."

"Heard you got tomatoes."

"Best on the river. Don't tell the sisters I said so, they'll want credit." He looked at her a moment longer, and whatever passed between them passed without words, the way it did between people who had been on opposite sides of a gun and ended up on the same side of a garden. "Fly careful."

"Always," said Wren, who had never once flown careful in her life, and meant it anyway.

* * *

They were home by four, as promised, and the whole settlement was at the strip for the Vasquez girl's recital, which was held on a flatbed wagon because the settlement had no hall, and which consisted of the girl playing three pieces on a piano that had come upriver on the steamer in nineteen pieces and been reassembled by six volunteers and one retired security chief who turned out to be very good with his hands. She was not, objectively, a prodigy. The river did not care. The river applauded until the tin roofs rang.

Wren stood at the edge of the crowd with Tam beside her and Ilsa's arm through hers, and she let the sound wash over her, and she thought: *this is the debt I couldn't see, the one I was paying interest on all those years without knowing it was even owed. Not money. Belonging. And the payment was just this—staying.*

Later, when the crowd had gone and the lanterns were lit along the settlement path, she walked out to the plane one last time, alone, because there were things a pilot said to her aircraft and to herself that no one else needed to hear.

The fog was coming up off the water again, soft as breath. The compass on the dash caught the lantern light, its needle steady, unwavering, pointed at the dark bend of the river where the settlement slept and the radio shack's antenna wire hummed faintly in the night air, carrying Tam's voice out to every settlement on the band, the evening traffic, the weather, the small good news of the river.

"Tomorrow," she told the plane, and meant the whole of it—tomorrow, and the week after, and the survey above the falls, and the schoolhouse maps, and every ordinary miraculous flight between here and the end of her life.

She touched the scar of solder under the fuel line's wrapping, once, the way you touch a medal.

Then she walked back toward the lights, and the compass stayed behind her in the dark cockpit, pointing home, which was, at last, where she was going.
