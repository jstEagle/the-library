## 1. The Blacklist

The *Estrella del Sur* smelled of diesel and wet wool and the particular despair of men who had once worked real boats. Paloma Almeida knew the smell the way other men knew their mothers' kitchens. He had been breathing it for eleven months now, since the morning the ferry company in Coyhaique had handed him a schedule of crossings so minor they barely qualified as water — the gray run between Puerto Natales and the estancia piers up the Última Esperanza, carrying mail sacks, schoolteachers, crates of chickens, and the occasional tourist too cheap or too curious to take the road.

He ran the wheel with his hands and let his mind go somewhere else. That was the discipline of it. Anyone could pilot a ferry; that was not the humiliation. The humiliation was that he piloted it well, that his hands still read current and wind the way a blind man reads a face, and none of it counted anymore. The wilderness had not revoked its trust in him. Only the ministries had.

"Almeida." The deckhand, a boy called Chico with a permanent cold and a permanent grin, leaned into the wheelhouse. "Man on the pier asking for you by name. Not by boat. By name."

Paloma didn't look up from the channel ahead, where the morning fog was peeling off the water in slow rags. "People ask for the captain. They don't ask for me."

"This one did. Suit's too good for the pier. Shoes too good for this whole town."

That was interesting, in the way that a change in barometric pressure is interesting. Paloma brought the *Estrella* alongside with the same economy he'd used on every mooring of his life — no wasted reverse thrust, no drama — and watched through the salt-hazed glass as the man in the good suit stood apart from the crowd of disembarking passengers, hands in the pockets of a coat that had never seen weather. He was fifty-something, silver at the temples, with the patient stillness of someone paid to wait rather than to act.

Chico whistled low. "That's money standing there."

"That's trouble standing there," Paloma said, and went down to meet it anyway.

* * *

The man introduced himself as Roldán and offered no first name, which told Paloma more than a résumé would have. They walked away from the pier together, because Roldán suggested it with a tilt of his head, and ended up in the back room of the hostal café where the owner owed Paloma a favor going back to a winter when Paloma had carried her son down off Cerro Dorotea with a broken ankle and a rising storm. The favor bought them privacy and coffee that tasted like boiled bark.

Roldán set a folded newspaper on the table between them, squared its edges, and left his hand on it like a man keeping a dog from bolting.

"You were with the Andes Sur expeditions," Roldán said. It wasn't a question. "Eleven years. Glacier rescue certification. Fjord navigation endorsement. You mapped half the channels south of the Golfo de Penas before you were thirty."

"I ferried chickens this morning."

"Yes." Roldán smiled without warmth. "I know exactly what you do now. I made it my business to know."

Paloma drank his bark-coffee and waited. Men like Roldán always filled silence eventually; it was the one weakness of people who talked for a living. Outside the window, the fog was burning off Última Esperanza, and the sound was named honestly — Last Hope — though Paloma had long ago decided the name referred to nothing but the geography of a bay.

"The survey vessel *Cautiva*," Roldán said. "Government hydrographic team, six men, working the inner fjords below the Serrano. She went out in March with sealed orders and a full complement. Four months later she comes back to port without her crew. Gear intact. Tents found pitched at their last anchorage. Radios charged. Rations stacked. No bodies, no wreckage, no tracks leaving the camp. Six men walked into Patagonia and out of existence."

"They'll turn up," Paloma said. "Men walk out of the cordillera all the time. Lost, ashamed, alive."

"These won't." Roldán said it flatly, the way a man states the price of something. "The search was called off in nine days. Nine days, Almeida. For six government employees. There are families who haven't been given a report. There's a widow in Punta Arenas who's been told to stop writing letters."

Paloma turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "And what is it you want from me? Condolences?"

"I want you to retrace their route. Anchorages, camps, the last known positions. Find what the official search didn't look for." Roldán finally lifted his hand from the newspaper and slid it across. Underneath was a photograph — grainy, aerial or satellite, showing a thread of dark water stitched between walls of rock and ice. A red circle marked an anchorage. "Off-book. No ministry, no paperwork, no names. You'd be paid in cash, well, and anything you find stays between us until we decide otherwise."

"And who is *we*?"

Roldán's smile again, thinner this time. "A man in my trade doesn't answer that question. It's how he stays in his trade."

Paloma looked at the photograph for a long moment. The channel in it was one he knew — or had known, years ago, when knowing such things had been his livelihood and his pride. The walls rose sheer from black water. There was no road within a hundred kilometers. Whatever happened to six men in a place like that, it happened far from any help that mattered.

"No," he said.

Roldán raised his eyebrows slightly, genuinely interested. "No?"

"I don't take jobs from men who won't say who they serve. I've worked for institutions my whole life. Institutions lie, but at least they lie in documents you can eventually read. A man with no name and a client with no face—" Paloma pushed the photograph back across the table. "Whatever happened to those men, digging it up serves somebody. I'd like to know who eats first."

"Your reputation might be worth more than your curiosity," Roldán said mildly.

"My reputation is worth exactly nothing. That's why I can afford principles." Paloma stood, put coins on the table for both coffees, and felt the small sour pleasure of a man who has just refused something and knows he'll regret it. "Good day, señor."

Roldán didn't stand. He gathered the photograph with two fingers and said, to Paloma's back, "The harbor office opens at nine. You should keep current on your paperwork, Captain. Things change."

* * *

They changed that afternoon.

Paloma hadn't intended to go to the harbor office at all. But the ferry schedule for the following week required a stamp — the endless, meaningless stamps of low-grade maritime work — and pride, which he told himself was the only thing he had left, demanded he keep his papers immaculate even if his name was dirt. So at four o'clock he climbed the stairs of the harbor authority building, nodded to the clerk who never met his eyes, and waited while the man thumbed through the registry.

The clerk's thumb stopped. He turned the page around on the counter and tapped it twice, apologetic, procedural, dead-eyed.

There it was. The notice of administrative disqualification — his blacklisting, renewed. Effective immediately, another year added. Grounds cited: *conduct unbecoming of certified mountain and fjord personnel; loss of client confidence; pending review.* The same phrases as before, recycled like the coffee grounds in the hostal café. No incident named. No appeal process listed beyond an address in Santiago that had swallowed two of his letters already.

Paloma stared at it. Eleven months he had carried the first notice like a stone sewn into his coat, telling himself it was temporary, that reviews concluded, that a man who had pulled forty-three people out of the ice alive could not be buried forever under a phrase like *loss of client confidence.* He had believed, in the stubborn animal way he believed in weather patterns, that competence would eventually be remembered.

The renewal meant someone was feeding the file. Someone wanted him under.

"Who signed this?" he asked.

"Ministry directive," the clerk said. "Comes down from above. I just stamp."

"From above what? Which ministry?"

The clerk's eyes flicked up for the first time in three years, held Paloma's for half a second, and dropped. "Captain, I have other files."

Paloma took his papers without the stamp being offered, folded the renewed notice along its existing crease, and walked out into the gray afternoon with the taste of copper in his mouth. The harbor lay flat and dull beneath the low sky, fishing boats knocking gently against their tires, gulls arguing over fish guts on the pier. Everything ordinary. Everything proceeding as if a man's entire working life had not just been extended into another year of exile by an anonymous signature.

He thought about the widow in Punta Arenas who had been told to stop writing letters. He thought about nine days of search for six men. He thought about tents pitched, radios charged, rations stacked — a camp abandoned mid-life, which was wrong in a way that itched at him even now, hours later, the way a bad knot itches at a guide's fingers. Camps like that didn't happen. Men leaving camp in a hurry left messes. Men taken from camp left signs. A camp abandoned mid-life with no tracks leaving it was not a disappearance. It was a stage set.

And someone had renewed his blacklisting the same week a nameless man came to hire him for the thing everyone else had been ordered to forget.

Those two facts sat side by side in his chest and refused to stay separate.

* * *

He found Roldán at dusk, or rather Roldán allowed himself to be found, standing at the rail of the public pier watching the light die over the Sierra Baguales, his good coat finally doing some work against the wind coming off the water.

"Nine days," Paloma said, without greeting. "You said the search lasted nine days."

"Nine days."

"For six men, in fjord country, in autumn. I've searched nine days for one lost German tourist and considered it a failure of effort."

"Yes," Roldán said. "So did many people. Their opinions were not requested."

Paloma came to the rail beside him. Below them the tide sucked at the pilings, and somewhere out in the sound the first evening bird was making its idiotic joyless noise. He looked at his own hands on the wet rail — guide's hands, scarred at the knuckles, rope-worn, hands that had never once failed him in the field and had only ever been failed by paperwork.

"Terms," he said. "Cash, half in advance. My route, my judgment, no check-ins. If I find them alive, you get me a helicopter and a medevac inside twenty-four hours, whatever your client says. If I find them dead, I bring back proof, and their families hear it from me before they hear it from a ministry press release. And when this is done—"

"When this is done," Roldán said quietly, "we discuss your file. Both provinces. Reinstatement is not impossible, Almeida. It's only expensive."

Paloma had spent eleven months telling himself he didn't want reinstatement, that the ministries could keep their certifications and their stamps and their committees, that he was free now, free as weather. Standing at the rail in the dying light, hearing the word spoken aloud by a man who could apparently reach into a harbor office and add a year to a man's exile with a phone call, he understood exactly how much he had been lying to himself, and the shame of it sat in his stomach like ballast.

He hated that he wanted it. He took the job anyway.

"One more condition," he said. "Nobody travels with me. No minders, no partners, no helpful local contacts your client arranges. I work alone in the field. Always have."

Something moved behind Roldán's eyes — amusement, or caution, or the flicker of a man revising a plan. "Agreed. For now." He reached into his coat and produced a heavy envelope, which he set on the rail between them. "Coordinates, the *Cautiva*'s last three anchorages, and a key to the fuel depot at Puerto Toro. The boat you'll want is the *María Elena*, blue hull, third slip. Her owner owes me."

"Everyone owes you."

"Only the ones who ask for things." Roldán pushed off the rail and straightened his coat. At the head of the pier he paused, silhouetted against the last bruised band of light on the water, and turned halfway back. "One thing more, Captain. The *Cautiva* carried sealed orders. Whatever you find out there — and you will find something — remember that sealed orders exist so that no one has to explain them. Including to you."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading up the plank road toward the town, and Paloma stood alone at the rail with the envelope in his hand and the wind coming off Última Esperanza like a long exhalation from the interior — from the black channels and the granite walls and the glaciers that ground forward a centimeter a year and forgave nothing.

He opened the envelope. Inside, on a single sheet, three sets of coordinates in a precise bureaucratic hand, and beneath them, circled in red ink by someone who was not Roldán: the anchorage where the *Cautiva* had last reported in.

Paloma read the numbers twice, committing them to memory out of old habit, then folded the sheet away. The first crossing of the season was tomorrow at dawn. He would tell the ferry company his mother was ill. It was even almost true; his mother was dead, and something in him had been ailing ever since.

Out in the sound, the fog was closing back over the water, erasing the far shore inch by inch, the way an institution erased a man — patiently, administratively, until nobody could swear the shore had ever been there at all.

Paloma turned up his collar and walked toward the slips, to find a blue-hulled boat and the beginning of whatever this was going to cost him.
