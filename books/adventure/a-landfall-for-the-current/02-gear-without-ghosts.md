## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The Cautiva's last anchorage announced itself before I saw it. The chart called it Caleta Sorda — a bite taken out of the western shore of a channel so narrow the water ran brown with tannin from the forest, like steeped tea. But what told me I'd arrived was the smell: woodsmoke, old and cold, soaked into the air the way salt soaks into rope. Someone had burned green lenga here within the week. Green wood holds its smoke like a grudge.

I came in against the current, engine at half-throttle because the channel was littered with deadheads — trees that had let go of the hillsides in some earlier flood and now drifted just proud of the surface, black as whale backs. A guide learns to read deadheads the way a sailor reads swell. Hit one wrong and you open your hull below the waterline in water cold enough to stop your heart in four minutes. I threaded them without thinking about it, which is the only way to do anything well: not thinking.

The anchorage itself was a horseshoe of gray shingle backed by a wall of coigüe and canelo, the trees running up into cloud so low it moved through their crowns like breath through teeth. And there, on the flat above the beach, stood the camp.

I killed the engine and let the boat drift the last fifty meters, because something about the sight of it made me want to approach quietly, the way you approach an animal you suspect is wounded. Tents — three of them, dome-style expedition grade, the kind the ministry buys by the pallet — pitched in a neat row on drained ground above the high-tide line. Guy lines tensioned properly. Rain flies on. A kitchen fly strung between two trees over a fire ring lined with stones, and beside it, stacked under a groundsheet weighted with rocks: ration crates. Fuel drums, lashed upright. A drying line with socks still clipped to it.

No people.

I brought the boat alongside the shingle, stern to the wind, and sat for a moment with my hand on the tiller, listening. Wind in the canopy. Water working the stones. Somewhere upslope, the thin complaint of a stream. No generator hum. No voices. No gulls fighting over scraps, which struck me even then as wrong — a camp that had been eating would have gulls, or skuas at least, hanging around like creditors.

I stepped ashore.

The first thing I did was what any guide does first: I read the ground. The shingle told me little — stone doesn't hold prints — but where the beach gave way to soil and moss at the top of the bank, the story changed. There were boot prints everywhere, churned and overlapped, the traffic of six or seven men over days. Deep prints, loaded-down prints. And then, at the edge of the trampled zone, nothing. Not faded. Not washed out. Nothing. The moss beyond the camp was unbroken, a green carpet with no door in it. It was as if the men had walked out of existence at the boundary of their own footprint.

I crouched and put two fingers into one of the deeper prints. The soil at its base was dry.

That was the first wrong thing, and I want to be precise about why it was wrong, because precision was the only trade I had left. This is Patagonia in the wet season's opening act. It had rained here — I could see it in the standing water in the fire ring, in the sag of the rain flies, in the way the groundsheet over the rations had pooled and darkened. Any print older than a day should have held water. Any print older than three days should have begun to slump at the edges, softened by the constant damp breathing up out of the ground. These were crisp. Dry-floored. Which meant either they'd been made after the rain stopped — and the rain hadn't stopped more than hours ago, if at all; the clouds overhead were the color of wet slate and hung no higher than the treetops — or someone had made them recently and carefully, in boots meant to leave convincing prints.

A camp abandoned mid-life leaves a mess. Men leaving in a hurry leave a different kind of mess. Men who never left at all leave this: nothing.

I stood and walked the camp the way I'd walk a client's route on inspection, and the wrongness accumulated the way water accumulates in a bilge — quietly, then all at once.

The tents were pitched correctly. That was wrong. Not tidy-correctly, the way a soldier pitches a tent for an inspector, but correctly the way tired men pitch tents at the end of a long day: one guy line a little slack, one tent door zipped halfway, a boot scraped of mud lying on its side inside the vestibule of the middle tent. Lived-in. Recently lived-in. I unzipped the nearest flap — I know, I know, but I was being paid to find out what happened, and besides, the camp had already invited me in by refusing to explain itself — and looked inside.

Sleeping bags, rolled loosely. Foam pads. A paperback novel swollen with damp, spine cracked, face-down on a pad as if its reader had stepped out mid-chapter. A wool hat balled at the foot of a bag. Everything in the condition of men who expected to come back tonight. Nobody packs a sleeping bag when they're going somewhere for good, and nobody abandons a book at page two hundred unless they mean to finish it.

The second wrong thing was the galley. Under the kitchen fly, a primus stove sat on a flat stone, and when I turned its valve — gently, out of habit — there was pressure in the tank. Fuel drums full, sealed, spare. Ration crates stacked in rotation order, oldest stock facing out, the way a competent cook runs a larder. Coffee in an airtight tin. Sugar in a screw-top jar with a spoon standing in it, and the spoon was dry.

Dry. In a camp where everything else wore its film of damp. As though someone had wiped down the surfaces. Or as though the rain had been kept off them — which it couldn't have been, not under a fly open on three sides in this weather.

The third wrong thing was the radios. Two of them, mounted in a waterproof case lashed to a tree at the edge of camp, antennas up, and both showing charge lights. Green. Steady. I checked the battery bank wired behind the case — solar panel rigged overhead, angled south, which was correct for this latitude, and clean, wiped of the moss-dust that settles on everything here within a day. Charged radios in an abandoned camp are a message. Radios die in weeks, days in the cold. Whoever left — whoever *was taken* — left them charged so that anyone coming after would find them ready. So that anyone coming after would try them.

I didn't try them. Not yet. Call it superstition, call it tradecraft; I've been accused of confusing the two and I've stopped defending myself. A radio is a door, and I wanted to look through the windows first.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what the government surveyors had evidently done for however many days they'd been here, and what I had once been paid handsomely to do across two countries and too many ranges: reading country. The anchorage was a good one — sheltered from the west, deep water close in, a stream for fresh water, timber for repairs. Whoever chose it knew their work. The Cautiva herself was gone, of course, but her anchoring points remained: a mooring line led ashore to a stout coigüe, chafe gear wrapped at the fairlead, and out in the bay, at low water, I could make out the darker shape of her other anchor's scope through the tea-colored water. She'd been secured by someone who cared whether she was still there in the morning.

So: a competent crew. A well-run camp. Supplies for weeks. Radios charged. And then, between one tide and the next, every man gone, no tracks leading anywhere, no note, no distress, no sign of struggle — and a vessel that had been anchored with care simply absent.

There are explanations. I ran them the way you run a checklist in bad weather, because the alternative to a checklist is imagination, and imagination in Patagonia has always been a worse killer than the weather.

Drowning. Six or seven experienced men don't all drown at once in a sheltered anchorage, and if they had, something would be on the shore. The sea gives back what it takes, eventually, especially in a cul-de-sac of water like this. The shingle was bare except for driftwood and one crusted crab shell.

Animal. Nothing in these fjords takes seven men. A puma might take a man asleep, once, in a bad season. Seven? No.

They walked out. Overland, in the wet season, without taking packs, boots, food, or the radios — leaving prints that the rain forgot to touch. No.

Which left the explanation I didn't want, the one that had followed me since Roldán slid the photograph across the bar table in Puerto Natales: that the Cautiva's crew had not vanished at all. That they had been *removed*. That the camp had been dressed afterward — wiped down, staged, the prints laid on like scenery — by people with time, equipment, and no fear of being interrupted. Because the people who stage a scene are the same people who control whatever comes to look at it.

Except someone had miscalculated. Someone had assumed the rain would erase the evidence of their housekeeping, and the rain had failed them, or they'd run out of time, or they'd never understood this country well enough to know that a dry spoon in a wet camp is a shout.

I sat on a supply crate as the light went the color of pewter and let the unease settle into me properly, because I've learned not to fight it. Unease is data. Every bad decision I've ever made in the mountains began as a small voice saying *something's wrong* and ended with me deciding the voice was nerves. My blacklistings — both of them, Chile and Argentina, twin scars on the same wound — began exactly there, on a glacier in whiteout, with me overriding the part of myself that knew. I don't override it anymore. The part of me that got men home is the part that listens.

And the voice was saying: this isn't a search anymore. Whatever Roldán thought he was hiring — a tracker, a finder of lost men, a mop for an embarrassing spill — this was a scene someone had built to be found and misread, and I was the first reader who'd caught the forgery. Which made me either the most useful man in the fjords or the most disposable, depending entirely on who learned of my discovery first.

* * *

Dark came early, the way it does under that ceiling of cloud, arriving less as night than as a thickening. I made camp fifty meters from theirs — not out of fear, I told myself, out of professionalism; you don't sleep in a stranger's bed until you know why he left it — and ate cold rations under my tarp while the rain started again, soft and businesslike, drumming on the nylon.

I went back to their camp once, with my headlamp hooded to a slit, and did the thing I'd been circling all evening. I keyed the radio.

Static. The long, empty hiss of a frequency with no one on it. I swept the survey band, then the general calling channels, slow, letting each hiss fill the tent of light around me. On the third sweep, low on the band, beneath the static, I heard it: a carrier wave. Faint, steady, rhythmic — not voice, not Morse I could parse at that strength. Just a presence. Someone, somewhere out in all that black water and blacker forest, holding a microphone key down, or transmitting on a loop, or simply keeping a channel warm.

Waiting for someone to answer.

I set the handset down in its cradle as gently as if it were sleeping, and stood a moment in the rain-smell and the radio-glow, feeling the hook go in — that cold, professional fascination that has cost me everything twice and will probably cost me the rest. Because I understood, suddenly and completely, what the charged radios meant. They weren't a courtesy to rescuers.

They were bait. Left for whoever came looking. To see who answered, and how fast, and from where.

Somewhere out in the dark, that faint carrier pulsed on, patient as a fisherman's line, and I stood in the ruined camp of seven men who had left no tracks, and realized I had just become the answer to someone's question.

Behind me, at the edge of the trees, a branch cracked — deliberate, heavy, the sound of a boot placed with intent — and a voice said, in Spanish flattened by the wind:

"Step away from the radio."
