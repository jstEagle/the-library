## 3. The Stowaway Logic

The letter had come in March, and it was wrong in the way that mattered.

Ines Vidal knew her brother's handwriting the way she knew the contour lines on a chart — every slant and abbreviation, the way he crossed his sevens like a man arguing with them. Tomás wrote to her from every posting. He wrote about weather, about bad coffee, about the petty tyrannies of ministry bureaucracy, always with a joke folded into the complaint like salt in dough. His letters were long. His letters were frequent.

The March letter was four lines. It said the survey was going well. It said he'd write again when they made the next anchorage. It was signed with his full name — *Tomás Ignacio Vidal* — which he never did unless something was formal or something was finished.

Then nothing. April, May, June. The ministry sent its standard notice: *the Cautiva's schedule has been extended for operational reasons; family communications will resume at the conclusion of the survey period.* Operational reasons. She had worked for the Survey Directorate herself; she had stamped those notices with her own hand once, back when she still believed the stamps meant something. There was no such form. Someone had invented it, which meant someone needed it, which meant someone was lying.

So she did what cartographers do. She plotted.

Tomás's last known position came from a supply requisition she should not have been able to read and could — credential, clipboard, and the particular blindness of clerks toward a woman who looks like she belongs where she's standing. The Cautiva had taken on fuel and fresh water at Puerto Edén on the twelfth of March. From there, the survey grid extended south and west into the channels, and the requisition listed rations for eleven days beyond resupply. Eleven days at survey speed put their farthest working anchorage inside a bay that appeared on no chart she owned under any name, marked only by a sounding number and a coastline drawn with the lazy confidence of someone who had never stood on it.

She bought a boat.

Not a good boat. A nineteen-foot fiberglass sloop with a diesel auxiliary that coughed like a consumptive and a hull that had, according to the seller — a fisherman in Puerto Natales who would not meet her eyes during the transaction — "seen some ice." She found the crack herself on the second day out, a hairline seam running two feet along the starboard side just above the waterline, weeping a slow dark tear whenever the boat rolled. She sealed it with epoxy and told herself the sea was calm this time of year.

The sea was not calm. The sea is never calm. That is the first thing the charts get wrong, and the last thing they teach you.

* * *

She came into the anchorage at dusk, running the last mile under bare poles with the engine at full throttle because the wind had backed into the northwest and begun to shove the fog ahead of it like a dog herding sheep. The bay opened around her the way secrets do — gradually, then all at once. Granite walls rising sheer out of black water, hung with curtains of rain that moved without wind. Water the color of gunmetal, deep enough to swallow sound. Somewhere above the cloud deck, she knew, there were icefields; down here there was only the drip and groan of a world still being made.

And then: light.

A campfire on the shingle beach, small and disciplined, the kind of fire a professional builds — low, sheltered, burning dry wood that must have been carried in. Beyond it, tents. She counted three before the fog took them back. And tied to a mooring buoy in the lee of the point, a vessel. Steel-hulled, workmanlike, flying nothing. She read the name off the transom as she slid past at a distance she hoped was casual: *Cautiva*.

Her hands went cold on the tiller, colder than the rain could account for.

The Cautiva. Not wrecked, not adrift, not burned to the waterline with her crew's bones scattered on some beach — anchored, tidy, alive. Which meant the men might be alive. Which meant everything she had imagined for six months — the funeral, the flag, the folded letter from a minister whose name she'd already learned to hate — might be unnecessary. She felt relief hit her like a wave over the rail, and then, immediately behind it, the cold undertow: if the ship was fine and the men were gone, then the men hadn't left the ship by accident.

She anchored in the mouth of the bay, too far off to be friendly, too close to be ignored, and sat in the cockpit watching the fire until full dark. Whoever was down there had seen her arrive. Anyone in that camp had seen her. She could see the shapes of the tents against the embers, one of them moving — a figure stepping out, standing, looking at her boat the way you look at a sound in the night.

Ines Vidal had spent her whole life being the person who looked at things carefully. She decided it was time to be looked at.

* * *

She rowed in at dawn, because arriving in the dark is how you get shot and arriving at first light is how you get measured. The tide was out and the shingle was slick with bladderwrack; she dragged the dinghy up beyond the wrack line and walked toward the fire with her credentials in her jacket pocket and her brother's photograph in her head.

He was already awake. Of course he was. Men like him slept like gulls, one eye on the weather.

He stood at the edge of the camp with a mug in one hand and an axe within reach of the other — she noted the axe, noted that it was near rather than in his hand, noted that this distinction had been chosen deliberately so that she would note it. Tall, weathered, somewhere past forty, with the specific economy of movement she associated with men who had spent decades being paid to keep other people alive in places built to kill them. Mountain guide. She'd heard the name Almeida whispered in the Directorate's corridors the way sailors say the names of wrecks. Blacklisted in two countries. The word they used was *unreliable*, which in ministry language means: he told the truth at an inconvenient moment.

"Paloma Almeida," he said. Not a question. Her arrival had been discussed, then.

"Ines Vidal." She let the name sit there. Watched his face.

Nothing moved in it except the eyes — a flicker, quick as a fish turning, gone before it became expression. But she'd seen it. He knew the name. He knew exactly whose sister was standing on this beach, which meant he knew whose letters had stopped, which meant whatever he was doing here, he had known more than she did before she ever launched her cracked little boat.

"You're a long way from a road," he said.

"So are you."

"I'm paid to be."

"Who pays?"

He drank from the mug. Steam curled off it and tore away in the wind. "That's not a question I answer on beaches."

"Then here's one I'll answer for you." She pulled the folded papers from her jacket — not the credentials, not yet; the chart, the one she'd drawn herself, the bay rendered in her own careful hand with the Cautiva's last known track inked in red. She held it out. "Eleven days of rations past Puerto Edén puts your employer's survey team here. Their ship is here. Their camp is here, pitched mid-life, radios charged, boots by the tent doors. And there are no tracks leaving it. I've walked the perimeter twice since I anchored. No trail, no scuff, no latrine dug and filled, no cairn, no signal arrangement. Eleven men don't evaporate, Señor Almeida. So either you're here to find out what happened to them—" she watched his face again, and this time he gave her nothing, gave her so deliberately nothing that the nothing itself was information— "or you're here to make sure nobody else finds out."

The wind came down the glacier valley behind the camp and flattened the grass and pushed the fog across the shingle in long white ropes. He looked at her for a long time. She made herself endure it. Most people look away first; she had learned in government offices that whoever looks away first loses.

"You surveyed the perimeter," he said finally.

"Twice."

"In the dark?"

"The second time."

Something shifted in his posture — not softening, exactly. Recalibration. The look of a man revising a load estimate after weighing the cargo properly. "Your boat," he said. "Nineteen feet, glass over plywood, older than you are. I watched you come in yesterday. You were carrying weather helm the whole approach and your engine's running rich. And you anchored stern-on to the williwaw line coming off that valley, which means either you don't know what a williwaw is, or you know and you were too tired to care."

"I know what a williwaw is."

"Then you were too tired to care." He set down the mug. "Which means you've been pushing hard, alone, on a hull you can't trust, to a place you got from a requisition you weren't cleared to read. And now you're standing in front of the one man in Patagonia who is legally forbidden to help you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was the first honest question either of them had asked, and it deserved an honest answer, and she hated giving it because honesty was a door and doors swing both ways.

"Because my brother is Tomás Vidal. He was on this survey. His letters stopped in March, and the ministry sent my mother a form that doesn't exist, signed by an office that isn't staffed. And because you looked at me just now the way people look at a problem, not a person — and I need one more problem in my life the way I need a hole in my hull." She stopped. Breathed. The rain ticked on her hood. "But I'm here. And I'm not leaving. And you can't make me, because making me would require explaining to someone why you're here, and you can't do that either. Can you."

The silence stretched. Out in the bay, her anchor chain grated over rock as the tide turned, a long metallic complaint, like the sound of the argument itself.

"No," he said at last. "I can't."

* * *

They negotiated over his camp stove, which felt absurdly domestic given that both of them were armed with nothing but leverage. He had fieldcraft, salvage rights on the Cautiva's stores, and the terrifying competence of a man who had clearly already assessed her boat's hull from fifty meters through fog. She had the government charts — the real ones, the restricted sheets with the corrected soundings that no blacklisted guide could touch without committing a second crime — and, more valuable, she had legitimacy. A survey credential opened radio channels, fuel depots, harbor offices. His face closed them.

"Terms," he said. "You follow my calls on the water. When I say reef, you tack. When I say run, you run. You don't go ashore without telling me. You don't touch anything in that camp without gloves."

"And yours?"

"I don't lie to you about what we find. You don't lie to me about what you know. And when this is done — whatever this is — we walk away from each other clean."

"Clean," she repeated. It didn't taste like a word either of them believed in.

They shook on it anyway, his grip dry and hard, hers steady, and Ines understood that what they had actually agreed to was smaller and stranger than terms: two liars' oaths, sworn by people who had each already caught the other measuring the distance to the exit.

She was carrying the folded chart back to her dinghy when she saw him stop at the tideline, half-turned, looking at the Cautiva with an expression she couldn't parse — and then wade out to the mooring buoy without a word, haul himself aboard, and disappear below.

She waited. Ten minutes. Fifteen. The fog came and went in slow tides of its own. When he surfaced again he was carrying a case — steel-edged, government gray, heavy enough that he carried it against his chest like a man carrying a wounded animal. Stamped across the lid, in faded but unmistakable purple: the seal of the Ministry of National Development.

He set it down between them on the shingle and looked at her, and she looked at him, and neither of them touched it.

"That wasn't in our terms," she said.

"No," Paloma Almeida said. "It wasn't."
