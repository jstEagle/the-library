## 4. Terms of Passage

The rain came back before dawn, the fine grey kind that doesn't fall so much as hang in the air like a decision not yet made. I woke in the Cautiva's wheelhouse with my boots on and my jacket buttoned to the throat, which is how a man sleeps when he doesn't trust the company he keeps — or himself for keeping it.

Ines Vidal was already awake. She sat on the chart table with her knees drawn up, her brother's letters spread around her like a hand of losing cards, and she watched me come out of sleep the way you watch an animal you've decided might be dangerous but haven't yet decided about anything else.

"You snore," she said.

"You read other people's mail," I said.

"They're my brother's letters."

"Then they're your business." I sat up and worked the stiffness out of my neck. "The boat's mine until we agree otherwise."

"That's what I want to talk about." She folded the letters along their old creases, one by one, precise as a surgeon closing. "I've been thinking while you slept. You're going to say no to me. I can see it sitting behind your face like weather. So before you do, understand what saying no costs."

I said nothing. In the fjords, silence is a tool, and I have used it longer than I've used most tools.

"I have the charts," she said. "The real ones. Not the tourist hydrographics, not the fishing charts with half the soundings missing. The ministry surveys. Restricted class. Bathymetry of the inner channels that hasn't been published anywhere, because the government doesn't publish what it doesn't want found." She tapped the table. "You are a blacklisted guide, señor Almeida. You cannot legally hold those charts. You cannot buy them, borrow them, or be caught within arm's reach of them. But I'm a credentialed cartographer with a ministry stamp on my license, and until someone revokes it — and nobody has bothered yet — I can walk into any survey office in this country and sign them out."

"And if they catch you?"

"Then I lose a credential I'm about to stop needing anyway." Her chin came up. "And you lose the only person who can tell you where the water is deep enough to float your hull and shallow enough to hide it. Because I've looked at you, Almeida. You know mountains. You know weather. I don't think you know these channels, not the way they need knowing in monsoon season, and I don't think you have the charts to learn them."

She had me, and she knew she had me, and the worst part was how cleanly she'd done it — no begging, no appeal to her grief. Just inventory. What she held, what I lacked. It was the first thing about her that reminded me of myself, and I hated it.

* * *

We negotiated on the foredeck under the awning I'd rigged from the Cautiva's spare canvas, because neither of us could stand being below decks together, and because out there the rain gave us something to look at instead of each other.

Her terms were simple. She came aboard as partner, not passenger. She got equal say on route decisions touching the survey work. And when we found out what happened to Tomás — she said *when*, not *if*, and I let her keep the word because taking it from her would have cost more than it was worth — she got the truth, all of it, whatever it was.

My terms were simpler still. On the water, in the hills, in bad weather, in anything that smelled like danger, my word was final. No debate, no vote. She'd obey instructions the way crew obeys a skipper, instantly and without argument, and if she couldn't promise that, she could take her cracked hull and her restricted charts and go drown somewhere I wouldn't have to watch.

"And if your judgment is wrong?" she asked.

"Then I'll be wrong alone, and I'll answer for it. That's the trade. My mistakes are mine. Your obedience is yours."

"You talk about yourself like a man describing a tool."

"I'm describing what you're buying."

She looked at me for a long moment. Rain dripped off the awning edge between us, counting seconds. Behind her the anchorage lay flat and pewter-colored, the Cautiva riding at her moorings with that terrible tidiness she'd never lost, tents zipped, rations stacked, everything waiting for men who had been gone eleven days without leaving a footprint.

"All right," she said finally. "But I want one more thing. We open everything together. Any documents, any evidence, anything we find that belonged to the team — we look at it side by side. You don't go through my brother's things alone, and I don't go through yours."

It was a strange term. I'd have expected her to demand the opposite — sole rights to anything of Tomás's. But she said it with the flat certainty of someone who has thought it through at three in the morning, and I recognized the logic: she didn't trust me, and she trusted herself less. Grief makes people honest about their own hands.

"Together," I agreed.

We shook on it. Her grip was dry and strong, a working grip, and I remember thinking that whatever else Ines Vidal was — runaway, stowaway, sister — she was not decorative cargo. Then she went below to fetch her charts, and I stayed on deck with the rain, feeling the particular unease of a man who has just made a bargain whose full price tag is still folded inside the paper.

* * *

The ballast was my idea, though I didn't know yet that it was a discovery.

Here is a thing blacklisting teaches you, among its other lessons: when a vessel has been searched by officials, they search the obvious places. Cabins. Lockers. The lazarette. The space under the bunks. They search like men following a checklist, because that's what they are. What they rarely search is structure — the parts of a boat that require tools and patience and dirty hands to open, because officials in good trousers don't do dirty hands.

The Cautiva sat wrong in the water. I'd noticed it the first evening and filed it away, the way you file away a smell of gas or a rope that looks load-bearing and isn't. Her trim was off by centimeters — stern heavy, a list to port that no amount of gear rearrangement explained. A survey launch carries instruments, and instruments are light. Something dense was riding low in her belly.

At midmorning, with Ines bent over her charts below, I went at the ballast boards in the bilge with a pry bar. The wood came away easier than it should have — the screws were bright, recently backed out and reseated. Underneath, wedged between pig-iron ingots that had been shifted aside to make room, was a case.

Grey composite, military-grade latches, sealed with tape over every seam. Stamped across the lid in faded official ink: MINISTERIO DE OBRAS PÚBLICAS — LEVANTAMIENTO HIDROGRÁFICO — USO RESTRINGIDO. And beneath that, smaller, almost apologetic: DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION OF THE SURVEY DIRECTORATE.

I sat back on my heels in the bilge slime and looked at it for a long time.

A survey case. Sealed. Hidden — not lost, not forgotten, *hidden* — under the ballast of a ship whose crew had vanished so completely that even their boot prints had been accounted for. Men who abandon a camp mid-life don't hide their own survey case beneath two hundred kilos of iron first. Men who are taken don't have time. Which meant someone had hidden it deliberately, calmly, with tools, in the hours before whatever happened happened — and then walked away from it, or been walked away from it, leaving it there like a message in a bottle sunk in iron.

"Almeida." Ines's voice from the companionway, sharp. "What did you find? I heard the pry bar."

I should have called down to her. Our terms were an hour old and already I felt the pull of the old habit — look first, decide second, share third. A man blacklisted twice learns that information is the only currency that never devalues, and he hoards it the way misers hoard, compulsively, against famines that never come.

"Together," I said aloud, mostly to myself, and lifted the case out of the bilge.

* * *

We put it on the chart table and stood over it like two people at a graveside who aren't sure they're related to the deceased.

"It's ministry property," Ines said. Her finger traced the stamp without touching it. "Restricted class. If the team left this behind, it means—"

"It means nothing yet. It means someone hid it. That's all we know."

"Someone hid it *before* they disappeared." She looked up. "You saw the camp. Radios charged. Rations stacked. Nobody packs a camp for departure and seals a document case under the ballast unless—" She stopped. I watched her arrive at it, watched the thought move through her face like weather moving over water. "Unless they knew they might not come back for it."

"Or unless they wanted whoever came next to find it."

"Who comes next, Almeida? Nobody was supposed to come next. This anchorage is a blank on every published chart. The only reason either of us is standing here is—" she gestured, taking in the letters, the blacklist, the whole crooked architecture of coincidence that had delivered us both to this floating tomb, "—is that we were both looking for ghosts."

I got a knife and slit the tape. Not the latches — the tape first, carefully, preserving the seal itself, because seals are testimony too and I have learned to treat paper the way other men treat wounded men: gently, and with an eye to what it can still tell you.

Under the tape, the latches opened clean. Inside, packed in oiled cloth, lay a document wallet, a brass sighting instrument wrapped in fleece, and a logbook bound in green board with the survey directorate's crest blind-stamped on the cover. Everything dry. Everything ordered. Whoever had packed this case had done it with the unhurried competence of a professional closing out a season, not a man fleeing in the night.

Ines reached for the logbook. I reached for it at the same moment, and our hands stopped above it, and for a second neither of us moved.

"Together," she said quietly. It wasn't a reminder. It was a test.

"Together." I turned the cover back so we could both see.

The opening pages were ordinary — anchorages, soundings, weather observations in a surveyor's cramped hand, dated through the first weeks of the expedition. Then the handwriting changed. Tighter. Angled harder against the ruling lines, as though the pen were being driven rather than guided. I'd seen that shift before, in mountain logs, in the entries men write when the weather has turned and they've started writing for whoever finds the book instead of for themselves.

Ines made a small sound. I glanced up. She was staring at the changed hand, and her face had gone the color of the rain outside.

"That's Tomás," she said. "That's his writing when he's — when he's frightened. He presses harder. He's done it since he was a boy. His school notebooks—" She stopped, swallowed. "These last entries are his."

The last entry was dated nine days before the camp was abandoned. It was short. Four lines, pressed so hard into the paper that they'd embossed the pages beneath:

*Sounding data transmitted ahead of schedule per orders. Coordinates do not match our readings. Requested clarification from Director. Response: proceed as instructed. I am logging the discrepancy here, in this book, and nowhere else.*

Below that, in the same crushed hand, a single line:

*If this book is found, find the man who signed the transmission order.*

Neither of us spoke. Rain ticked on the deck above us. Out in the anchorage, the Cautiva swung gently on her mooring, tidy as a stage set, and I understood suddenly that we had been living aboard a sealed envelope for two days — that the whole abandoned camp, the charged radios, the stacked rations, the vanished men, were the outer wrapping of a message that had been waiting in the dark under the ballast for someone with a pry bar and a reason.

Ines closed the logbook slowly, both hands, the way you close a door on a room where someone has died.

"The transmission order," she said. Her voice was level, which told me exactly how much it was costing her. "Signed by whom?"

"We'll find out." I took the logbook, wrapped it back in its oilcloth, and set it inside the case — but I did not latch the case shut. Some instinct, some old fieldcraft reflex, kept my hand on the open lid. "Not here. Not tonight. We read every page, we copy what matters, and we decide what we're carrying and where. Slowly. Because Ines—" I made myself say her name; she deserved to hear it said like a fact, "—a crew of six vanishes from a sealed anchorage and leaves no tracks, and the ministry stamps a case DO NOT OPEN and hides it under the ballast. That isn't an accident. That's a machine working exactly as designed. And we just put our hands into it."

She looked at me across the chart table, across her brother's frightened handwriting, across the whole grey weight of what we'd agreed to an hour ago on the foredeck in the rain.

"Then we carry it anyway," she said.

It wasn't a question, and I didn't answer it, because there was nothing to negotiate. We had shaken hands on partnership over a boat and some charts, two strangers trading what the other lacked. Neither of us had known we were also trading in this — a sealed case, a dead-or-dying man's last pressed words, a secret stamped with a ministry seal that neither of us understood yet and both of us now owned.

That night I anchored us in the lee of the point and stood the first watch alone. Ines slept in the wheelhouse with the logbook under her jacket, and I let her, because some things belong to family before they belong to a partnership.

Toward midnight the wind backed south and freshened, and the barometer — the Cautiva's own barometer, still ticking faithfully in its bracket, still doing its job for a crew that no longer existed — began to fall. I watched it fall and thought about the four lines in the green book, and about the phrase *proceed as instructed*, and about how far south the weather was coming from, and how much of it.

By dawn, the sky over the western channels had gone the color of wet slate, and the sea beyond the point had a long, low, organized swell that hadn't been there yesterday.

The monsoon was coming early. And we were going to be on the water when it arrived.
