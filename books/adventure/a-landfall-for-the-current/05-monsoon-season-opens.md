## 5. Monsoon Season Opens

The charts called it Canal Fallos, but the name was a courtesy. What it was, in truth, was a corridor of black water running between walls of granite and ice, so narrow in places that the forest on either side seemed to lean over us, and so deep that the soundings dropped away into numbers no one had ever bothered to verify. Ines Vidal had spent three years drawing coastlines for a government that had never once asked her to draw them honestly, and she knew the difference between a chart and a lie better than most. This channel, she thought as the *Cautiva*'s old tender — the boat Roldán had left them, a nineteen-foot fiberglass workhorse with a hairline crack in its hull that Paloma had found with his thumb inside an hour of boarding — this channel was the kind of place where the government's maps simply stopped pretending.

The rain began at dawn, or what passed for dawn under that sky: a slow brightening of gray into a different, wetter gray, like ink diluted in milk. It did not fall so much as arrive, filling the air between the water and the clouds until there was no boundary left. By midmorning we were running through weather rather than beside it.

I stood at the bow because sitting made me feel more afraid, and I had learned already that fear in a small boat is a thing you manage like ballast — you keep it low, you keep it distributed, you do not let it gather all on one side or it will capsize you. The water came off the strait in long gray rollers, then shorter, steeper ones once we turned into the channel, and each one lifted the hull and set it down again with a slap I felt in my teeth.

"Sheet in," Paloma said from the helm, not loudly. He never said anything loudly. His voice had the quality of the channel itself — flat, dark, carrying further than it seemed it should.

I hauled the line. My hands were already raw from two days of this, the skin at my palms gone soft and white and beginning to split at the creases. He had told me to wear gloves. I had told him I could read a chart better than he ever would. Neither of us had mentioned it since.

The crack in the hull was the thing I kept listening for. Not hearing — listening. A sound below the engine note, below the water slapping, a thin high whisper of water finding its way through glass fiber into a space where water should not be. Paloma had stuffed the crack with epoxy putty in Puerto Edén, working by lamplight while the fishing village's dogs argued over fish heads around our mooring, and he had done it with a patience that frightened me more than haste would have. Haste meant he thought the problem was small. Patience meant he thought we might die of it.

"Water in the bilge," I said, twenty minutes after the rain arrived. I hadn't checked the automatic pump. I didn't need to. There is a smell a boat gets when it is taking water — a cold mineral breath rising out of the deck boards, the smell of the sea coming up to meet you instead of waiting politely below.

"I know," Paloma said.

"You know."

"The pump's cycling every four minutes. It was every eleven yesterday." He adjusted the wheel a degree, maybe two. "The putty's holding. The flex is opening the seam a little at a time. We're not sinking. We're leaking at a rate I can manage."

*We're not sinking.* I wrote it down later in my log, because it seemed like the kind of sentence a person should preserve — the calmest possible arrangement of words wrapped around the least calm possible situation. But at the time I only said, "And if it opens faster?"

"Then we beach." He nodded toward the north shore, where a shelf of pale rock ran down into the water beneath a hanging glacier. "There. If it comes to it. That shelf runs half a kilometer. Good landing even in a sea like this."

He had been reading the shoreline the way I read contour lines — continuously, automatically, filing away every option before the need for options arose. I realized I had been doing something similar with him. Watching. Cataloguing. The way he sat in the boat was itself a kind of information: weight low, knees soft, always braced against the next motion before it came, as though his body had made peace years ago with the fact that the world was unstable and the only honest response was to be stable inside it.

The channel narrowed. The walls rose. And the wind, which had been a nuisance, became a creature.

It came down off the icefields in gusts that had no rhythm I could find — a lull, then a blow that laid the rain flat and turned it horizontal, stinging like gravel. Each gust hit the water first, tearing the tops off the swells into smoke, and then hit us, shoving the stern sideways so that Paloma's arms moved constantly, small corrections, a conversation held at the speed of reflex. Spray came aboard in sheets. My waterproofs were, I discovered, waterproof in the way government reports are accurate — technically, formally, and not when it mattered. Cold water found the seam at my collar and ran down my spine in a line I would feel for days.

"Ines." His voice again. "Take the tiller."

"I don't—"

"Take the tiller. I need both hands on the anchor line. There's a cove in two hundred meters, port side. You'll see the gap in the trees. I'm going to point. When I point, steer at it. Don't steer at where it was. Steer at where it is."

"What happens if I miss?"

He looked at me then, and there was nothing cruel in it, which was somehow worse. "Then we find out what the rocks are like today."

I took the tiller. The boat immediately became a living argument — the wind pushing the bow one way, the following sea pushing the stern another, the engine thrashing when the prop came out of the water on a swell crest. Every instinct I had said to fight it, to force the boat straight, to impose order. I fought it for perhaps thirty seconds and lost ground the whole time.

"Don't correct everything," Paloma said, moving forward along the gunwale with the coil of line over his shoulder, moving like a man walking a corridor he'd built himself. "Correct the important things. Let the small ones go. She'll tell you which are which."

*She'll tell you.* As if the boat were a colleague. As if the storm were a correspondent writing letters he'd learned to read.

Then he pointed, once, arm extended, finger steady despite everything — and I saw the gap. Or I saw what he had seen: a darkness in the tree line, a notch where the forest stepped back from the water, invisible from any angle except the one we happened to occupy at the moment he chose. I steered at it. The wind tried to make a liar of me twice; twice I let the small corrections go and saved the big one, and the boat came round the headland into the cove like a key turning into a lock.

The water inside went still so abruptly it felt like a trick. One moment chaos; the next, a pool of green-black quiet ringed by granite and dripping coigüe forest, the rain falling straight down now, gentle as an apology. Paloma had the anchor over the side before the boat finished losing way. Then he was bailing — not frantically, just steadily, a bucket moving in a rhythm older than engines, while I sat with the tiller in my white-knuckled grip and discovered that my whole body was shaking only now that it was permitted to.

"You missed it by less than a meter," he said. Bailing. Not looking at me.

"I've never steered anything in my life bigger than a dinghy on Lago Villarrica."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because you corrected everything." He tipped the bucket over the side. "People who've done it a while learn what to ignore. You'll learn faster than most. You hate being wrong too much to stay wrong."

I didn't know whether that was a compliment. I suspected it wasn't entirely. I suspected, also, that it was the truest thing anyone had said to me in years.

* * *

We stayed in the cove through the worst of the front, and the front, the radio said in its crackling official Spanish, was only the opening statement of a much longer document. *Frente de mal tiempo. Vientos de 60 nudos en los canales interiores.* Sixty knots. The announcer's voice had the same tone as the ministry bulletins I used to proofread — the voice of an institution describing catastrophe in the passive tense, as though the weather were a policy being implemented.

Paloma rigged a tarp over the cockpit and cooked rice with dried fish and something from a tin whose label had worn away, and it was the best meal I had eaten in a year, which said less about the food than about hunger earned honestly. While he cooked, I pumped the bilge and watched the water come up the hose in pulses and thought about Tomás.

Tomás had loved boats the way he loved everything — theoretically, beautifully, from a slight safe distance. Our father had a photograph of the two of us at Melinka, Tomás maybe nine years old, holding a tiller with both hands and an expression of pure administrative delight, as though steering were a form of paperwork going very well. He would have hated this channel. He would have hated the leak and the wind and the bucket. And he had been out here anyway, in a survey vessel with six other men, writing letters home that stopped in March, and some part of me understood now — sitting in a cove he had probably anchored in, following coordinates he had probably written — that he had known things in those last weeks that he couldn't say in letters, and that the silence itself had been a kind of message, sent in the only cipher available to a man being watched.

"You're doing the thing," Paloma said.

"What thing?"

"Where your face goes somewhere else and your hands stop moving. Out here that's how people get hurt. Grief can wait for harbor."

"Grief doesn't file a schedule."

"No," he agreed, and handed me the pot. "But it waits anyway. It has no choice. You do."

We ate. The rain ticked on the tarp. Somewhere up the cove a seal surfaced with a gasp and sank again, unimpressed by us. And I asked him, because the storm had loosened something in me the way storms do, "Why did they take your license? Really. Your version, not the harbor office version."

For a long moment I thought he wouldn't answer. He looked at the fire — the small spirit stove, blue flame in the gray light — with an expression I had begun to recognize as his thinking face, which was indistinguishable from his refusing-to-answer face, except that his jaw worked slightly.

"A client wanted a route through a glacier field in a season I judged suicidal. I refused. He found another guide. Four climbers went in. Two came back." He stirred the pot. "The inquiry found I had abandoned a client in a commercial dispute. The client had friends. The friends had ministries. Now I'm a danger to the public."

"And were you? A danger?"

"I was right about the season." He said it without heat, without self-pity, the way you'd state a bearing. "Being right is the most dangerous thing you can be out here. The mountain doesn't care. But men care. Men will spend enormous sums to avoid learning that someone was right when they were wrong."

I thought of the sealed case under the floorboards, the ministry stamp, the coordinates I had memorized in case the case itself was taken from us. I thought of Tomás's last letter, the handwriting suddenly cramped, the sentence that had haunted me for months: *Some of these numbers are going to look different when they get south, and I need you to remember which ones.*

"Paloma," I said. "When we open that case — and we're going to open it, don't argue — I want you to understand something first. Whatever's in it, my brother touched it. So whatever it makes him into, guilty or innocent, I'm going to know it. And I'd rather know it in a storm than in a courtroom."

He looked at me across the little stove, and for the first time since Puerto Natales, something in his face moved past assessment into something almost like respect.

"All right," he said. "In a storm."

* * *

The wind died near midnight, the way fronts die here — not gradually but all at once, like a door closing, leaving a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse. I took the middle watch because he refused to sleep through his own and someone had to, and I sat in the cockpit under the ruined stars watching the cove breathe.

That was when I heard it again. The whisper. Thin, high, patient, below the silence: water finding the seam, a little faster than before.

I woke him. He listened for ten seconds, nodded once, and said, "Dawn. We run for Puerto Edén, re-glass it properly, and then we go in behind the front. There's a window. Weather like this always leaves a window, if you're willing to move when everyone else is hiding."

"And if the window closes while we're in it?"

"Then we're committed." He lay back down. "Same as everything else worth doing."

I sat my watch and listened to the leak and thought: *this man reads water the way I read lies, and neither of us learned it in a classroom.* And far up the channel, beyond the headland, beyond the walls of granite, the inner channels waited with whatever the Cautiva had left behind — and now, in the bottom of our own hull, the sea had begun keeping its own account of how long we had to reach it.
