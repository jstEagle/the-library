## 6. What the Logbook Says

The case came up out of the ballast like something drowned.

Paloma had gone into the bilge at first light, when the rain had thinned to a fine grey whisper and Ines was still asleep in the wheelhouse with her boots on and her arms crossed over her chest like a woman guarding herself even in dreams. The *Cautiva* sat low on her moorings where they'd tied her off alongside their own crippled boat, and the water inside her hull was cold enough to make his hands ache after a minute. He worked by feel mostly, by the logic of weight. A boat that had been stripped of everything portable still carried forty kilos of lead shot lashed under the engine bed — more than she needed, more than any survey vessel needed — and men who hide things almost always hide them where the hiding looks like function.

He cut the first strap and felt the shift before he saw it. The lead came away in his hands, and behind it, glassed to the stringer with marine epoxy and wrapped in waxed canvas, was a case the size of a ship's chronometer box. Grey composite. No handle worn smooth from use. A ministry seal pressed into a lead disc over the latch, unbroken, the wax still sharp-edged, as if it had been closed yesterday instead of however many months ago the *Cautiva* had come to this anchorage and never left it.

He sat in the bilge water for a long moment with the case across his knees, listening to the drip of condensation off the deckhead, and understood that he was holding the reason six men had stopped existing.

* * *

Ines woke the way she seemed to do everything — abruptly, defensively, one hand already reaching for the chart tube beside her bunk. When he set the case on the chart table between them she went very still, the way animals go still, and he watched her read the seal twice before she said anything.

"That's a Ministerio de Energía y Minería seal."

"I know what it is."

"It's not a survey seal." Her voice had dropped half a register, flattening out. "The Dirección de Límites does hydrographic work. This is—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Paloma recognized the gesture of a woman editing her own knowledge in real time, deciding how much of it to spend. "My brother's letters never mentioned anything like this."

"Your brother's letters mentioned coordinates," Paloma said. "You told me that on the second day. Bearings he wasn't supposed to share, marked *for Papá, only*. Men don't smuggle bearings home to their fathers unless the official numbers are wrong."

She looked at him then, fully, and he saw the thing underneath the suspicion she wore like oilskin: not doubt of him, but dread of the answer. She wanted him to be wrong. He could see her wanting it, the way you want a weather window that isn't coming.

"Open it," she said.

* * *

The seal broke with a sound too small for what it ended. A crack of wax, a click of the latch, and then the lid came up on foam cut to fit: two brass-bound logbooks, a sealed envelope stamped CLASIFICADO in red that had faded toward rust, a roll of film canisters, and a folded transparency sheet that caught the grey light through the porthole and threw a faint green shimmer across the table like something alive under ice.

Ines took the logbooks. Paloma took the transparency.

He held it up to the light and the fjordlands appeared — the same channels he'd been reading for three days, the same granite shoulders and glacier-cut water — but overlaid now with contour lines in a color no hydrographic office used. Deep magenta, dense as bruising, pooled beneath a stretch of territory that every map in Chile drew with a dotted line and a footnote. The seam ran under the watershed. Under the line itself. Under whatever argument two nations were still having in conference rooms while the rock kept its own counsel.

"Madre de Dios," Ines whispered, and it wasn't prayer. It was arithmetic. She'd turned to the last pages of the first logbook, and her finger was moving down columns of figures, and Paloma watched her face do what trained faces do when the numbers stop making sense — the small contraction around the eyes, the lips parting slightly, the whole architecture of competence meeting its limit. "These readings are wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong deliberately." She pulled the second book toward her and opened it flat, pinning the pages with the chart weight, a brass horse that had belonged to someone who loved maps enough to carry one to the end of the world. "Look. Conductivity transects along the eastern shore, here and here and here. Same instrument, same calibration notes, same operator initials — T.V., that's Tomás — but the values don't agree with the core samples logged four days earlier. Not close. Not instrument drift. Someone moved the anomaly." She tapped the magenta pool on the transparency. "This is where the deposit actually is. The logbook puts it thirty kilometers northwest, on the other side of the line. On *their* side of the line."

Paloma stood very still. Outside, the rain had thickened again, ticking against the porthole glass, and somewhere down the channel a chunk of calving glacier went into the water with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"Say it plainly," he said.

Ines's hand had stopped on a page of marginalia — cramped pencil annotations in the margins of the official entries, written fast, written angry, the handwriting of a man arguing with his own signature. She read aloud, and her voice stayed level in the way voices go level when the alternative is breaking:

"*Transect 14 re-run at CO's instruction. Values adjusted per directive. I signed because he said refusal ends the contract and the contract ends the funding for the second season. Requested my objection be entered in the record. Request denied.*"

She looked up. "Dated eleven days before the last entry."

"And the last entry?"

She turned the page. There wasn't much of it. A position fix, a weather note, and then four lines in the same cramped pencil, but slower now, each word set down like a stone:

"*We are not being extracted. The launch that came is not ours. R. says cooperate and we go home; F. says cooperate and we go into the record. I have put the true figures where they cannot be burned. If anyone reads this, we did not desert. We were removed. God forgive me for the weeks I helped them lie.*"

The cabin was very quiet. Rain on glass. The creak of two hulls rubbing at their lines. Somewhere below, water finding its way, patient as everything else in this country.

Paloma found that he had braced himself against the chart table without noticing, both hands flat on the wood, as if the boat had taken a swell. It hadn't. It was the sentence that had hit him — *we were removed* — arriving not as words but as weight, the way a guide knows a slope has shifted before his eyes confirm it. Six months he had carried the question the way you carry a stone in your boot: what happened to the *Cautiva*. Tents pitched. Rations stacked. Radios charged. Boots by the flaps of tents, tobacco tins on mess tables, a camp abandoned mid-life with no tracks leaving it. He had built theories all the way up the coast — fire, flood, a crevasse party gone wrong, some stupid domestic catastrophe, a man knifed over cards. Ordinary explanations. Explanations a man could survive knowing.

There was nothing ordinary in this cabin now.

"They didn't vanish," he said. His voice sounded foreign to him, scraped. "They were taken. At night, from this anchorage, aboard another vessel — and everyone who signed the paperwork agreed to write *vanished* instead." He straightened. "That's why the gear was left. Gear tells a story. You leave the story intact so no one goes looking for the ending."

Ines hadn't moved. She was still looking at her brother's handwriting, and Paloma saw her lips shape the words *God forgive me* without voicing them, and something in her face closed and reopened, closed and reopened, grief and fury taking turns at the same door.

"He knew," she said finally. "He knew they might kill them, or bury them somewhere, and he still spent his last hours on shore hiding the truth in a case under the ballast instead of—" Her breath caught. "Instead of writing to me. Instead of saying goodbye. He spent them on *this*." Her palm came down on the logbook, not hard, but with finality, like a gavel. "And I've spent four months thinking he ran. That he deserted. That's what the letter from the ministry said — *the crew abandoned their post*. My mother believed it. I half believed it."

"The ministry wrote the letter," Paloma said. "The ministry wrote the story. That's what ministries do." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and let it stand. He had his own letters like that, filed in offices in two countries, each one a small official murder of his name. He knew exactly what paper could do to a man. He had simply never seen it done to six men at once.

* * *

They worked through the rest of the case in silence broken only by the turn of pages.

The classified envelope held a directive on ministry letterhead, unsigned in the space where signatures live but initialed in the corner — two letters, M.O., in a hand too confident to be ashamed — instructing the survey leader to "harmonize" the conductivity data with the geopolitical requirements of the pending boundary submission. Harmonize. Paloma turned the word over and found it beautiful in the way rot is sometimes beautiful, all symmetry and no substance. They had been ordered to move a mountain of lithium thirty kilometers to the left, and the order called it music.

The film canisters held photographs: core samples, instrument faces, shoreline markers. And in the middle of the roll, printed last, five frames of men standing on the *Cautiva*'s aft deck in field jackets, squinting into whatever camera Tomás had balanced on a rail or a rock. Paloma counted them twice. Six men. In the fifth frame they had arranged themselves shoulder to shoulder, and someone — Tomás, judging by the angle — had written on the back of the print later, in pencil, in that same cramped furious hand: *Equipo Cautiva, último día en tierra propia.*

Last day on their own land.

Ines held that photograph for a long time. Her brother stood third from the left, thinner than in the picture she carried, smiling the way people smile when the joke is private and bitter. She didn't cry. Paloma had expected tears and understood, watching her, that he had misjudged her — that this woman had already done her crying somewhere alone, in some port town, over a letter that lied, and what was left now was harder than tears.

"The man who initialled the directive," she said. "M.O. Do you know him?"

"No."

"Roldán will."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. Paloma thought about the radio sitting in their own wheelhouse fifty meters away, about the check-in schedule Roldán had imposed, about the voice that would ask, tonight or tomorrow, *anything to report?* — casual as a neighbor asking after the weather. He had taken money from that voice. He had told himself it was salvage work, recovery, a job like any other job for a man with no other jobs. And all the while the real cargo had been sitting twenty meters from his berth, sealed in wax, waiting for someone honest enough to break it open.

"Ines." He made himself say it plainly, because plainness was all he had left to offer anyone. "Understand what this changes. Yesterday we were two people looking for answers. Right now, on paper, we're two people holding state secrets in disputed territory, in possession of evidence that a minister — maybe *the* minister — falsified a survey and disappeared six men to protect the falsification. The moment anyone official learns we've opened this case, we stop being searchers. We become loose ends."

"You think I don't know that?"

"I think you know it about yourself. I'm telling you it applies to me too." He gestured at the case, the logs, the photograph of six men smiling on their last day. "I was hired to find this. Which means whoever hired me may not want it *found* — they may want it *handled*. There's a difference, and I walked past it at the harbor office because I wanted my name back." The admission cost him; he paid it anyway. "I need to know, before we go one channel further, whether you're with me because you trust me, or because I'm currently the fastest way to your brother."

Ines gathered the logbooks against her chest, both volumes, the way she'd slept with her arms crossed — guarding, always guarding. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she looked at the photograph lying on the table, at the thin man third from the left, and her voice came out quiet and absolutely certain.

"My brother hid these figures knowing it might cost him everything. The least I owe him is to be as brave with them as he was." She met Paloma's eyes. "You broke the seal. Whatever comes of it, that makes us the same kind of fool. That's enough trust for open water."

It wasn't reassurance. It was better — it was terms. Paloma nodded once, and began wrapping the case again in its waxed canvas, and was halfway through the knot when the VHF in the wheelhouse crackled to life across the water, carrying through the rain with terrible clarity.

"*Vessel at anchor, Cautiva anchorage. This is Armada patrol craft Isla Hoste. State your identity and business.*"

The voice was young, bored, and precise. And it was asking, in the polite grammar of the state, whether the loose ends intended to tie themselves off.

Paloma looked at Ines. Ines looked at the radio. Neither of them moved toward it.
