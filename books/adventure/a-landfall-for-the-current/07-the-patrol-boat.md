## 7. The Patrol Boat

The fog came first, the way it always did in the channels — not arriving but *condensing*, as if the sea itself were exhaling. Ines stood at the wheel of the little boat with her hands light on the spokes, watching the world shrink to a circle of grey water perhaps two hundred meters across, and she thought about how easy it would be to disappear out here. How easy it had apparently been for eleven men on a survey vessel called *Cautiva*.

Paloma was below, checking the bilge for the third time that hour. The crack in the hull was a fact they'd agreed to live with the way you live with a bad heart: carefully, attentively, without naming it aloud. The pump ran every twenty minutes. She kept count. It was a kind of prayer.

She heard the engine before she saw anything else — a deeper note than their own, diesel-heavy, patient. Then the fog thinned where something large was pushing it aside, and the shape resolved: grey hull, white superstructure, the Chilean naval ensign hanging limp and correct off the gaff.

A patrol boat. Forty meters of state authority, moving at six knots through water that belonged to no one.

"Ines." Paloma's voice from the companionway, low, already reading her face. "Don't stop."

"They'll make us stop."

"Then don't look like we mind stopping."

He came up beside her, and she watched him do the thing he did — the transformation she was beginning to recognize. His shoulders settled differently. The wariness in his eyes didn't vanish but went underground, replaced by the flat, competent boredom of a working skipper with nothing to hide. He'd worn this face for customs officers and harbor police for years, she realized. He'd built it the way you build a shelter, stone by stone, after the weather took everything else.

The loudhailer crackled. The voice was young but controlled, educated, carrying the particular clipped courtesy of an officer who has rehearsed his lines.

*"Vessel in the channel. This is the patrol vessel Quillén. Come to heading one-eight-zero and reduce speed. We will come alongside. Do not alter course further."*

Paloma eased the throttle back. "One-eight-zero," he murmured to her. "That's toward the Cautiva's anchorage."

"I know."

"So they know where we've been, or they're guessing, or someone told them." He said it without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "Smile if you can manage it. Officers like a woman who smiles."

"I'm not going to smile."

"No," he said, and there was something almost like approval in it. "Probably better you don't."

* * *

They came alongside with a fendered grace that spoke of practice — the patrol boat's hull kissing theirs, lines thrown and made fast, two sailors dropping aboard before Ines had finished killing their engine. Everything about it was routine. That was the frightening part. Somewhere in Santiago or Punta Arenas, men in offices had decided that boats in these channels should be stopped, and so boats were being stopped, and the machinery of it ran as smoothly as a clock in an empty room.

The officer who stepped down onto their deck was maybe thirty-five, tall, with the kind of pale, unweathered face that said he'd spent his career on bridges rather than fo'c'sles. Lieutenant Ferrer, according to the name tape. His eyes did a full circuit of the boat in four seconds — the deck, the gear, the sealed case lashed under the tarp amidships, Paloma, herself — and then returned to neutral, as though filing everything away in a drawer he could reopen later.

"Buenos días," he said. "Papers, please."

Paloma handed over his documents. Ines watched Ferrer read the name — Almeida — and saw the flicker, small as a fish rising, cross the officer's face. So he knew. Of course he knew. They hadn't sent a lieutenant who didn't.

"The guide," Ferrer said pleasantly. "I thought you were blacklisted."

"I am. I'm not guiding. I'm transporting cargo for a private client. Salvage assessment." Paloma's voice was gravel and indifference. "Paperwork's in order."

"It usually is." Ferrer passed the documents to a sailor without looking away from Paloma's face. "And your client's name?"

"Confidential. Commercial contract."

"Mm." Ferrer turned to Ines, and his manner changed — softened, warmed, became the manner of a man meeting a colleague rather than a suspect. "And you are?"

"Ines Vidal." She gave him her credential folder before he asked. Let him see she wasn't afraid of paperwork. "Cartographer. Hydrographic survey, third class."

"Third class," he repeated, smiling. "Modest. Most people round up."

"Most people haven't been audited."

Something moved behind his eyes — reassessment, quick and professional. She felt it land and felt, beneath the fear, a hot and unwelcome flicker of pleasure. *He believed me.* Or he appeared to believe her, which in the economy of power was the same currency.

"And what is a third-class hydrographic cartographer doing in the inner channels in monsoon season?" Ferrer asked. "With a blacklisted guide? Following, unless my chart plotter lies, almost exactly the track of the survey vessel Cautiva?"

The fog pressed close around them. Water dripped somewhere. A sailor shifted his weight, boots squeaking on wet decking.

Ines heard herself answer, calm and clear, in a voice she barely recognized as her own:

"We're not following the Cautiva. We're correcting its work." She let a beat pass — long enough to seem reluctant, short enough to seem honest. "The ministry contracted an independent verification of the hydrographic data from last season's survey. There were questions about some of the soundings. My firm was engaged to re-run three transects. Señor Almeida's boat was available and, frankly, cheap, because no one else will hire him. I assumed that was why your ministry chose him, actually. Deniability."

The word hung in the air between them. She watched Ferrer take it, turn it over, examine it for hooks.

"Deniability," he repeated.

"Isn't that why you're here, Teniente? Someone flagged our transit and now you need to be able to say you checked." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her everything she had; she hoped it looked like boredom. "Check us. You'll find charts, instruments, a leaking hull, and two people who are cold and want to finish the transects before the next front. If the ministry wanted us followed, they picked a strange way to do it — sending a patrol boat to announce itself."

For a moment — three seconds, five — she genuinely did not know which way he would go. Then Ferrer smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and that was when Ines understood the true shape of the danger. Not that he might arrest them. That he might *believe* her, and still do exactly what he'd come to do.

"Your transects," he said. "Which sectors?"

"Sectors four and nine. The narrows above the anchorage at—" she named a bay at random, a real bay, plausible, forty miles south of where they'd actually been.

Ferrer nodded slowly. He walked the length of the boat as he talked, hands clasped behind his back, a man inspecting rather than searching, and paused at the tarp covering the case. He didn't lift it. He rested one finger on the canvas, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't want to wake.

"Salvage assessment," he said to Paloma. "And verification transects. Two stories for one boat."

"Cheaper that way," Paloma said. "We split fuel."

"Of course." Ferrer lifted his finger from the tarp. "You know, this season has been bad for small craft. Three vessels lost in the channels since September. Weather like this, a cracked hull, alone out here — anything can happen. The sea is very thorough." He turned back to them, and his voice stayed pleasant, almost kind. "I'd advise you both to complete your work quickly and return to port. The channels will be closing soon. Officially, the advisory goes out next week. Unofficially—" he spread his hands, "—you're hearing it now."

"We hear it," Paloma said.

"Good." Ferrer signaled his sailors, and the boarding party withdrew with the same smooth routine it had arrived. At the rail he paused, half-turned, and said — to Ines, only to her — "Your brother must have taught you well, Señora Vidal. Tomás always read a chart faster than anyone in his cohort."

The world went very still and very loud at once.

"I don't—"

"No need," Ferrer said gently. "Verification transects. Sectors four and nine. I'll note it in the log." He stepped up onto the patrol boat's deck, and the lines came off, and the grey hull drew away into the fog with that same patient diesel note, taking the state's attention with it — for now.

* * *

Neither of them spoke until the engine sound had faded entirely and the fog had closed the circle again. Paloma restarted their own motor and held their course, and Ines stood at the wheel with her hands locked on the spokes because if she let go she thought they might shake visibly, and she could not allow that. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

"You lied to him," Paloma said finally. Not accusing. Measuring.

"Yes."

"You invented a ministry contract, a firm, two sectors, a reason. In under ten seconds. No hesitation."

"He knew about Tomás." Her voice came out flat. "He said his name like he was handing me something — like a card he'd been keeping. They know who I am. They've known since before we left Natales."

"Since before you found me, probably." Paloma exhaled through his nose. "Which means either Roldán reported you, or they've been reading your mail, or both. Which means this whole channel is a lit corridor, and we've been walking down it waving."

Ines stared at the compass card trembling in its binnacle. Sectors four and nine. She'd made them up, and now they existed — written into a naval log, attributed to her, a fiction with her signature on it. She thought about how easily the words had come, how she'd watched Ferrer's face and adjusted in real time, fed him the word *deniability* because it was the word a man like him would respect, had constructed the lie the way she'd once constructed coastlines: from soundings, from inference, from what had to be true beneath what could be seen.

She had thought of herself as an honest person. A careful person. A woman who filed things correctly.

"I was good at that," she said quietly. "Wasn't I."

Paloma was silent a moment. When he answered, his voice had something in it she hadn't heard before — not comfort, exactly. Recognition.

"Yes," he said. "And that's not a good thing to discover about yourself out here. But it's better to discover it now than when it matters less."

"When does it matter less?"

"It doesn't. That's the trap." He nodded forward, into the fog, toward the coordinates waiting in the case under the tarp — the seam, the falsified readings, whatever truth Tomás had died — had *disappeared* — trying to bury or save. "He gave us a warning dressed as advice. Complete your work quickly. The channels are closing. They're not closing because of weather, Ines. They're closing because of what we're carrying."

She looked back once, astern, at the empty grey water where the Quillén had been. Nothing there. Nothing anywhere. Just fog and swell and the enormous indifferent patience of the fjords.

But she knew now — the way you know a current under a flat surface — that the emptiness was inhabited. That the state's reach extended past the last charted point, past the last hamlet, into water so empty even the birds had given it up. And that somewhere behind that grey curtain, a man with a kind smile was writing her invented transects into an official log, building her a file, brick by brick, out of her own words.

"Paloma," she said. "Turn south. Whatever Tomás marked next — I want to find it before they decide we've found enough."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he put the wheel over, and the bow swung south into the fog, and the channel closed behind them like water over a dropped stone.
