## 8. Roldán's Second Offer

The radio crackled to life at dusk, when the light in the channels had gone the color of pewter and the rain had settled into that patient, vertical drizzle that Patagonia does better than anywhere on earth — a rain that doesn't fall so much as *insist*, filling the air until breathing feels like drinking.

Paloma was below, wedging the sealed case deeper under the bunk with his boot heel, when Ines's voice came down through the companionway.

"Someone's calling. It's for you."

Nobody called for him. That was the first wrongness. He climbed into the cockpit where she stood hunched under the dodger, her hand resting on the set's volume knob as if she could throttle the voice before it arrived. The antenna wire hummed against the backstay in the wind off the glacier, a thin metallic whine like a struck tuning fork.

"Almeida." The voice was Roldán's, unmistakable even compressed to static and syllables — that unhurried Santiago drawl, the voice of a man who had never once been cold or wet or in a hurry. "Good evening. Or is it morning there? Hard to keep track of the channels."

"It's evening." Paloma took the handset. His hand was steady. He noticed that about himself the way he'd notice a loose shroud or a frayed anchor line — catalogued it, filed it, didn't trust it. "You're transmitting on an open frequency."

"I'm transmitting on a frequency I own." A pause, the sound of ice shifting in a glass, three thousand kilometers north of any ice. "I'll be brief, because your battery life is brief and my patience, while considerable, is not infinite."

Ines had gone very still beside him. He could feel her attention like heat from a stove.

"You said you were working for an insurance concern," Paloma said. "Salvage assessment. That was the brief."

"That was the *cover*, Almeida. You're not naive enough to have believed it, and if you did believe it, then the blacklist was deserved for reasons other than the ones they printed." Roldán let the insult sit there, soft as snowfall. "The client is the Ministry of National Development. Third Undersecretariat. Territorial Resources Division. The name will mean nothing to you. The name behind the name might: Minister Arriaga."

The wind gusted. Somewhere up-channel, a calving glacier boomed like a door slamming in an empty house, and the sound rolled across the water and kept rolling, out past them, toward places with no one left to hear it.

"I don't know the man," Paloma said.

"No. But you know his signature now, don't you? You've seen the seal on the case. Ministry blue wax, the territorial crest, the lot number filed under a survey authorization that officially never existed." Another pause. Paloma pictured him in some glass office above Providencia, city lights smearing the window, a woman waiting at home with dinner going cold. "Here is what interests me, Almeida. You are a careful man. Blacklisted men who survive are careful by definition. So when you found that case — and don't insult us both by denying it; we've had eyes on the anchorage since before you arrived — you opened it. And now you know what's inside. And now the conversation changes."

Paloma looked at Ines. She was watching him with an expression he couldn't read — not fear exactly, something more like a woman counting exits.

"What do you want?" he said.

"The case. Delivered intact to a vessel I'll designate. And silence — which costs you nothing, because silence is the one commodity a blacklisted guide has always dealt in." Roldán's voice softened, and that softening was worse than any threat. "And in exchange—"

He laid it out. Not rushed. Savoring it, almost. The Chilean registry flag lifted from Paloma's file. The Argentine blacklist — the one that had followed him across two borders and cost him the Torres del Paine contract, the one that had made his name a synonym for *liability* in every guiding office from Coyhaique to Ushuaia — expunged. Certified reinstatement. Back pay, if he wanted to be vulgar about it. His name spoken in hiring offices again without the little flinch, the sideways glance, the polite email that says *we've decided to go with another candidate* in four different ways.

"Everything they took," Roldán said. "Returned. With interest."

* * *

Later — much later, after the transmission died and the rain thickened and Ines went below without a word — Paloma sat in the cockpit under the tarp and let the temptation come for him, because he knew better than to pretend it wouldn't.

This was the thing no one understood about disgrace. It wasn't the poverty. He'd been poor before and poor was just weather; you dressed for it and moved through it. It wasn't even the shame, not really — shame wore smooth eventually, like a stone in a riverbed. It was the *exclusion*. The way doors closed without slamming. The way his name, once spoken aloud in certain rooms, changed the temperature. For six years he had lived outside the glass looking in at men doing work he could do better than most of them, and every winter the glass got thicker.

And here was a voice offering to open the door. All he had to do was carry a box.

He thought about the case under the bunk. He hadn't told Ines everything he'd read in it — the survey readings, the seam running under disputed water like a vein of silver under someone else's skin, the annotations in a hand that wasn't Tomás Vidal's smoothing the numbers flat, making a mountain look like a molehill, making a lie look like arithmetic. He hadn't told her how the falsification worked, how elegant it was, how bureaucratic evil always is. He hadn't told her because telling her would make it real, and real things demand decisions, and he was already drowning in decisions.

*Carry the box,* the voice said. *Take the record clean. Take your name back.*

And the shameful part — the part he would carry longer than the case itself — was how long he considered it. Not a flash of weakness, dismissed in a breath. He sat in the rain and *negotiated*. He built the argument the way you build a shelter: frame first, then skin. *The evidence exists elsewhere. There were eight men on that team; surely copies exist. Delivering the case changes nothing except whether I eat this winter. And what good is truth in the hands of a man no court will let within a hundred meters of a witness stand? A reinstated guide can testify. A blacklisted one can't even get a hearing.*

It was good reasoning. That was the horror of it. It was the same reasoning he'd used once before, years ago, on another mountain, in another lifetime, when he'd made the call that put a client's body in a crevasse and his own name on two national blacklists. Good reasoning, deployed at the exact moment character should have overruled it.

He checked his watch. Forty minutes. Forty minutes of rain and rationalization before something in his chest — the same stubborn animal thing that had gotten him down off the mountain alive when everyone said he shouldn't have survived — finally stood up and said *no*.

Forty minutes. He was ashamed of every one of them.

* * *

Ines was awake when he came below. Of course she was. She sat at the chart table with the oil lamp turned low, and the duplicate logbook — Tomás's cache, pulled from the sea cave two days ago — lay open in front of her, her brother's cramped handwriting marching across the page like ants fleeing a flood.

"He called," she said. Not a question.

"He called."

"And?"

Paloma lowered himself onto the settee across from her. The boat rocked gently at anchor, hull creaking, the sound of a living thing talking in its sleep. "He offered me my record back. Both countries. Full reinstatement."

Ines looked up. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadows, sharp as the coastline on her charts. "What did it cost?"

"The case. And silence about the seam."

She held his gaze for a long moment. He watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic he'd done, forty minutes of it, and he wondered if she could see those forty minutes on his face the way a guide reads weather in a sky.

"And you said?"

"I said I'd need to think about it."

Her expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a door closing, quietly, not slammed but latched. "You said you'd think about it."

"I stalled. Stalling isn't answering."

"No," she agreed. "Stalling is stalling. My brother stalled too, I think. Before he signed whatever he signed. I imagine he told himself he was only buying time." She turned back to the logbook, but her finger stayed pressed on the page, holding Tomás's words flat under her fingertip as if they might fly away. "Roldán will call again."

"Yes."

"And next time he'll stop offering and start taking."

"Yes."

Ines closed the logbook with both hands, carefully, the way you close a coffin. "Then we'd better decide what we're taking to Punta Arenas before he does. Because I read the note in the cache, Almeida. There's a name in it. A minister's name. And I don't think my brother wrote it down expecting it to stay in a cave."

Outside, the wind backed two points and began to build. Paloma felt it in the rigging before he heard it — a rising note in the wires, thin and climbing, the sound the southern channels make when they're clearing their throat before they speak.

The storm was coming. And somewhere north of them, moving at the speed of a decision already made, so was the navy.
