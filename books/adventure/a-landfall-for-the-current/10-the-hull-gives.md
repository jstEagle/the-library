## 10. The Hull Gives

The sound came at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the monsoon — a gray thickening of the light, rain still falling in ropes, the channels the color of wet slate. Paloma heard it before he understood it: a knock from below the waterline. Not the sea hitting the hull. The hull answering.

He was on deck in four seconds, barefoot, life vests already in his hands.

"Ines." He said it once, level, the way you say a name when you want it to mean *wake up and don't waste time being afraid*. "Get the case. Get the duplicate log. Dry bags. Now."

She came up the companionway with her hair plastered to her face and no questions asked, which told him how far they had come since the anchorage where she'd stood on the rocks with a survey credential and a boat she didn't trust and looked at him like he might be the second-worst thing to happen to her that year. She'd learned the boat's voice by now. Everyone did, eventually. The sea taught it to you whether you wanted the lesson or not.

The crack had been there since before him — a scar in the planking below the waterline, glassed over by some previous owner with more optimism than skill. He'd known about it the day he stepped aboard. He'd known it would fail. What he hadn't known was when, and so for eleven days he had listened to the hull the way other men listen to their own hearts, cataloguing every flex and groan, waiting for the note that meant the conversation was over.

That note had come.

* * *

Open water between the islands — the long crossing Tomás's annotations had marked as unavoidable, the only gap in the archipelago's shelter wide enough for the storm swell to stand up and stretch. The wind had backed around in the night and now came out of the southwest with the full weight of the season behind it, and the sea had gone from gray to green-black, the color of deep water deciding things.

Paloma put the bow into it and drove.

There was no cleverness left in it. This was the part of the work that never made it into guidebooks — the hours of pure forward motion against a world that wanted you stopped, throttle held at three-quarters because full would hammer the cracked strake open and half would let the following seas board them. Ines wedged herself against the wheelhouse bulkhead with the dry bags strapped across her chest like a child, both of them, the ministry case and the canvas-wrapped logbook, and watched him with an expression he couldn't read through the spray.

"How long?" she shouted.

"Twelve minutes if we're lucky. Eight if we're not."

"To where?"

He pointed. A low dark shape off the starboard bow, half-dissolved in the rain — an island with no name on any chart he trusted, just a suggestion of land, a beach maybe, maybe not. On these charts the cartographers had worked from ship decks a hundred meters offshore and guessed at everything behind the first line of trees. Half the islands out here were cliffs wearing costumes.

"The beach," she said. "You're sure there's a beach."

"I'm sure there's land. The rest is negotiation."

The knock from below came again, longer this time, and then a new sound underneath it — a hiss, thin and constant, the sound of water finding its way in and liking the company. He could feel her doing the arithmetic beside him. Bilge pump rated for X liters per minute, ingress running at Y and climbing, distance to shore Z. He watched her arrive at the answer and watched her jaw set instead of her eyes widen, and thought, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the crisis: *she would have made a good guide.* Then he thought about what the profession had cost him and took the thought back, or tried to.

The hull gave its verdict two kilometers out.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing nobody who hadn't been through it understood — catastrophe at sea was mostly bookkeeping. A seam opened along the old repair, quiet as a zipper, and the pump went from losing ground to losing the war, and suddenly the arithmetic wasn't arithmetic anymore. Water over the sole boards. Cold coming up through his boots. The engine note flattening as the shaft bearings found saltwater.

"Ines!" He pointed aft. "Bail! Anything — bucket, cooler, cookpot — bail like your brother's life depends on it, because mine does!"

She moved. God help him, she moved well — no panic, no wasted motion, down into the flooding cabin hauling the bilge bucket and the fuel-drum funnel and anything with a concave side, and set up a rhythm passing water up and over the transom while he held the bow on and prayed to nothing in particular for a beach.

The island grew. That was the whole of his ambition now: let the island grow.

* * *

There was a beach. It was forty meters of gray shingle and black sand tucked behind a gravel spit, and it was the single most beautiful thing Paloma Almeida had ever seen, and he told it so, out loud, in Spanish and then in English, as he brought the dying boat in on the last of the engine and the last of his judgment together.

"Brace!"

The keel touched. The hull, insulted one final time, gave up its argument with the sea entirely — he felt the crack become a confession, felt the boat begin to settle even as the bow grounded — and he killed the engine and got the stern line ashore in one throw, waist-deep in freezing surf, Ines hauling from the beach with the dry bags already slung high on her shoulders because he'd made her practice exactly this drill twice in calm anchorages and she'd complained both times and done it perfectly both times.

They got everything off in nine minutes. He counted. It was the kind of thing you counted, afterward, when counting was all that kept the shaking orderly.

Then the tide took the boat.

Not all at once. It settled onto its port side with a sigh, almost dignified, water sliding green and smooth over the deck, and within an hour there was nothing to show for eleven days of channel-running but a mast tip and a hawser running taut to the beach, humming in the wind like a struck string. Ines stood at the waterline watching it go under and said nothing for a long time.

"It kept us alive," she said finally. "A boat with a cracked hull I bought off a man who lied to me about everything else."

"So did everyone else in this story," Paloma said. "Welcome to Patagonia."

* * *

The monsoon peaked through the afternoon the way a fever peaks — not gradually but all at once, the sky closing to a ceiling of dirty wool, rain going horizontal, the trees on the slope above the beach bending and streaming and holding, because these trees were born to this and never expected anything else. They dragged the gear above the storm berm and got the tent up in the lee of a fallen coigue, and then Paloma did the inventory, kneeling in the wet shingle with numb fingers, and the inventory said what he already knew.

Survival: yes. They had the tent, the stove, fuel for perhaps five days, rations for six if they were disciplined, a tarp, rope, the emergency kit. They would not starve and they would not freeze. There was fresh water running off the hill in a dozen streams. An uninhabited island in the middle of the richest rainfall on earth was, ironically, a generous place to be stranded.

Evidence: also yes. Barely.

The ministry case had taken water — of course it had; everything took water here, water was the local government — but the seal had held and inside, wrapped in oilcloth that someone aboard the *Cautiva* had packed with more care than the ministry had ever shown its crew, the original readings were legible. The duplicate logbook, Tomás's cache from the sea cave, had fared worse. Canvas sodden, pages swollen, ink beginning to bloom at the margins like frost ferns. Ines knelt over it turning pages with the delicacy of someone handling a wound, and Paloma crouched beside her and built a low screen from the tarp and lit the stove beneath it, low flame, patient heat, and they dried the book page by page for two hours while the storm screamed at them for wasting fuel on paper.

At some point he realized what he was doing.

Not drying a book. Guarding one. His body had organized itself around those pages without asking his permission — he'd placed the case uphill of the high-tide line without thinking, checked its position three times in an hour, caught himself calculating sightlines from the beach to the treeline as though the danger might come out of the forest rather than the sky. As though Ferrer's patrol boat could materialize out of the rain. As though Roldán's voice could reach him here, thirty miles of open water from anywhere, offering clean records in exchange for silence.

But it could. That was the lesson of the last two weeks. The state's reach extended into empty water; he'd watched it happen from the deck of a smaller boat. And here he sat on a nameless beach with the only physical proof that a minister had erased seven men, and somewhere out in the weather was a navy lieutenant whose questions were warnings, and the thought arrived with total simplicity:

*I'm not salvaging evidence anymore. I'm carrying a cause.*

He tested the thought for vanity and found, to his surprise, that it held weight. All his professional life he had served — ministries, agencies, clients, contracts, always someone else's purpose drawn on someone else's map. Blacklisted twice for refusing to falsify route reports, and even his refusals had been private, sullen, useless — pride, not principle. But this was different. This was a thing outside himself worth the keeping, and his hands knew it before he did, the way a guide's hands know a slope is rotten before his eyes admit it.

Ines looked up from the drying pages. Her brother's handwriting covered them, dense and urgent, coordinates cross-checked three times, and beneath the numbers a sentence she must have read twenty times already, because her lips moved with it.

"He wrote *if anything happens to me, the numbers are the witness*," she said. "Numbers can't testify, Paloma. People do. That's why they took the people."

"Then we make sure the numbers get to people who'll speak for them."

She closed the logbook carefully and looked at him across the stove flame, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod slowly, as if confirming something she'd suspected since the anchorage.

"You've decided," she said. "About Roldán's offer."

The rain hammered the tarp. Out past the spit, the drowned boat's mast tip rose and fell with the swell, marking time like a metronome for everything they'd lost and everything they hadn't.

"I'm deciding," he said. "Every hour. Ask me again tomorrow."

But they both knew the answer had already been given — written in the way he'd lashed the case to the highest point of the camp, above the gear, above the food, above everything except themselves.

* * *

Night came early and stayed hard. They took watches because Paloma refused not to — habit, discipline, the skeleton of a profession that had taken everything else and left him this. At midnight he sat under the tarp with the stove turned low and listened to the storm work the coastline, and Ines slept with one hand resting on the oilcloth bundle like a child with a dog, and Paloma Almeida, disgraced twice over, blacklisted in two countries, employerless and boatless and thirty miles from help, discovered that he felt something he had not felt since before the notices went up in the harbor offices of Puerto Natales and Bariloche.

Useful.

It frightened him more than the crossing had. Because usefulness was exactly what Roldán was selling — reinstatement, legitimacy, a name cleared — and here he was getting it free, from a soaked logbook and a dead-or-drowned man's handwriting, and the question that walked him out into the rain at one in the morning to check the case lashing yet again was this: when the choice came, and it would come, would he know the difference between protecting the truth and protecting the feeling of being the one who protected it?

The wind answered with a gust that flattened him against the coigue trunk. And through the noise of it — faint, impossible, gone before he could swear to it — he thought he heard an engine. Not the storm's voice. A steady, mechanical thrum, out beyond the spit, where no honest boat had any reason to be at one in the morning in the teeth of the monsoon.

He stood in the rain and listened until his ears ached.

Nothing. Only the sea, working the shingle, patient as a bureaucracy.

But he did not sleep again, and at first light he woke Ines with a hand on her shoulder and one word that turned her face gray:

"Smoke."
