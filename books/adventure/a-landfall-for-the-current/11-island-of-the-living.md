## 11. Island of the Living

The island had no name on any chart Ines owned, which meant it had a name somewhere, in some surveyor's private notebook, and that someone had stood where she now stood and decided it wasn't worth ink. It was a shoulder of granite and gravel rising out of the channel, crowned with wind-bent coigüe trees, and it was the first solid ground either of them had touched in thirty hours.

She knelt at the tideline and let the rain hit the back of her neck until the heat of the paddle finally drained out of her. Her hands were ruined — blisters opened, then salted, then opened again — and when she tried to close her fingers around a handful of shingle they shook like something dying.

"Drink." Paloma crouched beside her with the water bottle held to her mouth without ceremony, the way you'd water an animal too exhausted to argue. "Small sips."

She drank. The water tasted of the jerry can's plastic and of survival, which was its own flavor, and she found she could have wept over it if she'd had anything left to weep with.

Above the storm line, wedged between two granite slabs where the wind couldn't get its teeth in, there was a hollow floored with moss and sheltered by an overhang of rock. Paloma had seen it from the water before she had seen anything — before she'd even registered the island as land rather than a darker smear of weather. He carried her up to it without asking, one arm under hers, half-dragging, and she hated how much she needed it and said nothing, because he also said nothing, and between the two of them they built a silence that was almost a kindness.

* * *

By late afternoon the monsoon settled into its long mood — not the screaming squalls of the crossing but a steady, vertical, biblical rain, the kind that seemed less like weather than like a decision made about them personally. The temperature dropped. Paloma worked.

She watched him work the way she'd watched him work through the whole wretched journey: the stove assembled inside a windbreak of stones, the wet clothes strung on a line improvised from fishing trace, the tarp rigged so that condensation ran off its edge instead of dripping onto their bedding. Every motion economical, nothing wasted, nothing performed. He didn't talk while he worked. He barely talked at all. And yet the camp assembled itself around him like something growing, and she understood — not for the first time, but for the first time without resentment — why the wilderness trusted him more than any government ever had. He didn't ask anything of it. He just paid attention.

"Your hands," he said, without looking up.

"They're fine."

"Give me your hands."

She gave him her hands. He took them in his own — broad, scarred across every knuckle, warm from the stove — and began, matter-of-factly, to dress the worst of the blisters with strips of tape and a salve that smelled of pine tar. His touch was impersonal and exact. She stared at the top of his head, at the gray coming in above his ears, and felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was clenched.

"When did you last sleep?" she asked.

"Tuesday."

"It's Friday."

"Then I'm owed a nap." But he finished the bandaging first, tucked the tape back into the kit with the precision of a man who would need to find it blind in the dark someday, and only then sat back against the rock and closed his eyes.

Ines pulled the dry blanket around her shoulders and looked out through the curtain of rain at the channel they'd crossed, whitecapped and furious, and thought about Tomás.

* * *

She woke to darkness and the smell of rice cooking, and for a moment she didn't know where she was — the disorientation of deep, unplanned sleep, the body dragging you under and letting you go hours later without apology. The stove hissed. Rain drummed the tarp. Paloma sat cross-legged by the flame, feeding it split kindling one piece at a time, and the light moved on his face like water.

"You slept four hours," he said.

"You counted."

"I count everything. It's the job."

"What is the job, exactly? Still? Roldán's contract is at the bottom of the channel along with the boat."

He considered this, turning a piece of kindling in his fingers. "Finding out what happened to the Cautiva," he said finally. "That part floats."

Ines took the tin bowl he offered — rice with dried fish and something green he'd stripped off the rocks, sea kale or sorrel, bitter and alive-tasting — and ate in silence for a while. The food did something chemical to her despair. That was the thing no one told you about catastrophe: it ran on calories like everything else.

"My brother signed," she said.

Paloma's spoon paused, then continued. He didn't pretend not to understand. That was one of the things she had come, against all her instincts, to trust about him: he never wasted mercy on false comfort.

"The annotations in the case," she went on. "The ones flagging the falsified coordinates. I've been reading them all day in my head, over and over. They're corrections, Paloma. You don't correct a document unless you've read it carefully. You don't know exactly which readings were doctored, and how, unless—" Her voice caught on the arithmetic of it. "Unless you were there when they were doctored. Unless your own hand was one of the hands."

The rain filled the pause.

"He wrote to me every week," she said. "From Punta Arenas, from the staging camp, from the ship. Pages. He wrote pages about tide tables and about a cook who burned everything and about how the aurora looked over the icefields. And then the letters stopped, and the letters after that — the two that came — were short. Careful. Like someone writing with a censor looking over his shoulder, except there was no censor, there was just..." She set down the bowl because her hands had started shaking again and she didn't want to see it. "I know my brother's handwriting. The last letter was his handwriting wearing a stranger's voice."

"And now?"

"Now I think he saw what they wanted and he gave it to them. Signed the clean version. Took the ministry's money or its approval or whatever it was they were paying in. And then something happened — something he saw, or someone he couldn't look at anymore, maybe the men who wouldn't sign — and he recanted. Too late. Too quietly. He flagged the coordinates in a sealed case instead of going to a newspaper, and then he hid a duplicate in a sea cave like a man burying evidence for a trial he knew he might not live to attend." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He was always careful, Tomás. Careful men die of their carefulness."

Paloma fed another stick to the stove. When he spoke, his voice was low and even, pitched under the rain.

"You want him to be guilty."

"Yes."

"That's honest."

"It's worse than honest. I want him to be guilty because if he's guilty, then he's alive. Guilty men get to live with things. Innocent men get disappeared." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "And I want him to be innocent because he's my brother and I loved him before I knew what love cost. Both wants at once. All day. Every day since the letters stopped."

For a long time Paloma said nothing. Then he shifted, and the firelight caught the old scar she'd noticed once before, running from his left ear down into his collar, and he began to talk — not the way he talked when he was giving instructions, but slowly, like a man paying out rope.

* * *

"There was a German couple," he said. "Aconcagua, eleven years ago. My clients. A doctor and his wife, both strong, both experienced enough to be dangerous — experienced people stop listening. We got weather on the upper mountain. Whiteout, wind doing ninety. I called the turn. The husband wanted the summit; it was three hundred meters away and he'd trained two years for it. I put myself between him and the route and told him no."

Ines waited.

"He waited until I was breaking trail below a serac and went around me. Took his wife up. The weather closed behind them." Paloma's voice didn't change register at all; it stayed flat and careful, like a man walking on ice he already knew was thin. "I found her on the descent. Him I never found. The widow filed a complaint. So did the agency, because a dead client is bad business and a guide who gets overruled is worse. There was an inquiry. I was cleared of negligence and blacklisted anyway — cleared and punished in the same breath, which is how institutions apologize. Chile struck me off. Argentina followed within the year, because the agencies talk to each other faster than governments do."

"You weren't responsible."

"No. But I was the one who let it be possible. I turned my back on a client I knew was hungry for the summit, because I was tired and it was cold and I wanted the argument to be over." He looked at her then, and his eyes in the firelight were the color of the channel in bad weather. "The blacklist isn't the punishment, Ines. The punishment is that I agree with it. Every winter, some part of me agrees with it."

The rain came harder against the tarp, a long rolling drumroll, and eased.

"So when Roldán offered you your name back," she said quietly, "he wasn't offering you a job. He was offering you absolution."

"He was offering me the thing I'd sell anything for. That's how you know it's a trap. Real things aren't for sale; only the wanting is." He poked the fire. "You tracked your brother across four hundred kilometers of fjord on a cracked hull with a forged credential. What are you selling?"

"Nothing. I don't have anything left to sell." She thought about it. "That's not true. I have certainty. I sold that years ago — to the survey institute, to the idea that the map is the territory and whoever draws it owns the ground. I believed the state's lines were real lines. I drew them myself." She looked at her bandaged hands. "Tomás believed it harder than I did. He thought the survey was service. He thought the numbers were neutral. And then the numbers weren't neutral, and he found out what they were actually for, and he—"

"—did the careful thing," Paloma finished. "Sealed it. Hid it. Told no one."

"Told no one," she echoed. "Not even me."

"Not even you," Paloma agreed, "because telling you would have killed you. Look at where we're sitting. Look at what's chasing us. He did the math with the information he had and he chose your life over your opinion of him." He said it without softness, which was why she believed it. "That's not innocence and it isn't guilt. It's what people are. Both things, stacked."

Ines found she was crying — not dramatically, not the way grief looks in novels, but steadily, stupidly, tears running into the rice bowl she'd forgotten she was holding. Paloma didn't touch her. He passed her the water bottle instead, and refilled the stove, and kept watch on the dark mouth of their shelter with the patience of a man who understood that some weathers you simply wait out.

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had burned to a red heart and the rain had softened to a whisper — she heard him say her name.

"Ines."

"Mm."

"If Tomás signed, and recanted, and died for it — will you still want to find him?"

She turned the question over honestly, because he had earned honesty and because lying to herself in front of a witness was the one luxury she couldn't afford anymore. "Yes," she said. "More. A brother who failed and paid for it is mine. A brother the ministry invented for me isn't anyone's."

Something moved in Paloma's face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if he still kept one. "Then we're the same kind of fool," he said. "We both want the true version, even if it's worse."

"Worse is at least real."

"Worse is at least real," he agreed.

She lay back against the pack and looked up at the underside of the tarp, where the firelight trembled, and felt the strange weightless exhaustion of a person who has finally set something down. For six weeks she had been alone with her suspicions, carrying them like ballast, and now they were in the open air between two people and somehow lighter for it. Not resolved. Lighter. She hadn't known those were different things until tonight.

Paloma took the first watch. She heard him moving at the mouth of the shelter, unhurried, checking the line, the tarp, the case lashed in its waterproof wrap — the case they had nearly drowned for, the case whose contents named a minister and damned a survey and made two exiles into, possibly, witnesses.

She was almost asleep when his voice came again, quiet, angled toward the channel.

"Light," he said. "Out on the water. Moving against the current."

Ines was awake instantly, every ache forgotten. Through the gap in the rocks she saw it: a single white running light, far out in the black channel, steady and deliberate, sweeping south along the coast they'd abandoned — the track of something searching, patient, thorough.

A patrol boat's pace. Ferrer's pace.

Paloma reached over and pinched out the stove. The dark folded over them like a hand.
