## 12. Ferrer Returns

The rain had been falling for two days without conviction, a grey drizzle that soaked without drowning, and Paloma had almost begun to think of the island as survivable. That was the mistake. He knew better. In the fjords, comfort was the storm's way of lowering your guard before it showed you its teeth.

He was on the shingle beach at first light, checking the snares he'd set among the driftwood — nothing in them, as he'd expected; the island was too small to feed even its gulls properly — when he heard the engine.

Not an outboard. Not the coughing two-stroke of a fisherman's panga. This was a deep, disciplined thrum, four-stroke and heavy-hulled, the sound of government money turning over water. He stood very still among the bleached logs and watched the channel.

The patrol boat came around the southern headland at a quarter past seven, white superstructure, hull number stencilled in navy blue, the Chilean flag snapping wet at its stern. *Marinero Fuentealba*, if he remembered the class correctly. He did. He remembered every vessel class that had ever been used to look for him or look away from him.

Ines was still asleep under the tarp they'd rigged between two fallen coigües, her back against the salvaged case, one hand resting on it even in sleep, the way a child holds a parent's sleeve. Paloma crouched by the fire pit and fed dry kelp stems into the embers until they caught, then set the kettle on. If this was going to be what he thought it was going to be, they would both want hot water.

* * *

She woke when the boat was still half a kilometre out, because she'd learned that much already — that his silences had textures, and the texture of this one meant trouble. She came down the slope with her hair plastered flat by drizzle, eyes gritty from salt and broken sleep, and looked at the patrol boat as though it were a large animal that might be harmless and might not.

"Ferrer," Paloma said.

"You can tell from here?"

"I can tell from the way it's coming." Most boats approached a stranded party at speed, all urgency and goodwill. This one came at a walk. It slowed a full hundred metres off the beach and held there, engines idling, a figure at the rail raising a loudhailer but not yet using it. Everything about the approach said: *we have time, and you don't.*

"They found us," Ines said. It wasn't a question.

"Or they never lost us." Paloma poured tea into the tin mugs and handed her one. "Drink it while it's hot. And listen to me now, before they say a word." He turned to face her fully, and she saw something in his expression that frightened her more than the boat did — not fear, but a kind of cold, total attention, the look of a man reading avalanche terrain. "Whatever they offer, whatever they promise, however reasonable it sounds — the moment they mention the case, we are no longer people being rescued. We are cargo being recovered. Do you understand?"

Her jaw tightened. "And if they just want to help?"

"Then they'll take us aboard and ask questions later. They won't lead with terms." He drank his own tea in three swallows. "They'll lead with terms."

* * *

The launch came ashore at eight o'clock sharp, and Lieutenant Ferrer stepped out of it into ankle-deep water without waiting to be carried, which Paloma noted and filed away: a man who performed humility carefully. He was drier than the day deserved, in a pressed olive uniform, and he climbed the shingle with his cap under his arm like a man arriving at church rather than a wreck site.

"Señor Almeida." His voice was pleasant, unhurried. "And Señorita Vidal. You've given several ministries a great deal of anxiety."

"We gave them nothing," Paloma said. "We've been here nine days."

"Nine days." Ferrer looked around the camp with professional interest — the tarp shelter, the fire pit lined with stones, the case sitting under the oilskin where they'd left it visible, because hiding it would have been worse. "Remarkable work. The *Cautiva*'s tender, I assume? We've been tracking reports of a small craft in these channels since the flooding began. You're lucky. Another week and this coast stops forgiving anyone."

"We know," Ines said. "Our hull failed."

"So I see." Ferrer smiled at her, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and Paloma watched Ines receive it and hold herself very still. "Señorita Vidal, your family must be beside themselves. The ministry has been looking for you too."

"The ministry," Paloma said, "didn't know I existed until Roldán hired me."

Something flickered across Ferrer's face — not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. "Roldán is a contractor. Contractors have clients. Clients have concerns." He turned his cap slowly in his hands. "May I sit? Standing negotiations make everyone dramatic."

Nobody invited him to sit. He sat anyway, on a driftwood log, with the ease of a man who had done this on worse beaches.

* * *

Here is what Paloma understood, watching him: Ferrer was not a cruel man. That was the trap's finest engineering. Cruel men you could refuse on principle; cruelty gave you a handle to grip. Ferrer was a polite man doing a polite job, and the job was this:

"The survey team is alive," Ferrer said.

The words dropped into the camp like a stone into still water. Beside him, Paloma felt Ines go rigid.

"Alive," she repeated.

"All six of them. Recovered ten days ago by a ministry vessel during the flood emergency, taken to a facility in the north for medical observation and debriefing. They're being cared for. Tomás Vidal is in good health — I'm authorized to tell you that specifically, Señorita. Your brother asked about you."

Paloma heard the sentence the way he heard ice cracking underfoot — the surface holding, the structure beneath already gone. Because he'd read the logbook. Because he'd seen Tomás's annotations flagging falsified coordinates, and he'd stood in a sea cave and read a note naming a minister, written in a hand that expected not to survive to explain itself. Men who were rescued do not leave notes like that. Men who expect to be buried do.

"And the debriefing," Paloma said carefully. "What are they debriefing?"

"Standard procedure. Trauma assessment, operational review." Ferrer spread his hands. "You understand how these things work. There will be an official account. The account is already being drafted, in fact — that's partly why I'm here. The minister wants the story complete before the press gets hungry. A team lost in the floods, heroically recovered. It plays well. It closes the file." He paused, delicately. "There's only one element missing."

"The case," Ines said.

"The case," Ferrer agreed, as if she'd passed a test. "The survey materials. Instruments, logs, charts — the team's working documents. They were scattered during the evacuation, naturally. Chaos, flooding, heroic confusion. If those materials could be returned, the record would be whole. The team could be reunited with their work. Everyone could go home." He looked at Paloma with frank, reasonable eyes. "Including you, Señor Almeida."

"There it is," Paloma said quietly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Your rescue has a price tag, and you've just shown me the receipt." He stood up. He hadn't decided to stand; his body had simply refused to keep sitting across from this man a moment longer. "Let me tell you what I know, Lieutenant, so we can stop performing for each other. I know the *Cautiva* was abandoned mid-life — tents pitched, rations stacked, radios charged, no tracks leaving. I know the survey readings in that case show a lithium seam running under disputed territory, and I know someone upstream ordered the coordinates falsified, and I know at least one member of that team flagged it in writing. I know your 'recovered' crew left a cache in a sea cave containing a duplicate logbook and a note naming a minister, written by a man who believed he was going to die. So when you sit on my beach and tell me they're alive and well and being 'debriefed,' what I hear is: they're being held somewhere quiet until the story hardens. What I hear is that 'found alive' is a headline being manufactured while the men who wrote the truth sit in a room being persuaded to sign their names to a lie. Again."

The beach went very quiet except for the surf and the idling engines offshore.

Ferrer did not flush. He did not bluster. He folded his hands on his knees and regarded Paloma with something that looked almost like respect, and that was the most frightening thing yet.

"You should understand your position," he said mildly. "Your boat is destroyed. Your food is measured in days. The next front comes through tomorrow night, and it's the worst of the season — the forecasts alone would tell you that if you had a radio, which you don't. I am offering you a warm ship, hot food, medical attention, and a ride to Punta Arenas. All I ask in return is government property." He gestured at the case without looking at it. "Refuse me, and I sail away, and you two stay here and find out how the channels treat people the state has stopped looking for. No one will report you missing. No one will come. That's not a threat, Señor Almeida. It's arithmetic."

"It's a good offer," Paloma said. "That's what makes it filthy."

"Paloma." Ines's voice cracked on the second syllable. She wasn't looking at Ferrer; she was looking at him, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic of her own — the storm, the hull, the case, her brother's name spoken aloud by a stranger in a clean uniform. "Tomás is alive. Did you hear that? He's *alive*. If we give them the case—"

"If we give them the case, the logbook becomes scrap paper and your brother becomes a deserter or a madman, whichever serves the draft better. If we give them the case, those men signed their lives over twice — once in the cave, once on this beach." He kept his voice low, kept it steady, and hated how much it cost. "I know what I'm asking you to weigh. I'm asking you to weigh it anyway."

"He's right," Ferrer said softly, "that the men signed things. That's rather the point of debriefing." He rose, brushed driftwood grit from his trousers, and put his cap back on, and the transformation was complete: no longer a visitor, an officer again. "I'll make the offer once, formally, for the record neither of us is keeping. The ministry will recover its property. You will be treated generously. Or you will remain here, and the ministry will regretfully conclude that two civilians drowned in the flood — a story, I might add, that writes itself far more easily than the other one."

He walked back down the shingle toward the launch, then paused, waist-deep in the grey water, and turned.

"For what it's worth, Señor Almeida — the men are alive today. Whether they're alive next month depends a great deal on how complete the record is." He settled the cap on his head. "Consider whether your principles can swim."

* * *

They watched the launch retract, watched the patrol boat swing its stern to the wind and gather speed toward the northern channel, watched the flag shrink to a smudge and vanish behind the headland. The engines faded. The surf came back in, indifferent, filling the silence the way it always did.

Ines sat down on the driftwood log Ferrer had warmed and put her face in her hands.

"He said Tomás asked about me," she said through her fingers.

"People being held captive ask about their families. It doesn't mean what you want it to mean."

"You don't know that he's captive. You're guessing. You're gambling both our lives on a guess, and tomorrow night there's a storm coming that—" Her voice broke and reassembled itself into fury, which was, Paloma was learning, how she survived. "You could have said yes. One word. We'd be warm tonight."

"Yes. And in a year, when some journalist dug up the seam, we'd be the two civilians who helped bury six men." He crouched in front of her, close enough that she had to look at him. "Ines. Listen to me. I spent five years learning what it costs to be the man the agencies decide to erase. I will not spend the rest of my life being the man who erased someone else. Whatever else happens on this island, that price is not on the table."

She stared at him for a long time, rain dripping off her chin, and he couldn't read what moved behind her eyes — grief, rage, the beginnings of something harder than either.

"Then we'd better build a roof that survives a category-three front," she said finally, "and a hull that floats, and a plan that gets us off this rock before tomorrow night. Because if we die here for your principles, Paloma Almeida, I swear to God I will haunt you personally."

"That," he said, standing, "is the most reasonable thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

But as he turned toward the wrecked tender and the long day of canvas and resin ahead of them, he looked once more at the empty northern channel, and the arithmetic ran on without his permission. Ferrer knew exactly where they were. Ferrer had a radio, a ship, and a minister waiting for a completed file. Which meant the storm coming tomorrow night would not be the only thing hunting them across the water.

And somewhere north, in a room with a table and a tape recorder, six living men were being asked, politely, to disappear twice.
