## 13. The Long Paddle

The resin stank of pine and something older, something chemical and wrong, like the ghost of a forest that had been dead a century. I held the salvaged canvas taut over the split in the hull while Paloma worked the brush in short, furious strokes, and I tried not to think about how much of our survival now depended on a mixture he'd improvised from a paint tin, a length of fiberglass cloth he'd cut from the cockpit liner, and the last of the two-part epoxy we'd found in a sodden toolbox on the beach.

"Hold it flatter," he said. "It's rippling."

"It's rippling because my arms are shaking."

"They'll shake worse tomorrow. Hold it flatter."

The island gave us nothing and asked for everything in return. We'd been there two days, or maybe three — time had gone soft since the hull gave way, measured now only in the intervals between rain squalls and the tide's twice-daily theft of our beach. The Cautiva's replacement — Paloma's boat, a battered open workboat called the *Errante* — lay heeled over on the shingle like a beached animal, her starboard side split along three feet of seam where the sea had decided, somewhere in the open water off Isla Serrano, that she was finished. We had disagreed with the sea. We had dragged her above the high-tide line by main force, both of us up to our chests in freezing water, screaming at each other over the wind, and now she sat there drying out while Paloma stitched her back together with canvas and resin and a stubbornness I had come to understand was the only thing about him that had never been blacklisted.

The sealed case sat under the tarp with the duplicate logbook from Tomás's cache, wrapped in the emergency blanket, dry as a relic in a church. I checked it every hour. I told myself it was about the evidence, about the minister's name written in my brother's hand, about the story that had to reach Punta Arenas. It was also, though I didn't say it, the last part of Tomás that was still moving through the world with a purpose. I carried the case the way you carry an urn.

"Done," Paloma said, and sat back on his heels. The patch gleamed wetly, a pale scar across the dark hull. "It needs six hours to cure. Then we go."

"Go where? The channels are still white with foam. You saw the front coming down from the north — the fishing boat's radio said—"

"I saw it." He stood, worked his shoulders, and looked south down the strait where the water ran in long, bruised bands of gray and green. "Which is why we go. That front is moving faster than we are. If we wait for a window, there won't be one. There'll be a season. And we'll be on this beach eating limpets until the patrol boat finds us, and you remember how the last conversation with Lieutenant Ferrer ended."

I did remember. Paloma had refused the man's polite, poisonous offer of rescue, and Ferrer had left us on that rock with a smile that said *we know where you are, and we can wait longer than you can*. The memory sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Six hours," I said. "I'll sleep."

"Sleep," he agreed. "Eat first. Sleep second. You paddled eleven hours yesterday and you ate half a biscuit."

"I wasn't hungry."

"You're never hungry. That's not discipline, Ines, it's just a way of disappearing slowly." He said it without looking at me, the way he said most true things, and handed me a tin of cold sardines and the last of the crackers from the salvage. "The wilderness doesn't forgive it. Neither do I."

* * *

We launched at first light, if you could call it light — the monsoon had pressed the dawn down into a dim green murk, the sky and the water the same color, the horizon a rumor. The *Errante* sat lower than she should have, the patch holding but weeping a slow thread of water through the seam at its edge, and Paloma had rigged the hand bilge pump where I could reach it from the bow. My job, he said, was water and watch. His job was everything else.

The channels ran south and east toward the mainland, a maze of drowned valleys that the charts showed as confident blue lines and that the water itself showed as something else entirely — a living, shifting architecture of current and counter-current, where the tide poured through narrows like water through a broken dam and the rain fell so hard and so flat that the surface of the sea seemed to boil. Paloma read it the way I read a contour map, his head turning in small precise movements, his hands never idle on the yoke of the sweep oar he'd shipped at the stern.

"Paddle," he said, and I paddled.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only currency I have left, and I learned in those channels exactly how little of it I had. My arms failed. Not gradually, not with dignity, but suddenly and completely, somewhere in the third hour, in a reach of open water between islands where the swell came in from the Pacific in long gray hills and the rain drove into my face like gravel. My shoulders stopped answering. My hands, locked around the paddle shaft, had gone from pain into a white numbness that felt almost peaceful. The bilge pump needed working. I looked at it. I looked at my hands. And I understood, with a clarity that felt like a kind of falling, that I could not do both.

"Ines." Paloma's voice came flat across the water. "Pump."

"I can't — my arms—"

"I know. Pump anyway."

I pumped. I don't know how. The handle rose and fell and each stroke cost me something I could not name and could not afford, and water came out of the boat in a thin silver arc and the sea came back in through the seam faster than I sent it out, and Paloma watched me do it with those gray, unreadable eyes and did not take the pump from me, and I hated him for it, and I understood him for it, and the two feelings were so close together in my chest that I could not have told you where one ended.

"Again," he said.

"You could do it faster."

"Yes. And then no one would be watching the water, and no one would be paddling, and in an hour you'd believe you couldn't do it at all, and you'd be right." He shipped a stroke, corrected our angle against a current I couldn't even see. "The wilderness doesn't care what you believe you can't do. It only cares what you do."

"Is that supposed to be wisdom?"

"It's supposed to be a fact. Wisdom is what people call facts they don't like."

I laughed — a short, ugly, breathless sound, half sob — and pumped again, and again, and the islands came at us out of the murk one after another like dark thoughts, and we passed them, and passed them.

* * *

We made the narrows at slack water by maybe ten minutes, and Paloma said later that those ten minutes were the entire difference between the trip and the obituary. The current through the gap ran like a river in flood even at slack, standing waves breaking white in the middle of the channel, and we shot it with the engine — the engine, that faithful, asthmatic little two-stroke, running at a pitch I had never heard from it before, the boat slewing and wallowing, the patch flexing under the hull like a heartbeat. Paloma stood at the stern with the sweep oar as a rudder, and I watched his face in those minutes and saw something I had never seen on it: not fear. Concentration so total it looked like peace. He was, in that moment, exactly where his whole ruined life had been aimed, and the knowledge did not comfort me so much as frighten me, because I understood that a man could be built this way — that the wilderness could take everything from a person, blacklist him, break him, exile him, and leave him nothing but this: competence, and the cold joy of using it.

Then we were through, and the water behind us smoothed, and the engine coughed, caught, coughed again.

"No," Paloma said, to it, to the sea, to whatever was listening.

It coughed a third time and died.

The silence afterward was enormous. Rain on the canvas deck. The slap of water against the hull. Somewhere ahead, the low continuous mutter of the front coming down from the north, a sound like the sea clearing its throat.

"Oars," Paloma said, in the same voice he'd use to say *lunch*.

We rowed. There was no other word for what we did for the next nine hours, but *rowed* is too clean a word, too athletic. We ground the boat down the channels with our bodies, the two of us on the thwarts I'd rigged from a broken berth plank, taking turns — his turn was twice as long as mine and he never once mentioned it — the swell rolling us from the west, the rain filling the boat faster than the pump alone could empty it, so that we pumped and rowed in rotation, a rhythm we fell into without discussing it, the way you fall into a language. Pump forty strokes. Row two hundred. Pump. Row. The islands slid past. The light failed and did not quite go out, because this far south in the monsoon season the dark is never complete, just a deepening of the gray, and we rowed through it into a night that was only a darker gray, and Paloma navigated by a coastline he could barely see, counting headlands, timing the gaps between the flashes of the lighthouse on the far cape — a light we saw perhaps once every twenty minutes, a single white blink, and each time it blinked Paloma said, quietly, "There," like a man greeting a friend across a crowded room.

At some point in that endless gray I stopped being able to feel my hands and started crying without deciding to, the tears indistinguishable from the rain, and I rowed through my own crying the way you walk through a doorway, and Paloma pretended not to see, which was its own strange courtesy.

"You could tell me to stop," I said, somewhere in the ninth or tenth hour. "You could take the oar. You're stronger. We'd go faster."

"We'd go faster for twenty minutes," he said. "And then you'd have learned the wrong lesson."

"Which is?"

"That someone else will always carry you." He pulled, steady, metronomic, his breath in the same rhythm as his stroke, and I understood that his breath and his stroke had been married to each other for so long they were the same thing. "The wilderness doesn't carry anyone. That's not cruelty. It's the only honest thing left."

"And you? Who carries you?"

The oar turned. The boat turned. A headland we had been aimed at swung away and another slid into its place.

"Nobody," he said. "That's the point of me."

* * *

We made the anchorage behind Isla Trinidad at what the watch said was two in the morning and what my body said was the end of the world. Paloma got the hook down, got the tarp rigged, got the bilge dry, got the stove lit under the shelter of the spray hood, and only then, with all of it done, did his hands begin to shake — I saw it, the fine tremor as he held the matches, and I understood that he had simply postponed it, the way he postponed everything, hunger and pain and grief, until there was nothing left that needed him more.

I took the matches from his hands and lit the stove myself. He let me. That, too, was new.

"Your arms," he said.

"Are not attached to me anymore. I've disowned them." I wrapped my fingers around the hot tin mug he'd filled with the last of the tea, leaves boiled twice, tasting mostly of smoke and tin. "You didn't answer me. In the boat. Who carries you?"

He looked at the stove flame for a long time. The rain drummed on the canvas. Out in the black water, the front muttered to itself, still hours to the north, still coming.

"I had a partner once," he said finally. "Before. Before the first blacklist, before any of it. Rodrigo. We guided together on the Aysén ice fields, six years. He fell on the second day of a traverse I routed." The words came out level, worn smooth, like stones in a riverbed — a story told so many times inside his own head that all the edges were gone. "The inquiry said the route was sound. The client's family said the guide was sound. The agency needed someone to be unsound, and I was the cheapest thing to discard. That's what a blacklist is, Ines. It's not a judgment. It's a disposal."

"And you've carried him ever since. Rodrigo. The route. All of it."

"I've carried the fact that I was the one who drew the line on the map." He turned the mug in his hands. "The wilderness didn't kill him. A decision did. My decision. So no — no one carries me. I'm carrying enough."

The stove hissed. I thought about Tomás, about the letters that had stopped, about the annotations in the margin of the survey readings — *these numbers are not what the rock says* — and about what I still didn't know, what I was afraid to know, whether my brother had written those numbers himself first, whether his hand had been one of the hands that falsified the seam before it turned honest too late. I had told Paloma on the island, in the long honest dark of our first night there, that I suspected it. I had not yet said the rest: that if it was true, then Tomás had done what Paloma had done — drawn a line, made a decision, cost other people everything — and that I did not know which brother I was rowing toward.

"Paloma," I said. "When we get to Punta Arenas. When the logbook is printed and the minister falls and all of it is done. What happens to you?"

He almost smiled. It was the saddest thing I had ever seen him do.

"Roldán's employers will have a very complete file on me by then," he said. "A man who burned one ministry will burn another. The blacklists will be lifted on paper and reinstated everywhere that matters." He drank his terrible tea. "That's not a complaint. It's just the price. I told you — the wilderness forgives nothing. Neither do I. Least of all—"

"Yourself," I said. "I know. You've said it enough that it's practically your address."

He looked at me then, really looked, and for a moment the gray eyes were not unreadable at all, and what was in them was so bare and so tired that I had to look away first.

"Sleep," he said. "I'll take first watch."

"You'll take every watch."

"I'll take first watch."

I rolled into the dry corner of the cockpit under the tarp, the case against my chest, my arms aching in a way that felt almost holy, and I slept the sleep of the entirely spent.

* * *

I woke to his hand on my shoulder and a light in the north that was not dawn.

It was the front. The whole sky above the northern islands had gone a deep bruised violet, lit from inside, and the wind had come around and was pushing at the tarp like a hand testing a door. The barometer — Paloma had a real one, brass, salvaged from the Cautiva's chart table, and he consulted it the way other men consult scripture — had fallen all night, and it was still falling.

"How long?" I asked, already reaching for the case, already lashing it down.

"Six hours to the mainland coast, if we're lucky. The front makes landfall in four." He was already coiling the anchor line, his movements quick and economical, the tremor gone as if it had never been. "There's a fishing hamlet on the coast, Puerto Quemado. Off the charts. The fisherman — the one who saw the Cautiva's crew taken off at night. If we can reach it before the front closes the strait, we get his testimony, we get shelter, we get a head start on whatever Ferrer is doing."

"And if we don't reach it?"

Paloma looked north at the violet light, and then at me, and there was no lie in him to find, which I was learning was both the best and worst thing about him.

"Then we learn what the front wants to teach," he said, and threw the hook aboard, and took the oars.

The water was already changing as we pulled out of the anchorage — a new swell running beneath the old one, longer, deeper, the sea clearing its throat in earnest now — and the *Errante* climbed the first of those long gray hills and slid down its far side, and I set my ruined hands on the oars, and we went south, racing the storm, toward a hamlet that did not exist on any map, where a man who had seen my brother taken away alive was waiting — if he was still waiting, if he was still alive, if the ministry had not already found him first.

The first drops of the new rain came down like a warning shot, and Paloma did not look up, and did not slow down, and neither did I.
