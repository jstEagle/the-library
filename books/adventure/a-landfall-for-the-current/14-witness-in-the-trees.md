## 14. Witness in the Trees

The hamlet did not exist, and yet there it was.

Paloma saw it first as a smudge of woodsmoke against the rain — a grey thread rising from behind a headland that no chart in Ines's satchel bothered to name. The channel narrowed there, pinched between granite shoulders furred with coigüe and cypress gone wind-bent, all of them leaning east like men flinching from a slap. He had been running these waters long enough to know that the unnamed places were where the country kept its secrets. Named places had owners. Unnamed places had only people.

"Smoke," he said.

Ines lifted her head from her arms across the thwart. She had been rowing for two hours on his insistence and her own stubbornness, and her hands were blistered in the way he remembered his own being once, at nineteen, when an old guide named Fuentes had told him blisters were the mountain's signature on a contract you couldn't read yet. She looked at the smoke, then at him, and said nothing, which by now was its own language between them: *I see it. I'm afraid of it. I'm coming anyway.*

They came around the headland with oars shipped and the current doing the work, and the hamlet assembled itself out of the drizzle piece by piece — six houses on stilts above the tideline, their legs green with weed to the waterline; a rack of congrio split and salted under corrugated tin; three boats hauled up on rollers, hulls patched with tarred canvas the way theirs was now, because everything in this country floated or it didn't, and the sea did not care which. A dog announced them without conviction. Somewhere behind the houses, a generator coughed and caught.

"You should do the talking," Paloma said quietly.

"Why me?"

"Because I have a face people remember, and not for good reasons." He let that sit. "Because you look like what you are — a tired woman in a leaking boat. Nobody fears that. They fear me."

She almost smiled. "You're not wrong."

"I know."

He brought them alongside a floating dock of lashed logs that gave underfoot like something breathing, and they tied off, and for a moment they simply stood in the rain and let the hamlet decide what to make of them. That was the etiquette of the fjords. You did not stride into a place like this asking questions; you arrived wet and hungry and slightly broken, and you waited to be counted among the living before anyone would speak to you as one.

The counting was done by a woman who came out of the second house drying her hands on her apron. She looked at Paloma's dislocated-then-reset shoulder sitting crooked under his jacket, at Ines's rope-burned palms, at the case strapped flat beneath the tarp in the stern like a coffin lid, and she made some private arithmetic and called over her shoulder in a voice worn smooth by weather:

"Papá. Guests."

* * *

The old man's name was Aurelio Soto, though he offered it the way men offer things they've stopped needing — late, and without looking up. His house smelled of diesel, fish blood, and decades of pipe smoke soaked into the rafters. He was seventy if he was a day, with hands so knuckled and scarred they looked like coral, and eyes the pale grey of the water off a glacier face. He poured them tea that tasted of burnt sugar and something wilder underneath, and he asked nothing, and this silence stretched so long that Ines finally broke it herself, which Paloma suspected was the point.

"We're looking for someone," she said. "A survey team. Six men. Their boat was called the *Cautiva*."

Something happened to Aurelio Soto's face then. It didn't move, exactly. It *stilled* — the way water stills over a deep place, telling you the depth by its refusal to ripple. He set down his cup with great care.

"That's a government word," he said. "*Cautiva.*"

"It was a government boat."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "It was a boat. Then it was a government word."

Paloma leaned forward. "What do you mean?"

The old man looked at him for a long time — the look of a man measuring how much truth a stranger could carry without dropping it. Outside, the rain thickened against the tin roof, and the generator coughed its rhythm, and somewhere a child laughed and was shushed.

"I mean," Soto said, "that I watched six men step onto a navy launch at two in the morning, four months ago, alive. And I have spent four months pretending I didn't."

* * *

The story came out of him the way blood comes out of cloth — slowly, under pressure, in the wrong order.

He'd been night-fishing the outer edge of the channel, alone, because at his age sleep came in fragments and the sea was better company than the dark inside his house. No lights. He never ran lights; the ministry had taken his permit years ago in one of those administrative erasures that nobody appeals because appealing requires believing the administration listens. So he sat there in the black water, invisible, smoking, letting his lines drift.

And the launch came through.

"Not a patrol run," he said. "Patrols run their routes with their lights on, announcing themselves, bored. This one came dark too. Running silent, slow, right down the middle of the channel like it knew every rock by its first name." He turned his cup in his scarred hands. "There was a floodlight on the aft deck. It was aimed inward, at the deck, so it wouldn't show from seaward. Under it were six men. Young. Tired. Not tied — they didn't need tying. Two of them were crying. Quietly, the way men cry when they've already been told what happens next."

Ines had gone very still beside him. Paloma could feel her not-breathing.

"One of them looked out at the water," Soto went on. "Right at me, maybe — you can't know, in the dark. Tall fellow. Glasses. He had a notebook in a plastic bag, holding it like—" the old man's hands made the shape, cradling it, protecting it, "—like it was a child he was passing over a wall."

Paloma's heart went cold and hard. "Glasses. Tall. Was there anything else? Anything distinctive?"

"He wore one boot and one rubber sandal. Lost a boot somewhere, I suppose." Soto shrugged. "Men in custody lose things."

Tomás Vidal. One boot and one sandal. Paloma didn't look at Ines. He felt her looking at him anyway, and he felt the exact moment she understood, and he wished — briefly, uselessly — that he could stand between her and the knowledge the way the headland stood between the hamlet and the open sea.

"They transferred to a bigger vessel?" he asked. "Or did the launch keep going south?"

"Bigger ship waiting past the narrows. Grey hull, no markings I could see. The launch went alongside, the men crossed over, and both vessels went south toward the Bajo Cotapos area and then I lost them in the weather." Soto drank his tea. "I reported it. Of course I reported it. A fisherman sees a navy launch moving six men at night, he reports it, that's citizenship."

"And?"

"And the officer who took my statement thanked me, wrote it all down very carefully, and then explained to me that what I had seen was impossible. There was no launch movement that night. There were no transfers. The *Cautiva*'s crew was missing, presumed lost with their vessel, and the sea is treacherous, and grief makes old men imagine things." He smiled without any warmth in it at all. "Then he bought my fuel for the season. Full price. Generously over full price. And suggested that winter was coming and my boat was old and perhaps I'd fish closer to home this year."

The fire ticked. Rain drummed. Ines spoke, and her voice came out low and level in a way Paloma recognized now as her most dangerous register — the calm she built over fury the way he built shelter over panic.

"The logbook," she said. "The tall man with glasses. Did he drop it? Throw it? Did anything go into the water that night?"

Soto looked at her for a long moment. "No. But I've wondered about that notebook ever since. Why a drowning man holds a notebook like that." His pale eyes moved to the tarp in the boat outside, to the shape beneath it, and back. "Why a woman comes up my channel in a dying boat asking about dead men. Why the sea sends me guests twice in one lifetime carrying the same cargo."

"What cargo is that?" Paloma asked.

"Truth," the old man said simply. "It's heavy. Nobody wants to carry it. But somebody always ends up holding it."

* * *

They stayed the night because the storm decided it for them — the front Soto's daughter had seen building all day came down the channel like a hand closing, and even Paloma, who trusted weather the way other men trusted God, admitted that the canvas-and-resin patch on the hull would not survive the open stretch south. So they ate congrio and potatoes in Soto's kitchen while the wind walked on the roof, and the old man talked, and what he talked about was memory.

"There were others who saw," he said. "The Mancilla brothers, up the channel — they were hauling pots that night, they heard the engines. Old Rosa Ferrada, whose son works the dock at Puerto Edén — she got a radio call from one of the crew weeks before, asking if her grandson could carry supplies to a cache point. People saw things. People always see things. But seeing isn't saying, and saying isn't surviving." He tapped his temple. "In here, everyone remembers. Out there—" he gestured at the dark window, at the whole drowned wilderness beyond it, "—everyone has agreed to forget. That's the arrangement. That's been the arrangement since before your minister, since before mine. The state doesn't need to make people lie. It just needs to make forgetting cheaper than remembering."

"And for you?" Ines asked. "Which is it costing you more?"

Soto considered her with something close to affection. "My wife is buried on the hill there. My daughter's children play on that beach. You're asking an old man if he's afraid." He nodded slowly. "Yes. But I'm seventy-three, and I've been afraid since 1973, and fear that old stops being a master and becomes a companion. What I want to know is what you'll do with what I saw. Because if you take it and drown in the next channel, then my testimony dies with you, and I've handed the ministry a gift: one more witness erased, no paperwork required."

Paloma met his eyes. "We're taking it to Punta Arenas. To press. There's a journalist—"

"I know the one." Soto almost smiled. "Everyone out here knows the one Roldán warned you about. We listen to the radio, señor. We are invisible, not deaf." He rose, joints cracking like small-caliber fire, and went to a chest in the corner, and came back with a oilcloth packet, and opened it on the table: a cheap digital camera, its screen crazed but functional, and a folded paper.

"After the officer visited me, I started writing things down. Dates. Times. Bearings. What I saw, what I heard, what was bought and what was threatened. And I photographed my own statement — the one that 'didn't exist' — before I surrendered the original." He pushed the packet across the table. "I'm an old man. If I die, this dies. If you live, it lives. That's the whole arithmetic of witnesses, and it's the only vote we get out here."

Ines stared at the packet. "You don't know us."

"No," Soto agreed. "But I know the tall one with glasses held a notebook like it was a child. And I know you came asking after him in a boat held together with prayer and resin. That's enough character reference for me."

* * *

Later, when the storm had settled into its long midnight monotony and Soto's daughter had put blankets on them in the loft above the kitchen, Paloma lay listening to the rain and heard Ines crying — quietly, the way men cried on the launch deck, the way people cried when they'd already been told what happens next. He didn't go to her. Some griefs wanted company and some wanted a wall, and he'd learned the difference the hard way, by getting it wrong.

But in the morning she was up before him, packing the camera and the paper and the duplicate logbook into waterproofing with the methodical precision of someone converting grief into cargo. Her eyes were swollen and dry.

"He was alive," she said. "Four months ago. Alive, and holding the evidence, and they took him anyway." She cinched the last knot. "Every day since is a day someone decided to keep him alive, or didn't. And there's a minister deciding it right now, today, over breakfast."

"Yes."

"So we stop being careful." She looked at him, and there was something new in her face — not the runaway cartographer, not the grieving sister. Something harder and more navigable. "Roldán will call again. He always calls. And when he does, I want to be standing next to you when you tell him no."

Before Paloma could answer, the generator stuttered, and Soto's daughter appeared at the foot of the ladder with the old man's handheld radio in her hand and an expression that needed no translation.

"For you," she said. "Channel 16. He says his name is Roldán."
