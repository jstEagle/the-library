## 15. Roldán's Last Word

The fisherman's name was Aurelio, and he fed them conger eel stew in a house that smelled of tar and woodsmoke, and for one evening the world was small enough to hold.

He had no radio worth trusting — a set older than Ines, its casing cracked, its antenna lashed to a alerce post with wire that had gone green with salt — but he had fuel for the generator and a stubborn pride in his signal, and by midnight he had coaxed the thing into a crackle that carried. The hamlet sat in a fold of coast that did not appear on any chart either of them carried, a place the ministry's maps had simply declined to notice, and perhaps that was why the frequency still worked here, why the state's ears were thin this far into the wrack of islands. Or perhaps Roldán had been listening all along. By then Paloma had stopped believing in luck of either kind.

Ines slept in the loft above the stove, the duplicate logbook wrapped in oilcloth beneath her head like a pillow she would not surrender even unconscious. Tomás's handwriting was in it. Her brother's note naming the minister was folded inside her jacket, against her sternum, where she had carried it since the cave. Paloma sat at the table with the generator humming and the storm outside working itself up from a growl to something with intent, and he keyed the mic and said the call sign Roldán had given him in Puerto Natales, back when the job had still been a mystery instead of a wound.

Static. Then the voice, warm as ever, unhurried, a man who had never once in his life needed to raise it.

"Paloma. You're late."

"The sea was busy."

"The sea is always busy. That's what makes it useful." A pause, the sound of ice in a glass carried thin across two hundred kilometers of black water. "You have it. I know you have it. Ferrer reports you refused rescue on Isla Quemada, which tells me you understood exactly what rescue would have cost. I confess I found that disappointing. I'd hoped you'd be easier to read than this."

"You hoped I'd be cheaper to read."

Roldán laughed, low and genuine, and that was somehow worse. "Yes. All right. Let's not spend the night circling. The weather is coming in — you'll have felt it building since sundown, and I don't need to tell a man of your reputation what a system like this does to the channels. So I'll say my piece while the sky allows it, and then you can decide who you want to be when it lands."

Paloma looked at the stove, at the blue ghost of flame under the iron, and waited. It was an old habit from guiding: let the client talk themselves to their own price. But this time there was no client. There was only him, and the price was going to come out of his own hide, and he knew it before Roldán opened his mouth again.

"Reinstatement," Roldán said. "Full. Both registries — Chile and Argentina. Your Chilean file is already drafted; the Argentine side needs forty-eight hours and a signature, but it's arranged. Every blacklist lifted. Every notation expunged. You go back onto the books clean, Paloma. Cerro Torre permits, Aysén concessions, the whole country open to you again. No more ferry routes. No more watching lesser men take the work you were born for."

The words went into him like warmth into frozen hands — that first treacherous burn that feels like healing and is only blood returning to prove how badly you've been hurt. He thought of the harbor office in Natales, the clerk sliding the renewal notice across the counter without meeting his eyes. *Blacklist extended, twelve months.* Twelve months, every year, forever, a sentence renewed by men who had never stood on anything higher than a courthouse step.

"And Vidal," Roldán went on, softer now, and Paloma understood that the man had done his homework down to the last soft place. "Tomás Vidal's record. Cleaned. Quietly. When the crew surfaces — and they will surface, that's being handled — his part in it will read as it should read. A loyal surveyor caught in circumstances beyond his control. No annotations. No annotations *found*, rather. His sister gets her brother back without a stain on him. She's listening somewhere in that house, isn't she? Give her that much, Paloma. Give her the version of her brother that lets her sleep."

"He has a version of himself," Paloma said. "It's called the logbook."

"The logbook is paper." Roldán's voice cooled by a single degree, the way water cools just before it goes to ice. "Paper burns, drowns, fades. What doesn't fade is a career. Yours. Hers. His. Four lives, Paloma, for one case of readings that mean nothing once the official survey publishes. And the official survey publishes in three weeks regardless. That's the arithmetic. I'm offering you the only exit from these fjords that doesn't end with Ferrer's people writing your obituary as a boating accident."

Outside, the wind had found the harbor mouth. The boats in the anchorage knocked their hulls together like teeth chattering. Somewhere up the coast a tree came down with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

Paloma set the microphone down and walked to the window.

Aurelio's house perched on a shelf of rock above a shingle beach, and beyond the beach the channel ran black and ridged with white, and beyond the channel the mountains stood up into the cloud with no tops visible, just mass, just presence, the way they had stood for every year of his life whether he was permitted among them or not. Rain came off the water in slanted sheets. The generator light stuttered.

He thought about the first time he had been blacklisted. Not the second, not the paperwork renewal that had driven him to Roldán's contract — the first, the true one, the one with a body at the bottom of it. A client who had ignored his turn-back call on the Río Blanco approach. A client who had kept climbing because the summit fee was non-refundable, and who had come apart on the descent in a way Paloma still saw some nights, in the space between sleeping and not. The inquiry had asked Paloma whether he had done everything within his power. He had said yes. He had believed it. Then the family's lawyers had needed someone to blame who wasn't dead, and the agencies had needed someone to blame who wasn't insured, and the word *blacklisted* had entered his life like a stone entering a boot — small, permanent, felt at every step since.

Six years. Six years of ferry routes and cargo runs and watching other guides' rope teams move up ridgelines he could have climbed blindfolded. Six years of being spoken about in the third person by men in offices.

And here was a voice offering to give it all back. Not to earn it back — to give it, the way you toss a dog a scrap, the way power buys the loyalty it can't command. All it would cost was the truth about nine men who had been taken off a boat at night by a ministry vessel while their gear stayed pitched and their radios stayed charged, a camp abandoned mid-life, a lie so tidy it had required a government to maintain it.

All it would cost was Ines's brother, laundered. A man who had signed falsified readings and then recanted too late, whose conscience was real and costly and currently folded against his sister's sternum in ink — erased, in exchange for her sleep. Roldán was right about one thing: the clean version would let her rest. And it would be a lie, and she would carry it the way Paloma carried the Río Blanco, and someday it would enter her boot too.

He picked up the microphone.

"One question first."

"Ask it."

"The men. The Cautiva's crew. Nine men." He made himself say it plainly. "Ferrer offered to find us alive. Aurelio saw them taken aboard alive. So they were alive. Where are they now?"

The static filled the cabin like water filling a hull.

Roldán did not answer. He was a man who always answered; silence was the one currency he never spent carelessly, and Paloma heard the silence spending itself, coin by coin, in the crackle between continents of interference. Three seconds. Five. Long enough for the wind to hit the house hard enough to shake the lamp. Long enough for Paloma to understand that the question had been the whole negotiation, and everything before it had been decoration.

"They're being cared for," Roldán said at last, and the warmth was gone, and what remained was flat and administrative and more damning than any confession. "That's not your concern. That was never your concern. Your concern was coordinates."

"That was the job I took. This is the job I have."

"Paloma—"

"No." He heard his own voice come out level, and was grateful for it, because underneath the levelness was a shaking he didn't intend to show a man like Roldán. "You asked me once what happened to the Cautiva. I'm going to tell you, since nobody else will say it out loud. They refused to falsify the seam readings. Some of them did. One of them signed and hated himself for it and wrote down the truth anyway, twice, hiding it in ballast and sea caves like a man burying seed corn against a famine. And your minister couldn't risk the truth, so he disappeared the men and kept the map. And now you're offering me my life back if I agree the map matters more than they did."

"It's not a matter of agreeing. It's a matter of surviving."

"So is weather," Paloma said. "You learn to read it or it kills you. I learned."

He thought Roldán would argue. There was a part of him braced for argument, for the final escalation, the threat laid bare — but Roldán was too good for that, or too tired, or both. What came through the static instead was quiet, almost gentle, and it was the worst thing yet.

"I gave you the best offer anyone will ever make you. Understand that. After tonight, there's no version of this where you guide again. Not for them, not for anyone. You're choosing the wilderness over your own name, and the wilderness won't thank you. It never thanks anyone. It just keeps being what it is, and lets you freeze to death in it if that's your choice." A breath. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth anything — I think you'd have been good again. Better than good. That's why they wanted you for this. Men with nothing left to lose do careful work."

"I have something left to lose."

"Yes. Her. And the paper. And I'm telling you, as a friend of twenty days' standing, that you cannot keep both."

"I'm not keeping both," Paloma said. "I'm keeping neither. I'm giving them away. Both of them. To whoever prints it."

The line hissed. Then Roldán, and his voice had something in it Paloma had not heard before — not anger, not contempt. Something almost like respect, worn like a coat that didn't fit.

"Then you know what's coming. The system makes landfall tomorrow, early. Ferrer's boat is already moving, and this time there's no bargain attached. And Paloma — the story's already written. It goes to print whether you reach Punta Arenas or not. Desertion. Both of you. The sister of a deserter, abetting. Think about what that does to her before you do anything noble."

"She reads faster than you think."

"Everyone thinks that," Roldán said, "right up until the printing press starts." And the line went dead — not cut, but closed, the particular click of a man hanging up on his own disappointment.

* * *

Paloma stood in the lamplight a long moment with the microphone in his hand, feeling the shape of what he had just done settle over him like wet canvas.

Not relief. He had expected relief, the way you expect it after setting down a load, and it wasn't there. What was there was colder and cleaner: the knowledge that the door was shut behind him, locked, and that the key had been his own name and he had thrown it into the channel. Six years of exile, renewed voluntarily, indefinitely. No registry would touch him now. No agency, no concession, no permit. He had just made the blacklist permanent, and he had done it for paper and for men he'd never met and for a woman asleep in a loft who might, when she understood the full shape of what her brother had done, wish Paloma had taken the deal.

He climbed the ladder quietly. Ines lay curled against the oilcloth bundle, her face slack with a exhaustion that went deeper than muscle, and in sleep she looked young in a way she never permitted herself awake. Her hand was under the bundle even now. Even dreaming, she held it.

He could wake her. Tell her the offer. Let her weigh her brother's clean name against the truth, let her choose, spread the weight across two sets of shoulders the way a good guide spreads a rope team across a slope. It would be fair. It might even be wise.

But he had heard Roldán say *give her the version of her brother that lets her sleep,* and he had heard the trap in it, and he would not lay that choice on her at three in the morning in a stranger's house with a storm coming. If she chose the lie, let her choose it in daylight, knowing he had refused it first. If she chose the truth — and looking at her hand, white-knuckled even in sleep around the oilcloth, he suspected she would — then they would walk into Punta Arenas together carrying everything.

Below, the generator coughed. Aurelio appeared in the doorway, old and bent and unafraid, and looked at Paloma's face and read it the way coastal people read weather.

"They offered you money," Aurelio said. It wasn't a question.

"Worse. They offered me myself back."

The old man nodded slowly, and put another log on the stove, and said nothing else, because there was nothing else to say that the wind wasn't already saying better.

Paloma went to the window one more time. The southern horizon had gone a color he knew too well — a bruised, pressure-colored green-black, the sky lowering itself like something taking a grip. Tomorrow, Roldán had said. Early. And Ferrer already moving, and the story already written, and two hundred kilometers of the worst water on earth between this shelf of rock and the one printing press that mattered.

He began pulling their gear together by lamplight, checking seals, double-bagging the logbooks, lashing the case, working fast and steady the way he worked when the mountain had started talking. Behind him the storm built its argument against the coast, and ahead of it, somewhere south, Punta Arenas waited with its presses and its patrols and its single narrow chance.

They would have to leave before dawn. They would have to run ahead of the front, straight down the throat of it.

And the front, he could see even now, was going to be the largest of the season.
