## 17. Landfall

The barometer had been falling for two days, and by the third night the sky over the Straits stopped being a sky at all. It became a weight. I had seen Patagonian weather do many cruel things — turn a calm anchorage into a maelstrom in twenty minutes, fill a dry gully with a river that hadn't existed at dawn — but I had never seen it gather itself with this kind of intention. This was not weather happening to us. This was weather coming for us, with our names in its mouth.

Ines stood at the tiller with her hood cinched so tight only her mouth and chin showed, and even that was gray with salt spray. The repaired hull — canvas and resin, my own hands' work, laid down over her brother's cracked fiberglass on an island that had no name on any chart I trusted — was singing a note I did not like. A low, fibrous groan from below the waterline every time we took a swell wrong, like a man in his sleep remembering something painful.

"How long?" she shouted. Not *how bad*. She had stopped asking how bad somewhere around the second day. She only wanted the number now.

"An hour," I shouted back. "Maybe less. The eye is ahead of us — see the light there, that yellow smear? We drive for it. We put her on the first beach we find and we walk away from her."

"Walk where?"

I didn't answer, because I didn't know yet, and because the answer I had was one I wasn't ready to say out loud: *away from the road, away from Ferrer, into the hills with the case and the log and whatever's left of us.*

The strait ahead had gone white. Not the white of foam — the white of a surface so violently agitated it had stopped being water and become a kind of screaming solid. Rain came at us sideways, hard enough to sting through two layers of wool, and the wind had that particular Patagonian voice in it, the one that sounds less like air and more like a continent clearing its throat.

The case was lashed to Ines's back. We had done it an hour ago, in the relative calm of the storm's leading edge, when I still believed we might make the mainland with the boat intact. Waterproof pack, waterproof case, and over both a tarp cinched down with the last of our cord. It made her look hunched, deformed, a woman carrying a hump of government secrets. She had laughed when I said that — a short, ugly laugh, the kind you make when the alternative is screaming.

"If I go down," she had said, "cut it off me. Promise me."

"I promise."

"You're lying."

"I'm lying," I agreed. "Paddle."

Now there was no more paddling to do. The engine had died forty minutes ago — salt water in the intake, or the fuel line, or the simple exhausted refusal of a machine that had been dying since the island — and we had come the last miles on oars, the two of us, in shifts that shrank from twenty minutes to ten to five as the sea took more of each stroke back than it gave. My arms had gone past burning into something colder and stranger, a numbness that felt almost peaceful. Ines's hands were blistered through her gloves. I could see the blood on the oar shaft in the lightning flashes, a dark smear she kept gripping anyway.

The beach appeared the way beaches appear in storms: all at once, a pale suggestion in the chaos, then real. Shingle — gray stones, the size of fists, the kind of shore that forgives nothing and breaks everything, but a shore. Behind it, a dark rampart of trees bent and thrashing. Behind the trees, if the charts and my memory of them were both honest, a road. A dirt road, a lumber road, a road that led eventually to a place with telephones.

And on that road, I was nearly certain, a patrol.

I had seen the running lights the night before, far off across the water, holding a station no fishing boat would hold. Ferrer was not chasing us. He was smarter than that, and lazier. He was waiting. The storm would do his work for him, drive us onto whatever coast he had decided we'd wash up on, and then he would step out of his warm cabin with his polite voice and his offer — *rescue for the case, the minister wants the team found alive* — and the story would write itself with our signatures on it.

"Paloma." Ines's voice, ragged. "Paloma, the beach."

"I see it."

"Are we going to make it?"

I looked at the water between us and the stones. Two hundred meters, maybe less, of the worst sea I had ever tried to cross in anything, let alone a boat held together with canvas and stubbornness. The hull groaned again, longer this time, and I felt through the deck a softness under my feet that had no business being there. Water in the bilge. More than there had been an hour ago. The bail bucket was beside my knee, half full, and I had stopped counting how many times I'd emptied it.

"We're going to make it," I said. "But not the way you're hoping."

* * *

There is a way to beach a boat in heavy surf, and it is not a way that allows for hesitation. You wait for the wave behind you — the big one, the one that has been building its argument for a while — and you put your stern to it and you ride it like a man riding a falling tree, and at the last moment, when the bow is about to dig into the sand or stone, you turn everything you have into holding her straight. Broach, and the sea rolls you. Straight, and the sea gives you thirty seconds of grace to get off.

"Case stays on," I told her. "Oars down. Hands on the gunwale. When I say *now*, you go — not before, not after. You go up the beach until the water can't touch you and you keep going until you're under the trees. Don't look back. Don't wait for me."

"I'm not leaving you on that beach."

"You're not leaving me anywhere. I'll be along. But if you wait for me in the surf, we both die, and the case dies with us, and Tomás's name dies with it. *Now*, Ines. When I say now."

She looked at me through the streaming hood, and in the next lightning flash I saw her face fully for the first time in hours — white, set, terrified, and absolutely resolved. She was her brother's sister. Whatever Tomás had chosen in the end, the family had put iron in her the same way.

The wave came. I felt it before I saw it — a lifting, a gathering, the whole sea behind us deciding to move — and I put the bow at the beach and prayed to no one in particular.

"Now!"

She went. She went over the side like she'd been born doing it, into water that came to her thighs, then her waist, and I saw the sea take her once — a full second where she was simply gone, swallowed, the case and all — and then she surfaced twenty feet closer to the stones, coughing, clawing, and found her feet on something, and hauled herself up and out of the reach of the whitewater, and kept going, exactly as I'd told her, up the shingle and into the dark of the trees without looking back.

Then it was my turn, and the sea collected its fee.

The wave that should have carried me in broke early, or the boat's hull — that soft, groaning, canvas-sealed hull — gave way at the wrong moment, and instead of riding the crest I was inside it. The world turned into noise and pressure. I was aware of very little in sequence: the boat rolling over me, a stone striking my hip, the oar I'd been holding gone from my hand, and then the surf taking my left arm and pulling it in a direction arms do not go.

The sound my shoulder made was not loud. That was the strange thing. In all that roar, the body's worst moments are quiet. A deep, wet click, somewhere inside, and then a white sheet of pain that had nothing to do with the cold, and my left arm became a thing that belonged to the boat rather than to me, dragging in the foam.

I have been hurt in the mountains before. I broke my ankle at eighteen on a glacier in the Dientes and walked out on it for two days because the alternative was dying of the weather, and I have always believed that pain is information, and information can be set aside until it's useful. I set it aside. I got my feet under me — the water was waist-deep here, receding, the wave spent — and I stood up in the churning gray and looked at the beach.

The Cautiva's hull — no, not the *Cautiva*, our boat, the nameless one, the one Ines had bought with her brother's money and I had kept alive past all reason — lay on its side in the shallows, half in and half out, its canvas patch peeled back like a scab. It was finished. Whatever else happened, that boat was never going anywhere again, and some small, ungrateful part of me mourned it, because a boat that carries you that far stops being an object.

The stones were ten meters away. Ten meters, with one arm, in surf that wanted me back.

I walked. I did not swim, I did not crawl — I walked, leaning into the retreating water, timing the waves the way you time falling rock, moving on the draw-back, freezing on the surge. Each wave hit my right side and shoved me and I let it shove me and kept the direction. The pain in my shoulder came in pulses now, synced to my heartbeat, and my heartbeat was very fast, and so the pain was very constant, a white-hot wire from my neck to my elbow.

Then my boots found stone, and then smaller stone, and then I was above the reach of the sea, on my knees on cold gray shingle, and the storm was just weather again instead of a verdict.

* * *

Ines was under the trees, twenty meters up, and she had not kept going the way I told her to. She was at the forest edge, half in the dripping cover, watching the beach, and when I got to my feet she came to me at a run and caught me before I finished standing.

"Your arm—"

"Dislocated. Left side. I need a tree and a strap and about ninety seconds."

"Can you—"

"Yes. Help me or watch. Either way, hold the light low."

She had the small flashlight, shielded under her poncho, and she gave me its red glow while I found a leaning coigue trunk at the right height, thick and moss-slick, angled at maybe thirty degrees. The old method. You lie the arm across the trunk, you let the weight of your own body do the work, and you do not scream, because screaming in Patagonia at night, with a patrol boat somewhere on the water, is a luxury.

I knelt. I laid my left forearm over the trunk. I leaned.

The shoulder went back in with the same quiet click it had made coming out, and the relief was so total and so sudden that I did make a sound — not a scream, a grunt, an animal syllable — and then I was on both knees in the wet moss, breathing, with the rain coming through the canopy in cold intermittent ropes, and my arm was mine again, weak and wrong-feeling but mine.

Ines crouched in front of me. The case was still lashed to her back; she hadn't taken it off. She was soaked through, shaking — not from fear, I realized, but from the simple thermal arithmetic of a woman who had been in the sea and then in a gale, and whose body was now spending everything it had on staying warm.

"We have maybe an hour of the eye," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's, gravel and salt. "After that the back side of this system comes through and it'll be worse than what we just crossed. We can't stay here. We can't stay on the coast. Ferrer will have seen the boat or he'll find it at first light, and this beach is the one place he knows to look."

"The road," she said. "You said there's a road behind the trees. If we follow it—"

"No."

She stopped. "No?"

"The road goes somewhere, which means it comes from somewhere, which means it's the first thing he'll watch. A patrol boat, a truck at the trailhead, two men with a thermos and a radio — it costs him nothing to cover a road. We'd be walking into his arms and thanking him for the path." I got my feet under me, testing the shoulder. It held. It would hold. "We go up. The hills, the high ground, south along the ridgeline. No roads, no paths, no fisherman's hut, nothing. Two days of the worst walking in the world, and then we come down on Punta Arenas from the land side, from a direction nobody's watching."

"Two days." She looked at me, and I saw her do the arithmetic I'd already done: two days of open ground, in a storm's aftermath, with one of us one-armed and the other half-frozen, carrying a case that made her every step slower. "Paloma. We could die up there. Nobody would even know where."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point of it."

"And the road would take us to a telephone."

"The road would take us to Ferrer. Or to whoever Ferrer has already talked to. You heard the loudhailer, Ines. Amnesty for the case, prison for its holders. That offer is sitting at the end of that road right now, warm and dry, and every hour we spend on it is an hour inside his story." I reached out — with the good arm, clumsily — and touched the case on her back, the wet tarp over it. "This is the only story that matters now. Tomás's handwriting. The seam. The minister's name. It doesn't exist anywhere else in the world, and there are people whose entire careers depend on it never existing at all. I am not going to carry it down a road they own."

She was quiet for a long moment. The storm's eye moved over us — the rain thinned, almost stopped, and through a tear in the clouds I saw a patch of stars, absurdly calm, the same indifferent stars that had watched the *Cautiva*'s crew taken off their ship at night by men from a ministry vessel. The wilderness forgives nothing, I thought. It also takes no sides. It was the only honest party in all of this.

"Tomás walked the road," Ines said at last, very quietly. "That's what it comes down to, isn't it. He signed what they told him to sign, and he took the safe path, and it cost him everything anyway." She straightened. Under the trees, in the red glow, her face had changed — the fear was still there, but it had been moved to the back row. "All right. The hills."

"The hills," I agreed.

* * *

We went up.

The first hour was the trees — deadfall and devil's club and ground that sucked at our boots, climbing by the red light with it shielded to almost nothing, my right hand on Ines's pack strap when the slope steepened and my left arm hanging useless at my side. The case rode her back the whole way. I offered twice to take it and she refused twice, and the third time I didn't offer, because I understood: she was not carrying evidence. She was carrying her brother, and she was not putting him down.

Above the treeline the world opened, and the storm's back edge found us.

It came out of the south like a wall — not rain this time but sleet, horizontal, with a wind behind it that made conversation impossible and walking a full-body negotiation. We moved from boulder to boulder, from one shallow fold in the ground to the next, and the ridgeline we were following was less a path than a promise, a dark spine of stone running south into the dark. Somewhere below and behind us, I knew, the beach was filling with men. Ferrer's people, or the local police, or both. They would find the boat. They would find our tracks at the treeline. They would lose the tracks in the scree above it, because above the treeline there are no tracks, not in this weather, not for anyone. That was the whole argument for the hills. That, and the fact that the hills had never once lied to me.

My shoulder screamed with every fourth step. Ines stumbled and I caught her and she caught me, and we developed a rhythm without discussing it, her hand on my good arm, my weight shared, the case between us like a third traveler.

Near dawn — if it was dawn; the sky was just a slightly less black gray — we stopped in the lee of a granite shoulder and ate the last of the biscuits and shared the last of the water, and Ines looked back the way we'd come. Far below, tiny with distance, a light was moving along the coast road. Steady. Patient. A light that was looking for us and would not find us.

"He'll figure it out," she said. "Eventually. He'll figure out we went high."

"Eventually," I agreed. "And by the time he does, we'll be two days gone, and the story will be in Punta Arenas, and his offer won't matter anymore." I looked at my left arm, at the deep ache settling into the joint, an ache I suspected would outlast the winter. "This is what it costs, Ines. This is the price. Not the dramatic moment — the arm. The cold. The walking. You pay in small coin, forever."

She pulled the tarp tighter over the case and looked south, where the ridgeline ran on into the gray.

"Then let's keep paying," she said.

We shouldered into the sleet and went on, up and south, into the open hills, with the storm at our backs and the truth on her spine — and far behind us, on a road we had refused, the light kept searching for two people who had already become the weather.
