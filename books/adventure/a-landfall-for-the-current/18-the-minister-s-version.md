## 18. The Minister's Version

The forest took us the way the sea had refused to: without argument.

Paloma went first, his dislocated shoulder bound against his chest with a strap of the Cautiva's old awning, his good arm doing the work of two. He moved through the coastal forest like a man reading a language the rest of us had forgotten — testing moss with his boot before trusting it, choosing the deer trails over the straight lines, never once looking back to see if I followed. I followed anyway. There was no other direction that wasn't water or road, and both belonged to Ferrer now.

Behind us, down through the tiers of coigüe and tepú, the shingle beach where we'd driven the boat onto its last mercy was already filling with grey water and greyer men. I could hear them — boots on stone, voices flattened by wind, the mechanical complaint of a winch being assembled. They were going to salvage the hull. They were going to salvage everything except what we carried.

What we carried was a canvas-wrapped case lashed to my back, a duplicate logbook in an oilskin sleeve inside my jacket, and between us perhaps four hours of strength left in the world. My arms still burned from the crossing. When I flexed my fingers they answered slowly, like strangers woken from sleep. The rain came down through the canopy not as rain but as a slow cold dripping, patient as arithmetic, finding every seam in every layer of clothing I owned.

"Water," Paloma said, and stopped at a stream cutting black through the ferns. He drank cupping one hand, awkward, furious at his own body for making even drinking complicated. I watched him hate the shoulder silently, the way he hated everything that slowed him — not with heat but with a kind of glacial contempt, as though slowness were a personal insult the wilderness had arranged specifically for him.

I knelt and drank too. The water tasted of stone and leaves and nothing else. Somewhere above us a chucao tapped its strange double note out of the undergrowth, unbothered, and I thought: this forest doesn't know yet. It doesn't know what's coming up its trails behind us.

We climbed.

* * *

The loudhailer found us an hour later.

We had come up onto a shelf of land where the trees thinned and the land fell away toward the channel, and below us — absurdly close, a hundred metres of slope and shadow — lay the road. Not much of a road; a gravel scar cut along the coast, the kind the ministry built to say it had been somewhere without ever meaning to stay. And on it, idling with its light bar throwing slow blue revolutions into the wet air, sat the patrol truck. Behind it the patrol boat rode at anchor in the cove, and beyond both, out on the grey water, the storm was assembling itself again — a second front, the season's worst, dragging its skirt of cloud across the mountains like something arriving to collect a debt.

Ferrer stood beside the truck with a megaphone, and when he spoke, the loudhailer did the shouting for him, flinging his voice up the hillsides so that it seemed to come from everywhere at once, from the trees themselves, from the sky.

"Inés Vidal." My own name, distorted, enormous. "Inés Vidal and Paloma Almeida. You are not criminals. You are people caught in a bad situation created by others."

Beside me, Paloma made a sound low in his chest. *Bad situation.* As if the surf hadn't tried to keep us. As if Tomás —

"You have in your possession government property," the voice rolled on, patient, almost gentle, the gentleness worse than any threat. "Return it, and you will be treated as witnesses, not suspects. Full amnesty. Transport, medical attention, protection. No charges will be filed. This offer expires when the weather does."

I pressed myself flat against a fallen trunk, bark soaking through my jacket into my spine, and looked at Paloma. He was watching the road with the expression he wore when reading water — total attention, no feeling in it anywhere, feeling saved for later, banked like fuel.

"He's lying," I breathed.

"Of course," Paloma said. "But listen to which lie."

So I listened.

"The survey vessel *Cautiva* has been located," Ferrer announced to the empty hills, to the chucaos, to the rain. "All crew members are safe and accounted for. An investigation is underway into irregularities in the survey record, including evidence of falsified documentation introduced by a member of the survey team."

The words went into me like cold water into a boot.

"A member of the survey team." I knew the phrasing. I had grown up in a house where ministry phrasing was the household dialect; my father had translated his whole life into it, softened his disappointments into it, retired inside it. It was a language built so that nothing true could ever be said in it directly. *Irregularities.* *Introduced.* A member of the team.

They had already written it. Before we reached Punta Arenas, before anyone heard our side, the story existed, complete, typed, filed. Tomás — who had flagged the falsified coordinates in his own annotations, who had hidden a duplicate logbook in a sea cave because he understood exactly what would happen to the original — Tomás was going to be the falsifier. The deserter. The man whose signature explained everything, whose guilt would close the file, whose name would be the sandbag thrown on the leak while the minister walked away dry.

"They're using him," I said. My voice came out wrong, small, and I hated it. "He flagged it. He *flagged it*, Paloma, it's in his handwriting, and they're going to hang the whole thing on him—"

"I know."

"—and the crew, the ones who wouldn't sign, the ones who were taken off that beach alive at night — what happens to them? If Tomás is the villain, then the rest of them—" I stopped, because the arithmetic finished itself, and I didn't want to hear it. If Tomás was the falsifier, then the men who resisted him were either dupes or accomplices, and either way their testimony was worthless, and either way nobody would ever look for them again.

Below us, the loudhailer tried once more, softer now, the tone of a man talking someone in off a ledge. "Señorita Vidal. Your brother's situation is serious. Cooperation can improve it considerably."

And there it was. Not amnesty. Leverage. They had known all along that she — that *I* — would come looking for Tomás, and they had built the trap with me as the load-bearing wall: give us the case, or your brother drowns in a story we wrote about him.

Something happened in my chest that I can only describe as a door closing. Not fear. Fear had been with me since the hull gave, since the surf, since the shingle — fear was just the weather now, constant, familiar. This was different. This was the moment the running changed direction.

Until that morning I had been running to survive. Every choice since Puerto Natales — the cracked-hull boat, the stowaway logic, the channels, the cave, the paddle — had been survival wearing the costume of a mission. Find Tomás. Stay alive. Get clear. Even the case had been, in some honest part of me, cargo I was keeping dry because keeping things dry was what you did, the way you bailed a boat, reflexive, unexamined.

Now I stood on a wet hillside above a gravel road and understood that survival was no longer the point, because survival inside their version was just another way of disappearing. I could live. I could take Ferrer's amnesty, hand over the case, be ferried warm and dry to Punta Arenas, and watch my brother be buried alive in a ministry report — *a member of the survey team introduced falsified documentation* — and I would survive it. People survived worse. People survived everything. That was the horror of it; survival asked nothing of you but that you keep breathing while the world was rewritten around your brother's name.

"No," I said.

Paloma turned his head. Rain ran off the brim of his hood. "No what?"

"Not the road. Not the amnesty. Not their version." I got my knees under me. My legs shook and I let them shake and stood up anyway. "Punta Arenas. The press office. Roldán warned you about a journalist — you told me on the island, the one who kept calling the ministry about the *Cautiva*. We find her. Or him. Whoever it is. We put the logbook in their hands before the front closes the strait."

"That's three days of channels in a season's worst storm, with no boat."

"Then we find a boat."

"With what?"

"With whatever's here." I gestured at the hamlet I could smell before I could see — woodsmoke threading up through the trees downslope, the faint fish-and-diesel reek of a landing, nets hung to rot. The fishing hamlet off the charts, the one where the old man had seen the crew taken aboard the ministry vessel at night. Witnesses lived in places like this. Boats lived in places like this. Everything the ministry pretended didn't exist lived in places like this, which was precisely why the ministry needed the map to stay blank.

Paloma looked at me for a long moment. Rain ticked on the canvas case between my shoulders. Down on the road, the loudhailer was winding down, offering its expiring mercy to the trees, and Ferrer's blue lights swept the trunks like slow searchlights finding nothing.

"You understand what you're choosing," he said quietly. "Not the danger. That part's simple. I mean the rest. After this, there's no version where you go back to survey credentials and ministry charts. You'll be the woman who burned them. They'll make sure of it. They're better at that than they are at mapping."

"My brother is already burning," I said. "I'm just deciding whether to stand next to him or walk away warm."

Something moved across his face — recognition, maybe, or envy, or the memory of a harbor office in Puerto Natales where a blacklist notice had been renewed and a man had discovered what necessity tasted like. He nodded once, the smallest nod, and turned upslope.

"Then we don't use the road," he said. "Fisherman's track along the ridge. It'll cost us the day and half our strength, and we'll arrive looking like drowned animals."

"Drowned animals with documents."

That almost — *almost* — reached his eyes.

* * *

We went up instead of down, and the forest closed over us like water.

The fisherman's track, if it had ever been a track, was now a suggestion the mountain occasionally remembered. We climbed through tepú tangles that grabbed at the case on my back as if the forest itself had been briefed. Paloma led with his one working arm and his whole stubbornness, and twice he stopped, head cocked, listening past the rain, and made us stand motionless for minutes among the ferns while somewhere below a motor searched the coastline and gave up.

At the top of the ridge the wind found us, the leading edge of the new front, and it came off the icefields with a sound like a held breath finally released. Below and behind, the fjord system we had crossed was already vanishing into white. Ahead, far to the north, a smudge of lower country — and somewhere in it, Punta Arenas, presses, telephones, a journalist with a stubborn streak and a phone line the ministry couldn't cut fast enough.

I thought about Tomás's handwriting in the sea cave, the note naming the minister, the careful oilskin wrapping of a man preparing evidence for a future he doubted he'd reach. *If you are reading this,* he had written, and then all the things he couldn't say to me directly, translated at last out of the ministry dialect into plain truth. He had chosen his career first. I knew that now; I had known it since the island, since I admitted it aloud to Paloma in the dark with the rain on the tarpaulin. But he had recanted, and the recanting had cost him everything, and now they meant to make his conscience itself the crime.

I put my hand flat against the oilskin sleeve over my ribs, where the duplicate logbook rode against my heartbeat, and made it a promise the way you make promises in Patagonia — knowing the weather will try to break it, saying it anyway.

"Ferrer!" I said under my breath, to the wind, to the whole grey machinery of the state rolling its blue lights along the coast below. "You want a deserter? Wait until we print."

Paloma glanced back. "Say again?"

"Nothing," I said. "Keep walking."

But it wasn't nothing, and by the set of his shoulders I think he knew it wasn't nothing. Fury is a fuel like any other; it burns dirty and it burns hot and it gets you up ridges your legs have no business climbing. All my life I had been taught to translate anger into patience, into procedure, into the soft ministry dialect where nothing true is said directly. The loudhailer had cured me of the dialect in six sentences.

We dropped down the northern side of the ridge as the light began to fail, toward woodsmoke, toward the hamlet that wasn't on any chart, toward the old man who had seen the crew taken alive. Behind us the storm arrived in earnest and swallowed the road, the truck, the blue lights, the minister's version — all of it gone into the white like writing in rain.

Ahead of us, downhill, a dog started barking.
