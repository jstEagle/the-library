## 19. Press and Pressure

Punta Arenas smelled of diesel and wet wool and, beneath it all, the sea's old iron breath. Paloma came into the city the way he'd come out of the hills — soaked to the sternum, one shoulder bound in a sling of torn tent-fly, Ines a half-step behind him with the case strapped to her back under a fisherman's oilskin. They had walked out of the coastal forest at dawn and caught a fuel truck on the Ruta 9, and the driver had asked no questions, which in this part of the world meant he had already guessed them.

The city was too loud after three weeks of channels. Traffic hissed on wet asphalt. A gull screamed from a lamp standard like something being tortured. Paloma stood on the corner of Calle Bulnes with water running off his cuffs and pooling around his boots, and felt the strange vertigo of a man returning from a country that did not appear on any map — the country where he had been someone else entirely.

"You're favoring the left side," Ines said.

"I'm favoring both sides. They're both ruined."

"Then we find the journalist before you fall down."

He knew the name before she said it. Roldán had given it to him two years ago, over bad coffee in Puerto Natales, in the tone of a man laying a card face-up on the table: *If anyone ever comes asking about your blacklisting, it will be her. Marcela Quintanilla. She keeps files the ministry doesn't know exist. Stay away from her, Almeida. She's the kind of woman who thinks a story is worth more than a man's life.* At the time Paloma had taken it for a warning against trouble. Now he understood it for what it was — a map, drawn by an enemy, of the only safe ground left.

*La Prensa Austral* occupied a narrow building wedged between a marine chandler and a shuttered travel agency, its windows fogged with the breath of old newsprint. The lobby smelled of toner and cold coffee. A receptionist looked at the two of them — salt-stiff jackets, Ines's split lip scabbed black, Paloma's arm hanging wrong — and reached, very slowly, for the telephone.

"We're not here to rob you," Ines said. "We're here to make you rich."

The receptionist dialed anyway.

Marcela Quintanilla came down the stairs herself. She was smaller than Paloma had imagined, sixty or thereabouts, silver hair cut short as a crewman's, reading glasses pushed up into it like a carpenter's pencil. She stopped three steps from the bottom and looked at them the way Paloma looked at weather coming over a saddle — not with alarm but with assessment, weighing what it would cost and whether it could be survived.

"Quintanilla," she said. "You have ten minutes before my editor calls the police on principle."

"Roldán told me to stay away from you," Paloma said.

That got the ghost of a smile. "Then Roldán is finally telling the truth about something. Upstairs. Both of you. And if either of you is carrying a weapon, leave it on the coat rack like a civilized person."

* * *

Her office was a cave of paper. Filing cabinets stood along every wall, their drawers labeled in a hand so small it might have been typeset. On the desk, under a brass lamp, lay a half-finished editorial about salmon farm licenses, abandoned mid-sentence. She cleared a chair with her forearm, sat, and folded her hands.

"Now," she said. "Tell me who sent you, because nobody walks out of those fjords in November looking like that unless somebody very powerful wants them dead or discredited, and I've been waiting four years for one of those somebodies to slip."

Paloma set the case on the desk.

It took the better part of two hours. He gave it to her straight, the way he'd give a route briefing — no embellishment, no drama, just terrain and sequence. The Cautiva's last anchorage, gear intact, no tracks. The sealed case under the ballast. The survey readings, the lithium seam running under disputed territory, Tomás Vidal's annotations flagging coordinates falsified upstream. The cache in the sea cave, the duplicate logbook, the note naming Minister Osorio. The patrol boat, Ferrer's polite offers, the loudhailer in the trees promising amnesty for the case and prison for whoever held it. The fisherman in the chartless hamlet who had watched nine men taken aboard a ministry vessel at night, alive.

Ines filled in what he couldn't — the letters, the survey credential, the shape of her brother's handwriting when he wrote *I may not be able to send these much longer.*

Quintanilla listened without interrupting once. That was the first thing that convinced Paloma she was dangerous; amateurs interrupt. When they finished she took off her glasses, cleaned them on her sleeve, and put them back on as though the world required recalibration.

"The fisherman," she said. "Name?"

"He wouldn't give it. Old man, hamlet south of Puerto Edén, blue hull, engine held together with wire. He'll talk to you if you come alone and bring nothing official."

"I never bring anything official. It's allergic to me." She pulled a legal pad toward her and wrote for a full minute without stopping. "Here's what you don't understand yet, so I'll say it plainly. A logbook is evidence. A note naming a minister is allegation. But a living witness who saw nine men loaded onto a government ship at night — that is a story, and stories are the only thing in this country that can't be disappeared, because everyone who reads one becomes a witness too." She tapped the pad. "But understand the price. Once I print this, you two stop being people and become exhibits. Every friend you have will be asked why they still have you. Every enemy you have will be handed a budget."

"We've been exhibits since Puerto Natales," Ines said. "At least this way we choose the frame."

Quintanilla looked at her for a long moment. "Whose brother?"

"Tomás Vidal. Survey team."

"Ah." Something moved behind the glasses — recognition, and pity, and the discipline to show neither. "Then you should know I've been sitting on a letter for eleven days. Anonymous. Postmarked Punta Arenas. It says the survey team deserted their post and crossed into Argentine waters, and that the ministry regrets the loss of government property." She slid the letter across the desk. "I didn't print it because it stank. Deserters don't seal their radios and charge their batteries first."

Ines read it twice. Her face did something complicated and then went very still, which Paloma had learned was worse than crying.

"They wrote it while we were still in the channels," she said. "They'd already decided the story before we found the proof."

"That's how it works now. Narrative first, facts later, if convenient." Quintanilla stood. "Go home — no. You have no home. Go to the Hostal Español, ask for the room at the back, pay cash, don't use the telephone. I need forty-eight hours. I need the fisherman. And I need you both alive and unavailable until the presses run, because if Osorio's people reach you first, this dies in a drawer like everything else."

"And after?" Paloma asked.

She was already halfway up the stairs to the archive. She paused on the third step, hand on the rail, and looked back at him with the flat honesty of a woman who had buried too many stories to lie about this one.

"After, you'll be famous for about a week, and marked for about thirty years. I can promise you the truth gets printed. I can't promise you anything else. Nobody honest ever could."

* * *

The Hostal Español was a warren of dark wood and cooking smells run by a widow who asked nothing and saw everything. The back room had two beds, a window onto an airshaft, and a radiator that clanked like a small ship in heavy seas. Paloma sat on the bed with his back to the wall — habit, not comfort — and watched Ines stand at the window with the desertion letter in her hands, reading it again as though repetition might change the ink.

"My brother," she said quietly, "has been named a deserter in a government letter, and I'm hiding in a hostel smelling of fried onions, waiting for a newspaperwoman to save him."

"Not save. Publish. Different animal."

"Don't do that. Don't be precise at me right now." But there was no edge in it; exhaustion had sanded everything down to grain. She sat on the other bed, facing him, the letter flat on her knees. "What happens to us after this? Genuinely. Not the version where you protect me."

Paloma considered lying, decided he'd done enough of that for one lifetime, and gave her the truth instead. "After this, no ministry in Chile hires me. Argentina follows within the month — they always copy each other's lists. Roldán will see to it personally; it's the only loyalty he has. I'll be the guide who burned his client. There's no blacklist for that, because no one needs one. Word does the work."

"And you'd do it again."

"Yes."

"No hesitation?"

"A hesitation. A long one. Roldán offered me my name back, Ines. Both lists lifted, clean record, Tomás's file scrubbed. All I had to do was hand over the case and let nine men stay lost." He turned his good shoulder to the wall. "I thought about it longer than I'll ever admit to anyone. That's the part that stays. Not the refusal — the considering."

Ines was quiet for a while. The radiator clanked. Somewhere below, the widow's television murmured the evening news, which tonight said nothing about survey teams or missing ministers' problems.

"When they publish," she said, "my name will be in it. *Sister of team member.* They'll photograph me outside the courthouse looking grief-stricken and photogenic, and I'll be that photograph for the rest of my life. And Tomás—" Her voice caught, steadied. "Tomás will come back and be a hero who survived, or a coward who signed, and either way I'll be the sister. We don't get to be people after tomorrow. We get to be captions."

"Yes."

"How do you stand it? You've been a caption for years. *Disgraced guide.*"

Paloma almost smiled. "You get a boat. You get weather. Weather doesn't read."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her — and it did more for the room than the radiator ever had. Then a knock came at the door, two soft raps, and they both went silent in the way of people who had spent weeks learning what silence was for.

Paloma crossed the floor without a sound and put his eye to the peephole. The airshaft light showed him a boy of maybe fifteen holding a folded paper, and behind the boy, briefly, at the stairwell's turn, a man in a rain jacket who made no attempt to hide — who wanted, in fact, to be seen. Broad shoulders. The patient posture of a man paid to wait.

Ferrer's people. Or Osorio's. In this city the distinction had probably dissolved.

Paloma opened the door on the chain. The boy held up the paper. "From the lady at the newspaper," he said. "She said tell you: it runs at six. And she said—" he squinted at the back of his hand, where something was written in ballpoint— "*the fisherman talked.*"

Paloma closed the door and leaned his forehead against the wood for one breath. Then he turned and held the note out to Ines.

"It runs at six," he said. "And the fisherman talked."

Outside, far off toward the Strait, a ship's horn sounded once, long and low, like something clearing its throat before speaking.
