## 22. A Landfall for the Current

**Paloma**

The ice went out of the fjord in October, and with it went the last excuse to stay ashore.

I stood on the dock at Puerto Natales in the blue hour before dawn, watching the water shrug off its winter skin. The channels had been locked for months — a season of hearings and hotel rooms and coffee gone cold while men in suits asked me the same question forty different ways. *Why did you refuse the rescue, Señor Almeida? Why did you choose the hills?* As if there were an answer that would satisfy them. As if any man who had ever trusted a mountain or a sea with his life could explain it in words that fit inside a courtroom.

The *Cautiva II* sat at the berth where we'd left her — not our boat, but her sister in every line. Ines had found her through a fisherman who owed Tomás a favor, a thirty-year hull of larch on oak that had survived more winters than most marriages. We'd spent the spring refitting her: new planking below the waterline, canvas and resin where resin would hold, a proper bilge pump instead of the bucket-and-prayer system she'd been born with. My shoulder had healed crooked, the way shoulders do when you drive them into shingle at speed, and some mornings it complained about the cold like an old man complaining about taxes. I let it complain. It had earned the right.

The government contracts had come back in September. All of them. The blacklists lifted, my credentials restored, letters on ministry letterhead offering me expeditions into the very fjords where they'd once paid to have me erased. I read them at Ines's kitchen table while she pretended not to watch, and I understood exactly what they were. Not apologies. Investments. A man who burns one employer is a risk; a man who burns one and is then welcomed back is an asset — proof that the machinery forgives anything if you're useful enough afterward. They wanted me back inside the machine so the machine could point at me and say, *see, the system works.*

I wrote my refusals by hand. Four of them. It took less time than writing this account has taken, and it felt better than anything I'd done since landfall on that shingle beach with my arm hanging wrong and the case lashed to Ines's back like a second spine.

What I offered instead was smaller and harder to kill. A survey partnership, independent, answerable to no ministry — just a boat, a crew, and a reputation built on the only currency I had left: the truth about what happened to the first *Cautiva*, published and proven and therefore worthless as leverage against us. You cannot blackmail a man who has already told everything. It is, I have decided, the most comfortable way to live.

Tomás Vidal came down the dock as the light came up, carrying two thermoses and the particular carefulness of a man who knows he's been forgiven but hasn't yet been forgotten-forgiven. His crew followed: Ossandón with the echo sounder, the Mendoza brothers arguing about rope as usual. Men who had signed their names to lies under pressure and then refused to disappear quietly when the lies were meant to bury them. They had lost eleven months to detention and come out thinner, quieter, and somehow more certain than before. I trusted them the way you trust weathered timber — not because it looks strong, but because it has already been tested past its rating.

"Coffee," Tomás said, handing me a thermos. "Ines says you'll want it bitter."

"Ines is right."

"She usually is." He looked out at the water, at the channels opening southward toward the work. "She still won't talk to me about the cave. About what I wrote."

"She will. When she's ready. Grief keeps its own schedule." I drank. The coffee was terrible and hot and exactly what I wanted. "For what it's worth — the note saved us. The duplicate log. Everything."

"I know." He said it quietly. "That's the part I can't make peace with. That doing the right thing late still counted. It shouldn't have. But it did."

We stood together while the gulls started their argument with the morning. Behind us, footsteps on the planks — Ines, with the charts rolled under her arm and her hair still wet from the dockside tap, moving with the economy of someone who had learned, over one long punishing season, exactly how much energy every motion costs.

"We leave on the tide," she said. No good morning. There never was, anymore. Good mornings belonged to people who hadn't shared a sinking hull.

"The tide's in ninety minutes," I said.

"Then we have eighty-nine minutes to finish loading."

* * *

**Ines**

My brother signs his letters now with a small drawing of a cormorant, which is how he used to sign them when we were children, before the ministry got hold of his signature and bent it into something that served other people. I have decided to accept the cormorants. It is not forgiveness, exactly. It is more like landfall — the decision to stop fighting the current and let it carry you somewhere you can stand.

The night before departure, I finally asked him about the cave.

He told me all of it. How Ferrer's predecessor had come aboard the *Cautiva* in the third week with sealed orders; how the readings from the eastern transect were rewritten in Santiago before the team ever saw them; how Tomás signed the falsified sheets because refusing alone would have stranded five men in the ice with no radio and no allies, and how he recanted the moment they made port — too late, he said, always too late, and the word *too* did a lot of work in that sentence. He cried, my brother, who had not cried at the hearings, not at the detentions, not even when the official story named him a deserter. He cried in our mother's kitchen in Punta Arenas with the cormorants drawn on a dozen unsent letters spread across the table.

"You knew," I said. "When you hid the cache. You knew you might not come back to get it."

"I knew the fjords keep what you give them," he said. "I wanted to give them something true."

So we are taking him back to the fjords. Not as penance — he has served enough of that — but as work. Real work. The seam under the disputed territory is real; the lithium doesn't care whose flag flies over it, and neither does the water above it. Our contract is with a consortium of fishermen and a university department that answers to no minister, mapping the channels honestly so that whatever gets decided about that ground gets decided on true numbers. It pays badly. It pays in something else too, though I'm still learning the name for it.

Paloma calls it *answerability*. Answerable to the water, to the crew, to the record. I call it home, though I say it quietly, because the last time I loved a thing with a name on it, the name turned out to be a lie.

We cast off at noon. The *Cautiva II* handles heavy for her age, the way honest boats do, and Paloma stands at the helm with that stillness he has — the stillness that terrified me the first storm, when I thought it was indifference and learned it was attention. He reads water the way Tomás reads handwriting: seeing the correction underneath the surface.

"Eastern channel first?" I asked, unrolling the charts.

"Eastern channel first." He glanced at me. "Your brother's transects. His numbers. We finish what he started — the true ones."

The wind came up the fjord behind us, fair and cold and smelling of ice. Somewhere ahead the mountains stood in their spring whites, indifferent as ever, keeping their accounts.

* * *

**Paloma**

We ran the eastern channel for six days, and the wilderness behaved exactly as it always does: without mercy and without malice.

A squall caught us in the narrows on the third day and tried the new planking, and the planking held, and I found myself standing in the rain listening to the hull speak — that low groaning conversation between wood and water — and feeling something I had not felt since before the blacklisting. Not happiness. Happiness is a fair-weather emotion. This was older: the sense of being where the consequences are. On land, among ministries and fixers and men like Roldán, consequences are deferred, laundered, assigned to other people. Out here they arrive on schedule, wearing weather, and you meet them yourself. I had missed meeting them myself. That is probably the most damning sentence I will ever write about myself, and I write it without apology.

On the fifth day we raised the anchorage where it began — the camp with the tents pitched and the rations stacked and no tracks leaving it. The site had been catalogued and abandoned by the inquiry, but the tents were still there, sagging under a winter of snow, and the rations had fed the foxes. Ines walked the perimeter in silence. Tomás stood at the edge of it and did not go in.

"It's just canvas," Ossandón said gently.

"It's a photograph of us," Tomás said. "Taken the moment before."

We struck the tents ourselves and packed them out. Some things you don't leave for the fjords to keep.

* * *

**Ines**

On the last evening, anchored in a bight with the peaks going rose-colored around us, Paloma showed me something I didn't expect. He took out the old harbor notice — the blacklisting renewal he'd seen posted in Puerto Natales, the one that had driven him onto Roldán's boat in the first place — folded soft as cloth now from a year of carrying. He looked at it for a long time.

Then he tore it in half, and quarters, and fed the pieces to the stove.

"You're not angry anymore?" I asked.

"I'm furious," he said. "I'll be furious until I die. That's not the same as being owned by it." He watched the paper catch. "The wilderness forgave nothing, Ines. It forgot nothing either. Every mistake I've made is still written in these channels — I can point to the water where each one lives. And do you know what I've learned? That's what makes it home. A place that remembers you honestly. Everything else — the ministries, the records, the reinstatements — that's all amnesia for sale."

I thought about my brother's cormorants. About the cache in the cave, giving its truth to whoever came looking. About the fisherman in the chartless hamlet who had seen the men taken alive and had carried that seeing through a year of silence until we arrived.

Everyone I respect now is someone the wilderness refused to let forget.

"Spring comes to the fjords," I said, "and the ice goes out, and the water remembers everything underneath it anyway."

"Yes," Paloma said. "That's the whole religion, as far as I can tell."

Tomás joined us at the rail, and for the first time since the kitchen in Punta Arenas, he stood beside me without the carefulness. The three of us watched the last light leave the peaks. Tomorrow we would run the final transect, close the eastern numbers, and turn north with charts that told the truth. Somewhere behind us, in Santiago, a resigned minister was learning to live in exile, and Roldán was already fixing for someone new, and Lieutenant Ferrer was saluting somebody's flag. None of it would stay fixed. Water never does.

But the channels would be here, keeping their accounts. And we would be here to answer them.

Home, I have decided, is not where you are safe. It is where you are known — by the place, by the people, by the current that carries you and asks nothing except that you tell the truth about where you've been.

The tide turned. Paloma weighed anchor without being asked, because he always knows, and the *Cautiva II* swung her bow north with the ebb, and the fjords closed behind us like a book that intends to be read again.
