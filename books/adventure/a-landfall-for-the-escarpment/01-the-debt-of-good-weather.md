## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The storm was a liar, and Yara Crane loved it anyway.

It had spent the whole morning sitting fat and purple over the eastern flats, promising hail the size of fists, and now, at four in the afternoon, it was finally making good — or making something. The cell had gone green-black at its base, the color of bruised fruit, and the anvil top spread out like a fist unclenching over half the sky. On his dash-mounted screen, the velocity couplet bloomed red and gold in the way that made chasers either pull over or press harder.

Yara pressed harder.

"Come on, sweetheart," he told the truck, because talking to machinery was cheaper than therapy and roughly as effective. "One good funnel. One clean shot. Mr. Aldous pays by the minute of usable footage, and I intend to be *usable*."

He was thirty-one years old, he owned a truck with two hundred and forty thousand kilometers on it and a hail-dented roof he'd stopped apologizing for, and he owed the Territorial Credit Union eleven thousand marks, which didn't sound like much until you did the arithmetic on what happened when you missed three payments. He'd done the arithmetic. He did it most nights, lying awake listening to the wind test the seams of his rented room, and every time the answer came out the same: *chase more storms, sell more footage, stay ahead of the number.*

The number was patient. That was the terrible thing about numbers. They never got tired, never got bored, never decided you weren't worth the trouble.

The storm, by contrast, was already getting bored. It had begun its slow right turn, dragging its tail across the flats toward the river country, and Yara swung north on the gravel to cut the angle. Dust boiled up behind him in a rope. The light went strange and yellow-green, the color the old-timers called *tornado weather* without ever explaining what else it could possibly be.

His phone buzzed against the console. He glanced down — one eye, half a second, the way every chaser swears they don't do — and saw the name **ALDOUS** flash up, which meant money, which meant he answered it on speaker while keeping both hands on the wheel like a responsible adult.

"Tell me you're on it," Aldous said. Aldous was a footage broker in Corvane with a voice like gravel poured through a sieve and a habit of paying late and complaining early.

"I'm on it. Cell's turning right, base is lowering, I'm positioning on the north road for—"

"Forget the north road." A pause, the sound of papers being shuffled, or maybe just someone who wanted to sound busy. "Crane. There's no easy way to say this, so I'll say it sideways. Your account came up in a review."

Yara laughed. He had a laugh for moments like this — quick, bright, a little too loud, the kind of laugh that filled a room so nobody could hear what was underneath it. He deployed it now, into the cab of a truck doing ninety on loose gravel under a rotating mesocyclone.

"A review," he said. "Of my account. What is this, an audit? I paid last month. Mostly."

"Not an audit." Gravel-sieve voice, careful now, the care itself a warning. "Your debt was sold, Crane. Three weeks ago. I only just heard. I thought somebody should tell you before—"

"Before what?" And here was the thing about Yara Crane: even as he asked it, some cold quiet part of him already knew, the way you know a storm has turned before the radar confirms it, from the feel of the air alone. "Sold to who?"

The lightning answered first. It hit somewhere off to the west with a crack like the sky splitting along a seam, close enough that the radio spat static and the hair on his forearms stood up inside his jacket. In the sudden white afterimage, the road ahead resolved into detail it hadn't had a moment before: the cattle grid, the washout beyond it, the water sheeting across the low place where the culvert had given up sometime around spring and nobody had come to fix it, because this was the Escarpment country and nobody fixed anything out here until it killed somebody important.

"Vellum," Aldous said.

Yara laughed again. Wrong moment. Exactly the wrong moment, because laughing made him take his attention off the road for one heartbeat longer than the heartbeat he had to spare, and the truck's front wheels found the washout at seventy kilometers an hour.

* * *

Later — much later, hours later, lying in a ditch with rain hammering the mud around him like applause — Yara would try to reconstruct those four seconds and fail. There was the lurch. There was the steering wheel going light in his hands, the particular weightless horror of a vehicle deciding it is now an aircraft. There was the fence post arriving in the corner of his vision with the calm inevitability of a debt collector. There was the world turning over, once, twice, the sky and the ground trading places like partners in a dance neither of them wanted.

Then stillness, upside down, seatbelt cutting into his chest, blood in his mouth where he'd bitten his cheek, and the rain coming through the shattered windshield in long silver threads.

"Well," he said to the truck, conversationally, because the truck deserved to hear it first. "That could have gone better."

He hung there a moment longer, taking inventory. Toes wiggled. Fingers worked. Ribs announced themselves as present and aggrieved but not, he judged, broken — bruised, maybe cracked, the kind of injury you'd walk off if walking off injuries were a personality trait, which in Yara's case it arguably was. His camera rig in the back had been strapped down; he could hear it swinging gently, intact, which meant more to him than the ribs.

Getting out took five minutes and cost him a layer of skin. He emerged into a world gone soft and gray, the storm already sliding east, indifferent, having used him for nothing. The truck sat on its roof in the ditch like a dead beetle, wheels turning slowly, dripping. Behind it, the fence wire sang in the wind. Ahead, across the flooded flat, the lights of a farmhouse glowed yellow and steady, the single most beautiful thing he'd seen all day.

He stood in the rain and did the arithmetic. It arrived instantly, unbidden, the way it always did: a totaled truck, a camera rig he couldn't replace, a season of income gone, and underneath it all the number — eleven thousand marks, plus whatever the wreck added, compounding quietly in the dark like something growing under a floorboard.

And Vellum owned it now.

He laughed a third time. This one came out wrong, ragged at the edges, and he clamped his jaw shut around it and stood in the downpour with his arms hanging at his sides, letting the rain decide what his face was doing.

* * *

The farmhouse belonged to a widow named Mrs. Tannhouser, who fed him stew whether he wanted it or not, which he did, enormously, and put him on her landline to call a tow, and then sat across the table from him with her knitting while he waited, watching him with the frank appraisal of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons and had therefore lost all patience with men who lied about how they were feeling.

"You're hurt," she said.

"I'm fine."

"Mm." She turned a heel. "There's a spare room. You'll sleep in it. You can argue in the morning, when arguing costs you less."

He slept in it. He woke at dawn to a sky scrubbed clean and blue, the particular merciless beauty of the day after a storm, and found his phone — dried out, wrapped in rice in a jar on the kitchen counter, because Mrs. Tannhouser was that kind of woman — blinking with a message.

Not from Aldous. From a number he didn't know. The message read:

**MR. CRANE — WE UNDERSTAND YOU'VE HAD AN EVENTFUL EVENING. PLEASE PRESENT YOURSELF AT OUR CORVANE OFFICE AT YOUR EARLIEST CONVENIENCE REGARDING THE RESTRUCTURE OF YOUR OBLIGATIONS. — VELLUM SYNDICATE, ACCOUNTS DIVISION**

*Restructure.* Yara stood in Mrs. Tannhouser's kitchen holding the phone, wearing borrowed clothes two sizes too big, smelling of woodsmoke and other people's soap, and read the word three times. Restructure. As though his life were a building and they were contractors. As though the collapse were a design feature.

Mrs. Tannhouser looked up from her porridge. "Bad news?"

"Debt collectors."

"Ah." She nodded with the gravity the subject deserved. "My husband owed the Vellum people, back in the dam years. They were polite about it. That's the worst thing I can say about anyone." She pushed the porridge pot toward him. "Eat. You can't fight anybody hungry, and from the look of you, fighting is coming."

* * *

Corvane took two days to reach, riding buses and once, memorably, the back of a fertilizer truck whose driver talked the entire way about his opinions on drainage policy. The Vellum Syndicate's offices occupied the third floor of a building on Ledger Street — Yara had always assumed the street was named for something else, but standing in the marble lobby he revised the assumption — behind a door of smoked glass with the company name etched in letters so understated they were practically an apology.

Inside, everything was quiet. That was the first thing. No phones ringing, no raised voices, none of the sweaty urgency of the credit union back home with its flickering fluorescent lights and its posters about financial responsibility. Just carpet the color of oatmeal, and clerks moving between desks with the smooth unhurried gait of people who had never once been chased by anything, and a smell of paper and lemon polish that Yara would remember for the rest of his life.

They gave him tea. That was the second thing. A clerk named Miss Ferrow — gray suit, gray eyes, a face like a closed ledger — brought him tea in an actual cup and asked after his health with what sounded like genuine interest, and Yara understood, sitting there with his bruised ribs and his borrowed coat, that he was being handled. He'd been handled before, by bill collectors and repo men and one memorable bailiff, and it had always been loud. This was different. This was courtesy as an instrument.

"Mr. Crane." Miss Ferrow opened a folder. "Thank you for coming. Before we begin, may I say we were sorry to hear about your vehicle. Accidents are distressing."

"It wasn't an accident," Yara said. "I drove into a washout because I was on the phone. That's not an accident, that's a decision with consequences."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Approval, maybe. Or the memory of approval, filed away. "Honesty. Good. It will make this faster." She turned the folder around. "Your obligations. As of the twenty-third of last month, your outstanding balance — principal, accrued interest, and penalty provisions — was acquired by this firm from the Territorial Credit Union. You were notified by mail."

"I get a lot of mail."

"So we've observed." She slid a paper across. "Your total obligation currently stands at nineteen thousand, four hundred marks."

The number landed in the room like a dropped anvil. Yara looked at it. Nineteen thousand. It had been eleven thousand in the spring. He'd known the penalties were bad. He hadn't known they were *architectural.*

"Huh," he said, and then, because dread dressed as bravado was the only outfit he owned, he laughed. Right there in the quiet office, in front of Miss Ferrow and the two clerks who had paused their work to listen, he threw his head back and laughed like a man hearing a good joke at a party, loud and easy, while his heart performed maneuvers that would have concerned a physician.

Miss Ferrow waited him out with the patience of a woman who had heard every possible response to bad news and ranked laughter somewhere in the middle.

"Done?" she asked, when he wound down.

"Yeah. Sorry. Go on."

"The Syndicate," she said, "does not operate as a conventional lender. We do not want your truck — you no longer have one. We do not want your wages — your income is, forgive me, theoretical. What we want is resolution. Debts that sit are debts that die. We prefer our accounts active." She produced a second document, thicker, bound with a clip. "Accordingly, we offer restructure through service."

"Service," Yara repeated.

"A task. One task, completed to standard, and your obligation is discharged in full. Nineteen thousand marks, erased. Clean slate." She said it smoothly, but Yara had grown up in flood country, and he knew what smooth water meant. It meant depth. It meant current underneath. "You have a reputation, Mr. Crane. Resourceful. Willing to go places other people won't. Comfortable with discomfort."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said about me since the tow driver."

"The task involves travel. Upriver." She let the word sit there. "North, into the high country. A recovery job."

Recovery. Yara turned the word over. Recovery of what, she didn't say, and he didn't ask, and the not-asking felt like the first signature on a document he hadn't seen yet. Outside the smoked glass, Corvane went about its morning — carts and gulls and a man arguing with a fishmonger — a whole city of people who owed things, all of them certain, the way he'd been certain yesterday, that their debts were their own business.

He should have asked questions. He knew that even then, some honest corner of himself taking notes: *ask what the job is, ask why they need a storm chaser for it, ask what happens to the last person who tried.* But the number was on the table — nineteen thousand, four hundred — and winter was coming, and the honest corner of himself had never once paid a bill.

"Say I say yes," Yara said. "What's the catch?"

"There is no catch, Mr. Crane. There are terms." Miss Ferrow uncapped a pen and set it beside the contract with a small precise click, like a safety being taken off. "Sign today, and your winter belongs to us. Complete the work, and you owe nothing. Refuse—" she smiled, briefly, a professional warmth with nothing behind it, "—well. Everyone refuses at first. Then they look at the number again."

Yara looked at the number again. Nineteen thousand, four hundred. He thought about Mrs. Tannhouser's spare room, and the ditch, and the truck sitting dead in it with its wheels still slowly turning, and the long gray slide of a winter with no income and no vehicle and a debt growing under the floorboards.

He picked up the pen. It was heavier than it looked, or his hand was lighter than it should have been; he never determined which.

"You know," he said, signing, laughing again — softly this time, at himself, at the whole ridiculous architecture of his life — "my father always said I'd end up working for somebody."

"And did he say who?"

"No. He died before the punchline." Yara set down the pen and blew on the ink. "So. Where exactly upriver are you sending me?"

Miss Ferrow closed the folder with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a lid on something alive.

"Corvane's not the place to discuss it," she said. "Come back tomorrow. Bring a bag."
