## 2. A Job Upstream

The office of the Vellum Syndicate smelled like a lawyer's briefcase that had been left out in the rain — leather and damp paper and something underneath both that Yara decided not to think too hard about. He sat in a chair that cost more than his truck had, before the river ate it, and watched a man named Corben Halvorsen pour coffee from a silver pot with the ceremony of someone baptizing a child.

"Sugar?" Halvorsen asked.

"No, thank you."

"Of course not. Storm men don't take sugar. You people like your weather the way you like your coffee." He smiled as if he'd said something charming instead of nothing at all.

Yara's arm was still in a sling from the crash, and his ribs had opinions about the chair, and the whole left side of his face had gone the color of a plum left too long in a hot window. He'd spent two days in a clinic on the syndicate's dime — a phrase that had been doing quiet, terrible work in his head since the doctor said it. *On whose dime, exactly.* He knew the answer. The answer had bought his debt the same afternoon his truck went into the Cutler River, with the kind of timing that wasn't luck. Luck didn't read credit ledgers.

He took the coffee because refusing it felt like a longer conversation than accepting it. It was good coffee. That was somehow worse.

"Mr. Crane," Halvorsen said, settling behind a desk of dark wood that had probably once been a very old tree with a very good opinion of itself, "I want to begin by saying how sorry we all were to hear about your accident."

"My accident," Yara said. "Right."

"The bridge railing on the Cutler road has been flagged for repair three times. The county is negligent. You could sue."

"I could also grow gills. Neither is likely."

Halvorsen laughed — a real laugh, which was the most unsettling thing in the room so far. He was a compact man, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that aged well because it had never done anything strenuous. His hands were clean. Yara kept looking at them. There was something about a man's hands that told you more than his eyes, and Halvorsen's hands told you he had never in his life carried anything heavier than a fountain pen.

"You're wondering why we bought your paper," Halvorsen said.

"The thought had crossed the river."

"Three lenders held your debt. We consolidated. It's what we do — we find obligations scattered across a territory, and we gather them up, and we make them simple. One debt. One debtor. One conversation." He folded his hands on the desk. "I find that people sleep better when their obligations are simple. Don't you?"

"I sleep fine."

"You owe the syndicate eleven thousand, four hundred marks, Mr. Crane. At our standard rate, that's a number that grows faster than you can chase weather for hire. In four years it's twenty thousand. In eight, it's a house you don't own and a season you can't afford to fly." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis he's delivered ten thousand times. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you. I'm telling you because I respect you enough to skip the part where we pretend."

Yara drank his coffee. It was still good. He was beginning to hate it.

"Here's what pretending sounds like," Halvorsen continued. "Pretending sounds like *we can restructure your payments*. Pretending sounds like *work it off in the shipping yards*. You'd be fifty before the number stopped moving. So instead, I'd like to offer you a job."

"A job."

"A job. Paid at triple your chase rate, expenses covered, and — this is the part I'd underline if we were doing this on paper — upon completion, your ledger is closed. Zero. A clean page."

Yara set the cup down. It made a small sound against the saucer, and in the quiet office it sounded like a gavel.

"What's the job?"

Halvorsen opened a drawer and produced a folder, thin, government-gray, the kind of folder that wanted very badly to be unremarkable. He laid it on the desk but didn't slide it across.

"Two months ago, a survey expedition went up the Vessel River. Nine people. Geologists, a surveyor, a cartographer, support staff. They were mapping the high country above the Escarpment." He paused, and Yara watched him choose his next words the way a man chooses footholds on loose rock. "They carried with them a case of documents. Deeds, grants, title papers, the legal history of the land they were walking on. Standard practice. You don't survey land without knowing who owns it."

"And?"

"And they stopped transmitting. The last signal was garbled. Weather, we assumed — it was that kind of season. Search parties went as far as the gorges and came back. The river's in flood, the trails are washed out, the high passes are already dusting with snow. Nobody wants to die for a filing cabinet." He smiled again. "But the documents matter. To us. To a great many parties. And you, Mr. Crane, are the only man I know who has flown that gorge in weather that grounded everyone else, who has walked the Escarpment's back side, and who is currently — forgive me — between engagements."

"Recovery work," Yara said.

"Recovery work."

"Find the team."

"Find the team, or find what's left of the team. Bring back the case. The documents are syndicate property in a sense — they concern holdings we administer. This is an errand, Mr. Crane. An expensive, uncomfortable errand, and you are the expensive, uncomfortable man for it."

Yara looked at the folder. It sat there being gray. He thought: *recovery*. The word had a nice solid feel to it, like a stone you could stand on. Recovery was what you did when something was lost. It was honest work. Fishermen recovered nets. Farmers recovered fences after the flood. There was a whole respectable category of human effort that consisted of going out into the weather and bringing things home.

He was aware, in a distant and not entirely comfortable way, that he was doing something with the word that a word shouldn't be asked to do. He did it anyway. It was a very good word.

"The crew," he said. "I'll need a boatman for the gorges. Odell, if he's sober enough to say yes. A radio man — there's a kid named Pim who can hear a whisper through static. And a guide who knows the high country. Sarel Vance."

Halvorsen raised an eyebrow. "Friends of yours?"

"Odell owes me money. Pim owes me money. Sarel and I are even, which between old friends is its own kind of debt." He shrugged with his good shoulder. "Desperate people make loyal crews. You'd know."

Something flickered behind Halvorsen's pleasant expression — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Your friends' debts are their own affairs," he said. "Though I'll note the syndicate is always open to consolidation. It's what we do."

"I'll tell them you said that. It'll warm their hearts."

"See that you do." Halvorsen finally slid the folder across. "The terms are inside. Read them tonight. One condition I'll state aloud, because it matters: the case comes back intact, unopened by anyone but its rightful handlers, and the survey team's personal effects are to be treated with respect. We are not scavengers, Mr. Crane. We are an institution."

"Of course," Yara said, and was surprised to find he mostly believed it, which was the trick, he'd understand later, the whole trick, the door swinging open on hinges he couldn't see.

He stood to go. His ribs objected. Halvorsen rose too and extended the clean hand, and Yara shook it, and the hand was dry and cool and closed around his with the exact pressure of a man who had practiced.

"One more thing," Halvorsen said at the door, casual as weather talk. "The Marchetti team. The surveyor was a woman named Ilse Marchetti. Family name on the old grants. If you find her alive — and people are sturdier than we think — you bring her out too. We take care of our people."

"Is she your people?"

"She's the territory's people," Halvorsen said pleasantly. "Aren't we all?"

* * *

The folder, read that night in a rented room above a closed cannery, contained eleven pages. Nine of them were logistics: boat permits, gorge charts with the syndicate's own soundings penciled in — better soundings than the county's, which should have been its own warning — supply manifests, a radio frequency, a payment schedule. The tenth page was the ledger summary, his debt rendered in neat columns, and seeing eleven thousand four hundred written in someone else's tidy hand made it feel both more real and less like something he had done. Debts you built yourself had a shape you recognized. This had been *arranged*.

The eleventh page was a single paragraph, typed, unsigned:

*In consideration of services rendered, the Vellum Syndicate agrees to extinguish all outstanding obligations of Yara T. Crane upon verified recovery of the Marchetti case materials. This agreement is void in the event of disclosure of its terms to any third party, including but not limited to members of the recovery crew.*

He read it four times. The phrase *including but not limited to members of the recovery crew* sat in the middle of it like a stone in a shoe. He'd be hiring Sarel and Odell and Pim, and he wouldn't be able to tell them what the trip was worth to him, or what it would cost them if it went wrong. He'd be lying by structure rather than by statement, which was the kind of lying that let you keep looking yourself in the mirror, because you never technically said anything untrue.

*It's recovery work*, he told the ceiling. The ceiling, which had heard better arguments from worse men, said nothing.

He thought about the survey team instead, because thinking about them was easier. Nine people, two months gone. He'd flown over the Vessel headwaters in June and remembered thinking, looking down at the green thread of the river through the gorges, that it was the kind of country that either gave you exactly what you needed or kept it. He'd mapped storms for eleven years. He knew how country swallowed things — a truck, a barn, a whole town's worth of certainty — and how it did it without malice, which was the frightening part. The land didn't take sides. It just took.

And if the team was dead, then the case was sitting somewhere in the high country waiting for him, and bringing it home was a kindness to everyone, a way for the dead to have meant something, paperwork as a kind of burial. That was a comfortable thought too. He was having a very comfortable evening.

He got up, found the bottle he'd sworn off at the clinic, and poured one finger, because a man should mark the signing of a contract with something, even a contract with himself, especially a contract with himself.

"To recovery," he said to the room.

The room, like the ceiling, declined to comment.

* * *

He found Odell the next morning at the fuel dock on the Cutler, where Odell was technically employed and actually asleep, sitting upright on a coil of rope with his hat over his eyes like the sun had personally offended him. Yara nudged his boot. One eye appeared under the hat brim, took Yara's measure — the sling, the plum-colored face — and disappeared again.

"Truck or face?" Odell asked.

"Both. Same event."

"River?"

"River."

Odell nodded with the gravity of a priest. "River keeps what it takes." He removed the hat and looked up properly. "You're here because you need a boatman and I'm the only one stupid enough to run the Vessel gorges this late."

"I'm here because I missed your face."

"Same thing." Odell stood, joints reporting in one by one like a crew taking roll. "What's the pay?"

"Triple chase rate. Expenses covered."

"Whose expenses?"

"Mine to distribute. Yours to spend."

"That's the kind of arithmetic I like." Odell spat thoughtfully off the dock. "The Vessel's dropping. Two more weeks of water and then it's ice and nobody runs anything. You want to go now."

"I want to go the day after tomorrow."

"Then tomorrow," Odell said, "because the day after tomorrow I'll have thought about it, and thinking is where I keep all my good sense." He stuck out a hand like a dock cleat. Yara shook it. "Who else's fool enough?"

"Pim. And Sarel Vance."

Odell's face did something small and complicated. "Sarel," he said. "Well. He knows the high country better than anyone breathing. And he wants out of his debts worse than you do, and that's saying something, because you've got the look of a man doing sums in his sleep."

"I sleep fine."

"Sure you do." Odell settled his hat back down. "Go get your radio kid. I'll get the boat ready. And Crane —" the eye appeared again under the brim — "who's paying for all this? Triple rate and clean ledgers don't grow on trees."

"The syndicate."

The eye stayed on him a long moment. The river slapped the dock pilings. Somewhere down the shore a gull was being disagreeable about something.

"Recovery work," Odell said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite a statement either. It was a word being held up to the light and turned over, and Yara watched it turn and said nothing, and Odell put his hat down over his eyes and went back to sleep, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

Pim said yes before Yara finished the sentence, which was how Pim said everything. He was nineteen, all elbows and enthusiasm, with a radio shack in a shed behind his mother's house that hummed day and night like a hive. When Yara told him the route — the Vessel gorges, the high country, the Escarpment's back side — Pim's whole face lit up like a dial finding a frequency.

"The Marchetti expedition? The one that went dark?" He was already pulling a battered logbook off a shelf. "I've been listening for them for two months. Everybody has. There's a whole thread on the territorial band about it." He flipped pages, hunting. "Their last transmission came through a relay at Fort Ansel and half the operators in the territory copied fragments of it. Nobody agrees on what it says. The official version is weather damage to their antenna array, but —"

"Pim."

"— but I've got three separate copies and they don't all match, and the differences are in the interesting part, and —"

"*Pim.*"

Pim stopped, vibrating.

"Pack for cold," Yara said. "We leave tomorrow."

"Tomorrow!" Pim said, in the voice other men used for *tonight we feast*. Then, quieter, with the first flicker of the seriousness that lived in him under all the static: "Yara. If they're alive up there, two months — do you think —"

"I think people are sturdier than we think," Yara said, borrowing Halvorsen's words without noticing he was doing it, and Pim nodded and started pulling gear off shelves with both hands, and that was that.

* * *

He saved Sarel for last, which was not an accident. He told himself it was scheduling — Sarel ran traplines up the Cutler drainage and was hard to find — but the truth, which he examined for exactly as long as it took to put down again, was that Sarel was the one whose face he didn't want to watch while he lied.

He found him at the trapline cabin at dusk, splitting wood in the blue cold, and Sarel saw the sling and the bruised face and the syndicate's gray folder sticking out of Yara's coat, and read the whole story off him in about four seconds, because Sarel had been reading Yara since they were fourteen and stealing a boat together.

"Syndicate," Sarel said. Not a question either. Everyone in Yara's life had stopped asking questions and started making statements.

"Recovery work," Yara said.

Sarel set the axe against the block. He was a lean man going gray early, with a stillness to him that the woods had put there and nothing since had disturbed. "How much?"

"Enough to clear me. Triple rate besides."

"And what do they want recovered?"

"Documents. A survey team went up the Vessel and went quiet. There's a case of title papers."

Sarel looked at him. The dusk came down around them, and somewhere upslope a pine dropped its load of snow with a soft whump, and Yara had the strange sensation of standing on both sides of a conversation at once.

"Title papers," Sarel said. "For land."

"For the high country. The Escarpment holdings. It's an errand, Sarel. An expensive, uncomfortable errand, and I'm the —"

"— expensive, uncomfortable man for it. Yes. I've heard you practice that." Sarel picked the axe back up, considered a length of birch, and split it in one clean stroke. "I'll come."

Yara blinked. "That was fast."

"I want what you want." Sarel set another length of birch on the block. "A clean page. You think I haven't done the arithmetic? My ledger's smaller than yours and my season's shorter. This is the one door out that doesn't lead through the shipping yards." He swung. The birch fell apart. "Besides," he said, and here he smiled, the old smile, the one from the stolen boat, "somebody has to keep you alive up there, and Odell's going to be drunk by the second gorge, and the radio kid is going to talk the whole mountain into avalanching just to shut him up."

"You're a good friend," Yara said.

"I'm a desperate one," Sarel said cheerfully. "Same thing, up here."

They shook on it in the blue dusk, and Yara walked back down toward the river feeling the strange lightness of a man who has just made everything simple: one job, one crew, one debt, one conversation. The stars were coming out over the Escarpment, sharp and cold, and the mountain stood against them like a held breath, and Yara looked up at it and thought, with the particular satisfaction of a man who has stopped asking questions at exactly the right moment to remain happy:

*Recovery work. Find them, bring it back, come home clean.*

Behind him, Sarel stood at his woodpile and watched him go, and did his own arithmetic, and said nothing at all.

* * *

The last thing Yara did before leaving was stop at the syndicate's Cutler office, a narrow storefront with brass letters, to sign the final papers and collect the advance. Halvorsen wasn't there; a clerk handled it, efficient and incurious. But as Yara was leaving, the clerk handed him one extra item — a single sheet, folded in thirds.

"Mr. Halvorsen asked me to pass this along. He said you'd want it for the trip."

Yara unfolded it. It was a radio transcript, the Marchetti expedition's final transmission, copied from the Fort Ansel relay. Most of it was static rendered in typewriter ink, a storm of dots and dashes and fragments — *...position... ridge line... cannot...* — the ordinary debris of a dying signal.

But near the bottom, one line had come through whole, and the clerk had underlined it, or Halvorsen had, in pencil, in a neat and careful hand:

*...the escarpment is moving. I say again, the escarpment is —*

The rest was static.

Yara stood on the sidewalk in the cold with the paper in his good hand, and read it three times, and told himself it was a figure of speech. Surveyors talked like that. *The ground's moving* meant the instruments were drifting, the benchmark was off, the fog was playing tricks. It was a technical phrase. Men who worked with the land had a hundred ways of saying the land was misbehaving.

It was a figure of speech. He folded the transcript into his coat, next to the folder, next to the eleventh page with its careful stone-in-the-shoe sentence, and went to find Odell and the boat.

The river was already dropping. You could hear it — a lower note in its voice than yesterday, the water getting ready to leave. Two weeks, Odell had said. Maybe less.

Yara stood at the dock in the last of the light and watched the Vessel run gray and fast toward the gorges, toward the high country, toward whatever was up there moving or not moving, and he thought: *I sleep fine.*

Then he went to pack.
