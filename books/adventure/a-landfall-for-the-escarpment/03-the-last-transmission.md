## 3. The Last Transmission

The tape player in Yara Crane's truck had exactly two settings: play and a grinding noise that sounded like the machine was chewing its own opinions. He'd learned to live with both. What he hadn't learned to live with was listening to a dead man's voice eleven times in one night, but here he was, twelfth time's the charm, hunched over the dashboard of a borrowed rig outside a fuel depot that smelled like diesel and regret.

The depot lights buzzed overhead and drew moths in numbers that suggested they were holding some kind of convention. Yara ignored them. He ignored the cold seeping through the door seals, too. He pressed PLAY again.

*"—Marchetti expedition, day forty-one, position—"*

Static. A long wash of it, like the river itself had gotten into the recording.

*"—repeat, if anyone is receiving, this is Tomas Marchetti, survey vessel* Kestrel *—"* A cough, wet and wrong. *"—day forty-one. We've pulled up to the second gorge. The instruments are — Ilse says the instruments are reading movement we can't account for. Not seismic. Something structural."*

Yara knew that voice now better than his own father's. That was what happened when you played a man's last words on a loop: he became family. Tomas Marchetti had a habit of pausing before numbers, as though each one needed to be introduced politely. Day forty-one. Forty-one days into a survey that should have taken thirty, sent upriver to map a stretch of the Escarpment nobody with sense went near after midsummer.

The syndicate had given him the tape in a manila envelope along with the contract. "For orientation," the broker had said, a woman named Dree with a voice like a filing cabinet closing. "The last confirmed transmission. It'll help you understand the terrain."

What it helped him understand, so far, was dread.

He'd told himself all evening that he was doing due diligence. Professionals reviewed their materials. Storm chasers reviewed radar loops; recovery men reviewed last transmissions; men who owed the Vellum Syndicate more than they'd earn in six lifetimes reviewed whatever the syndicate put in front of them and said thank you. This was diligence. This was not obsession. The moths could judge him all they wanted.

PLAY.

*"—the escarpment is moving."*

There it was. The sentence. Eleven plays in and it still landed like a dropped wrench.

Yara had heard a lot of garbled things come out of radios. Half his working life was garbled things: tower chatter shredded by storm static, maydays from lake barges, his own voice bounced back at him off cloud banks sounding like a stranger having a worse day than he was. You developed an ear for it. You learned which distortions were weather and which were machinery and which were fear changing how a man held his mouth around a microphone.

This wasn't weather. Weather smeared. Weather made voices soft and far away, drowned them in white. Whatever had gotten hold of Tomas Marchetti's transmission didn't smear — it *clipped*. Words arrived whole and then vanished whole, cut clean at both ends, like someone upstream was editing the tape with scissors. And underneath the voice, so faint you had to lean your whole skull into the speaker, there was something else. A rhythm. Low, arrhythmic, patient. Not wind. Wind didn't keep a beat.

He'd run it through Pim's spectrum analyzer — no. Wrong name. He hadn't hired Pim yet. That came later, or earlier, depending on how you counted chapters of a life. Right now it was just Yara and the truck and the tape and the moths, and the analyzer was a battered unit he'd bought off a decommissioned weather buoy tender who'd sworn it worked fine except for the parts that didn't.

He ran it through the analyzer anyway. The rhythm showed up as a band of low-frequency noise, steady as a heartbeat, rising slightly toward the end of the transmission. Toward the moment Tomas stopped talking mid-word.

*"—it's not a slide, do you understand me, a slide has an edge, this has no edge, the whole face of it is—"* Static, three seconds, four. Then, quieter, the way men speak when they've stopped talking to the radio and started talking to whoever might someday listen: *"Ilse wants to push on to the high camp. I want to go downriver. We're putting it to a vote tonight. If anyone receives this — the escarpment is moving. Not falling. Moving."*

END OF LOG.

The machine made its grinding noise, which Yara had privately decided was its way of clearing its throat.

He sat back. Outside, a moth the size of a thumb landed on the windshield and regarded him with what he chose to interpret as sympathy.

Here was the thing he kept circling. Here was the thing that had turned twelve plays into thirteen.

The syndicate's file on the Marchetti expedition was thorough in the way syndicate files always were — every debt, every equipment lease, every signature Tomas had ever inked, cross-referenced and annotated like scripture. Yara had read it twice on the drive up. And nowhere, in forty pages of meticulous accounting, was there any mention of geological instability. No hazard assessment. No note from the syndicate's own surveyors flagging the gorge. The Vellum Syndicate did not send quarter-million-dollar expeditions into terrain they believed was failing. They were creditors, not gamblers. They knew the difference between risk and ruin because their entire business was selling the first and collecting on the second.

So either the syndicate didn't know the escarpment was moving —

— or they knew and hadn't cared, because what they wanted out of that gorge wasn't maps.

Yara laughed. It came out at the wrong volume for an empty parking lot, and a moth left the windshield in protest. He'd been told before that he laughed at the wrong moments. His ex-wife had catalogued it like a symptom. Funerals, bad news, the moment the bank manager said the word *foreclosure* — that was when the laugh came, this bright idiot bark, his body's way of slamming a door on a feeling too big to fit through it.

Because here was the other thing. The small ugly thing he'd been stepping around all evening like a hole in a floor.

Tomas Marchetti's log mentioned a vote. Push on to the high camp, or turn downriver. A crew of nine, voting on whether to climb deeper into a mountain that was talking to them. And the syndicate's file listed the expedition's insurance rider — Yara had checked, because checking was diligence — and the rider covered loss of equipment up to the full insured value, but only *above the second gorge*. Below it, the coverage dropped by ninety percent.

Somebody had written a policy that paid best exactly where the mountain was worst.

You could call that coincidence. Yara had spent enough years chasing storms to know that coincidences were just patterns that hadn't finished introducing themselves. A cell that rotates is a cell that's organizing. A policy that pays best where the danger peaks isn't insurance. It's a wager. Somebody at Vellum had bet on the mountain, and the Marchettis had been the stake.

And the deed — the documents, the case, the thing he was actually being sent to fetch, dressed up in language about *recovery of company property* — sat somewhere in that country like a coin at the bottom of a well full of knives.

He pressed PLAY again. Thirteen. Fourteen. Who was counting. The moths, probably.

*"—day forty-one—"* Tomas began, polite as ever, introducing his numbers, and Yara let the voice run while he stared through the windshield at the dark line of the Escarpment far to the north, a black seam between the mountains and the sky, sixty miles off and visible even now, even here, because it was that big. People who hadn't seen it thought *escarpment* meant cliff. It didn't. It meant a wall of country four hundred miles long, a step in the world where the lowlands gave up and the highlands began, forested to the shoulders and stone above, and rivers came down it in ropes of white water, and the maps of its interior were mostly polite fiction.

Somewhere inside that fiction, nine people had voted, and then gone silent.

The official story — the one in the newspapers, the one the territorial office had printed in its tidy missing-persons bulletin — was weather. Early season storm, unseasonable, unprecedented, tragic. The bulletin used the word *presumed* four times. Presumed lost. Presumed deceased. Presumed beyond assistance. Yara had noticed that *presumed* was a word that did a lot of work for institutions. It let them grieve without searching.

But the storm record for that week was sitting right there in his own head, because storm records were the one library he kept faithfully. He'd chased professionally for eleven years; he remembered cells the way other men remembered ball games. And the week the Kestrel went quiet had been — he closed his eyes and walked back through it — mild. Genuinely mild. A ridge of high pressure had parked over the whole upper basin like a fat cat in a doorway. He remembered being annoyed about it, because mild weather meant no work, and no work meant the debt collectors got closer, and ha, look at him now, the debt collectors had solved that problem by buying him outright.

No storm. No storm anywhere near the gorge that week. The bulletin's weather was a lie so lazy it hadn't even bothered to check the almanac.

Which left the log. Which left the clipped transmissions. Which left the low patient rhythm under Tomas's voice, rising toward the end like something waking up.

Yara had a theory forming, and he hated it, because theories formed at one in the morning in fuel depots were usually wrong, but this one had the specific awful weight of things that are true. The clipping wasn't interference. Interference didn't cut words off clean at both ends. But a transmitter with failing power did — voltage sagging, signal dropping below threshold, words guillotined mid-syllable. Except the Kestrel's rig, per the manifest, was a new unit with a battery bank rated for a year.

Or somebody else was transmitting on the same frequency, close by, and every time they keyed their mic, it stepped on the Kestrel's signal. Clean cuts. Whole words arriving, whole words vanishing. Someone else on that channel, near that camp, talking to someone else entirely — and Tomas Marchetti, day forty-one, broadcasting his little votes and his readings into a conversation he couldn't hear.

Who talks on a dead expedition's frequency? Nobody honest. Honest men hail and identify. Whoever shared that channel with the Kestrel in its last hours hadn't identified, hadn't hailed, had just talked over a dying man like he was static.

Yara looked at the analyzer's trace one more time. The low rhythm. Steady, patient, rising. He'd spent eleven years learning to read the moods of atmosphere, and atmosphere had taught him a humility he resented: everything large moves slowly, and everything slow looks permanent until the day it doesn't. Glaciers were supposed to be furniture. Rivers were supposed to stay in their beds. The escarpment was supposed to be the one fixed thing on the map, the step the world rested its foot on.

*Not falling. Moving.*

He ejected the tape. Held it up to the depot light. On the label, in the broker Dree's efficient hand: *MARCHETTI / FINAL / FOR ORIENTATION.* Nothing classified about it. She'd handed it over like a bus schedule. Which meant either she didn't hear what he heard — possible; most people heard static where he heard structure, that was the whole basis of his trade — or she'd heard it a hundred times and filed it under *acceptable losses*, and the word *final* on the label was less a description than a conclusion already reached.

"Orientation," Yara said to the moths. "Sure."

One of them landed on the tape reel, which he took as a vote of no confidence.

He should sleep. He had a long drive tomorrow, down to the river town where the syndicate's boat waited, where he'd meet whatever crew the contract budget allowed — and the contract budget, he'd noticed, allowed for a boatman, a radio tech, and a guide, and not one person more, which was either thrift or arithmetic of a kind he was starting to recognize everywhere now. Four men upriver. Nine men hadn't come back. The math had a smell to it.

But sleep wouldn't come, and the tape was in his hand, and there was one more thing he could do with it, one more trick from the storm-chaser's kit that he'd never expected to use on anything but thunder.

Doppler. You couldn't get true doppler off a single receiver, but you could get a cheap cousin of it: play the same recording against itself, offset by fractions of a second, and listen for the beat frequencies where the sound interfered with itself. Chasers used it to hear rotation inside storms before the funnel showed — a low moan under the rain, the storm's true voice saying *I am turning, I am turning.* Every chaser learned it eventually, usually from a tornado that introduced itself personally.

He wired the loop, offset the playback, and listened.

Under Tomas's voice, under the static, under everything, the low rhythm resolved. Not random. Not machinery. A pulse with a shape to it — long swell, short fall, long swell — the exact signature a chaser learns to dread, except upside down and underground, slowed a hundredfold, as though the storm-rotation trick had been performed on solid rock.

Something up in that gorge was turning over in its sleep. Had been, for weeks, while nine people camped on its shoulder and wrote polite logs introducing their numbers.

And the syndicate had read those same logs — the file included transcriptions, Yara was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure about a floor joist that gives under your boot — and had sent the expedition *deeper* after day thirty-five. There was a supply drop scheduled for day thirty-eight. Upriver. Further in. Someone at Vellum had read *the escarpment is moving* and responded by sending more rope.

Unless the moving escarpment was the point. Unless the plan had never been maps at all.

Yara shut off the machine. The silence afterward had texture, like felt. He sat with it a while, and the moths sat with him, and far north the black seam of the Escarpment held its line against the stars, patient as a creditor.

He thought about Sarel Vance — old friend, old debts, the guide the contract would pair him with, a man he'd known since they were boys stealing apples off the same crooked tree. He thought about telling Sarel what he'd heard tonight. And he found, with a small cold surprise, that he wasn't sure he would. Not because Sarel couldn't be trusted — or not only that — but because some knowledge changes the people who carry it, and Yara could feel this particular knowledge settling into him like silt, changing his draft, making him ride lower in the water.

A man who knows the mountain is moving walks differently. Hesitates at different places. And the syndicate would be watching for exactly that hesitation, the way lenders watch for flinches.

So: he'd say the weather was the concern. He'd say the season was early and the gorges were treacherous and they'd want margin. All true. None of it the truth. He was becoming, he realized, a man with compartments, and the compartments were filling faster than he could build them.

Yara started the truck. The engine caught on the second try, grumbled, settled. On the passenger seat the tape lay in its case, and he looked at it for a long moment before sliding it under the seat mat instead of leaving it in plain view — a small act, instinctive, the first thing he'd done in weeks that wasn't in the contract.

Thirteen plays. He'd promised himself fourteen, hadn't he. Well. Some debts you don't pay.

He drove south through the moth-clouded light, toward the river, toward the boat, toward the crew he hadn't met yet, and behind him the Escarpment stood against the stars with its secret pulse going — long swell, short fall, long swell — counting something out in the dark, patient as interest accruing.

* * *

The radio log of the survey vessel *Kestrel*, recovered from the syndicate archive, entry fragment, day 41, final:

*"...this is Tomas Marchetti... Ilse says the readings doubled overnight... I'm beginning to think we weren't sent here to map anything... if anyone receives this, tell my daughter the case matters more than we do... tell her it names her... tell her not to sign anything..."*

*[transmission ends]*
