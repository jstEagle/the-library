## 4. Hired Hands

The Vellum Syndicate kept its offices in the old customs house down by the ferry landing, a building that had spent forty years pretending to be a bank and had given up even that pretense the day they hung the brass plate. I stood outside it in the gray morning light with a hangover shaped like my own signature and tried to remember at what point in the previous evening I had agreed to anything.

All of it, apparently. I had agreed to all of it.

The clerk — a small, precise woman named Dree who looked at me the way a jeweler looks at paste — slid a paper across the desk and turned it so I could read it right-side up. That was the first kindness anyone at Vellum had shown me.

"Four names," she said. "You'll want a boatman. The river above Kettle Narrows isn't navigable by anyone who talks about it much. A radio operator. And a guide who knows the Escarpment trails. You have three days."

"And if I can't find three people desperate enough in three days?"

She looked up. Her eyes were the color of ledger ink. "Mr. Crane. You found yourself."

I took the paper. It was a list of approved expenses, which is a phrase that means nothing until you have owed money to people who count buttons, and then it means everything. Fuel, provisions, hazard pay — hazard pay, I noticed, was listed as *discretionary*, a word doing more work in that sentence than any word should have to do.

I walked out into the morning and started making a list of my own.

* * *

Odell found me before I found him, which was the first thing I learned about Odell, and for a long time the only thing.

I'd gone down to the boatyards at the east slip, where the rivermen wintered their craft under tarps like sleeping animals, and I was standing at the rail of a flat-bottomed workboat reading the name stenciled on her hull — *Patient Answer*, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer — when a voice behind me said, "She draws eighteen inches loaded. You're thinking she doesn't. Everyone thinks that."

He was a big man built along the lines of a dock piling, with a beard that had clearly won several arguments and a face that had given up on expressing surprise sometime in the previous decade. He stood with his hands in his coat pockets and looked at me with the mild patience of someone waiting for weather.

"You're Odell," I said.

"I'm Odell."

"Someone at Vellum said you were the only man who'd run the gorges above the Narrows this time of year."

"Someone at Vellum owes me money," he said, "and this is how they pay it. By sending me men who ask questions." He scratched under his beard. "You ask questions, Crane?"

"I'm told it's my worst quality."

"Then we'll get along. Questions are fine on a boat. It's answers that sink you." He stepped past me and ran a hand along the gunwale, checking something I couldn't see — a seam, a stress line, some private conversation between him and the wood. "What's the run?"

"Upriver. Past the Narrows, past the old trading posts, up to where the Marchetti survey went quiet."

That got a reaction, if you could call it that. His hand stopped moving on the wood for maybe two seconds, then resumed. With Odell, I would later learn, two seconds was a shout.

"They'll say the river's too low," he said.

"The river's too low."

"They'll say the ice comes early this year."

"It's supposed to."

"They'll say nobody comes back from up there." He turned and looked at me fully for the first time. His eyes were a pale river-gray, and there was something in them that I recognized, because I'd been seeing it in the mirror for a year: the flat arithmetic of a man who has run the numbers on his own life and found a remainder he doesn't like. "All true. When do we leave?"

I blinked. "That's it? No negotiation?"

"Negotiation's for people with options." He patted the hull of the *Patient Answer* the way another man might pat a dog. "You want to know what I want out of this trip, Crane?"

"I assumed I'd have to guess."

"A boat paid off. Clear title. No Vellum stamp on the registration." He said it without heat, the way you'd read a grocery list. "That's all. That's the whole of my ambition. A boat that belongs to me and a river that doesn't belong to anybody." He looked upriver, where the water went gray and narrow between the first shoulders of the hills. "You'll do."

He turned back to his boat and started checking things, and I understood I was dismissed, and that I'd just hired the best boatman on the river, and that neither of us was going to say one honest word about why.

* * *

Pim I found in the telegraph office above the chandlery, and I found him because he found me — or rather, because his radio found me. I was still half a block away when I heard my own name crackling out of an open window in a voice too young for the words it was saying.

*"—repeat, Crane, if you're hearing this, your landlady says your rent is—"* a burst of static — *"—and also that you left your stove on and she turned it off and you owe her a—"* static — *"—Crane?"*

I leaned in the window. A kid looked up from a wall of equipment that hummed and blinked like a nervous organism — a boy, really, seventeen maybe, all elbows and ears, with a shock of dark hair he'd clearly lost a fight with that morning and a pair of headphones pushed up around his neck like a crown he was trying on for size.

"You're broadcasting my private business," I said.

"Your landlady's business," he said. "I just relay it. There's a difference. Legally." He pulled off one headphone. "You're Crane? The storm chaser?"

"Depends who's asking."

"Pim Osterhaut. Radio tech. I've heard your position reports for two seasons — you've got the cleanest field transmission on the coast, everybody says so, you hold your mic right, you don't shout, you—" He stopped himself, ears going red. "I listen. To transmissions. Professionally."

"Uh-huh." I looked at the wall of equipment, then at the kid. "How old are you?"

"Old enough to hold a license and young enough to not have a wife asking why I'm going upriver in October." He said it fast, like a card trick he'd rehearsed. Then, quieter, with the sudden honesty of the young, which is worse than any lie: "I want off this desk, Crane. Three years I've relayed other people's adventures. Flood reports. Survey coordinates. Somebody's last words, once, from up past the Narrows, and I took it down neat and clean and they gave me a commendation and kept me at this desk." His jaw set. "I want to be on the sending end. Just once."

The Marchetti transmission. He'd have been the one who logged it. I filed that away and didn't let it show on my face.

"Pay's bad," I said. "Odds are worse. And the last radio operator who sat where you're sitting is somewhere up that river."

"Then somebody should go find out what happened to him," Pim said. "That's the whole job, isn't it? Somebody has to be on the sending end."

I looked at him a long moment. He looked back with the fearless, uncalculated hope of someone who has not yet learned that hope is a currency and Vellum prints it.

"Report to the east slip tomorrow at dawn," I said. "Bring your own headphones."

He grinned so wide I worried about his ears.

* * *

Sarel Vance took some finding, because Sarel was very good at being found by exactly the people he wanted and invisible to everyone else, a skill he'd been perfecting since we were boys running traplines together above Miller's Fork.

I finally ran him to ground at the back table of the Tin Lantern, the saloon where the guides drank, which is to say the saloon where the guides sat in the corners and watched everyone else drink. He was playing solitaire with a deck missing two cards, and he didn't look up when I sat down across from him.

"Heard you crashed your truck," he said.

"Heard you're broke," I said.

"Everybody's broke. It's the season." He turned over a card, frowned at it, turned it back. "What's Vellum got you doing, Yara?"

"Recovery work. Upriver."

"Recovery." He tasted the word like a wine he suspected. "That what we're calling it now."

"It's what's on the paper."

"Ah," Sarel said. "The paper." He gathered the cards, squared them, and looked up. He was grayer than the last time I'd seen him, a year and a half ago, and thinner in a way that spoke of meals skipped on purpose rather than by circumstance. But his eyes were the same — quick, brown, and always, always doing a second calculation behind the first one. Sarel Vance had never in his life said the first thing he thought, and I'd known him long enough to find that comforting, which should have told me something. "The Marchetti job."

"You've heard."

"Everyone's heard. Everyone's also heard the survey team's dead, and that whatever they were carrying is worth more than the whole of Kettle Narrows, and that Vellum wants it back so badly they're paying hazard rates." He smiled, and it was the old smile, warm and crooked, and it made my chest hurt with a nostalgia I didn't examine too closely. "You need a guide."

"I need the best guide on the Escarpment."

"You need a guide who'll work for debt relief instead of wages, because that's all Vellum's offering, and you thought of me because I'm the only man you know stupid enough to take it." He dealt himself a hand of nothing and looked at it. "You're right on all counts. When do we leave?"

"Day after tomorrow. Dawn."

"Then buy me a drink and we'll call it settled." He raised two fingers toward the barkeep, and while the man poured, Sarel leaned back in his chair and studied me with that second calculation running behind his eyes. "It's good to see you, Yara. It's been too long. Whatever else happens up there — and something always happens up there — I want you to know I'm glad it's you asking."

"Glad it's me, or glad it's anybody?"

"Those aren't different things," he said, "for men like us."

I should have heard it. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this — I should have heard it, the way Odell's two-second silence was a shout, the way Pim's hunger was a wound. Sarel told me exactly what he was, in plain words, over a cheap whiskey, and I heard only an old friend being honest about the shape of our shared ruin.

We drank. We talked about the old days, the traplines, the winter we'd gotten snowed in at the Kettle Fork cabin and eaten nothing but grouse and boiled pine tea for eleven days until we hated each other so thoroughly we came out the other side into a friendship that felt, at the time, permanent. He laughed at the grouse story. He always laughed at the grouse story. And when the lantern burned low and we parted in the street, he gripped my shoulder and said, "Dawn, day after tomorrow," and walked off into the dark with his hands in his pockets, and I stood there a moment thinking — and I remember this precisely, because it's the kind of thought you only get to be ashamed of later — *well, at least one good thing came out of this year.*

* * *

We gathered at the east slip the next dawn but one, and I stood on the deck of the *Patient Answer* and looked at the three of them and felt something I hadn't felt since before Vellum bought my debt — something adjacent to hope, or at least to company.

Odell was checking the fuel lines for the third time, saying nothing, which for Odell was a full conversation. Pim sat cross-legged on the cargo hatch with his headphones on, tuning through bands, his lips moving as he logged frequencies in a notebook with a pencil worn down to a stub. And Sarel stood at the rail looking upriver, where the gorges narrowed and the hills shouldered up into the gray mass of the Escarpment, and his face had the settled calm of a man going home.

"Provisions for twelve days," I told them. "Fuel to the Narrows and back, if the river cooperates. Radio contact with the coast every evening at six." I looked at the three faces turned toward me — the patient one, the hungry one, the calculating one — and I gave them the speech I'd rehearsed on the walk over, the one about recovery work and hazard pay and everybody coming home. It was a good speech. I'd believed most of it.

"Questions?" I finished.

"Two," said Odell.

"Three," said Pim.

"None," said Sarel, smiling.

Odell's two were about load distribution. Pim's three were about whether he'd get to send the position reports himself or whether I'd insist on doing it, and when I said it was all his, he looked like I'd handed him a medal.

Sarel asked nothing, and that should have been the loudest answer on the deck.

We cast off as the sun cleared the hills and the river ran gold and cold under the bow. Pim sent our first position report at six that evening, clean and clear, and got a reply from the coast station, and I watched him hold the message slip like it was a love letter.

By the second day the hills had closed in. By the third, the last farm lights were behind us and the river ran through country that had never much cared for people. And on the fourth evening, as we tied up below the Narrows and the first real cold came down off the Escarpment like a held breath finally let out, Pim came to find me on the deck with his headphones around his neck and a strange look on his face.

"Yara," he said. "I've been catching something on the bands, low power, intermittent. It started last night." He held out the message slip, and in the lantern light I read the pencil characters he'd transcribed, and the cold that came down off the mountain settled into my chest and stayed there.

It was the Marchetti frequency. And someone — or something — was still sending.
