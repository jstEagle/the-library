## 5. Upriver in Falling Light

The river changed its mind about us on the second day.

For the first stretch it had been wide and lazy, all brown patience, carrying our boat like a mother carrying a sleeping child. Odell poled us along with the economy of a man who considered effort a personal failing, and Pim sat in the bow with his radio gear wrapped in oilcloth, narrating every heron and driftwood snag as though filing a report to an audience of one. Sarel lounged against the cargo crates with his hat over his eyes and claimed he was navigating.

"By what?" I asked.

"Instinct," said Sarel.

"You're asleep."

"Navigating in my dreams. It's where I keep my best maps."

I laughed, because it was easier than saying what I was actually thinking, which was that every mile upriver was a mile further from the man I'd been last week — a man who chased storms for money and owed that money to nobody in particular, or at least to nobody who could make him feel it. Now I owed the Vellum Syndicate my whole winter, and they'd dressed the debt up as employment. Find the Marchetti survey team. Bring back their case documents. Simple recovery work, Halvorsen had called it, tapping ash off a cigarette into a dish shaped like a swan.

Recovery work. That's what a thief calls it when he's wearing a lanyard.

The gorge began just past the old ferry landing at Corben's Eddy, where the water remembered it had somewhere to be. The walls came in on both sides like a book closing. Grey stone streaked with rust-colored seeps, and the river threading through the middle of it fast and green and cold enough to make your teeth sing if you so much as trailed a hand. The light arrived late and left early, falling down the slot in one long amber blade that lasted maybe four hours before the shadows swallowed everything above the waterline.

"Falling light," Odell said, apropos of nothing, on the morning of the third day.

"That a warning or a weather report?"

"Yes," said Odell, and pushed us onward.

Odell was a man of perhaps sixty words a day, and he spent them like coins he'd counted twice. He was broad in the way of rivermen, hands like cured leather, and he had agreed to this job for reasons he hadn't shared and I hadn't asked. Everybody on this boat was being paid in something other than money. Pim wanted to be the kind of man who'd done things worth putting on a radio log. Sarel wanted — well, Sarel wanted what Sarel always wanted, which was out from under, though I'd have sworn then that his under and mine were the same under. And me? I told myself I wanted my winter back. I was getting very good at telling myself things.

* * *

The first abandoned camp appeared around noon on the fourth day, tucked into a gravel bench where the gorge widened just enough to let a tent breathe.

It was a survey camp, no question. We knew the Marchetti expedition's kit from the manifests Halvorsen had shown us: olive canvas, brass fittings, equipment stenciled with the little mountain-and-key mark of the Territorial Survey Office. This camp had all of it, arranged with a neatness that stopped Pim mid-sentence.

"They didn't leave in a hurry," he said, crouched over a cook stove that had been scrubbed clean and stacked with its pans nested inside one another. "Look. They *cleaned*."

People who flee don't wash pots. People who are taken don't have time. But these tents were struck properly, guy-lines coiled, stakes bagged and hung from a ridgepole in matching sets of eight. A surveyor's transit stood on its tripod in the middle of the bench, capped and levelled, aimed at nothing in particular — or aimed at something we couldn't see.

"Maybe they consolidated camps," Sarel offered. "Moving upstream, lighter load."

"Then why leave the transit?" Pim said. "That's two months' wages. Nobody leaves that unless—"

"Unless they're coming back," I finished.

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The river talked instead, doing its cold green arithmetic against the hull.

We found more of them as we went. Every few miles, another bench of flat ground, another ghost of occupation. Some were older, weathered grey, their canvas gone soft as old skin. One had a message board nailed to a tree — the survey teams used them to leave routing notes for crews following behind — and the last chalk entry had been rained into illegibility except for a single word: *WAIT.*

Underneath, in different handwriting, smaller and harder: *how long.*

No answer had ever been chalked back.

Pim copied both words into his logbook without being asked. He'd been doing that since the first camp, quiet and thorough, and I noticed I liked him better for it. There's a kind of young man who treats other people's misfortune as material, and there's a kind who treats it as evidence, and the difference between them is the difference between a vulture and a doctor. Pim was still deciding which he'd be, but he was leaning doctor.

* * *

The burned cache found us on the sixth day, in a side canyon where the map promised a supply depot marked with the Syndicate's own key-and-mountain stamp.

What the map did not promise was the smell. Char and kerosene, days old but stubborn, hanging in the cold air of the narrows like a guest who won't take the hint.

The depot had been a rough timber shed built against the canyon wall, and it had not burned so much as been *executed*. Kerosene poured, lit, done — the timbers blackened evenly, the roof collapsed inward in a tidy fan. Whatever had been stored inside was gone past guessing, melted into a crust of slagged tin and glass at the center of the ruin.

Sarel walked the perimeter twice, reading the ground the way he read everything, which was faster than anyone I knew and slower than he let on. He crouched at the edge of the burn scar and picked something out of the ash with two fingers.

"Boot heel," he said. "Big fellow. Stood here while it burned." He turned the heel print toward me. "Facing the fire. Not running from it."

"So somebody torched our supply line and stayed to watch."

"Somebody torched it and *enjoyed* watching." Sarel stood, dusted his knees. "There's a difference, Yara. First one's war. Second one's a hobby."

I didn't love the direction of that sentence. If someone was burning Syndicate caches ahead of us, then either the Marchetti team had gone feral, or someone else was upriver making sure the Syndicate's business stayed unfinished — and neither option made our errand any safer. I kept this to myself. A boat full of doubt rides lower than a boat full of cargo.

We took what little remained: a dented kettle, half a coil of rope that smelled of smoke but held, and a tin of matches that Pim declared, after solemn testing, to be "seventy percent committed to igniting." We were richer by almost nothing and poorer by the knowledge that whatever had happened upriver, it had happened deliberately, and recently, and to people equipped exactly like us.

* * *

It was Odell who found the claw marks.

He found them the way he found everything — silently, and then pointed, and waited for the rest of us to catch up to where he already was. We beached below a shelf of pale limestone where the gorge pinched to hardly a boat's width, and there, chest-high on the rock face beside an old portage trail, the marks ran in a line.

Four gouges, deep, repeated in a rhythm. Bear, I thought first. Cats leave claw marks too, though usually on soft bark, not limestone. But these weren't torn the way an animal tears. An animal drags and slips; its marks wander, deepen where it pulled, vanish where it hesitated. These were even. Spaced. Each set the same distance from the last, each cut to the same depth, marching along the rock in a straight line for twenty feet before stopping clean at the edge of the trail as if the maker had simply decided to stop.

"Bear doesn't use a ruler," Odell said.

"Could be erosion," Sarel said, but his voice had the careful flatness of a man testing ice.

Pim put his fingers in one of the grooves. They went in past the first knuckle. "In limestone? With what?" He looked up at me, and I saw him arrive somewhere I'd been arriving at for two days. "Yara. What cuts stone like that?"

"Tools," I said. "Patience. Or something with a great deal of time and no better hobbies."

I didn't say the third thing, which was: *or something that wanted whoever passed this way to see it.* Because that was the thought that wouldn't leave me alone, standing there in the falling light with the marks marching past my shoulder. Animals mark territory to warn rivals away. This felt like the opposite. This felt like an invitation written in a language none of us spoke, addressed to whoever came next — to *us*, specifically, because nobody else was fool enough to come.

The wilderness had been scenery when we started. Pretty, hostile, expensive scenery, but scenery — the stuff a job happens in front of. Somewhere between the scrubbed cook stoves and the chalked word *WAIT* and the boot print facing the fire, it had stopped being backdrop. It was paying attention now. It had opinions. It was keeping records, and the record it was keeping said: *others came this way, and the gorge remembers them better than you ever will.*

I caught myself talking to it that evening, which is how you know. We made camp on a shingle bar with the cliffs leaning over us like chaperones, and while Pim wrestled the radio mast and Sarel cooked rice with the confidence of a much better cook than he was, I stood at the water's edge and heard myself say, out loud, to the dark mouth of the gorge upstream:

"All right. We see you too."

"What?" called Sarel.

"Nothing. Rice is burning."

"It is *not* burning, it is developing character—"

Smoke, mild cursing, Pim laughing into his antenna wire. For a minute it was almost the old feeling, the road-trip feeling, four fools and a boat and a river going wherever rivers go. I let myself have the minute. You have to bank those; you spend them later whether you want to or not.

* * *

The radio woke us all at 2 a.m., or rather Pim did, shaking my shoulder with a hand that wasn't steady.

"I've got something. On the survey band. It's — you should hear it."

I crawled out of my bedroll into air cold enough to have texture. The stars were a hard white scatter between the cliff tops, and the fire was down to one orange eye, and Pim sat hunched over the receiver with his headphones on one ear, holding the other cup out to me like an offering.

Static. Then, threaded through the static, thin as wire: a voice. Male, exhausted, clipped by interference. Not words yet — fragments. Then, for three seconds, clear as a bell across forty miles of night:

"—the escarpment is moving, do you copy, the escarpment is *moving*—"

Gone. Swallowed. Static again, patient as snowfall.

Pim was scribbling the time and frequency in his logbook, his breath fogging the page. "That's the Marchetti band," he whispered. "Has to be. Their call sign prefix was in the brief."

"Did you answer?"

His face did something complicated. "Twice. No reply." A pause. "Do you think they heard?"

I looked upriver, where the gorge ran on into a darkness deeper than the sky's, toward whatever was moving — the land itself, if the voice was to be believed, which was a thing I filed under *problems for daylight.* Behind me, Sarel had gone very still by the dead fire, and Odell stood at the boat with his pole in his hands like a man who'd already decided the river might be the safest place left.

"Get some sleep," I said, to all of them, and to myself most of all. "Tomorrow we find out what's up there."

And the gorge, witness that it had become, said nothing at all — which I was beginning to understand was its way of agreeing.
