## 6. The Silent Camp

Sarel Vance had a rule about bad places: you learned them with your feet before your eyes ever agreed to see them.

The ground told him first. The gravel bar where Odell beached the boat was swept clean in one direction, the way a floor is swept before company comes. Not flood-swept — flood-sweep goes every which way, leaves its hair and bones behind. This was tidier than that. This was *arranged*, though he couldn't have said arranged by what until he stood on it and felt the wrongness climb up through his boot soles like cold water wicking.

"Here?" Odell asked. He said it the way he said everything, as if the answer hardly mattered because he'd already decided what he'd do either way.

"Here," Sarel said.

The Marchetti camp sat on the bench above the river, tucked against a rock face the way a sensible surveyor would tuck it — out of the wind, above the flood line, near water, near wood. Everything about its position said competence. That was the first thing that bothered him. Good camps don't die.

Yara came up the bank behind him, breathing hard from the climb, Pim puffing after with the radio case hugged to his chest like a schoolbook. Sarel heard Yara stop when he topped the bench, heard the particular quality of silence a man makes when his mouth opens and nothing useful comes out.

"Well," Yara said finally. "Somebody's mother raised them tidy."

That was Yara all over. Sarel had known him eleven years — since the flood year, since the winter they'd both been young enough to think debt was a season that would pass — and Yara met every horror the world offered with a joke held out in front of him like a lantern. It wasn't cowardice. Sarel had thought so once, in his meaner years. It was more like courtesy. Yara Crane joked at terrible things the way you'd take off your hat at a funeral: a small civility offered to something much larger than himself.

Sarel didn't feel like being civil. Sarel felt like counting.

He counted the tents first. Four of them, expedition-grade, the kind that cost more than Odell's boat. Every one of them slashed open along the seams — not torn, not clawed, but cut, long deliberate strokes from inside outward. The fabric bulged through the cuts like skin through a wound.

"Inside," Pim said quietly. "They're cut from inside."

"I can see that," Sarel said, gentler than he felt, because the boy was twenty-two and had signed onto this trip for the pay and the story he'd tell afterward, and neither of those things existed anymore.

Tents slashed from within meant the people inside had done it themselves, or had been made to do it, or — and here Sarel's mind laid out the third option flat and gray as slate — there had been no people inside at all when it happened, and someone outside had wanted very badly for it to look otherwise. He filed that away. Filing things away was most of what Sarel Vance was good for. It was why men like Halvorsen kept his number.

* * *

The instruments were the worst part.

Survey teams travel like traveling circuses — cases everywhere, cables coiled wrong, coffee rings on the transit covers, somebody's socks drying on the tripod. Sarel had run enough crews to know the archaeology of a working camp. This camp had none of it. The theodolite sat on its tripod at the center of the bench, leveled, capped, its case closed beside it with the latches turned home. Around it, in a careful arc, were ranged the other instruments: the levels, the ranging poles bundled square, the steel tapes wound tight on their reels, the notebooks stacked in a row with their spines aligned to the millimeter. Eleven notebooks. He counted twice.

"It's like they left," Pim said. His voice had gone thin. "Like they finished up and packed proper and—"

"They didn't pack." Sarel crouched by the notebooks without touching them. "You don't cap a theodolite and leave it standing in the rain. You case it. You don't leave your books out overnight unless—" He stopped.

"Unless what?" Yara asked.

Unless the leaving-out *was* the point. Unless the whole arrangement was a sentence written in equipment, meant to be read.

Sarel walked the arc slowly. There was an order to it that his hands understood before his head did. He'd spent nine years running lines for mining concerns before the debts took his license, and there was a grammar to instruments, a way they spoke to each other across a site. This arrangement wasn't a camp. It was a *station*. Someone had set the bench up as if to take a sighting — but the theodolite wasn't pointed at any landmark. It was pointed downriver.

Downriver. Toward the lowlands. Toward the syndicate offices, the courthouses, the registry where deeds lived in their fireproof drawers.

"Yara," he said. "Come look at this. Tell me what you see."

Yara came over, hands in his pockets, and looked the way he looked at weather — whole-system, patient, waiting for the pattern to declare itself. Sarel watched his face do three things quickly: recognition, refusal, acceptance.

"It's aimed," Yara said. "At us. At whoever comes up after them."

"At whoever they knew would come up after them." Sarel straightened. His knees cracked; he was forty-six and the mountains collected on that daily. "This isn't wreckage, Crane. Wreckage is honest. This is a letter. Somebody staged this camp the way you'd stage a room for the police — except the police aren't coming up here. Only we are. And whoever set this knew exactly who we'd be."

"You can't know that."

"No," Sarel admitted. "But I can know who it *isn't* for. It isn't for searchers. Searchers get a mess — that's what messes are for, they say *accident, grief, go home*. And it isn't for the syndicate, because the syndicate doesn't need telling anything about its own work." He let that sit in the air between them a moment longer than was comfortable, and watched Yara decide not to pick it up. Good. There would be time. There was always time, right up until there wasn't.

Odell had drifted to the cook shelter, a lean-to of peeled poles. He came back holding a kettle and turned it in his big hands. "Dry," he said. "Everything's dry. Been dry a while." He set it down among the instruments, off the arc, and Sarel noticed he'd placed it carefully so as not to disturb the arrangement, and noticed Odell noticing that he'd noticed, and neither of them said anything about it, because some understandings are better left unwrapped.

* * *

They searched properly after that, because searching was the job even when the job had curdled. Sarel worked the perimeter while Yara and Pim went through the supply cache and Odell walked the bank with that slow rolling gait of his, reading the ground the way Sarel read ledgers.

The cache had been burned. Not looted — burned, thoroughly, and then raked. Under the ash Pim found tin cans opened neatly from the bottom, licked clean, their labels peeled and pressed flat under stones in another little row. Somebody had eaten well and then curated their garbage.

"Claw marks," Yara called from the rock face, and Sarel went to him.

There were marks in the soft stone at the base of the cliff, four parallel gouges repeated in a dozen places, waist-high, uniform. Bear, Pim said hopefully, and Sarel knelt and put his fingers in them and wished he hadn't. Bears are sloppy. Bears gouge high or low depending on the animal, deep or shallow depending on temper, and never, ever the same depth twelve times running. These were the same depth. Same spacing. Same stroke length. A hand had made these marks, or a tool held by a hand, patiently, over hours, the way a man whittles when he has nothing but time and a message to carve.

"Deliberate," Sarel said. "Too deliberate for animals."

"For people too, mostly," Yara said. His joke came late and landed flat, and he knew it, and Sarel saw him reach for another one and decide against it. That frightened Sarel more than the claw marks did. Yara Crane running out of jokes was like a river running out of bank.

* * *

No bodies. That was the thing he kept circling back to as the light went long and coppery over the gorge walls. Four tents, an eleven-man expedition by the manifest Yara carried, and not one body, not one grave, not so much as a boot. Expeditions don't evaporate. Men die in country like this constantly, stupidly, of cold and current and their own confidence, and they leave remains, and the remains tell you something true. Absence told you nothing true. Absence was a language with all the words removed.

Except it wasn't, was it. That was the thought that found him at dusk, sitting on the bench edge with the others building a fire below, careful to build it small and downwind of the camp as if the dead arrangement might object to smoke. Absence *was* the message. Whoever had staged this place had taken the bodies precisely so that there would be nothing else to read. They had erased every honest word and left only the ones they'd chosen. Tents cut from inside. Instruments in a sighting line pointed downriver. Claw marks too neat for bears. A camp that said, in a voice pitched just loud enough to carry downstream:

*We were taken. We did not struggle. Look how orderly. Look how calm. Come and find what we left.*

And what had they left? Sarel went back through the notebooks in the failing light, not touching them, reading spines. Dates, grid references, initials. The last date was nineteen days old. Nineteen days, and the kettles dry, and the cans clean, and the snows coming early this year — everyone on the river said so, the ice was already forming in the eddies at night.

"Sarel." Yara crouched beside him. In the firelight from below his face was half amber, half shadow. "You've got the look. The ledger look. Talk to me."

Sarel weighed it. Here was the trouble with knowing things: knowledge was currency, and he was a man with debts, and a man with debts learns to hold his coin. But Yara had come upriver for him — had vouched for him to the syndicate, had put his own neck next to Sarel's on the same rope, and whatever else Sarel Vance was, and the list was longer than he liked, he paid his friends.

"This camp is addressed," he said. "Not to us generally. To someone specific. Someone who'd recognize the grammar — who'd know a staged station from a real one, who'd read the theodolite's bearing and understand it. That's not a searcher's skill. That's a surveyor's, or a broker's, or—" he tapped his own chest, "—a bookkeeper's. Whoever set this expected a particular reader."

"And?"

"And I think the reader they expected is still ahead of us. Upstream. Because you don't write a letter that elaborate unless you're sure it'll be delivered, and the only way to be sure is to keep the recipient alive." He watched Yara absorb that. "Eleven men go into the mountains and vanish, but somewhere up that gorge there's at least one person alive, and alive on purpose, and everything we're looking at is a trail of breadcrumbs leading to them. Or leading them to us. I haven't decided which, and I'd advise you not to decide either."

Yara was quiet a moment. Then he laughed — softly, that wrong-moment laugh of his, the one Sarel had learned to hear as a kind of prayer. "A message meant for someone specific," he said. "Country full of ghosts and we're the mail route."

"Something like that."

Below, Pim had the radio going, sweeping frequencies out of habit more than hope, and Odell was feeding the fire one stick at a time like a man paying out rope. Sarel looked upstream, where the gorge narrowed to a black slot and the first stars stood over the Escarpment like nails driven in. Somewhere up there, a survivor. Somewhere up there, the documents Yara had been sent to fetch — and Sarel knew exactly what was in that case, had known since Halvorsen slid the assignment across the desk with his soft voice and his softer eyes, and knew also what Sarel's own cut of it would buy: a clean ledger, a burned contract, a life handed back.

He had told himself the girl would be a stranger. A name on a deed, nothing more. But sitting above that silent camp, reading a message written in eleven vanished men, Sarel understood with a cold settling certainty that the person who'd arranged all this — the survivor, the intended reader, whoever waited upstream — was not cargo. Was not a parcel. Was someone who had looked at the syndicate's whole machine and answered it with a theodolite pointed downriver and eleven empty tents, and that kind of person did not stay a parcel for long.

The radio crackled below. Pim's voice, startled: "I've got something. Carrier wave — weak, intermittent. It's repeating."

Sarel and Yara looked at each other.

"Repeating what?" Yara called down.

Pim fiddled with the dial, and static breathed in and out of the speaker like something sleeping, and under the static, faint as a pulse, came a recorded voice, clipped and calm, saying coordinates. Their coordinates. The camp they were sitting in.

Sarel felt the horror turn over, slow and complete, into something worse. Not a message from the dead. A message *about* them — broadcast to anyone listening, marking their position the way the theodolite marked its bearing. Someone upstream knew they were here. Had known, perhaps, before they'd launched the boat.

"Kill it," Sarel said quietly. "Pim. Kill the radio."

But the voice went on, patient and precise, reading them into the dark.
