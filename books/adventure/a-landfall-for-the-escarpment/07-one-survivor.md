## 7. One Survivor

The cave announced itself before we saw it.

Pim heard it first — a thin, high whine on the radio that he swore wasn't interference. "It's got a pattern," he said, hunched over the set in the grey light of dawn, fingers working the dial like a man trying to soothe a cat. "Three bursts, pause, three bursts. Over and over."

"Distress code?" Odell asked from the fire, where he was performing his daily miracle of making river fish taste like punishment.

"Old survey pattern," Pim said. "Pre-war. Nobody uses it anymore except—" He stopped, and we all finished the thought together in the silence: except people who'd been out here long enough for their equipment to become a museum.

Sarel stood at the edge of camp with his binoculars up, studying the cliff face across the gorge. He'd been quiet since the silent camp — quieter than his usual quiet, which was already the kind you could store grain in. "There," he said. "Smoke stain. High up, under the overhang. Somebody's been keeping a fire small enough to hide."

We crossed the gorge by noon, Odell leading us up a goat track that he described as "a path" in the same tone other men describe natural disasters. The climb took two hours and cost me most of my dignity, which I had been carrying as ballast anyway. By the time we reached the ledge below the overhang, my hands were shaking — partly from the altitude, mostly from the knowledge of what we might find. After the slashed tents and the careful rows of instruments, after the burned cache and those deliberate claw marks in the birch bark, I had stopped believing we were going to find anyone alive. I had started preparing speeches for the dead.

The cave mouth was maybe four feet high, screened by a curtain of frozen seep that glittered like bad jewelry. And standing inside it, half in shadow, was a woman holding a surveyor's case in one arm and a broken ice axe in the other hand, pointed at us like the world's most exhausted spear.

She was a skeleton wearing a person's face. Cheeks gone to hollows, lips cracked, eyes sunk back into her skull and burning there like coals someone had banked for winter. Her parka hung off her the way laundry hangs off a line. She had been out here — I did the arithmetic against the expedition's last transmission and came up with a number that made my stomach fold — she had been out here alone for better than five weeks.

"Well," said Pim behind me, breathless from the climb, "she looks great."

"Pim," I said.

"Shutting up."

The woman didn't lower the axe. Her gaze went across each of us in turn — Sarel first, longest; then Odell; then Pim; then me last, and on me it stayed, because I was wearing the syndicate's gear, good wool and good boots bought on credit I hadn't earned, and people who have survived five weeks alone in a cave develop an eye for that sort of thing.

"Ilse Marchetti," I said. Not a question. We all knew.

Her voice, when it came, was a dry rustle, like wind through a dead cornfield. "You're the ones who've been following our camps."

"We've been following your camps, yes."

"Why."

Not *who are you*. Not *what do you want*. Why. Five weeks alone will strip a conversation down to its load-bearing questions. I respected it enormously and it terrified me completely, because I had prepared answers for every question except the honest one.

"We were sent to find you," I said.

"By whom."

"The Vellum Syndicate."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. The look of a woman whose worst suspicion had just reported for duty on schedule. The axe head came up an inch.

"And what does the Syndicate want found?" she rasped.

I looked at Sarel. Sarel looked at the view. Odell looked at his boots with the concentration of a man reading scripture in them. Pim looked at me with the open, horrified face of a young man watching an adult walk into a door he'd been warning about for days.

Here is the thing about lying to a starving woman on a mountain ledge: it's easy. It would have been the easiest thing I did all week. *We're a rescue party*, I could say. *Sent when your expedition missed its check-in.* Half true, which is the best kind of lie, the kind that comes pre-insulated. She couldn't verify anything. She was too weak to run. I could have the case off her by nightfall and be halfway downriver with my debt cancelled and my winter intact.

I had been telling myself versions of that story since the day Halvorsen slid the contract across his desk. *Recovery work*, I'd called it. *Document retrieval.* Words with soft edges. But standing there on the ledge, looking at what five weeks of guarding a metal box against the whole indifferent mountain had done to a human being, the soft words finally failed me. There are moments when self-deception runs out of fuel, and mine chose this one — cold, public, and at altitude.

"They want the case," I said.

Nobody moved. Somewhere below us a raven said something rude.

"The Marchetti survey carried original land documents," I went on, because once the dam cracks you may as well let the flood do its work. "A grant. Deed. Whatever it is, it's worth more than every claim the Syndicate has filed on this range, and they can't legalize what they've already sold until it's erased. My job was to find you, take the case, and bring it back downriver. That's why I'm here. That's why any of us is here." I paused. "They hold my debts. All of them. They bought them in the spring, and the price of walking out from under them was this trip."

Silence. The frozen seep curtain ticked and dripped behind her.

"You're confessing," she said at last, and there was something in the rustle of her voice that might, in a healthier throat, have been astonishment. "On a mountainside. To the person you were sent to rob."

"It seemed rude to lie to you on top of everything else."

"It is rude." She shifted her weight, and I saw how much the shift cost her — a small wobble, corrected by fury. "You came all this way to steal from a dead woman's family and now you're being *polite* about it."

"Actually," Pim offered from the back, "he came all this way to rescue you, probably, if you asked him directly, but he'd rather die than—"

"*Pim.*"

"—I'm just saying what everyone's thinking."

Ilse Marchetti looked at Pim. Then at Odell, who shrugged with the eloquence of a man who had known Yara Crane exactly four days and had already formed opinions. Then at Sarel, who still hadn't looked away from the view, and something flickered across her ruined face at that — a note taken, filed, not yet acted upon. Then back to me.

"Put down the axe," she said.

I blinked. "That's — that's yours."

"I know whose it is. Put yours down. All of you. Weapons on the stone." When none of us moved fast enough: "I have spent thirty-seven days deciding whether every sound outside this cave was a person coming to kill me. You have walked up my mountain in a straight line to my only door. If I am going to die of company, I would at least like it to be disarmed company."

So we disarmed. Sarel surrendered his knife with a smoothness that suggested he had another one, which he did, which she made him surrender too, which told me she'd spent thirty-seven days learning to read men the way Pim read radio bands. Odell gave up a hatchet and a hook knife and, after a pause, a second hatchet nobody had known about. Pim had nothing but a multitool and surrendered it with such relief you'd think it had been burning him.

Then Ilse Marchetti sat down. It wasn't graceful — it was a controlled collapse against the cave wall, the case pulled into her lap, arms wrapped around it, and I understood suddenly that the case wasn't luggage to her. It was the last organ of a body she refused to let die.

"Odell," I said quietly. "The fish."

He was already moving. He always was, that man; he communicated primarily through logistics.

* * *

We ate on the ledge in the weak afternoon light. Ilse ate the way starving people eat when they're trying to pretend they're not — slow, deliberate, trembling slightly between bites, as though her body kept forgetting food was real and had to be reminded. Odell had made broth first, without being asked, because broth is what you give a body that's forgotten how to digest, and I watched her take the first spoonful and go very still, and I watched her decide not to cry about it, and I decided right along with her.

"So," she said, when the bowl was empty. "Crane. That's your name? The Syndicate's man."

"Yara Crane."

"What did they promise you?"

"A clean ledger. My debts gone. Maybe a bonus if I delivered the documents intact." I turned my cup in my hands. "My father worked the flood gates on the Corren bend. Thirty years. When the levee failed in the spring flood — the one the Syndicate's dredging caused, though no court ever said so out loud — he stayed at his post. They gave my mother a form letter and a bill for the gate mechanism he broke holding it shut."

Ilse studied me for a long moment. "And you signed on with them anyway."

"They own everything I owe. It's hard to be principled at interest rates like theirs."

"It's not hard. It's expensive. There's a difference." She said it without heat, the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. Then, softer: "My grandfather surveyed this range when the territorial government still honored its own paper. The grant covers the Escarpment — the whole ridge, crown to river. The Syndicate's mining claims are built on filings that pretend the grant never existed. As long as the original is out here, unrecorded, unextinguished, every ton they pull off this mountain is theft with paperwork." Her hand settled flat on the case. "They haven't been hunting a document, Crane. They've been hunting the proof that they're criminals."

"And you've been sitting in a hole in the rock for five weeks keeping their evidence warm."

"Somebody had to. The rest of the team—" She stopped. Started again, in a flatter voice. "The rest of the team tried to carry the case out in September. Downriver, fast, before the Syndicate's scouts closed the gorges. I was the mapkeeper. I argued for the high route. They voted me down." Her jaw worked. "The last transmission was mine. From the ridge. *The escarpment is moving.* Everyone laughed at the phrasing. It wasn't a figure of speech. The thaw and their blasting have the whole slope unstable — you can hear it at night, a grinding, like the mountain chewing. The team went down into the gorge country and the Syndicate's men went in after them, and I took the case and went up, because up was the one direction nobody with a contract wanted to go."

"The claw marks," Sarel said. His first words since the ledge began. "At the birch stand. Too deliberate for animals."

"Sarel cut them," she said, nodding at him. "Our guide. Marking the route for whoever followed. He was supposed to come back for me with supplies." A beat. "That was six weeks ago."

Sarel's face did nothing at all, which was itself an event, and I felt the ground shift under the whole expedition in a way I couldn't yet name. Ilse saw it too. She was watching him with those banked-coal eyes, patient as geology, and whatever she was filing away, she filed it deep.

"So here's where we are," I said, mostly to keep myself from thinking about Sarel's face doing nothing. "The Syndicate thinks I'm bringing them the case. When I don't come back, they'll send more men — better armed, worse mannered. Winter's early. The river's going to lock up within the week. And the woman holding the deed to this entire range can barely stand."

"I can stand," Ilse said, and proved it, rising in one motion that cost her visibly and conceded nothing. "What I can't do is trust you. Yet. You confessed beautifully, Crane, but confession is cheap on a mountainside. So here's my offer." She held out the ice axe — her broken one, the weapon she'd guarded her life with for thirty-seven days. "You carry this. Not the case — the axe. If you're what you say, you'll want to be. And if you're not, I'll know soon enough, and I'll take it back along with everything else you own."

I took it. It weighed almost nothing and more than anything I'd carried all year.

"Where are we going?" Pim asked. "If — hypothetically — we were going somewhere."

"Over," Ilse said. She nodded east, at the wall of white peaks closing off the sky above the treeline. "Through the col and down the far side to the territorial court at Graywater. The grant has to be recorded by a living hand, witnessed, entered before the Syndicate's claims are finalized. There's a window. Three weeks, maybe less." She looked at the sky, at the light going thin and metallic over the ridge. "And the snow is coming early. I've watched these clouds for thirty-seven days. They aren't waiting for the season."

"That's a nine-day crossing in good weather," Odell said. "In autumn. With a starved woman."

"With a *surveyor*," Ilse corrected, and for the first time since we'd found her, something that was almost a smile touched the ruin of her face. "Gentlemen, I have mapped every pass on this range and dreamed about most of them. I know a way over that isn't on the Syndicate's maps, because it isn't on anyone's maps. My grandfather called it the Hanging Valley. He also called it 'the place that forgives nothing,' so pack accordingly."

Behind me, I heard Sarel murmur something to himself — too low to catch, too smooth to be innocent — and step away toward the packs, and I noticed that his hand went briefly to the inner pocket where he kept his papers, and I noticed that I noticed, and that noticing felt like the first cold drop of a storm still far upstream.

Above the treeline, the clouds had begun to arrive.
