## 8. What the Deed Says

The cave smelled of woodsmoke and old stone, and Ilse Marchetti had decided — somewhere around the third day of starving in it, or possibly the fourth; hunger made arithmetic unreliable — that if she was going to die, she would at least die having kept one thing true.

Then six men had come up the river gorge with a boat and a radio and a guide who walked like a man reading his own obituary, and the keeping-things-true had gotten considerably more complicated.

She sat now with her back against the cave wall, the metal case flat across her knees like a sleeping animal she'd rather not wake. Across the fire sat the one they called Crane — Yara Crane, though he hadn't introduced himself properly; Sarel Vance had done it for him, with a look on his face that Ilse had spent two days trying to decode. The others hovered near the entrance: the big quiet one who moved like furniture deciding to be helpful, and the boy with the radio, who kept glancing at her ration tin the way a dog looks at a table.

"You've been staring at me for an hour," she said.

"Twenty minutes," Crane said. "I counted."

"That's worse. That's methodical staring." She pulled the blanket higher. She was aware of how she must look — cheekbones like survey stakes, hands cracked from cold, three weeks of eating lichen and pretending it was cuisine. Starvation was not, she had learned, dignifying. It made you honest in the wrong ways: weepy over a burnt match, furious at a ptarmigan for existing where you couldn't catch it.

"We need to know what's in the case," Crane said.

"No."

"Ilse." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "Sarel told you why I came. Or I did. One of us did."

"He did. You confirmed it. You said—" she waved a hand, mimicking his flat river-guide cadence— "*I was sent to recover documents, not people.* Very romantic. You should have it engraved."

"It's the truth. I figured you'd had enough of the other kind."

That, annoyingly, landed. She looked at him across the fire — this lean, weather-scoured man with storm-chaser's eyes and a laugh she'd heard exactly once, aimed at nothing funny, which was how she knew it wasn't really a laugh. He had admitted, without flinching, that he'd come upriver as a thief. Most men would have dressed it up. She found she trusted him slightly more for not doing so, and resented finding that out.

"The case stays closed," she said, "until I decide otherwise."

"And when do you decide otherwise?"

"When I'm sure you won't do what you were paid to do."

He nodded slowly, accepting the terms, and she almost laughed herself — because here was the joke of it, the bitter little joke she'd been carrying since the camp went silent: *she* didn't know what was in the case either. Not all of it. Not until the night everything ended, when her father's last letter had finally made sense.

* * *

Her father had been a careful man, which was another way of saying a frightened one. Emil Marchetti read contracts the way other men read scripture — aloud, twice, looking for the verse that would damn him. Ilse had grown up watching him do it at the kitchen table, lips moving, pencil hovering, while outside their window the Escarpment stood in the evening light like a wall someone had built and then abandoned mid-argument with the sky.

"Read it again," she used to say, bored. "It says what it always says."

"A document never says what it always says," her father would reply. "It says what it says *today*. Today is different. There's a new signature. There's always a new signature."

She had thought it was just his way of being difficult. Then the survey commission came — the syndicate hiring Marchetti & Associates to map the upper Escarpment for "mineral assessment," paying triple rate, and her father taking the job with a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. He packed the metal case himself. He wouldn't let her touch it. When she asked what was inside, he said, "Our future," and then, quieter, "or theirs. Depends who reads it first."

They went upriver in September. Eleven of them. By the second week, the mountain had begun making noises it shouldn't — deep structural groans from the ridge above the old workings, rockfall where no rock had fallen in living memory. Her father stopped sleeping. He spent nights with the case open under lamplight, reading, and one evening he called her over and put the papers in her hands and said, "Now you read it. Aloud. I want to hear it in your voice, so I know it's real."

So she read it. And that is how Ilse Marchetti learned, at twenty-six years old, sitting in a tent that smelled of kerosene and fear, that she was not a surveyor's daughter.

She was the landlord.

* * *

The original land grant. Not a copy, not an extract — the document itself, vellum and wax and ink gone brown as old blood, signed before the territory had laws worth the name. Her great-great-grandmother's name on it. A hundred and forty thousand acres of the Escarpment, granted outright, the inheritance passing down through four generations of Marchettis who had somehow, each of them, failed to mention it to the next — or perhaps had mentioned it and been believed by no one, because who believes they own a mountain?

Her father had found the grant in a probate archive two years ago and had spent those two years being quietly terrified of it. Because a deed like that didn't make you rich. A deed like that made you a problem. The Vellum Syndicate's mining claims — every one of them, every shaft and tailings pond and blasted terrace — sat on land that belonged, by the oldest law on the books, to the Marchetti family. The claims weren't weak. They were void. And the only thing standing between the syndicate and legal certainty was a piece of vellum in a metal case, carried by eleven people into country where things had begun to go wrong.

"They didn't lose us," Ilse said aloud, later, to her father, in the dark. "They sent us up here to be lost."

Her father hadn't answered. He'd been dead by morning — the slope failure above the lower camp, the one the radio log would call weather. She had heard the last transmission go out, Pim's counterpart on their end shouting coordinates into static, and her father's final instruction pressed into her hand along with the case: *Don't let them read it. Don't let them burn it. Make them hear it.*

She had been making them hear it ever since, mostly by refusing to speak at all.

* * *

Now there was a fire, and food — actual food, beans with actual salt, courtesy of the big one, Odell, who cooked the way he did everything else, silently and competently — and Ilse sat watching Yara Crane watch her, and thought about the arithmetic of trust.

She had counted them, these rescuers-turned-captors-turned-something-else. Six came upriver. Five remained at the silent camp after whatever had happened there that Vance wouldn't discuss. Of the five: the boy, Pim, was transparent as glass and twice as fragile; Odell was loyal to Crane and indifferent to everyone else; Vance was a locked door with a draft coming through it; and Crane —

Crane was a debt walking around in a coat. She could see it on him the way you can see a load-bearing crack in a wall. Everything he did was priced. Every kindness had a ledger line behind it. And yet he had sat down across from her, a hired thief, and told her plainly what he was, and asked for nothing but the truth in return.

There was a version of events where she gave him the truth and he sold it. There was a version where he didn't. She had spent three weeks learning that most versions never happen, and the ones that do are chosen by whoever moves first.

"All right," she said.

Crane looked up.

"You want to know what's in the case. I'll tell you. But I'm going to tell all of you, because if it's going to get me killed, I want witnesses." She raised her voice slightly toward the cave mouth. "Pim. Odell. Your guide too, wherever he's skulking."

Vance materialized from the shadows with the unhurried air of a man who had been listening the whole time. Of course he had. Ilse filed that away.

She set the case on her knees. Her hands were steadier than she expected. Three weeks of hunger, and here was the moment her whole life had been bent around, and her hands were steady, because — she realized — this was the one thing she still knew how to do. Read a document aloud. Her father's voice in her mouth.

"This is the original land grant of the Escarpment Territorial Survey," she said. "Executed ninety-one years ago. Grantor, the territorial authority. Grantee—" and here she paused, because saying it aloud, in front of strangers, in front of a syndicate man, made it realer than it had ever been even to her— "Grantee, Adelheid Marchetti. My great-great-grandmother. All mineral rights, surface rights, water rights, and subsurface estate, in perpetuity, to her heirs and assigns."

Silence. Pim's mouth was open. Even Odell had stopped moving, which she suspected was his version of applause.

"The syndicate's claims," she continued, "all of them, sit inside these boundaries. Every one. They aren't contesting my family's title — they don't know it exists, or they didn't, or—" she looked at Crane— "they sent my father's team upriver to make sure it never got back down. Eleven people, Mr. Crane. To erase a piece of paper, they buried eleven."

"Not me," Pim said quickly, then went red. "I mean — I'm not — nobody told me anything."

"I know," Ilse said, and meant it kindly. "You're the radio boy. You're the reason anyone knows we existed at all."

Crane hadn't spoken. He was looking at the case the way a drowning man looks at a rope — no. No, that wasn't right. She watched him more closely. He was looking at it the way a man looks at a rope that's attached to something heavy. Something that might pull him under instead of up.

"How much do you owe them?" she asked.

His jaw worked. "Enough that recovering that case was supposed to clear it."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a cave listening to a dead woman's heir explain that I've been carrying the antidote in my pocket for a week." He laughed — that same wrong-place laugh. "They'll kill for it. You understand that. Halvorsen doesn't negotiate. He erases."

"Then we have something in common," Ilse said. "He tried to erase me too. It didn't take." She tapped the case. "This isn't cargo, Mr. Crane. This isn't documents. This is the claim itself. I'm not what you were sent to fetch. I'm what the fetch was for. Whoever holds this holds the mountain — and the syndicate has been standing on my property for thirty years, calling it theirs."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned low. She told them about the camps, the slope failures, the deliberate claw-marks she'd seen gouged into bark at eye height — too regular for animals, too patient for panic. Someone had been herding the expedition. Someone had wanted them alive long enough to abandon the case, and dead soon after. She told them about the weeks alone, the cave, the lichen, the ptarmigan she'd cursed with real creativity. Pim laughed at that — a startled, honking laugh he immediately apologized for, and she found she liked him enormously.

It was Vance who broke the warmth. He'd been quiet at the edge of the firelight, and when he spoke his voice was careful, the care itself a warning.

"If the deed names your family," he said, "why carry it into the field at all? Why not leave it in a bank vault in the lowlands?"

Ilse had wondered the same thing, once, angrily, in the worst weeks. But she'd had time to understand.

"Because a vault can be bought," she said. "A judge can be bought. A fire can be arranged. My father understood that the only safe place for a dangerous truth is inside a witness." She touched the case. "Paper can be stolen. I can't be — not without everyone knowing exactly what was taken and why. He made me the vault. He just didn't warn me the vault would have to walk out of these mountains on its own legs."

Vance held her gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then he nodded, slowly, and looked away, and Ilse thought: *there*. There was the draft through the locked door again. She didn't know what it meant yet. But she had grown up with a man who read documents for a living, and she had inherited more than land. She knew how to read the small print of a face.

Later, when the others slept, Crane stayed awake across the embers. She pretended to sleep and listened to him not-sleeping, and eventually he spoke into the dark.

"My father died in a flood the syndicate caused," he said. "Upstream diversion, spring melt, a valley full of people who'd signed nothing. I've spent my whole life chasing storms because at least storms are honest about wanting to hurt you." A pause. "I came upriver to steal from you. I want you to know that I know that."

"I know," Ilse said. "I heard you the first time."

"But you told us anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She considered the question against the darkness, against the weight of the case beside her, against the memory of her father's voice saying *make them hear it*.

"Because a claim is only worth what a witness will swear to," she said. "And I've decided you'll do."

Across the fire, she heard him exhale — not quite a laugh, not quite anything else. Above the cave mouth, through the crack of the entrance, the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the wind had changed, and it smelled — faintly, impossibly early — of snow.

Neither of them said so. But both of them lay awake a long time, listening to the mountain breathe.
