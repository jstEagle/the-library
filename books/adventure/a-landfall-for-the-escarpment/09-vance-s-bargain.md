## 9. Vance's Bargain

Sarel Vance had always believed that a man's worst decisions were made in the hour before dawn, when the fire burned low and the body was too tired to lie to itself and the mind, perversely, decided that was the moment for honesty.

So it was no surprise to him — not really — when he found himself awake at that hour, three nights out from the silent camp, easing the radio case out from under Pim's sleeping bag with the tenderness of a man lifting a sleeping child.

Pim slept like the dead. The boy could sleep through a rockslide, and had, on the second day, though Sarel suspected the rockslide had been more of a gravel trickle and Pim's snoring had done most of the damage. Odell slept like a man keeping one eye on the river even unconscious. Yara Crane slept badly, in short violent shifts, the way men sleep who have spent years listening for weather.

Which meant Sarel had perhaps forty minutes. He'd counted them out all evening, watching the fire, watching Yara's breathing change, doing the arithmetic he was so very good at.

* * *

Here is what nobody understood about Sarel Vance, and what he had long ago stopped trying to explain: he was not a coward. Cowards ran from danger. Sarel ran toward it, measured it, shook its hand, and asked it for references. He had guided parties through the Escarpment for nineteen years. He knew every goat trail, every deadfall, every stretch of river that looked calm and ate boats. People trusted him with their lives because he treated trust as a professional obligation, like rope maintenance or water discipline.

The trouble with being trustworthy your whole life, Sarel thought, feeding a twig to the coals, is that eventually you learn exactly how much a life weighs. And once you know the weight of things, you can't stop weighing.

He weighed Yara Crane. Old friend. Good man in the specific way that good men are dangerous — willing to burn himself down for a principle, and lately looking like a man shopping for matches. Yara would die for this girl. Sarel had watched him realize it over the cook fire two nights ago, watched the knowledge land on him like a dropped pack, and Yara hadn't even flinched. That was the terrifying part. Most men flinched.

He weighed Ilse Marchetti. Half-starved, sharp as a filleting knife, and carrying a metal case she hugged in her sleep like a child. She'd told them what was in it — the original grant, her name written in it, the whole Escarpment signed away to her family before the syndicate's grandfathers had learned to spell *mineral rights*. Sarel had listened with his face arranged into sympathy while inside him a number grew larger and larger. A deed like that wasn't paper. It was a lever long enough to move the Vellum Syndicate off the mountain, and anything that could move the syndicate could, in the right hands, move debts.

His debt. Fourteen hundred in scrip, compounding at a rate the syndicate's clerks had clearly invented during a fever dream, inherited from his brother Tomas's failed trading post along with Tomas's widow's disapproving letters. Fourteen hundred that had been eleven hundred five years ago and would be eighteen by spring if he did nothing.

Everyone had a number. That was the thing about the syndicate nobody said aloud: they didn't want your soul. Souls were bad collateral. They wanted a figure, clean and legible, and they wanted you to carry it the way a mule carries ore — patiently, forever, up the same hill.

Sarel looked across the dead fire at the shape of the girl, curled against the case, and weighed her too. Not coldly. He wanted that on the record, wherever records were kept. He liked her. She reminded him of his sister Ruth, who also refused to eat when she was frightened and got twice as stubborn when fed. If there had been any other way —

But there wasn't, and here was the tender heart of it, the part Sarel held onto like a handrail in the dark: he wasn't going to hurt anyone. He was going to *deliver*. There was a difference. Delivery was honest work. Men had made entire careers of delivery. The syndicate wanted the girl and the case; the girl would be fed, paid off, released somewhere south with a story and a settlement, because Halvorsen was many things but stupid wasn't one of them, and martyrs made terrible business partners. And the crew — Yara, Odell, Pim — would be standing right there when the syndicate men came, and whatever happened next would happen to all of them equally, which was more fairness than the mountain offered on a good day.

Besides. Sarel thought, and this thought came to him with the gentle certainty of a river finding its level. *Yara would do the same.* Not the radio part, maybe. Yara was too proud for the radio part. But if you put a ledger in front of Yara Crane and told him his freedom cost one delivery, one transaction, one unpleasantness distributed among strangers — Sarel had known the man twenty years. Yara would hate it. He'd hate it beautifully. He'd write a speech about it. And then he'd do it, because Yara's debts were bigger than Sarel's and Yara's imagination about his own goodness was the only thing bigger still.

Everybody would do the same. That was the whole architecture of the world. The syndicate hadn't built the arithmetic; it had just been clever enough to write it down.

* * *

The radio took four minutes to warm up. Sarel knew because he'd timed it weeks ago, back when timing it had been merely foresight instead of intention, and he crouched behind the gear cache with the set balanced on his knees, earpiece in, volume down to a murmur, and felt — he was surprised to find — calm.

Not guilty. He'd expected guilt, had budgeted for it, and its absence unsettled him more than its presence would have. What he felt instead was the particular lightness of a man who has finally stopped pretending he has options. For five days he had carried two futures, the loyal one and the solvent one, and the weight of holding both had been worse than either. Now he held one. His shoulders actually ached less.

The channel crackled. A voice he recognized — Rennick, the syndicate's relay man at Cold Harbour station, a dry bureaucratic voice that sounded like it filed its own teeth.

"Station. Identify."

"Vance," Sarel said quietly. "Guide, Crane party. We have the Marchetti woman and the case."

A pause long enough to hold an entire career.

"You're calling at three in the morning, Vance."

"I'm calling when my party sleeps," Sarel said. "I'd think a man in your line would appreciate operational discretion."

Another pause. Then something that might have been a laugh, filed down to almost nothing. "Halvorsen said you'd come in. He had money on it being the boatman."

"Odell doesn't know the frequency."

"He knows it now, probably. Go ahead."

Sarel closed his eyes. Behind his eyelids he saw the camp: Pim's boots sticking out of his bedroll at angles that defied anatomy, Odell's knife within reach even in sleep, Yara turned toward the girl's side of the fire like a compass needle, and the girl herself, Ruth's ghost in a borrowed coat, breathing slow with her arms around fourteen hundred acres of other people's greed.

"The woman's weak but walking," he said. "The case is intact. Crane thinks we're crossing at the Col because I told him the low route ices out early." He paused. "It does ice out early. But not yet. You have six days, maybe seven, before the snow makes the Col impassable for your people too. Come at us from the north fork. I'll deliver them tired, strung out, and grateful to see anyone."

"And your price."

And there it was. Sarel opened his eyes. Above the black teeth of the ridgeline the stars were out in their terrible thousands, indifferent and gorgeous, the same stars his father had named for him when he was six — *that one's the Ferryman, lad, carries souls across* — and Sarel found he couldn't look at the Ferryman just now.

"Not money," he said. "Money's a treadmill. I want the ledger entry. Vance, S., cancelled. Same date as Crane's. Both of us walk out owing nothing, and you get your paper."

"Crane's debt isn't mine to cancel."

"Then wake whoever owns it," Sarel said, and heard his own voice go flat and hard, a stranger's voice, a voice with a number in it. "Because here's the arithmetic, Rennick, and you can carry it up the chain verbatim: Crane won't sell the girl, and I can't hold him forever, and if your recovery team comes at us loud, someone dies, and dead guides don't deliver cases. My price is cheap. Funerals are expensive."

Silence on the channel. Wind moved through the pass above them with a sound like a long exhalation, like the mountain itself considering the offer.

"Both entries," Rennick said finally. "Cancelled on confirmed delivery. Halvorsen will want to meet you personally, Vance. He likes to meet the sensible ones."

"Tell him I'm flattered."

"Are you?"

Sarel looked at the fire, banked to embers, at the small humped shapes of the only people who still called him by his first name without a figure attached.

"No," he said. "But I'm reliable. It's nearly the same thing, and it pays better."

He broke the channel, killed the set, and sat in the dark for a while longer than he needed to, letting the valves cool, doing one last piece of arithmetic he hadn't planned on: the weight of a first name, versus the weight of a number. He'd assumed the numbers were heavier. Everyone assumed that. That was why the syndicate used numbers.

The trouble, he thought, zipping the radio case shut and sliding it back under Pim's snoring bulk, was that names kept accruing interest too. Quietly. Compounding. And there was no clerk anywhere who'd let you see that ledger until it came due.

* * *

He was back in his bedroll before the sky greyed, and he slept — this was the humiliating part — he slept wonderfully, deep and dreamless, better than he had in months. Solvency, even prospective solvency, was a sedative.

He woke to the smell of burnt oats and Pim's voice, high with outrage: "—the *porridge*, Odell, you don't stir porridge with a *trowel*, where did you even—"

"It was clean," Odell rumbled.

"It was *archaeological*—"

Sarel sat up, stretched, and performed the morning the way he'd performed every morning for nineteen years: checking the sky, checking the ropes, checking faces. Yara was already up, talking low with Ilse over a scrap of map, her finger tracing the Col, his head nodding. Trusting. The girl glanced up and caught Sarel watching, and gave him a nod — wary, but a nod. Progress. Two days ago she wouldn't meet his eyes.

"Sleep well?" Yara asked him, easy, friendly, twenty years of friendship folded into four words.

"Like a stone," Sarel said, which was true, and reached for the oats.

They broke camp in the blue half-light. The air had changed overnight — Sarel felt it before he could name it, a thin metallic edge under the smell of pine, the kind of air that came down off the high country ahead of schedule. He frowned at the peaks, at the odd bruised color sitting on the western summits like a thumbprint.

"Early for that," Odell said beside him, following his gaze.

"Early for a lot of things," Sarel said.

He meant the season. But as they shouldered packs and started up the valley toward the Col — six days, he'd promised Rennick, maybe seven — Sarel looked at the sky again and felt the first cold thread of doubt go through him, fine as wire. Because the mountain had been making its own arrangements all week, and mountains, unlike syndicates, did not negotiate, did not keep ledgers, and never, ever honored a bargain made on someone else's behalf.

By noon, the wind had turned. By mid-afternoon, Pim's radio — the boy chattering happily through his scheduled check-in — caught a fragment of weather traffic from the lowlands, and Sarel, walking point, heard the words *unseasonal* and *frontal system* and *early accumulation above four thousand meters*, and did the only arithmetic left to him.

Six days. Maybe fewer.

He walked faster, and prayed — sincerely, for the first time in years — that the snow would hold off just long enough for everyone to arrive at the Col alive.

Whatever happened after that, after all, was simply delivery.
