## 10. The First Snow

The snow came on a Tuesday, which felt like a personal insult. Storms, in Yara's experience, at least had the decency to arrive dramatically — a wall of cloud, a drop in pressure you could taste, the birds going quiet like an audience before the curtain. This one just started. One moment the river was running gray and sullen below their camp, the next the air was full of small dry feathers that hadn't been there a second ago, and Pim was standing in the middle of it with his face tipped up like a man being blessed.

"It's October," Odell said, in the tone of a man reading an indictment.

"It's snow," said Pim.

"It's *October*," Odell repeated, as though the month were a legal technicality that ought to protect them.

Yara stood at the water's edge and watched the first flakes land on the current and vanish, and felt something in his chest go quiet and heavy, the way a house goes quiet when someone you love has left it. He'd been telling himself the same story for six days now: *up the river, around the gorge, down to the low road, two weeks, maybe three, and then a warm office and a broker with soft hands and a contract that would make all of this somebody else's problem.* The river had been the whole plot. The river was the easy route, the forgiving route, the route where you could still pretend you were delivery men instead of fugitives.

He watched a skim of ice form along the slack water near the bank — not thin, not shy, but forming with the confidence of ice that had been waiting all year for its cue — and understood, with the particular clarity that only arrives when it's too late to be useful, that the river was done being a route.

"Well," he said. "There goes the easy way."

Nobody answered. Down the bank, Ilse Marchetti was already crouched, two fingers in the shallows, reading the water the way other people read letters. She didn't look up when she spoke.

"It'll lock tonight. The narrows first, then the bends. By morning you couldn't get a canoe through it with a hammer."

"You sound like you've done this before," Yara said.

"I grew up on this river." She stood, wiped her fingers on her coat, and looked at him with that flat gray steadiness she had, the look that made him feel like a man being audited. "Before it was anyone's to take away."

* * *

They spent the afternoon in the particular busy silence of people avoiding a conversation. Pim climbed the one tall spruce near camp with the radio and came down reporting nothing — no syndicate chatter, no weather band, nothing but the dry hiss of the upper atmosphere, which he insisted sounded *smug*. Odell checked the boat's seams twice, though the boat was a day from being a lawn ornament. Sarel sat by the fire sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, and Yara caught himself watching him a beat too long, and made himself stop.

The camp was the expedition's old cache site, or what remained of it — a lean-to, a stack of split wood someone had left with the optimism of spring, a rusted stove. Ilse had known it was here. Ilse knew where all of them were, every cache and camp and cave along the Escarpment, and she doled this knowledge out in small exact portions, like a woman paying out rope and watching to see who tied knots.

Yara put water on for coffee and did the arithmetic he'd been not-doing all day. Food for five, careful rations, ten days. The low road, on foot, over the range: six days if the weather held, nine if it didn't, and the weather had just made its position extremely clear. Waiting for the river to thaw — if it thawed — was a month, in a place with no food, with syndicate men somewhere behind them who did not require rivers to travel.

"Odell," he said. "If we wait, how long?"

Odell considered the river with the respect one fisherman gives another. "Ice'll be thick enough to walk by December. Thick enough to trust by January. If it's an honest winter." He spat, ceremonially. "That one's not going to be honest."

"And the low road?"

"Six days. Nine." Odell looked at him. "You already know the answer. You just want somebody else to say it first."

That was the trouble with Odell. He was a man of perhaps forty words a day and every one of them landed where it was aimed.

* * *

They gathered at the fire when the light went, five of them in a half circle, the metal case between them like a sixth member who didn't talk. Yara had built the fire up big, against every rule of concealment he owned, because some conversations deserve light.

"So," he said. "The river's closed."

"Observation of the year," said Sarel.

"I'm serious, Sarel."

"I can see that. You get a particular line in your forehead. Very grave. Very captain-of-the-ship." Sarel smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, and Yara thought: *there it is again, that flicker, that thing behind the face I keep deciding not to see.* He was getting tired of deciding not to see it.

"Here's the situation," Yara said. "We can't go down the river. We can't stay here — no food, and the syndicate doesn't stop for winter, they just buy better coats. That leaves the low road. Over the range, on foot, before the snow gets serious."

"It's already serious," said Pim.

"It's going to get *rude*," Yara said. "There's a difference. Serious snow you can walk in. Rude snow you die in. We want to be over the top before it gets rude."

"And if we don't make it?" Pim asked. He asked it plainly, without drama, which was somehow worse.

Yara looked at the fire. He thought about lying — about the warm easy confidence that had gotten him through a decade of chasing weather for money, the grin that made clients feel like the storm was a problem for professionals. But he'd spent the last two weeks learning what lying cost. He'd lied to himself about this whole trip being recovery work. He'd let Sarel lie by omission. He'd watched Ilse trust exactly no one, and understood that she was the only honest person present, and that her honesty had been purchased at a price he didn't want to imagine.

"If we don't make it," he said, "some of us don't make it. That's the truth. I'm not going to dress it up. The pass is real, the cold is real, and I can't promise the weather. I can promise I won't leave anybody behind who can still walk, and I won't pretend the odds are better than they are."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere up the valley, the river made a sound like a door closing — the ice shifting, settling into its long argument with the current.

Ilse spoke first. "I can lead the first day. There's a route the survey team used — a bench above the gorge, then the scree. It's steep but it's honest ground. No avalanche paths until the col."

"You've walked it in winter?"

"I've walked it in worse."

Odell grunted, which from Odell was a signature on a contract. "I've crossed worse ranges with worse company," he said, and looked pointedly at Sarel, and Yara couldn't tell if it was a joke, and he suspected Odell couldn't either.

They all looked at Sarel.

Sarel turned his knife over in his hands, once, twice. "You're asking me to vote on freezing to death versus starving to death," he said. "Fine. The range. But I want it understood —" and here his voice changed, went quiet and reasonable, which was how Sarel always sounded when he was about to say something that would keep Yara awake later — "that we're doing this because of a piece of paper. A girl and a piece of paper. I'd like someone to tell me, honestly, what that's worth."

Yara felt the question land in his chest like a stone in still water. Because the honest answer — the answer he'd have given a month ago, on the river, with his debt ledger folded in his jacket like a second spine — was *nothing, it's worth nothing, it's worth whatever the syndicate pays.* But a month was a long time. A month was a burned cache and claw marks too deliberate for animals and a woman in a cave who'd guarded a metal case with a broken survey tool and pure spite.

"It's worth what it says," Yara said. "It says the land belongs to somebody. That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing."

Sarel looked at him for a long moment, firelight moving on his face, and whatever was behind his eyes did its arithmetic and kept the total to itself.

"The range, then," Sarel said. "Unanimous?"

"Unanimous," said Pim, too fast, the way young people agree to things that frighten them.

"Unanimous," said Odell.

Ilse said nothing. She just reached over and put her hand flat on the metal case, the way you'd rest a hand on a horse's neck, and that was a yes too.

* * *

Yara took first watch, because he always did, and because he wanted the cold to himself. He sat with his back against the lean-to and watched the snow thicken over the river until the water disappeared altogether, and the valley became a place that had never had a river in it at all, and the world went soft and white and utterly indifferent.

He thought about the easy route the way you think about a person. All those years he'd chased storms for money, there had always been a river — a road, a truck, a plan B, some forgiving channel that would carry him out if things went wrong. He'd never once in his life made a decision that couldn't be undone. Every contract he'd signed had an exit clause, even the syndicate's, even that one — *especially* that one — because men like Halvorsen always leave themselves a door, and they let you believe the door is yours.

There was no door on the range. There was a bench above a gorge and a scree slope and a col, and weather that had already announced it was going to be rude, and five people who had just, for the first time in this whole sorry expedition, decided something *together* — not because a contract said so, not because debt said so, but because the alternatives were worse and they'd all said so out loud, around a fire, looking at each other's faces.

It was a small thing. It was, Yara thought, the first honest thing he'd been part of in years, and he'd had to walk two hundred miles upriver and lose a river to find it.

The snow kept falling. Behind him, in the lean-to, he heard Pim's breathing go slow, and Odell's, and Ilse's — hers quiet and even, the sleep of someone who'd earned it. Sarel's he couldn't hear at all, and told himself that meant nothing.

Toward dawn the snow stopped, and the clouds tore open, and the moon came out and lit the range to the west — the whole white shoulder of it, enormous, patient, glittering like something newly made. Yara looked at it a long time.

Then, far up the valley behind them — back the way they'd come, downriver, in the direction of everything they were running from — he saw a light. Small. Orange. Moving.

Not a campfire. A campfire doesn't travel.

He stood very still and counted, and by the time he'd reached four, there were three lights, moving in a line, following the river. Following *them*, patient as winter.

Yara went and crouched by the lean-to and put his hand on Ilse's shoulder, and her eyes opened all the way, instantly, no fog in them at all.

"Change of plans," he said. "We leave now."
