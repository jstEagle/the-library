## 11. Above the Treeline

The trees gave up before Pim did.

That was the thing about treeline that nobody warned you about — it wasn't a line at all. It was a surrender, gradual and grudging, the spruce getting smaller and more apologetic until one of them simply decided it had done enough for this world and stopped trying. After that there was nothing but krummholz, wind-bent and knee-high, and then not even that. Just rock, and sky, and the sound of your own breathing turned into a stranger's.

Pim had never been above ten thousand feet in his life. He was now, and his body had filed a formal complaint.

"Your lips are blue," Ilse said, falling into step beside him. She said it the way she said most things — flat, factual, like reading an instrument panel.

"They're fashionably blue."

"They're hypoxic."

"Fashionably *hypoxic*," Pim amended, and she made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter were run through a rock crusher first. Four days on the mountain had done something to all of them. It had sanded Ilse down to her essentials — competence, suspicion, and a dry humor that surfaced like fish in a winter lake, once, then gone. It had done worse things to Pim's legs, which currently felt like two lengths of frozen rope someone else was operating by remote control.

He shifted the radio pack higher on his shoulders. Twenty pounds of batteries and copper coil, because Yara had said *we'll want ears up high*, and because Pim had said yes before remembering he was the youngest person present and therefore contractually obligated to carry everything.

The party strung out along the ridge trail in the order they'd settled into without discussing it: Odell in front, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who'd spent forty years letting mountains tell him where to put his feet. Then Sarel, then Yara, then Pim, then Ilse, who nobody had assigned to the rear but who had taken it anyway, and whom nobody had argued with, for reasons everyone understood and no one said aloud. You didn't put the person carrying the metal case anywhere but where she could see everybody's hands.

The case rode against Ilse's chest in a sling she'd woven from tent cord. She slept with it. Once, half-asleep in the snow cave below the col, Pim had heard her talking to it — low, steady, in a language he didn't know — and had decided some things were better left as mysteries. A woman who whispered to a box full of paper was either mad or certain, and Pim had seen enough of her in the last week to bet heavily on certain.

"Radio check," Yara called back, not turning around. His voice carried strangely up here, flattened by wind, stripped of its usual easy warmth.

Pim thumbed the handset. "Five by five." Static, mostly. The set had been eating static since dawn, great mouthfuls of it, spitting back nothing but white noise and the occasional ghost of a voice from three hundred miles away, some freight dispatcher on the lowland line, calling numbers into the dark.

* * *

They made camp at midafternoon, because above the treeline you camped when the light told you to, not when your body did, and the light was already going the color of old pewter. Odell built a windbreak out of stacked slabs while Sarel got the stove going in the lee of a boulder, and Pim climbed twenty yards upslope with the antenna wire to find what the radio techs back home called *a good take* — clear sky to the south, no iron in the rock, no interference.

What he found instead, at 4:17 by his watch, was a voice.

It came through in fragments, the way voices do when they're bouncing off weather fronts and mountain faces and God knew what else. Not the freight dispatcher. Not the territorial weather net. This voice was clipped, bored, and using words Pim had learned to dread over the past year of his life: *confirm*, *grid*, *party of five*.

He sat down hard on a rock and pulled the headset tight over his ears.

"—say again, last was garbled—"

Static.

"—movement consistent with survey party, heading northeast along the—"

Static, long this time, and Pim found himself leaning into it physically, the way you lean into a door that's trying to close.

"—acknowledge, Vantage. Coordinates received. Maintain watch. Out."

Then nothing. Just the freight dispatcher again, far away, calling numbers into the dark for people who weren't listening anymore.

Pim took the headset off very slowly, the way you move when you've realized the floor is thinner than you thought. He looked down the slope at his four companions, small against the gray immensity of the ridge, huddled around the little blue flame of the stove. Odell's broad back. Sarel crouched at the burner, feeding it fuel. Yara gesturing at something, making a point, probably a joke — even from here you could tell when Yara was joking, because Sarel shook his head and Odell didn't react at all and Ilse stared at him with that expression that meant she'd understood the joke perfectly and disapproved of it on principle.

Five of them. *Party of five.* Someone down there had said *party of five* to somebody else, and somebody else had said *coordinates received.*

Pim counted the party twice, hoping he'd miscounted himself out of it somehow. No luck. Five.

He went back down the slope with the antenna wire coiled in his hands and his heart going like a trapped bird, and he spent the whole twenty yards deciding how to say it, and by the time he arrived he still hadn't decided, so he said it the worst possible way, which was:

"So. Don't panic."

* * *

They didn't panic. That was almost worse.

Yara took the news the way he took most bad news — with a grin that arrived a half-second too late and didn't reach his eyes. "Well," he said, "on the bright side, we're famous."

"This isn't funny," Pim said.

"I know it's not funny. I'm doing the thing where I laugh so I don't do the other thing." He held out his hand for the headset, and Pim gave it to him, and for a long moment the only sound was the stove hissing and the wind worrying the guy-lines of the tents.

Sarel didn't look up from the burner. That was the detail Pim would keep coming back to, later, in the dark, turning it over like a stone with something underneath it: Sarel didn't look up. He kept his eyes on the flame and fed it another measure of fuel, and his face did nothing at all, and maybe that was just Sarel being Sarel — the man had a face like a locked drawer — but Pim had known Sarel Vance since he was fourteen years old, since the man had taught him Morse code on a fishing boat using a busted flashlight, tapping letters against the hull while Odell pretended not to notice. And Sarel always looked up when there was trouble. It was a point of pride with him. *Eyes on the problem,* he used to say, *or the problem gets a free shot at your back.*

Now his eyes stayed on the flame.

"Could be anything," Odell said. He said it mildly, the way he said everything, but his hands had gone still on the windbreak stones. "Freight traffic bounces weird up here. Could be a hunting party. Could be the territorial survey checking on us."

"It said *Vantage*," Pim said. "And it said coordinates received. Somebody gave them coordinates, Odell. Of *us*. Moving. Northeast." He swallowed. "That's us. We're the northeast-moving party of five."

"Thank you, Pim," Yara said gently, "for that crystal-clear summary of our situation."

"You asked me not to panic. Panicking is loud. Summarizing is quiet."

Ilse had gone very still. She stood a little apart from the others, the case hugged to her chest, and Pim watched her do the same arithmetic everyone else was doing, watched her arrive at the answer, watched her refuse to say it out loud. Because the answer was ugly. The answer was: there were exactly five people on this ridge, and one of them was talking to the syndicate, and every one of them could see every other one of them right now, and none of them was holding a radio except Pim.

Unless.

Unless somebody had a second set. Unless somebody had been carrying more than they'd shown at the gear inspection in Odell's boathouse, six days and a lifetime ago.

"We should search the packs," Ilse said.

The words dropped into the camp like a stone into a well, and for a moment nobody moved, and Pim felt the cold come up through his boots in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"That's a hell of a suggestion," Sarel said quietly. Still not looking up.

"It's a practical one."

"It's an accusation is what it is."

"It's arithmetic," Ilse said. "Somebody is transmitting our position. There are five of us. I know I'm not doing it. So the field is four, and shrinking."

"And how do we know *you're* not doing it?" Sarel asked, and now he did look up, and his face was still doing nothing, which was somehow the loudest thing Pim had ever heard him not say. "You show up out of a hole in the ground with a box everybody's supposed to die for. Maybe the syndicate wants us chasing our tails. Maybe the girl with the deed is the bait."

The silence after that was the kind that has weight. Pim actually felt it press on his ears.

Yara stood up. He did it slowly, without the grin, and Pim realized he'd never seen Yara Crane stand up slowly before; the man operated at a permanent low simmer of motion, jokes and gestures and restless hands. Now he was very still, and very calm, and it was frightening in the way deep water is frightening.

"Sarel," Yara said. "Sit down."

"I'm sitting."

"Then keep doing it." Yara looked around the circle, at each of them, and when his gaze reached Pim it softened by a degree, and Pim hated how much that helped. "Here's what we're not going to do. We're not going to turn on each other at twelve thousand feet in November because a radio caught a scrap of chatter. Whoever's talking to the syndicate wants exactly this. They want us burning our fuel watching each other instead of watching the mountain. Halvorsen doesn't need to catch us. He just needs us to stop trusting each other, and the cold does the rest."

"And if he's right here?" Ilse said. "In the circle?"

Yara held her look for a long moment. "Then he's right here in the circle," he said, "and he's been sleeping ten feet from me for six nights, and he hasn't killed me yet, which means he needs me alive, which means I have time. We all have time. What we don't have is enough of it to waste on a trial tonight." He sat back down. The grin came back, smaller, wearier. "Besides. If anybody here is a syndicate plant, it's obviously Odell. Look at him. Man's built like a filing cabinet. Nobody's that reliable."

Odell, deadpan, without looking up from the windbreak: "I have considered a career in organized crime. The hours are better than boat work."

"*Thank* you," Pim said, weakly grateful, because the joke let air back into the circle, and everyone breathed a little, and even Ilse's shoulders came down maybe half an inch.

But Sarel smiled too, and Pim watched the smile, and thought: *that's wrong too.* Sarel's smiles came slow and crooked, earned. This one arrived fast and even, like it had been waiting in storage.

*Stop it,* Pim told himself. *You're tired and your brain is starving for oxygen and you're suspecting your oldest friend because he smiled at the wrong speed.* But the thought wouldn't leave. It curled up next to him inside his parka like a second, colder body, and it stayed there through dinner, through the watch rotation, through the long gray dusk settling over the ridge like ash.

* * *

Pim drew first watch, which suited him. He didn't want to sleep anyway. Sleep meant dreams, and lately his dreams had all featured the same garbled voice saying *coordinates received* in a bored tone, over and over, like a hymn sung by somebody who didn't believe in the church.

He sat with the radio on low volume, headset around his neck, and watched the stars come out in that brutal high-altitude way, all of them at once, as if someone had thrown a handful of nails across black velvet. Below and behind them, the valley they'd climbed out of was a river of darkness. Ahead, the pass they needed — the Col, Odell called it, in the tone other men reserved for ex-wives — was a notch of deeper black against the sky, still two days' climb away.

At midnight, the radio spoke.

Not the freight dispatcher. Not weather. The clipped, bored voice again, closer now, or stronger, or the atmosphere had simply decided to cooperate with somebody's plans. Pim jammed the headset on and caught it mid-sentence:

"—Vantage confirms visual at dawn. Hold position at the col. Do not engage above treeline, repeat, do not engage. Let the cold do the work. Acknowledge."

A pause. Then a second voice, answering, fainter, and Pim's blood went to ice water, because the second voice was using a callsign, and the callsign was a word, and the word was—

Static ate it. Swallowed it whole. One syllable, maybe two, gone into the noise floor like a coin into deep water.

Pim sat with his pencil hovering over his log book, hand shaking badly enough that the mark he made looked like a scratch from an animal. He'd had it. For one full second he'd had it, the name, the answer, the traitor's callsign, and the mountain had taken it back.

He ran the phonetics in his head, over and over, trying to reconstruct the shape of the lost syllable. Had it started with a vowel? With an S? Was it a name or a code word? Every reconstruction sounded like somebody different. *Sable. Anchor. Vance.* His own exhausted mind supplying the names it feared most.

Below him, four shapes slept in two tents, breath steaming, frost feathering the fabric. Any one of them could be Vantage. All of them might not be. He thought about waking Yara, and imagined the conversation — *I heard a word, half a word, maybe, possibly* — and knew exactly what Yara would say, kindly, reasonably, in that voice that made you feel cared for and dismissed in the same breath: *Get some sleep, Pim.*

So he didn't wake anyone. He wrote the fragment in his log in careful block capitals — *VANTAGE CONFIRMS VISUAL AT DAWN. HOLD AT COL. CALLSIGN: [ILLEGIBLE]* — and he zipped the log into the inner pocket of the radio pack, and he sat his watch, and he watched the col, and he listened.

Dawn came up the color of dishwater. Odell relieved him with a nod and a tin mug of something hot and terrible, and Pim crawled into the tent beside Yara and lay there shivering, not from the cold, running the half-word through his head one more time.

Above them, somewhere past the col, men were holding position. Waiting to let the cold do the work.

And one of the four people sleeping around him knew exactly where those men were — because one of them had told them.

Pim closed his eyes and made himself a promise, young and fierce and frightened: *before we reach the col, I'm going to hear that callsign again. Whole this time. And whoever it belongs to is going to wish the mountain had gotten to them first.*
