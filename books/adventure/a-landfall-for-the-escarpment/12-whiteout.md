## 12. Whiteout

The snow did not fall so much as arrive.

Ilse Marchetti had watched weather come in off the Escarpment her whole life — her father used to say you could smell a storm three valleys away if you paid your nose its wages — but this was different. This was the sky simply deciding to be somewhere else, and taking the mountain with it.

"Wind's backing around," she said to Yara, who was squinting at a horizon that had stopped existing about ten minutes ago. "Northwest to due west. That's not a squall line. That's a front parking itself on top of us."

Yara looked at her the way he always looked at her when she said something competent — a flicker of surprise, quickly buried under that crooked half-smile of his, as though admitting she knew things might cost him money. "How long?"

"An hour, maybe less before it's on us. We need walls."

They didn't have walls. They had a slope of broken scree above the treeline, a party of five moving single file along a ledge no wider than a kitchen table, and a sky turning the color of dishwater. Odell was ahead, breaking trail with his shoulders more than his legs, a habit from years of poling barges through ice. Sarel came behind him, then Pim, then Yara, and Ilse took the rear because someone had to watch their backs and because, privately, she still didn't entirely trust any of them not to walk away and leave her.

She'd been living with that arithmetic for six weeks. Alone in a cave with a metal case and half a jar of rendered fat, you learn exactly how much company is worth. The answer was: everything, and nothing, depending on the hour.

The first gust hit like a hand shoved flat against her chest. She staggered, caught herself on her ice axe, and heard Pim yelp somewhere ahead — a small, startled sound, quickly swallowed by wind.

"Pim!" Yara's voice, torn sideways.

"I'm fine! I'm — my radio —"

Then the whiteout closed like a book.

* * *

Here is what people don't understand about whiteouts, Ilse thought later, when there was time for thinking: it isn't darkness. Darkness would have been a mercy. A whiteout is light without information — every direction equally bright, equally blank, up indistinguishable from down, the horizon erased so completely that your own feet seem to float. Your eyes hunt for something, anything, and finding nothing, they begin to invent. She had seen grown surveyors weep in whiteouts. She had seen one man sit down in the snow and refuse to move, insisting with great politeness that the ground had been stolen.

Her father had trained her for them anyway. *You don't trust your eyes,* he'd said, crouched beside her in a storm on the north ridge when she was eleven, both of them roped together with fence wire because proper rope cost money. *You trust your instruments, your rope, and your counting. Eyes are liars. Everything else tells the truth.*

The wind screamed. Somewhere ahead, voices — Odell's low bellow, Yara shouting names. And somewhere, not far enough away, a sound that made her stomach drop: the scrape and clatter of loose rock going over the edge, followed by silence where a voice should have been.

"Pim." She said it out loud, to fix it in the world. "Pim's off the ledge."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and it happened because Ilse had spent six weeks alone doing nothing but think, and one of the things she had thought about, endlessly, uselessly, obsessively, was the survey team's protocols. Her father's protocols. Rope intervals. Sweep patterns. The boring, unglamorous choreography of looking for a person in bad terrain.

She caught Yara's sleeve as he lunged toward the edge — blindly, terribly, the way men lunged in whiteouts, ready to leap after a boy they couldn't even see.

"Don't," she shouted into the wind. "You'll go over too. Get everyone anchored. Now. Axe or knife, anything, into the snow, and hold the rope."

"The rope's with —"

"With Pim. I know. So we make rope." She was already uncoiling the cord from her pack frame — thirty feet of braided line she'd carried since the camp, since before, because her father had taught her that cord was the cheapest insurance ever sold. "Odell! Anchor here and pay me out. Sarel, second anchor, ten feet back. Yara—" she shoved the end of the line into his hands, and for once the crooked smile was nowhere on him, just a man's bare face, scared, "—you're my brake."

"And you?" he shouted.

"I'm going over the edge, obviously."

Later, she would remember the absurd clarity of those seconds. The wind went from roar to a kind of pulsing shriek, rhythmic, almost musical, and she thought, wildly, *it sounds like the generator at Camp Two, the way it used to cough before catching.* She remembered thinking that her boots were the wrong boots, that she'd traded her good ones in the valley below for food, and that her father would have had opinions about that. She remembered the exact moment her crampon-bitten heels went over the lip of the ledge and the world became vertical whiteness, and she let herself slide, feeding the cord through gloved hands, counting.

Counting mattered. That was the whole secret nobody believed until it saved their life. You counted the rope out in arm-lengths, you counted your breaths, you counted the sweep pattern left to right, right to left, each pass two paces wider than the last. A lost person in a whiteout was almost never far. They were close — horribly, stupidly close — curled behind a rock or wedged in a crevice or hanging by their fingernails twenty feet below the trail, screaming themselves hoarse into a wind that ate sound whole.

Four arm-lengths. Five. Her boots found slope instead of air. Six.

"PIM!" Nothing. She swept left. Two paces. Swept right. "PIM MARCHE— PIM!"

A sound. Faint, high, more animal than human, off to her right and below. She fixed on it the way her father had taught her to fix on a bearing — not with her ears, which the wind owned, but with her body, turning until the sound sat square in her chest.

Three more lengths. The cord snapped taut above her and she knew Yara had felt her weight change, knew he was paying it out inch by inch, and something in her — some cold little accountant she hadn't known she employed — noted that he was doing it correctly. No panic. No hauling. Trusting her count.

She found him in a slot between two slabs of rock, wedged like a dropped coin between sofa cushions, one arm bent wrong beneath him, his radio dangling from its strap and glowing its tiny stubborn green light into all that white. His goggles were gone. His eyes were wide and streaming and utterly blind, and when her glove touched his cheek he screamed.

"It's Ilse," she said, close against his ear, in the flat, unhurried voice her father had called the surveyor's voice — the one you used because panic was contagious and calm was, too, if you lied well enough. "You're found. You're done being lost. I need you to do four things and then we're finished. Can you count to four?"

He nodded, sobbing.

"One: don't move your left arm. It's broken, probably, and it's going to hurt, and that's fine, pain is just information. Two: I'm putting a sling on you and you're going to help me with your good hand. Three: when I say, you climb. Not before. Four—" and here she paused, because her father had always said the fourth thing should be true, "—four: you're going to be fine, and I don't lie about fieldwork."

* * *

Getting him up took forty minutes that felt like a season. The sling was a coil of cord and her spare shirt, tied off with the quick-release hitch she'd learned at fourteen and never once needed until now. The radio she clipped to her own chest, because a green light in a whiteout was a lighthouse and she wasn't leaving it behind. When they came over the lip — Ilse pushing, Yara hauling, Odell reeling the line with the steady patience of a man winching a skiff — the others made sounds she hadn't heard from them before. Small sounds. Relief doesn't cheer; it exhales.

They got below the treeline just as the storm reached its full voice. Sarel found a hollow under a leaning spruce, half a root cave, and they packed Pim into it with every scrap of insulation the party owned. His arm was broken, clean, mid-shaft. Ilse set it with Odell holding the boy's shoulders and Sarel looking anywhere else, splinted it with tent stakes and strapping tape, and gave him willow bark tea from her tin because morphine was a word from another country.

"You knew," Pim kept saying, glassy-eyed, staring at her. "You knew exactly — you counted, you said you were counting, why were you counting?"

"Because counting works." She tucked the blanket under his good shoulder. "Eyes lie in a whiteout. Numbers don't. Your father a numbers man?"

"He sold insurance."

"Well, there you are. It's in the blood."

That got a wet, cracked laugh out of him, and Ilse felt something shift in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet. It had been six weeks since anyone had laughed at anything she said. Six weeks since anyone had believed her at all.

* * *

Yara sat down beside her against the spruce trunk when Pim finally slept, exhausted into honesty. The storm hammered the branches overhead. Somewhere in the white world beyond, the mountain went on with its business of becoming winter.

"That was the most useful forty minutes I've ever spent terrified," he said.

"I've had better reviews."

"I mean it." He turned, and in the grey light his face was stripped of everything performative. "I hired these people with money I don't have. I bought their loyalty with promises. And the first time something actually went wrong, it wasn't my promises that went down that slope after him. It was you."

Ilse looked at her hands — raw, rope-burned, steady now only because she refused to let them be otherwise. "My father ran surveys on this range for twenty years. He taught me that competence is the only currency that holds its value in a storm. Everyone else's runs on trust, and trust freezes."

"That's a bleak ledger."

"It's an accurate one." She pulled her collar up. "Yara. In the morning, this storm won't be done. It'll have friends. We can't go back down — the river's icing, you know it as well as I do. And we can't stay here; there's no wood within a day's walk and Pim needs warmth or he loses more than an arm. There's a hanging valley two days east. Frozen falls, sheltered bowl, game trails. My father wintered a full survey crew there in '09."

"You know this how?"

"Because I've been memorizing maps since I could read, and because for six weeks in a cave, maps were the only conversation available to me." She met his eyes. "I'm telling you the route. Not asking."

Something moved across his face — pride wrestling with the habit of command, and losing, and looking strangely relieved to lose. "Then tomorrow you lead."

"Tomorrow I lead," she agreed. Then, because fairness demanded it: "And you carry Pim's pack."

"Obviously," said Yara Crane, and for the first time since she'd met him, Ilse believed him completely.

She slept in shifts after that, one ear open, and near dawn she surfaced briefly to see Odell adding wood to a fire he'd somehow coaxed alive in the root hollow, and Sarel sitting apart, awake, watching the storm with an expression she was too tired to read.

In the morning the whiteout broke just long enough to show them the eastern ridgeline — and, faint and unmistakable on the wind, a sound none of them wanted to hear: engines. Below them. Behind them. Coming up the river valley they'd abandoned.

Sarel was the first to start packing.
