## 14. Ambush at the Col

Odell had a theory about mountains, which was that they were rivers that had stopped pretending. Water found the low way and kept going; rock found the high way and stayed put, sulking for ten thousand years until the wind talked it down. Either way, everything moved, and everything eventually arrived somewhere it hadn't meant to be.

He'd been thinking this, in his slow way, for most of the morning as they climbed toward the col — the saddle of bare stone between two shoulders of the range where the maps promised a crossing and the weather promised nothing at all. He thought it partly because it was true and partly because thinking about rivers kept him from thinking about the fact that he had not slept properly in four days, that his boots were held together with wire and prayer, and that Pim had eaten the last of the honey sticks without asking, which Odell had decided to forgive but never forget.

"You're doing your river face," Pim said, falling into step beside him.

"I don't have a river face."

"You do. It's the one you make when you're deciding whether to say something. You almost never say it, so it just sits there like a heron."

Odell considered this. "Heron's a good face," he said. "Patient. Catches fish."

"Sometimes I think if I fell off a cliff you'd wait a full minute before saying anything."

"Fifty seconds," Odell said. "I'm not made of stone."

Pim laughed, a bright cracked sound in the thin air, and Odell felt the old warmth in his chest that he'd been carrying since the day he'd pulled the boy out of a flooded boathouse on the Vell, half-drowned and apologizing for the inconvenience. He'd been telling himself all trip that Pim was Yara's responsibility, that the crew was Yara's problem, that Odell was only here because Sarel Vance had asked him with that particular look Sarel had when he wanted something and didn't want to owe anyone for wanting it.

He'd been telling himself a lot of things. That was what a man did on a mountain. He told himself stories to keep his feet moving, and the mountain listened politely and waited to see which ones would hold weight.

Ahead of them, Ilse Marchetti moved like she'd been poured onto the slope from a surveyor's flask — steady, measured, no wasted motion. She carried the metal case strapped across her back the way other people carried children: close, protective, unthinking. Yara walked behind her, always behind her now, which Odell had noticed and said nothing about, because some things were better left as herons.

Sarel brought up the rear. That had been Sarel's own suggestion — *I'll take sweep, I know how to read backtrack* — and Odell had agreed without thinking twice, because Sarel Vance had been reading backtrack since before Odell could swim, and trust, once given to a man over thirty years of rivers and rope and bad winters, was not the kind of thing you rechecked every morning like a bootlace.

He would think about that later. There would be a great deal of later, and then suddenly none at all, and he would spend the last of it thinking exactly this.

* * *

The col announced itself first by wind. It came through the saddle like water through a notch in a weir, flattening the snow crust into long gray banners, and they crouched behind a rib of granite to breathe and eat cold oats and let the wind decide whether it was done shouting.

"We cross in one push," Yara said, mouth close to their huddle. "No stopping in the open. Ilse says there's shelter in the lee scree on the far side — an old survey hut, maybe still standing."

"If it's standing," Pim said, "it'll be full of marmots."

"Marmots are honest," Ilse said. "They announce themselves. It's people who hide."

Odell looked at her when she said that. She wasn't looking at anyone in particular, but her eyes went, briefly, down-slope — the direction they'd come from — and something in her jaw set the way it did when she was counting something she didn't like the total of.

He looked down-slope too. Nothing. Gray rock, gray sky, the timberline far below them stitched out in dark thread. A ptarmigan exploded out of a drift near his boot and scared him worse than anything had in years, and Pim laughed so hard he had to sit down, and for a moment the whole thing was funny — the five of them, the ridiculous mountain, the honey stick grudge, the world being ordinary.

That was the moment Odell heard the click.

Not a sound, exactly. More the absence of one. Thirty years on rivers teaches you to hear the gap before the drop — the flat silence where the current should be talking. The wind here had a rhythm, gust and lull, gust and lull, and in the last lull there had been a small hard sound from the rocks above the saddle, like a boot shifting on shale, or like metal finding metal.

"Gust coming," Odell said quietly. "Hold."

They held. The wind came through and took their breath and rattled the frost off the stones, and under it — under it, faint as a coin dropped in another room — Odell heard voices. Two of them. Maybe three. Downwind, up-slope, in the rocks.

He didn't look up. Looking up is a thing eyes do, and eyes get seen. He watched Yara instead, and saw Yara's face go through the same arithmetic his own was doing: *how many, how close, how fast can five people move across two hundred yards of open saddle with men in the high rocks.*

The answer was: not fast enough. Not even close.

"Sarel," Yara breathed. "Backtrack. Quietly. We go down and around—"

"There's no around," Ilse said. "I've read this ridge. The col is the only crossing inside three days, and we don't have three days of food."

"Then we wait for dark—"

"They won't wait for dark." Her voice was flat and certain. "They've been herding us for two days. This is where they stop herding."

Nobody argued. That was the worst part, Odell thought later. Nobody argued, because everyone had already known, in the place where you keep the things you don't say, that the easy route had closed behind them days ago and every step since had been borrowed.

Yara turned to him. "Odell. You've got the best eye. How many?"

Odell let his gaze travel the way a man lets his gaze travel when he's admiring scenery. Above the saddle, a jumble of boulders made a natural blind on the eastern shoulder. The snow there was wrong — trampled in a crescent, and one dark shape that might have been a pack frame, might have been a man sitting very still. Further along, a glint that was either ice or glass. Glass, he decided. Ice doesn't catch light twice in the same spot.

"Four," he said. "Maybe five. Rifle on the east shoulder, glass further right. They're waiting for us to be halfway across. Open ground, no cover, wind at our backs so we don't hear them coming."

"And the west side?"

"Same trick, probably. They want the middle of the saddle. Kill box." He said it the way he'd name a rapid. Just information. The river doesn't care what you feel about it.

Pim had gone white. "So what do we do?"

What they did, Odell decided in the next ten seconds, was what a boatman does when the drop turns out bigger than it looked from upstream. You don't go back — backwater is its own kind of drowning. You pick your line, you commit, and you give the river something else to look at while the important cargo goes through.

He'd been carrying the least important cargo for thirty years. It was finally useful.

* * *

"I'm going to stand up," Odell said.

Three faces turned to him. Ilse's narrowed immediately — she was the fastest thinker among them and he could see her getting there already, arriving at his idea before he finished saying it, and disliking it on arrival.

"They're watching for movement in the group," he went on, calm as a depth sounding. "One man standing up in the rocks, that's nothing. That's a man stretching. But a standing man draws the eye, and the rifleman will track him, because riflemen track what moves alone. And while he tracks me—" he nodded at the saddle "—the rest of you go low and fast along the north edge, behind that fallen slab. It'll hold for ninety seconds, maybe less."

"No," Yara said.

"It's not a question, Crane. It's a briefing."

"Odell—"

"You've got a girl with a paper worth more than this whole mountain, and a boy who can work a radio, and yourself, and a guide who knows the descent." He said Sarel's role easily, warmly, the way you hand a man his due. "And you've got me, who's slow on the downhill anyway and always has been. Ask Pim. I walk like a moor."

"That's not—" Pim's voice cracked clean in half. "That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." Odell dug in the side pocket of his coat and produced, with some ceremony, his last honey stick, and pressed it into Pim's mittened hand. "For the record. I knew you took mine Tuesday. This settles us."

Pim stared at the honey stick like it was a live coal. His eyes were doing something terrible and young, and Odell looked away from them, because a man needed his eyes dry and working for what came next.

"Odell." Yara had his arm now, gripping hard. "There's another way. We wait them out, we—"

"The food runs out tomorrow. The girl said so. And those men up there have a stove and a kettle, I guarantee it, syndicate boys never climb without a kettle." He smiled, and found, to his mild surprise, that the smile was real. "Crane. I've spent my whole life reading water. This is just a rapid with a bad line. Somebody takes the bad line so the boat makes it through. That's the job. Always was the job."

Ilse spoke, quiet and fierce. "You don't have to be the one."

"Somebody does, and I'm the heaviest and the slowest and the least needed, which makes me the cheapest. That's not heroics, Marchetti, that's accounting." He shrugged his pack off and set it down, and took from it the one thing he'd carried since the Vell — a battered brass river gauge, dented, useless, beautiful, the first tool he'd ever owned. He put it in Yara's hands. "You'll want to know how deep things are. You were always bad at that."

Yara's jaw worked. Thirty years of friendship stood in the space between them wearing a hundred faces — the flooded spring they'd run together against all sense, the winter Sarel broke his leg and the two of them hauled him forty miles of ice, the thousand evenings of cards and cheap whiskey and comfortable silence. None of it fit into words. Odell had never believed words fit anything, which was why he saved them.

"When it's done," Odell said, "you'll want to know who called them here. Because somebody did, Crane. Four men don't sit on a col in a white wind for two days on spec. Somebody gave them our line and our pace." He let that settle, gentle as silt. "Think on who's known our route every single day. Think on who suggested taking sweep."

Sarel, ten feet away, had gone very still, the stillness of a man standing in a river feeling the bottom start to move.

"Don't," Sarel said. Just that. *Don't.* Not a denial. Odell noted that the way he noted everything: without hurry, without mercy.

"Ninety seconds," Odell said to Yara. "Count them. Go on my third step."

* * *

Afterward — in whatever afterward there was, in the place where the river puts the things it takes — Odell would remember it in pieces, the way rapids come back to survivors: not as a whole but as a series of bright, separate instants.

The wind rising on cue, as if the mountain had agreed to the plan.

His first step, casual, a man stretching his legs among the rocks. His second. The rifleman's scope finding him — he felt it, actually felt it, a cold point between the shoulder blades, the way you feel a heron watching from a piling.

His third step, and behind him the soft scramble of four people going low and fast along the north edge, Pim's breath hitching, Ilse's case clinking once against stone.

The shot came early. Riflemen always fire early; the river taught him that too, that everything in nature jumps the drop. It took him high in the chest, on the left side, and the sound arrived a half-second after the blow, absurdly late, like thunder after the lightning's already burned the tree.

He sat down. Not fell — sat, deliberately, the way you sit on a bank after a long pull, and the rocks were kind enough to be there. The second shot spanged off stone a foot from his head, and he understood dimly that they were shooting again because he was still moving, twitching, trying to do something with his hands, and he made himself stop, made himself be still, because still men draw fewer bullets and every bullet aimed at him was one not aimed at the slab where his people ran.

*Good,* he thought. *Keep looking at me. I'm the interesting one.*

Through the ringing in his ears he heard shouting up-slope — syndicate voices, angry, confused, someone yelling about the north edge, about *they're moving, they're moving, goddamn it* — and then a scatter of shots chasing along the slab, and then, blessedly, nothing. Silence, except the wind, except his own blood making a sound like a small stream under ice.

Footsteps crunched near him. A syndicate man leaned over him, young, frightened, wind-burned, holding his rifle like it might bite him. Behind him, others were scrambling down toward the saddle's north edge, cursing the lost quarry.

The young one looked at Odell and Odell looked at the young one, and for a moment neither of them did anything at all, and it was almost polite.

"Where'd they go?" the young man demanded.

Odell smiled. It cost him more than the bullet had. "Downstream," he said. "Everything goes downstream, son. You just built your camp on the wrong bank."

The young man's face did something complicated — fear, and under the fear, the first hairline crack of doubt, the sound a ledger makes when a page tears. Then he was shouted at from up-slope and went running, and Odell was alone with the wind.

He found he could still move his right arm. He used it to drag himself, inch by patient inch, behind the rib of granite, out of the wind, because a man ought to die out of the wind if he can manage it. It took a long time. He had time. That was the one thing the mountain had given him plenty of.

He thought about rivers. He thought about the Vell in flood season, brown and muscular and utterly indifferent, and how he'd loved it anyway, loved it the way you love anything that tells you the truth. He thought about Pim eating the honey stick, and hoped the boy would eat this one slowly, make it last, learn something from it about savoring. He thought about Yara's face when he'd handed over the gauge, and hoped Yara would be angry — anger keeps a man walking — and hoped, too, that someday, on some river, Yara would hear a heron and think of him and laugh at the wrong moment, the way Odell always had.

And he thought about Sarel Vance, and the stillness, and the word *don't*, and the shape of thirty years rearranging itself around that single syllable like water finding a new channel. He wasn't surprised. That was the terrible thing, the thing he'd carry to the very last: he wasn't surprised. Somewhere in him he'd always known that Sarel's survival arithmetic had a column for friends, and that the column was short, and that the entries were erasable.

Rage came, at the end. Real rage, hot and clean, the best thing he'd felt all day. But it had nowhere to go — the men who shot him were hired hands, weather, symptoms. The disease was elsewhere, wearing a familiar face, walking away down a mountain with his friends.

So he spent the rage the only way left. He rolled onto his side, fumbled with numb fingers until he found the little tin whistle he'd carried since he was a boy — boatman's superstition, a whistle means *man overboard*, means *someone is in the water, come running* — and he put it to his lips.

If any of them looked back — if Yara, from the far side of the saddle, turned at the sound — he wanted the message to be unmistakable.

He blew three long blasts. Man overboard. Come running.

Then, with the last of the air in him, and the last of the strength, he blew it again — three more, weaker, ragged, but clear enough, clear as a bell over water:

*It was Sarel. It was Sarel. It was Sarel.*

The wind took it east across the col, over the heads of the syndicate men who barely glanced up, down the north slope where four figures crouched behind a fallen slab with the far side of the mountain in their eyes.

Whether they heard it, Odell never learned. The river had him now. Everything goes downstream.

But on the far side of the col, Yara Crane — counting ninety seconds, gauge in his fist, grief arriving ahead of understanding — stopped dead in the snow, turned back toward the sound, and went pale as the ice around him.

Because he'd counted. And the count had ended long ago.

And Odell was still blowing.
