## 15. The Descent

The mountain let us go the way a creditor releases a debtor — reluctantly, and with an eye on what we might still be worth.

We came down through timberline in the grey hour after the ambush, when the light hadn't decided whether to be morning or just a longer version of night. Odell was two hours behind us under a cairn of stones that Pim had stacked with shaking hands, and none of us had said his name since, because saying it felt like spending something we couldn't afford. Ilse led. She moved through the krummholz — those twisted, wind-bent trees that grow sideways out of spite — like a woman reading a familiar book in the dark, and the rest of us followed her spine and her silences.

Yara Crane, storm chaser, debt collector's errand boy, walked at the rear and told himself he was guarding the flank. What he was actually doing was watching Sarel Vance.

* * *

Sarel had been my friend for nineteen years. I want that on the record, even if the record is only this: nineteen years. He'd taught me to read weather off the backs of my own hands when we were both too young to know that weather could also read you. He'd pulled me out of a flash flood on the Miren Cut with one arm and a rope he'd tied wrong, and we'd laughed about the wrongness of the knot for a decade afterward, the way you laugh about the things that nearly killed you because the alternative is weeping at dinner parties.

And two nights ago, on a ridge above the hanging valley, I had watched him key a radio in the dark and speak four words I wasn't meant to hear: *they're at the col.*

He hadn't known I was awake. He hadn't known that Pim's radio, tuned low, would echo his transmission back in fragments like a ghost learning to talk. He hadn't known anything except what he'd always known, which is that Sarel Vance does the arithmetic and then lives inside the answer.

So now I walked behind him down through the timberline, carrying a rifle I didn't want to use and a question I couldn't put down.

* * *

The forest came up around us by degrees. First the krummholz, waist-high and stubborn. Then stunted spruce, leaning away from the prevailing wind like a crowd flinching. Then real trees, dark and tall, and with the trees came shadow, and with the shadow came the feeling — which had been with us since the silent camp, since the slashed tents and the instruments laid out in careful rows like an accusation — that the woods were not empty.

Pim kept close to Ilse. The boy had changed since the whiteout, since Ilse had dug him out of a snow coffin with survey-team patience and saved his life with a tent pole and a thermos of tea. He watched her now the way sailors watch a lighthouse: not because they love it, but because they have decided it is the only fixed thing.

"Water," Ilse said, stopping. "There's a creek below. We drink, we fill everything, we keep moving. The syndicate men who hit the col will have called ahead. There will be someone on the river trail."

"You know this country," Sarel said. It wasn't a question. It was the tone of a man confirming figures in a ledger.

"I grew up in it," Ilse said. "Before it was worth killing for."

"Everything's worth killing for," Sarel said mildly. "It's just a question of the price."

I felt that sentence go into me like a splinter working toward the bone. I don't think anyone else caught it. Pim was staring at his boots. Ilse was already moving downhill toward the sound of water. But Sarel said it looking straight ahead, at the middle distance, at nothing — and I understood, with the particular clarity that grief and cold can give a man, that he was talking to me. That maybe he'd been talking to me for days, in the only language he had left, which was arithmetic.

* * *

We drank at the creek. The water was snowmelt and tasted of stone. Ilse filled the bottles, Pim filled the skins, and I stood apart, doing the thing I'd been putting off, which was deciding.

Here is what I knew: Sarel had sold our coordinates to the syndicate. Here is what I also knew: Odell was dead because of it, a bullet through the chest at the col, and his last word had been Sarel's name, spoken not in anger but in something worse — in confirmation. Odell had suspected. Odell had died certain.

Here is what I did not know: how to kill a man who had once tied a bad knot to save my life.

I looked across the creek at Sarel. He was crouched, drinking from cupped hands, and the grey light caught the grey in his beard, and for a moment he looked exactly like the man who had laughed with me on the Miren Cut, and for another moment he looked like a stranger wearing that man's coat, and the terrible thing — the thing I'll carry longer than the cold or the debt or any of it — is that both moments were true. They were both the same man. That's the arithmetic nobody warns you about. People don't split into good and bad. They add up.

"Sarel," I said. "Walk with me."

* * *

We went upstream along the creek, away from the others, far enough that the water-noise would cover us and the trees would screen us. I had the rifle slung. He had his knife on his belt and his hands empty, and I noticed — I'll always notice — that he kept his hands empty on purpose. A man who wants to look harmless. Or a man who genuinely is, at the last, too tired to be anything else.

"You heard the radio," he said. Not *what radio.* Not *I don't know what you mean.* Nineteen years, and he skipped the whole theater of it, went straight to the confession like a man skipping stones.

"I heard it."

"The col was the best ground for them. Open sightlines, one approach. If it had to happen, better there than in the trees, where they'd shoot Pim by accident and the girl in the back of the head and dump you all in a crevasse." He said it flatly, the way you'd read a supply list. "I did the math, Yara. I've been doing it since the silent camp. Somebody was going to deliver that girl to the syndicate. The only question was whether it happened on my terms, where some of us walked away, or on theirs, where none of us do."

"Some of us," I said. "Odell didn't walk away."

Something moved behind his eyes then. Not guilt — I'd have recognized guilt, I've seen it in mirrors. It was more like a man checking a sum and finding it correct and hating the correctness. "No," he said. "Odell didn't."

"You knew he suspected you."

"I knew he watched me. Odell watched everybody. It was his whole personality, God rest him." Sarel sat down on a fallen log, suddenly, like a man whose legs had filed a complaint. "Sit down, Yara. If you're going to shoot me, sit down first. It's undignified otherwise."

I didn't sit. I also didn't shoot him, which he noted, and which I noted him noting, and the whole exchange had the awful intimacy of two men who know each other's tells.

"Explain the math to me," I said. "Walk me through it. Because from where I'm standing, the math has a dead boatman in it, and a boy who can't sleep, and a woman who's spent a month starving in a cave guarding her own inheritance, and I'd like to see where any of that balances."

"Here's the ledger," Sarel said, and his voice didn't change at all, and that was the worst part. "The syndicate has forty men on this river and a broker named Halvorsen who has never once in his career left a loose end. We are five. We are out of food in two days. Winter came early and the river is ice and the pass behind us is watched. Every path from here ends at the syndicate except one, and that one ends at a court in the lowlands that the syndicate owns half the judges of." He spread his hands. "I looked at all of it and I asked: what does Sarel Vance control? And the answer was: almost nothing. Almost nothing, except the coordinates. So I sold the only thing I had. And I negotiated. I told them: the girl and the case, delivered alive, in exchange for my debt and Yara's. Both ledgers burned. That was the price. I got them to agree to two debts, not one."

I stared at him. "You sold me out to save me."

"I sold you out to *maybe* save you," he said. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and it's also nothing, because maybe is the best number anyone's holding on this whole mountain." He looked up at me then, and his eyes were wet, and he let them be wet, which from Sarel was a kind of nakedness worse than any confession. "You'd have done the arithmetic, Yara. You'd have hated it, and you'd have done it. You signed your winter away to the Vellum Syndicate with a laugh. I watched you do it. You laugh when you're most afraid — you've done it since the Miren Cut. The only difference between us is that I stopped laughing first."

The creek went on talking its cold talk. Somewhere behind us, downhill, Pim laughed at something Ilse said — a small, startled sound, a boy remembering he was young — and it went through me like a blade through wet canvas.

"I'm not going to kill you," I said.

Sarel closed his eyes. "I know," he said. "I've known since the col. You carry that rifle like a man carrying a piano. You'd break your back before you'd play it."

"That's not mercy," I said. "I want that understood. This isn't mercy. Mercy is a gift. This is — " I searched for it, and found it, and it was ugly and true. "This is me refusing to let you make me into the kind of man who'd do the arithmetic on a friend. You don't get to cost me that too. You've cost me Odell. You don't get my whole self."

"I understand," Sarel said quietly.

"So here's what happens. You walk. Downstream, alone, whatever direction isn't ours. If the syndicate finds you, that's between you and them and whatever deal you made — and I hope to God they honor it, Sarel, I mean that, I hope they burn your ledger like they promised, because if they don't, then you sold Odell for a number in a book that was already a lie." I unslung the rifle and held it a moment, feeling its weight, its stupid honest weight, and then I set it against the log beside him. "Take this. You'll live longer."

He looked at the rifle like it might be a trick. "Yara —"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't thank me. Don't explain. Don't do the arithmetic out loud about how this proves you were right. Just go. And Sarel —" and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized, cracked down the middle like river ice, "— the knot on the Miren Cut was tied wrong. I've known for years. I never told you because you were so proud of it. I want you to know I knew. I want you to carry that down the mountain with everything else."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he laughed — one short, broken sound, the laugh of a man who has just been handed, in the same instant, his life and his sentence — and he picked up the rifle, and he stood, and he was Sarel Vance again, all of him at once, the friend and the traitor, the rescuer and the cost.

"The girl," he said. "They'll take her at the river trail. Two scouts, not more — Halvorsen's stretched thin since the col went bad. They won't kill her; the case is worth more with her testimony. They'll hold her at the forward camp." He paused. "That's not arithmetic. That's the last thing I'll ever give you for free."

And he went. Down through the trees, unhurried, a grey man in a grey forest, getting smaller, and then a shape, and then a suggestion, and then only the creek and the wind and me standing alone with a hole in my chest shaped exactly like nineteen years.

* * *

I washed my face in the creek because it was that or fall down. The water was so cold it rang. I thought about my father, who died in a syndicate flood with his boots on and his ledgers balanced, and I thought: he would have shot Sarel. And then I thought: and he'd have been wrong, and dead, and still wrong. And then I thought nothing at all for a while, which is sometimes the only honest prayer.

When I came back down to the others, Ilse read my face in about a second and a half. She was, I was learning, a woman who read country and people with the same instrument.

"Where's Sarel?"

"Gone. He was the one on the radio. He's been theirs since the silent camp." I said it plainly, because she'd earned plain. "He sold our position at the col. Odell died of it. I let him walk."

Pim's head came up sharply. "You *let* him —"

"I let him," I said, and dared the boy with a look to make something of it, and the boy, to his credit, didn't.

Ilse was quiet a moment. Then: "Did he tell you anything?"

"He told me they'll take me at the river trail," she said, before I could. "Two scouts. A holding camp." She almost smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a woman loading a weapon. "Good. I've been wanting to meet some more of the syndicate's hired help. The last two who caught me are probably still wondering what happened to their ledger."

I looked at her and felt the strange lift in my chest that I'd been refusing to feel since the cave — the feeling that this woman was not the package, not the prize, not the cargo. She was the claim. She was the deed with legs.

"River trail's ahead," Pim said, pointing downhill through the trees, where the land fell away toward a thin grey ribbon of ice and open water far below.

"Then we'd better not keep the scouts waiting," Ilse said, and started down.
