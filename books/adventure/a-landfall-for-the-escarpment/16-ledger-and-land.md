## 16. Ledger and Land

They took her at the treeline, which was embarrassing, because Ilse Marchetti had spent eleven days not being taken by anyone.

She'd been walking point — a courtesy Yara extended her after the whiteout, when she'd pulled Pim out of a crevasse and the crew had quietly reorganized itself around her competence the way iron filings reorganize around a magnet. She'd been reading the slope for avalanche pockets, doing the thing her father had drilled into her since she was nine years old: *look twice, walk once, and never trust snow that looks like meringue.* And she had looked twice. She simply hadn't looked *behind*, because no one had told her that syndicate scouts traveled without dogs, without fires, without any of the noise that honest men make in the woods.

The first she knew of them was a rope loop settling over her shoulders like a badly knitted scarf.

"Ma'am," said a voice behind her, apologetic as a dentist. "You're going to want to come along quiet."

Ilse considered her options. The case was strapped to her back under her pack, which meant they didn't know what they had yet, which meant they hadn't been briefed properly, which meant — even now, even with a rope around her and four men materializing out of the timber like bad weather — some small scholarly corner of her mind was taking notes on syndicate operational discipline and finding it wanting.

"I'm going to want," she said, "to do a great many things."

* * *

They marched her down into a hollow where their camp sat under a green tarp, and this was how she learned they were only a scouting party — five men, one stove, and a radio that crackled like someone frying bacon in another room. Their leader was a broad, tired-looking man called Brack who had the posture of someone who had once been in an army and now missed it the way you miss a tooth.

He went through her pack with gloved hands while two of his men held her arms, which was unnecessary, because Ilse had done the arithmetic already. Four against one, in the open, with her hands free? No. But she let them hold her anyway, because being underestimated was a tool, and her father had also taught her that a surveyor's most important instrument was patience.

"No case," Brack said finally, frowning at the spilled contents of her pack: a spare shirt, dried fish, a coil of wire, Pim's borrowed signal mirror. "Where's the case?"

"What case?"

"The metal case. The deed."

"You'll have to be more specific," Ilse said. "I'm a surveyor's daughter. I've seen forty cases. Theodolite cases. Map cases. One memorable case containing my father's lunch, which he guarded more fiercely than anything else he owned."

Brack looked at her the way tired men look at problems they don't have the budget for. "The girl's got a mouth on her."

"She does," agreed the man holding her left arm. His name, she would learn, was Tetch, and he had a way of speaking about people in the third person while standing close enough to smell them.

"We know Crane has it," Brack said. "We know the crew split after the pass. We know you were carrying it before that." He crouched to her eye level. He smelled of woodsmoke and cheap tobacco. "So here's what happens. You tell us where it is, or we radio it in, and then it stops being a conversation between professionals."

"And becomes what?"

"A retrieval."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. Then, because she was cold, hungry, and had been composing this particular speech in her head since the hanging valley — refining it the way you refine a survey line, checking it against every fixed point she knew — she said:

"Do you have the ledger on you?"

Brack blinked. "What?"

"The ledger. The debt book. Your outfit carries them — I know because I've read one. My family's copy." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report a bearing. "Every debt Vellum holds in this territory is written in those books. Signatures, amounts, terms. I read ours so many times I could recite it. So can you answer my question? Is there one in this camp?"

"Why," said Brack slowly, "would that matter to you?"

"Because," Ilse said, "I'd like to read it to your men."

* * *

Here is what nobody in the Vellum Syndicate's chain of command had ever understood, in Ilse's opinion, and she came from four generations of people who understood land: paper is not neutral. A ledger is not a record. It is a weapon that has been lying on a desk pretending to be furniture, waiting for someone to pick it up.

They put her in the tent under guard — Tetch again, and a young one called Fenner who couldn't have been more than twenty and kept glancing at her the way you glance at a dog that might be rabid or might just be scared. Through the canvas she heard Brack arguing with someone over the radio, low and frustrated, and caught the words *Crane* and *the col* and *Halvorsen wants confirmation*, and filed all of it away.

An hour later, Brack came in carrying a book.

It was black, oilcloth-bound, corners worn soft, and she recognized it instantly — the same binding as the one that had lived in her father's desk drawer, the same greasy thumb-wear on the spine. Brack set it on the camp table outside, in view of the other three men, who had gathered with the instinctive curiosity of people whose work involves long stretches of nothing.

"You get ten minutes," Brack said. "And then we do this the hard way."

"Fine," Ilse said. "Open it to the C's."

"Why?"

"Because I want to start with Craddock, Eben. Or should I say *Eben Craddock, deceased, balance outstanding: four hundred and twelve marks, plus interest accruing.*"

The hollow went very quiet. One of the men by the stove stopped chewing.

"Eben Craddock drowned in the Kessel flood eight years ago," Ilse continued, before anyone could stop her, and here was the thing — she didn't perform it. That was the trick, if there was a trick. Her father had taught her to read survey notes aloud in the field, clear and level, so the numbers could be checked against the ground, and she read the ledger exactly that way: like a bearing, like something true. "His widow paid two hundred marks over six years toward a debt owed by a dead man, because the syndicate held a note signed by Eben's brother — a brother, according to the parish rolls in Lowmark, who died in childhood. There is no signature of Eben Craddock anywhere in this book. There is a signature *shaped* like one. I've seen the forgery. It curls the tail of the final *k*. Whoever wrote it was right-handed and hurried."

She turned the page. Brack made a move toward the book and she looked up at him, and something in her face — the same face that had stared down a collapsing ice bridge two winters ago and calmly measured its rate of failure — made him stop.

"Marta Ostrow," she read. "Balance: ninety marks. Original loan: thirty. Term: one season." She looked up. "Marta Ostrow is my neighbor. She is sixty-one years old. She borrowed thirty marks to replace a roof beam and has paid back ninety and still owes, by this book, sixty more. Does anyone here know what interest rate produces those numbers? Because I do. It isn't in any territorial statute. It isn't legal anywhere in the province. It's legal only because nobody it's been done to can afford a lawyer, and everyone it's been done *by* owns the courthouse clock."

"That's enough," Brack said.

"It really isn't," Ilse said, and turned another page. "Haller, D. Balance: two hundred marks. Collateral listed: *one boat, one rifle, one daughter's labor.*"

The youngest one — Fenner — made a sound. Small, involuntary, like a hinge.

"You," Ilse said gently, finding his eyes. "Haller. D. That's your father, isn't it?"

"I never signed—"

"*He* signed. For you. When you took this job, did they tell you it would go against his balance? Did they show you the entry?" She watched the boy's face do the thing faces do when a load-bearing assumption gives way. "They didn't, because they never do. That's the whole architecture of it. You're not employees. You're collateral that walks."

"Shut her up," said Tetch, but there was no force in it, and Ilse noticed — the way she noticed everything, fault lines, water tables, the tremor in a slope before it moves — that Tetch had drifted half a step away from the table.

"Kessler, Rone," she read on. "Balance: one thousand, one hundred marks. Note appended: *non-recoverable; asset assigned to field operations until worked off.* Do you know what 'worked off' means in this book, gentlemen? It means dying in a gorge upstream so that a number goes to zero instead of a person going home. I found the Marchetti survey team's names in a book just like this one. All seven of them. All marked *assigned to field operations.* All dead except me." She let that sit. "Your employer sent me up that river to die too, and sent Mr. Crane to fetch the paperwork off my family's ruin. And when you're done being useful — when the mountain's crossed and the case is in Halvorsen's hands — there will be an entry in a book like this one for each of you, and it will say whatever Halvorsen needs it to say, because the only signature in the Vellum Syndicate that has ever been real is the one at the bottom of the page that says *write whatever you like; no one checks.*"

Brack reached for the ledger.

Ilse closed it first and put both hands flat on the cover, and looked at him, and said, quietly, in the voice of a woman who had spent eleven days in a cave eating lichen and had therefore lost all fear of anything smaller than geology:

"Check it. Check one entry. Just one. Radio Lowmark and ask after Marta Ostrow's roof. Ask the parish clerk about Eben Craddock's brother. I'll wait. I'm very good at waiting — I once waited out a three-day fog on a ridge with nothing but my father's slide rule and my own thoughts, and I'll tell you, the thoughts won."

Nobody moved. The stove ticked. Somewhere upslope a branch cracked under snow-load, and every man in the camp flinched, and Ilse thought: *there it is.* They were afraid of the woods, afraid of the mountain, afraid of the radio, afraid of Brack, afraid of Halvorsen, afraid of each other — a whole economy of fear, and every coin in it minted by the same office in the lowlands.

"Fenner," Brack said at last, heavily. "Get the rope."

Fenner didn't move.

"Fenner."

"He said my da signed for me." The boy's voice cracked down the middle. "Is that — is that in there? Is that *true?*"

Brack looked at the boy, and then at the ledger under Ilse's hands, and then at the trees, and Ilse watched him arrive at the same conclusion she'd been walking him toward for twenty minutes: that he was fifty years old, that he had served the syndicate for eleven of them, that his own name was certainly in some book somewhere with a number beside it that never went down, and that the only difference between him and the people in the entries was that he was still vertical.

"Tetch," Brack said. "Give me the ledger."

Tetch gave him the ledger. And then Tetch picked up his pack.

"Where in God's name are you going?" Brack asked.

"Lowmark," Tetch said. "My sister's in Lowmark. I've got a balance too. I always figured I'd settle it someday." He shouldered the strap. "Turns out today's someday." He paused at the edge of the tarp-light, and looked back at Ilse with an expression she'd seen on miners' faces looking at a seam they'd walked past for years. "The big party's at the base camp below the ridge. Halvorsen's there. They've been blasting the north face all week — said it was road-building." His mouth twisted. "It didn't sound like road-building. It sounded like the mountain arguing back."

Then he was gone into the timber, and after a moment — a long moment, in which Brack stood holding the ledger like a man holding a snake he'd mistaken for a rope — Fenner went too, snatching his father's entry page out of the book first, folding it into his coat like a letter from home.

* * *

That left Brack, Ilse, the stove, and the ledger.

He sat down across from her, heavily, the way old furniture sits. For a while neither of them spoke. He poured coffee from the dented pot and, after a visible internal negotiation, poured a second cup and pushed it across the table.

"My name's in there," he said eventually. "Page forty. I've never seen it written down. I just always knew."

"I know," Ilse said. "That's how they keep you. Not with ropes. With arithmetic."

"Crane's crew. Where are they?"

"Behind me. Ahead of me. Both, depending on the hour." She drank the coffee. It was terrible, and she was profoundly grateful for it. "Brack. The man who runs your radio — when Halvorsen asks if you have the girl, what will you tell him?"

Brack looked at the ledger. At the fire. At the dark mouth of the timber where two of his men had just become deserters, and where, if he were smart, he'd follow them.

"I'll tell him," he said slowly, "that we found you, that you had no case, and that the trail went cold above the scree." He rubbed his face with both hands. "And then I'm going to sit here and read this damn book until sunrise, because apparently I've spent eleven years working for a fiction and I'd like to know the ending."

"One correction," Ilse said. "You haven't been working for a fiction. You've been working for a *forgery*. Fictions at least admit what they are."

Brack made a sound that might have been a laugh, in a better-managed life.

She slept two hours by the stove, hands unbound, the case warm against her spine where no one had thought to look, and woke before dawn to Brack shaking her shoulder — gently, the way you wake someone you've decided not to be afraid of.

"Radio's been chattering all night," he said. "Something's wrong up at the base camp. Halvorsen's pulled everyone inside. They're saying—" He hesitated, and Ilse saw genuine puzzlement in his face, the puzzlement of a practical man confronting data that doesn't fit the form. "They're saying the ridge is making noise. That the ground's moving up there. That the escarpment itself—"

Above the hollow, faint through the timber, came a sound Ilse knew from her father's stories and had hoped never to hear firsthand: a deep, grinding groan, like a door the size of a mountain being forced open.

"—is coming down," she finished for him.
