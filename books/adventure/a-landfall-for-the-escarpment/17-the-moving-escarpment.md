## 17. The Moving Escarpment

Pim had always believed that a radio was an honest instrument. It didn't care who you were. It didn't flatter you or lie to you or pretend to be your friend so it could borrow money later. It just carried whatever sound fell into its ear and set it down again somewhere else, faithful as a mirror. That was why he loved the work, and that was why, crouched in the lee of a granite shoulder two thousand feet above the river valley with his headset clamped over his ears and his fingers going stiff as candle wax, he felt personally betrayed by what he was hearing.

The syndicate chatter had started at dawn.

He'd been sweeping frequencies out of habit more than hope — Odell was dead, Sarel was gone, and nobody left in their little band of frozen fugitives much cared what the world was saying about them — but old habits were the only warm things Pim owned anymore, so he swept anyway. And there it was: a burst of encrypted traffic on the syndicate's working band, tight-beamed from somewhere south and east, bouncing off the very ridge they'd spent three days bleeding across.

He couldn't crack the encryption. He wasn't that kind of genius. But he didn't need to. Encryption kept the *words* secret; it couldn't do anything about the *pattern*. And patterns were just words wearing disguises, and Pim had spent four years learning to read radio the way other people read faces.

This pattern said: *coordinates, repeated, hourly.* Somebody down there was feeding the syndicate their position. Had been for days. Sarel's voice, probably, coming through some handheld he'd carried off into the trees like a man carrying a letter of divorce.

Pim sat with that for a while and let it hurt. It hurt less than he expected, which was its own kind of sad. You could only be surprised by betrayal once per friendship, and Sarel had used up everyone's surprise back at the col where Odell died.

* * *

The second thing on the frequency was stranger.

Beneath the encrypted bursts — under them, literally, a low-frequency rumble that shouldn't have been there at all — the radio was picking up something else. A groan. Not mechanical. Not human. A deep, tectonic complaint, like a house settling, if the house were eleven miles long and made of stone.

At first Pim assumed his equipment was failing. Cold did terrible things to batteries; he'd lost two radios already to the simple physics of lithium giving up in the dark. He smacked the receiver twice, checked his grounds, swapped the power cell for the last warm one from inside his coat. The groan persisted.

Then, at midmorning, the encrypted traffic changed character. The coordinates stopped. In their place came longer transmissions, still coded but longer, and one of them — one glorious, catastrophic slip of operational sloppiness — came through in the clear. Somebody at the syndicate base had keyed their mic on the wrong channel, and for eleven seconds Pim heard a man's voice, harried and flat, saying:

"—third blast this week, tell Halvorsen if they want the north cut open by thaw they can come explain the seismographs themselves—"

And then the channel snapped shut, and Pim sat very still in the snow with his heart doing something complicated against his ribs.

*Blasts.* Mining blasts. Three this week. North cut. Thaw.

He thought about the last transmission. The Marchetti expedition's final broadcast, the garbled message Yara had played for him a hundred miles ago, back when the world was green and Pim had been a different, softer person: *the escarpment is moving.* Everyone had heard it as panic, as frost-madness, as a dying man's poetry. Weather. Always weather. The mountains ate people and weather got blamed, same as always.

But Ilse hadn't talked like a woman whose team had died of cold. She'd talked like a surveyor. And surveyors didn't run from weather. Surveyors ran from *data.*

Pim pulled out his notebook — the good one, the waterproof one, the one he guarded like scripture — and began, with fingers he had to thump awake between entries, to do the thing he was actually trained for. Not chasing voices. Chasing signals to their sources.

Triangulation was simple geometry wearing a fancy name. Two bearings on a fixed signal gave you a position, and the syndicate's relay tower was nothing if not fixed — it had been squawking from the same bearing since the pass, southeast, down-valley. Which meant the syndicate base was southeast, down-valley, tucked into the throat of the gorge below the escarpment's northern face.

Directly below the northern face.

Directly below the face that had been groaning all morning like an old man getting out of a chair.

* * *

He found Yara first, because Yara was easiest to find — he stood at the edge of every overlook like a man reading a horizon instead of a view, and Pim had learned to simply walk toward whichever skyline looked most judged.

Yara listened without interrupting, which was the thing Pim liked best about him. Most adults interrupted. Most adults treated a nineteen-year-old radio tech's sentences like rough drafts they were welcome to improve. Yara just went still and let you finish, and then asked the question you were hoping someone would ask.

"Say the last part again."

"The blasts are destabilizing the ridge. Their own mining. They've been shooting the north face for weeks — thaw season, soft ground, cheap rock — and the whole escarpment section above their camp is letting go. Slowly." Pim swallowed. "Or maybe not slowly anymore. The groaning's getting louder. I've logged it. Listen."

He played the recording. Even Yara, who had grown up around these mountains and claimed to be unimpressable by them, went pale in a way the cold couldn't account for.

"That's not wind," Yara said quietly.

"No. Wind doesn't repeat at forty-second intervals. Stone does. When it's deciding something."

"Deciding," Yara repeated.

"Geologists say it like that. My instructor did. Mountains don't fall, she said, they *decide*, and your job is to overhear the decision early enough to leave." Pim hugged the recorder to his chest. "Yara. The Marchetti team wasn't killed by weather. They surveyed the north face. They found the instability — probably found it *because* the syndicate's blasting opened it up — and the last transmission wasn't madness. It was a report. 'The escarpment is moving' wasn't a cry for help. It was a finding. They died — or scattered, or whatever happened to them — because they knew what the mountain was about to do and they were trying to get clear, or get word out, and the syndicate couldn't allow either one."

Yara was quiet for a long moment. Below them the valley lay white and innocent, the river iced to a dull pewter seam, and somewhere down in that pretty picture sat a camp full of men who thought they were the hunters.

"They're sitting under it," Yara said. "All of them. Halvorsen, the scouts, the master ledger, everything. They built their den directly beneath the cliff they've been blowing apart."

"The land's collecting its debt," Pim said, and then winced, because it sounded better in his head than out loud, too neat, too much like something you'd carve on a memorial. But Yara didn't wince. Yara huffed — almost a laugh, the tired kind, the kind that had replaced most of his real laughs somewhere above the treeline.

"Odell would've liked that," he said. "He always said the river keeps books too."

* * *

Ilse took the news differently. Ilse took all news the way water takes a slope — instantly, finding every channel, no wasted motion.

She made Pim play the recording three times. Then she unrolled her father's survey maps on a flat rock, weighted the corners with stones, and traced the northern face with a cracked fingernail.

"Here." Her nail stopped at a fold in the contour lines, a place where the elevation marks crowded together like people pushing toward a door. "The north cut. This is where their claims are richest — my father mapped the ore body years ago, before the syndicate forged the grant away from us. Shallow deposit, fractured host rock. Easy money, easy digging. They'd have been blasting it since spring." Her finger moved up-slope, following the contours to the crest. "And everything they've loosened up here—" the nail tapped the crest line, twice, "—has nowhere to go but down. Onto them."

"How long?" Yara asked.

Ilse looked at Pim. Pim realized, with a small vertiginous lurch, that she was looking at him *as the expert.* Somewhere between the whiteout and this moment, without anyone announcing it, he had become the person whose answer mattered.

"Days," he said. "Maybe less. The interval on the groan is shortening — forty seconds this morning, thirty-one when I logged it at noon. That's acceleration. If it keeps that curve..." He did the arithmetic in his head, hated it, did it again hoping for a kinder number. "Tomorrow night. Maybe the morning after. If we get another hard freeze it might hold a little longer — ice is glue, sort of — but they're blasting, which means they're shaking the glue loose on purpose."

"So the syndicate has roughly two days," Yara said slowly, "before their entire operation, their ledger, and their payroll are buried under several million tons of the property they stole."

"And they don't know," Ilse said. "They think the Marchetti data died with the Marchetti team. They think the only threat up here is us." Something moved across her face — not quite a smile, too grim for that, but adjacent to one. "They're right that we're a threat. Just not the kind they're watching for."

Pim raised his hand, feeling suddenly like he was back in the classroom he'd fled at seventeen. "Um. Question from the cheap seats. Do we *warn* them?"

The silence that followed had texture. Yara looked at Ilse. Ilse looked at the map, at the ore body her family had owned before signatures were forged over it, at the camp where men had hunted her across a mountain for carrying a piece of paper that proved she existed in the eyes of the law.

"I keep asking myself that," she said finally. "There are hired guns down there. Men like the two who walked away from the scout party after I read them their own ledger. Men who didn't choose this any more than Yara did. Some of them might listen, if anyone told them the ground itself was about to collect." She pressed her palm flat against the map, over the buried camp, as if she could feel the heat of it. "But Halvorsen won't listen. Halvorsen will hear a warning and know exactly what it means — that the evidence is about to destroy itself, that the ledger dies with the camp, that every debt he holds becomes ash. He'll burn the deed the moment he smells trouble. And if we go down there shouting warnings, we hand him the excuse to do it while the mountain does his erasing for him."

"So we can't warn them," Pim said.

"We can't warn *him,*" Ilse corrected. "That's the ugly arithmetic. Every hour we spend being merciful is an hour closer to the deed burning."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Above them the escarpment groaned, patient and enormous, a creditor unlike any the Vellum Syndicate had ever faced — one that didn't negotiate, didn't forge, didn't threaten. It just waited, and then it collected.

It occurred to Pim, not for the first time, that the mountain had been running the longest con of anyone. For years it had let them believe it was scenery.

* * *

They told the others around the small fire that evening — the defected scouts, Renn and the quiet one called Marn, who still flinched at sudden noises but had stopped flinching at Ilse, which Pim counted as progress. Renn took the news about the base with the flat practicality of a man recalculating his wages.

"Blasts," he said. "Three a week. Yeah. I ran powder crews two seasons. Nobody ever checks the crown wall after a shoot. Nobody ever checks anything." He spat into the fire. "Halvorsen counts money. Money doesn't groan."

Marn said nothing, but he looked up-slope at the black silhouette of the crest against the stars, and Pim saw him mouth something — a prayer, or a number, or a name. Maybe Odell's. People had started doing that. Carrying the dead uphill in their mouths.

Later, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and the others slept in their frost-furred bedrolls, Pim sat up with his radio and did the thing he'd been avoiding all day: he tuned, one more time, to the syndicate band, and listened to the encrypted bursts tick past, hour after hour, each one a little heartbeat from a camp full of men who had no idea they were living on borrowed ground.

He could send a warning. Broadband, uncoded, plain speech. *Your ridge is failing. Run.* It would take ninety seconds. It would also reach Halvorsen's ears, and Halvorsen would look at the mountain, and then he would look at the strongbox, and Pim knew — the way you know a radio is honest — exactly which one Halvorsen would save.

So instead Pim did something smaller and stranger. He keyed his mic, low power, short range, aimed down-valley at nothing in particular, and spoke into the dark the way you'd speak at a graveside.

"This is Pim Okafor, mobile station, high escarpment route." His breath fogged the mic. "For anyone listening on the working bands below. Check your crown walls. Check them tonight. That's all. That's free."

He released the key. No acknowledgment came, which was expected; the transmission had the reach of a shout into a pillow. But it mattered to him that it existed somewhere in the air, that he had put the truth onto a frequency, because radios were honest instruments and somebody ought to use them honestly at least once today.

Above the fire, the escarpment groaned. Twenty-six seconds now. He wrote it down.

* * *

Yara appeared beside him in the gray before dawn, silent as the snow, and sat down without asking. For a while neither of them spoke. Then Yara nodded up-slope.

"It's louder."

"Twenty-two seconds," Pim said. "I timed it at four o'clock. We're past the point where freezing saves it. Tomorrow. Maybe tomorrow night." He turned the notebook toward Yara, showing the shrinking intervals written in columns, a countdown disguised as data. "Which means if we want the ledger — if Ilse wants her day in court with actual proof, if we want anything down there but ash — we move *now.* Today. Down the service routes the scouts know, ahead of the slide, into a camp that's about to become the inside of a bell."

Yara studied the columns for a long time. When he looked up, there was something in his face Pim hadn't seen before — not fear, exactly, and not calm either, but the strange third thing that lived between them, the thing Pim had started privately calling *the landfall look.*

"Get the others up," Yara said. "Wake Ilse first. Tell her the mountain's given us a deadline." He rose, stamped feeling back into his boots, and managed half a smile — the wrong-moment kind, the kind Pim had learned meant Yara Crane was about to do something either brave or stupid, and had stopped bothering to distinguish. "And Pim? Log the intervals all the way down. However this ends, I want the record to show the mountain warned *us* fair and square."

Down in the valley, faint as a settling house, the escarpment groaned again.

Nineteen seconds.

Pim wrote it down, and went to wake the dead-camp quiet of the shelter, and the day that would decide everything began.
