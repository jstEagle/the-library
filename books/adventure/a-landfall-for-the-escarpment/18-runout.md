## 18. Runout

The mountain groaned like an old man getting out of a chair he'd sat in for ten thousand years, and we ran.

I want to be honest about what that looked like, because later — if there was a later — people would ask me about it and I'd be tempted to make it heroic. It wasn't heroic. It was four people and two turncoat scouts flailing downhill through knee-deep snow while the entire eastern face of the Escarpment decided, after millennia of patience, to renegotiate its lease.

"Left!" shouted Dree, the taller of our two defected scouts, pointing at a gully that looked identical to every other gully. "The tunnels come up behind the fuel depot!"

"And how do you know?" Pim yelled back, arms pinwheeling.

"Because I helped dig them!"

That was the thing about the syndicate's hired men, I was learning. They weren't loyal. They were *informed*. Loyalty you had to buy every morning; information stayed bought forever.

Behind us, above us, the sound changed. I'd spent my whole adult life listening to storms, so let me tell you what a mountain sounds like when it lets go: it doesn't roar, not at first. It *hisses*. A long intake of breath, like the whole ridge drawing air through its teeth. Then the roar comes, but by then the roar is already behind you, and the only question left is whether you're a person running or a statistic.

Ilse grabbed my sleeve. "Yara."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you have the face of a man doing arithmetic."

She was right. I'd been calculating odds since the river froze — distance to the base, speed of the slide, weight of the case against my ribs, the odds any of us saw another spring. And somewhere in the last hundred yards of white screaming chaos, I'd stopped. Not given up. Stopped. There's a difference, and the difference felt like taking off a pack I'd forgotten I was wearing.

"No more math," I told her. "From here on, we just run."

"That's the stupidest strategy I've ever heard from a professional."

"It's not a strategy. It's a vacation."

Despite everything — the cold, the terror, the fact that the world's largest avalanche was drafting us like a debt collector — she laughed. Short, sharp, alive. Then we ran some more.

* * *

The tunnel mouth was a steel door set into rock, painted the gray of things meant to be forgotten, and Dree hit it with his shoulder like he'd been waiting years for permission. Inside: darkness, diesel smell, and a ladder going down into the mountain's guts.

"In," Odell would have said, except Odell wasn't with us anymore, and the space where his voice should've been was its own kind of silence. We went in anyway. He'd have wanted that. Or he'd have wanted us to be sensible, but Odell had been dead two days and sensible had died with him.

Pim came down last and hauled the door shut, and the roar outside became a rumor, then a memory, then just pressure in my back teeth.

Flashlight beams crossed in the dark. Six of us on a catwalk over black water, breathing hard, steaming like kettles.

"This way," said Fen — the short scout, the quiet one, the one who'd put down his rifle at Ilse's reading of the ledger and hadn't picked it up since. "Service tunnels run under the whole camp. Power, water, ore carts. Halvorsen never comes down here. He says it smells like work."

"Halvorsen," I said, testing the name in my mouth. The broker. The man who held my ledger page, my winter, my life priced out in ink. "He'll be evacuating. With the master ledger."

"He'll be evacuating with the master ledger, the payroll strongbox, and the whiskey," Fen said. "In that order. Man's got priorities."

"How long do we have before this place is a hole?"

Dree answered by holding up his hand. Through the rock around us came a deep, grinding shudder, dust sifting down through flashlight beams like the mountain shedding dandruff.

"That long," he said.

We moved.

* * *

The tunnels were older than the syndicate's claims — survey marks from three different companies, dates going back forty years, all of them lies stacked on lies. Somebody had always known what was under the Escarpment. Somebody had always been willing to kill for it. Ilse walked with her hand flat against the rock wall, and when I caught up to her she said, quietly:

"My grandfather surveyed this section. 1961. His notes are probably still down here somewhere."

"You think he knew it would come down one day?"

"I think he knew what blasting does to frozen ground. I think he wrote it down. I think men like Halvorsen read it and filed it." Her jaw tightened. "The escarpment is moving. That transmission wasn't panic, Yara. It was a report nobody wanted to hear."

The case rode against her chest now, strapped tight, the deed inside it worth more than every tunnel, every camp, every forged signature in the syndicate's books. She carried it the way you carry a sleeping child through a crowded room — carefully, and with a violence coiled underneath.

Ahead, Pim's radio crackled. My boy had kept that radio working through whiteout, ambush, and grief, and now it earned its keep one more time.

"—all personnel to helipad two, repeat, helipad two, extraction in twenty minutes—"

"Twenty minutes," Pim repeated, looking back at me. His face was smudged and young and absolutely certain we could do something impossible in twenty minutes. I loved him for it. I also wanted to sit him down and explain odds to him, except I'd just sworn off odds myself, so instead I said:

"Then we'd better be rude guests and arrive early."

Fen led us off the main gallery into a maintenance crawl barely tall enough for crouching, and for ten minutes the world was rust, pipe joints, and the sound of six people breathing in a space built for none of them. Twice the mountain shrugged and the pipes sang. Once something large shifted far above us and everyone froze, and in the beam-light I saw Ilse's eyes find mine, steady as a surveyor's transit.

"Not yet," she mouthed.

Not yet. The land collecting its own debt, but slowly, giving us a grace period no creditor had ever granted me. I intended to insult it by surviving.

* * *

The crawl opened into the lower camp — a concrete throat lit by emergency amber, crates stacked for evacuation, and the strongroom door standing open like a confession.

Empty.

"No," Pim said. "No, no, no—"

"Helipad two," Dree said grimly. "Told you. Whiskey first, though. Look — spill marks. He took a minute to pour himself a drink before running from an avalanche. That's the man you're chasing."

I stood in the strongroom doorway and felt the strangest calm settle over me, the one I'd found on the slope and hadn't lost. Somewhere between the frozen river and here, I'd stopped being a man trying to survive his debts and become a man settling accounts. Those are different professions. One pays in years. The other pays in whatever this was — this lightness, this terrible clarity.

The stairs to the surface were ahead, shaking fine grit off each step like a dog shaking off rain. Above them, faintly, rotor noise.

"Yara." Ilse touched my arm. "Whatever happens up there — the ledger burns or I do. Not both. Promise me you remember which one matters."

"The ledger," I said. "Always the ledger."

"And you?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I usually manage. "Me, I'm negotiable."

"Don't you dare," she said, and there was such fury in it, such plain human refusal, that I laughed all the way up the stairs.

The mountain roared behind us like it disapproved of the joke. Fine. Everyone's a critic.

Above, the rotors turned, and Halvorsen was loading his sins onto a helicopter, and I had maybe four minutes of life left in which to commit the best crime of my career.

I took the steps two at a time.
