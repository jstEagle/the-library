## 19. What Burns

The service tunnels smelled of diesel and old rain, and the defected scouts — Brannick and the young one called Teodor, who still looked like he couldn't believe his own feet were carrying him this direction — moved through them with the ease of men walking home in the dark.

"Main corridor's flooded," Brannick said, holding up a hand at a junction. "Blast damage from Tuesday's shots cracked the cistern. We go under."

"Under," Pim repeated, in the voice of a man who had recently learned that under mountains was where mountains kept their grudges.

"Under," Brannick confirmed, and dropped through a hatch without waiting for agreement.

I went last, because someone had to count heads, and because I had stopped trusting my own courage somewhere above the treeline and had found, to my surprise, that it worked fine anyway when there was no time to think about it. Ilse went ahead of me with the case strapped across her back like a second spine. She'd refused every offer to carry it since the col. Odell had died so it could keep moving; she wasn't going to let it move on anyone else's shoulders but hers.

The tunnel under the cistern was ankle-deep in cold water and lit by nothing but Pim's hand torch and the occasional stutter of the base's failing generators, which came through the rock as a kind of arrhythmic heartbeat. Somewhere above us, the Escarpment was settling its accounts. Every few minutes a deep groan rolled through the stone, long and tectonic, like an old man turning over in bed who has decided he is not getting up today after all.

"Does your boss know?" Ilse asked Brannick quietly. "That the ridge is coming down on him?"

"Halvorsen knows what Halvorsen wants to know." Brannick didn't turn around. "He got the survey reports in October. He filed them under *inconvenient*."

We waded on. The water rose to our knees, then mercifully fell away as the tunnel climbed toward the base proper. And all the while that sound followed us — the mountain clearing its throat, patient as winter.

* * *

We came up through a maintenance hatch into the loading bay of the Vellum Syndicate's river station, and I will say this for the place: they'd built it to last. Steel frame, poured concrete, floodlights on independent power. It was built to outlast everything except the ground it stood on, which is a mistake people make about a lot of things, including themselves.

The bay was chaos with a schedule. Men hauling crates toward the boats; a crane operator shouting about a jammed winch; somewhere a radio crackling with the flat, repeating language of evacuation orders nobody quite believed yet. Nobody looked at us twice. Two more hired hands in wet gear, moving crates. That was the whole trick of it. Desperation looks exactly like employment if you dress it right.

Teodor peeled off toward the barracks — his job was the boats, cutting them loose at the right moment so no one could flee downriver with anything heavier than a suitcase — and Brannick took us along the bay's edge toward the interior offices, walking with the unhurried gait of a man whose conscience had finally caught up with his boots.

"The master ledger's in Halvorsen's office," he said. "Iron cabinet, left of the desk. He keeps it close because half the men in this camp would burn it themselves if they could read past page one."

"Can you read past page one?" Pim asked him.

"I'm on page one," Brannick said. "It's got my name in it."

Then the lights went brown, surged, and held, and the mountain groaned again — closer now, a sound with weight behind it, like a door swinging on a very large hinge — and we stopped talking and started moving.

* * *

Halvorsen's office was on the second floor, glass-walled, facing the river, because brokers like to watch the water carry things away. He was alone, which surprised me until I understood it wasn't bravery. Everyone else had already decided the building was dying. Halvorsen was the kind of man who stayed at his desk during a fire alarm because leaving would mean admitting the fire was real.

He looked up when we came in — four soaked, frostbitten ghosts dripping on his carpet — and I watched him do the arithmetic. Ilse Marchetti, alive. The case, intact. Me, whom he'd paid good money to have handled quietly upriver. His face went through surprise, calculation, and arrived somewhere near relief, which told me everything about how the rest of the conversation was going to go.

"Crane." He set down his pen with great care, as though pens were the only things in the world behaving properly today. "You're a difficult man to spend money on."

"So I've been told. Mostly by creditors."

Behind me, Pim slipped sideways to cover the corridor door, and Brannick took the stairwell, and Ilse stepped forward and set the metal case on Halvorsen's desk with a click that sounded, in that room, louder than the mountain.

Halvorsen looked at the case the way some men look at churches.

"You have no idea what you're carrying," he said.

"I've had it explained to me," Ilse said. "At length. In several settings. Once during a blizzard."

"The grant is void. It has been void for sixty years. My principals hold perfected claims, surveyed, registered—"

"Forged," Ilse said. "Registered by forgery. I've read the file, Mr. Halvorsen. My grandfather's signature appears on documents dated nine years after he drowned. He was apparently a very committed correspondent."

Outside the glass wall, the river ran gray and fast, and beyond it the lower slopes of the Escarpment were shedding their first snow in long white sheets. A crack rolled through the air — not thunder, not blasting, something between — and the water glasses on Halvorsen's shelf rang together like nervous guests.

"You don't understand the scale of what's invested here," Halvorsen said, and now his voice had changed, gone quieter, the way men's voices do when they stop selling and start pleading. "There are principals. There are interests in three territories. That paper—" he touched the case, just the edge of it, one finger, "—that paper is worth more than everyone in this valley. More than the valley."

"And the ledger?" I asked.

His eyes came to me then, and I saw him remember, all at once, why I was standing in his office instead of lying in a gorge upriver. He opened the iron cabinet beside his desk — the lock was already open; a man evacuating keeps his most dangerous book where he can reach it — and lifted out a ledger bound in green vellum, thick as a Bible, corners worn soft by handling.

Every debt the Syndicate owned in the territory. Every name. Mine on page forty-something, under the truck loan and the storm-chase advances and the interest that compounded like weather. Sarel Vance two pages later. Odell, God help him, entered neat and final. Half the men downstairs hauling crates were in that book, and every one of them was working off a number that never got smaller, because that was the design.

Halvorsen set it on the desk next to the case and put his hand flat on both.

"A trade," he said. "Here is what tonight offers, Crane, and it will not be offered twice. The mountain is coming down. My boats leave within the hour. You walk onto one of them with your debt torn out of this book — torn out, witnessed, done — and I take the grant into the fire. One paper burns, one life clears. You go home owing nothing. *Nothing*, Crane. Do you know what that word costs? I'm offering it to you at a discount."

The room hummed. The lights browned again and steadied. Somewhere below us a crate hit concrete and a man swore with feeling.

And here is the thing I want to be honest about, because honesty is the only inheritance I ever got clean: for about three seconds, I wanted it. Three whole seconds I stood there and felt the weight lift before it had even happened — the number gone, the winter mine again, the road open in every direction. Three seconds of a life where I was nobody's asset.

Then I looked at the ledger. All those names. Brannick on page one. The boy Teodor, who couldn't have been twenty. Names I knew and names I didn't, all of them entered in the same tidy hand, all of them compounding.

"You're doing the arithmetic," Halvorsen said, watching my face. "Good. You're finally learning."

"No," I said. "That's the trouble with you, Halvorsen. You think everybody's doing arithmetic. Some of us are just bad at math on purpose."

I picked up the ledger.

He actually smiled — a broker's smile, warm as a filing cabinet — because he thought I'd chosen. He thought I'd weighed the deed against the debt and picked myself, and that the case was about to go into the stove in the corner, the little iron one he kept burning against the river damp, and that in five minutes we'd all be reasonable men on a boat.

"Open the case," he said to Ilse, almost gently. "It goes easier if you do it yourself."

Ilse didn't move. She was looking at me. So was Pim, from the doorway, torch in hand, face pale and steady.

I carried the ledger to the stove.

I want to report that I paused dramatically, or that some great speech assembled itself in my throat. What actually happened is that the stove door stuck, because cheap iron sticks, and I wrestled with it for an undignified ten seconds while Halvorsen's smile curdled into confusion, and then the door gave and I shoved the green book in among the coals and shut it.

For a moment nothing. Then the vellum cover caught, and the fire turned a color I hadn't seen it turn before — a brief, oily green-gold, sixty years of other people's servitude going up as chemistry — and the smell was foul and wonderful, like burning money, which is what it was, except it had never really been money. It had been us. We were just the collateral.

"*What are you doing.*" Halvorsen's voice had lost every ounce of broker in it. Underneath, it turned out, there was just a man. "That book is worth— Crane. *Crane.* Your debt. Your freedom. I am handing you your freedom—"

"You can't hand out what you stole," I said. "You want the deed? Come take it. But you'll be taking it from her—" I nodded at Ilse, "—and from every name in that book, and the mountain's already taken your camp, and your boats are leaving, and I'd hurry, because I hear the escarpment moves."

The ledger curled and blackened. Pages lifted, flared, and I saw names go — whole columns of them, debts dying mid-sentence — and I swear the fire sounded different after that. Lighter. Like it was enjoying itself.

Halvorsen lunged for the stove.

I'll give him this: he was fast for a man who'd spent his life sitting down. He got the stove door open and his arm in before I caught him, and we struggled there, two fools wrestling over a burning book in a falling-down building, and it would have been almost funny except his elbow caught my split lip and the pain went through me like a struck bell. Pim shouted. Ilse pulled the case off the desk and backed away with it, and Halvorsen twisted toward her — toward the deed, always the deed, the man had one note and he played it — and in that half-second of divided attention I got my shoulder into him and we went over together, into the desk, into the glass.

The window didn't shatter. It bowed, flexed, held — modern glass, built to last — and for one absurd moment Halvorsen and I stood pressed against it, cheek to cheek, looking out at the river and the snow and the dark flank of the Escarpment, where a seam of white powder was now running down the slope like sugar poured from a great height.

"There it is," I said, mostly to myself.

Then the mountain spoke in earnest.

It wasn't a sound so much as a decision. The whole south face let go at once — I felt it through the floor before I heard it, a deep structural sigh, the sound of a thing too heavy to argue with finally agreeing with gravity — and the noise arrived a heartbeat later, enormous, rolling, the avalanche coming down the syndicate's own cut slopes, through their own blast-shattered rock, gathering everything they'd spent thirty years loosening.

The lights died for good. Emergency amber came up, thin and sickly. Down in the loading bay, men were screaming the useful kind of screaming, the kind with directions in it.

Halvorsen pushed off the glass and stood there in the amber light, and I watched him understand it — really understand it, maybe for the first time in his life — that the land he'd been carving up had been keeping its own books, and the interest had come due, and there was no deferment, and no forgery big enough.

"The boats," he said. Just that. The great broker of the Vellum Syndicate, reduced to a single syllable pair, and then he was gone, out the office door, running for the river with his coat half-on, leaving his ledger burning and his empire groaning behind him.

Pim appeared at my elbow, breathing hard. "Was that — did we just—"

"We burned a book," I said. "The mountain's handling the rest."

Ilse stood by the window with the case hugged to her chest, watching the slide come down through the emergency light — a slow-motion white tide swallowing the lower yards, the crane, the flooded main corridor we'd come through an hour ago. Her face wasn't triumphant. It was tired and clear, the way faces get at the end of a very long shift.

"It's not finished," she said. "The court still has to hear it. Halvorsen's principals are still out there. This is just—" she searched for the word, "—the receipt."

The floor tilted, gently, like a boat taking a swell. Dust sifted from the ceiling tiles in long gray ropes.

"Receipts first," I said. "Then the rest. Everybody out — Pim, the stairs, Ilse, stay behind me, and if anyone asks, we're crate handlers."

The stairwell was full of smoke and men going the same direction as us, which was the best camouflage in the world. Behind us, in the dark office, the last pages of the ledger glowed in the stove like a banked coal, and I did not look back at them, because I already knew what was written in the ashes: nothing. For the first time in six years, when the Vellum Syndicate checked its books, my name would not be there.

Owing nothing. It turns out the word costs exactly what Halvorsen said. It cost me the easy way out, the trade, the three seconds of arithmetic — and it bought back something I'd pawned so long ago I'd forgotten I owned it.

We hit the loading bay at a run, into the crowd, into the snow blowing in through the shattered doors, and ahead of us, down at the black water, Teodor was already casting off the first boat — early, eager, a boy defecting with style — and the mountain came down behind us like applause.
