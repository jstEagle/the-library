## 20. After the Mountain Settles

I woke to a silence so complete it had texture.

Not the silence of the high country, which was never really silent — wind worrying the rock, ice ticking in its seams, the mountain muttering to itself all night like an old man turning over in bed. This was the silence that comes *after*, the held-breath quiet of a world that has just finished shouting and is embarrassed about it.

Dawn was coming up gray-gold over the shoulder of the ridge, and the syndicate camp was gone.

Not damaged. Not scattered. *Gone* — swallowed whole by the slope failure Pim's radio had been screaming about for two days. Where the bunkhouses and the assay shed and Halvorsen's long timber office had stood, there was now a smooth apron of debris fanning out toward the frozen river, dotted with the tips of things: a stovepipe, half a doorframe, the corner of a crate stamped VELLUM SYNDICATE — TERRITORIAL DIVISION in letters so cheerful they looked like a joke someone had forgotten to finish.

The land had collected its own debt. I stood at the edge of our camp above the treeline with my hands around a tin cup of snowmelt Odell would have called tea if he'd been feeling generous, which he never was, and I thought: *my grandfather surveyed this ground with a chain and a brass transit, and he would have said the mountain simply took back what was always his.*

Then I thought of the men under it, and put the cup down.

* * *

We went down into the wreckage because we had to — not for salvage, though Yara would not say no to a working stove, but because you don't leave people buried without looking. Even people who came up the river to erase you.

Yara led, moving through the debris field with that strange calm he'd carried since the tunnels, the calm of a man who has stopped calculating odds and started simply walking. Pim followed with the radio slung on his back like a wounded bird he'd adopted. I came last, because somebody had to watch the slope above us, and because I'd spent six weeks learning to read ground, and this ground was still reading wrong. It had settled, mostly. Mostly is a word you learn to respect when your family's fortune is a landform.

"Here," Yara said, and stopped.

A hand, first. Then an arm in a good wool coat, attached to a man sitting half-buried against a fallen beam as though he'd sat down to rest during a long walk and simply never gotten up. His face was gray with dust and cold. His eyes were open.

Sarel Vance.

Pim made a sound like a kettle taken off the heat too late. Yara didn't move for three full seconds — I counted them, because counting is something surveyors' daughters do instead of screaming — and then he knelt and pressed two fingers to Sarel's throat and stayed there a long moment.

"Alive," he said. "Barely. Leg's pinned."

He said it flatly, and I watched him decide, right there in the space of a breath, not to say anything else. Not *him*. Not *of course it's him*. Not any of the things a man might say about the friend who sold him to the syndicate and cost Odell his life at the Col. Yara Winterbourne folded all of that away behind his face the way you fold a map you're done with but can't throw out, and got to work digging.

So I dug too. That's the thing nobody tells you about hatred: it's heavy, and after a while your arms get tired, and you either set it down or you drop everything else to keep carrying it.

* * *

It took two hours to free him. The beam had Sarel's left leg pinned at the shin, and by the time we levered it off with a salvaged pry bar, his eyes had focused and found each of us in turn — Yara, Pim, me — and I watched him do his own arithmetic. Survival is arithmetic; he'd told Yara that once, Pim said, repeating it later around the fire in a voice pitched to carry. Sarel did the sums now: three faces, one traitor, one dead boatman who wasn't there to name him again.

"I'm sorry," he said. His voice was a ruin. Dust and cold and whatever else lives in a man after a night under a collapsed building. "About Odell."

Yara tied off the splint without looking up. "He died naming you."

"I know." A pause. "I heard him."

That stopped even Pim, who could talk through anything. Sarel lay back against the beam and looked at the sky, which was doing its pale gold morning business overhead, indifferent and lovely, and said, "I heard him say my name, and then I heard the mountain start, and I thought — well. That's fair, isn't it. That's the ledger balancing."

"The ledger doesn't balance," I said.

Everyone looked at me. I hadn't meant to speak. But I had spent the night not sleeping, going over and over the thing I'd done in the scout camp — standing in front of four armed men with their own debt book in my hands, reading aloud, watching doubt move through them like weather — and I knew something about ledgers now that I hadn't known before.

"It doesn't balance," I said again. "That's the whole trick of them. A ledger only balances if everyone agrees on what's owed. The Syndicate wrote both columns. They wrote what *we* owed them, and they wrote what they were owed for taking my family's land, and none of it was ever true. It was true because they held the pen." I crouched down so Sarel had to look at me. "You think you settled accounts by selling us. You didn't settle anything. You just let them keep the pen."

His eyes closed. "What are you going to do," he said, and it wasn't quite a question. It was a man asking how much.

And here is the honest part, the part I will tell the territorial court exactly as I'm telling it now, because testimony is worthless if you sand it smooth: I wanted him hurt. I wanted it the way you want water — simple, animal, below thinking. This man had fed our coordinates to hired guns. Odell was dead. Pim had frostbitten fingers and nightmares. Yara had walked out of a collapsing base carrying a deed instead of his freedom, and Sarel Vance had helped build the trap that made that choice necessary.

I could have left him. Nobody would have questioned it. The mountain had already voted.

Instead I said, "You're coming down with us. And when we reach the lowlands, you're going to sit in a courtroom and tell a magistrate everything you told the Syndicate, every message, every payment, every name. You're going to be the pen."

He opened his eyes. "They'll hang me."

"They might," I agreed. "Or they might not, if the story you tell is worth more than the man telling it. That's not mercy, Sarel. Don't mistake it. Mercy would have been quick." I stood up. My knees cracked — six weeks of cold will do that to a person, and I was twenty-six years old and felt seventy. "This is testimony. You're going to spend the rest of your life paying in the only coin they can't forge."

Yara was watching me with an expression I couldn't read at first, and then I could: it was the look of a man recognizing a choice he'd already made himself, reflected back from an unexpected direction. He'd burned the ledger instead of the deed. He'd kept the truth and destroyed the weapon. I was doing the same arithmetic in reverse.

"You'd make a terrible outlaw," he said quietly.

"I'd make a worse one than you," I said. "You at least tried."

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of the assay office, which had somehow remained half-standing at the edge of the slide path, tilted like a hat knocked askew. Pim got a stove working out of the wreckage and declared it "the finest piece of engineering in the territory," which was generous, since it smoked like a politician denying things. We ate tinned beans and salvaged hardtack and drank real tea, because the Syndicate had imported actual Ceylon tea in tin canisters, and if there is one law older than property law it is that spoils go to the survivors, and we had survived.

Sarel lay by the fire with his splinted leg propped on a crate, shivering in blankets, alive. Every so often his eyes would find Yara across the flames, and Yara would be looking somewhere else, deliberately, the way you look somewhere else when looking anywhere is costing you.

Pim broke the silence, because Pim always breaks the silence eventually; it's why we kept the kid along, besides the radio.

"So what happens now?" Pim asked. "To the deed, I mean. And the camp. And—" a gesture that took in the smoking stove, the ruined valley, the buried company, the general catastrophe. "Everything."

"Now," I said, "the deed goes to the territorial court in Landfall. With my testimony. And Sarel's. And yours—" I pointed the spoon at Pim, "—because you caught their chatter on the wire, and that's evidence, and you're going to write it all down tonight while you remember it, timestamps and all."

Pim groaned. "I hate writing things down."

"You're a radio tech."

"Which is why I *talk* into things. Talking is free. Writing is labor."

"Then talk," Yara said, "and I'll write. My hand's steadier anyway." He flexed it as he said it, and I saw him wince — the burns from the burning ledger, the ones he'd earned choosing justice over survival price. He caught me noticing and gave me a small shrug, the shrug of a man who has decided his scars are receipts and he intends to keep them.

"And the camp?" Pim persisted. "The Syndicate's other camps? There's a whole division downstream, there's brokers in three towns—"

"One thing at a time," I said. But even as I said it, I was looking at the dark mouth of the valley, where the slide had come down, and beyond it the long line of the Escarpment catching the last light, ridge after ridge going violet and then gray, my grandfather's grant, my inheritance, the ground itself, patient as a creditor.

One thing at a time. But the list was long, and for the first time in my life, I was holding the pen.

* * *

Near midnight, Yara sat down beside me at the edge of the firelight. Sarel was asleep — genuinely asleep, or committed enough to the performance that it amounted to the same thing. Pim had finally surrendered to writing things down and was scratching away by stove-light, tongue between teeth, narrating timestamps under his breath like a prayer.

"He'll testify?" Yara asked, nodding at Sarel without looking at him.

"He'll testify. Fear makes better witnesses than courage ever does. Courage wants to be admired; fear just wants it to be over."

Yara huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You've changed," he said. "Six weeks ago you were a case in a cave with a knife."

"I was a case in a cave with a knife and a very good reason." I pulled my blanket tighter. "You changed too. Six weeks ago you were a man who signed away his winter laughing at the wrong moment."

"How do you know about that?"

"Pim talks. Constantly. About everything. You should hear what he says about your driving."

"My driving saved all our lives at least twice."

"According to Pim, it ended them at least five times, and the mountain simply declined to cooperate."

That got a real laugh out of him, short and rusty, the kind of laugh a man produces when the machinery hasn't been used in a while. Then it faded, and he looked into the fire, and I understood he was looking at Odell's absence, which was shaped exactly like a man, and always would be.

"I owed him," Yara said. "Not money. Something else. He came upriver knowing what I was, and he covered the pass anyway, and I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't know how you carry that."

"You don't," I said. "You just don't put it down. It gets lighter. Not light. Lighter." I thought of my father, dead in the flood the Syndicate released to drown a claim dispute, and of the years I'd spent carrying that, and of how the weight had changed shape but never left. "And some mornings you wake up and forget it's there for almost a minute, and then you remember, and that minute is the whole point. That's what we're testifying *for*. Other people's minutes."

Yara was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Halvorsen got out. Through the service tunnel, ahead of the slide. The scouts said he had a head start and a horse."

"He'll surface somewhere. Brokers always do. New town, new ledger, new pen." I fed a stick to the fire. "But he'll surface into a territory where the deed is in open court and his own hired guns are giving depositions. We didn't kill the Syndicate, Yara. We made it *legible*. Everything it did is written down now, in handwriting it doesn't control. That's slower than a landslide. It's also permanent."

He nodded slowly. "Justice begun, not finished."

"Did I say that out loud?"

"No. But you had the face for it. You've had the face for it since the scout camp." He glanced over at Sarel's sleeping shape, then away. "Does it ever stop wanting the other thing? The quick thing?"

I considered lying, decided he'd earned better. "No. But it gets quieter. And you get faster at reaching for the pen before you reach for the knife. That's all growing up is, I think. Speed."

Above us, the Escarpment stood black against the stars, settled now, quiet, holding its new scar the way it held all its old ones — without complaint, without forgetting. Somewhere downriver, in three towns, clerks were opening ledgers that no longer meant what they used to mean. Somewhere south, a broker with dust on his coat was riding hard for the border, and every mile he rode made his story smaller and ours bigger.

Pim looked up from his notes. "Hey. Ilse. How do you spell 'Halvorsen'? For the deposition."

"H-A-L-V-O-R-S-E-N," I said. "Get it right. He's going to be hearing that name in courtrooms for years."

Pim grinned and bent back to the page. The stove smoked. The fire popped. And far off down the valley, so faint it might have been the mountain settling, or ice shifting, or nothing at all, I heard — or thought I heard — a single long note of sound rolling up from the lowlands, like a whistle, like a train, like the future arriving early and impatient, asking whether we were ready to come down and say our piece.

We would be. In the morning, we'd walk out of these mountains owing nothing but answers, and I intended to give every single one of them freely.
