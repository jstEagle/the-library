## 21. Landfall

The river gave us back to the world in stages.

First it was the color — brown water going green, then green going gold with silt and sunlight, the way a bruise heals in reverse. Then it was the smell: pine smoke and cold stone giving way to cut hay, woodsmoke from actual chimneys, someone frying onions in a kitchen that had never once been buried under an avalanche. Then it was sound, which was the strangest part. Dogs. A church bell that had gone rusty at its one job. Pim laughing at something, high and cracked, like a boy remembering how.

We came down off the last ridge on foot because Odell's boat was somewhere behind us under forty feet of new lake, and because none of us trusted wheels yet. The road, when we found it, was a miracle of boredom: two ruts, a mailbox, a hand-painted sign advertising FRESH EGGS that had faded to FRESH E— which Pim declared the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen and photographed twice.

Ilse walked beside me with the case strapped across her chest like a bandolier. She hadn't let go of it since the mountain stopped trying to kill us, which is to say since roughly an hour after we left the syndicate base, when the settling dust told us the Escarpment had finished its arithmetic. She carried it the way other people carry sleeping children — carefully, but without thinking about it anymore. It had become part of her breathing.

"You're limping," she said.

"I'm walking with character."

"Your left boot is held together with radio wire."

"Pim's best work. I'm thinking of having it framed."

She snorted, which by Ilse standards was a standing ovation. Three weeks ago she'd pointed a broken flare gun at my face in a cave and asked me how much the syndicate was paying me to drag her down the mountain. Now she catalogued my injuries with the fond exasperation of a woman who'd already decided I wasn't worth abandoning, only possibly worth shooting later if I got sentimental.

The town, when it finally arrived, was called Ferrow's Landing, though there was no Ferrow anyone remembered and no landing to speak of, just a gravel bar where the ferry used to tie up before the ferry drowned or retired or married rich. It had a general store, a courthouse with a bell tower, and a population that came out onto its porches to stare at us the way you'd stare at four people who'd walked out of a story they'd only half believed.

We must have been a sight. I was wearing one boot and half of another man's coat. Pim had frostbite scars on his cheeks he'd wear the rest of his life and seemed, disturbingly, proud of. Ilse looked like famine's bookkeeper. And trailing behind us, wrists still raw from the rope the defected scouts had cut, came Sarel Vance, who had not spoken a full sentence in two days.

* * *

The territorial magistrate was a woman named Corliss Hume, and she received us in a courtroom that smelled of floor wax and old paper, which after three weeks of snow and kerosene was so luxurious I nearly asked if I could sleep there.

She listened. That's the thing I remember most about her — she listened the way surveyors measure, completely, marking everything. Ilse opened the case on her bench and unwrapped the oilcloth, and there it was: the original land grant, vellum gone tea-colored at the edges, the seal intact, the names legible. Marchetti. Her grandfather's name in ink that had outlasted every attempt to make it disappear.

"The Vellum Syndicate filed mining claims on this land eleven years ago," Ilse said, "on the basis of a title search that concluded the original grant was lost. It wasn't lost. They knew where it was. They sent a survey team upriver last spring to find it and destroy it, and when the team read what they were carrying—" she paused, and I watched her decide how much truth the room could hold "—they tried to bring it home instead. The syndicate's response is buried under the east ridge now. Along with most of their ledger."

"Not all of it," Pim said, and set a water-stained, fire-scraped bundle of pages on the bench. Two scouts named Brack and Ovid had pulled it from Halvorsen's strongroom while the ceiling came down around them, an act of larceny I intend to defend as heroism for the rest of my natural life. Every debt. Every forged signature. Every name the Syndicate owned, including mine, including Sarel's, including — I learned this in the tunnel, reading by flashlight while the mountain groaned — including Magistrate Hume's late brother's.

Her face didn't change when she saw it. But her hand rested on the cover for a long moment, flat, the way you'd touch a grave.

"I'll need statements," she said. "From all of you. Written, sworn, and detailed." Her eyes came to me. "You first, Mr. Crane. You have the longest history with these people."

I owed nothing now except answers. It turned out answers were heavier than I expected — or maybe lighter, and it was the carrying that had been the weight all along.

* * *

They put us up at the Ferryman's Rest, which had one working stove, a landlady named Tanta who fed us like we were her personal project, and a bath situation that reduced Pim to tears of gratitude he made no effort to hide. I soaked until my fingers pruned and my left boot — replaced, temporarily, with a pair of Tanta's dead husband's galoshes two sizes too big — sat drying by the stove like a small rescued animal.

Ilse found me in the parlor that evening. I was writing my statement by lamplight, ten pages in, and discovering that telling the truth in longhand was harder than telling it out loud. Out loud, the words leave and you're done with them. On paper they sit there, looking at you.

"How far did you get?" she asked.

"To the part where I signed my winter away to the Syndicate because I laughed at the wrong moment and Halvorsen thought it showed spirit."

"That's very honest."

"It gets less flattering. Wait till you reach the chapter where I tell myself recovering stolen documents isn't robbery."

She pulled a chair around backward and sat on it the wrong way, arms folded on the rest, chin down, watching me write. Outside, the first real thaw dripped off the eaves in a rhythm almost like rain. Almost like the lowlands' version of forgiveness.

"When do you leave?" she asked.

I kept writing. "Court sits Thursday. Testimony takes as long as it takes. After that—" I shrugged. "Storm season starts early this year. There's a cell building over the basin already; Pim swears he can feel it in his fillings."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

She waited. Ilse was very good at waiting; she'd had three weeks of practice doing it in a cave with a flare gun and a grudge.

"I don't know," I said finally. "A month ago I would've said the answer was money. Pay the debt, buy the truck back, get upstream of my own life again. But the debt's ash, and the truck's under a rockslide, and it turns out I don't miss either one." I turned the pen over in my fingers. "My father died in a flood the Syndicate engineered to drive claimants off their land. I spent twenty years chasing storms because weather was the only thing that ever scared me more than they did. Seems stupid now. Weather never once lied to me about what it wanted."

"And now?"

"Now I owe nothing except answers, and I've given most of them. What's left over—" I gestured vaguely at the room, the lamp, the dripping eaves, her. "Leftover's new territory. I'd want a map."

"I'm a surveyor," Ilse said, and the corner of her mouth moved. "Mapping's the family business."

* * *

Thursday, the courthouse filled past standing room. Farmers who'd lost grazing rights. Widows of men who'd died in the mines with their wages garnished into nothing. Two of Halvorsen's own hired guns, sitting in the back with their hats on their knees, come to hear the ledger read aloud in daylight where nobody could shoot them for it.

I testified for six hours. I told them about the slashed tents and the instruments arranged in careful rows. About the burned cache and the claw marks too deliberate for animals. About Odell, who took a bullet at the col and spent his last breath naming the traitor, and who had steered us upriver through falling light humming a song he never finished. I watched Sarel sit through all of it with his hands flat on his knees, taking his sentence like a man accepting a bill he'd known was coming. He'd confessed everything the night after the slide, unprompted, in a voice so quiet Pim had to light a lantern to be sure he was talking at all. Survival is arithmetic, he'd told me on the descent, and I'd told him arithmetic was how the Syndicate counted people, and he'd nodded like a student who'd finally understood the lesson a term too late.

Magistrate Hume asked me one question at the end. "Mr. Crane, you were sent upriver to steal these documents. Why didn't you?"

I thought about lying, just to see if I still could. It felt like trying on an old coat.

"Because a man offered me a trade," I said. "Burn the deed, walk free, forget the mountain. And I realized I'd been making trades like that my whole life — small ones, reasonable ones, each one costing a little more than the last. The mountain taught me the exchange rate." I looked at Ilse, sitting straight-backed at the plaintiff's table with the grant laid out before her like a heartbeat on a chart. "Some things aren't currency. You spend them, you don't get change."

* * *

The verdict came down Friday noon, quick as weather: the mining claims void, the grant confirmed, the Syndicate's territorial charter suspended pending investigation, warrants issued for Halvorsen — fled, they said, south over the pass with whatever he could carry, which knowing Halvorsen was probably a folding chair and a grievance.

The town celebrated the way small towns do, which is to say everyone cooked too much and told stories slightly louder than necessary. Tanta closed the Rest to strangers and opened it to us. Pim got drunk on two glasses of cider and gave a toast to Odell that started as a eulogy and ended, through some alchemy of youth and cider, as a sea shanty that half the room joined. Brack and Ovid drank ginger ale in the corner, unemployed men with clean consciences for the first time in years, looking like dogs who'd wandered into a better family's yard and weren't sure they'd be allowed to stay.

I stepped out onto the porch near midnight. The sky over the valley was doing something enormous and slow — clouds stacking up over the basin in ranks, lightning ticking inside them like a clock winding itself. Spring storm. First of the season. My hands itched the way they always did.

Ilse came out and stood beside me, and for a while we just watched the weather rehearse.

"They'll want you to testify again," she said. "In the capital. The charter case. Months of it, maybe."

"I know."

"You could say no. You've given enough."

"So have you, and you'll be there anyway."

"That's different. It's my name on the vellum."

"It's my signature in the ledger," I said. "Turns out we're both documents."

She laughed — actually laughed, full voice, the first time since the cave — and the sound went out into the dark like something released. Below us, the town slept. Above us, the storm gathered its courage. Somewhere south, a man with a folding chair was learning what it cost to run from a mountain that remembered.

"I'm walking out at first light," I said. "Before the roads get soft. Pim's staying — Hume wants him running the court's radio wire, and honestly the boy deserves a roof. Sarel goes to trial, does his time, and I'll testify for leniency, because mercy's a wound I apparently enjoy giving myself." I turned to look at her. "You could come with me. Partway. There's high country east of here nobody's surveyed properly since the quake. Good storms. Terrible lodging."

Ilse looked at the storm a long moment. Then at the case, sitting inside on the bench where she'd finally, deliberately, set it down — the first time in a year it had been anywhere but against her body.

"First light," she said. "But I'm carrying the map."

* * *

We left as the sky broke open, warm rain on the gravel, the whole valley smelling of wet earth and beginning. Behind us the courthouse bell rang out the hour, rusty and earnest, still bad at its one job. Ahead the country climbed toward the Escarpment, green and patient, keeping its accounts.

And if the land remembers longer than we'd like — well. These days, so do I.
