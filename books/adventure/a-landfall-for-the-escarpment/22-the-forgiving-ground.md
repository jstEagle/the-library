## 22. The Forgiving Ground

Spring came to the Escarpment the way Ilse's grandmother used to say forgiveness came: slowly, unevenly, and never quite where you expected it.

She stood on the porch of the old Marchetti homestead with a tin mug of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the meadow do its stubborn work. The snow had retreated up the slopes in ragged tiers, leaving the lower ground black and sodden and then, within the space of two weeks, absurdly green. Crocuses first, pushing up through the mud like small insistent questions. Then the grass, thick and shameless. Then the wildflowers her mother had planted along the fence line thirty years ago, coming back from seed nobody had bothered to save, as if the ground itself had kept a copy.

The territorial court's ruling had arrived in April, folded into an envelope so thin it seemed an insult to what it contained. *The grant of 1897 is affirmed. The claims of the Vellum Syndicate are void.* Eleven words that had taken four generations, one winter, one avalanche, and one burned ledger to earn. Ilse had read the letter standing in the post office at Landfall, and the postmistress — a woman named Dree who had known Ilse's mother and therefore felt entitled to opinions — had said, "Well. About time somebody in this valley got good news," in the tone of someone handing back a borrowed umbrella.

Mining had stopped. That was the strangest part, how quickly it stopped. The syndicate's equipment still sat up on the benchlands where the slide had buried the camp — the dredges and the drilling rigs, the trestle works, all of it frozen mid-gesture like toys abandoned by a child called to dinner. There was no money left to move them and no legal right to use them, and so they rusted in place, orange-brown and patient, becoming part of the view. The mountain, Ilse thought, had simply picked them up and put them in its pocket.

She drank her coffee and let all of this sit in her chest, where it had been sitting all spring, too large to swallow and too true to spit out.

* * *

The homestead had needed everything. That was the honest way to put it. The roof had needed half its shingles, the well had needed a new pump, and the kitchen had needed a woman with a crowbar and a grudge against forty years of neglect. Ilse had done the work herself through April and May, because there was no one to hire and, more truthfully, because her hands needed to be busy or they would start shaking.

Pim had come up for two weeks in late April, ostensibly to help with the roof and actually, Ilse suspected, to be somewhere that wasn't the lowlands, where the inquiry was still grinding through its slow machinery. Pim was a different creature at altitude. In town the boy — no, Ilse corrected herself, not a boy anymore; the winter had settled that question — Pim was quiet and watchful, flinching at radios. Up here, Pim talked. About the survey instruments they'd recovered from the silent camp, which Ilse had cleaned and oiled and set up again in the south window. About the transcripts of the syndicate chatter, which Pim had annotated in a small furious hand and delivered to the territorial magistrate personally. About Sarel Vance.

"He asked about you," Pim said one evening, hanging shingles with the grim efficiency of someone who had discovered that manual labor was cheaper than grief. "In the letter. Whether you'd speak at the hearing."

"What did you tell him?"

"That it was your mouth." Pim drove a nail. "I didn't tell him what I thought he could do with the rest of it."

Ilse had thought about Sarel more than she wanted to. He was alive — broken in body, one leg pinned and plated, and broken in some other way too, the way a man is broken when the arithmetic he built his whole life on turns out to have been written in the wrong book. He had testified, in the end. Halvorsen was still at large somewhere south with whatever was left of the syndicate's money, but Sarel had sat in a wheelchair in a courtroom in Landfall and told the truth in a flat voice for six hours, and when they'd asked him why — why he'd sold them and then saved them, in whatever order a man does such things — he had looked at his own hands and said, "Because I did the sums twice, and the second time I counted myself in."

Ilse had not forgiven him. But she had sent him, through Pim, a packet of seeds from the meadow, with no note. She wasn't sure what it meant herself. Maybe that was the point. Some things you couldn't say, so you posted them in a small brown envelope and let the other person do the math.

Odell had no grave, so the river was his grave, and in spring the river ran high and loud below the homestead, and Ilse found that she could sit by it now without turning her head. In the fall it had been different. In the fall every sound of water had been a name.

* * *

The deed itself lived in the courthouse now, under glass, and people came from three valleys over to look at it, which Ilse found privately hilarious. It was, after all, just a piece of vellum with a wax seal and her great-grandfather's name on it — a name he'd bought with money made in ways the family didn't discuss. The land had never cared whose name was on it. The land had been here before the grant and would be here after the court, and it had demonstrated this past winter with a thoroughness no lawyer could match.

But the paper mattered to people, so she went down for the final hearing in May, and she gave her testimony, all of it — the silent camp, the case, the winter, the ledger she'd read aloud to the scouts until two of them put down their rifles and asked, in voices like children, *is that really what we're owed?* She gave the names of the dead. Odell. The survey team. The two scouts, Ferrow and Adeyemi, who had walked them through the service tunnels and then stood up in the courtroom and confirmed every word.

The magistrate asked her, at the end, what remedy she sought.

"Remedy," Ilse said. "That's a word for debts. What I want is the land left alone. What I want is for the men who are still owed money to be paid it. What I want—" and here she had paused, because the true answer was strange, "—is for the mountain to be allowed to finish what it started."

She hadn't been able to explain that to the court. She wasn't sure she could explain it to herself. But standing on the porch now, in the green roar of June, she thought she understood it a little: the slide had not just destroyed a camp. It had settled an account. The Escarpment had been keeping its own ledger all along — every ton of rock the syndicate's blasts had loosened, every cut into the slope, every debt of damage — and in February it had called the whole balance due at once. The wilderness forgets less than people hope. She had learned that the hard way, at thirty below, with the wind trying to take her name off the face of the earth.

But it forgives more than they deserve. That was the other half, the half she was still learning. The meadow had forgiven the dredge tracks. The creek had forgiven the tailings, running clear again above the old camp. Even the burned supply cache site, upriver, was green now, soft with new willow, as if fire had just been another kind of plowing. The ground took everything people did to it and held it, and held it, and then — given half a chance and a spring — grew something over the top of it. Not forgetting. Growing over. Which was maybe the same thing done honestly.

* * *

Yara Crane left on a Tuesday, because Ilse later learned he considered Tuesdays lucky, on the grounds that nothing bad had ever specifically happened to him on one.

He came up from the lowlands the day before, on foot up the old pack trail, carrying everything he owned in a pack that Ilse judged — professionally, with a surveyor's eye — to weigh perhaps forty pounds. He had a new jacket. That was the first thing she noticed, and the second thing was that it was already scuffed, because Yara Crane could not own a nice thing for longer than a day without testing it against a fence or a rock or the business end of his own optimism.

"You walked up," she said, from the porch.

"Trucks and I are in a period of separation," he said. "It's not permanent. We just need space."

"Your truck is at the bottom of a river."

"My truck is *resting* at the bottom of a river. There's a difference. One of us is being dramatic about it and it isn't the truck."

She made him eat at the kitchen table, because that was what you did, and he told her about the lowlands in the way he told everything — sideways, in pieces, with the important parts disguised as jokes. The inquiry was winding down. The magistrate had asked him whether he wanted restitution for his services, and Yara had said he wanted his debts audited, and it had turned out that with the ledger burned and the syndicate's records buried under a hundred feet of slope, there was nothing left to audit. The Vellum Syndicate, such as it still existed, could no longer prove he owed it anything.

"So you're free," Ilse said.

"I'm *unencumbered*," Yara said. "Free is a bigger word. I'm working up to it."

He was going south, he said. Storm country. There was a whole season coming on the plains below the range — the big cells, the ones that walked across the flatlands like slow gods — and there was money in chasing them if you were good, and Yara was good, and more to the point Yara could not be happy anywhere the sky wasn't trying to kill him. It was, Ilse realized, the same condition she had. She needed a mountain that might fall on her. He needed a sky that might. They were both, in their different ways, people who only felt honest in weather.

"You could stay," she said. It surprised her coming out. "Through the summer. There's survey work. The territory will want the slopes mapped now, properly, after the slide. I could use—"

"Ilse." He said it gently, which from Yara was so unusual it stopped her mid-sentence. "If I stay, I'll start being useful. And if I start being useful, I'll start being needed. And I have been needed my whole life by people who kept books on it." He turned his mug slowly on the table. "I'd like to find out what I am when nobody's counting."

She looked at him for a long moment. "That's the smartest thing I've ever heard you say, and I resent it."

"Write it down. It won't recur."

* * *

In the morning she walked him as far as the fence line, because that was where her ground ended and the trail began, and it seemed right to say goodbye at the edge of the thing that had caused all of it.

The meadow was loud with bees. Somewhere up-slope, the snowmelt was talking to itself in the creeks. Yara shouldered his pack and stood there a moment in the sun, squinting up at the Escarpment the way a man looks at an opponent he's decided to respect.

"It's a good mountain," he said. "Terrible neighbor, but it keeps its word."

"Most people around here would call that a compliment."

"It is. The highest kind I give." He stuck out his hand, and she took it, and his grip was warm and brief and entirely without ceremony, which was the most Yara Crane thing about him. "Marchetti."

"Crane."

"If the sky ever does anything interesting down south, I'll send word."

"Don't. I'll know." She nodded up at the ridgeline. "It travels."

He laughed — the real one, the one she'd heard maybe four times, the one that started in the chest — and turned, and went down the trail with that rolling, unhurried walk of his, forty pounds of everything he owned swinging easy, getting smaller against the green. She watched until the trail bent at the birches and took him, and then she stood a while longer anyway, because some departures you honor by not hurrying.

Then she went back up the path to the house, past the crocuses gone to seed and the wildflowers just opening, past the survey instruments gleaming in the south window, past the fence posts her mother had set that were holding, still, after thirty years, doing the one quiet job they were made for.

Behind her the mountain stood in the morning light, scarred and green and keeping its accounts. The snow was still up there, high in the hanging valleys, in the couloirs the sun wouldn't reach until August — and Ilse knew, the way her family had always known, the way you know a debt in your own ledger, that some of it would still be there next winter. The ground kept everything. It forgot less than people hoped.

But it had let her come home. It had let the grass come up through the dredge tracks, and the creek run clear, and a man walk out unencumbered into a new season with nothing in his hands. It forgave more than they deserved.

She filled her mug, sat down on the porch step in the sun, and opened the survey notebook to a fresh page. The slopes would need mapping. The summer would need her. And somewhere south, under a sky gathering its first big storms, a man who owed nothing was walking toward the weather.

She wrote the date at the top of the page, and under it, in her mother's habit and her own hand: *Ground holds. Sky moves. Begin.*
