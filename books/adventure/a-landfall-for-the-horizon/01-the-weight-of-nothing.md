## 1. The Weight of Nothing

The salt flats began where the world's patience ended.

Rosalind Marchetti had been walking since before dawn, and in all that time the land had offered her exactly three things: glare, silence, and a set of footprints that belonged to a man who did not know she was following him. The prints were good ones—deep at the heel, rolling off the outside of the boot, left leg favoring an old injury—and they stitched away northward across the crust like a sentence someone had started writing and then abandoned.

She crouched over them now, one knee on the salt, and let herself feel the small satisfaction of a tracker reading clean ground. Then she stood, and the satisfaction drained away, because the ground would not stay clean. By noon the wind would come up off the dry lakebed and sandpaper every trace of him off the face of the earth, and after that she would be tracking nothing across nothing, which was its own particular kind of madness.

She checked her water. Two liters and a swallow. She had planned for two days; the thief had made it three by moving faster than a man with a stolen compass had any right to move.

*Your father couldn't plan his way out of a paper bag,* her mother used to say, back when saying such things was still allowed, before the disgrace got so large it filled the house. *All those maps, all those surveys, and the man gets lost in his own reputation.*

Rosalind drank a mouthful, no more, and capped the flask with the care other people reserved for handling explosives.

The flats stretched to a horizon so flat it looked ruled. Heat stood on the salt in shimmering columns, and through them the distant hills to the north wavered like a mirage of hills, which was what they were, mostly—except that somewhere past them was the river country, the flooded lowlands, green and secret, and somewhere in that green was a man carrying her father's compass out of the desert like a thief carrying a piece of her own spine.

She walked.

* * *

Her father had taught her to track on ground like this. Not these flats specifically—he'd never brought her here, though now she wondered if that had been deliberate—but ground just as empty, just as unforgiving, the year she turned eleven. He'd knelt beside a line of jackrabbit prints and said, *The desert doesn't hide things, Rosie. People do. The desert just tells the truth about them afterward.*

He'd been a surveyor then. A government man with a brass instrument case and a creased uniform shirt and a name that opened doors. Two years later the doors closed, all of them at once, and the name became a thing people lowered their voices around. Falsified reports, they said. Incompetence, they said. A survey of the eastern watershed so wrong it had cost the ministry a season and a fortune, and old Hartwell had stood in the hearing room and said almost nothing in his own defense, which everyone took for guilt, because innocent men protest.

Rosalind had been fourteen. She had watched him come home and take the compass off his belt—the heavy brass one, the one with the dented lid he thumbed when he was thinking—and put it in a drawer, and not touch it again for a decade.

She had taken it from that drawer the day she earned her ranger's badge. He'd been dead eight months by then. She'd told herself it was inheritance. Standing alone on the salt now, with the sun climbing toward its noon cruelty, she admitted what it actually was: she had taken the compass because taking it felt like picking up the argument he'd never finished, and she had spent nine years trying to win it for him without ever once knowing what the argument was about.

The service had given her what the disgrace had taken: a uniform, a post, a reason to be looked at without flinching. That was the bargain she'd made, and she'd kept her side of it scrupulously. Fourteen years of patrols, of flood warnings run on time, of poachers turned back and settlers pointed to safe ground. The badge sat against her chest like a second heartbeat.

Which was why the theft report had gone in within the hour. Stolen property, one brass compass, value nominal. Which was why she had requested leave instead of waiting for the constabulary, and ridden hard for two days, and was now walking into the emptiest place in the territory after a thief who had, by every reasonable measure, gotten away.

Reasonable measures. Her father had been convicted by reasonable men.

She walked.

* * *

At midday she found where he'd stopped.

It was a shallow scrape behind a shelf of crusted salt—a camp of one, hours old, marked by the ash of a tiny fire built from dried reeds carried in rather than gathered, because there was nothing here to gather and he'd known it. A careful man, then. She studied the fire ring, the flattened sleeping hollow, the way he'd pitched nothing because there was nothing to pitch, and revised her picture of him upward. Thieves who planned their fuel were thieves who planned everything else.

Beside the fire ring, pressed into the salt as if dropped and hastily retrieved, was a scrap of paper.

She knew it was bait before she touched it. No careful man left paper behind. But she picked it up anyway, because the alternative was standing in the heat wondering, and wondering was more expensive than walking into a trap.

A hand-drawn map fragment. Ink, fine-lined, professional hand. A river system she half recognized—the lowlands, the great bend, the sump lakes—and along one channel a notation in a tight architectural script: a structure of some kind, drawn in section, with depth figures. And beneath it, smaller: *the old works are not on any chart.*

No signature. No mark of the ministry. The paper was good stock, government issue, but the drawing was done by someone who loved the line work, someone who drew the way her father had drawn, with an engineer's precision and a cartographer's tenderness.

Rosalind stood very still on the burning salt and felt the first cold thread of something that was not suspicion go through her.

Thieves did not carry maps like this. Thieves stole purses and ledgers and, apparently, compasses. This was a surveyor's fragment. This was—

She folded it away, checked the tracks leading out of the little camp, and found that they bore north-northeast now, angling toward the river country with new purpose. As if whatever he'd been waiting for or working toward had resolved, and he was moving.

So was she. The wind was coming up. She had perhaps four hours of readable ground.

* * *

The memory came at the worst hour, the dead flat stretch past three o'clock when the sun stopped being weather and became a fact of geology.

She was sixteen, and her father was dying slowly in a chair by the window, and she had asked him—the question she'd been carrying for two years, thrown down finally like a gauntlet—*Did you do it? Did you falsify the survey?*

He had looked at her for a long time. Not hurt. That was the thing she could never make peace with, even now. Not wounded, not indignant. He had looked at her the way he looked at a difficult contour line, with patience and a kind of sorrowful interest, and he had said:

*Rosie, when you're older, you'll learn that some maps are drawn to show you where things are. And some are drawn to show you where things aren't. The second kind is worth more. It's also worth more to certain people that you never see it.*

Then he'd coughed, and asked for tea, and she had gone to the kitchen hating him a little for answering a question with a riddle, and she had never asked again, and he had died in the spring with the drawer full of the compass and whatever else, and she had joined the service in the autumn because belonging somewhere was better than belonging to a question.

Now she was thirty-four, walking across a salt pan after a thief, rationing water and memory in equal sips, and the question had followed her out here anyway. It always did. The flats were honest ground. They showed you what you were carrying.

What she was carrying: one flask, two-thirds empty. One badge. One father's ruin, worn smooth as river stone from nine years of handling.

And ahead of her, closing by degrees, a man who had taken the one object that connected her to all of it.

* * *

She caught sight of him at the last hour of light, and nearly lost him again immediately, because the land changed.

The salt gave way to cracked clay, the clay to scrub, and the scrub rose in low gray hummocks toward a line of tamarisk and deadfall marking the first seepage of the river country, forty miles south of anything worth flooding. Against that broken ground a single walker could vanish between one step and the next. But she'd had four hours of his tracks and she knew his gait now the way you know a voice on the radio, and when the figure flickered between two hummocks a quarter mile out—tall, lean, moving with that favoring hitch on the left—she went to ground herself and began the last stalk properly.

Not running. Running was for people who wanted to arrive tired and meet resistance at full strength. She cut the angle, using the hummocks, letting the terrain do the work, closing the distance the way water closes on a low place. The light went long and copper. Shadows stretched east like spilled ink. Somewhere a bird called once and thought better of it.

She came around the last hummock at a walk, weapon drawn but low, and found him sitting on a fallen tamarisk trunk with his boots off, rubbing his bad ankle, a tin cup steaming beside him on a fire no larger than a cupped hand.

He didn't startle. That was the first thing she noticed, and it annoyed her more than the theft had. He simply looked up, took in the uniform, the weapon, the dust of two days' pursuit written all over her, and said, in a voice dry as the ground they were standing on:

"You're farther from your patrol route than I'd have guessed, Ranger."

"Rosalind Marchetti. District Ranger Service." She let him see the badge, then the weapon, then her eyes. "You're wanted for questioning regarding a theft in Salt Hollow. You'll return with me."

"Will I." He set the cup down with exaggerated care, as if the fate of nations hung on not spilling it. "Ezra Voss." He didn't offer a hand. "And no, I don't think I will. Not yet. Ask me again tomorrow."

"The compass. Give it back, and this becomes a conversation instead of an arrest."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear, not guilt. Recognition, maybe, or its cousin. "Marchetti," he repeated slowly, tasting it. "Hartwell Marchetti?"

The use of her father's name on this stranger's tongue landed like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall a long way before it hit bottom. "Where did you hear that name?"

"Where do you think?" He reached slowly—she tracked the hand the whole way—into his coat, and brought it out, and held it up between two fingers so the last of the light caught it.

Brass. Dented lid. Her father's thumb-worn compass, turning gently, catching the copper sun.

"It wasn't yours to steal," she said, and heard her own voice go quiet in the way it went quiet when she was most dangerous, to others or to herself.

"No," Ezra Voss agreed. "It wasn't mine. And here's the trouble, Ranger—it wasn't your father's either. Not originally." He turned the compass over, and his thumb found the dent in the lid the way her father's used to, an unconscious gesture borrowed across a stranger's hand. "I took it from the survey camp. The government team, out past the flats. Eleven days ago. Tents still pitched, instruments still running, supper still on the plates." His eyes came up and held hers, and whatever she'd expected to find in them—street cunning, thief's bravado—wasn't there. What was there was the same cold thread she'd felt at noon on the salt, the one that wasn't suspicion.

"Nobody was there," he said. "No bodies. No tracks leaving. Six men, vanished off the face of the earth, and your father's compass sitting in the chief's tent like it had been waiting for me."

The wind moved through the tamarisk. Behind him, far to the north, the first clouds of the season were building over the high country, stacked and blue-black, too far away to mean rain yet. Too far away, and getting closer.

"Give me the compass," Rosalind said.

Ezra Voss looked at her for a long moment across the small fire, and then he did something she had not prepared for. He turned it over, worked a thumbnail under the bezel, and lifted the faceplate off entirely, and held it out to her open-palmed, offering.

"Beneath the dial," he said. "Look beneath the dial. Then tell me whose compass this really is—and tell me you still want to arrest me for finding it."
