## 2. What the Compass Hides

The thief did not run.

That was the first thing Rosalind Marchetti understood as she came down off the salt ridge with her boots grinding brine-crusted sand and the last of the daylight bleeding out behind her. She had spent the whole day imagining the moment—how he'd bolt, how she'd run him down the way her instructors at the academy said a ranger should, all economy and authority. Instead Ezra Voss sat on an overturned packing crate at the edge of the flats with his back to her, elbows on his knees, watching the sun go down like a man who had paid for the view.

He'd heard her coming. Of course he had. The flats carried sound like a drum skin; she'd been audible for half a mile. He simply hadn't cared.

"Ranger," he said, without turning. "You walk loud for someone whose job is not being heard."

"Give me the compass."

"You're welcome," he said, "for the water I left you. Half your canteen was gone when you started. You'd have collapsed by noon tomorrow." He turned then, and in the failing light his face was younger than his voice—young twenties, sharp-boned, sunburned across the cheekbones, with the particular stillness of someone who had learned young that stillness kept him alive. "Sit down. You look like a fence post that's been out in weather."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He held up the compass.

Even now, even knowing what it was, her breath caught. Brass case gone dark with handling, the glass scratched into a grey haze, the bezel worn smooth on one side where a thumb had worried it for years. Her father's compass. The one thing of his that had survived the disgrace—not because anyone had given it to her, but because she'd taken it from his effects before the service could inventory them, before they could box up a man's whole life and stamp it *returned to department stores*. She had carried it through six years of patrols. It had pointed her home through two whiteouts and one flood. It was the only object in the world that had never lied to her.

And this stranger had lifted it off her belt while she slept.

"When?" she asked. Her voice came out flat, which took effort. "Last night? While I was banking the fire?"

"While you were checking the horse lines," he said. "You do everything in order. Fire, water, lines, perimeter. Same order every time. A man could set a watch by you." He turned the compass over in his fingers. "I almost didn't take it. Then I saw the maker's mark and I couldn't leave it there."

"The maker's mark."

"Verraneau and Sons, Low Basin Foundry." He said it carefully, the way a man handles something fragile. "There were four compasses made from that batch. My father worked the bench that finished them. He used to talk about them—the survey instruments, the government contract, how the brass had to be hand-lapped so the needles wouldn't drag in the heat." His eyes came up to hers. "One of those four belonged to a surveyor named Hartwell."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped stone.

"My name is Marchetti," she said.

"I know what your name is. I read your saddle tag while you slept, same as I read your water ration and your ammunition count." He wasn't smiling. "But that compass has been in a Hartwell's pocket since before you were born, or I'm a mule. And I've been looking for it for three years."

* * *

She made him build the fire. Not because she needed warmth—the night would be mild, the way desert nights are mild when the day has cooked every degree out of the air—but because she wanted his hands occupied while hers stayed free, and because a firelight interrogation favored whoever was angrier, and she intended to be angrier.

He built it well, small and hot, screened by the crate so the glow wouldn't carry. That told her things too. This was a man who had lit many fires he didn't want found.

"Start again," she said. "From the beginning. Where you got the idea that compass was yours to steal."

"It isn't mine to steal. It's mine to find." He fed a twist of dry scrub into the flames. "Three years ago my brother Tomas took a government contract. Survey work, lowlands division. Good money, better than anything on the river, and Tomas was good with instruments—he had our father's hands. He wrote me twice from the field. Second letter said they'd found something in the valley that didn't match the official charts. Said if he didn't come home by the end of the season, I shouldn't wait."

"And?"

"And he didn't come home." The words were level, but his jaw tightened around them. "No body. No effects returned. The ministry sent a form letter saying his contract had been *concluded*. Concluded. Like a sentence." He looked at her across the fire. "So I started asking questions in the kind of places where questions get answered. Took me two years to learn that the survey team's equipment manifest had been sealed. Another few months to learn that one item had been pulled from the seal before it closed. One item, transferred out under a private requisition, signed by—" he paused, savoring it, or maybe flinching from it, "—signed by a ranger named Dominic Hartwell. Thirty years ago."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the flats, wind moved over the salt crust with a sound like paper turning.

"That's impossible," Rosalind said. "My father died eleven years ago. If he signed a requisition thirty years ago, fine—that's before me. But the compass has been mine since his death. I've carried it every patrol since I was nineteen."

"Then explain the second serial number."

"What?"

Ezra reached into his coat—not fast, deliberately slow, showing her his palm first—and drew out a small flat tool, a jeweler's driver with a blade no wider than a hair. "When I got the compass open tonight, under that face plate, there's a second serial stamped inside the case. Verraneau compasses were double-marked for the survey contract. Outer number for the department ledger. Inner number for the foundry book." He held the compass up between finger and thumb, the firelight catching the scratched glass. "Outer number says it was issued to D. Hartwell, field survey, thirty years ago. Inner number says it was re-cased twelve years ago. New face, new needle pivot, old bones." He turned it so she could see the bezel, the worn spot where a thumb had rubbed it shiny. "Somebody loved this instrument enough to rebuild it rather than replace it. Was that you?"

"No." Her mouth had gone dry. "I never opened it. There was no reason to—it worked."

"There's always a reason to open a thing," Ezra said. "Most people just don't want to know what's inside."

He set the compass on the crate between them, and took up the driver again, and looked at her—a question, offered. She should have said no. Every protocol she owned said no: evidence stays sealed, recovered property gets logged, you don't let a confessed thief disassemble government-adjacent material by firelight in the open desert. But protocols were the service's voice, and the service had just been described signing away her father's compass under a private requisition, and Rosalind found that the voice had gone suddenly distant, like a radio losing signal.

"Do it," she said.

* * *

The face plate came away easier than it should have. Ezra worked the driver around the bezel's seam with the patience of a man defusing something, and the scratched glass cover lifted free on its gasket, and beneath it—where any honest compass would show nothing but the card and the pivot pin—there was a disc of thin brass, fitted flush, engraved.

Not a maker's mark. Not a serial.

A map.

Rosalind leaned in until the heat of the fire pressed against her face. The engraving was fine, machine-cut but annotated by hand in a draftsman's slanted capitals, and age had darkened the lines so they stood out black against the metal. She recognized the conventions immediately—she'd grown up with her father's field maps pinned to the kitchen walls, had learned contour lines before she learned cursive. A river, running diagonally across the disc. Tributaries. A gorge marked with hatching. And downstream of the gorge, a symbol she knew from flood-control surveys: a rectangle with a stepped line through it.

A spillway. An old one, drawn in a style the ministry hadn't used in decades.

Beside it, in letters smaller than the rest, a single word: *VERITAS*.

"That's not on any chart," she said quietly. "I've flown that valley. I've boated that reach twice a year for flood inspection. There's no structure below the narrows. Nothing but canyon."

"Then either the engraver was a liar," Ezra said, "or the charts are."

She wanted to argue. She reached for the argument the way you reach for a handrail in the dark—and found nothing there. Because she had boated that reach, and she remembered now, remembered something she'd filed away years ago as an anomaly and never followed up: a stretch below the third narrows where the current ran strangely smooth, where the echo off the canyon wall came back wrong, doubled, as though the sound were falling into a space that shouldn't exist. She'd logged it. The log had come back to her stamped *no action required*, initialled by someone in the district office whose initials she'd never thought about again.

Until this minute.

"Who engraved it?" she asked. "Your father?"

"No. The foundry did plain instruments." Ezra turned the disc toward the light, tilting it so the shadows deepened in the cuts. "This was done after. Look at the hand annotations—they're cut over the original machining. Someone took a finished compass apart and hid a map inside it, and then put it back together so well that nobody noticed for thirty years." He glanced up. "Except the man who rebuilt it twelve years ago. Except maybe the man who ordered it rebuilt."

Her father's hands. She could see them suddenly, absurdly clear—big knuckles, a burn scar across the left thumb from a camp stove, turning the compass over and over on the kitchen table the winter before he died, when he'd started drinking again and wouldn't say why. She'd been seventeen. She'd asked him what he was looking at, and he'd said, *the past, Rosie. It keeps better than people think.* She'd assumed he meant the bottom of the bottle.

"He knew," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Whatever this is—whatever's down in that valley—he knew, and he hid it where no one would look, inside the one thing they let him keep."

"They let him keep it," Ezra repeated slowly. "That's an interesting way to put it. Almost like it was a condition."

The word hung between them. *Condition.* Rosalind thought of her father's last years—the silence, the shame that the whole district wore like weather, the way other rangers' children had been gently steered away from her at school. *Disgraced.* That was the word everyone used. He falsified his flood reports, they said. He took payment to mis-survey the lowlands. He was a cautionary tale told to every cadet: this is what happens when a man sells his instrument.

And here was his instrument, hiding a map to something the current ministry's charts insisted did not exist.

"Ezra." She used his name for the first time, and watched him register it. "The survey team. Your brother's team. What exactly were they surveying?"

"Water," he said. "Same as always. The ministry's been pushing a dam project in the lowlands for years—everyone knows, nobody says. Three villages in the flood path, bought out or ignored. But the project kept stalling, because the original hydrology surveys from thirty years ago don't support the numbers. So two seasons ago they sent a fresh team to re-survey." He tapped the compass, once, lightly. "Tomas's team. And according to his letter, they found something in the field that didn't match the official charts. Something old. Something that changed the math."

"The spillway."

"Maybe. Or whatever the spillway was built to hide." He wrapped the face plate back over the disc, seated the glass, and pressed the bezel home with his thumb until the compass was whole again—an ordinary battered instrument, telling time by the stars like any other. "Here's what I can't figure, Ranger. Your father signed this compass out of a sealed evidence transfer thirty years ago, which means it was already evidence of something. Then he kept it private for decades. Then, twelve years ago—right around when he died, you said—it was rebuilt, and somehow it ended up issued to you, a brand-new ranger, his daughter, carrying it into the exact valley where he made his name and lost it." His eyes were very steady across the fire. "That's not coincidence. That's a chain. Somebody handed you this compass on purpose. The question is whether they handed it to you as a legacy—or as a message they expected you to deliver."

"To whom?"

"To whoever's listening." He nodded downstream, toward the darkening lowlands, where the air had begun to smell faintly of green water and rot, the first breath of the flooded country. "Or maybe the question is simpler. Maybe somebody expected you to follow it. And somebody else expected you never to get this far."

Rosalind took the compass from the crate. He let her—opened his hands, leaned back, watched her weigh it. It felt the same in her palm as it had for six years, and completely different. She thought of every bearing she'd trusted it through, every storm it had walked her out of, and understood that it had never once been pointing where she thought. All that time, the true instruction had been folded underneath, hidden in brass, waiting for someone to ask the right question of it.

Her father had tried to tell her. At the end, drunk and silent at the kitchen table, turning this thing over and over—he had been trying to tell her the only way left to a disgraced man, which was without words.

"We go to the survey camp," she said.

Ezra raised an eyebrow. "We?"

"You stole from a ranger. That makes you my prisoner until I decide otherwise." She stood, brushing salt dust from her knees, and looked east, where the land fell away toward the flooded lowlands and the first stars were pricking through above the horizon. "Besides, your brother's team is camped somewhere along that river, and their last known position is on my patrol chart. If they found what your letter says they found, they're either rich, dead, or sitting on the biggest story this district has seen in thirty years."

"And if they're dead?"

"Then somebody buried them," Rosalind said, "and buried them badly enough that my father spent thirty years carving the location into a compass to make sure it surfaced." She clipped the instrument to her belt, where it had always lived, and felt its weight differently now—a weight with a direction. "Sleep. We move at first light."

Ezra studied her for a long moment, firelight moving on his face, and then, unexpectedly, he smiled—not the streetwise smirk from the salt flats, but something quicker and more genuine, gone as soon as it came.

"You know," he said, "for a woman who just found out her whole inheritance is a locked door, you're taking it calm."

"I'm not taking it calm," Rosalind said. "I'm taking it downstream."

Above the eastern ridgeline, far off, a single light blinked twice in the darkness—white, deliberate, spaced like a signal—and then went out, as if it had never been there at all.
