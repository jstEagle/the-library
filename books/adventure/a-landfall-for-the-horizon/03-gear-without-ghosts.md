## 3. Gear Without Ghosts

The camp announced itself before they saw it.

Rosalind smelled it first—kerosene and rot, a sweet-sour tang riding the dawn wind off the lowlands—and then heard it: the thin mechanical whine of a theodolite's stabilizer motor, still turning, still drawing power from a solar array that had been angled with professional care toward the eastern sky. Someone had built this camp to last. Someone had intended to come back to it.

Nobody had.

She crouched at the lip of the levee grass and studied the site for a long time before moving. Beside her, Ezra Voss had gone uncharacteristically quiet, his stolen—her—compass closed in his fist like something he was afraid to look at too closely. The floodplain spread below them in the gray half-light: six canvas tents in two neat rows, a cook shelter, an instrument tripod gleaming with dew, a radio mast guyed with wire that caught the early light like spider silk.

"Six tents," Ezra said finally. "I counted five when I ran through here."

"You ran through here?"

"I told you. I took the compass from the chief's table." He didn't look at her when he said it, which she noted, and filed away. "There were men sleeping. Four of them, maybe five. Snoring."

Rosalind looked at the tents again. All six flysheets were tied down. All six doors were fastened from the outside with the small brass toggles survey teams used to keep out wind and vermin—not locked, exactly, but shut, the way you shut a house you expected to return to.

"Then we're going to find out where they went," she said, "and why they didn't take their boots."

* * *

She made him wait while she worked, because reading ground was not a thing you could do with an audience chattering at your shoulder, and because she wanted, more than she wanted to admit, to be good at this one thing. Her father's disgrace had taken everything else—the name, the pension, the right to sit at the front table of any mess hall in the territory—but it had not taken what he'd taught her before it all came down. *The land keeps honest books, Rosie. Your job is just to read without flinching.*

She read.

The camp sat on a slight rise above the old channel scar, high enough to have stayed dry through the spring seeps, low enough that the water table was two spits deep. She circled the perimeter first, wide, letting her eyes do the walking. The ground here was black clay threaded with couch grass, the kind of surface that held a print for days if it didn't rain and erased one in minutes if it did. It hadn't rained in nine days. She knew that the way she knew her own pulse; she'd been counting the sky since the salt flats.

Nine days of preserved record. And the record said nothing.

That was the first wrong thing, and it was so wrong it took her a moment to accept it. Six men—or five, or four, whatever number had slept here—could not walk out of a clay floodplain without writing themselves into it. Not possible. Men carried weight; weight printed. Boots, wheels, the drag-marks of loaded packs, the scuffed crescents where someone had knelt to tie a lace—all of it should have been there, radiating outward from those tents like the lines of a hand.

Instead the ground around the camp was smooth as poured wax.

Not trampled-smooth. Not smoothed-over. Smooth the way ground is smooth when nothing has touched it since the last rain—which had been eleven days ago, two days *before* the camp was pitched. She knelt at the edge of the cook shelter and pressed two fingers into the soil. Firm. Undisturbed. A beetle track stitched across it in a fine dotted line, uninterrupted, running straight through where a man's footprint should have been.

"Voss," she said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "When you came through here. What was under your feet?"

He'd drifted closer, drawn despite himself by the change in her tone. "Mud. Sucked at my boots. I left prints a hand deep."

"And they're gone."

He frowned and dropped to a crouch beside her, and she watched him see it—the beetle line, the unbroken crust, the absence where his own passage should have been written. Something moved behind his eyes that she recognized, unwillingly, as fear.

"That's not weather," he said. "Weather doesn't pick and choose."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "It doesn't."

* * *

Inside the camp, the wrongness compounded like interest.

The cook shelter held a cast-iron stove, cold now, its firebox raked clean with a care that bordered on ritual. On the prep table sat food in various stages of surrender: a sack of rice gone soft and gray, tins bulging gently at their seams, a wheel of hard cheese wearing a pelt of white mold thick enough to hold its own shape. Flies had found it, bred in it, died in it. The smell hit the back of her throat like a physical blow.

"Nobody eats this and walks away hungry," Ezra said, holding his sleeve over his nose. "And nobody leaves cheese to die unless they think they're coming back tomorrow."

"Or unless they left in a hurry."

"In a hurry they'd have taken the cheese." He said it with the flat authority of a man who had stolen food professionally and knew its logistics better than most quartermasters. "Cheese is money out here. Rice is money. You don't leave money on a table."

She moved tent to tent while he watched the treeline, and each one told the same impossible story. Bedrolls laid out, some still bearing the long body-shaped compression of sleep. Personal gear stowed with military neatness—boots paired at the foot of each bedroll, water bottles full and stoppered, a folding razor, a tin of pipe tobacco, a paperback novel swollen with damp and abandoned at page ninety-one. In the fifth tent, a framed photograph lay face-down on a crate: a woman and two children squinting into sun, the kind of picture a man props up where he can see it every morning.

Face-down. As if someone couldn't bear to look at it on their way out. Or as if someone had turned it over deliberately, after.

In the sixth tent—the largest, set slightly apart, the chief's tent by every convention of survey-camp hierarchy—she found the instrument table, and stopped breathing for a moment.

The theodolite stood on its tripod under a rain cover, and its stabilizer was still running. She put her ear close and heard the faint hum, felt the almost imperceptible tremor in the tripod legs. The solar panel outside fed a battery bank whose charge gauge sat at three-quarters. The instrument had been drawing power continuously, keeping its gyroscopic heart alive, waiting for hands that hadn't come back.

Survey instruments didn't get left running. They got shut down—that was drilled into every trainee before they ever touched one, because a stabilized gyro left spinning unattended drained batteries, wore bearings, and occasionally, in temperature swings, cracked its own housing. Leaving one running was the kind of mistake a green second-year made once and never repeated.

Five experienced men had made it anyway. Or had been made to seem to.

On the table beside the instrument lay the field ledger, open, its last entry dated nine days ago. Rosalind read it twice. Routine soundings. Water-table notes in a cramped, confident hand. A sketch of the channel bend with depths penciled in. And then nothing. No closing entry, no *camp struck*, no *moving downstream to station seven*. The book simply stopped mid-thought, mid-sentence even—one line trailing off after the word *elevations*, the pencil resting in the gutter of the pages as if its owner had meant to come back after lunch.

She looked at the pencil for a long time.

"Ezra," she said quietly. "Come look at this. Tell me what you see."

He came. He read faster than she'd expected—he'd struck her as a man who survived on his feet and his nerve, not letters—but his face when he finished was pale in the greenish light filtering through the canvas.

"It stops," he said. "Like they got up from the table."

"Mid-word."

"People don't leave mid-word."

"No," she said. "They don't."

* * *

By midmorning she had walked the whole site three times, expanding her circles wider each pass, and the result never changed. Within the camp itself, the ground bore the prints of exactly one recent visitor: Ezra Voss, size ten, left-side gait favoring the right knee, entering from the north and leaving the same way. His trail was a week old, faded but legible, and it ended where the erased ground began—as if he'd stepped into the camp and the camp had swallowed his tracks behind him.

Outside the camp: nothing. No path to the latrine trench except one set of prints, Ezra's. No wood-gathering trails to the dead firewood pile. No tracks to the river for water, though the water bottles were full. No drag marks, no vehicle ruts, no hoofprints, no boat landing worn into the bank. Six men had lived here for at least a week—she counted the layers of ash in the fire pit, the depth of the latrine, the beaten patch in front of the cook shelter—and then they had risen one morning, eaten nothing, packed nothing, shut nothing down, and ascended into the air like smoke.

Except smoke leaves a smell. This left nothing at all.

Rosalind stood at the center of the camp with her hands on her hips and felt the strange, vertiginous sensation of her own competence turning against her. Ten years in the ranger service. Ten years of reading sign, of tracking stock thieves across the mesa country and smugglers through the cane brakes, of finding the story the ground always told, because the ground always told it. The ground was the one witness that couldn't be bribed. Her father had taught her that. Her father, whose own field reports had supposedly been sloppy, self-serving, fabricated—

She shut that door firmly. Not now. Not yet.

"There's blood somewhere," Ezra said behind her. He'd been circling too, in his own fashion, checking the tents' back walls, the guy-lines, the places a nervous man checks. "There has to be. Five men don't vanish. Either they walked, or they were carried, and either way something bled or something dragged."

"Nothing dragged. Drag marks chatter." She demonstrated with her hand skimming the turf. "A body leaves a stuttering line, breaks in the grass stems, smears when the soil's wet. There's none. And if there'd been a fight—" she nodded at the nearest tent, its canvas wall sagging intact, "—canvas tears, poles snap, guy-lines part. This camp was never touched. It was *left*. Perfectly. By people who apparently floated."

"So what does your scripture say, Ranger?" There was an edge under the flippancy. He was scared, and covering it, and she found she didn't blame him, because she was scared too, and covering it worse.

She looked out over the floodplain, at the brown river sliding past the distant cane, at the clouds beginning to stack in the far southwest like masonry. Then she said the thing she'd been avoiding saying since her third circuit of the site.

"It says someone didn't want us to know which way they went. And that means whoever took them is organized enough, and careful enough, and close enough, to have come back here with brushes and brooms and all the time in the world—to erase a week of five men's living from clay ground, without disturbing a single tent flap." She paused. "Ezra. Erasing takes longer than making. Whatever happened here, someone spent hours afterward making sure it never happened. That's not panic. That's policy."

The word hung in the wet air between them. Policy. Institutions had policies. Governments had policies. Rangers had policies, stamped and numbered, and she had spent her whole career believing that policies were what stood between the frontier and chaos.

Now the word tasted different.

* * *

It was Ezra who found the last thing, because he was looking where she wasn't. She was reading the ground; he was reading the camp, the way a thief reads a house—for what's missing rather than what's present.

"The weapons," he said.

She turned. "What about them?"

"There aren't any." He gestured at the tents, the crates, the instrument locker standing open on its hasp. "Five government surveyors, working alone in flooded lowlands on the edge of disputed country, and there's not one rifle rack, not one sidearm, not a single box of cartridges. I've robbed enough camps to know what they carry. Even the mapping crews carry shotguns for snakes." His voice had dropped. "Their guns are gone, Marchetti. Everything else is here. Their boots are here. Their tobacco is here. Their family photographs are here. But the guns went with them—or the guns went somewhere else first."

Rosalind stood very still.

An orderly camp. A running instrument. An open ledger. Full water bottles and rotting food and no tracks leading anywhere, and no weapons anywhere at all. Each detail alone was odd. Together they assembled into a shape she didn't want to look at directly: a scene not abandoned but *arranged*, composed like a stage set by someone who understood exactly what a search party would expect to find and had provided it, minus the one element that would tell the truth.

"They want it to look like a retreat," she said slowly. "Sudden recall, hasty departure—no. No, a hasty departure would have tracks. They want it to look like—" She stopped, because the answer arrived all at once, cold as well water. "They want it to look like nothing. Like the team was never really here at all. A camp nobody can prove was occupied. Men nobody can prove existed. If we hadn't had Voss's memory of snoring men and my compass pointing downstream, we'd stand here and doubt our own eyes."

The compass. Ezra's hand had gone to it again, unconsciously, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. She stared at it—at her compass, her father's compass, the brass case worn bright along one edge by thirty years of a thumb she knew better than anyone's—and felt the dread in her chest shift, deepen, acquire a second floor beneath it.

Because the camp had been erased by professionals. And her father had been destroyed by professionals. And the compass that both of them had risked something to keep was sitting in the palm of a thief, humming, if brass could hum, with the same frequency as all of it.

"We're leaving," she said. "Now. Before whoever swept this place decides to sweep us."

"Which way? The tracks say nowhere."

"The tracks lie. The map doesn't." She nodded at the compass. "Open it. Show me that etching again, all of it, and show me slow. Because whatever these men found that was worth erasing them for—" she looked south, toward the drowned lowlands and the villages she could not yet name, "—I think my father found it first."

Ezra hesitated, thumb on the case. And then, from the chief's tent behind them, clear across the empty camp, the radio crackled to life.
