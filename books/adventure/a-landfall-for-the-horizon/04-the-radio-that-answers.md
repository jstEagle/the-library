## 4. The Radio That Answers

Ezra Voss had spent eleven years learning which silences meant danger, and the survey camp was humming with all of them at once.

The tents stood in their neat rows like men waiting for an order that never came. The solar array still tracked the sun on its slow axis, faithful as a dog. A kettle sat on a cold burner beside two enamel mugs, one of them half full, and the tea inside had gone to a skin of green mold that told him three weeks, maybe four. Nobody drank half a mug of tea and walked away from it. Nobody walked away from a camp this well built at all—not without taking the instruments, not without burning papers, not without leaving so much as a boot print heading out.

But there were no prints. He'd watched Rosalind Marchetti prove it twice.

She moved through the camp now the way he imagined surgeons moved—hands behind her back, eyes doing all the touching. She crouched at the edge of the compound where the hardpan began and read the ground the way he read rooftops: for weight, for direction, for the story that had been written on it and then scrubbed out.

"Nothing," she said again, to no one, to herself. "Wind's been light for a month. Anything heavier than a jackrabbit would show." She straightened. "There were six people here. Six people leave tracks."

"Maybe they flew," Ezra said.

She gave him a look that could have stripped paint. He'd been collecting those looks since the salt flats, and he'd decided somewhere around dawn that he liked them better than most people's gratitude. At least her contempt was honest. Everything else about her—the badge, the procedure, the way she said *the Service* like it was a church—was a kind of performance he'd seen a thousand times in a thousand government offices, usually right before someone's paperwork got lost or someone's brother got disappeared.

He kept his hands loose and his mouth shut and drifted toward the command tent, because that was where the radio was, and because a man who'd grown up running messages through the river districts had never yet met a radio he didn't want to put his ear against.

* * *

The set was a military-surplus transceiver bolted to a folding table, the kind the Ministry issued to field teams and then pretended not to know how they'd been used. Its dial glowed a soft amber even in daylight, drawing power off the camp's batteries, waiting. Someone had left the volume up. Someone had expected to be called.

Ezra touched the casing. Warm. Not sun-warm—circuit-warm. It had been transmitting recently, or listening hard.

"Don't," Rosalind said from the tent flap.

"It's already on."

"That's not the point. It's evidence. Chain of custody. If we touch it before it's documented—"

"Documented by who?" He turned, leaning his hip against the table. "Your Service? The same Service whose survey team vanished out of a sealed camp like smoke through a grate? You going to file a report, Ranger? To whom, exactly?"

Her jaw worked. He watched her reach for the answer she'd carried across forty miles of salt flat—the answer that had kept her walking when the water ran low—and he watched her find it thinner than she remembered. *I'll report to my superiors.* Four words, and they weighed nothing out here.

"The regional office," she said finally. "Deputy Director Halloway. He was my father's—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "He'll want to know."

"He'll want to manage it," Ezra said. "There's a difference. I've been on the wrong end of managed my whole life."

"You've been on the wrong end of your own choices your whole life. There's a difference too."

He grinned at her, because it was easier than admitting the line had landed somewhere under his ribs. "Then let's both be wrong together. Come listen. If I'm about to commit tampering, you can arrest me after. Consider it evidence gathering."

She came into the tent like a woman wading into cold water. Up close, in the amber glow, he could see what the flats had done to her—cracked lips, sunburn peeling at the nose, the fine tremor in her hands she thought she was hiding. She was running on discipline and maybe a liter of water a day. So was he. It made them even, more or less, and he found he minded less than usual being even with a law officer.

He keyed the receive side gently, not transmitting, just opening the channel. Static poured out, the valley's evening voice—all its dust and distance compressed into white noise.

Then, beneath it, a carrier tone. Someone was holding a frequency open somewhere. Waiting for this camp to answer a call that would never come.

"They're pinging it," Ezra murmured. "Every few hours, I'd guess. Checking if anyone's alive to pick up."

"Or checking if anyone else has picked it up," Rosalind said quietly.

That was the thing about her, he was starting to understand. Underneath the badge and the by-the-book armor, she had a mind that went straight down the dark stairwells. She just hated what she found there.

They waited. The static breathed. Somewhere outside, wind worried the tent guy-lines with small patient fingers, and the sun went down behind the western ridge and turned the canvas walls the color of old blood.

At 19:42 by the camp's own clock, the radio answered.

* * *

It came in clean—too clean, a signal with money behind it, relayed off some high tower with more wattage than any regional station in the basin deserved. The voice that followed was male, middle-aged, clipped in the way that officials are clipped when they're reading.

*"Survey Team Kestrel, Kestrel, Kestrel, this is Ministry Directorate Three on behalf of the Office of Water Resources. Be advised: your survey mandate is rescinded effective immediately. Stand down all operations. Abandon equipment in place. Proceed by fastest available transport to the capital for debriefing. Acknowledge on this frequency within twelve hours. Repeat: acknowledge within twelve hours. This message repeats."*

It repeated. Ezra let it play through twice, watching Rosalind's face in the amber light.

She looked relieved. That was the first thing—she actually looked relieved, shoulders dropping a centimeter, breath coming easier. Of course she did. Here was the institution speaking, the great machine she'd given her life to, and the machine had an explanation. *Rescinded. Debriefing. Stand down.* Bureaucratic language was a warm bath; you could drown in it comfortably.

"You see?" she said. "There's the answer. They were recalled. Officially. That's why the gear's here—they were ordered to abandon it. That's why—" She stopped, because he was shaking his head, and something in the way he did it made her stop.

"No," Ezra said.

"No?"

"That's not the chief." He nodded at the speaker grille. "Whoever that is, that is not Dr. Aris Thorne."

Rosalind blinked. "You knew the survey chief?"

"I knew *of* him. My brother—" The word caught, the way it always caught, a fishhook behind his sternum. He swallowed past it. "Dario wrote letters. Real ones, paper, because he said the Ministry read everything electronic. He complained about Thorne constantly. Affectionately. Thorne talked too much, Thorne sang off-key on the trail, Thorne had a stutter on his R's that got worse when he was tired, and Dario imitated it so well I could hear it through the page." He tapped the radio. "That man has no stutter. And more than that—he reads like a clerk. Thorne was a scientist. Scientists argue with orders. Even when they obey them, they argue first. That message wasn't read by a man who'd ever argued with anything in his life."

"Ezra." Her voice had gone careful, the tone you use near an edge. "A recorded message wouldn't—"

"It wasn't recorded. Listen." He held up a hand. The message was cycling again, and on the third pass he caught it, the thing his ear had snagged on the first time and refused to release: a faint wet click between *rescinded* and *effective*, the sound of a dry mouth working. "Live. Somebody's standing in a room right now, reading this over and over, every few hours, hoping nobody's listening. Why would a recall order need to repeat live? Why not a scheduled broadcast, why not a courier, why not a letter to the regional office—which, by the way, is where any legitimate recall would go first, not out here to a camp that's supposed to have six radios' worth of redundancy?"

Rosalind opened her mouth. Closed it. He watched the institutional trust and the ground-reader's instinct fight it out behind her eyes, round after round, and for once he didn't push. He just waited, the way you wait on a rooftop for the mark to make his move.

"Twelve hours," she said at last. "It wants acknowledgment within twelve hours."

"Yeah."

"If the team were alive and free, they'd have acknowledged days ago."

"If the team were alive and free," Ezra said, "they'd have walked out. There's no tracks, Ranger. Six people don't get recalled. Six people get *collected*." He heard his own voice flatten on the word and didn't try to soften it. He'd spent years keeping that particular flatness sheathed. Tonight it came out bare. "And whoever collected them wants to know if anybody saw."

* * *

She didn't argue after that. That frightened him more than arguing would have.

They made camp in the surveyors' mess tent because neither of them suggested it and neither of them objected—a tacit treaty signed in silence and shared tinned peaches Rosalind pronounced safe by some ranger's alchemy of smell and date-stamp. Outside, the first stars came up over the ridge, and the radio in the command tent cycled its dead order to a camp of ghosts, patient as a spider.

Ezra sat sharpening his knife against a stone he'd picked up on the flats, not because the blade needed it but because his hands needed the motion. Across the low table, Rosalind laid out her things with parade-ground precision: water skins, the Service-issue field kit, a folded photograph gone soft at the creases. She caught him looking and, to his surprise, turned it toward him.

A younger Rosalind, maybe nineteen, standing rigid beside a heavyset man with gray in his beard and a ranger's hat worn the old style, brim pinned up on one side. Both of them squinting into the same sun. Neither smiling.

"Your father," Ezra said. "The disgraced one."

"My father." She took the photo back. "Everyone in the Service knows the name Marchetti. Falsified survey data, they say. Recommended protections for a watershed that didn't exist. Cost the Ministry two million in cancelled contracts before they proved the maps were invented." Her thumb moved over the crease. "He died before the inquiry finished. Conveniently."

"You believe he lied?"

"I believe—" She exhaled. "I believe I've spent nine years proving I'm not him, and I don't know anymore whether I was proving him guilty or proving everyone else right." She looked up. "Why are we talking about this?"

"Because you're scared," Ezra said, "and when you're scared you reach for the Service like a rosary, and I want to know what happens to you when the rosary breaks." He set the knife down. "Because tomorrow that radio expects an acknowledgment, and we're the only ones left to give it or withhold it, and whichever we do, somebody on the other end is going to start asking questions about two extra people in this valley. Because your Deputy Director Halloway is either going to send help or send Corliss—I'm guessing you know which—and I'd like to know, before that happens, whether you're going to stand with the badge or stand with the six people who vanished from this camp."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered—the monsoon clearing its throat, weeks early, the whole sky out of schedule just like everything else in this drowned valley.

"I don't know," Rosalind said.

It was, he realized, the first honest thing she'd said since the salt flats. He respected it more than any speech.

"Good," he said. "Neither do I. That makes us even again."

* * *

They took turns sleeping, and it was Ezra's watch when the radio changed.

Not a new message. A new *silence*. At 03:17 the carrier tone dropped away mid-cycle, cut clean, and the frequency went truly dead for the first time since they'd arrived—and then, thirty seconds later, a different signal slid into its place. Weak, distant, battery-starved, the kind of transmission that had traveled a long way on dying power.

A voice. Thin, warped by distance and interference, but carrying the cadence of a man reading coordinates.

*"—any unit receiving, this is Thorne, Aris Thorne, Survey Team Kestrel, we are—"*

Static swallowed it. Ten seconds of roar. Then, barely, like a face under floodwater:

*"—not recalled. Repeat, we were not recalled. Whoever answers that frequency, do not acknowledge, do not return to—"*

Gone. The channel collapsed back into ordinary static, and did not come back.

Ezra sat very still in the dark, the knife forgotten in his hand, his heart going like a fist on a door. In the corner of the tent, Rosalind was already awake—he could tell by her breathing, had known for an hour, the way you know things about people you've decided to survive alongside—and when she spoke, her voice had no institutional warmth left in it at all.

"The compass," she said. "Show me the map again. All of it. Where does that signal come from?"

Ezra turned the little brass instrument over in his fingers, thumb finding the hidden seam, the etched lines beneath the face that pointed downstream, into the flooded country, toward a mark his brother might be breathing behind.

"Downriver," he said. "Past the villages. Into the part of the map the Ministry says doesn't exist."
