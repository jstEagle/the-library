## 5. Downstream of Silence

The river told them where to go before the compass did.

Rosalind had learned to read water the way other people read letters—her father's doing, back when he still had a daughter's attention to waste on such things. Moving water spoke. It said *depth* in the color, said *obstruction* in the way it braided and healed itself downstream of whatever broke it. This morning it was saying something she didn't want to hear.

"Look at the banks," she said.

Ezra crouched at the edge of the channel, forearms on his knees, and studied the mud with the expression of a man who distrusted everything on principle. "Mud."

"Mud at what height?" She pointed. "See the stain line? That's not seasonal rise. That's a controlled release—water held back somewhere upstream and let go all at once. Twice, maybe three times this season." The marks ran clean along both banks, level as a carpenter's string, and no flood made lines that honest. Floods were sloppy. Grief was sloppy. Only engineers drew straight lines through a living valley.

Ezra wiped his palms on his trousers and stood. "Test releases," he said. He'd used the phrase twice since the survey camp, turning it over like a coin he suspected was shaved. "That's what the paperwork would call it."

"That's what the paperwork *did* call it." She'd found the memo in the camp's document case, wedged under a rotting bedroll: *routine flow management trials, lower basin, authorized.* Signed by someone three ministries away from anything that ever got wet. "Routine," she repeated now, and heard how flat her voice had gone.

They walked. The compass rode in Ezra's breast pocket—he'd refused to surrender it again that morning, and Rosalind had stopped asking, mostly because watching him check it every hour like a man checking a wound told her more than any argument would have. The needle swung as they moved, and beneath its glass face, when the light came sideways, the etched lines appeared: a river's course rendered in hairline scratches, and branching from it, a spur of older markings that matched nothing on any chart she'd been issued in eleven years of service.

The spur pointed downstream. So they went downstream.

The land changed around them as they walked, the way country changes when it's already halfway to becoming something else. Salt scrub gave way to cane, cane to stands of tamarisk, and the soil darkened until each footstep left an impression that filled slowly with seep water, like the ground was bleeding from below. Birds she knew from the highlands were absent. In their place: herons, standing too still, and once—a thing that stopped them both mid-stride—the sound of a bell.

A single bell, far off, tolling without rhythm. Not ringing for service. Ringing the way a bell rings when the rope is tied off and the wind works the clapper, or when there's no one left who knows how to stop it.

Ezra looked at her. She looked at the compass. Neither of them said *village*, because saying it would make the next hour real.

* * *

They smelled it before they saw it. Not decay—not yet, the water was too cold and moving too fast for that—but the particular sourness of drowned houses: wet plaster, soaked grain, lamp oil spreading in rainbow skins across the current. Then the roofs.

They came around a bend of cane and the village simply *was*, or had been. Low stone houses in two rows facing the water, their walls standing to the windowsills, doors hanging open or gone entirely. Water moved through the street where a street had been, brown and patient, carrying a chair, a section of fence, a child's sandal turning slowly like something deciding whether to surface. A fig tree stood in what had been a courtyard, submerged to its middle branches, its leaves going yellow in a ring at the waterline.

Nobody screamed. Nobody ran. That was the first horror—that there was no one to do either.

"Kel—" Ezra started, then stopped. "No. This isn't Kel Anoor. This is further down. Serat, maybe. Or what was Serat."

"You know these villages?"

"I know *of* them." His voice had gone careful, the cynicism stripped out of it, which frightened her more than the drowned street did. "Everyone downstream knows the names. You don't think about them being *places*."

Rosalind stepped to the waterline and did the thing she was trained to do, the thing that had earned her the badge she wore inside her shirt like a splinter: she read the ground. Debris line—two meters above the current level, meaning the release had crested and fallen. Silt laid down in even sheets, not scouring, meaning the water had come up slow enough to be survivable if you had high ground, fast enough to drown anyone asleep on a ground floor. Dates were harder, but the fig tree's yellowing gave her a week, maybe ten days. Ten days ago, a government memo signed three ministries away had turned this street into a canal.

"No tracks," she said quietly. It came out before she meant it to, an echo of the survey camp, and she hated herself for it. But it was true here too—or true in its opposite. There were tracks, dozens of them, but all of them led *away*: boot prints and cart ruts and bare feet pressed into the drying mud above the debris line, a whole village's worth of departure fanning out toward the ridge road. People had run. People had gotten out, some of them. What she couldn't find—and she circled the entire settlement edge looking, kneeling, parting the cane—was a single print leading back in. No rescue parties. No assessors. No one from the service she belonged to had come to count the dead or the living, and that absence had a shape, and the shape was deliberate.

"They were moved out ahead of it," Ezra said behind her. "Partially. Look—some of these prints are days older than the flood. They were *told*."

"Told what?"

"That it was a drill. That the dam authority needed an evacuation rehearsal." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "My brother used to say the ministry's favorite word was *temporary*. Temporary closure. Temporary relocation. Temporary measures." He looked at the water moving through the doorway of a house where someone had hung a charm against the evil eye, and the charm was underwater, spinning gently. "Nothing about this is temporary."

Rosalind's badge felt hot against her sternum. Eleven years she'd worn the service's trust like armor over the old wound of her father's name, and here was the service's signature written in silt across a village of four hundred people, and the armor fit differently now. Tighter. Like something that could become a shroud if you held still long enough.

"We should search for survivors," she said, because procedure was the last solid thing she owned. "High ground, outbuildings, boats—"

"There." Ezra wasn't looking at the village. He was looking past it, upstream along the flooded street, where the roofline of what might have been a granary broke the current—and beside it, impossibly, a thread of smoke.

Thin. Gray. Alive.

* * *

The smoke rose from the upper story of the granary, whose ground floor was a lake and whose ladder was a rotted suggestion. They reached it by wading, chest-deep in the last stretch, Rosalind holding her pack overhead while the current tried to negotiate it away from her. Ezra went first and hauled himself through a window whose frame crumbled politely under his grip.

Inside: hay gone to mold, sacks split and sprouting, and in the corner farthest from the wet, a small iron stove vented through a hole knocked in the wall, and beside the stove, wrapped in three blankets and a tarpaulin, an old woman feeding a fire with broken furniture.

She didn't startle. She looked up at the two dripping figures in her doorway with the mild irritation of someone interrupted at a chore, and said, "You're late."

Rosalind blinked water out of her eyes. "Late?"

"The relief boat." The woman gestured with the chair leg she was using to stoke the fire. "They promised a relief boat. I've been counting days on the beam there." She nodded at a row of notches cut into the rafters, thirty-one of them, precise as a clerk's ledger. "Thirty-one days. Either the boat is very lost or the ministry is very embarrassed, and I've decided to stop waiting and start eating my house instead." She indicated the furniture pile. "It burns poorly. The table was better than the chairs. Sit, you're dripping on my floor."

"It's a lake," Ezra said.

"It's my floor." And there was something in the way she said it—flat, unarguable, rooted—that made Rosalind understand this woman had decided the water was a temporary guest and she was permanent, and no amount of evidence would move her.

She was seventy if she was a day, small and dense as a knot in timber, with white hair cut short for practicality and hands that had clearly done fifty years of work and intended another twenty. Her eyes were the problem. They were clear, unhurried, and they went from Rosalind to Ezra to the pack on Rosalind's shoulder with an assessment that took perhaps two seconds and missed nothing.

"You're not relief," she said. "Relief wears uniforms. You—" her gaze settled on Rosalind, "—you wear yours under your shirt. Ranger. And you—" Ezra, "—you walk like a man who's stolen something recently. Don't argue, I raised six sons." She poked the fire. "So. Either the ministry has started sending thieves and rangers instead of boats, or you came following that." She pointed at Ezra's breast pocket.

The compass.

Rosalind felt the moment tilt. "How do you know about the compass?"

The old woman set down the chair leg. She looked at Rosalind for a long time, and something moved behind her eyes—grief, yes, but grief that had been carried so long it had become structural, load-bearing, part of the house.

"Because I helped bury it," she said.

* * *

Her name was Marta. She wouldn't say more at first—wouldn't say much of anything until they'd eaten, because she was firm on the point that no conversation of consequence should happen between hungry people. So they ate mold-trimmed grain cakes and tea brewed from tamarisk needles, sitting on the granary's dry loft boards while the flood moved through the streets below with the sound of a crowd murmuring in its sleep, and gradually, in the way of people who have survived something together even if the something was only an afternoon, the story came.

"I was born in Serat," Marta said. "My mother was born in Serat. My grandmother dug the foundation stones of the mill with her own hands. Thirty-five years ago, a young surveyor came through with instruments and a tent and a manner about him that made the old men laugh and the young women look twice." She smiled briefly, and the smile was a door opening onto a room Rosalind suddenly didn't want to enter. "He was polite. He was thorough. And he was frightened, though he hid it well—I only understood later, when I saw the same fear in better-hidden men. He asked questions the ministry hadn't sent him to ask. About the old spillway. About the heights of things."

Rosalind's tea had stopped halfway to her mouth. "What was his name?"

"You know his name." Marta said it without cruelty, which was worse. "You have his chin. You hold your head the way he held his when he was measuring something and didn't want to be interrupted."

The loft seemed to sway. Rosalind set the cup down carefully, because her hands had developed opinions of their own. "My father surveyed the eastern basins. That's public record. He was dismissed for falsifying—"

"For reporting," Marta said. "For reporting what he found. There's a difference, and the difference has a price, and your family paid it." She leaned forward. "The valley had an old spillway, girl. Older than the ministry, older than the dam plans, built by our grandparents' generation when the first reservoir failed and drowned the low fields. Your father found it. Documented it. Sent it south with a recommendation that made certain men in the capital recalculate their profits, because a spillway means limits—you can't build a dam bigger than what the escape route allows. So they buried the report. And when he wouldn't stop asking after it, they buried him too. Not in the ground. In disgrace. Disgrace holds better."

The stove ticked. Below them the water murmured. Rosalind sat very still, because if she moved she was afraid the whole architecture of herself—ranger, daughter of a disgraced man, loyal servant of the service that had given her a place to stand after the world took her father's name away—would come apart along seams she'd never known were seams.

"He gave me the compass," she heard herself say. "When I got my commission. He said—" Her voice caught on the memory, thirty years of misreading compressed into a sentence. "He said, *it points where I couldn't go.* I thought he meant it sentimentally. I thought he was being bitter about his career."

"He meant the map," Marta said. "And he meant the truth. Both. That man never wasted a word, which is why they were able to ruin him—one honest man with few words is easy to misquote." She turned to Ezra. "And you. Thief. Where did you get it?"

"From her," Ezra said, and Rosalind watched him decide, visibly, to tell the truth—watched it cost him something. "I stole it from her kit at the waystation. I thought it was just a good compass. Then I saw the etching under the lens and I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "Someone I'm looking for was on the survey team that vanished upriver. The compass map leads to places the charts don't show. That's all I'll say yet."

Marta studied him the way she'd probably once studied weather. "Then you'd best hear the rest," she said, "because the rest concerns him too."

She told them about the survey team. Six of them, come through Serat in early spring, official papers, polite manners, setting up instruments on the ridge. And she told them about the evening the strangers arrived—not ministry men, not exactly; men with clean boots and a document case and a way of asking questions that weren't questions. The team left the next day, ahead of schedule, and the villagers noticed that two of the six looked like men walking to their own funerals and pretending it was a transfer.

"And then the releases began," Marta said. "Small ones, at night, always at night. The river would rise a meter and fall. Rise and fall. Practicing, we thought. Rehearsing. And then one morning the notice came—*temporary evacuation for flow management trials*—and half the village believed it, because believing the ministry is easier than believing you're being erased. We walked out. Some came back for their goods. Some never left at all—old Ferro swore the water would never take his house, and the water obliged him by taking the house with him in it, or near enough; we never found him." Her voice stayed level throughout, and Rosalind recognized the technique because she used it herself: levelness as a load-bearing wall. "I came back. Someone should be here. Someone should see it and remember the order things happened in, because forgetting the order is how they win. First they flood the record. Then the valley. Then the people, if the record stays flooded."

"Why tell us?" Rosalind asked. "You don't know us."

"I know the compass." Marta reached out, and Ezra—after a glance at Rosalind—passed it to her. The old woman held it in both palms like something warm-blooded. "Your father stayed with us a month, that spring thirty years ago. He mapped the spillway entrance from our boats. And on his last night he sat where you're sitting and he told me: *if I'm ruined, and someday someone comes asking with this in hand, tell them where the key fits.*" She closed her fingers over the case. "He called it the key your father buried. He said the valley would need it again, and that the valley keeps its own records—in stone, in old mouths, in things the ministry can't requisition." She opened her hands and offered the compass back to Rosalind. "Take it. Finish what he started. And when you find the others—the team, the missing ones, whoever your thief is hunting—remember that they'll be kept somewhere the charts show as nothing. The ministry is very good at nothing. It's their most productive product."

Outside, the light had begun to fail, and with it the weather turned its face. Rosalind stood at the granary window and looked south, where the horizon had spent all day assembling something: clouds stacked in ranks, blue-black, orderly as troops, building themselves a thunderhead cathedral over the upper valley. The monsoon, weeks early. Or summoned. She was no longer sure she believed in coincidence at altitude.

Ezra came to stand beside her. "We follow the spur on the map," he said. "Upstream. The old spillway, the mine shafts, whatever's marked there. That's where they'd keep people they don't want found."

"That's where the flooding will hit worst," Rosalind said. "If those clouds mean what they look like."

"Then we'd best be fast." He paused. "Your father. All those years—they told you he lied."

"They told me he lied," Rosalind said, "and I built my whole life on the foundation of his lie. Every patrol, every promotion, every time I did it *by the book*—that was me proving I wasn't him." She watched the storm build. "And the book was written by the men who buried him."

Below them, the flood moved through Serat's streets, patient as bureaucracy. Behind them, Marta banked her fire and cut another notch into the beam—thirty-two—and Rosalind understood, with a vertigo that felt almost like freedom, that the notch wasn't counting days until a relief boat.

It was counting witnesses.

"Rest tonight," Marta said from the shadows of the loft. "Leave at first light. And girl—" Rosalind turned. The old woman's face was half firelight, half dark. "When you find what your father found, and it ruins you the way it ruined him—remember that he left you the key anyway. He trusted you with the ending. That's not a burden. That's a inheritance."

Rosalind lay down that night on moldy grain sacks with the compass open on her chest, its needle swinging slow arcs in the stove-warmed air, pointing not north but along the scratched spur of the hidden map—upstream, into the gathering dark, toward whatever the ministry had built its nothing around.

The first rain hit the roof an hour before dawn.
