## 6. A Debt in Salt Water

The fire was a mistake. Ezra knew it the moment Rosalind struck the match.

Out on the salt flats you could burn whatever you wanted and the horizon swallowed the light whole. But here in the flooded lowlands, where the ground drank everything and gave nothing back, light carried. It pooled in the hollows between the dead reeds, climbed the trunks of drowned acacias, and announced to anyone with eyes and a pair of binoculars exactly where two people who should not exist were sitting.

He said as much, and she said, "We need hot water or we'll be drinking silt," and that was that. She had a way of ending arguments before they started — not by winning them but by simply proceeding as though they'd already been won. He'd noticed it back on the flats, when she'd caught him at dusk with her compass in his pocket and her pistol level with his sternum. Even then, half-dead from thirst and three days of tracking him across country that hated travelers, she hadn't shouted. She'd just stated facts. *That's my compass. You're going to give it back. I've walked forty miles to say so.*

He'd liked her immediately, which was inconvenient for both of them.

Now she crouched at the fire's edge, feeding it sticks of driftwood bleached white by years of seasonal flooding, while the water stood black and mirror-still around the little island of dry ground they'd found. The lowlands at night were a strange country — not land, not quite lake, just a thousand shallow pools strung together like spilled mercury under the moon. Frogs sang somewhere out in the dark. Something larger moved through the water with a sound like a slow oar.

Ezra sat with his back against a fallen log and turned the compass over in his hands. Her compass. Except it wasn't hers, and they both knew it now, and that knowledge sat between them like a third person at the fire.

He'd pried the faceplate off an hour after she'd gone to sleep — or pretended to sleep; he suspected she never really slept, just banked herself like a fire — and there it was, etched into the brass beneath the dial in lines fine as spider silk: a fragment of map. A river bend. A contour line. A set of coordinates rendered in someone's careful, patient hand. And beneath it, smaller, almost hidden in the scrollwork of the compass rose:

*H. MARCHELLI — SURVEY 3RD CLASS.*

Her father. The disgraced ranger. The name every kid in the territorial schools learned as a cautionary tale, the man who'd falsified a survey, who'd been stripped of his badge, who'd drunk himself quiet in some border town and died owing money. Ezra had grown up on that story the way kids in the capital grew up on fairy tales about wolves.

And here was the wolf's own compass, carried for thirty years by his daughter, who wore her badge like armor and quoted regulations like scripture.

"You're staring at it," Rosalind said, without looking up from the fire.

"I'm thinking."

"That's new."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You want to know how I got it. You've been chewing on that since the flats. You keep circling it like a buzzard." He turned the compass face toward the firelight, let the etched lines catch the orange glow. "So ask me straight, Ranger. You're good at straight."

She looked up then. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadow, cheekbones like something carved for a monument nobody would ever build. "Where did you get this compass?"

"From the survey team," he said. "Not from you. Whatever you think you know about how it ended up in your kit — I didn't take it off you. I took it off a dead man's cot."

The frogs kept singing. The thing in the water kept moving. Rosalind didn't blink.

"The survey camp," she said slowly. "You'd been there before I tracked you."

"I lived there. Off and on. For three weeks." He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the flats, walking alone at night with her a hundred meters behind and gaining, rehearsing lies he could feed her in pieces. But lying to her felt like pouring water into sand. Everything he gave her just vanished without effect. So he'd stopped rehearsing somewhere around mile thirty, and now all he had left was the truth, which was heavier and worth less on any market he knew.

"Tell me," she said.

* * *

"My brother's name is Tomas," Ezra said. "Tomas Voss. Two years older than me, which he never let me forget, and smarter than me by a distance he also never let me forget. We grew up in the Stilt Quarter, downriver — you know it?"

"Everyone knows the Stilt Quarter."

"Then you know what it is. Water below, rot above, and the tax collectors come by boat because the streets flood eight months of the year." He shrugged, keeping his voice flat, workingman's flat, the voice he used for card games when he didn't want anyone reading his hand. "Tomas got out. That was the miracle of our family. Scholarship boy. Hydrography, of all things — measuring water. Our street measured water too, but only with our shins. He went to the capital, got certified, and came home one summer wearing boots that cost more than our roof. He said, *Ezra, one day they'll pay me to tell them where the water goes.* And I said, *Brother, everyone in this valley knows where the water goes. Downhill. Onto us.* And he laughed and threw me in the canal."

Rosalind had gone very still. Not the stillness of suspicion — he'd learned that one on the flats, and it looked different, tighter, jaw-set. This was another kind of stillness. Listening-stillness. Attentive as a heron.

"Three months ago he wrote me," Ezra went on. "First letter in a year. Said he'd been contracted onto a government survey — the lowlands watershed assessment, the big one, the one in all the papers. Said the pay was good and the work mattered. Said—" His throat tightened and he took a breath and made himself say it. "Said he'd found something in the old records. Something about the reservoir upriver. Said if anything happened to him, I should find a woman named Marchetti."

The name landed between them like a stone dropped into still water. He watched the ripples move across Rosalind's face.

"He didn't explain," Ezra said. "He wasn't a man who explained twice. But I knew the name. Everyone knows the name. So when the letters stopped — when his crew missed their resupply rendezvous and the ministry put out some statement about *schedule adjustments* — I went looking. And the first place I looked was the survey camp, because that's where he'd be, schedule adjustment or no."

"And it was empty."

"It was *full*," Ezra corrected. "That's the thing you don't understand yet, Ranger, because you read ground and I read people. That camp wasn't abandoned. Abandoned camps look abandoned — scavenged, wind-torn, doors flapping. That camp looked *paused*. Kettle on the stove. Boots by the tent flap. One man's journal open to the day's entry, mid-sentence. Like the whole crew stepped out to look at something and never came back." He turned the compass in his fingers. "Except your compass was there. On Tomas's cot. Under his pillow, wrapped in a shirt. Which means either he stole it from you—"

"Or someone gave it to him," Rosalind said quietly. "Or he was given it to hold. Or—" She stopped. He watched her do the arithmetic she didn't want to do. "My compass was stolen from my quarters at the ranger station six weeks ago. Reported it myself. Never recovered it."

"Six weeks ago Tomas was alive and at that camp."

"Yes."

"So somebody brought it to him. Somebody who could walk into a ranger station and walk out with a ranger's compass." Ezra let that hang. "Somebody inside."

She shook her head, but it was a small shake, the shake of a woman arguing with her own conclusion. "The station isn't — we're vetted. We're *vetted*."

"Corliss is vetted too," Ezra said, and watched her flinch at the name. Word traveled fast in the lowlands; the ministry man's arrival downstream had been noted by every fisherman and ferry-wife for fifty miles. "Vetted people are the easiest to buy, because nobody checks them. But that's not the point tonight. The point is—" He stopped. Here it was. The center of it. He'd walked forty miles ahead of her across burning salt to avoid saying it, and now he was going to say it to her face over a fire that was broadcasting their position to every patrol boat on the river.

"Say it," Rosalind said, because of course she saw him gathering himself. She missed nothing. It was exhausting.

"I didn't steal the compass to sell it," Ezra said. "I stole it — from the camp, from his cot — because it was the last thing he touched that I could carry. And because I'm a thief, and thieves grieve with their hands." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it and kept going anyway. "I took it because taking it was the only prayer I know how to say. And then I found the etching under the face, and the etching had his handwriting on the margin — I'd know it anywhere, he had this little hook on his sevens — and suddenly it wasn't a prayer anymore. It was a map. To wherever he thought the truth was buried. Literally buried, if the coordinates are right. The old reservoir. The one that isn't supposed to exist anymore."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the large thing in the water surfaced and sank again, unhurried.

"Tomas is alive," Ezra said. "I have to believe that, because the alternative is that I'm carrying a dead man's treasure map, and I can't—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes for a second. When he lowered it, he made himself meet her gaze. "You tracked me across the flats for a compass. I understand that. It's your father's, it's evidence, it's yours. But I need you to understand what it is to me. It's my brother. It's the only thread I've got, and I will not cut it. Not for you, not for your badge, not for anybody. If it comes down to the compass or me, take the compass and leave me, because I'll crawl after you on my hands."

Silence. The frogs filled it, absurdly cheerful.

* * *

Rosalind was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Ezra started cataloguing exits — the log, the pool beyond it, the drowned acacia whose branches might hold weight. Old habits. Then she reached across the fire, and he flinched, and she ignored the flinch and held out her hand.

"Give it here."

"Ranger—"

"I'm not taking it. Give it *here*."

He gave it. She turned it over in her fingers, thumb finding the seam where the faceplate lifted away, and studied the etched lines in the firelight with the same total attention she'd given the ground on the flats — reading it like scripture, like tracks, like weather. Then she did something he hadn't expected. She turned it so the maker's mark faced him.

*H. MARCHELLI*, it said. And beneath it, in that same fine engraving, a date. Thirty-one years old.

"This compass was issued to my father during the first watershed survey," she said. "The one they scrubbed. The one that ended with his disgrace. When he died, his effects were supposed to go to the service archive. They never arrived. I told myself they'd been lost. Misfiled. I told myself that story for fifteen years." Her thumb moved over the coordinates. "It wasn't lost. Someone kept it. Someone kept it for thirty years, and then, six weeks ago, put it into the hands of a hydrographer who'd found something in the old records. Your brother wasn't holding stolen property, Voss. He was holding a message. *Find Marchetti.*"

"Your father's dead."

"My father's *map* isn't." She looked up, and in the firelight her eyes had a brightness that wasn't entirely the flames. "You asked me once why I tracked you instead of filing a report and letting the service handle it. I told you it was procedure. That was a lie. I tracked you because when I held this compass, the first time, before it was stolen — I felt like I was holding the only honest thing my father ever owned. And I needed to know why that felt true, when everything the service taught me about him says otherwise."

Ezra stared at her. "You're telling me you doubt the official story. You. The regulation quote machine. The woman who cited the trespass statute at me *while arresting me*."

"I cited the statute because it was true," she said, with the ghost of a smile. "That doesn't mean I believe everything else I've been handed." She passed the compass back, and her fingers brushed his, brief and deliberate, a transaction completed. "Your debt and mine run parallel, Voss. Your brother vanished holding my father's compass. My father was destroyed trying to map whatever that compass points to. Same river. Same crime, maybe, thirty years apart. I'm not giving you the compass. And I'm not leaving you behind."

It was the closest thing to kindness anyone had offered him since Tomas's last letter, and it hit him harder than the pistol had, back on the flats. He looked away, into the fire, because his eyes were doing something he refused to permit them to do in front of a government employee.

"Allies, then," he managed. "Provisional. You betray me, I vanish. You know I can."

"Noted. Same terms apply to you." She pulled her bedroll closer to the coals. "Sleep in shifts. I'll take first."

He lay back against the log with the compass against his chest, under his shirt, over his heart, where it had lived since the camp. Above the drowned trees the stars came out in their cold multitudes, indifferent as bureaucrats. He thought about Tomas's laugh, the canal, the hook on his sevens. He thought about a hydrographer opening a thirty-year-old file and going pale over a contour line.

He was nearly asleep when Rosalind spoke again, softly, from her post at the fire.

"Ezra. The journal at the camp. The one open mid-sentence. What was the last line?"

He'd memorized it. Of course he had. He'd read it forty times, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into sense.

"*Level readings confirm the original spillway is intact,*" he recited. "*If Corliss learns we found it, we are all—*"

The sentence ended there. It had ended there for three weeks.

In the silence that followed, far downstream, thin and wrong against the frog-song, they both heard it at the same moment: the sound of an engine. More than one. Moving slow, moving dark, running without lights — the way boats run when they're hunting, not traveling.

Rosalind was already on her feet, kicking dirt over the fire.

"They've found the camp too," Ezra said, scrambling up. "Or they've found *us*. Either way, Ranger — we're about to learn what Corliss does with loose ends."
