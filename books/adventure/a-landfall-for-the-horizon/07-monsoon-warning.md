## 7. Monsoon Warning

The clouds came up out of the west like something being poured.

Rosalind had watched weather build over the salt flats her whole career—thunderheads that stood up straight and mean on the horizon, promising nothing but noise. This was different. These clouds had weight to them. They stacked in gray terraces from the skyline all the way down to the earth, and where they met the land the air itself seemed to darken, as if a hand were drawing a curtain across the valley one fold at a time.

She stopped on the levee road and let Ezra catch up. He was favoring his left ankle again—the old break, he'd admitted somewhere around the second day of walking—and she'd stopped pretending not to notice.

"That's not rain," she said.

He squinted at it, shading his eyes with a hand that still smelled of the survey camp's rot. "That's the monsoon deciding whether it's early."

"It's early."

"Then we'd better hope the river depot has more than a rowboat."

They walked faster after that. Neither said it aloud, but she could feel it in the way he set his jaw, in the way her own stride lengthened without her deciding it: the valley was closing behind them. Every hour the sky thickened was an hour the lowlands filled. Marta's village was already under water. Kel Anoor would be next unless someone reached it with a warning worth believing, and the only proof they had was a brass compass etched with a dead man's map and a government radio that lied in a stranger's voice.

The levee road ran along the top of an earthen berm built by some generation that had believed in public works. On their left, cane fields stood waist-high and windless, waiting. On their right, the river slid past, brown and swollen already, carrying branches and whole fence posts downstream like it was clearing its throat.

*Flood management.* The words from the camp radio kept surfacing in her mind, calm and official, a voice that had never belonged to Survey Chief Okafor. *Stand down. Return to the capital. The valley is closed.*

Closed. As if a valley could be closed. As if three villages could be padlocked and the key thrown into the reservoir.

By midday the light had gone the color of old pewter, and the first gusts arrived—not rain yet, just the monsoon's outriders, shoving through the cane in long rolling waves so that the whole field bent and hissed and rose again, bent and hissed and rose. Rosalind's shirt stuck to her back. Her canteen sloshed with maybe two swallows. She'd been rationing since the flats, and her body had begun keeping score: a headache lodged behind her eyes, a fine tremor in her hands when she held them out.

Ezra noticed anyway. He always noticed. "Depot's close," he said. "There'll be water there."

"There'll be whatever the Ministry didn't think to lock up."

"They locked up six people," he said quietly. "I don't think locks are their problem."

She didn't answer. There was nothing to say that wasn't either despair or a promise she couldn't keep, and she'd learned on the salt flats that you rationed both of those harder than water.

* * *

They heard the depot before they saw it—the groan of a corrugated roof flexing in the wind, the clank of something loose banging against something else in a rhythm too regular to be natural. Then the buildings resolved out of the haze: a long shed, a fuel store, a slipway running down into the river, and above them a signal mast with its halyard slapping the pole like applause for no one.

No lights. No smoke. No boat tied at the dock.

"No," Ezra said, before she could say anything. He broke into a lurching run along the slipway, and she followed, boots ringing on wet planks, and together they stared down at the empty mooring cleats, the coiled lines gone, the cradle tracks running bare into the current.

"The patrol boats," Rosalind said. "Corliss pulled everything upstream."

"He wouldn't leave a depot unguarded." Ezra was already moving, wrenching open the shed door. "Unless he wanted it empty. Unless—" The door shrieked back on its track and he stopped so abruptly she walked into him.

Inside, in the gloom smelling of diesel and mildew, sat a single boat.

It was a workboat, twenty-some feet, flat-bottomed, built for pushing cargo through shallow water. It sat on a trailer with one flat tire, and even from the doorway Rosalind could see the story of it: canvas half off, hull furred with bird droppings, a rope fender rotted to string. Someone had dragged it out of retirement rather than take it with them. Someone had decided it wasn't worth the trouble.

Or someone had left it deliberately, the way you leave a trap baited.

Ezra must have thought the same thing, because he stood very still in the doorway for a long moment, letting his eyes do the walking. "Check the corners," he murmured. "If Corliss wanted us here, he'd want us comfortable."

Rosalind swept the shed the way she'd sweep a campsite for sign—corners first, then sightlines, then the small places where a man could hide a wire or a man himself. Nothing. Dust lay undisturbed on the window ledges except where pigeons had been in and out. The fuel drums in the corner were sealed, though when she cracked one the smell told her the diesel inside had gone sour with age. The other drum was better. Half full, usable.

"Boat first," she said. "Paranoia after."

They rolled the trailer out onto the slipway together, the flat tire grinding, and got the hull down to the waterline by main strength and bad language. Up close the boat was worse than it had looked. The paint was blistered everywhere the sun had hit it. A seam below the port gunwale had opened enough to show daylight through when Ezra pressed it. The transom was soft around the drain fitting—you could sink a thumbnail into the wood—and the bilge held four inches of standing water that moved when they moved, like something alive and patient.

"She leaks," Ezra said, stating the obvious with the gravity of a eulogy.

"She's what exists." Rosalind hauled herself aboard and began pulling the canvas off the gear well. Underneath: two oars with split handles, a bail bucket with the bottom rusted through, a coil of polypropylene line gone brittle as straw, and a toolbox containing three screwdrivers, a hammer, and a roll of tarred marlin twine that might have been salvageable if she boiled it first. No pump. No anchor. No flares.

In a locker under the stern seat she found the supply crate the Ministry had left behind, and for one bright moment she thought their luck had turned. Then she pried the lid.

Inside: a case of compressed ration bars, expired two years, swollen tins of them straining at the wrappers. A carton of water purification tablets gone to powder. One jerrican of drinking water, sealed, blessedly real—twenty liters, maybe less with evaporation. And a stack of printed notices, crisp and new, bound in government green.

She lifted one out and read it twice because the first time refused to make sense.

*BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY OF WATER AND WORKS,* it began, *the Lower Meridian Valley is hereby CLOSED TO ALL TRAVEL effective immediately, pending FLOOD MANAGEMENT OPERATIONS. All residents are directed to evacuate via designated corridors. Unauthorized persons found within the closed zone will be subject to detention. This notice supersedes all prior notices.*

Below the text, a map of the valley. Three villages marked with small black squares. Kel Anoor among them. And across the whole lower valley, drawn in a confident blue wash, the projected flood line—reaching higher than Marta's drowned rooftops, higher than the levee they'd walked in on, lapping at the edges of Kel Anoor's fields like a tide chart for a drowning.

"Flood management," Ezra read over her shoulder. His voice had gone flat, which she'd learned was worse than angry. "They're calling it management."

"They're calling it a schedule." She tapped the corner of the map, where a date was stamped in red. "Look."

The date was nine days out.

Nine days until the official release. Except the test releases had already started, and the clouds stacking on the horizon hadn't read the schedule, and men like Corliss never let a good crisis wait for paperwork. The nine days were a lie the way the radio voice had been a lie—a number designed to be believed by people who wouldn't be here to check.

"We have to get ahead of this," she said. "Kel Anoor is a day downriver, maybe less if the current's with us. We warn them tonight, tomorrow at the latest, and start moving people up the ridge roads before the water takes the low crossings."

"And then what? We're two people with a leaking tub and a compass." Ezra took the notice from her and read it again, slower, and she watched something shift in his face—not fear exactly, but the particular grimness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "Six villages' worth of grandmothers are going to look at a ranger's badge and a thief's word and ask why the government that sent this nice notice is lying about it."

"Then we show them the flood line. Show them Marta's village. People believe water."

"People believe water right up until it's on top of them." He folded the notice and shoved it inside his shirt. "We need the boat floating and the truth in writing. Okafor's logs—if any of the survey records survived at the camp—"

"The camp's a day back the way we came." She looked at the sky. The leading edge of the cloud wall had crossed a third of the valley while they talked. The light under it was going strange, greenish, the color the sky turns before hail or worse. "We don't have a day back. We barely have a day forward."

* * *

They worked on the boat through the afternoon while the storm ate the horizon.

Rosalind had grown up around hulls—her father's little skiff, the patrol launches at the academy—but she'd never worked on one with a monsoon breathing down her neck, and the difference was everything. Every task became a negotiation with time. She recaulked the opened seam with twine and the last of a pot of pitch she found in the shed, driving oakum in with the blunt screwdriver while Ezra held a lantern and fed her strand after strand, their hands moving in a rhythm they hadn't agreed on and couldn't have explained. The transom around the drain fitting she saturated with pitch and hoped. Hope, she was learning, was a marine material. It failed under load, but so did everything else eventually.

The bilge pump problem she solved the way poor people solve everything: with a bucket and stubbornness. She cut the rusted bottom from the bail bucket, riveted in a patch of tin from an old drum, and sealed the joint with pitch. It would move water. Slowly. In calm water. In a chop, with two hundred pounds of extra passengers, it would be a joke they told afterward or didn't tell at all.

Ezra, meanwhile, went through the depot like a man who'd grown up stripping abandoned houses, and came back with a hoard laid out on the planks: the jerrican of water; fourteen edible ration bars after they'd thrown out the bloated ones; the mended bucket; a hatchet; a coil of newer line he'd found spooled inside the signal mast's base, dry and strong; a signal mirror; and, wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards of the shed office, a river chart of the lower Meridian, official issue, dated eleven years ago.

Rosalind spread the chart on the trailer bed and weighted the corners with bolts. The river unrolled in blue ink, and she felt the familiar click of a professional mind engaging, the one clean pleasure left to her. Channels, depths, cutbanks, the narrows eight miles down where the valley pinched between rock ribs. And there, on the western bank above the narrows, a contour line she knew from the compass etching—the old spillway, drawn here as a dashed line and labeled, in faded type, *DECOMMISSIONED — SEE AMENDED SURVEY.*

Amended. Her father's word. The word that had ended him.

"They amended the surveys," she said aloud. "Thirty years ago. They drew the spillway out of the record, and now they've drawn the villages out of the valley, and nobody at the Ministry sees a pattern because the pattern is the Ministry."

Ezra crouched beside her. For a while neither of them spoke. The wind had risen steadily all afternoon, and now it carried the first smell of rain—not the petrichor of a passing shower but a vast mineral coldness rolling off the cloud wall, the smell of a season changing its mind about the land.

"Nine days on paper," he said finally. "Two days in reality. Maybe one."

"Then we launch at first light."

"Why not now?"

She pointed at the narrows on the chart, then at the darkening river. "Because I've run rapids at night in a sound boat with a crew of four, and I'm not doing it in this thing with a crew of two and a bucket. We bivouac here, fix what we can fix, sleep in shifts, and take the narrows in daylight with the storm on our heels instead of on our heads."

He looked like he wanted to argue, and then the first fat drops hit the tin roof—one, two, five, a scattered drumroll—and he looked at the sky instead and nodded. "First light."

* * *

They made camp in the shed office, a room the size of a cell with a desk, a chair, and a window facing west toward the coming weather. Rosalind split the ration bars, and they ate in silence, chewing the dense sweet paste, drinking measured sips. Outside, the rain arrived in earnest—not the storm itself but its herald, a hard steady fall that turned the world beyond the window into gray wool and set the river rising audibly against the pilings.

She should sleep. She'd ordered Ezra to sleep first, in fact, and he'd stretched out on the desk with his jacket for a pillow and was out within minutes, worn down to the studs by the walk and the worry. But Rosalind sat by the window with the compass open in her lap, and sleep stayed on the far side of the glass with the rain.

Her father had held this brass disc. That fact had stopped feeling impossible somewhere on the levee road and started feeling like a debt. He'd stood in valleys like this one with instruments and orders and a young man's faith in the service, and he'd written the truth down, and the truth had been taken from him along with everything else—his commission, his name, his daughter's belief. And now the same machinery was turning again, smoother this time, better oiled, with a radio voice and printed notices and a date stamped in red, and the only things standing in its path were a disgraced ranger's daughter, a thief with a broken brother, and a boat that leaked.

The badge on her chest—she touched it without meaning to, the worn metal warm from her body—had come from that same machinery. It was the one thing she'd salvaged from her father's ruin, the proof that the Marchetti name could still mean something official, something issued, something that belonged. Every morning for eleven years she'd pinned it on like armor against his ghost.

And every mile of this valley had been peeling it back off.

Through the window, lightning walked along the cloud wall, silent at this distance, illuminating the whole western sky in stroboscopic flashes. Between flashes the darkness was total. She thought of Kel Anoor down there in the dark—rooftops, cooking fires, dogs, children asleep, a whole web of ordinary life strung across the flood line on the Ministry's map—and her chest tightened with something she recognized reluctantly as fear, but not for herself.

The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, she knew, the water was gathering behind whatever gates Corliss's engineers controlled, patient as bureaucracy, obedient as concrete. The land itself had become the weapon. You could outrun a patrol boat. You could not outrun a reservoir with an order.

Ezra stirred on the desk. "Your shift," she said, before he could speak. "Wake me at first light or if the river starts singing."

"The river sings?"

"When it rises fast it pulls debris over the shallows. Sounds like a choir warming up." She handed him the hatchet and the lantern. "If you hear singing, we're already late."

He took the watch, and she lay down on the floor with her jacket rolled under her head, and sleep took her like the river takes a leaf—fast, spinning, down.

She dreamed of her father's hands holding the compass over a chart, drawing a dashed line to safety, and woke once in the black hours to the sound of rain like static and Ezra humming low to himself, keeping time with the water, and slept again.

* * *

First light came the color of dishwater.

The storm's vanguard had passed in the night, leaving a bruised, rinsed sky and a river transformed. What had been a brown slide yesterday was now a freight train—swollen over its banks, dragging entire trees, the pilings of the slipway wrapped in foam. The levee road they'd walked in on was gone under two feet of moving water. The valley had begun closing its doors.

Rosalind stood on the slipway in the gray dawn and did the final arithmetic aloud, because saying numbers made them real. "Fourteen bars, twenty liters, one bucket, one hatchet, one mirror, one chart, one compass, one leaking boat, two liars with badges"—she touched hers, then nodded at Ezra, who had none and never had—"and one day to reach Kel Anoor before the water does."

"Put that way," Ezra said, hauling the bowline, "it sounds almost like a plan."

They loaded in silence, fast, practiced already, strangers no longer quite. Rosalind took the tiller. Ezra shipped the oars as auxiliary engines and ballast both. The boat settled into the current with a list to port and a seepage around the patched seam that the bucket would meet, shift by shift, mile by mile.

As they swung away from the depot, Rosalind looked back once. On the office door, flapping in the wind, someone had nailed a fresh copy of the green-bordered notice, and beneath it, smaller, a second sheet she hadn't seen in the dark:

*NOTICE OF REWARD. Information regarding two fugitives, one female ex-ranger, one male, last seen Lower Meridian Valley. Substantial compensation offered.*

Ezra saw it too. He didn't say anything. Neither did she. The reward meant Corliss had stopped pretending they were lost and started pricing them, and priced things get hunted.

Ahead, the river bent toward the narrows, white-capped even at this distance, and the clouds behind them stacked and climbed and came on like a verdict.

"Hold on," Rosalind said, and gave the boat to the current.
