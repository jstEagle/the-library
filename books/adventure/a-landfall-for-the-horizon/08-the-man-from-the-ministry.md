## 8. The Man from the Ministry

The notice was nailed to the depot door with four brass tacks, and the brass was the first thing that told Rosalind it was a lie.

Government notices in the lowlands were pasted up with flour paste or left to flutter under a single bent nail. Nobody spent brass tacks on paper meant for villagers who couldn't read. She stood in front of the door while Ezra checked the boat's seams behind her, and she read the notice three times, letting the words arrange themselves into their official shapes.

*By order of the Ministry of Water and Reclamation, the Lower Serrano Valley is hereby closed to all civilian traffic for the duration of seasonal flood management. Unauthorized persons will be subject to detention and removal.*

Flood management. The words sat on the page like furniture in an empty house—present, polished, belonging to no one.

"They closed the valley," Ezra said, coming up beside her with a coil of rope over his shoulder. "The valley where they're about to commit a crime."

"Or already have." She touched one of the brass tacks. Cold, new, expensive. "This went up recently. Within days."

"So we're not just trespassers anymore. We're *detainable*."

"We were always detainable. You stole a compass."

"I liberated a compass," he said, "from people who appear to have been disappeared. There's a difference, and I intend to argue it at length if anyone asks."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked downriver, where the water ran high and brown with the first melt of the high country, and the smile died before it finished forming.

The boat was worse than she'd hoped. A flat-bottomed work skiff, government-stenciled, with a cracked transom board someone had patched with tarred canvas and optimism. The fuel tank held maybe enough for two days' running if the current cooperated. There was a bilge pump that worked on a hand lever, a tarp, half a can of lamp oil, and nothing else. No radio. No flares.

"It'll float," Ezra said, in the tone of a man negotiating with a river god.

"It'll float until it doesn't."

"That's all any boat ever does."

* * *

They made eleven miles the first day, running the channel with the current, Rosalind at the tiller reading the water the way her father had taught her to read ground—the V of a submerged snag, the smooth tongue of a safe channel, the ugly boil where something waited under the surface. Ezra bailed when he wasn't navigating, which was often; he knew this stretch of river the way she knew the salt flats, by rumor and by feel, and twice he called a turn before she saw the reason for it.

The clouds began stacking in the west near midday. Not rain clouds yet—just a long gray shelf building itself above the mountains, patient as a creditor.

By evening they'd put in at a bend where an old ferry landing rotted into the current, and Rosalind was measuring out rice and dried fish when Ezra said, quietly, "Light."

She looked up. Downstream, around the far bend, a white shape was coming toward them against the current—a patrol launch, running its engine hard, a searchlight sweeping the banks even though the light was still good.

"No," she said. It came out flat and stupid. "No, they can't—"

The searchlight found them anyway.

* * *

The launch put in below the landing with a competence that told her everything before the man aboard said a word. Two crewmen in ministry gray held position at the rail. The man who stepped onto the mud did so without hurrying, without getting his boots dirty, which on that bank was a small miracle of timing—and he was smiling.

He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in a field uniform so well cut it looked like civilian clothes. He carried a leather document case under one arm and nothing else. No weapon visible. He didn't need one; there were two rifles on the launch behind him.

"Ranger Marchetti," he said. "Or should I say former ranger? I understand your commission lapsed some months ago."

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"Corliss. Ministry of Water and Reclamation, Office of Special Projects." He pronounced *Special Projects* the way other men pronounced a saint's name. "May I?" He gestured at the fire as though asking permission to sit at her own camp, and then sat anyway, on a stone he'd clearly selected for dryness, arranging his coat around him with the care of a man who had never once been uncomfortable outdoors.

Ezra drifted closer, hands loose at his sides, and Corliss noted him the way you note weather. "And Mr. Voss. Yes. We know who you are too."

"Flattering."

"Let's not waste the evening." Corliss opened the document case and drew out a single sheet of heavy paper, unfolded it, and laid it on his knee without offering it to her. "Rosalind Marchetti. Commissioned ranger, nine years' service. Commendation for the Kessel Basin search. Suspended pending review following the incident at Dry Fork—which, I'll note for the record, was not your fault. Your father's name did the damage there, not your conduct. Everyone in the service knows it. Very few are willing to say it."

The words went into her like a blade going into a lock. Nine years. Nine years of extra reports filed, extra inspections volunteered for, of being the first one up and the last one asleep, of earning back inch by inch a name she'd never damaged in the first place. And here was a stranger holding it out to her like a returned passport.

"What do you want, Agent Corliss?"

"The compass."

Just that. Two words, delivered gently.

"And in exchange," he continued, "you receive a full reinstatement. Back pay for the suspension period. A posting of your choosing—I'm told you've always wanted the northern district. Clean record. Your father's file, sealed. All of it." He folded his hands. "You walk away from this valley, Ranger. You forget what you think you've seen. And Monday morning you're a ranger again, with a badge and a future and nobody's daughter written after your name."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the river a night bird started up and thought better of it.

Rosalind became aware that she had stopped breathing, and made herself start again.

It would be so easy. That was the obscene part. She could hand over the compass—Ezra would fight her, but she was stronger and armed and he knew it—and by winter she'd be in the northern district, and the whole ugly business downstream would belong to other people. Men like Corliss, who smiled and kept their boots clean. The villages would drown or they wouldn't. The survey team would stay vanished or they wouldn't. None of it would be hers to carry.

Nobody would ever again look at her and see Hartwell's daughter.

"You're very good at this," she said.

"At what?"

"Making surrender sound like a promotion."

Corliss's smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a small recalibration, like a man adjusting a level. "I'm making you an offer I am authorized to make exactly once. After tonight, the terms change. Detention. Charges. Your association with a known thief entered into the record." He glanced at Ezra without malice, which was somehow worse. "I'd rather not. You strike me as a woman who was very nearly wasted by the service, and I dislike waste."

"Wasted." She tasted the word. "Is that what you'd call my father?"

Something flickered across the agent's face—there and gone, fast, but she caught it. Recognition. Not of the name. Of the *trap*.

"I never met your father."

"No. But you know what happened to him." She stood, slowly, and was pleased to see that Corliss's eyes tracked her the way men track things that might go off. "Thirty years ago he surveyed this same valley. He wrote a report the ministry didn't like. And the ministry took everything from him—his commission, his credibility, his name—and they did it politely, with documents and hearings and brass tacks, exactly the way you're doing it now. So don't stand there and offer me my father's life back, Agent. I've seen where that road goes. My family has been walking it my whole life."

For a moment the only sound was the river.

Then Corliss rose, brushed off his coat, and the warmth drained out of him like water through a cracked hull. What remained underneath was not anger. It was something more administrative than that, and infinitely colder.

"A pity," he said. "Genuinely. You'd have been useful."

"I'm useful where I am."

"Where you are is a closed valley, in possession of stolen government property, in the company of a fugitive, eleven miles inside a flood zone that will be—" he paused, selecting the word with care— "*managed*, within the week. Whatever you imagine you're protecting, Ranger, the water won't care. Neither will the record. History is written by whoever files first." He snapped the document case shut. "My offer stands until dawn. After that, every patrol on this river has your description."

He turned to go, then stopped, half-facing her, and for one unguarded instant he looked tired—an old man standing in the mud at the edge of a drowning valley, doing a job he'd done so long he'd forgotten whether he believed in it.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your father was right about the spillway. It exists. It's on none of our charts because it was never supposed to be on any chart. And it changes nothing. Structures don't testify, Ranger. People do, and people can be managed." He stepped into the launch. "Dawn."

The engine turned over. The searchlight swept the bank once, deliberately, like a man closing a gate, and then the launch backed off the mud and swung downstream into the dark, and Rosalind stood watching its wake flatten against the reeds long after the sound of it was gone.

* * *

Ezra fed the fire while she stared at the river.

"You okay?" he asked finally.

"No."

"Good answer." He handed her a tin cup of tea that tasted mostly of smoke. "For what it's worth, I believed him. Right up until the part where he offered you everything you've ever wanted for the price of a compass. That's when I knew he was lying."

"He wasn't lying." Her voice came out strange and quiet. "That's the worst of it. He'd have done all of it. Reinstatement, back pay, the sealed file. They can afford to keep promises like that. Promises to one woman cost less than paper." She wrapped her hands around the cup. "Nine years, Ezra. Do you know what I did to get back into their good graces after my father fell? I volunteered for every assignment nobody wanted. Salt flats in summer. Border surveys in winter. I made myself the most reliable ranger in the district because reliable was the only thing I was allowed to be. And tonight a man in a nice coat offered it all back to me in ninety seconds, and some part of me—the part that's been starving for nine years—wanted to take it."

"I know that part," Ezra said. "The part that wants to belong so bad it'll sign anything." He poked the fire. "My brother used to say the ministry gives you a badge instead of a family, and then holds the badge hostage whenever you step out of line."

"Your brother sounds smarter than mine."

"You don't have a brother."

"No," she said. "I have a father who tried to warn everyone and got erased for it. Which means I've got a choice he never got to make." She looked up at him, and the exhaustion in her own voice surprised her. "Because I'm not taking the deal, Ezra. You understand that. From this minute on I'm not a ranger anymore, whatever the paperwork says. I'm whatever comes after."

Ezra studied her across the fire for a long moment. Then he reached into his jacket and brought out the compass—not handing it over, just holding it up between them, the brass face catching the firelight.

"Then I suppose you'd better learn what else it hides," he said. "Because Corliss isn't the only one who knows about the spillway. Look at the map fragment again. There's a second set of markings—faint, older, different hand. Somebody annotated this thing after your father drew it. Somebody who knew the valley even better."

Rosalind took the compass. Beneath the etched channels and contour lines she'd memorized, there they were: fine scratches, almost invisible, marking a route that left the river entirely and climbed into the western bluffs.

Toward the old mine workings. Toward whatever the ministry had decided the valley must never give up.

Downstream, far off, thunder rolled along the front of the cloud shelf—soft yet, distant, patient. The monsoon clearing its throat.

"Dawn's in five hours," she said. "We'd better be gone in four."
