## 9. Running the Narrows

The rain came like a rumor first—a hiss on the water ahead of them, a gray curtain walking up the valley—and then it arrived for real, hammering the deck of the *Marta's Blessing* hard enough to sting.

Ezra Voss stood at the bow with one hand wrapped around the rail and the other shielding the compass in his shirt pocket, as if the storm might reach in and take that too. Behind him, Rosalind had the tiller jammed against her hip, boots braced wide, hair plastered to her face. She hadn't complained once since they'd cast off from the depot. He was starting to suspect she didn't know how.

"Tell me again," she shouted over the drumming. "How far?"

"Narrows start at the bend past the black cliffs." He squinted through the sheeting rain. The river had gone the color of wet slate, and the current had picked up a voice of its own—low, insistent, pulling. "Maybe two kilometers. After that it's rock walls on both sides and water moving faster than sense."

"And Corliss is behind us."

Ezra turned. Far back down the channel, where the river straightened toward the depot, a white wake cut the gray water in two clean lines. The patrol boat rode high and mean, its running lights burning even in daylight, because men like Corliss wanted to be seen coming. It was maybe three kilometers back and closing. A government hull with a government engine would eat their leaking tub alive on open water.

But not in the narrows. Not if he read the river right.

"He won't follow us in there," Ezra said.

"You're sure?"

"No."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her—short, sharp, half-swallowed by the rain. "Good. I'd have worried about you otherwise."

He went aft to check the bilge. The leak they'd discovered at the depot had not improved; the pump was a hand-cranked antique with a leather gasket gone soft as old bread, and every hundred strokes bought them another few minutes of floating. He cranked while he talked, because talking kept the fear in a cage.

"My brother used to run this stretch," he said. "Tomas. Before the survey took him upstream. He ran cargo—salt, lamp oil, whatever the villages needed. He knew every rock in this river by its nickname." The crank resisted; he leaned into it. "The narrows were his favorite. Said the river tells you what it wants if you shut up long enough to listen."

"And did you listen? Or did you just inherit the nicknames?"

"Both." He glanced up at her. Rain streamed off the brim of her hat, and beneath it her eyes were fixed downstream, reading the water the way he'd seen her read ground—tracks, spoor, the story written in disturbance. She'd been doing it since the salt flats. He'd mocked her for it at first, called it desert superstition. He wasn't mocking anymore. "There's a rock mid-channel at the entrance called the Priest. You go left of it or you die. Then the channel dog-legs right around a shelf we call the Widow's Teeth. After that—" he hesitated.

"After that what?"

"After that it's Tomas's water. I've only run it twice, and both times I was fourteen and terrified."

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. The pump wheezed under his hands. Ahead, the black cliffs rose out of the rain like the shoulders of something submerged, waiting to surface.

"Then we'll be terrified together," she said. "It's worked so far."

* * *

The Priest came out of the murk all at once—a fang of black stone taller than the mast, wearing the river's spray like a collar. The current here had split itself around the rock into two tongues: the left one smooth and glassy, the right one boiling white over hidden teeth.

"Left," Ezra said. "Hard left, then hold your line until you clear the eddy below it."

"I see it."

She saw it, but seeing and doing were different animals in water this fast, and Ezra watched her hands on the tiller with the particular attention of a man watching someone else defuse a bomb. Rosalind didn't fight the current—that was the thing he noticed, the thing that made him revise his opinion of her yet again. Desert people understood scarcity, and scarcity had taught her not to waste anything, including effort. She gave the river exactly what it demanded and no more. The bow swung left. The Priest slid past close enough that Ezra could have touched it, could have counted the strata in its flanks, and then they were through, into the relative calm of the eddy pool, and the patrol boat's engine note rose behind them, angry and near.

"He's stopping at the Priest," Ezra said, craning back.

"Of course he is. His pilot isn't suicidal." Rosalind's jaw tightened. "Which means he'll go around. There has to be a road on the rim."

"There's a track. Two hours by vehicle to the lower landing." Ezra did the arithmetic and didn't like the answer. "We buy ourselves maybe an hour. Less, if he radios ahead."

"Then we don't stop for anything. Not even the boat."

"The boat is taking on water faster than—"

"I said not even the boat." Her voice cracked across the cockpit like a whip, and for a second he saw the ranger in her, the woman who'd chased him across forty miles of salt flat on half a canteen, and he understood that she meant it. "Bail. I'll steer."

* * *

The narrows swallowed them.

The walls closed in—basalt on both sides, sheer and dripping, so near that the rain couldn't reach the water between them and the river ran unbroken by weather, pure current, fast as a gallop. The sound changed. Out in the open channel the storm had been a roar; down here it became a pressure, a hum in the chest, the whole gorge resonating like a struck bell. Ezra bailed with the dented pot they'd found under the thwart, throwing water over the side in a rhythm he set against the boat's motion, and watched Rosalind work.

She steered like she'd been born on rivers, which he knew was a lie—she'd told him about the desert station, the dry years, the father who'd come home smelling of dust and disgrace. But hands didn't lie. Her hands knew boats. Somebody had taught her, somewhere, before the desert took the rest.

"Widow's Teeth!" he shouted, pointing. The shelf jutted from the right wall, a staircase of broken stone with the river piling against it and rebounding in a standing wave that looked solid enough to walk on. "Dog-leg right, then immediately left—the channel reverses behind it!"

"How far behind?"

"Immediately! There's a rock in the reversal—Tomas called it the Deacon. Small, green, barely shows. If you hit it we broach."

"Green rock, small, invisible." She said it flatly, but her eyes were already hunting. "Wonderful."

They hit the wave. The *Marta's Blessing* climbed it like an animal climbing a fence, hung for one impossible instant with water cascading off both gunwales, and slammed down into the reversal, and there it was—the Deacon, a moss-slick hump breaking the surface dead ahead, exactly where the current now wanted to throw them.

Ezra stopped breathing.

Rosalind didn't. Later, trying to describe it, he would say she moved the tiller the way a card sharp moves a card—one small, obscene, impossible motion—and the boat's stern kicked sideways, pivoting around the rock with a hand's width to spare, and then they were through, into the lower gorge, and the current took them like a hand closing around a wrist.

She exhaled. That was all. One long breath, and then her eyes went forward again.

"That," Ezra said, when he could speak, "was not seamanship. That was witchcraft."

"That was my father." She said it without looking at him, and something in her tone closed the subject like a door. "He sailed the coast before the service sent him inland. He taught me on a lake, summers. He said a boat doesn't care who you are. It only cares whether you're honest with it."

Ezra bailed. The bilge was winning—slowly, stubbornly, the way bureaucracies win—but it was losing too, because Rosalind had begun reading the current the way Tomas used to, finding the seams between the boils where the water ran truest, steering the boat into the river's own preferred line so the hull did half the work. They made a strange machine, the two of them: her hands and his knowledge, his pot and her tiller, neither sufficient alone.

Somewhere in the next kilometer, without either of them announcing it, they stopped being strangers.

* * *

The gorge opened like a mouth.

One moment the walls were close enough to shout across; the next they fell away into a broad, rain-lashed basin where the river slowed and spread and forgot its anger. Ezra sagged against the thwart, arms shaking, the bail pot dangling from his fingers. His shirt was soaked through, though whether from rain or sweat or spray he couldn't have said.

"Lower landing's around the next point," he managed. "If Corliss drove hard, he's already there."

"Then we don't land." Rosalind brought the boat around the point herself, scanning the bank. "We stay mid-channel and keep moving. Where does the river go after the landing?"

"Down to the floodplain. Braided channels, sandbars, cane breaks. Fifty ways to disappear." He paused. "And fifty ways to run aground, in this light, in this rain."

"Pick the best one."

He almost laughed. Almost. Because ahead, at the lower landing, he could see the patrol boat tied up at the jetty, running lights still burning, and figures on the dock—four, five of them, hunched in oilskins, and one figure apart from the rest, tall and dry under an umbrella, watching the river come down out of the narrows as if he'd been certain all along that it would deliver them to him.

Corliss raised one hand. Not a threat. An invitation. Even at this distance, even through the rain, Ezra could read the gesture: *Come alongside. Let's talk. There's no need for any of this.*

"He's alone at the rail," Rosalind murmured. "His men are at the trucks. He wants to negotiate."

"He always wants to negotiate. That's how he eats people." Ezra's hands had gone cold around the bail pot. "Rosalind. We can't outrun them on open water. The leak, the load—we're done if it comes to a chase in the floodplain."

"Then it doesn't come to a chase." Her eyes never left the umbrella'd figure. "We give him something to look at. Ezra—is there a side channel? Anything that looks like an escape?"

He understood then, and a grin broke across his face despite everything, wild and exhausted. "The old millrace. Left bank, just past the landing—it looks like a dead slough, choked with cane. Everyone thinks it's silted in. Tomas used it to dodge customs men. It comes out two miles down, behind the bluff."

"Does it have depth?"

"Enough. Barely. Once."

"Once is what we need." She eased the tiller over, casual as a woman changing her mind about a destination, letting the boat drift toward the right-hand channel as if surrendering to the obvious route. Letting Corliss watch them choose the main stem, where his engines could follow. "Keep bailing. And when I say now—"

"Now," Ezra repeated.

"—we turn hard and pray your brother's river remembers us kindly."

The landing slid closer. Corliss's umbrella tilted, and Ezra imagined the man's voice, reasonable and warm: *Miss Marchetti. Your badge. Your future. All you have to do is hand me that compass.* He thought of Tomas, somewhere upstream, alive or not alive, and of Marta in her flooded village, and of the word *test releases* spoken in a voice that had never belonged to the survey chief.

The jetty drew abeam. Forty meters. Thirty. Corliss stepped to the edge of the dock, umbrella lowered now, rain streaking his polished face, and called out—words lost in the storm, but the shape of them gentle, coaxing, a hand extended over the water.

Twenty meters.

"Now," Rosalind said.

Ezra threw his weight to the port side as she swung the tiller hard over, and the *Marta's Blessing* heeled, dug her shoulder into the current, and shot for the gap in the cane like a fox going to ground. Shouts erupted behind them—an engine turning over, Corliss's voice cracking into command—but the cane closed around them, whipping the deck, hiding them in a green corridor barely wider than the boat, and the noise of pursuit collapsed into rustling and rain.

The millrace was everything Tomas had promised and nothing anyone would have chosen: brown water moving slow between walls of cane eight feet tall, the channel threading and splitting, depth unknowable. Rosalind shipped the tiller half-way and let momentum carry them, eyes flicking from the water's color to the cane's lean, reading the deep line from the way the plants bent. Twice the hull kissed mud and Ezra's heart stopped; twice she found water again with a nudge of the rudder.

Neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the boat wasn't already saying.

When the cane finally opened onto the lower river—wide, gray, empty, the patrol boat nowhere in sight—they both sat down abruptly, as if the same string holding them upright had been cut.

Ezra laughed. It started small and got away from him, breathless and slightly hysterical, and to his enormous surprise Rosalind joined in—her head tipped back against the thwart, rain on her face, laughing at the sky like a woman who'd forgotten how.

"We're alive," he said.

"Barely."

"They'll sweep the floodplain by morning."

"Then we move tonight." She wiped her face and looked at him, and the laughter drained out of her expression, replaced by something steadier and more frightening. "Ezra. When we get where we're going—wherever the compass leads—I need to know you'll tell me the truth. About your brother. About why you really stole from my father's things."

The question landed harder than any wave in the narrows. He looked at the compass bulge in his soaked shirt, at the map fragment etched beneath its face, at the woman who had every reason to hate him and had instead just trusted him with her life on a green rock in a reversing current.

"Not tonight," he said quietly. "But soon. I promise."

Above the bluff, far behind them, a flare went up—white, searing, briefly turning the rain to falling fire. Somewhere in the floodplain, Corliss's men were marking the water.

Rosalind took the tiller again. "Then let's make sure 'soon' gets here before they do."
