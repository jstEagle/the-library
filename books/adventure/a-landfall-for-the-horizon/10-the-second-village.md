## 10. The Second Village

The rain found them an hour before Kel Anoor did.

It came as a fine grey hiss on the river's skin, not yet the monsoon proper but its herald—the sky to the south stacked like slate, layer upon layer of cloud with lightning moving inside it slow as thought. Ezra stood in the bow with the tiller line wrapped twice around his fist and read the water ahead the way Rosalind read ground: for sign, for lie, for what the surface refused to say.

"Shoal past that bend," he said. "See how the current wrinkles? It's climbing over something. Keep left."

She kept left. The boat—*Wren*, the depot man had called her, though she leaked like a wren with a broken wing—answered sluggishly, her seams weeping a steady teaspoon of river into the bilge that Rosalind had already stopped pretending not to notice. They bailed in shifts now. It had become a rhythm between them, wordless, like breathing.

The bend opened, and Kel Anoor appeared out of the rain.

Rosalind had seen a hundred settlements from the water—depot towns, ferry landings, the huddled government stations where rangers signed manifests and drank bad coffee. She had expected something like those: functional, temporary, a place where people waited to be somewhere else.

Kel Anoor was not waiting to be anywhere.

It rose from the riverbank in terraces, stone and timber and thatch, houses built into the slope like they'd grown there. Laundry lines sagged between rooftops heavy with rain. Smoke stood up from cookfires despite the wet, stubborn columns of it. A temple or shrine of some kind crowned the highest terrace, its bell dark against the storm-light, and below it gardens climbed the hillside in green steps—taro beds flooded and gleaming, fruit trees staked and tied, every inch of earth claimed and kept and *known*. There were carved posts at the waterline, old ones, their faces worn smooth by decades of flood seasons, each one notched with marks she couldn't read but understood instinctively: high-water years. Bad years. Survived years.

People had been dying here and being born here and burying each other here since before the Ministry had a name for this valley.

"They won't run," Ezra said quietly. He wasn't looking at the village. He was looking at her, gauging her reaction, and she realized he'd already accepted what she was only now understanding. "I told you. You can warn people all you want. You can't uproot them with words."

"We haven't tried yet."

"No," he agreed. "We haven't."

He brought the boat alongside a stone landing worn concave by generations of hulls, and Rosalind stepped onto Kel Anoor's ground feeling like an intruder in a house whose family was still home.

* * *

They came out to meet them, because strangers on the river were news in a place like this. A dozen villagers gathered under the eaves of the landing shed—fishermen with nets over their shoulders, women with market baskets, children peering around legs with the frank stare of kids who had never been taught to be afraid of outsiders because outsiders were rare and mostly harmless.

An older man stepped forward. Broad through the chest, white-haired, hands like tree roots. He looked at Ezra first—there was recognition there, or at least category: river-rat, trader, someone who might owe or be owed—and then at Rosalind, and his gaze snagged on her uniform the way a hook snags on a sunken log.

"Ranger," he said. Not a question. His voice carried the flat vowels of the valley, softened by age. "Long way from your territory, ranger."

"My name is Marchetti." She'd rehearsed this on the river and it fell apart anyway; there was no clean way to say it. "I need to speak with whoever leads here. Tonight if possible. There's going to be a flood—a release from the upper dam, engineered, deliberate. Bigger than any flood season. The whole lower valley will go under. Your village is in the path."

Silence, except the rain thickening on the shed roof.

Then the old man laughed. Not cruelly—that was the worst part. He laughed the way you laugh at a child who has announced the moon is made of bread, with genuine fondness, and several others smiled with him.

"The dam," he said. "Yes. We've heard about the dam. For three years we've heard about the dam. Papers come, men come with instruments, they measure and mark and leave. And every year the river rises and falls exactly as it has since my grandfather's grandfather notched that post." He gestured at the carved markers at the waterline. "You know how many times we've been told the valley is finished? My grandmother was moved for a project in '61. She walked back. Took her chickens." A ripple of laughter again, warmer. "The government says many things, ranger. The river says other things. We listen to the river."

"The river isn't making this decision anymore." Rosalind heard her own voice harden and couldn't stop it. "There are men upstream right now preparing to open gates that will put this entire valley under thirty feet of water. I've seen what they did to the first village downstream of the test releases. Have you?"

That killed the laughter. The old man's smile faded into something more careful.

"Drown Hollow," a woman said softly, using the name the river people had given the first flooded village. "We sent boats. There was nothing to send boats *for*."

"There will be something to send boats for here," Rosalind said, "if you don't move. People. Children. Everything you—" she gestured helplessly at the terraced gardens, the smoke, the bell tower, everything rooted and green and loved, "—everything you've built."

"You're asking us to leave," the old man said. Not hostile. Just precise, the way a man is precise when he intends to say no.

"I'm asking you to live."

Ezra shifted beside her. She felt him decide to speak the way you feel a boat change trim. "Tomas," he said to the old man. "You know me. You know my brother's family traded through here ten years. I'm not a ranger and I'm not a liar, and I'm telling you the water is coming. Not next season. This one. Days, maybe."

Tomas looked at Ezra for a long moment, and something passed between them—some ledger of small debts and honest dealings that Rosalind would never have access to, never be able to audit. Then he looked back at her, and his face had gone from amused to grave without passing through belief.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

They showed him everything. The compass map fragment spread on a table in the longhouse while rain drummed the thatch like a crowd demanding entry. Marta's testimony, retold. The survey camp with its running instruments and rotting food and no tracks leading away. The official notice from the depot declaring the valley closed for flood management—which Tomas read twice and handed back with the expression of a man reading his own death warrant written in a language he didn't quite trust himself to translate.

Some believed. That was the thing Rosalind hadn't anticipated—not the disbelief, which she'd braced for, but the believing, and how much harder it was to watch.

A young woman with a baby on her hip listened to Rosalind describe the water path down the valley and went white as bone meal. She left without a word and came back an hour later having already loaded a cart. A fisherman named Orlin, who owned two boats, began at once calculating capacity aloud, hauling his sons out of the rain to assign them tasks. A schoolteacher closed her little schoolhouse and started walking door to door with a list of names.

But others—

Others stood in their doorways and would not come out. An ancient woman sat on her porch shelling beans and told Rosalind, without looking up, that she had buried a husband and two sons on this hillside and the water could take her standing up if it wanted them back. A man whose family had fished the same weir for six generations physically blocked the path to his boathouse and said, politely, absolutely, that the river had never lied to his family and he would not insult it by running from it now.

And some simply could not hear it. Rosalind watched a farmer listen to the whole account—the dam, the gates, the dates—and then turn back to mending a fence, because the fence needed mending, because the fence had always needed mending, because a world in which the fence didn't matter was a world he had no tools to live in.

By midnight, maybe forty of the village's three hundred had decided to go. The rest had decided to stay, or to decide tomorrow, or to decide never.

Rosalind stood in the longhouse doorway in the small hours, too wired to sleep, and watched lanterns move on the terraces—the ones packing, the ones pretending not to pack—and felt something twist in her chest that took her a long moment to name.

Envy.

Not of the danger—they had plenty of that coming. She envied the *weight* of them. Every person on that hillside, believer and refuser alike, was anchored to something she had never once had. Their refusal was rooted in six generations of high-water notches. Their fear was rooted in the graves on the hillside. Even their denial was a kind of belonging—the deep human luxury of assuming the world would stay put because it always had, because everyone you'd ever known had confirmed it together, generation after generation.

Rosalind had grown up in postings. Barracks. Transit housing. Her father's disgrace had followed them like weather, and after he was gone she had volunteered for the emptiest territories on the map because empty places asked nothing of you except competence, and competence she could give. She had spent twenty years being *assigned* to places. Never once belonging to one. The badge on her shoulder was the closest thing she had to roots, and she had learned tonight that even that was a lease, not a deed—it could be revoked by a polite man named Corliss in under five minutes.

She wanted what these people had so badly it frightened her. She wanted a hillside with her dead in it. She wanted a weir with her family's name worn into it. She wanted the terrible, beautiful stubbornness of a woman shelling beans on a porch she would die defending.

"Couldn't sleep either?"

Ezra lowered himself onto the bench beside her, a tin cup in each hand. The tea was terrible and hot and she took it gratefully.

"They're not going to run," she said. "Most of them."

"Some are. More than I expected, honestly. Tomas talking to the other elders helped. And the schoolteacher—she's worth ten rangers." He blew on his tea. "You did good today."

"I failed today. Two hundred and sixty people are going to be asleep when the water comes."

"You can't make people believe you." He said it gently, but it landed like a blow, because it was true, and because she suspected he'd learned it the way he learned most things—the expensive way. "You can only make sure they can't say nobody told them. After that it's theirs to carry, not yours."

"That's a coward's arithmetic."

"It's a survivor's." He glanced at her sideways. "You think I don't know what it costs? I've got a brother missing and half my life I've watched people refuse to see things that were right in front of them. You learn to stop breaking yourself against it."

She looked out at the lanterns. One by one they were going dark, the packing done or abandoned, the village settling into its last ordinary night—or not its last; she refused to let it be its last; there was still time, there had to be time.

"Tomas asked me something," she said. "After the meeting. He asked me where I was from. And I said the capital, and he said no—he said, where are you *from*, girl, where do your dead live. And I didn't have an answer." Her throat tightened. "Twenty years in the service and I don't have an answer to that question."

Ezra was quiet for a moment. "My dead live in Drown Hollow," he said finally. "Or near it. Under whatever's left of it now." He turned the cup in his hands. "Maybe that's why I can't stop going toward the water either."

The rain eased to a whisper. Down at the landing, Orlin's boats knocked gently against the stones, loaded and covered, ready for a dawn departure that only a fraction of the village would join.

"First light," Rosalind said. "We should be on the river before they are. If Corliss is sweeping the valley, he'll check the villages. I don't want his patrol finding us moored under a temple bell."

"He'll find us anyway," Ezra said. "Men like him always do. But later is better than sooner."

She drained the tea and stood, and as she did, a sound drifted down from the highest terrace—the temple bell, struck once, soft, by some insomniac hand. Whether it was warning or mourning or just the hour, she couldn't tell. It hung in the wet air over the sleeping village and slowly faded, and Rosalind stood listening until it was gone, wishing with a homesickness that had no home behind it that she understood what it meant.

She was halfway to her bedroll when the schoolteacher came running up through the rain, lantern swinging, breath ragged.

"The radio," she gasped. "The depot frequency—we keep one for the ferry schedule. Someone's been broadcasting for the last hour. Calling for you by name. Both your names." Her eyes were wide in the lantern light. "They're saying you're armed, and dangerous, and that anyone sheltering you will answer for it."

Behind her, across the black terraces of Kel Anoor, doors began to open—one, then another, then a scatter of lamplight blooming through the rain like a constellation deciding whether to fall.
