## 11. Bailing by Lamplight

### Rosalind

The storm found them an hour after dark, and the boathouse found them twenty minutes after that.

It was less a building than a memory of one—a timber shed on stilts at the river's edge, its corrugated roof singing under the first hard rain, its doors hanging open like a man who'd given up caring what came in. But it had walls, and walls meant they could stop rowing, and stopping rowing meant Rosalind could finally look at the boat's hull without lying to herself about what she'd see.

She saw it. A seam below the waterline, weeping in a thin silver thread.

"Out," she said. "Everything out. Now."

Ezra moved without argument, which she was learning to trust more than his charm. They hauled the boat up the ramp on rollers gone green with moss, and by lamplight—their single lamp, hung from a nail some depot worker had driven decades ago—the damage declared itself plainly: a split plank, maybe a hand's width long, where the boat had kissed a submerged fence post during their flight downriver. Not fatal. Not nothing.

"Can you fix it?" Ezra asked.

"I can fix it." She crouched, running her thumb along the seam. "Whether it holds through what's coming is a different question."

Outside, the rain went from sound to presence. It hammered the roof, sheeted off the eaves, turned the river into something churning and blind. The monsoon hadn't truly arrived yet—this was only its herald, the vanguard squall—and already the world beyond the boathouse walls had been erased. There was no valley anymore. No river, no hills, no direction. Just water falling out of black air, and the two of them inside a wooden box with a broken boat and a compass that had ruined her father's life.

Rosalind set to work.

The depot's shelves yielded what depots always yield when nobody's watched them: a tin of pitch, a roll of tarred canvas, a plane with a rusted blade she spent ten minutes sharpening on a whetstone whose grit was mostly gone. She worked with her sleeves pushed up, hair tied back, hands remembering motions her mind had half forgotten—her father's hands, doing exactly this, on exactly this kind of night, thirty years and a lifetime ago. He'd taught her caulking before he'd taught her to read. *A boat forgives almost everything,* he used to say, *except a lie you tell yourself about its hull.*

She wondered, for the thousandth time since the salt flats, whether he'd been talking about boats at all.

"Ezra." She nodded at the compass, sitting on an overturned crate between them like a third person. "While I work. Show me."

He looked up from the fire he'd coaxed alive in a rusted drum cut in half. Firelight moved across his face, and she saw again what she'd seen at the survey camp, at Marta's drowned village, at every hour since: the thief's mask wearing thin over something rawer. Grief, maybe. Or guilt. With Ezra Voss it was hard to say where one ended.

"You're sure? You said—" He hesitated. "You said looking at it felt like reading someone else's mail."

"It is someone else's mail." She drew the plane along the replacement sliver of timber she'd split from a shelf plank. "And I'm done pretending I don't want to read it."

* * *

### Ezra

She didn't know how to ask for things. That was the thing about her that kept surprising him. Rosalind Marchetti could track a man across salt flat for three days, could read ground like a priest reads scripture, could face down a Ministry agent with her chin up and her badge burning a hole in her pocket—but ask her to admit she wanted something, needed something, and she turned into a woman planing a board very, very carefully so she wouldn't have to look up.

Ezra opened the compass.

He'd had it apart twice now, once in a jail cell in Harrow Bend and once in the reeds downstream of the first drowned village, and both times the trick was the same: the face plate unscrewed counterclockwise against all sense, and beneath the dial, etched into the brass housing itself, the map waited. Not drawn—etched, fine as spider silk, visible only when light raked across it at a low angle. Someone had wanted this map findable but not readable. Someone who understood that paper burns and ink fades and men get searched.

"Here." He tilted the housing toward the lamp. "You need the light almost flat. Like this."

The lines swam up out of the metal. The river, unmistakably—the same river running black and furious fifty feet from where they sat, rendered here as a calm ruled line. Tributaries. Contour marks. And then the thing that had stopped Ezra's breath the first time he'd seen it: a second channel, parallel to the main river, branching off upstream and rejoining far below, passing through a structure marked with a symbol like a keyhole lying on its side.

"That's not natural," Rosalind said. She'd abandoned the pretense of working; the plane lay still in her hands. "That channel. Rivers don't run parallel to themselves."

"No," Ezra agreed. "But reservoirs do."

He'd spent the last day and a night turning it over, fitting pieces together the way he'd once fit locks to keys back in the capital's thieving years. The symbol. The contour numbers climbing the valley walls higher than any flood Marta's generation remembered. The way the channel bypassed the villages entirely—as if it had been built to carry water *around* them.

"My grandfather helped build something up here," he said slowly. "I always thought it was a story. He talked about a spillway like other men talk about a war. Said he poured concrete until his hands shook for the rest of his life. Said the government paid them well and then made them sign papers saying most of what they built didn't exist."

Rosalind went very still.

"Misdocumented," she said. The word came out flat, careful, like she was setting down something fragile. "Deliberately."

"That's what the papers would have said. If there were papers." Ezra rotated the housing another degree, and new lines surfaced: small rectangles along the bypass channel, evenly spaced. Access galleries, maybe. Or gates. And at the downstream end, where the channel rejoined the river, the keyhole symbol again, larger, with a number beside it that might have been a depth or a year. "They built a spillway big enough to drain a whole reservoir around three villages, and then they wrote it out of the record. Why would you do that?"

He watched her not answer. Watched her jaw tighten, her eyes go somewhere thirty years away.

"Rosalind."

"My father surveyed this valley," she said.

* * *

### Rosalind

Saying it aloud was like opening a door she'd painted shut.

She told him because the storm gave her no excuse not to—because the rain erased the world and left only the lamplight and the fire and a man who had just shown her his grandfather's ghost in a piece of brass, and fairness demanded she show him hers.

"He was a surveyor too. Water service, like me. Before me." She picked up the plane again, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and began smoothing the patch she'd fitted into the hull. "I was nine. He came home from a posting in the high valleys—younger than I am now, or close to it—and he was wrong. That's the only word I've ever found for it. Wrong in a way I didn't have language for yet. He'd left confident. He came back quiet. He started drinking the way men drink when they're trying to drown something specific instead of everything generally."

The plane whispered along the wood. Outside, thunder rolled down the valley like furniture being dragged across heaven.

"The charges came six months later. Falsified survey data—they said. Inaccurate flood modeling. Negligence that endangered communities." Her voice stayed level. She'd rehearsed this testimony her whole life without knowing it. "There was a hearing. He stood in a room full of men in good coats and tried to explain, and I was old enough to be in the gallery, and I watched him fail. Not because he lied. Because whatever he tried to say, they'd already decided what he was. He lost his commission. We lost the house. My mother lost—" Rosalind stopped, adjusted the patch, drove a copper tack home with three precise blows. "She lost him, eventually. He took work hauling freight on the lower river. Died when I was nineteen. A lock gate failure. Ironic, if you like irony."

"And you joined the service anyway."

"I joined the service *because.*" She looked up at last, and let him see it, whatever it cost. "Everyone said I was proving something. Redeeming the name. That's what I told myself too. Marchetti's daughter, straightening the record, earning back what he threw away. I believed that story for fifteen years, Ezra. I built my whole life on it. The badge wasn't a job. It was the answer to a question everyone in every post I ever served asked with their eyes: *aren't you the disgraced surveyor's girl?*"

The fire cracked. Rain hammered.

"But that's not why you joined," Ezra said quietly. It wasn't quite a question.

"No." She set the plane down. "I think—I think I joined to finish his sentence. To stand in a room full of men in good coats and say what he couldn't. I just never knew what it was he couldn't say." She reached across the crate and turned the compass housing until the etched channel caught the lamp again, the keyhole symbol gleaming. "Until now. He surveyed this valley, Ezra. Thirty years ago. And whatever he found here is what broke him. What they broke him *with*. This spillway your grandfather poured—it's in this map. Under my father's compass. Which means either he found it, or—"

"Or he put it there," Ezra finished.

The words settled over the boathouse like the storm settling over the valley. Rosalind stared at the brass and felt the ground shift under fifteen years of certainty. Her father's disgrace. Her father's shame, which she had carried like a stone in her chest since she was nine years old, polishing it, honoring it, defining herself against it—

What if it had never been his?

* * *

### Ezra

He knew what it was to inherit a debt you never signed for. His brother Tomas had gone into government service for the same reason Ezra had gone into theft: because the family needed money and only one of them could hold a shovel. Every coin Ezra had ever lifted, he'd told himself, was a payment on that account. Then Tomas vanished into a survey team, and the account came due in a currency Ezra didn't know how to steal.

Now he watched Rosalind Marchetti discover the same arithmetic—that the ledger she'd been balancing her whole life had been falsified at the top—and he understood her the way he'd never understood anyone he'd robbed.

"We should sleep in shifts," he said, because someone had to say something practical before the silence ate them both. "Storm'll bring patrols to the depot eventually. Corliss doesn't strike me as a man who takes weather days."

She nodded, grateful for the mercy of logistics, and took first watch—which meant, he realized an hour later when he woke to lamplight, that she hadn't slept at all. She'd gone back to the compass. She'd gone back to the map, and she was sitting cross-legged on the crate with her father's disgrace in her lap, tracing the etched channel with one fingertip, over and over, like a woman learning a prayer.

"There are galleries," she said without looking up. "Along the bypass. See—here, here, here. Evenly spaced. And this mark at the downstream end." Her finger stopped on the keyhole. "That's not a symbol. I've seen it before. On the old engineering standards, the ones they stopped teaching. It's a draft mark. It means *intake structure*."

"So it's real. The spillway."

"It's real, and it's mapped, and it's been sitting under my father's compass face for thirty years while the Ministry told the valley there was nothing here but river." She laughed—one short, unamused note. "They didn't just misdocument it. They built the new dam project on top of the lie. All those hydrology reports Corliss quoted—all those *modeled flood scenarios*—they can't acknowledge the spillway exists, because if it exists, then the original builders planned for the valley to survive a catastrophic release. And if the original builders planned for survival, then drowning Kel Anoor isn't engineering necessity. It's a choice."

Ezra sat up fully. The fire had burned low; he fed it a shelf plank and watched her face in the new light. "Then the map isn't just a map."

"No." Rosalind's voice had changed—steadied, the way water steadies when it goes deep. "It's a refuge. Those galleries, if they're dry, if the gates held—" She looked at him, and he saw the last of the ranger's institutional caution go out of her eyes, replaced by something older and harder and more dangerous. Hope, weaponized. "My father didn't leave me a map to a crime, Ezra. He left me a map to the *answer* to the crime. Somewhere in this valley there's a door my grandfather's generation built and my father found, and the Ministry has spent thirty years pretending it isn't there."

Thunder walked the ridgeline. The river rose audibly in the dark, a low continuous roar like the valley clearing its throat.

Ezra opened his mouth to answer—

—and the lamp guttered as, somewhere upstream, carried thin through the rain, came a sound neither of them could mistake: an engine. More than one. Slow, methodical, working the bank line, coming down through the storm toward the depot.

Patrols. Corliss hadn't taken a weather day after all.

Rosalind pinched out the lamp. In the sudden dark, her hand found his shoulder in the blackness—steady, certain, a surveyor's hand fixing his position.

"Boat first," she breathed. "Then the keyhole."
