## 13. Corliss Closes In

Ezra woke to the sound of dogs.

Not the village dogs—those he'd learned in three days at Marta's house, the yellow bitch that slept under the drying racks and the young one with the torn ear who barked at herons. These were farther off, upriver, a deep baying that carried across the water in the grey hour before dawn, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, the low thrum of an outboard motor.

He lay still on the woven mat and listened the way he'd learned to listen in the capital's back streets: not for the sound itself but for what it meant. One boat, maybe two. Moving slow. Dogs on the bank, working a line.

Beside him, Rosalind was already awake. He could tell by her breathing—the shallow, deliberate rhythm of someone cataloguing every noise in the dark. In the lamplight her eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling beams, and when she turned her head their gazes met and neither of them said anything, because saying it would make it true.

The survey team had been in the mine shaft four days now. Four days of ferrying food downriver in Marta's flat-bottomed skiff, of Rosalind dressing wounds with river-boiled water and strips of sailcloth, of Ezra's brother Tam drifting in and out of fever and calling once, horribly, for a mother who had died nine years ago. Four days of the valley holding its breath around them.

And now the breath was coming out.

* * *

They were on the water before full light, running without lamps through the mist that pooled on the river like spilled milk. Marta poling at the stern because she knew every sandbar by feel, Rosalind forward with the compass open on her knee, Ezra amidships with Tam's head in his lap, feeding him sips of watered wine because it was all the old woman would allow—"Fever wants thinning, not drowning."

"You should not have come back last night," Marta said. She said it without turning, her pole dipping and lifting, dipping and lifting. "Two trips in one dark. The river sees everything."

"The shaft has no door," Ezra said. "If we don't feed them, they die in the dark, and then Corliss doesn't need to find them at all."

"The river sees everything," she repeated, "and so do neighbors." And there was something in her voice—not fear exactly, but a kind of weary arithmetic, an old woman counting costs she had counted before. Ezra thought of the stories Rosalind had told him about her father, about the way disgrace worked in small places, how it soaked into everyone who touched you. He was beginning to understand that being near them was its own kind of contamination.

They left Tam and two of the weaker surveyors at the shaft with three days of food and a signal arrangement—a strip of red cloth on the cane stump above the vent, raised if patrols came close, lowered if all was well. The cloth hung limp and green-grey in the mist when they poled away, and Ezra made himself not look back at it, because looking back was how you jinxed things, and his whole life he had needed luck like a lung needs air.

By midmorning they were back at Marta's house, and that was when the boy came running down the bank path with his shirttail flapping, shouting before he was even in earshot.

"Boats! Boats at the weir crossing—they stopped the ferry—"

* * *

It unfolded over the next hour the way disasters always did for Ezra: in fragments, each one ordinary alone, together forming a net.

A fisherman reported two patrol boats anchored below the narrows, turning back every craft. A trader who'd come up from the delta said the road head at Kel Anoor had a ministry checkpoint, new, manned by men who weren't asking questions so much as writing names. And old Somchai, who kept bees along the eastern slough and saw more than anyone gave him credit for, sent word by his granddaughter that soldiers had walked the levee trail at first light with dogs, moving slow, moving like men following a line on a map rather than men searching blind.

"They have something," Rosalind said. She stood at the window with the shutter cracked to a finger's width, and the light through it striped her face. "Dogs don't work a line unless someone's given them a line to work. A scent article. Something from the camp, or—" She stopped.

"Or from us," Ezra said.

"Or from someone who talked."

He watched her arrive at it, watched the professional calm hold while everything behind it went white. Because they both knew the shape of the valley's loyalties now, after six days of hiding among these people. Most would have died before selling them. But most wasn't all. There was the ferryman whose brother worked for the water authority. There was the shopkeeper in Kel Anoor who owed back taxes and had a son wanting a ministry posting. There was always someone, in every place Ezra had ever lived, whose fear was bigger than their love, and the ministry knew how to find those people the way water found cracks.

"We move tonight," Rosalind said. "All of them. Out of the shaft, down the eastern slough, into the cane. If we can reach the old logging cut we're a day from the delta channels, and the delta is a thousand doors."

"And if the dogs are already on the line?"

"Then we run faster than the line."

Marta, packing rice and dried fish into baskets with hands that shook only slightly, spoke without looking up. "You will take my skiff to the slough mouth and leave it sunk where I say. You will not come back here. Whatever happens, you will not come back here." Now she looked up, and her eyes were dry and fierce and very old. "I am too old to run, children. My bones are in this house. But my name is not for sale, and I will not have it bought secondhand either. Go."

Ezra wanted to argue. He wanted to tell her they'd stay, that they'd hide in her cane loft and take their chances beside her, because leaving felt like abandoning a fortification. But he'd grown up watching people make noble stands in rooms too small to hold them, and he knew how those ended. He took her hands instead, both of them, rough as bark and warm as bread.

"When this is over," he said, "I'm coming back to fix your roof. The whole thing. New thatch."

"Your roof-fixing better be better than your lying," Marta said, "because that roof has needed fixing since before your father was born." And she smiled, one crack in the stone, and turned back to her baskets.

* * *

They moved at dusk, which was the worst possible hour and the only one available. Six of them: Ezra carrying Tam on his back in a sling of woven cane, Rosalind ahead with the compass and a machete, the survey chief Delacroix walking with a stick and refusing all help, and the other three strung between, hollow-eyed, moving with the shuffling gait of people whose bodies had learned to expect nothing good.

The eastern slough was a green throat of water between walls of cane twelve feet tall. They poled the skiff until Marta's mark—a dead fig tree leaning over the channel—then sank it with stones, as instructed, and waded ashore into the standing cane, and the world closed around them like a fist.

Ezra had grown up in cities, in alleys and stairwells and the spaces between buildings, and he had thought he understood enclosure. He hadn't. Cane was different. It moved. Wind came off the river and the whole field leaned and whispered and clicked, ten thousand dry stalks talking to each other overhead, and every sound was a footstep and every footstep was theirs. Tam's weight burned across his shoulders. His boots sucked in the mud. Somewhere ahead Rosalind moved like a blade through it, cutting a line only she could read, following the compass map's faint etching toward the logging cut that thirty years of official denial had erased from every chart but one.

They walked for two hours in the failing light, and the light failed faster than it should have, clouds stacking in the west like quarried slate. The monsoon was coming. Everyone in the valley could smell it, that green-metallic edge on the wind, and Ezra thought: *Corliss knows it too. He's racing the rain.*

Tam stirred against his neck. "Ez." His brother's voice was a thread. "Ez, are we going home?"

"Soon," Ezra said, which was the kind of lie he was best at, the kind with a door left open in it.

* * *

The flare went up at full dark.

It rose over the western slough—miles behind them, he told himself, miles—and burst into a stuttering white bloom that turned the underside of the clouds the color of bone. For one held breath the whole valley showed itself: the river a black ribbon, the cane fields a dark sea, the drowned village of Marta's people a scatter of rooftops breaking the floodwater like cobbles at low tide.

Then the second flare, closer. Then a third, and this one was not miles behind them. It was north, over the levee trail, close enough that he heard the pop of its launch a half-second before the light.

"They've got the pattern," Rosalind breathed. She had pulled them into a dense stand where the cane grew in a tangled hummock, and they crouched in its shadow while the sky strobed. "West slough, levee, and—" her eyes tracked, calculating "—they're driving toward the logging cut. They know where we're headed. Someone didn't just sell our location. Someone sold our plan."

"Who knew the plan?"

She looked at him. He looked back. And the same name arrived in both their heads at the same moment, and it was not a villager's name at all.

"Pell," Ezra said. Pell, the youngest surveyor, the one who'd wept the first night in the shaft and asked over and over whether his pension would survive, whether his mother would be told he'd deserted. Pell, who had walked free of the mine on his own legs and therefore remembered the route best. Pell, who had been quiet for two days in the particular way of a man rehearsing something.

"He wouldn't," said Delacroix, from the darkness. But his voice had no conviction in it. Fear did that. Fear made men sell the hands that pulled them from holes.

Another flare. Closer. And beneath the whisper of the cane, clear now, unmistakable: dogs. Not baying anymore—silent, which was worse, because silent dogs were working dogs, and working dogs had a scent.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way Ezra later reconstructed it in pieces, because living through it had been no slower and no clearer than a storm seen from underwater.

Rosalind got them moving again, off the bearing toward the logging cut—which was death now, a corridor with soldiers at both ends—and instead due south, straight into the deepest cane, toward the flooded ground beyond it where dogs lost scent and boats couldn't follow. It was the wrong direction for escape and the right direction for survival, and she made the call in the space of three heartbeats, and Ezra loved her a little for it, in the way you love someone for being exactly as ruthless as the moment requires.

They broke from the cane at a run, all seven of them, splashing into shin-deep water among dead orchard trees, and that was when the patrol lights came on along the levee—white beams swinging, voices amplified through a megaphone calling a name that wasn't any of theirs but might as well have been:

*"Rosalind Marchetti. Ezra Voss. By order of the Ministry of Water and Reclamation, surrender yourselves—"*

And behind them, back toward Marta's house, a single flare went up and hung there, and Ezra watched it fall, and he understood with a sick drop of his stomach that the sweep they'd escaped was never meant to catch them. It was meant to push them. And the net's other knots were closing on everything soft: on the house with the broken roof, on the old woman who had fed them, on the boy who had run down the bank shouting warnings with his shirt flying.

"Marta," he said.

"Ezra—" Rosalind's hand closed on his arm.

"She's there. They're at her house right now, and she's there, and she told us not to come back because she knew, she *knew* this was—"

"If you go back, you confirm everything. You hand them the whole story with a bow on it. Her, you, the shaft, the survivors—all of it."

"I know." His voice came out ragged. "I know what it costs. I'm telling you what it buys."

For a long moment the only sounds were the megaphone dying away across the water, the rain beginning at last—fat, scattered drops striking the flood surface like thrown stones—and Tam breathing against his shoulder.

Then Rosalind let go of his arm.

"Not you," she said. "You go on with them. South, to the delta channels, the fisher stilt-houses—old Tuan owes me; I'll explain someday. Get them hidden. Get Tam safe." She was already unslinging the pack, already pulling out the compass, pressing it into his hands with both of hers wrapped around his. "I go back for Marta alone. If I'm not at the third stilt-house by tomorrow's dusk—"

"Don't finish that."

"—then you take them east and you don't stop, and you finish what we started, and you tell the truth so loud that nothing they do to any of us matters anymore." Her face was rain-wet and set, and behind her the flares were still blooming and fading, blooming and fading, a sky full of short-lived suns. "This is the job, Ezra. This is what it actually is. Not the map. Not the badge. It's who you go back for."

She squeezed his hands once, hard, around the compass, and then she was gone—low and fast through the dead orchard, toward the flares, toward the house with the broken roof, toward the old woman whose name was not for sale.

Ezra stood in the rising rain with his brother's weight on his back and five frightened strangers around him and the compass cold in his fists, and he turned them all south, into the dark, hating every step, and behind him the valley lit and dimmed, lit and dimmed, like something enormous trying to get their attention.

At the third stilt-house, Tuan took one look at Rosalind's absence, one look at Tam's face, and said nothing—just pointed them up the ladder and put a pot on the fire.

Ezra sat by the window with the compass open on his knee and watched the southern sky, where the flares had stopped, and the silence afterward was the loudest thing he had ever heard.
