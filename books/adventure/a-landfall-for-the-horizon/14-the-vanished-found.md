## 14. The Vanished Found

The mine shaft was marked on the compass map with a symbol Rosalind had taken for a surveyor's bench mark—a small circle with a cross through it, inked in her father's hand thirty years ago and etched into brass by someone who had never met him. It sat on the eastern flank of the valley where the hills folded in on themselves like a closed fist, above the flood line of even the ministry's most ambitious charts. Which was, she understood now, precisely why it was marked at all. Her father hadn't been noting a landmark. He had been leaving an instruction.

*If you ever need to hide, or to be found—here.*

The trail up from the river was a goat track choked with cane and slate scree, and they climbed it in the last grey hour before dawn, when the monsoon clouds hung so low they seemed to press against the backs of their necks. Ezra went first. He had not spoken more than twenty words since they'd pulled his brother's name out of the mine foreman's old ledger two days ago—*Voss, T., shaft inspector, contracted*—and those twenty words had all been variations of *faster*. Rosalind let him climb. She counted her own breaths instead, the way her father had taught her, four in, six out, because panic made people loud and loudness on exposed rock got you killed.

The adit opened in the hillside like a mouth that had forgotten how to close. Timber framing, black with decades of water stain, sagged over a darkness that breathed cold air outward—the mountain exhaling. A rail bed ran into the dark, its ties rotted to lace. And across the boards, fresh as this morning, were boot prints.

Many boot prints. Going in.

None coming out.

Rosalind crouched over them with her lamp low and felt the dread she'd been carrying since the empty survey camp change shape entirely. At the camp, the absence of tracks had meant absence of people, and absence of people had meant bodies somewhere under the water. But these prints were shallow in some places, deep and dragging in others. These were the prints of men who could still walk but only just.

"Ezra." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "They're alive."

He turned. In the lamplight his face was all planes and shadow, and for one unguarded second she saw what lived underneath the streetwise cynicism—not hardness but terror, held in place by will alone. Then he nodded once and stepped into the dark.

* * *

The shaft descended in switchbacks, following the ore seam, and the deeper they went the more the signs multiplied. A cache of ration tins—ministry issue, the same olive-green labels Rosalind had eaten from a thousand times in ranger stations—stacked with bureaucratic neatness. A chemical toilet behind a canvas screen. A coil of new rope. This was not abandonment. This was logistics. Someone had provisioned this hole in the ground the way you'd provision a warehouse, and warehouses were for things you intended to keep.

"Careful," she whispered, catching Ezra's sleeve before he rounded the next bend. "Six starving men cornered in the dark might not know friends from guards."

"There are supposed to be seven," Ezra said. His whisper was flat. "The team was seven."

She had no answer for that. They went around the bend together.

The lamplight reached the chamber before they did—first a glow, then shapes: a timbered gallery maybe forty feet long, and along its walls, pallets. Bedrolls. Figures.

One of them saw the light and made a sound Rosalind would remember for the rest of her life. Not a shout. A kind of cracked, disbelieving keening, the sound of a man who had stopped believing anyone would ever come and now had to rebuild the world around the fact that someone had.

"It's all right," she called, raising the lamp high, turning it on herself so they could see her face, her hands, the ranger's badge she still wore though she no longer knew what it stood for. "We're not with them. We're not with the ministry. My name is Rosalind Marchetti—I'm a desert ranger, out of the Karst district. We've come to get you out."

A man crawled toward the light. He had been a big man once—you could see the architecture of it still, the shoulders, the hands—but starvation had eaten down to the frame, and his eyes burned in sockets gone hollow. He stopped three feet away and stared at her badge as if it were written in a language he'd once known.

"Marchetti," he said. The name moved his lips strangely, like a word in a dead tongue. "There was a Marchetti on the old valley surveys. Before my time."

"My father," Rosalind said. Her heart was doing something complicated in her chest. "Thirty years ago."

The man laughed—or made a sound adjacent to laughing—and it turned into coughing that bent him double. When he straightened, there were tears cutting clean lines through the grime on his face. "Then God has a sense of humor after all," he said. "Because we have been sitting in your father's hiding place for five weeks, waiting to die in it. I'm Anselm Reyes. I'm the chief of the government survey team that officially does not exist anymore."

* * *

They came out of the dark one at a time into the lamplight, and Rosalind made herself look at each of them, because looking away would have been a kind of lie and she had sworn off lies somewhere back on the salt flats.

Reyes, the chief, who spoke in complete sentences and was therefore the strongest. A hydrologist named Okonkwo whose left leg was badly infected from a fall in the first week; he described the injury in clinical terms while sweat poured off him. Two junior surveyors, Ferro and Adeyemi, who sat pressed together like children in a storm and had not spoken above a whisper in weeks, Reyes said, because the guards had punished noise. A drill operator named Sato who kept checking his own pulse with two fingers, over and over, as if confirming he still existed.

And at the far end of the gallery, wrapped in both his own blanket and someone else's, a man who did not move at all until Ezra Voss said his name.

"Tomas."

The word broke something open in the air of the chamber. The figure stirred. Turned. Even hollowed as he was, even with the fever-bright eyes and the beard grown wild, the resemblance hit Rosalind like a physical blow—the same jaw, the same stubborn set of the brow. Ezra crossed the distance between them in three strides and dropped to his knees on the mine timbers, and Tomas Voss lifted a hand that weighed nothing and touched his brother's face, feeling for it, the way you'd confirm a map against the ground.

"You're real," Tomas said. His voice was a ruin. "I told them you weren't. I told them nobody would come for survey trash." He laughed, and the laugh became a rattle, and Ezra gathered him up the way you'd gather water in your hands, careful, desperate, certain it would spill.

"They didn't come," Ezra said into his brother's shoulder. "I stole my way here. That counts."

Rosalind turned away to give them the moment and found Reyes watching her with those burning eyes.

"How many guards?" she asked quietly. "And where?"

"One rotation of two, at the adit. They come down twice a day with rations and take nothing away." Reyes's mouth twisted. "That's the part you have to understand, Ranger. They're not feeding us out of mercy. We're the evidence. We're being kept alive exactly as long as the flooding takes, and then—" He drew a thumb across his throat, almost gently, the way you'd describe weather. "Corliss's orders. We heard them through the timber. The reservoir fills, the villages drown, the witnesses die in a collapsed mine shaft, and the official record says seven careless men died in an accident years ago. There's already a memorial plaque in the capital. Sato's sister sent flowers to it."

Sato, at the sound of his name, checked his pulse again.

"The dam won't just drown the valley," Rosalind said. "We think it'll fail. The spillway records were falsified—"

"We know." Reyes said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Okonkwo ran the numbers before they took us. The cutoff wall sits on fractured limestone. Under a hundred-year monsoon load, the whole face lets go inside of a decade. Maybe sooner. That's why we wouldn't sign the certification. That's why we're in a hole in the ground." He looked around the gallery—at the rotting food stores, the timber, the dark. "We thought refusing to sign would be enough. We thought the truth was a shield." He shook his head slowly. "We were men of the service too, Ranger. We believed the institution would protect us for telling it what it needed to hear."

Rosalind's badge sat against her chest like a coal.

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the storm built overhead, a pressure she could feel in her teeth, in the seep of water that had begun running in thin threads down the shaft walls. Six broken men. One leaking boat moored twelve miles downstream through cane country patrolled by Corliss's searchlights. Rations for two.

She laid it out on the floor of the gallery with a stick of surveyor's charcoal, drawing on a sheet of spare chart paper Okonkwo produced from a waterproof roll he had guarded with his life: the mine, the goat track, the cane fields, the river, the narrows, Kel Anoor upstream and the depot downstream.

"Options," she said. "We can't carry Okonkwo on the goat track. Not with that leg. So we split—no. Stop me if this is wrong, but listen first." She drew a line. "Ezra and I go down, bring the boat up the old irrigation cut to the flooded quarry below the ridge. It's slow water, and Corliss's patrols don't run it because it's marked as silted. It's not. Marta's grandson runs fish traps there. If the cut holds, we bring the boat within half a mile of the adit and move the men at night, in one lift."

"And if the cut doesn't hold?" Reyes asked.

"Then we move them on foot, in shifts, and we bury two of them on the way." She said it plainly because they deserved plainness. "I won't pretend otherwise."

Silence in the gallery. Water ticking down stone.

"I can walk," Okonkwo said, from his pallet. His voice was thin but level. "I'll walk on the bad leg if I have to. Don't build the plan around my weakness. Build it around the storm."

"The storm is the other problem," Rosalind said. "It peaks tomorrow night. When it does, Corliss opens the release gates early—that's the schedule we pulled from the depot notice. Everything below the hundred-meter contour goes under. Including, if the ministry has its way, this mine." She looked at Reyes. "You said they're keeping you alive until the flooding. That means the collapse order comes with the release. We're not evacuating you ahead of a disaster. We're evacuating you ahead of a murder."

Ferro, one of the silent juniors, made a small sound. Adeyemi put an arm around him.

"So we move tomorrow," Ezra said. He had not left his brother's side, but his voice carried the length of the gallery, hard and clear. "Tonight I sleep holding a knife. Tomorrow we walk."

"No," Rosalind said, and every head turned. She heard her own heartbeat in the quiet. "We move tonight. Now. While the guards' relief is due and the rain is starting—they'll be under shelter, lazy, watching the weather instead of the track. Every hour we wait is an hour the river rises and an hour Corliss gets closer. Marta is already in their hands for questioning. Every person who helped us is a hostage waiting to happen." She rolled the chart, tied it, and looked around at the ruined faces of the survey team. "I know what I'm asking. You haven't stood upright in weeks. But I'd rather you die moving toward the river than lying down in a hole dug for your graves. And I don't intend for any of us to die at all."

Reyes studied her for a long moment. Then he began, joint by joint, to stand.

"Thirty years ago," he said, bracing himself against the timber, "a ranger named Marchetti filed a report about this valley and was destroyed for it. I read the file when I took this posting—it was required reading, framed as a cautionary tale about obsessive incompetence." He got his feet under him. His legs shook. He stayed standing. "It wasn't incompetence, was it? He found the spillway. He found everything we found."

"He found it first," Rosalind said. "And they buried him for it. And then they buried us for believing him." She shouldered her pack. "So tonight we finish his survey. All of it. Every page, every reading, every number that proves what this dam is—and we carry it out of this valley if we have to carry it in our teeth."

She turned to the adit end of the gallery, where the dark breathed cold, and found Ezra standing beside his brother, holding him upright, Tomas's arm over his shoulders, Tomas's eyes open and fixed on her with the fierce attention of a man spending his last reserves on listening.

"Can he walk?" she asked gently.

"He can walk," Ezra said. Then, softer, only for Tomas: "You walked out of worse. You walked out of the flood year. You told me that story a hundred times."

Tomas smiled with cracked lips. "I lied in that story," he rasped. "But yes. I can walk."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of slate and stone, the first rain of the peak arrived—not a patter but a roar, the whole sky letting go at once, the monsoon coming down on the valley like a verdict. Somewhere far off, too far to see, gates were being winched open by men in dry offices.

Rosalind raised her lamp and pointed it up the shaft, toward the rain, toward the river, toward the testimony waiting on the far side of the water.

"Move," she said. "All of you. Move like the flood is already behind you—because it is."

And six broken men, and one thief, and one ranger with her father's compass in her hand, began the long climb out of the dark.
