## 15. Six Souls, One Leaking Boat

The boat was named *Charity*, which Ezra Voss found funny in the way a man finds things funny when he's too tired to be anything else. Someone had painted the name on the stern in flaking gold letters, and beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic: *Property of the River Depot, Kel Anoor Reach*. The government had declared this stretch of valley closed for flood management, which meant the boat had been left to rot, which meant it was theirs.

It was also taking on water at a rate that would have been comical if six lives didn't depend on it.

"Port side seam," Rosalind said, without looking up from where she knelt in the bilge, her hands slick with pitch and river water. "Two fingers wide. It's holding because the wood's swollen, but the moment we hit cold water or the sun bakes us, she'll open like a mouth."

Ezra crouched beside her, passing the tar brush. Around them, the survey team lay slumped against the gunwales like cargo that had given up pretending to be passengers. Six of them. He counted them the way he'd counted them every hour since the mine shaft—out of habit, out of terror that one of them might stop being countable. His brother Dev sat propped against the mast step, gray-faced, his wrists still ringed with the raw marks of whatever they'd bound him with down there in the dark. He hadn't said more than ten words since they'd carried him out. He kept looking at his hands as if he didn't trust them.

"He'll eat," Ezra said quietly.

"He'll eat when his body remembers it wants to," Rosalind said. "Same as the rest of them." She wiped her forearm across her face and left a smear of pitch across her cheekbone, and Ezra thought, absurdly, that she looked more like herself than he'd ever seen her—more than the ranger who'd put a rifle across his chest on the salt flats and demanded her compass back. That woman had been armor all the way through. This one had seams too. She just kept patching them.

He took the bail bucket and started the shift. Scoop, lift, throw. Scoop, lift, throw. The rhythm of it got into his arms and stayed there, an ache with a pulse.

* * *

They ran the river by day and hid by what passed for night under the storm clouds, which had stacked themselves along the western horizon like slate roofing gone wrong—layer over layer, purple-black at the edges, no sun getting through anywhere. The monsoon wasn't coming anymore. It had arrived, and it was simply waiting for its cue.

Rosalind rationed the food at dawn of the second day. Ezra watched her do it: hardtack broken into eight portions instead of four, the dried fish divided until each piece was barely a mouthful, the water measured out in a tin cup that had once belonged, judging by the stamp, to a survey crew that had never come home.

"Halved rations," she announced. "We're two days from the capital reach if the current holds, three if it doesn't. Nobody rows on a full stomach anyway."

One of the surveyors—a geologist named Okonkwo with a beard gone white in five weeks of captivity—laughed at that. It was a thin laugh, cracked down the middle, but it was a laugh, and Ezra saw two of the others straighten slightly at the sound of it, like plants finding light.

"You've done this before," Okonkwo said. "Run rivers."

"She grew up on them," Ezra said, before he could stop himself. He heard how it sounded—not teasing, not quite. Proud. He covered it by hauling on the oar. "I grew up under bridges. Between us we make one competent person."

"Between you," said the survey chief, Anselm Reyes, speaking for the first time since dawn, "you make something better than competent. You make a crew."

Reyes was the one Ezra watched most closely, and not only because the man had spent five weeks in a mine shaft and still sat like he was chairing a meeting. Reyes had been chief of the vanished survey team. Reyes knew what the ministry had wanted buried, and why, and how deep. And on the first night out of the shaft, wrapped in a tarp while rain needled the canvas, Reyes had said a thing that Ezra had been turning over ever since, the way a tongue worries a broken tooth.

*"You think they mean to drown the villages," Reyes had said. "That's the crime. But it's worse. The dam isn't built to hold. The foundations were cut into karst—dissolved limestone, hollowed like honeycomb. My team proved it. That's why we were silenced. Not because the reservoir will flood three valleys. Because within five years of filling, the whole structure will let go at once, and everything downstream of the gorge goes with it. Every village. Every town on the lower reach. Tens of thousands."*

Ezra had asked him then, in the dark, if he was sure.

Reyes had answered with the flatness of a man who had checked his figures eleven times in a hole in the ground by candlelight: *"I'm sure. I've been sure for six months. It's the only sentence I have left."*

Now, on the second morning, with the river rising around them and the banks already swallowing their own trees, Ezra raised it again, because Rosalind needed to hear it in daylight, and because saying it aloud made it a plan instead of a nightmare.

"Tell her," he said. "What you told me. About the dam."

Reyes looked at Rosalind for a long moment. Rain dripped off the awning between them.

"The ministry's story is managed flooding," Reyes said. "Controlled releases, compensation, relocation. A tragedy, but an orderly one. What my survey found is that there will be nothing orderly about it. The abutments sit on karst. Caverns. When the reservoir fills, the water pressure will find every void in that rock and pry it open. The dam won't erode slowly. It won't give warning. One night—and it will be a night, because that's when the reservoir peaks—the whole face will come down at once, and the flood wave will hit the lower valley in under forty minutes." He paused. "Forty minutes is not enough time to run. We measured the evacuation routes. They assumed roads. There are no roads."

Rosalind had gone very still. Ezra knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish.

"They're not drowning three villages," she said.

"They're drowning the proof," Reyes said. "And everyone in it. The flooding looks like negligence. A catastrophic failure looks like an act of God. No one hangs for an act of God."

The river said nothing. It just rose.

* * *

They bailed in shifts through the afternoon: Ezra and Rosalind, then Rosalind and Okonkwo, then Ezra and the youngest surveyor, a hydrologist named Pell whose hands shook so badly at first that half the water went back in the boat. By evening Pell's hands had stopped shaking. Not because he'd steadied. Because he'd emptied himself past trembling. Ezra recognized it. He'd seen it in men coming off long sentences, in his mother after his father died, in himself the night he'd decided to steal a ranger's compass instead of selling the information about it. There was a place past fear where the body just worked, and the working was a kind of mercy.

Dev bailed too. That was the thing that undid Ezra a little—watching his brother, barely able to stand, take the bucket in both ruined hands and scoop, lift, throw, matching the rhythm exactly, refusing help, refusing rest, as if the bailing were the only proof left to him that he was a person who did things rather than a thing that had been done to.

"You don't have to—" Ezra started.

"I know what I don't have to do," Dev said. His voice was gravel. He didn't look up. "Give me the bucket, Ez."

Ezra gave him the bucket.

Rosalind steered from the stern with a broken oar lashed to a jury tiller, reading the water the way other people read print. Ezra had watched her do it for two days now and still couldn't see half of what she saw—the difference between a boil and an eddy line, the dark seam where two currents argued, the place where the surface went glassy-smooth over water deep enough to drown a church. Once, near dusk, she'd called a channel change thirty seconds before the reason for it became visible: a strainer, a whole fallen fig tree strung across the flow like a harp, branches combing the current. Any one of them could have taken the boat apart with everybody in it.

"How far ahead can you see?" he'd asked her afterward.

"My father used to say a river tells you everything twice," she'd said. "Once in the water and once in the land. Most people only listen to the water." Then she'd gone quiet in a particular way she went quiet whenever her father came up, and Ezra had learned not to push. There was a country inside Rosalind Marchetti that she hadn't opened to anyone yet. He suspected the map to it was etched somewhere under her face, the way the map was etched under the compass dial, and that she'd show him when showing cost less than hiding.

* * *

The searchlights came behind them on the second night.

Ezra heard the engines first—a low, patient thrum, upstream and gaining, the sound of a boat that didn't care about rocks because it drew too much water to worry about them. Then the light: a white blade sweeping the bend behind them, cutting the rain into bright shreds.

"Down," Rosalind said, and it wasn't a suggestion. "Awning stays up. Everyone flat. Bail quietly or don't bail."

They flattened. Six bodies and one leaking hull among the reeds at the tail of a long island, the current carrying them sideways toward the lee bank while the patrol boat's light raked the water they'd just left. Ezra lay with his cheek against wet planking and Dev's shoulder against his own, and he could feel his brother breathing—fast, shallow, the breathing of a man back in a shaft with the door closing—and he put his hand over Dev's wrist and held on, and felt the breathing slow by degrees, matched to his own.

The light passed over them once. Held. Passed on.

Through the gap under the awning Ezra watched the patrol boat slide by downstream of them, running lights green and red, a silhouette at the rail that might have been Corliss and might have been any man in a ministry coat. It didn't turn. It kept going, hunting the dark water ahead of them for a leaky boat full of ghosts.

"They'll figure it out," Rosalind whispered, when the engines had faded to memory. "An hour, maybe two. Then they'll come back upriver with the light."

"Then we don't give them two hours," Ezra said.

He meant it as tactics. But he heard, as it left him, that it was more than that—that somewhere in the last three days, without either of them deciding it, the shape of their command had changed. On the salt flats she had led and he had followed because she had the rifle. In the narrows he had led and she had followed because he had the river. Now it moved between them like the bail bucket, passed hand to hand without discussion, and neither of them dropped it.

She looked at him across the dark boat, rain running off her jaw, and he saw that she'd noticed it too.

"Reyes," she said. "How fast can your people move?"

Reyes lifted his head from under the tarp. Five weeks in a hole, and the man's voice came out level as a spirit gauge. "Slowly. But steadily. We walked out of that mine on legs we didn't have. We can walk into whatever comes next."

"Then here's what comes next." Rosalind turned and pointed upstream, back the way they'd come, toward the black shoulder of the gorge where the old dam's grandmother slept—the original reservoir works, the ones her father's compass mapped and the ministry's charts pretended didn't exist. "There's a cut in the east wall above the high-water line. The compass shows a service gallery leading to the old spillway tunnel. Dry stone. High enough that even a record flood won't touch it. If the boats are being watched, we go overland, up the goat track, and we put these six people somewhere Corliss can't find them while I get the evidence to the capital."

"That track hasn't been walked in fifty years," Ezra said.

"Neither had we," said Dev, from the darkness, "until yesterday."

Ezra laughed—really laughed, for the first time since the mine shaft—and heard Okonkwo join him, and then Pell, ragged and surprised at itself, laughter moving down the boat like warmth through cold hands.

Rosalind didn't laugh. But she smiled, briefly, in the rain, and Ezra thought: *there it is. The seam under the face.*

"Then we move now," she said, "while their light is pointed the wrong way."

* * *

They grounded the *Charity* in the willows below the goat track and Ezra stood in the shallows a moment, hand on the flaking gold letters, feeling oddly like he was abandoning a friend. Two days of bailing. Two days of the old girl swallowing the river and complaining about it and keeping them alive anyway.

"Come on," Rosalind said from the bank. "She's earned her rest."

They went up single file into the rain—six broken surveyors, one thief, one ex-ranger, and a compass—and behind them the river rose and rose, patient as a debt, carrying their wake toward a valley that did not yet know what was coming for it.

Ezra climbed with his brother's arm over his shoulders and did not look back.
