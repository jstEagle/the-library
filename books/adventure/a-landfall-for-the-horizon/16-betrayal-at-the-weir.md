## 16. Betrayal at the Weir

The rain had found its rhythm by the second day downriver—a steady, drumming insistence that turned the world to gray wool and made every sound arrive late. Rosalind bailed with a dented tin pan, passing it to the surveyor named Okafor on the even strokes and taking it back on the odd ones, and she told herself the rhythm was the only thing keeping her from thinking about what waited downstream.

Six souls. One boat built for three. And behind them, somewhere in the rain, Corliss's searchlights sweeping the banks like slow lighthouse beams hunting for something to break.

"Even," Okafor said, and handed her the pan.

She took it. Dipped. Poured. The river was coming up through the hull faster now; she could feel it in the way the boat sat lower in the water, in the way the current grabbed at them when they crossed the channel's throat. Ahead, through the rain curtain, the old weir showed itself in pieces—first the white seam of broken water, then the dark comb of the spill gates, half-collapsed, their iron teeth furred with sixty years of moss.

Ezra stood at the stern with the sweep oar, reading the water the way she read ground. He'd been doing it for two days without rest, and it showed in the hollows under his eyes, but his hands were steady.

"We go left of the third gate," he called back over the storm. "There's a chute where the old fish ladder used to be. It'll be rough, but it's deep water all the way through. Right side is rocks."

Rosalind looked at the weir and felt her father's map superimposed over it—the compass etching, the spillway line running beneath this structure like a vein beneath skin. Her father had stood here once. Had measured this water. Had been destroyed for what he found.

"Left of the third gate," she confirmed.

In the bottom of the boat, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, were the survey chief's records: field notebooks, core samples, the engineering assessment that said what everyone in the boat already knew—that the new dam wasn't just an atrocity, it was a mistake. Built on shale that would not hold. Designed to fail within a decade, catastrophically, sending a wall of water into towns the ministry's own maps didn't bother to name. The evidence weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Get it to the capital. That was the whole mission now. Get it to someone who couldn't be bought, before the flood buried the last of it.

* * *

It was Okafor who asked to stop.

They'd put the weir behind them—or so Rosalind thought—and were running a long, slow reach where the valley widened and the rain came down gentler, almost apologetic. The rescued surveyors lay packed together under the tarp, six men reduced to the size of their hunger. Ezra's brother Tam slept with his head in Ezra's lap, his breathing a thin whistle.

"There." Okafor pointed. "The relay station."

A concrete hut on the left bank, government-gray, its antenna mast leaning like a drunk against a fence post. A relic of some earlier survey, probably. Probably dead.

"The radio could be dead too," Rosalind said.

"It could also call ahead to the capital," Okafor said. "Medical. Police. Anyone." His voice cracked on the last word, and she saw it then—not treachery in his face, just fear worn down to the wood. Two weeks in a mine shaft will do that. Two weeks listening for boots. "We're dying by inches in this boat, Ranger. All due respect."

Ezra's eyes met hers across the boat. She knew what he was thinking: every hour stopped was an hour Corliss gained. But she also knew what six starving men needed, and it wasn't her schedule.

"Ten minutes," she said. "We check it fast. If it's dead, we move on."

The relay station smelled of bat guano and rust. But when Okafor worked the power switch, a generator coughed twice in the back room and caught, and the radio panel lit up amber, and every man in the boat came ashore as if pulled by wires.

Rosalind let them. She stood in the doorway watching the rain and hated herself a little for how good it felt to see hope on their faces—even the wrong kind of hope, even hope aimed at the same government that had caged them. People needed to believe in something. She'd spent her whole life believing in a service that had ruined her father, because the alternative was believing in nothing, and nothing was a weight no one could carry.

The set crackled. Found a frequency. Okafor's hands shook as he keyed the mic.

"This is Surveyor Okafor, Kesh Valley expedition, calling any station, we have survivors—"

Static. Then a voice, smooth as poured concrete.

"Okafor. This is Director Corliss's office. We've been expecting you."

Rosalind turned. Okafor had gone the color of the concrete walls.

"They told me—" he started. "When they took us, they said if I ever got out, if I cooperated, my pension would be protected. My family. They said—" His eyes skittered away from hers. "They gave me a frequency. They said I'd know when to use it."

The rain filled the silence.

"You've known since the mine," Ezra said quietly. He wasn't angry. That was worse. He sounded like a man confirming a diagnosis. "You've been waiting for a radio."

"I have children," Okafor whispered.

"So did Marta," Ezra said. "So does everyone in Kel Anoor, and Corliss is going to drown their houses and call it flood management. You know that. You were down there because you wrote the report that said it."

Okafor's hand moved toward the transmit key.

Rosalind didn't think. She crossed the room and closed her hand over his, and for a moment they stood there, her fingers around his fist around the future, both of them trembling.

"Okafor." She kept her voice level, ranger-calm, the voice she'd used on panicked hikers and flooded campsites for eleven years. "Look at me. If you make that call, Corliss doesn't protect you. Men like him use people until they're used up—you know that better than anyone, you watched him do it to your whole team. Your pension isn't in his gift. It's in the record." She lifted the oilcloth bundle slightly. "This is your pension. This is your family's protection. Not a promise from a man who buries witnesses."

Okafor looked at the bundle. Looked at her. Something warred in his face—she watched it happen, watched forty years of a man's smallness fight thirty years of a man's decency.

Then the radio spoke again, unprompted, patient as a spider.

"Okafor. We know the Ranger is there. Tell her: Director Corliss offers full reinstatement. Clean record. Her father's name cleared in the official register. All she has to do is put down the bundle and step away from the boat."

* * *

Her father's name.

The words went through her like a spear through water—no resistance, straight to the center. Thirty years she had carried that name like a stone in her boot. *Disgraced.* Struck from the honor rolls. The reason she'd joined the rangers at seventeen, the reason she'd never missed a shift, never bent a rule, never given them a single inch of excuse—they'd taken her father and she'd spent her whole life proving she was not him, proving loyalty could be earned back, one unblemished season at a time.

And here it was, offered back to her. Wiped clean. By the same machine that had dirtied it.

That was how she knew it was a lie. Not because Corliss was a liar—though he was—but because the offer itself was the confession. They didn't clear names out of kindness. They cleared names the way you'd pay a ransom: because the hostage was valuable. Because her father had been right, and they had destroyed him for it, and the proof of both facts was wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs.

Beside her, Okafor had gone very still. On the bunk by the wall, Tam Voss opened his eyes—fever-bright, barely conscious—and said, in a voice like dry leaves:

"Don't take it, girl. Names aren't theirs to give."

* * *

They were back on the water within four minutes. Rosalind had ordered it herself, cold and fast, leaving the generator running and the radio open—a decoy heartbeat, a signal fire of static to suggest they'd stayed longer than they had. It bought them maybe twenty minutes. She'd take twenty minutes.

But she'd seen Okafor's face as they pushed off. Seen the way his eyes stayed on the receding relay station the way a dog watches a door.

"He'll do it," Ezra said beside her at the stern. Not accusing. Just certain. "Not today. Maybe not tomorrow. But at night, when we're camped, or at the next stop—he'll find a way. Fear doesn't drown, Rosalind. It just swims alongside."

"Then we don't stop again," she said. "Straight through. Capital by tomorrow night."

"And if the boat doesn't make it?"

She looked at the hull. At the water seeping up between the planks faster than the tin pan could chase it. At the sky, which had gone the color of wet slate and was stacking itself into towers along the southern horizon—the monsoon's main body, arriving early, arriving angry.

"Then we swim with it," she said.

The old weir appeared around the next bend sooner than she wanted.

* * *

She understood later that it was the perfect place for an ambush, and that she should have seen it coming, and that the fact she didn't was exhaustion talking. The weir forced every boat on this river through a single throat. Left of the third gate, Ezra had said, the fish-ladder chute. One way through. One way to wait.

The first flare went up as they entered the broken water.

It burst red above the far bank, and in its light she saw them: two patrol boats tucked into the lee below the spill gates, engines idling, men crouched behind mounted lamps. Corliss hadn't guessed. Corliss had *known*. Which meant the radio call had already been made—which meant Okafor had done it back at the relay station, or Corliss had simply tracked them the whole way and let them run themselves ragged into this bottle.

"Ezra!" she screamed, and he was already moving, throwing his weight on the sweep oar, hauling the bow hard toward the right side—the rocks, the side he'd sworn was death—because the left side was worse now, the left side was guns.

"Everyone down!" She shoved the tarp flat over the surveyors, over the records, felt the boat heel as the current took them sideways toward the gate teeth. Water broke over the gunwale, cold as a slap. Somewhere behind her a loudspeaker was barking words she refused to hear—*surrender the documents, surrender the vessel*—words that belonged to another life, another Rosalind, one who still believed orders were a kind of safety.

The first shot hit the hull.

Not near the hull. *In* it. A splinter geysered up off the inner planking a hand's width from her knee, and the sound of it—the flat, businesslike crack of it—stripped away the last romantic fog from the whole enterprise. This was not a negotiation. This was a drowning with paperwork.

"Right side!" Ezra roared. "Trust me!"

She trusted him. God help her, after everything—after the theft and the lies and the salt flats—she trusted him completely, and she threw her body against the port side to counterbalance his turn, and the boat swung like a compass needle finding north, and they shot the gap between two drowned boulders with inches to spare on either side.

The rocks should have killed them. Instead the rocks saved them—the patrol boats couldn't follow into the boulder garden, too deep-drafted, too expensive to risk—and for ninety glorious seconds they were threading white water in the rain with the flares burning red overhead and Rosalind laughing, actually laughing, a wild sound she didn't recognize as her own.

Then the second patrol boat rounded the downstream point.

It had been waiting below the weir. Of course it had. Corliss thought in layers; she was learning that. The boulder garden spat them out directly into its path, forty meters of open water between them and a boat with an engine that worked properly and a deck gun bolted to its bow.

"Hard aground," Ezra said, very calm, the calm of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "They'll board us in two minutes. There's a shingle bar—" he pointed left, to a gravel spit in the river's elbow, "—we beach there, we scatter on foot, they can't chase six directions in the dark."

"On foot," Rosalind repeated. "With Tam. With the records. In this rain."

"With Tam carried. With the records split between pockets." He was already passing the oilcloth bundle to her, dividing the notebooks, stuffing pages inside shirts, inside boots. "You take the capital road east. It's eight miles to the telegraph office at Harrow Bend. You can wire the whole record from there."

"*We* take the capital road."

"No." He said it gently, which was how she knew he'd decided it before he said it. "Someone holds them at the boat. Someone makes them believe the records are still aboard, that the prize is still in play. Otherwise they don't chase you, they just shell the shoreline." He smiled, and it was Ezra's real smile, the thief's smile, quick and crooked and infuriating. "I'm the one they want anyway. Compass thief. River rat. Expendable."

"You are not—"

"Rosalind." He took her face in both wet hands, and the rain ran down between them, and for one second neither of them was running from anything. "My brother is in that tarp. Everything I stole, everything I lied for—it's all in that bundle now. You're the one who gets it there. That's not a sacrifice. That's a promotion."

The patrol boat's searchlight found them, pinning the boat to the water like a specimen.

He kissed her forehead, once, brief as a stamp on a document. Then he stepped to the transom, raised both arms high, and shouted into the light with a thief's magnificent theatrical timing:

"All right! All right, you've got me! Nobody shoot—I'm coming in! The woman's a hostage, she's innocent!"

And he dove over the side into the black water, swimming toward the patrol boat with strong, showy strokes, hands visible, surrendering beautifully, loudly, drawing every eye and every lamp and every gun toward himself and away from the shingle bar where Rosalind was already hauling Tam Voss onto her shoulders and shouting the others into the shallows.

* * *

They scattered. That part worked exactly as he'd designed it—six figures splitting into the cane grass and the dark, the patrol boats' lights swinging wildly, trying to track them all, tracking none of them well. Rosalind went east with Tam on her back and half the records against her skin, and she did not look back, because looking back was a luxury for people whose hearts weren't being towed downstream in enemy custody.

She made a mile before she let herself stop, in the lee of a fallen fig tree, and set Tam down, and listened.

Behind her, downriver, the searchlights had converged into a single bright knot. She heard the loudspeaker, faint now, triumphant. She heard—she was almost sure she heard—laughter, men pleased with their night's work.

They had Ezra Voss. The compass thief. The man who'd taught her that stolen things could still be true.

Tam stirred against her shoulder and murmured, feverish: "Don't... don't trade yourself, girl. He wouldn't want..."

"Hush," she said. "Save your breath."

But she was already doing the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was cruel. Eight miles east to the telegraph office and the truth delivered. Four miles back downriver to whatever stockade Corliss used, and a bargain offered—herself for the thief, the ranger's badge, her freedom, everything, traded for one man while the record traveled on without her.

She could not do both. That was the whole lesson of her life, finally made flesh and standing in the rain: loyalty and truth, wearing each other's faces, holding out their hands, and only enough of her to go to one.

Rosalind Marchetti stood in the flooded dark with her father's map against her heart and made her choice.

She turned west.
