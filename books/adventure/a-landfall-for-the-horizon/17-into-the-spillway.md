## 17. Into the Spillway

The rain came down like a verdict.

Rosalind stood at the mouth of the gorge with the compass open in her palm, water sheeting off her hood, and watched six broken people huddle against the canyon wall behind her. The survey chief, Okafor, leaned on a driftwood crutch. His glasses were gone—lost somewhere in the mine shaft—and he squinted at the world through rain-blurred eyes as if it were a document he no longer trusted. Ezra's brother Tomas hung between two of his colleagues, his feet dragging twin furrows in the mud. The man who had betrayed them, Pell, kept his face turned away from everyone.

And the river was rising.

She could hear it before she saw it—a deep, arterial thrum through the rock beneath her boots, the sound of a valley being filled like a basin. The ministry's charts, the ones Corliss had waved at her in the depot, showed this gorge as a dead-end box canyon. *Impassable. Seasonally flooded. No egress.*

Her father's map showed something else.

She tilted the compass until the etched lines caught the grey light. Beneath the brass face, scratched into the metal with a jeweler's patience, the hidden cartography ran down the gorge and terminated at a symbol she had stared at for three days without understanding: a circle bisected by a stair-step line, annotated in her father's small, slanted hand on the paper insert in the case lid—*the old throat. It breathes. Trust it.*

"Rosalind." Okafor's voice was a rasp. "The ministry survey from ninety-one mapped this canyon. It terminates in a rockfall. There's nothing through."

"The ministry survey from ninety-one," Rosalind said, "also reported that this valley had no permanent settlements worth compensating." She looked at him. "I'm done trusting their maps."

She turned and led them into the gorge.

* * *

The walls closed in and the rain became a roar funneled overhead, a white river of noise. They moved in single file along a ledge barely wide enough for their shoulders, the flood-swollen tributary churning six feet below. Rosalind counted steps the way her father had taught her to count—*pace, pace, check your bearing, trust the pace*—and matched each landmark to the etching. The split boulder like a broken tooth. The seep spring staining the rock orange with iron. The lightning-scarred juniper, still clinging to its impossible shelf.

Every one of them was there. Thirty years old, the map, and every mark still true.

*He walked this ledge,* she thought, and the thought arrived with a physical weight, like a hand on her shoulder. *He stood where I'm standing. He counted these same steps.*

"Marchetti." Pell's voice, thin and querulous from the rear. "How far?"

"As far as it takes."

"You don't even know if—"

"Then don't waste breath finding out."

Ezra was behind Pell, and Rosalind heard him murmur something low—she caught only the word *brother*—and Pell went quiet. Tomas had rallied a little in the last hour, enough to walk with a hand on the wall instead of on two men's necks, and every time Rosalind glanced back she found Ezra watching his brother with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: the look of someone carrying a person they could not afford to drop.

The ledge narrowed. Then it ended.

Ahead, exactly as the ministry promised, the canyon wall had fallen—a jumble of house-sized blocks choked with flood debris. But her father's line didn't stop at the rockfall. It went *through* it, down and left, into what the etching showed as a slot between two fallen slabs—a throat of stone.

She found it: a gap three feet wide, black as a well, breathing cold air against her wet face.

*The old throat. It breathes.*

"Torches," she said.

* * *

Inside, the world changed.

The slot opened within twenty paces into a gallery—a vaulted corridor of dressed stone, wide enough for six, tall enough for Okafor to stand straight. Their torchlight ran along walls of fitted blocks, dry as bone, and the sound of the monsoon fell away behind them until it was only a memory in the rock, a heartbeat in the mountain's chest.

Okafor stopped dead. He took off his ruined hat, which he had clung to through everything, and held it in both hands like a man entering a church.

"This is cut stone," he said. "This is *dressed* stone. This isn't a mine."

"It's a spillway," Rosalind said. Her voice echoed down the corridor and came back changed. "The original one. Before the ministry's dam, before the ministry's charts—there was a reservoir here. My grandfather's generation built it. Then the ministry built its dam downstream, and the old works were—"

"Misdocumented," Okafor said quietly. "I've read the ninety-one survey. I *wrote* half of it. There is no mention of—"

"There was no mention of a lot of things."

They went on. The corridor descended in long, gentle ramps—engineered, deliberate—and at each bend Rosalind checked the compass and found the etched line waiting, faithful as a pulse. Twice they passed side galleries choked with rubble. Once they crossed a bridge of stone over a dry channel that, she understood with a chill, would carry water again before morning.

Then the torchlight found the tools.

They were racked against the wall in an alcove, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: picks, shovels, a hand-crank drill, coils of rope still coiled. And beside them, a wooden crate, its stenciled lettering faded but legible in the torchlight:

**R. MARCHETTI — SURVEY PARTY — 3RD EXPEDITION**

Rosalind's father's name.

Her hands were shaking so badly she could barely work the crate's lid. Inside, packed in straw that had waited three decades for this moment: tinned food, rusted at the seams but sealed. Matches in waxed blocks. Blankets. A medical kit. And on top of it all, wrapped in oilcloth like the tools, a leather journal with a brass clasp.

She knew the handwriting before she opened it. She had grown up watching it across a kitchen table.

*If you are reading this, then the map held, and you are one of mine, or one of theirs, and either way you are welcome here.*

She read it aloud. Her voice cracked on the second line and she kept going anyway, because six starving people were standing in the dark behind her and the words were rations too.

*They will tell you I falsified my findings. Let them. I surveyed this valley the way I was taught—pace, bearing, trust the pace—and the old spillway is exactly where I have marked it, and it is sound, and it will hold against any water the sky can send. I have stocked it against the day someone needs it. I do not know who that will be. I only know that a map is a promise you make to people you will never meet. Keep it dry. Keep it lit. Keep going.*

Below the signature, in smaller script, almost an afterthought:

*Ros. If it's you—I'm sorry I let them make you ashamed of me. I was never ashamed of the work. Look at the walls. Look at the walls and you'll understand.*

Rosalind lowered the journal.

The torch hissed. Water ticked somewhere in the deep of the mountain, patient as a clock. Behind her, no one spoke. Then she felt a hand on her shoulder—Ezra's, brief and hard, the grip of a man who did not offer comfort often and meant it when he did—and she turned to the wall her father had told her to look at.

She looked at the walls.

Fitted stone, jointed with a precision no government crew would bother with for a structure officially declared nonexistent. And there, chiseled beside the tool alcove, small and neat as a signature: a surveyor's benchmark. Her father's benchmark. The same mark that stood on the ridge above their old house, the one she had touched a thousand times as a girl without knowing what it meant—*I was here. I measured honestly. I will be found.*

He had known. Thirty years ago, he had walked this corridor in the dry season with a crate on his back and a chisel in his hand, and he had built a refuge for a daughter he couldn't warn and strangers he'd never meet, and he had trusted the stone and the map and the rain to do the rest.

"He planned this," she said. Not to anyone. To him. "You planned this. All of it."

The echo carried her voice down the corridor, into the dark, and it felt—not like an answer. Like company.

* * *

They made camp in a side gallery off the main ramp, above the high-water line her father had chiseled into the wall with a date: the flood of thirty-one years ago, and a mark two feet below the gallery floor. The old works had been tested. The old works had held.

Okafor and his people worked with the grim efficiency of the starving. Tomas lay wrapped in a blanket, awake now, watching Ezra with eyes that were too bright. Rosalind distributed the tins—her father's tins, thirty years old, and every seal held, because he had never in his life done a job halfway—and set the waxed matches to a small fire in a stone hearth that was already there, blackened by use, waiting.

He had even built a hearth.

She sat against the wall with the journal open on her knees and read it by firelight while the mountain boomed around them. Page after page in the small slanted hand: flow measurements, stone tests, rainfall records going back decades, gathered in secret after his disgrace, when he was supposed to be a broken man tending a dry-goods store. He had come back. Year after year, he had come back, walking the ledge she had walked, checking the seals on the tins, rewrapping the tools.

And every visit ended the same way, in the same four words, written at the bottom of the final page:

*The throat still breathes.*

"Rosalind." Ezra crouched beside her. He nodded toward the corridor, toward the deep rumble that had been building for the last hour, felt now in the floor, in her teeth. "That's not rain anymore. That's a release. They've opened the gates."

"I know."

"Okafor says if the ministry's dam fails the way he thinks it will—"

"It will," Okafor called from across the fire. "I've seen the spillway specs. They were falsified. I signed them. I'll say so in whatever court will listen, if there's a court left to listen in."

"—then this valley is gone by morning," Ezra finished. "Kel Anoor. The lowland farms. Everything downstream of the weir." He looked at her, and in the firelight his face was stripped of all its streetwise armor. "The people who wouldn't leave."

Rosalind closed the journal. She thought of the rooftops of Kel Anoor, the woman who had pressed bread into her hands, the children who had stared at her boat as if rescue were a rumor. She thought of the radio in the depot, the polite voice of the Ministry of Water announcing *scheduled flood management.* She thought of her father's last line, the one he'd written for her and no one else.

*Keep going.*

"Then the evidence can't drown with the valley," she said. "Okafor's records. The journal. The photographs from the mine. The testimony of six people the ministry tried to bury." She stood, and her legs ached, and her eyes burned, and she had never in her life been more certain of anything. "It all reaches the capital. Before Corliss can write his report."

"There's a radio relay on the ridge above the weir," Okafor said slowly. "Old line, pre-ministry. If it still functions—"

"Then someone has to take it there." She was already repacking the compass, the journal, the oilcloth bundle of photographs. "And someone has to stay here and keep these five alive."

Ezra rose with her. "You're not going alone."

"Someone has to stay with Tomas."

"Then you're *certainly* not going alone."

She looked at him. He looked back, jaw set, rain still dripping from his hair, and she understood that this was not an argument she was going to win, and that she didn't entirely want to.

"The flood peaks at dawn," she said. "We move now."

* * *

At the mouth of the gallery, where the corridor bent down toward the old throat and the sound of the rising water came up through the stone like the voice of something vast turning over in its sleep, Rosalind stopped. She set her palm flat against the fitted blocks of the wall, against the stone her father's hands had measured and her grandfather's generation had dressed, and she felt it trembling faintly under the flood's weight—and holding.

*Look at the walls and you'll understand.*

"I understand," she said quietly.

Then she turned up the ramp, toward the ridge and the relay and the truth, with Ezra at her shoulder and the whole valley holding its breath below.
