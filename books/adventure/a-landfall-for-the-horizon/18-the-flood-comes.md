## 18. The Flood Comes

The water spoke first.

Ezra heard it before anyone else—a low, grinding murmur beneath the spillway's stone floor, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. He had grown up on the river; he knew its voices the way other men knew their mothers'. This was a voice he had never heard before, and every instinct in him said *run*.

"Rosalind." His own whisper came out cracked. "It's starting."

She was thirty feet down the tunnel, hanging a lantern from an iron hook her father's generation had driven into the rock before either of them was born. She turned, and the lamplight caught the map spread open at her feet—the old surveyor's chart, ink faded to the color of weak tea, marked with her father's careful hand. "The release isn't scheduled until dawn," she said. "Corliss's timetable—"

"Corliss's timetable is for men who think they control rivers." Ezra pressed his palm flat against the wall. The vibration was faint but unmistakable, a pulse moving through forty-year-old masonry. "Listen. That's the headgate. They're opening it now."

Behind them, huddled against the far wall of the spillway's first chamber, the six survivors of the survey team went quiet. One of them—Okafor, the hydrologist, his wrists still ringed with the raw marks of his shackles—began to pray under his breath in a language Ezra didn't know. Tam Voss lay on a bedroll of sacking, his brother's hand wrapped around his, his breathing shallow as paper. He had spoken maybe twenty words since they'd pulled him out of the mine shaft, and half of them had been Ezra's name.

"How long do we have?" Rosalind asked. No panic in her voice. That was the thing about her that still surprised him, after everything: she got quieter as the danger grew, the way deep water runs smooth over rocks that would tear a boat open.

"An hour. Maybe less if they've opened more than one gate." Ezra looked back down the tunnel toward the entrance, where gray daylight showed through the great iron grate that sealed the spillway's mouth. Beyond it, the valley. Kel Anoor. Marta's village, or what was left of it after the patrols came through. The cane fields where they'd run with flares blooming overhead like dying stars. The river itself, which had been his whole childhood, his whole education, his whole inheritance.

All of it about to become the bottom of a lake built by men who signed their names in triplicate.

"We can't stop it," Okafor said. It wasn't despair. It was arithmetic. "The gates are steel, the mechanisms are behind locked housings, and even if we reached them—" He gestured at himself, at the others. Six people who could barely walk. "We'd die trying, and it would change nothing."

"I know." Rosalind rolled up the map with hands that didn't shake. "So we make sure it changes nothing *else*. Everything we brought out of the mine—the chief's logs, the photographs, the correspondence—it goes into the dry chamber, third level, above the high-water line. Tonight. Before the water finds us."

Ezra stared at her. "You want to stay *in* it."

"I want to be alive when it's over, and this is the only place in the valley that will be." She met his eyes across the lamplight. "Your grandfather helped build this. My father mapped it. Two generations of men who were called liars, and they were both right. I'm not going to bet our lives on Corliss's engineering when we're standing inside the proof of theirs."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the daylight, and Ezra learned what it meant to move six starving people through stone.

Tam could walk ten steps before his legs quit; Ezra carried him the next twenty, his brother's weight shocking him—not how heavy he was, but how light. When had Tam gotten so light? Tam had been the strong one, the one who'd rowed the ferry crossing at flood stage while Ezra bailed, the one who'd laughed at the storm. Now he weighed less than the survey equipment they'd hauled out of the shaft, and he kept apologizing, over and over, in a voice worn down to a thread.

"Stop it," Ezra told him, settling him against the wall of the second chamber. "You don't get to apologize. You held on. That's the whole job. You held on for four months in a hole in the ground, and now you hold on for one more night, and then you're done holding on forever."

Tam's eyes found his. In the lantern light they were fever-bright. "The others," he whispered. "In the shaft. There was a seventh. Before us. An old man from Kel Anoor. They took him out one day and he didn't come back." A pause, and then, worse: "I think it was Marta's husband."

Ezra closed his eyes. He thought of Marta's hands around the compass, calling it *the key your father buried*, thought of her standing in her doorway watching them go downstream, thought of her taken by the patrol two nights ago because some frightened neighbor had sold their location for a sack of rice. He put the thought away where he kept all the others, in the place that was getting fuller every day, the place that was turning from grief into something with edges.

Purpose, Rosalind called it. He was starting to understand what she meant.

By full dark they had everything that mattered stacked in the upper chamber: the survey chief's field books, forty pages of measurements proving the dam's foundations sat on shale that would liquefy; the photographs of the test releases that had drowned the first village without warning; the carbon copies of ministry memos, smuggled in with supply crates, ordering the team to revise its findings or remain guests of the government indefinitely. Rosalind packed them in oilcloth, in tin boxes, in anything waterproof, and labeled each bundle in her precise ranger's script.

"The water won't touch this chamber," she said, running her fingers along the ceiling. "My father made sure of it. Look at the tool marks—he widened it himself, or had it done. This wasn't just a spillway. It was an ark. He built a place for the truth to survive the flood."

Ezra looked at the old chisel scars in the rock, decades old, and felt the hair rise on his arms. Somewhere down there in the dark, a disgraced surveyor had stood exactly where they stood, knowing what was coming, preparing for it, and no one had ever known. Thirty years of shame laid on a man who had been right the entire time.

"He planned for us," Ezra said quietly. "Before we existed. He planned for strangers."

"That's what belonging is, maybe." Rosalind hung the last lantern. "Doing the work before you know who needs it."

* * *

The water arrived at midnight, and it did not knock.

Ezra heard the roar come up the valley like weather given a body—a sound he felt in his teeth, in the soles of his feet, in the cage of his ribs. The vibration in the walls became a shudder. Dust sifted down from the ceiling of the outer chamber in fine gray veils. And then, through the iron grate at the tunnel mouth, the darkness beyond began to glow.

Not firelight. Moonlight on moving water, rising.

He couldn't help himself. He went to the grate, and Rosalind came with him, and together they knelt in the dark and watched the end of the world arrive at their door.

The flood came down the river channel not as a wave but as a wall—a broad, brown, muscular wall of water, taller than a man, rolling forward with the patience of something that had been ordered rather than unleashed. It hit the lower fields first. He saw the cane go under all at once, an acre of green swallowed between one heartbeat and the next. Then the water found the houses.

Kel Anoor had been a village for two hundred years. Its rooftops were tile and thatch, its lanes were crooked, its well-house had a bell that rang for festivals and funerals alike. Ezra had drunk palm wine on those rooftops during the harvest festival three years ago, back when he was only a thief and not yet a witness. Now he watched the water climb the walls of those houses like a slow animal, patient, certain, and take them one by one. Tile roofs lifted and spun. A whole house tore free of its foundations and sailed past the grate, turning slowly, a lamp still burning inside it, a little yellow eye going under.

He heard someone behind him begin to sob—one of the surveyors, a young man named Pell—and heard Okafor murmuring comfort, and Ezra could not turn around. He owed the dead his eyes. Somebody had to watch. Somebody had to remember exactly how it happened, in what order, at what hour, so that when the time came he could stand in a hearing room and say: *at eleven minutes past midnight, the water reached the mosque, and here is how long the minaret stood.*

The minaret stood nine minutes. He counted.

Beside him, Rosalind was perfectly still. Her face in the reflected light was the face of a woman memorizing a crime scene—which, he realized, was exactly what it was. Every rooftop that vanished was evidence. Every drowned field was a signature. The ministry had chosen the monsoon precisely so the flood would look like weather, and here they crouched behind the only wall in the valley that the water could not breach, watching the alibi being written in mud.

"They'll call it natural," Ezra said. His voice sounded foreign to him, scraped flat.

"They'll try." Rosalind's jaw was set. "But the gates opened early. On schedule, the release would have been gradual, metered—that's what their own documents say. What we just watched was a dump. Someone panicked. Corliss knows we're loose, knows what we carried out of that mine, and he flushed the valley to bury it." She turned to look back at the survivors, at Tam sleeping fitfully against the wall. "Which means somewhere in that water is a paper trail with his fingerprints, and he thinks it's gone."

"It is gone. It's all gone." Ezra gestured at the brown expanse where the village had been. The water had stopped rising against the grate—or slowed, rather, climbing now by inches instead of yards—but the valley beyond was a lake already, flat and moon-silvered and utterly silent. No dogs. No bells. Nothing.

"No." Rosalind touched the oilcloth bundles stacked in the dry chamber behind them. "The evidence is right here. And there's more than us. The chief sent duplicates—remember, the night at the boathouse, he told us he'd radioed his deputy in the capital weeks ago, coded, before the arrests. If Corliss buried the valley to bury the records, he's buried nothing. He's destroyed the villages for nothing." Something moved through her face then, grief and fury braided into a single hard cord. "We just have to live long enough to say so."

* * *

Dawn came gray through the grate, and the lake outside had settled into a terrible calm.

Ezra stood watch while the others slept in shifts, though sleep came to him in shallow scraps. The water had risen to within four feet of the tunnel mouth and stopped, held by some balance of inflow and outflow that the old engineers had designed into the mountain. Their grandparents' work, holding true while the ministry's dam groaned somewhere upstream. He thought about that a lot in the small hours: the difference between things built to last and things built to pass inspection.

Near morning, Tam woke coughing, and Ezra sat with him, propping him up, giving him water in small sips.

"I dreamed about the river," Tam said. His voice was stronger today, or maybe Ezra needed it to be. "The real one. Before all this. You remember the narrows in spring, when the meltwater comes down?"

"I remember you flipping the boat and blaming me for it."

"You *were* steering." A ghost of the old grin crossed his ruined face, and Ezra had to look away for a second, blink hard, breathe. "When we get out of here," Tam went on, quieter, "I want to run the narrows again. Properly. Both of us. I've been thinking about it for four months, down in that hole. Just that. The water, and the boat, and you yelling at me."

Ezra gripped his brother's shoulder, careful of the bones. "Then that's the plan. We get out, we testify, we burn Corliss's whole rotten project to the ground, and then we run the narrows in spring. You steer. I'll yell."

Tam slept again, and Ezra stayed by him, and outside the grate the lake lay still as poured metal, hiding everything it had taken.

It was mid-morning when Rosalind came to him, moving fast, the map in her hand and an expression he'd learned to fear and love in equal measure.

"Ezra. The water level." She pointed through the grate. "Look at it. It's dropped. Three inches since dawn—maybe four."

"So the outflow's catching up. Good, isn't it?"

"No." She was already unrolling the chart, her finger stabbing down into the labyrinth of tunnels below them. "It means the water's going somewhere else. Downstream. Through channels my father marked on this map—channels that lead out of the reservoir system entirely." Her finger traced a line through the rock, down and down, and stopped at a notation in faded ink, her father's handwriting, a single word beside a junction of tunnels:

*Exit.*

She looked up at him, and in the lantern light her eyes were burning.

"There's a way out of this mountain that isn't the way we came in. And if the water's already using it—" a thin, fierce smile, the first he'd seen on her since the flood began— "then so can we. Get the others up. We're leaving."
