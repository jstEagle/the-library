## 19. Corliss's Reckoning

The rain had thinned to a fine gray sift by the time Rosalind reached the weir, and the world smelled of wet stone and diesel exhaust. She came down the last stretch of the towpath with her hands empty and visible, the way you approach a spooked animal or a man with a gun—because Corliss was both.

He stood on the concrete apron below the old sluice gates with two patrol officers flanking him and Ezra between them, wrists bound in front of him with the plastic ties the Ministry favored, cheap ones that bit into skin. His lip was split. He was grinning anyway, that crooked river-rat grin she'd first seen across a salt flat at dusk, aimed at her like he couldn't help it even now.

"You're supposed to be gone," Corliss said when she stopped ten paces out. He didn't raise his voice; the rain did the work of making everything sound like an accusation. "You were given a boat and a head start and a very generous interpretation of your movements. Instead you're here."

"I'm here to trade."

His eyes narrowed—not suspicion exactly, but the wariness of a man who has spent his career being offered things that turn out to cost more than they're worth. "Trade what?"

"Me for him." She kept her voice level, ranger-calm, the voice she'd used on panicked hikers and flooded homesteads for eleven years. "You want the compass. You want me off the record, contained, quiet. I'm offering all of it. Him, you let walk. He's nobody to you. A thief with a dead-end file. Let him go downriver and he disappears into the delta and you never think about him again."

"And you?" Corliss tilted his head. Rain ran off the brim of his ministry-issue hat in a thin curtain. "What do I do with you?"

"Whatever you were going to do anyway. Question me. Detain me. Put me on a plane to the capital with a statement drafted before I land." She shrugged, and made the shrug look like surrender, which took more effort than anything she'd done in three weeks. "I'm one disgraced ranger whose father was already a disgrace. Who'll believe her? You told me that yourself, at the depot. You said it like it was a kindness."

Something moved behind his face—satisfaction, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. He gestured, and the officers pushed Ezra forward a step.

"Don't," Ezra said. It was the first thing he'd said aloud, and his voice came out rougher than she'd ever heard it. "Rosalind. Don't you dare. The evidence is already moving without us—you know it is. Marta's people, the survey records, the chief's logs—they don't need you standing in the rain negotiating with this man. Walk away. Take the boat. That's an order from the guy in handcuffs."

"It's a bad order," she said. "I've never taken your orders anyway."

"First sensible thing you've done," Corliss said, and nodded to the officers.

They cut Ezra loose—or rather, one of them did, shoving him toward the towpath with a contemptuous flick of the wrist, the way you'd release something you considered beneath killing. Ezra stumbled, caught himself, turned. For a moment the rain stood between them and she saw him whole: soaked, bleeding at the mouth, furious, alive. He looked at her the way people look at a shoreline after a long crossing.

"The spillway," he said. "If it goes wrong—the chief said the eastern face—"

"I know where to be."

"That wasn't what I—" He stopped. Swallowed whatever the rest of it was. "Rosalind Marchetti. You are the most stubborn—"

"Go," she said gently. "Go be free. It's annoying how good you'll be at it."

He went. She watched him until the gray of the rain took him, and then she let them put the ties on her wrists, and the plastic bit in just as advertised, and she thought: *worth it.*

* * *

Corliss interrogated her in the cabin of the patrol boat while the storm rebuilt itself outside the windows, clouds stacking black over the western ridge like furniture being moved into a room no one would live in again.

He was good at it, she'd give him that. No shouting, no theatrics. He poured her tea he didn't let her drink and asked questions in a soft bureaucratic cadence, building a paper version of her that would fit inside a file folder: *rogue officer, emotional instability, family history of insubordination.* He wanted her to confirm the compass was hidden in the spillway. He wanted names. He wanted her to say the word *alone*—that she'd acted alone, planned alone, that no villagers had helped her, that Marta's testimony was the rambling of an old woman, that the survey chief's records were forgeries.

She gave him nothing but weather reports. The rain gauge in her head had been running for weeks; she recited figures at him until his jaw tightened.

"You're stalling," he finally said.

"I'm waiting," she said. "There's a difference. Stalling is what you're doing."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the world to catch up to you." She looked at him across the little table, at the man who had offered her reinstatement like a coin tossed to a beggar, who had drowned three villages on paper before the water ever touched them. "You think this is still a story about a stolen compass. It stopped being that a long time ago. You're the only one who doesn't know it yet."

His radio crackled on the bulkhead. He ignored it. It crackled again, insistent, the capital frequency, the one that outranked everyone in the room.

"Answer it," Rosalind said. "I'll wait. I'm very good at waiting. Eleven years in the service—I once waited four days in a blind for a poacher who never came. This is nothing."

Corliss picked up the handset the way a man picks up a snake he suspects is dead but isn't sure.

She heard only his side. It was enough. The yes-sir started strong and lost a syllable every time. His spine changed shape. By the end of the call he was sitting very straight and very still, the way buildings sit after the earthquake has passed through them and they haven't yet decided whether to fall.

He set the handset down. Looked at her. And for the first time since the depot, since the polite offer and the generous interpretation, Dominic—no. Since the beginning, Corliss looked *afraid*.

"What," he said quietly, "did you do?"

"Nothing," Rosalind said. "That's the point. I did nothing. I got arrested, remember? Everything else happened because other people chose to act. Marta's neighbors, the ones you took for questioning—they talked. All night, apparently. To anyone who'd listen, and then to a radio operator in Kel Anoor who owed Marta her life from the cholera summer, and who had been sitting on a transmitter for three weeks waiting for exactly this. The survey chief's field logs—the real ones, the ones you thought burned with the camp—went out in code on the hydrological band, which your people don't monitor because you assumed no one downstream could read it. Her father taught half the valley that code. It's in the old irrigation manuals." She smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "You silenced a survey team and left a valley full of people who could read water. That was your mistake. Not me. I'm just the one who carried the compass."

"The capital—"

"Has the testimony. Has the logs. Has the schedule of test releases cross-referenced against the rainfall projections showing the eastern face will fail, not just flood—*fail*—and take the ministry's own engineers down with the villages. Your superiors read that at six this morning. That call was them deciding which of them you are." She leaned back as far as the ties allowed. "So. Am I under arrest, Agent Corliss? Or am I a witness?"

* * *

They untied her on the deck an hour later, when the rain came down hard enough to make the question academic—no one could go anywhere, and everyone knew it. The patrol boat rode at anchor below the weir while the river climbed the concrete in slow brown inches, and the officers kept their distance from her with the particular awkwardness of men who had handcuffed someone who was about to be right about everything.

Ezra found her there at dusk, when the storm paused to breathe. He came up the ladder dripping, having talked, bribed, or charmed his way past the patrol skiff—probably all three—and stood in front of her with his bound-wrist marks still red and his expression caught somewhere between relief and outrage.

"You traded yourself," he said. "For me."

"It was a good trade. You're worth at least one disgraced ranger."

"That's not—" He dragged a hand through his wet hair. "That's not how debts work. You don't get to just—"

"Ezra." She said it softly, and he stopped. "Your brother is alive because we went into that mine shaft. Marta is safe because her neighbors wouldn't stay quiet. The surveyors are eating hot food tonight because the chief trusted a radio operator he'd never met. Do you see it yet? Nobody in this story saved anybody alone. My father tried to do it alone, thirty years ago, and they buried him in paperwork for it. I almost did the same thing—carried it all myself, the compass, the map, the truth, like it was mine to hold." She shook her head. "It was never mine. It belonged to everyone who lives downstream. All I did was hand it back."

He was quiet for a long moment. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere upstream, faint through the rain, came the deep industrial groan of the spillway gates being tested—Corliss's people, or their replacements, opening the old channels her father had mapped, letting the water go where water had always meant to go.

"When this is over," Ezra said, "when the hearings are done and they've thrown the book at whoever survives the book-throwing—what happens to you? They'll take your badge. They might have already."

"They might." She watched the water rise over the apron below, covering the place where she'd surrendered herself, erasing it the way the valley erased everything and kept going. "Eleven years ago I joined the service because my father lost everything and I wanted to belong to the thing that took it from him. Prove it was fair. Prove the institution was bigger than one bad decision." She laughed, short and unamused. "It wasn't. But you know what I found instead? Kel Anoor feeding strangers in a storm. Marta's neighbors refusing to sell us out even with flares over the cane fields. A radio operator who sat on a transmitter for three weeks on faith. That's a service too. Nobody issues you a badge for it. You just show up."

Ezra looked at her sideways, that thief's assessment, measuring what she'd said against what he knew of her, and finding, apparently, no discrepancy worth stealing.

"My brother asked about you," he said. "Half-conscious, IV in his arm, first coherent thing out of him: 'Is the ranger okay?' Not *am I okay*. You." He shook his head slowly. "You're going to be insufferable about this."

"Already am."

Below them, the flood came through the reopened channels—not the killing wall the ministry had planned, but a managed rush, dirty and loud and, for the first time in weeks, honest. The valley took the water the way valleys do, reluctantly and completely, and the rooftops of the lowest hamlets went under as they'd always been going to, but the people were on the ridge, and the ridge was full of cooking fires, and the fires stayed lit through the dark.

Rosalind stood at the rail until full night, watching the lights of the displaced villages scatter across the high ground like a constellation someone had finally gotten right.

Then the patrol boat's radio crackled again, and an officer called up the deck stairs with an expression she'd learn to recognize over the coming weeks: the look of a man delivering news too large for his rank.

"Ma'am? Ma'am—there's a priority transmission from the capital. A summons." He swallowed. "They want you to testify. In person. Before the commission." A pause, and then, because he was young and hadn't yet learned to keep his face flat: "They also say—ma'am, they say the dam project is suspended. Effective immediately. And Agent Corliss is relieved of command, pending investigation."

Above them, thunder rolled along the ridge like applause from a very large, very indifferent audience.

Ezra exhaled beside her, long and shaking, and then started to laugh—that thief's laugh, helpless and delighted, echoing off the concrete.

"Well," he said. "Better pack dry clothes, ranger. The capital's a long way upriver."

"And for once," Rosalind said, turning toward the cabin, toward the light, toward whatever came next, "we're going the same direction."
