## 20. Testimony in the Dry Season

The capital smelled of dust and diesel and cut stone, the way it always did at the end of the dry season, when the fountains ran thin and the bougainvillea along the ministry walls bloomed defiantly out of cracked mortar. Rosalind had not been back in eleven years. She had told herself she'd never come back.

She stood outside the Hall of Inquiry at half past seven in the morning, three weeks after the flood, wearing her dress uniform because Corliss's lawyers would expect her to look like a disgraced woman trying to claw back respectability. Let them read it however they wanted. The uniform was hers. The body inside it was hers too, thinner now, the burn scar on her forearm from the spillway grate still scabbed pink, but hers.

Ezra waited by the steps with his hands in his pockets, dressed in the only clean shirt he owned, which was to say a shirt that had been clean sometime before Kel Anoor drowned. His brother sat behind him on the low wall, wrapped in a blanket against a morning chill that Rosalind knew was mostly in his head. Tam Voss had gained six pounds in three weeks of hospital food. He still moved like a man made of kindling, but he could walk, and he could talk, and he had spent every one of those three weeks talking—into recording devices, before inquiry clerks, once for eleven hours straight until a stenographer wept and asked for a break.

"You don't have to do this part," Ezra said as she came up the steps. "The chief's records are already entered. Marta's testimony arrived yesterday. The villagers' petitions. You could sit in the gallery like a spectator."

"I'm not a spectator." She looked up at the hall's facade, at the carved scales above the doors that some mason forty years ago had given faces like closed eyes. "I'm the one who found them."

"You're the one who lost everything finding them," Ezra said quietly.

"That's what makes me credible."

Tam laughed—a dry rustle of a sound—and pulled the blanket tighter. "She sounds like you when you're about to do something stupid, brother."

"He does it more often," Rosalind said, and went up the steps alone.

* * *

The hearing chamber was smaller than she'd imagined, all pale wood and brass fittings, with a horseshoe bench where nine commissioners sat under electric fans that turned the hot air without cooling it. Reporters filled two rows at the back. A stenographer's fingers hovered. Somewhere a clerk coughed into his sleeve, and the sound carried like a shot.

Commissioner Adeyemi presided—a broad-faced woman with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair, who had spent the first week of hearings letting ministry lawyers talk themselves into corners. She looked at Rosalind now over the rims of those glasses with an expression Rosalind couldn't parse: not hostility, not warmth. Assessment. The look of someone measuring water depth before stepping in.

"State your name and former posting for the record."

"Rosalind Marchetti. Senior ranger, Southern Basin District. Formerly." The word *formerly* sat strangely in her mouth. Her resignation letter had been accepted four days ago, processed with bureaucratic speed that felt, in its way, like mercy. "I resigned my commission on the ninth."

"On your own initiative."

"On my own initiative, yes."

Counsel for the Ministry—a soft-spoken man named Ferran who reminded her of a well-fed cat—rose to object to something procedural, was overruled, and sat down smoothing his lapels. Then they asked her to begin, and she began.

She told it plainly, because plainness was all she had left and it turned out to be enough. The salt flats. The thief. The compass. She watched the commissioners' faces as she described the survey camp—the instruments still running, the food rotting in its tins, the ground around the tents swept clean by no natural wind she had ever read in twenty years of reading ground. She described the radio transmission, the voice that ordered the survey team home in words their chief never used. She described the drowned village, the test releases, old Marta standing hip-deep in what had been her kitchen garden, saying *the key your father buried.*

At that name—her father's name—something shifted in the room. She felt it the way you feel weather change: pressure, attention, the fans seeming to slow.

Ferran cross-examined her for two hours. He was good. He never raised his voice, never sneered; he simply asked, again and again, whether she could confirm that Ranger Marchetti had pursued a known thief across protected land without authorization, whether she could confirm that she had subsequently traveled downriver in a stolen government boat, whether she could confirm that she had been offered reinstatement by a senior ministry official and had refused it.

"I can confirm all of that," she said.

"So you admit your conduct throughout was—" he consulted his notes delicately— "irregular."

"My conduct was insubordinate, unauthorized, and in violation of about nine regulations I could recite for you if it would help." She let the silence stretch. "It was also the only conduct available to a ranger who found six people locked in a mine shaft and three villages scheduled to disappear from the maps."

Commissioner Adeyemi leaned forward. "Ranger Marchetti. In your professional judgment—as a surveyor's daughter, if you'll permit the phrase—was the dam project viable?"

Rosalind had rehearsed this answer in a hospital corridor with the survey chief, going over his figures until dawn. She gave it now without notes. "No. The foundation geology won't hold the designed load. Chief Osei can show you his core samples; the ministry's own consultants flagged it twice and were overruled both times. But viability wasn't the question being asked. The project was approved to move money, and the valley was in the way. The flooding wasn't a risk they failed to anticipate. It was the plan."

The stenographer's keys clattered. Ferran asked for a recess. It was granted, and as the chamber emptied, Adeyemi caught Rosalind's eye and held it for one long second—something almost like apology in it—before turning away.

* * *

They broke for the midday heat, and Rosalind walked out into the white glare of the plaza feeling hollowed and strangely light, the way she'd felt once after bailing a boat through a whole night of storm—too tired to be afraid anymore. Ezra was waiting under a jacaranda with two paper cones of roasted chickpeas, because he had learned in three weeks of city waiting that she forgot to eat.

"Well?" he said.

"They asked me if I regretted it."

"What did you say?"

"I said I regretted the boat. It leaked." She took the chickpeas. "Everything else I'd do again, except faster."

He grinned, then sobered, nodding toward the hall. "Tam testifies tomorrow. The doctors cleared him this morning. He's terrified." A pause. "So am I, honestly. He's not built for rooms like that. He builds things. Bridges, pumps, the aqueduct at Kel Anoor that's older than the ministry and still runs true. They're going to take him apart in there."

"No," Rosalind said. "Adeyemi won't let them. I've watched her for a week. She's not running an inquiry. She's building a case she can survive the politics of. Which means she needs us whole, not broken."

"And after?"

"After, I don't know. Compensation, probably. A line item. Maybe a plaque." She heard the bitterness come out of her own mouth and didn't try to take it back. "The dam project is dead—that much is real. Adeyemi's interim order suspended it indefinitely pending geological review, and there isn't a review in the world that saves it. The funding's frozen. Two deputy ministers have resigned. Corliss is—" she hesitated.

"Corliss is what?"

"Gone. Resigned the same week I did. No charges filed yet, but the inquiry has his correspondence. He offered me my badge back, Ezra. In writing. That letter is evidence now, and somewhere in a filing room it's doing more damage than anything I said today."

Ezra looked out across the plaza, at the fountain running thin, at the pigeons arguing over a dropped roll. "My brother keeps asking me what happens to him now. Not the compensation. Him. He was gone eight months. The service listed him as transferred. There's no category for what happened to him." He turned to her. "You know something about that. Having no category."

She did. She had spent her whole life in one—daughter of a disgraced surveyor, the girl whose father falsified reports, who drank, who died small in a rented room while she grew up proving herself to an institution that had destroyed him. Every promotion had been an argument with a ghost. Every clean inspection, every commendation, a brick laid over his grave.

Except it hadn't been like that. She knew that now the way she knew the grain of the spillway stones—her father had surveyed this valley thirty years ago and reported the truth, and the truth had cost him everything, and he had hidden the map in a compass and left it to a daughter he couldn't warn without endangering. *The key your father buried.* All those years she'd thought he'd left her nothing but shame. He'd left her a lifeline, timed for a flood he'd predicted decades out.

"Rosa." Ezra was watching her face. "Where'd you go just now?"

"To the spillway," she said. "I go there a lot lately. It's dry in there. Good place to think."

* * *

The hearing resumed at three, and the afternoon belonged to the villages. Marta testified by recorded deposition because travel was beyond her, and her voice filled the chamber anyway—flat, unhurried, merciless. She described the water coming up the lane at night, described the ministry men who had come in spring to buy signatures on documents the signers couldn't read, described how long it takes for a house to fall when the foundations have been standing in released floodwater for a month. When the recording ended, no one spoke for a moment. One of the junior commissioners removed his glasses and pressed his knuckles to his eyes.

Then Adeyemi called the final matter of the day, and Rosalind understood, from the way the clerk handed over a single sheet, that this was the thing the whole week had been bent toward.

"The commission notes," Adeyemi said, "that Ranger Marchetti's resignation was tendered under circumstances the commission finds—its word—'coerced by institutional failure.' The commission further notes that her actions, though unauthorized, resulted in the recovery of six citizens, the preservation of material evidence, and the prevention of a catastrophic engineering failure. The commission is empowered to recommend reinstatement with full restoration of rank and back pay."

Rosalind's heart did something complicated. Eleven years of wanting, folded into one sentence spoken aloud in a marble room.

"It will not be doing so," Adeyemi continued. "Not because the commission doubts her fitness. Because she has declined."

Every head turned. Rosalind stood.

She had written the statement at midnight in her rented room, crossed out half of it, rewritten the rest, and slept badly, and now it came out steadier than she expected.

"Commissioners. I joined the ranger service at nineteen because it was the first place that ever issued me a purpose. I believed the badge meant the land was safe in my keeping. This spring I learned that the badge can also mean the land is safe *from* my keeping—from my questions, my surveys, my reports. I learned that the institution I served had erased a village, imprisoned a survey team, and destroyed my father, and that it would have erased me just as cleanly if I'd been less stubborn or more obedient." She paused. "I am resigning—not because the service failed me, but because I finally understand what my father was trying to tell me, and I intend to finish his work outside any wall that can order me to stop. Belonging isn't issued. It's built. I'm going to build it where the water actually is."

She sat down. The chamber was very quiet. Commissioner Adeyemi looked at her for a long moment, then said, "So entered," in a voice that sounded, to Rosalind, almost relieved—like a woman glad to be spared the pretense.

* * *

That evening the news reached them in the courtyard of the boarding house where Ezra and Tam had taken rooms: the full report would be published, unredacted; the dam project was formally cancelled; a reparations commission would sit in Kel Anoor itself, in the schoolhouse, so the villagers wouldn't have to travel to testify. Compensation figures were attached. They were not generous. They were, for the first time in the history of such things, actual.

Tam read the notice twice, then set it down on the stone table and put his hand flat on it, as if testing whether it was solid.

"Three villages," he said. "Mine was the middle one. We lose the aqueduct. Everything my grandfather built." His voice cracked and he let it. "But the water stays honest. The river gets to be a river." He looked up at Rosalind, and his eyes were wet and fierce. "Your father's map saved my life. Tell me what he'd want built there. I'll build it."

Rosalind thought of the spillway—dry stone, old tools, the chalk marks in her father's hand on the wall where he'd calculated flow rates thirty years ago, planning a refuge for people he'd never meet. *He'd want the water mapped,* she thought. *All of it. Every forgotten channel. So nobody gets to drown a valley in the dark again.*

"I'll tell you tomorrow," she said. "Over breakfast. Bring paper."

Ezra walked her out through the gate into the blue evening. The dry season was ending; the air had weight in it, the far-off smell of rain on dust, and somewhere beyond the rooftops thunder muttered like a promise.

"So," he said. "No badge. No posting. No salary." He was smiling. "What does a woman do with all that freedom?"

"She buys a leaky boat," Rosalind said, "and hires a man who knows rivers."

He laughed, and the sound went up into the warm dark, and far to the south, over the hills where the water waited, the first stars were coming out over a valley that would be green again by spring.
