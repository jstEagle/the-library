## 22. A Landfall for the Horizon

**Rosalind**

The river had a new name now. Officially, on the ministry's revised charts—the ones that had cost three careers and one dam project their existence—it was the Marchetti-Voss Survey Reach, which Ezra refused to say out loud because it made him laugh every time. The people of Kel Anoor still called it what they'd always called it: *the old water*, as if it were a relative who'd gone away and come back.

Rosalind stood at the rail of the *Landfall*—a flat-bottomed survey boat, twenty-two feet of salvaged oak and stubbornness—and watched the morning burn off the reeds. Two years since the spillway. Two years since she'd walked out of dry stone into a drowned valley with six survivors and a satchel of evidence that had unmade a minister. Two years since she'd handed her commission across a desk in the capital and felt, instead of grief, something terrifyingly like relief.

"You're doing the thing again," Ezra said from the stern, where he was coiling rope with the economy of a man who'd learned knots before he'd learned letters.

"What thing."

"The staring-at-the-horizon-like-it-owes-you-money thing."

"It doesn't owe me money. It owes me a weather report." She nodded east, where the sky had begun to stack itself—high cirrus first, thin as scraped bone, then the heavier cumulus behind it, building in silence. "That's early. Monsoon's not due for eleven days."

Ezra squinted, and she watched him do the thing he did—reading the sky not as a ranger read it, by training, but the way he read everything, by having survived it. His brother Tomas had taught him clouds before the survey team took him, before the mine shaft, before the slow climb back to health that had left Tomas walking with a cane and laughing more than he had any right to.

"Early," Ezra agreed. "Or wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong place." He tied off the coil and came to stand beside her at the rail. Downriver, past the bend where the valley opened into the lowlands, smoke rose from a dozen cooking fires—Kel Anoor, rebuilt higher this time, on the terrace Marta had sworn by her whole life was the true flood line. The engineers had laughed at her. Then the ministry's own hydrologists had measured the spillway's capacity, run the numbers Rosalind's father had scratched into a compass case thirty years ago, and stopped laughing.

"New chart says the flood pulse comes down the eastern channel," Ezra said. "Old chart—the one your father drew—says the river remembers its western bed when the water gets high enough."

"And if the river remembers?"

"Then everything we mapped last spring is in the wrong place, and everyone downstream is building on our mistake." He said it lightly, but his hand had gone still on the rail.

Rosalind looked at the compass where it sat in its bracket by the wheel—not her father's original, which rested open on a stone marker above Kel Anoor where Marta tended it like a shrine, but its twin, built by the same instrument-maker from the same drawings, with the same hidden plate beneath the face. On the hidden plate, in her father's cramped surveyor's hand, ran the western channel, faint as breath on glass.

"We should have caught it," she said.

"We caught everything else."

"We should have caught it." She heard her father's voice in her own words—*the river doesn't lie, Rosie, but maps do, because men draw them*—and for once the memory didn't ache. It steadied.

"Turn us around," she said. "We'll warn them tonight, sound the western channel tomorrow at first light."

Ezra was already moving. That was the thing about him she'd stopped being surprised by and started depending on: he never asked whether they could. He just asked what came next.

* * *

**Ezra**

The trouble with running your own outfit, Ezra had decided somewhere around the fourteenth leaky boat, was that you couldn't blame anyone else when the gear was bad. When he'd been a thief, everything had been someone else's fault by design—that was practically the job description. Now the *Landfall*'s engine was his engine, the depth lines were his lines, and the fact that they had exactly one functioning echo sounder and it made a noise like a dying goose was, regrettably, his problem.

He told Tomas all of this over the radio that evening, crouched in the wheelhouse while rain began to tick against the roof, soft and experimental, like the sky testing its instruments too.

"So buy a second sounder," Tomas said. Static chewed the edges of his voice. He was transmitting from the capital, where he'd spent the winter testifying—patiently, endlessly, with that maddening calm he'd inherited from their mother—before the commission on water governance. "You're famous now. Charge people."

"People don't pay to be warned about floods, Tom. They pay to be told there won't be one."

"Then charge the government."

"They don't pay either. They just arrest you less often than they used to." Ezra grinned at the radio. It was an old joke between them, worn smooth, and underneath it ran the thing neither of them said: that Tomas had come back from the mine shaft forty pounds lighter and had decided, with the same stubbornness that had kept him alive, that he would spend what came next making sure no one else went into the dark. He was training surveyors now. Three of them, all village-born, all the kind of kids the ministry had never bothered to count.

"How's the leg?" Ezra asked.

"Broke the cane again."

"That's the third one."

"I'm hard on equipment. Runs in the family." A pause, static filling it like water filling a hull. "Ez. The western channel. You're sure?"

"Rosie's sure. Her father drew it before either of us was born, and the man knew rivers the way I know locks."

"Then warn them tonight," Tomas said, "and don't wait for permission from anyone. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? That's what it cost."

Ezra looked out the wheelhouse window. Across the water, Kel Anoor's lamps were coming on one by one, and figures were moving on the terraces—people hauling nets in, shuttering windows, living their ordinary evening. Marta's house stood highest, its lamp already lit, and he thought about the old woman's hands on the compass two years ago, pressing it back into Rosalind's palms like a debt being returned.

"Yeah," he said. "That's the lesson."

He signed off and went to find Rosalind. She was on the foredeck in the rain, which had thickened from experiment to intention, checking the anchor line with her collar up and her hood back because she'd never once in two years worn the hood up and he'd stopped arguing about it.

"They'll want to argue," she said, without turning around. She always knew when he was behind her; he'd given up finding it eerie. "Marta will listen. Half the council will tell me the ministry's new chart has a stamp on it."

"The ministry's new chart has a stamp on it."

"I know." She turned, and rain ran off her jaw, and she was smiling in the way she smiled when she was about to enjoy being right in the most expensive possible way. "So we bring the stamp a rival. Get the lantern. We're going up to the terrace."

* * *

**Rosalind**

The council met in Marta's kitchen, because the council met wherever Marta was sitting, which Rosalind had understood within a week of knowing her was the actual constitution of Kel Anoor. Eleven people crowded around a table scarred by sixty years of knives and arguments, and Rosalind laid the compass twin in the center of it, face up, hidden plate exposed.

She told them plainly: the eastern channel was the ministry's guess. The western channel was her father's measurement, taken at flood stage thirty years ago, and the river would take the west when the water rose high enough—which, if those clouds kept stacking, would be inside ten days. Everything downstream of the western channel—the new granary, the rebuilt ferry landing, the lower terraces where the Sadi family had replanted—was drawn on hope.

An argument started, as she'd promised Ezra it would. A councilman named Brek held up the ministry chart with its official stamp and spoke about authority, and investment, and the shame of telling farmers to move their seed twice in three years on the word of a disgraced ranger and a former thief.

Rosalind let him finish. That was another thing she'd learned, mostly from losing arguments badly in her first year with the service: you let people finish, because the moment you interrupt, they stop hearing you and start defending themselves.

Then she said, "Brek, I carried that same stamp for nine years. I know exactly what it weighs." She put her hand flat on the table. "It weighs nothing. What weighs something is this—" she tapped the hidden plate, her father's faded ink, "—because a man died ruined rather than redraw it. And this—" she tapped the table itself, the wood of Marta's kitchen, the house Marta had insisted on building high, "—because an old woman bet her life on a flood line and won. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to trust the two people in this valley who've already been right about the water, and got punished for it both times."

Silence. Rain on the roof, harder now.

Marta reached out one brown, knuckled hand and turned the compass so it faced the room.

"My husband helped carry that man's instruments," the old woman said. "Thirty years ago, when they drove him out. My husband said, *they didn't drive him out because he was wrong.*" She looked around the table. "I'm moving my goats tomorrow. Anyone who wants to help can. Anyone who wants to argue can argue with the river, and see who wins."

It went the way those things go—not a vote so much as a tide. Brek held out longest, then asked, gruffly, whether the survey outfit would stake the ferry landing's new mooring posts itself, and Rosalind said yes before Ezra could name a price, and Ezra kicked her ankle under the table, and she kicked back.

Walking down through the village afterward, through the smell of wet woodsmoke and the sound of families already shifting stores to higher ground without being told twice, Ezra said quietly, "You realize that's the first time you've ever called yourself disgraced without flinching."

Rosalind considered it. The badge was long gone—surrendered, framed ironically by Tomas, and now hanging in the *Landfall*'s head, which visitors found alarming until they heard the story. But the word had been a splinter for two years, working deeper.

"She took the sting out of it," Rosalind said. "Your grandmother-in-spirit, Marta. Being driven out for being right isn't disgrace. It's just expensive."

"Everything true is expensive. That's why most people buy the stamp instead."

Ahead of them the river slid black and swollen under the rain, already carrying more water than the charts expected, already muttering about the west.

* * *

**Ezra**

They sounded the western channel at dawn, in rain steady as a metronome, and the river confirmed everything. Ezra worked the lead line while Rosalind held the *Landfall* against the current, calling depths, and the numbers came back shallow where the ministry chart promised deep, and deep where it promised shoal—the old bed, alive under sixty years of silt, waiting.

By noon the sky had gone the color of wet slate and the wind had swung around, and Ezra stood in the wheelhouse with the radio crackling warnings from three stations at once and felt something he hadn't felt standing in a storm since he was a boy: readiness.

Not calm. He wasn't sure he'd ever be calm in weather again; the mine shaft had cured him of calm permanently. But ready. They had a boat that leaked only in the places they knew about. They had fuel, and line, and a signal lamp, and a list of every household on the western channel with a name beside it, because Rosalind had spent the whole previous autumn going door to door putting names on lists, insisting that a warning without a name attached was just weather.

"Corliss would be proud," Ezra said, half to himself, working the throttle upriver toward the first homestead.

"Don't say that name in my wheelhouse."

"He did teach you the flood protocols."

"He taught me that institutions protect themselves first, downstream second. I kept the useful half." She glanced over, rain-streaked, tired, lit up anyway. "Besides. If Corliss saw this boat, he'd have us cited for improper registration."

"Improper registration," Ezra repeated solemnly. "The crime of the century. Right after stealing a compass."

"You never gave that compass back, technically. You stole the twin."

"I stole the *idea* of the compass. There's a difference. Ask any court."

They were still arguing about it—happily, endlessly, the way married-to-the-work argue—when the first homestead came into view through the rain: the Odum place, low fields, a boy already running down to the bank waving both arms, because Marta's messengers had gotten there ahead of them and the family was loading a cart.

And behind the boy, behind the homestead, behind the whole gray curtain of the storm, the horizon had changed. The clouds had finished stacking and begun to lean, and somewhere upcountry the monsoon had made landfall and was coming down the valley like a debt called in.

Ezra cut the engine to half and looked at Rosalind, and she looked back, and neither of them said the obvious thing, because they'd earned the right not to say it: that two years ago this sky had meant drowning, and capture, and a valley full of people who didn't know. That this time the lists were written. That the boy on the bank was waving *hello*, not *help*.

"Odums first," Rosalind said. "Then the ferry landing. We've got maybe six hours of light."

"Six hours, one leaking boat, and a river that lies." Ezra grinned and pushed the throttle home. "Wouldn't trade it."

Behind them, the storm leaned closer. Ahead of them, on every terrace and landing down the western channel, lamps were being lit early—and this time, people were watching the water, and the water had people watching it back.
