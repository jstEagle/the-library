## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The rain came off the harbor in long gray sheets, and by the time Ansel Marchetti reached Corvane Street the water had found every seam in his coat and was working patiently at the rest of him. Saltwater town, the locals called it, though there was nothing salt about it — the sea lay forty miles east and only its weather arrived here, exhausted and weeping. The gutters ran brown with coal dust and roof tar. Somewhere behind the fishmongers' awnings a bell counted six, muffled, as if the hour itself had been drowned.

He kept his head down and his collar up and thought about arithmetic. That was the trick of it now, the whole occupation of his days: arithmetic. Fourteen pounds owed to Hask & Vane, the note called due on the first. Nine to the widow Prentiss for two months' lodging he could no longer pretend he'd pay. Three and change to the ferryman at Quaymouth who had, in a moment of charity Ansel still resented, let him ride anyway. And beneath all of it, like ballast, the great shapeless sum of what a man owes when his bridge has failed and eleven people have been carried downriver in the dark.

They said it wasn't his fault. The inquiry had said so in its careful, bloodless way — unforeseen scour, inadequate soundings, an act of God — but acts of God do not send letters, and the letters came weekly now, on paper so fine it felt like an accusation. *We are given to understand you retain certain personal effects of value.*

His father's watch. Yes. That.

The pawnshop on Corvane Street was wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold nothing but umbrellas, which in this town must have made someone rich. Its window displayed a dead man's estate: wedding rings, a stuffed owl, a violin with no strings, a sextant missing its mirror. Ansel stood before the glass a long moment, rain running off his brim, and watched his own reflection hover over the owl like something waiting to be appraised.

Inside smelled of oil cloth and old brass. The broker was a small woman with spectacles thick as portholes and hands that moved over metal the way a physician's move over a body — reading, diagnosing, pronouncing.

"Watch," Ansel said, and set it on the counter without ceremony, because ceremony would have undone him.

She turned it over once. Opened the case. The lid sprang with the same soft click it had made against his father's ear in a kitchen in Vellamark thirty years ago, and Ansel looked away, out the fogged window, at the umbrellas next door, hundreds of them, black ribs open like a rookery of drowned birds.

"Railroad issue," the broker said. "Silver case, gold train. Engraved." She tilted it toward the lamp. "*To T. Marchetti, for the Sorrow Cut, completed under impossible conditions.*" Her eyes came up over the spectacles. "You'd be the son, then."

"I'd be the son."

"The one from the papers."

"The one from the papers," Ansel agreed, and heard how flat his voice had gone, how much like a rail itself, laid straight across feeling because feeling was a gradient no engine could climb.

She named a figure. It was insultingly small and exactly enough. He signed the ticket she wrote out — *redeemable ninety days* — and told himself, as men in pawnshops have always told themselves, that he would come back for it. That this was not an ending but a loan against a future. The bell above the door gave a wet rattle as he stepped back into the rain, and he did not look at the window again, because the watch was already in the glass among the dead things, and grief is a thing a man can carry only if he refuses to look at it directly.

* * *

The boarding house on Tanner's Lane took him in because the widow Prentiss had learned that a ruined engineer pays eventually or dies quietly, and either way the room turns over. It was a tall narrow house the color of wet ash, five floors of stairs that groaned like a ship in weather, and Ansel climbed them that evening with bread and a heel of cheese in his pocket and the pawn ticket folded flat against his chest where the watch used to sit.

He heard the coughing before he saw the man.

It came from the third-floor landing, where the gaslight failed and the stairwell narrowed, a sound like wet gravel turning over in a barrel. Then a silence worse than the coughing. Then a thin, deliberate scraping, as of someone trying very hard to stand and failing with dignity.

Ansel found him crumpled against the banister: an old man in a canvas coat stiff with dried mud, boots split at the seams, a prospector's pack beside him big as a coffin and twice as heavy-looking. His beard was white and untrimmed and his lips were the color the rain had made everything else. There was blood on his knuckles, bright against the gray, and more of it, Ansel saw with a lurch, spotting the boards beneath him in small dark coins.

"Stairs," the old man said, as though reporting a finding. "Third floor. They don't tell you. Nobody tells you about stairs."

"You're bleeding."

"I'm aware." He tried the standing again. Ansel took his arm — light, terribly light, a frame with a coat hung on it — and got him upright. "Ede," the man said. "Fourth floor. Room at the back. If you're going up anyway."

Ansel went up anyway. It took ten minutes to cover one flight, the old man stopping twice to cough into a rag that came away blotched red-brown, and neither time did he apologize, which Ansel found himself respecting. Men who apologized for dying were asking you to carry something; this one simply carried it.

The back room was cold and nearly bare: a cot, a spirit stove, a washstand, and the pack, which Ede lowered onto the bed with the tenderness of a man setting down a child. He sat breathing while Ansel lit the stove, and the little blue flame threw their shadows huge against the damp plaster.

"You're Marchetti," Ede said at last.

Ansel's hand stopped on the stove key. "I don't know you."

"No. But I know the face from the papers, and I know the name from somewhere older than the papers." He coughed again, shorter, managing it. "Your father surveyed the Vellamark line. Before the closure."

"Everyone surveyed the Vellamark line. It's what killed half of them."

"And closed the rest." Ede's eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they did not wander the way dying men's eyes were supposed to. They fixed. "Sit down, engineer. I've a trade to put to you and I haven't the wind for haggling."

"I'm not buying anything."

"You're selling. Everything. That's rather my point." He worked at the pack's buckles with fingers that shook until Ansel, against every instinct of self-preservation he possessed, reached over and opened them himself. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, in wool, in more oilcloth, lay a tin box the size of a hymnal. Ede lifted the lid. Inside was a map, folded into eighths, its edges sealed shut with dripping lines of wax gone amber with age, and on top of it a brass compass whose needle swung once and settled, pointing — Ansel noted it with the idle professional habit of a lifetime — not north, but down along the floorboards, toward the foundations, as though the building itself had iron in its bones.

"A map of the closed line," Ede said. "Not the survey your father drew. Older. Different. It shows what the company sealed, and why sealing it didn't help, and where the water goes when the monsoon comes up the valley. It's the only copy. I burned the others myself, years ago, when I understood what I'd been carrying."

"Sell it to the syndicate," Ansel said. "They're reopening the high country. They'd pay—"

"They'd pay," Ede agreed, "and then they'd pay me differently, because the syndicate does not buy things, Marchetti. It buries them. I spent twenty years learning that lesson and it cost me—" he touched his chest, where the cough lived, "—well. Everything except this."

Rain hammered the window. Downstairs a door slammed and someone laughed, brief and hard, and the house settled around its groaning bones. Ansel found he had not sat down after all; he was standing with his hand still near the compass, watching the needle tremble against its glass like a small trapped animal.

"What do you want from me?"

"One promise." Ede drew a breath that whistled. "Take it up the line. Bring it back whole — the map, the compass, both, to me or mine. And tell no one what it finds. Not a creditor, not a magistrate, not a priest. No one. Whatever's under those mountains, it doesn't belong in any pocket but the ground's own."

"That's not a trade. That's a burden."

"It's the only inheritance I have to give, and I mean to give it to a man who understands burdens." The pale eyes held him. "You built the Sorrow Cut. Your father built the Vellamark grade. You know what it is to make a road through stone and know, all the while, that the stone doesn't want you. I need a man who knows the mountain isn't the enemy. The enemy is what follows the road in."

Ansel should have refused. Every prudent instinct, every letter from Hask & Vane stacked in his trunk, every sensible voice said: you cannot eat a promise, you cannot pawn a map, the first of the month is coming like a train on a single track. He thought of the watch in the window among the dead things, redeemable ninety days, and understood suddenly that he had already sold the last object tying him to the man he'd been, and that whatever he picked up now would be the whole of what he carried forward.

"I owe fourteen pounds," he said, because it seemed important that Ede know exactly what kind of man he was hiring. "Nine to my landlady. Three to a ferryman. And there are people who say I owe them eleven lives, and they may be right."

"I know," Ede said. "That's why I waited on these stairs three days for you to come home."

The stove ticked. The compass needle shivered and pointed down.

"Swear it," Ede said, "and take it tonight. I won't see morning honest, and I'd rather not spend the dishonest part wondering."

Ansel looked at the wax-sealed folds, at the compass, at the old man's bloodied rag lying on the cot like a dropped flag. He thought: *this is either the end of my ruin or the middle of it.* He put out his hand.

"I swear. Whole, and silent, and returned."

Ede gripped it with surprising strength, and smiled with bloody teeth, and said, "Then mind the fourth landing on your way down. The stairs here are worse than they look."

By dawn the room was empty of everything but the smell of coal oil and the shape of a man in the blankets, and the widow Prentiss was hammering on Ansel's door to say there was a girl below, soaked through and furious, demanding by name the man her father had died waiting for.
