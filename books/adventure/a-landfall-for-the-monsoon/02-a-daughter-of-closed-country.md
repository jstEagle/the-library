## 2. A Daughter of Closed Country

The canal had a smell for every hour, and Wren Hartwell—no, Wren Ede, though she hadn't signed that name in six years—knew them all. Dawn was diesel and cut reeds. Noon was hot tar off the lock gates. But the hour she loved, the one she'd stayed for when everything else in her life had gone to rot, was dusk: woodsmoke from the bankside stoves, the green rot of the shallows, and under it all the cold mineral breath of water that had been underground and remembered it.

She was poling the *Magpie* through the last reach before Tarnholt Lock when the boy came running along the towpath, barefoot, waving a telegram like it might bite him.

"Pilot! Pilot Ede!" He used her father's name without knowing he did. Everyone on this stretch did. She'd never corrected them, and she'd never forgiven herself for why.

She shipped the pole and let the boat drift. "I'm the pilot here."

"It's for Edvard Ede." The boy's chest heaved. "They said at the office you'd know where to find him."

Six years, and the name still landed like a hook behind her ribs. "He doesn't live here."

"They said—" The boy faltered, reading her face the way children read weather. "It says *regret to inform*. It came up from Corvess Harbor."

Corvess Harbor. Four hundred miles south, where the rail lines died and the sea began. Where her father had gone prospecting the year she turned nineteen, and where she had told him, in words she'd rehearsed for weeks and delivered like a verdict, that she wanted nothing more to do with him or his maps or his hunger for what lay under mountains.

She took the telegram because her hands decided before she did.

*REGRET TO INFORM YOU EDVARD EDE DIED CORVESS HARBOR 14TH INST PNEUMONIA COMPLICATIONS EFFECTS HELD BY BOARDING HOUSE PROPRIETOR INQUIRE MARCHETTI*

Marchetti.

She read the name three times. It meant nothing. That was the worst of it—a stranger's name standing between her and her father's last effects, holding them like a customs officer holds luggage.

"Thank you," she told the boy, and gave him a coin she couldn't spare, and stood a long while at the tiller with the dusk coming down and the canal breathing its mineral breath around her.

* * *

She left the *Magpie* moored at Tarnholt with a neighbor's boy paid to watch her, and took the packet steamer south, and hated every mile of it. Hated how the country changed as it flattened, how the hills she'd been raised among sank below the horizon one by one like men going under water. Hated that she knew those hills so well—their watersheds, their old logging grades, the places where her father had taught her to read rock the way other fathers taught daughters scripture—and had spent six years pretending she didn't.

Hated most of all that she was going. That grief, which she'd assumed had drowned years ago, had simply been holding its breath.

Corvess Harbor received her with rain. It always seemed to be raining there, in her imagination and now in fact: a hard horizontal rain off the sea that turned the town into something sketched in charcoal. She found the boarding house by its brass plate—*MRS. QUILL, LODGINGS FOR PERSONS OF SOBER HABIT*, the last word worn nearly away—and climbed stairs that smelled of boiled mutton and lamp oil, rehearsing as she went. *I'm his daughter. I've come for my father's things.*

What she actually said, when a tall man opened the door of the second-floor room, was: "Where is he?"

The man blinked. He had the look of someone who had once been solid and was now mostly edges—hollow cheeks, a jaw two days unshaved, eyes that had learned to calculate. His shirt was good but tired. On the table behind him lay a watch chain, empty, coiled like something dead.

"You're—" He stopped. Recalculated. "You're the daughter."

"I'm the daughter." She stepped past him into the room without invitation. It was small and mean and it smelled of sickness, of camphor and blood and the sweetish ghost of cough syrup. A washbasin held pink-tinged water no one had emptied. "Where are his things?"

"Mrs. Quill has them. Inventory." He said the word like a man who'd recently learned its full weight. "I'm Ansel Marchetti. I found him on the stairwell. I sat with him at the end."

"At the end." She turned on him. He was close enough now that she could see the exhaustion ground into him, the fine tremor in his hands that spoke of either fever or want. "And what exactly were you doing on a stairwell with a dying man, Mr. Marchetti? Because I've met men who find the dying. They're usually waiting for exactly this."

His face did something complicated—flinched, steadied, flinched again. "You think I robbed him."

"I think strangers don't sit with dying men out of kindness in a port town. I think his effects are 'held,' which is a word with fingers on it. And I think you're thin as a lath and dressed in your last good shirt, which means you need money, and dead prospectors' effects can be worth money." She heard her own voice go flat and cold, canal-pilot voice, the voice she used on drunk lock-keepers and flood-season panic. "So. What did you take?"

"Nothing." Then, because he was apparently incapable of leaving a lie clean: "He gave me something. Before he died. He asked me to carry it, and I said yes, and I haven't opened it, and it isn't mine to sell."

"What is it?"

"A map."

The word went through her like a spike through a hull. Her father had drawn maps the way other men wrote letters—constantly, compulsively, in pencil on anything that would hold a line. She had grown up inside his maps. She had also grown up watching what they cost him: seasons gone, money gone, a wife buried young and a daughter slowly buried under his obsession until there'd been nothing left of her to bury.

"Show me," she said.

"No." He said it quietly, and to his credit he didn't dress it up. "He made me promise. Deliver it whole, tell no one what it found. Those were his terms. I keep my promises."

"You've known him four days."

"Four days was enough to hear a man cough his lungs out and still ask about *me*. About whether I was all right. Nobody's asked me that in years." Something moved behind his eyes and was shut away. "I'm sorry he's dead. I'm sorry you weren't there. But the map goes where he sent it, and that's the end of it."

Wren looked at this stranger—this ruined, stubborn, lying-or-maybe-not-lying man who had held her father's hand at the end when his own daughter had been four hundred miles away polishing her pride—and felt something in her chest crack along an old seam.

"Then I'm coming with you," she said.

"That's not—"

"He was my *father*." Her voice broke on it, and she hated that, hated the tears standing in her eyes, hated the room and the rain and the pink water in the basin. "You got four days. I threw away six years. You don't get to stand there keeping his promises like you earned them. Whatever's on that map, whatever he found—it's mine to see too. It's mine to *carry*."

* * *

He didn't give in that night. He gave in at dawn, which she later understood was simply how Ansel Marchetti did everything: late, reluctantly, and after calculating every angle twice.

They spread the map on Mrs. Quill's kitchen table while the rain worked at the windows. It was sealed in wax, and Marchetti broke the seal with a care that surprised her—almost reverence—and unrolled a sheet of drafting linen covered in her father's hand. She knew that hand. The cramped capitals. The way he drew water as fine parallel lines, like comb marks. The way he annotated everything, even his own uncertainty, in margins gone soft with erasure and revision.

"This is the Vellamark valley," she said, before she could stop herself.

Marchetti looked up sharply. "You know it?"

"I was raised on it. Every summer until I was twelve we walked that line—the old survey track. He'd point at the mountain and tell me there was a seam under it worth more than the whole coast." She traced the contour lines with one finger, following them up from the drowned valley floor to the shoulder of the massif, and her throat tightened, because it was all there, exactly as she remembered, plus things she didn't remember. New work. Recent work. Her father had kept drawing this valley for twenty years. Had kept going back.

"There's a tunnel here." Marchetti's finger stopped at a mark high on the eastern wall, above the reservoir line, where the contours crowded together like people pressing toward a door. It wasn't a standard survey symbol. It was a small, careful inscription, in ink darker than the rest:

*M. — she waited — E.*

Wren stared at it. The rain filled the silence.

"My mother's name was Maren," she said finally. "Everyone called her M."

"She waited," Marchetti read aloud, softly, like a man handling something explosive. "*She waited.* Waited for what?"

"For him. For the mine. For—" Wren pressed her palm flat against the linen, over the little dark letters, as if she could feel warmth in them. Her mother had died when Wren was nine, in winter, of the fever, and her father had gone quiet for a year and then louder than ever, driving himself into the hills as if the answer to death were somewhere under stone. Wren had always believed the obsession started with the mother's dying. Now she wondered if it ran the other direction—if the map had come first, and her mother had simply married into it, and waited, and died waiting.

"What did he find?" she asked. "Up there. What was worth twenty years?"

"I don't know. He wouldn't say. He said some finds aren't ore." Marchetti's jaw worked. "He said the company knows it's there. That they closed the line to keep everyone out, and they're not keeping everyone out—they're keeping it *in*. He made me swear, Wren. Tell no one what it found. Not even you, maybe especially not you."

"Then we'll find out together or not at all." She rolled the map with hands that only shook a little. "But understand me, Marchetti. I'm not asking to join your expedition. There is no expedition without me. I know that valley—I can read its water, its weather, every slide-path and spring-line on that mountain. You have a map. I have the country it was drawn from. Without me you'll drown in the first week, and the monsoon starts in eleven days."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him do it—watched the calculation run, the weighing of a stranger against a drowning season. She recognized the look. It was her father's look, the prospector's arithmetic, and she despised how much comfort she took from seeing it on another face.

"The monsoon," he said at last. "Eleven days."

"Ten, if the pressure drops early. It always does."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, thin and crooked, the smile of a man remembering how. "My father was a railroad engineer," he said. "Broke, like me, but he taught me gradients. Water follows gradients. So does debt." He extended his hand across the table, across the rolled map that lay between them like a sleeping animal. "We leave tomorrow. Freight, not passenger—cheaper, and nobody checks freight tickets."

"Nobody checks tickets nobody buys," Wren said, and shook his hand.

His grip was dry and warm and desperate, and she held it one beat longer than a bargain required—not for him, but for the map, for the small dark letters drying on the linen. *M. — she waited.* Her mother had waited her whole life for a man chasing a hole in a mountain.

Wren Ede was done waiting. Whatever her father had found up there in the closed country, she would dig it out herself—with her own hands, in the teeth of the rain, if that was what it cost.

Outside, far to the north, over hills she could not see, the first pressure was already falling.
