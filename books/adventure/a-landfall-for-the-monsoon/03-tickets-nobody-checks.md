## 3. Tickets Nobody Checks

The freight yard at Kessel's Reach smelled of creosote and drowned things, and the rain came down in ropes rather than drops, as though the sky had given up on elegance and settled for volume. Ansel moved through it the way he'd learned to move through all his troubles: quickly, apologetically, and with an eye always fixed on the nearest exit.

"Third carriage from the end," he said, not looking back to see if Wren followed. "The one with the broken door. It rides worst but nobody checks it, because nobody wants to stand in the wet long enough to try."

"You've done this before," Wren said. Her voice carried no admiration in it. It was a question shaped like a statement, and he had already learned that most of her sentences were built that way — traps dressed as observations.

"I've been poor for a long time," Ansel said. "Poverty is a kind of education."

He shouldered the tarpaulin roll containing everything Ede had left behind — the map sealed in waxed linen, a change of clothes that smelled of another man's pipe smoke, and a bundle of letters tied with tarred string that Wren had refused, so far, to let him read. She carried them herself. He had noticed that. He noticed most things about her now, the way a man notices weather: constantly, and with a low-grade dread.

The broken-door carriage was exactly as advertised. Inside, sacks of undyed wool had shifted into dunes against one wall, and the floor was a lattice of rust that printed orange ghosts on anything that touched it. They wedged themselves into the lee of the wool, and after a while the train shuddered, groaned like something being woken against its will, and began to move.

Through the gap where the door should have been, Kessel's Reach slid away — the pawnshop where his father's watch now lived under a stranger's glass, the boarding house stairs where an old man had coughed his life out between one promise and the next. Ansel found he could not look at any of it directly. He watched instead the rails unspooling behind them, two bright threads stitching the town to the country, and thought about how much of his life had been spent moving along lines someone else had laid down.

Wren sat across from him with her knees drawn up and the letter bundle in her lap like a sleeping animal she didn't trust.

"How did you know which carriage," she said. "Exactly. Third from the end. That's not poverty's education. That's practice."

"It is practice," Ansel admitted. "I rode freight for two years after the Marrowvale inquiry. Before the creditors found their teeth." He turned his palms up, an old habit, showing empty hands to juries that weren't there. "You learn the timetables of things nobody admits exist."

"The Marrowvale inquiry." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "I read about that. Eleven dead when the trestle went. They said an engineer falsified the load reports."

"They said a great many things." He kept his voice level, because the alternative was to let it shake, and shaking was a luxury. "Some of them were even true. Not the ones that mattered."

She studied him for a long moment, rain-light flickering across her face through the door gap. Then she looked down at the letters, and he understood that whatever verdict she'd reached, she intended to keep it filed away for later use. She was, he was beginning to realize, very good at keeping things. Better than he was. He told people what they needed to hear the way other men breathed; she hoarded her truths like a miser counting coins in the dark.

It occurred to him, somewhere past the second river crossing, that this might be a problem.

* * *

They changed at Harrow Fen for the ferry, and this was where the trouble started, because Harrow Fen was where the photographs started.

Ansel saw him first: a narrow man in a grey coat standing at the ferry office window with a camera on a wooden tripod, photographing every passenger who crossed the gangway. Not the crowd — the individuals. One plate each, methodical as a tax collector. The man had the patient, bloodless face of a clerk, and he worked without hurry, as though the queue would wait for him forever, and in a sense it would, because no one dared object.

"That's company work," Ansel said quietly. He turned them both toward the tea stall, bought two cups of something that claimed to be tea, and drank his while watching the reflection in the wet brass urn. "Nobody photographs ferry passengers. Ferry passengers are not worth the plates."

"Vellamark Mining Consortium," Wren read off the man's collar pin, her voice barely above the rain. "That's the syndicate. That's who closed the line."

"That's who closed the line," Ansel agreed, "and who apparently wants to know everyone traveling toward it. Which means we are interesting, or we are about to be." He set down the cup. "So we are not interesting. We are boring. Boring is armor. Can you be boring?"

"I can be silent," Wren said. "Boring I make no promises about."

"Silent will do."

He constructed their story the way he'd once constructed load reports — plausibly, quickly, and with just enough flaw to be believable, since perfection invited scrutiny. They were surveyors' assistants, contracted for drainage assessment on the upcountry estates. He produced, from memory, the name of a firm in the capital that had dissolved years ago, on the reasonable assumption that no one this far from the capital would know it. He coached Wren on it during the walk to the gangway, feeding her the details like rope to a climber.

At the camera, the grey man raised one finger, and they stopped.

"Purpose of travel?" His voice was dry as ledger paper.

"Drainage survey," Ansel said. "Corben and Hale, out of the capital. We're the field pair. Estates above the reservoir complain their terraces are washing out." He smiled the small, tired smile of a man who had been rained on professionally for weeks. "Though between us, I think they're paying to have a reason to leave. Nobody drains land they love."

Something flickered in the clerk's eyes — not suspicion, exactly. Recognition, perhaps, of a fellow professional. "Corben and Hale," he repeated. "I'll note it."

"Do," Ansel said warmly. "And put down that we're terrible at our jobs. It saves disappointment later."

The camera shutter clicked. The flash-powder smell hung in the wet air a moment, sharp as a struck match, and then they were through, climbing to the ferry deck, and only when the shore had shrunk to a grey smudge did Ansel allow himself to breathe fully.

Wren was looking at him with an expression he couldn't parse, and didn't enjoy trying to.

"You lied," she said.

"I improvised."

"You lied like breathing. You had a firm name, a backstory, a joke prepared. How long have you been carrying that joke?"

Ansel looked out at the water. The reservoir country was beginning around them now, drowned valleys showing their shoulders — hilltops become islands, fence lines running into green depths. Somewhere under that water was the Vellamark line, and under the line, whatever Ede had found and sworn him to.

"A long time," he said finally. "When you're blamed for something you didn't do, you learn that the truth is just one story among many, and usually the worst-told one. After that, lying stops feeling like a sin and starts feeling like a trade."

"And which story am I in?" Wren asked. "The true one, or the well-told one?"

He had no answer that wouldn't have been another lie, so he gave her silence, and she accepted it, and something between them quietly closed, like a door being eased shut by someone polite enough not to slam it.

* * *

The customs shed at Tarnholt was the last checkpoint before the highlands, and it nearly ended everything.

They had separated deliberately — Wren walking ahead with the letters hidden in her coat, Ansel trailing with the map sewn into the lining of his own, a trick he'd learned from a smuggler in a previous, unlamented life. But the shed was crowded, the rain driving everyone indoors, and the inspectors were nervous, and nervous men search coats.

Ansel saw it happen: a young inspector, bored and ambitious, drifting toward Wren with his hand already rising toward her shoulder. And Wren — Wren stood perfectly still, too still, the stillness of a woman deciding whether to run.

Ansel crossed the shed in six strides and arrived laughing.

"There you are!" He clapped the inspector's arm with the easy familiarity of a man who owned the gesture. "Forgive me — my sister has hated customs sheds since she was a child. Lost a doll to one in Corvane. Still brings it up at every family dinner." To Wren, in a tone of fond exasperation: "Show the man the manifest, it's in your left pocket, honestly, you'd lose your head—"

There was no manifest. There were letters in her left pocket, forty years of a dead man's handwriting, and Ansel watched her eyes flick down toward that pocket and then up to his face, and in that half-second he saw her decide — not to trust him, exactly, but to trust the performance, to step inside the lie he'd built and help him hold it up.

"He means my sample book," Wren said. Her voice came out steady, even amused. "Ore samples. For the drainage survey. He calls everything a manifest because he can't spell it."

The inspector chuckled, waved them through with the weariness of a man surrounded by paperwork he'd never finish, and turned to the next traveler.

Outside, in the alley behind the shed, out of the sightlines of the grey-coated clerks who seemed to breed in these towns like damp, Wren stopped and pulled out the letters. Her hands were not quite steady.

"He almost touched them."

"Yes."

"And you talked your way past it in ten seconds. You didn't even hesitate." She stared at him, and there was something raw under the anger, something that looked almost like fear. "What else could you talk your way past, Ansel? If I were the obstacle?"

He held her gaze. Rain ran off the shed's eaves between them, a thin silver curtain.

"If you were the obstacle," he said, "I'd have paid the inspector to look elsewhere, because I don't sacrifice people. I sacrifice stories. There's a difference, and it's the only thing I've got left that's clean."

She didn't answer. Instead she knelt, right there in the mud of the alley, and untied the tarred string, and drew out the topmost letter. Ede's handwriting crawled across it, cramped and failing.

"Then we do this together," she said. "Whatever he wrote. Whatever it costs us. We burn it here, both of us watching, neither of us keeping anything back. Because I don't trust you, Ansel Marchetti. But I trust that we're both hiding from the same people, and right now that's worth more than trust."

She struck a match against the wet wall — three tries, hands cupped — and touched it to the corner of the page. Ansel crouched beside her. The fire took the paper slowly at first, reluctant, then greedy, and in the curling light he read what he could before the words went: fragments, names, a phrase repeated like a refrain — *it remembers better than we do* — and then black lace, ash, gone.

They fed the whole bundle to the rain-soaked alley, one letter at a time, until the string alone remained, and Wren dropped that in the puddle and ground it under her heel.

Neither of them spoke until the last ember died. Then Wren stood, brushed the ash from her knees, and looked at him with an expression that had shifted — not to warmth, nothing so generous, but to something more dangerous and more durable: complicity.

"Well," she said. "Now we're both liars."

"Now we're both free," Ansel said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it sounded like one of his lies, and she heard it too — he saw it in the tilt of her mouth.

They walked on toward the highlands through the failing light, and it was Wren who spotted it first: on the customs shed's outer wall, fresh-pasted despite the rain, a notice offering reward money for information regarding "unauthorized persons traveling toward the Vellamark district." Below the text, still drying, was a photograph.

Grey coat. Patient, bloodless face. A camera on a tripod, and beneath it, printed in small type, a name:

*Ossler. Records Division.*

He was not following them, the photograph said.

He was ahead of them.
