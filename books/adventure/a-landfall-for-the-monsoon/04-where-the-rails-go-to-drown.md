## 4. Where the Rails Go to Drown

The valley announced itself by smell before sight: cold iron and drowned timber, a sweetness like rotting fruit beneath it, the particular breath of water that has been standing too long over something man-made. Wren smelled it from the ridge road, three hours before they saw the reservoir, and her stomach tightened around nothing, the way it did before locks opened on the canal.

"You smell that?" she asked.

Ansel didn't answer at once. He was walking with his head down, shoulders hunched against the drizzle that had followed them for two days, and when he finally lifted his face she saw he'd been smelling it all along. "Coal tar," he said. "Old creosote on the sleepers. The line's under there." He nodded toward the mist ahead as if pointing into a grave.

They came down through terraced slopes gone wild with fern, and then the trees thinned and there it was: the Vellamark valley, filled to its collarbones with grey-green water. The reservoir stretched away north until the far shore became a rumor of darker grey, and out of the near water rose the stumps of dead pines, bleached bone-white, hundreds of them, standing in ranks like a congregation that had refused to leave. Between them, here and there, the water gave back the shapes of rooftops — just the ridgelines, just enough to tell you a village had once decided this was home.

And the rails. That was the thing that stopped Wren's breath. The rails ran straight off the shore and into the flood without apology, twin lines of rusted steel descending at a gentle engineered grade, visible for perhaps thirty meters through the clear shallows before the green took them. They did not end. They simply went down, the way a sentence goes down when a man stops speaking mid-word.

"They never pulled them up," she said.

"Too expensive." Ansel crouched at the waterline, rain dripping off his nose. He touched the nearest rail the way other men touched relics. "You close a line, you're supposed to lift the track, salvage the steel. They left it. Whole bridges too, probably. There's a viaduct about nine kilometers up-valley, if the map's honest, tall enough that its deck might still be above water."

"If," said Wren.

He glanced at her, and she watched him decide not to argue. Two days of freight cars and ferry decks had taught them both the economy of silence. She had learned something else besides: that Ansel Marchetti lied the way water found cracks — not from malice but from gravity, constantly, effortlessly, downhill toward whatever was easiest. She'd watched him talk a ferry clerk past three questions with a smile and a story about surveying gravel rights, and the lie hadn't cost him a flicker. She'd begun keeping her own counsel after that. A woman whose father had been a professional keeper of secrets knew what it looked like when a man practiced.

The squatters' camp announced itself by laundry. Lines of it strung between carriages — shirts, sheets, a child's dress gone translucent with wear — all of it dripping in the drizzle, so that the whole settlement seemed to be weeping gently. The carriages themselves stood on a shelf of ballast where the embankment widened into what had been a passing loop: six or seven of them, wooden-bodied, their paint boiled down to bare grey grain, windows replaced with oilcloth and tin sheet. Smoke rose from a stovepipe in one. In another, a woman sat sewing in the doorway with a rifle across her knees, and she looked up at them with the flat patience of someone who had shot dogs before.

"We're looking for Ivo," Ansel said. "Stationmaster."

The needle paused. The woman tipped her chin toward the last carriage in the row, the one with its wheels sunk to the axles in the ballast and moss climbing its flanks like green fire. "Third door. If he wants to see you." She went back to her sewing, which was its own kind of verdict.

* * *

Ivo turned out to be a small man made almost entirely of forearms and opinion, sitting inside his carriage at a table built from a cable drum, mending a kerosene lantern with hands that shook only when he wasn't thinking about them. The carriage interior had been a station office once; you could still read the ghost of a timetable painted on one wall, routes and departures rendered in faded cream, most of it scratched out with something sharp. What remained unscratched was a single column of times and the word VELLAMARK, and beneath that, in smaller letters, a destination Wren had to lean close to read: *Deep Adit — staff only.*

"Ivo," Ansel said. "We've come about the line."

"Line's closed." The old man didn't look up. "Closed twenty-two years. Nothing to come about."

"We know it's closed. We want to know why."

"Why?" Now he looked up, and his eyes were pale river-stone eyes, wet and hard and smooth. "Why does anyone close anything? Money. Politics. Water." He held up the lantern, squinted through its chimney, put it down. "Company flooded the valley for a hydro scheme. Moved the village. Paid what it paid. End of story."

Wren had grown up around men who told stories for a living — her father among them — and she knew the sound of a rehearsed ending. It was too neat. It arrived too fast. It left no room for the questions that mattered. Her father used to say you could always tell a true account because it stumbled; a false one glided.

"That's the company's version," she said. "What's yours?"

Something moved behind the stone eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing a key turn in a lock he'd hoped was rusted shut.

"There's no mine," Ivo said.

Neither of them had mentioned a mine.

The rain ticked on the carriage roof. Somewhere outside a child shouted, and gulls — gulls, this far inland, following the drowned country's new coastline — cried their thin complaint. Ansel had gone very still beside her, the stillness of a man watching ice form underfoot.

"No one said mine," Wren said quietly.

"You were going to." Ivo set down the lantern and folded his shaking hands on the drum-table, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — flattened, sanded smooth, the voice of a statement learned by heart. "There was never any mining on the Vellamark line. It was a passenger and timber road. The deep adit was a drainage tunnel. The closure was economic. The flooding was lawful. I was stationmaster here for nineteen years and I'll thank you not to come into my house asking after things that don't exist."

He delivered it all in one breath, level as a laid rail, and then he breathed, and his hands started trembling again, and Wren understood that the trembling was the truth trying to get out through the only door left open in him.

She thought of her father then — Ede, who had died coughing blood onto a boarding-house stair while a stranger held his hand — and of the map sewn into her coat lining, its second layer that bloomed only when wet, its tunnel marked with her mother's name. *Staff only,* said the wall. Her mother's name had been on a door marked *staff only*, in a mine that had never existed, in a valley that was now a lake.

"Who taught you that speech, Ivo?" she asked.

The old man's jaw worked. For a long moment she thought he would throw them out, and she braced for it, already cataloguing exits. But instead he stood, crossed to the timetable wall, and put his palm flat over the scratched-out routes as though warming them.

"Nineteen years," he said, softer now, to the wall rather than to them. "I knew every driver on this road. Knew their wives, their debts, their drinking days. And then one autumn the company men came with papers, and by spring there was no mine, and there never had been, and the men I'd known were gone — not dead, gone, transferred, scattered, some of them walking around with different names and different childhoods, if you can believe such a thing—" He stopped himself as though he'd stepped on a tripwire. His hand dropped from the wall. "There's no mine," he said again, but this time it sounded like a prayer rather than a speech, and prayers, Wren thought, are for things that frighten you.

Ansel spoke for the first time since entering. "The dredges," he said. "On the far shore. How long have they been working?"

Ivo's face did something complicated and closed. "Dredges?"

"Don't." Ansel's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "You've been rehearsing 'no mine' for twenty years. Don't make me rehearse 'no dredges' for you too."

* * *

They climbed the spine of the embankment as the light failed, because Ivo would say nothing more except one thing, muttered at the door as they left, so low Wren nearly lost it to the rain: *"Don't be on the water after dark. They work nights, and they don't like witnesses."*

From the crest of the embankment, the far shore resolved out of the murk.

The dredges were real. Three of them, moored in a line off the drowned northern shore, each one a black industrial island bristling with cranes and gantries, every joint and rail lit by work-lamps burning harsh white against the dusk. Even at this distance Wren could hear them — a deep rhythmic slushing, the heartbeat of pumps, and beneath it a thinner sound, metallic and wrong, like a spoon dragged across the bottom of a great pot. Cable after cable ran from the dredges down into the water, taut and humming faintly, and where they entered the lake the surface shivered in long silver scars.

They weren't dredging silt. Silt didn't need lights like a cathedral. Silt didn't need armed launches patrolling in slow circuits, their running lamps winking through the rain like patient, malevolent fireflies.

"They said hydro scheme," Wren murmured. "Twenty-two years ago. You don't dredge a hydro reservoir. You especially don't dredge it at night, under guard, a generation after the fact."

Ansel had the map out despite the wet, sheltering it under his coat, and even in the failing light she could see the second layer rising through the waxed paper — lines and annotations surfacing as the damp reached them, ink that drank rain and answered it. One of those lines ran from the near shore, down under the flood, up-valley, to a mark the mapmaker had drawn twice and pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

Her mother's name. Above a tunnel. Nine kilometers distant, under forty feet of dark water, somewhere near a viaduct whose deck might still break the surface.

"The rails go down," Ansel said quietly, watching the dredges. "Everything goes down. The track, the adit, the village, the truth. Twenty-two years at the bottom and they're still afraid of what's down there — enough to post guards over a lake." He folded the map away. When he turned to her, his face was unreadable, and she noted, distantly, how much practice that must take. "First light tomorrow. We find the viaduct."

"And if the water's risen overnight?"

He looked at the lake, at the rain stitching itself into its surface by the thousand, at the drowned pines standing their endless vigil. "Then we swim," he said, "or we learn to breathe."

Behind them, down in the carriage camp, a lamp went out in Ivo's window — deliberately, Wren was sure, the way a man closes his eyes against something he cannot bear to watch. On the far shore the dredges ground on through the dark, hauling up the past one cable-load at a time, and the water over the Vellamark line lay still and deep and full of everything that was supposed to have stayed buried.
