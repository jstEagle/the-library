## 5. The Compass Points Down

The rain had followed them into the valley like a debt collector.

It came down in grey sheets off the shoulders of the Vellamark peaks, drumming on the corrugated roof of Ivo's abandoned signal box where we'd sheltered the night, and by morning it hadn't slackened so much as changed its mind about how to fall — sideways now, needling, finding every gap in oilskin and resolve alike. Wren stood at the broken window with the map unrolled against the sill, and I watched her do what she'd done three times since dawn: hold it under the drip from the eave and wait.

"Nothing," she said, for the third time.

"Give it more."

"It's been soaking for an hour, Marchetti. If there's a second layer, it's a lie." She said it flatly, but her thumb kept moving along the edge of the wax seal Ede had pressed into the corner, worrying it the way a man worries a loose tooth. She hadn't slept. Neither of us had. The stationmaster's rehearsed denial — *there was never any mine* — sat between us all night like a third passenger, and neither of us had spoken of it, which is its own kind of speaking.

I took the map from her. She let me, which was progress of a sort.

Ede's hand was fine work, surveyor's work, every contour numbered in that cramped upright script. But I'd spent eleven years reading rails the way priests read scripture, and there was a thing about the drawing that had bothered me since the ferry: the hachures marking the valley walls were too regular. Machine-regular. Nobody draws two hundred identical strokes by hand unless they're hiding something inside the pattern.

I held the map over the tin basin I'd set beneath the worst leak and let the water pool in the paper's grain. And there — faint as breath on cold glass — the strokes began to move. Not move. *Separate.* The sizing Ede had used was some resin that swelled unevenly when wet, and the top layer of ink lifted microscopically off the second, and the second layer rose through the first like a body surfacing in dark water.

"Wren."

She leaned in. Rain on the roof. Water creeping across the floorboards toward our boots.

Beneath the valley contours, in ink that must have been laid down with a stylus finer than anything I'd ever owned, ran a second line. It left the mapped tunnel — the one marked with her mother's name, *SERA HARTWELL*, in letters worn nearly to ghosts — and it did not follow the track grade. It went *down*. Steeply, switchbacking through the bedrock itself, past a symbol I didn't know and then one I did: the old Vellamark & Coast surveyor's mark for a ventilation adit, with a depth figure beside it.

"Forty-one feet below the reservoir line," Wren read aloud. Her voice had gone very quiet. "And here—" her finger stopped on a small annotation in Ede's hand, almost illegible— "*the compass knows. Let it choose the hour.*"

We looked at each other. Then, together, at my compass, sitting on the sill in its leather case where I'd set it while we worked.

The needle was not pointing north.

It was pointing down-valley, steady and certain, toward the drowned mouth of the line — and trembling, the way a needle trembles near iron, except there was no iron out there. Only water, black and patient, standing forty feet deep over the portal where the Vellamark rails ran into the mountain and did not come out again.

* * *

We went down to the water in the last grey hour before full light, because Wren argued — correctly — that the dredges on the far shore would be blind until mid-morning, their crews hungover or indifferent, and because I argued nothing at all. I had passed some point in the night where argument became pointless. The map wanted this. The compass wanted this. And some stubborn engineer's part of me needed to know whether the track I'd spent my career disgraced by still existed anywhere whole, even underwater, even sealed, even mine.

The reservoir filled the valley like something poured. No waves to speak of — just that flat, rain-pocked blackness, and the tops of dead trees standing out of it fifty yards from shore, silver-grey, like the masts of a fleet that had gone down standing up. The old railbed ran straight out under the surface; you could see it for the first few yards, gravel pale through the water, then the ties, then nothing but green-black murk.

The portal was exactly where the second layer said. We found it by swimming the railbed with a rope around my waist and Wren counting knots, and I felt it before I saw it — the current change, a slow suck pulling toward the mountain, the water going colder in a band six feet wide. I dove. Ten feet down the murk thinned, and there it was: the arched concrete face of the tunnel mouth, weeping white mineral streaks, the words VELLAMARK & COAST — MILE 41 still legible above an opening that stood half-filled with water.

Half-filled. Which meant the other half was air.

I surfaced, spat lake, and found Wren treading water beside me, hair plastered flat, eyes enormous.

"There's air beyond the arch," I said. "The grade rises inside. Ede knew — that's why the adit figure matters, that's why—" I stopped, because she'd already gone under.

She came up gasping, rope in fist. "I'm going first."

"You are not—"

"My mother's name is on that tunnel, Marchetti. You can bill me for the argument later." And she was gone, a pale shape descending the railbed, and I cursed her and the dead prospector who'd raised her and myself most of all, and followed her down.

The passage underwater lasted perhaps fifteen feet. I will be honest about what those fifteen feet were: the longest of my life. Cold closing in like a hand. The compass — I'd lashed it to my chest — pressing its brass weight against my sternum, needle straining downward even as I swam forward, as if it were less an instrument than a leash. Then my head broke surface in darkness and cold clean air, and I heard Wren breathing beside me, fast and alive, and somewhere ahead, water dripping off stone with the patience of centuries.

We waded out onto ballast. Real ballast. My hands found the rail in the dark and I nearly laughed — sixty-pound Vellamark section, still true, still spiked, the fishplates tight. Eleven years I'd carried the ruin of this line on my back like a millstone, and here it lay intact in the belly of the mountain, dry as a sermon, preserved by the very flood meant to kill it.

"We'll need light," Wren whispered. "Proper light. There's a lamp in the kit—"

"I know. Back in twenty minutes. Don't wander."

She didn't answer, which should have warned me.

* * *

By the time I returned with the storm lantern and we'd walked a quarter mile up the rising grade, the tunnel had stopped being a tunnel and started being something else.

It began with small things. A boot, placed neatly at the track's edge, laces tied, pointing inward. Then another. Then dozens — no, hundreds — arranged along both walls in ranks so precise they might have been set with a spirit level. Boots, coats, hats, lunch pails, pipes, wedding rings on strings, a child's tin horse. Miner's belongings, every piece of it, and every piece of it *arranged*, curated, tended. Dust lay thick on everything, years of it, but the arrangements themselves had not collapsed or drifted. Someone had come back, season after season, and straightened them.

The chamber opened without warning where the grade crested — a natural cavern the engineers had hollowed wider, its ceiling lost in black above the lantern's reach, and the walls of it were shelved floor to darkness with the belongings, tier upon tier receding into the dark like the stacks of some drowned library. And on every shelf, on every object, hung tags.

Brass tags, stamped with names.

I lifted the nearest one. *ALDRIC VOSS.* Not a miner's name — I knew the old Vellamark pay ledgers, had audited them during the closure inquiry, and no Aldric Voss had ever drawn wages on this line. The next shelf: *MARGUERITE OKONKWO.* The next: *TOMÁS ILLYICH BREEN.* Names that sounded like money, like ministries, like the society pages of the coastal papers. Names belonging to people who had never swung a pick in the Vellamark dark and yet whose effects — or things made to look like their effects — sat here in the wet stone silence, enshrined.

"What is this?" Wren's voice came thin off the stone. She was standing before a shelf near the chamber's heart, and she wasn't touching anything, and her face in the lantern light was the face of someone doing arithmetic they don't want finished. "These aren't miners. These are—"

"I don't know."

"You always know. That's the one honest thing about you. You look at a thing and you know how it works." She turned on me. "So tell me how this works, Ansel."

And I couldn't. Because the truth was I could feel how it worked, the way I'd felt the current change at the portal — a hum in the fillings of my teeth, a pressure behind the eyes, the compass at my chest twitching against its stitching whenever I turned toward the deeper shelves. Whatever sat in this mountain, whatever ore the syndicate's dredges were worrying at from the far shore, it lived here. It soaked these shelves. It had soaked these *names*.

I told myself I only meant to read it.

The tag was on a lower shelf, at knee height, on a coat of good wool long since gone to mould. Brass, octagonal, the stamping crisp where the others were weathered — newer, or better kept. I held the lantern close.

*MARCHETTI.*

My father's name. My name. Stamped in brass in a shrine chamber forty feet under a drowned valley, on a coat I had never owned, in a place I had never been, among the belongings of people who had never lived here at all.

The sound I made wasn't a word. Wren looked over. I couldn't hide it — the tag was in my hand, and my hand was shaking, and the compass against my chest had begun, very softly, to sing — a fine high vibration, felt more than heard, like a wire singing in wind.

"Ansel." Wren crossed to me. She read the tag. She looked at my face. "That's your name."

"That's my father's name." My voice came out sanded flat. "He died in a yard accident outside Corvane when I was nineteen. There was a settlement. There were papers. There was a *body*, Wren, I stood at the grave." I turned the tag over. On the reverse, in tiny punched figures, was a date — three years ago — and a lot number, and a price.

A price. In crowns. My father's name, sold.

The wonder that had carried us down through the black water curdled in my chest and went cold and heavy, and I understood — not fully, not yet, but the way you understand a bridge is failing before you can say which rivet — that Ede's map had not led us to treasure. It had led us to a warehouse. A warehouse of selves, inventoried, priced, and waiting for buyers. And someone, three years ago, had bought a Marchetti.

Wren was staring at the shelves now with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors: the look of a person realizing the ground under them is not ground.

"Ansel," she said slowly. "My mother's name is on the tunnel mouth upstairs."

"Yes."

"And Ede sold memories. Before he renounced it. He told me that much before he died — I thought he was raving." Her hand rose toward the nearest shelf and stopped, hovering, afraid. "Then who," she said, "is buried under the name I've been grieving my whole life?"

Down the tunnel behind us, far off toward the drowned portal, came a sound that did not belong to dripping water.

Voices. And the knock of boots on rail.
