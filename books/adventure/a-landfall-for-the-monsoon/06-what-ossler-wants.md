## 6. What Ossler Wants

Ossler kept his photographs in a tin box under the floorboard of the customs shed, and every night he laid them out on the blotter like a man dealing patience to himself. Forty-one faces, so far, from the upcountry ferries alone. He knew them all by now — the way he knew all of them, the way he had known eleven hundred passengers in nine years of service to the Consolidated Vellamark Mining & Navigation Company. Faces were his trade. A face was a ledger entry that walked.

He held the newest pair to the lamp.

The engineer first: Marchetti, Ansel. Disgraced, indebted, photographed twice at the port pawnbroker's window with the stooped posture of a man carrying more than luggage. Ossler had pulled his file before the ferry even docked. Railroad surveyor, once commended for flood-routing on the Kessel cut, then struck off the register over an embankment failure that had killed no one but had cost the right people money. Creditors in three towns. A father's watch, recently redeemed and re-pawned. Desperation made men predictable, and predictable men were easy to file.

The woman was harder. Wren Ede. No photograph existed of her anywhere in the company's records until four days ago, which meant she had spent her adult life being careful or being nowhere. Canal pilot's license, lapsed. Daughter of one Ede, prospector, deceased — and there the paper simply stopped, as though she had stepped off the edge of the record and declined to climb back on. Ossler disliked people who did that. Records were how the world stayed legible. A person outside the record was weather, and weather could not be reasoned with, only endured or diverted.

He turned the print toward the lamp and studied her eyes. In photographs, most people looked at the camera as if it were a stranger they hoped to impress. She looked at it the way you look at something standing between you and a door.

*Two intruders,* he wrote in the margin, in the small copperplate hand the company paid him to maintain. *Route: valley approach, north shore. Probable objective: the line.* He blotted the ink carefully. Beneath his neat script, almost invisible unless you tilted the page, ran the watermark the company pressed into all its stationery — a coiled serpent swallowing its own tail, and inside the coil, so faint it might have been a flaw in the paper, the words *WHAT IS KEPT IS OWNED.*

Ossler had been with the company long enough to stop reading the watermark. That was the trick of service, he had learned. You did not need to believe in a thing to serve it well. Belief was for the buyers.

* * *

The order came down on Thursday, in the grey hour before the dredges started, delivered by the company's own courier — a boy with rain-slicked hair and no expression at all, who handed over a sealed folio and waited, as regulation required, while Ossler read it.

The folio contained two pages. The first was administrative: confirmation of the intruders' route, authorization of "corrective measures," a schedule of the monsoon's expected crest against which Ossler was invited to time his work. The second page was not administrative. It was written in the hand of Director Vasse himself, and it said only:

*You will remember what you are for.*

Ossler sat with that sentence for a long time while the boy stood dripping by the door and the lamp hissed and somewhere out on the black water a dredge coughed itself awake.

Then he opened the drawer where he kept the key to the strongroom below the shed, and went down into the mountain's throat to remember what he was for.

* * *

The seam lay behind three doors, and each door required a different key, and Ossler carried all three on a ring against his chest like a pectoral. The company called the vault the Chapel, without irony, because the miners who had first broken into it had come up covered in glittering dust and refused, afterward, to go back down. They had wept in the mess hall. They had called their mothers by names their mothers had never used. One of them had signed a confession to a murder committed forty years before he was born, in another province, and described the victim's kitchen in such detail that the old case was closed with honors.

That was when the company understood what it had found, and sealed the mine, and changed its business.

Ossler unlocked the third door and lit the lamps.

The ore did not look like much. That was the first thing everyone noticed and the last thing anyone believed. It was a dull blue-grey mineral shot through the quartz like veins through flesh, and it lay in the cutting walls in great slow waves, and when you held a lamp close the veins seemed to move — not flicker, not gleam, but *move*, the way a sleeper's eyes move beneath the lids. The engineers called it retentive lodestone. The miners, before they were reassigned or retired or quietly ended, had called it the Remembering. The buyers called it nothing at all. They sent lawyers with blank cheques and left with small lacquered boxes, and whatever was in the boxes never appeared on any manifest, because what the boxes contained was not cargo.

It was past.

A memory, pressed into metal, could be carried. Could be worn against the skin, could be slept beside, could be — this was the delicate part, the part that took the company's surgeons years to refine — *transferred*. A dying magnate could buy twenty years of a dead soldier's youth and wake knowing how to ride, how to kill, how to love a woman whose grave he had never visited. A judge facing inquiry could purchase the blameless childhood of some farm boy drowned in a canal, and thereafter remember a mother's lullaby in place of the bribes. The powerful did not want immortality; immortality was vulgar. What they wanted was *substitution*. A clean past, fitted like a coat, tailored from the memories of the poor and the dead, who had no use for them anymore and no lawyer to object.

The company sold identities the way other companies sold coal: by weight, by grade, by season. And the seam under Vellamark was the richest ever struck — a whole geological age of remembering, compressed and waiting, enough purchased pasts to refit the governing class of half a continent.

Ossler walked the cutting with his lamp raised, and the veins moved in the walls, and he felt — as he always felt — the faint pull at the edges of himself, like a tide testing a mooring.

Because here was the thing the company did not put in its prospectuses, the thing Ossler knew better than any living man:

He had been bought too.

Not his labor. His *self*.

There had been a man named Ossler once — a customs clerk in a river town, meticulous, friendless, fond of patience and tin boxes — who had died of fever at thirty-one, unmarried, unremarkable, his memories appraised by a company assessor at a fair price and entered into stock. And there had been another man, before, whose name Ossler did not know and would never know, because the surgeons had taken that man's past out and set this one in, and what walked around now wearing the clerk's careful handwriting and the clerk's love of ordered ledgers was neither of them and both of them: a coat of a person, tailored, fitted, owned.

He remembered a childhood in a house with green shutters. He had verified, once, at considerable personal risk, that no such house had ever existed. The shutters were real to him. The smell of the paint was real. The dog — a brown dog named Bristle — had never lived anywhere except in the metal, and now lived in him, and Ossler loved that dog with a grief that had no origin and no right to exist, and every night the dog came back to him in dreams, tail going, on a lawn that had never been mown under a sky that had never rained.

*What is kept is owned.* He was kept. Therefore he was owned. The arithmetic was simple and it closed every night around his throat like a collar.

Director Vasse's sentence came back to him: *you will remember what you are for.* Not who. What. The company had never pretended he was a who.

His purpose, per the folio, was standing on the north shore with a dead prospector's map, walking toward the Chapel with a disgraced engineer's hands and a canal pilot's knowledge of water. Two entries to be corrected. The word the company used was *erasure*, and Ossler appreciated its precision, because erasure was exactly what he was: proof that a man could be rubbed out and the rubbing-out could walk and file reports and feed a dog that had never lived.

* * *

He spent the evening preparing, because preparation was the one pleasure left to a man made of borrowed furniture.

He laid out the photographs again. Marchetti. Ede. He wrote their names on cards and set the cards beside the prints, and then, because the ritual demanded it, he wrote beneath each name the words *RECOVERABLE?* and considered.

Marchetti: recoverable. Debts, disgrace, a talent for lying smoothly — such men could be turned, bought, folded back into the record. There was even a use for him; the company always needed engineers who understood water and had reasons to keep silent.

Ede: not recoverable. She had already stepped off the page. People outside the record could not be filed, and what could not be filed had to be flooded. The monsoon would do most of the work anyway. The drainage adits were scheduled to be sealed at crest-water; the company had done this twice before, in other valleys, with other findings, and the valleys had stayed quiet, and the drowned had stayed drowned, and the buyers had gone on sleeping soundly inside other men's childhoods.

Ossler packed the tin box of photographs last, sliding each print into its sleeve, and paused over the woman's face a moment longer than procedure required.

She looked, in the lamplight, like someone who had also lost a name — or found out the one she had was not hers — and had decided to go looking for the difference. He recognized the look. He saw it every morning in the mirror above the basin, in the eyes of a man with green shutters in his head and no house anywhere in the world to hang them on.

For one moment — one, no longer — Ossler allowed himself the thought that if their positions were reversed, if she found him first, she might look at him and see not an obstacle but a fellow exhibit. Two things the company had made instead of born.

Then he closed the box, because sentiment was a leak, and leaks sank vessels, and he was a vessel, and the company held his hull.

He blew out the lamp. Below his feet, three locked doors down, the Remembering moved in its sleep, patient as water, waiting to be sold.

Upstairs, the rain began in earnest — the first true rain of the break, drumming on the shed roof like fingers counting. Ossler checked his watch against the crest schedule, gathered his keys against his chest, and stepped out into the dark to find the north shore path and the two people walking along it toward everything the company had buried.

By morning, he calculated, he would have them. By the next crest, the adits would be sealed, and the valley would keep its secrets, and Ossler would go on keeping his —

— all but one. Because as he locked the shed door behind him, he realized he had done something he had never done in nine years of flawless service.

He had left the woman's photograph face-up on the blotter, where anyone might see it.

And some buried, unbought remainder of himself, some scrap of the original man that the surgeons had missed, had wanted it found.
