## 7. Two Names for One Woman

The chamber did not want them to leave.

Wren felt it the way a canal pilot feels a current under her hull — not force, exactly, but inclination, the water's quiet opinion about where she ought to go. The shrine room sat at the end of a spur tunnel where the rails fanned out and died in rusted points, and its air was dry as paper, dry as a held breath, though somewhere behind the walls the mountain was filling with monsoon water like a cistern with a cracked lid.

Ansel had gone back along the track to check their retreat — his word was *assess*, always an engineer's word, as if naming a thing plainly could keep it from being terrible. She had told him she would wait. She had not promised to wait still.

Her lamp swung on its hook and threw the shrines into slow rotation: crates of miners' belongings arranged with a care that had nothing to do with mining. Boots laced and set heel to heel. A tin cup placed upright on a folded coat. Photographs in oilcloth, faces turned to the wall as if ashamed. And on each offering, a brass tag stamped with a name.

She had already understood that the names were wrong. Ansel had found one bearing *his* name — Marchetti, in a stamping-house hand, decades old — and had gone pale in the lamplight and said nothing for a long time, which for Ansel was a kind of screaming. She had watched him pocket the tag like a man stealing bread he didn't believe was real.

Now her own light caught a tag at the far end of the chamber, on a shrine built from a woman's things: a shawl gone soft with age, a hairbrush with three strands still in it, a small brass compass case polished bright by handling.

The tag read **MAREN EDE**.

Not Maren Vellamark. Not any of the four spellings Wren had grown up hearing spat or wept over. Maren Ede — her father's surname welded onto her mother's given name, the way you'd stamp a tool that belonged to you.

"No," she said aloud, and the chamber took the word and gave nothing back.

She should have called for Ansel. She knew that even as her hand came up. But grief is a pilot too, and it had the tiller, and it steered her fingers down onto the cold brass before any wiser part of her could object.

* * *

The childhood arrived whole, the way floodwater arrives through a doorway — all at once, and everywhere, and smelling of things it had no business carrying.

A kitchen with blue tiles gone crazed with heat-cracks. A woman at the stove with flour to her elbows, singing low and off-key, a song about a boat that wouldn't sink. Rain on a tin roof in a rhythm Wren's body knew before her mind could name it. *Come here, little wren, let me braid it before the damp gets in.* Hands in her hair. The smell of woodsmoke and cardamom. A scraped knee kissed better on a porch overlooking — not the canal country, not anywhere Wren had ever lived — but a valley of red earth and eucalyptus, terraced gardens, a white house with green shutters.

*Maren.* The voice again, warm, from inside the memory itself, calling her home across that impossible yard. *Maren, supper.*

And Wren stood inside it, seven years old, loved, certain, safe —

— and it was wrong. It was wrong the way a forged banknote is wrong: every detail perfect, the paper crisp, the ink exact, and yet no honest hand had ever printed it. Because Wren Hartwell had grown up above a chandlery in a town that smelled of tar and herring, and her mother had died when she was four, and what remained of her was a photograph, half a locket, and a silence her father filled with work and then with drink and then with distance. There had been no white house. No eucalyptus. No braiding hands.

But oh, she remembered the cardamom.

She tore her hand off the tag and staggered back into a crate and the memory did not leave. That was the horror of it. It stayed, settled into her like silt, took up residence alongside the true memories and began arranging itself among them, unremarkable, domestic, *hers*. She could still see the blue tiles. She could still hear the song. Somewhere in her head a seven-year-old girl who had never existed was waiting for supper in a house that had never been built.

"It puts them in you," she whispered. "It doesn't show you. It *puts them in you*."

The ore. It had to be the ore — the seam the syndicate was dredging for, the thing worth sealing a valley and drowning a railroad over. Memory held in metal. She thought of the miners' belongings, the careful shrines, the tags. Not memorials. *Stock.* Each shrine a life, pressed and preserved, ready to be sold to someone who wanted a past they hadn't earned. A dead man's boyhood. A stranger's mother. A love, pre-owned.

And Ede. Her father, who had left this place rich enough to buy a canal boat outright, who had come home strange and silent and once, drunk, had gripped her wrist and said, *Never let anyone give you a gift you can't carry back.* Who had spent his last hours pressing a map into a stranger's hands with a promise instead of a price.

He hadn't just known about this. He had worked here. He had been one of the stampers of the tags, one of the arrangers of the shrines, one of the men who taught metal to remember other people's lives — and he had sold them, crate by crate, until something in him broke and he ran, and everything he'd ever given Wren — the boat, the schooling, the years of quiet provision — had been paid for with stolen childhoods.

Including, possibly, hers.

Because if a memory could live in a tag, then a name could too. If some buyer somewhere had wanted a daughter's grief made genuine, wanted a child who would mourn Maren Ede with her whole body — no. No, that was madness. That was the chamber talking, the way deep water talks to drowning men.

Wasn't it?

She looked down at her own hands, competent, rope-scarred, unmistakably hers, and for the first time in her life could not fully swear to what they had done.

* * *

She heard Ansel's boots on the ties before she saw his lamp, and by the time the light rounded the last truck she had the tag wrapped in her scarf and her face arranged into something she hoped passed for calm. It must have failed, because he stopped several paces off, the way you stop at the edge of ice.

"Wren." Careful. "Water's rising faster than I — we need to talk about the drainage adits, there's a sound in the east wall like—"

"My father sold memories."

The words came out flat and hard, ballast dropped on a deck. Ansel went still.

"The ore holds them," she went on, because now that the dam had gone she couldn't stop, and her voice climbed. "Whole lives. You touch the metal and it *installs* them, Ansel, it doesn't show you, it makes them yours, and there are shrines in here — dozens — tagged and shelved like freight, and my father's name is on my mother's grave-tag because she wasn't—" Her breath snagged. "I remember a kitchen. Blue tiles. A song. I never had a kitchen like that. I remember it anyway. He put it in me or someone did, or I'm standing in a room full of merchandise and one of the items is *me*."

"That's not—"

"Don't." She had the lantern up between them now, and she saw him clearly: the fine sweat, the eyes that went to the wrapped tag and away again too quickly. "You lied your way upcountry so smoothly I almost admired it. You burned Ede's letters without asking me. And you're carrying a brass tag with your own family name on it, and you haven't once asked yourself the obvious question, so I'll ask it for you. How does a disgraced engineer from nowhere come to have his name sitting in a syndicate vault thirty years before he was born?"

"I don't know." His jaw worked. "I've been trying not to know."

"That's the trouble with you, Marchetti. You're very good at not knowing. It's how you lost everything the first time, isn't it? Not knowing, or pretending not to, right up until the bridge went down." She heard the cruelty land and did not take it back. Grief had weaponized her and she let it. "Ede gave you a map and a promise and you took it from a dying man without asking what he'd done to earn the guilt. Well, I'll tell you what he did. He sold people their own lives back at whatever price the market bore. Judges. Ministers. Anyone with money and a hole where a self should be. And the promise he made you keep — *deliver it whole, tell no one* — how do you know that promise wasn't bought too? How do you know you're not just another courier in the shipment, carrying stock to market and calling it honor?"

Something moved behind Ansel's face — hurt, yes, but underneath it a flicker of recognition, ugly and quick, and she seized on it.

"You were going to sell a piece." It wasn't a question; she saw it land. "You found the seam and you thought, *one sample, one sale, debts cleared, clean slate.* Didn't you. Don't lie to me now, Ansel. Lying is the one thing you do beautifully."

The chamber dripped. Somewhere far off in the rock, water boomed against stone, patient as a creditor.

Ansel opened his mouth. Closed it. And when he finally spoke, his voice was low and stripped of all his smoothness, down to the joists.

"Yes," he said. "For about ten minutes, in the dark, I was going to sell a piece. And then I touched my father's name in a dead man's vault and understood what the price of clean money is." He held her gaze. "You want proof I'm not bought? Here it is: I'm ruined, Wren. Ruined men are the only ones you can trust, because there's nothing left to sell."

"Ruined men are the easiest ones to buy," she said quietly. "That's rather the point of ruining them."

Behind the east wall, the booming stopped — and in its place came a new sound, thin and rising, a whistle of water finding a throat. Ansel's head snapped toward it, and every line of him changed.

"That adit's giving way," he said. "We have minutes. Maybe less." He was already moving, hauling the lantern, reaching for her arm — and she let him take it, because the water did not care whose memories were real.

But as they ran for the flooded portal, one thought kept pace with her, cold and clear as the cardamom smell that would not leave her:

If the chamber drowned, the tags drowned with it — and the only proof of what her mother had been, or hadn't, would go down into the dark forever.
