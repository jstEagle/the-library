## 8. The Monsoon Breaks the Line

The water found them before the men did.

Ansel heard it first as a change in the dark — a hollowness in the tunnel's breathing, the way a pipe sings just before it splits. He had spent twenty years listening to mountains, and he knew that sound the way other men knew their mothers' voices. The Vellamark line was groaning under pressure it had never been built to hold.

"Listen," he said, and put Wren's hand flat against the rail.

She felt it. Her face went still in the lamplight. "That's not water dripping."

"That's water *arriving*." He crouched and pressed his palm to the ballast. Cold seeped up through his glove. "The drainage adits on the north face. They were cut in 'sixty-one to bleed off spring melt, but nobody's maintained them since the closure. The monsoon's been sitting on that ridge for three days like a drunk on a keg." He straightened. "When those adits let go, everything above us comes down this grade at once."

"And Ossler's men?"

"Worse news than the water, or better, depending how you count." Ansel lifted the lamp and its light shivered along the tunnel walls, over the soot of forty years, over the faint wet gleam of the map's second layer still ghosting in his memory. "They're not chasing us anymore. They're sealing doors."

* * *

They had heard the blasting an hour before dawn — three concussions, muffled, patient, spaced like a man nailing shut a coffin. Then the sound of machinery: pumps, winches, the industrial cough of generators carried down the bore of the mountain until it seemed to come from everywhere. Ossler was not hunting them through the tunnels. Ossler was closing the tunnels, one by one, with steel plate and concrete and the calm thoroughness of a clerk filing papers.

"He's drowning the evidence," Wren said. "Us included."

"He's drowning the *stockpile*. Us is a rounding error." Ansel spread Ede's map across the flatbed of the handcar they'd found stabled at the second portal, its wheels rusted but true, its handle still oiled by some squatter's care. His finger traced the line they'd ridden in on, then moved to the junction chamber where the shrine room opened off the main bore — where hundreds of brass tags hung from their nails, each one a name, each name a life someone had bought or sold. Where crates of raw ore sat stacked in the dark like grain in a granary, waiting for the mill.

Memory in metal. Fortunes forged from other men's pasts. All of it sitting below the waterline of a valley that was about to become a lake.

"There," he said. "The old drainage gallery runs under the shrine chamber. It was cut to carry floodwater *past* the workings, out through the south adit into the gorge. If I can open the bulkhead between the gallery and the chamber—"

"You want to let the flood in."

"I want to *aim* it." He looked up at her, and the lamplight caught the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the dried mud on her jaw, and something in him steadied itself against all of it. "Water's honest, Wren. It goes where you tell it if you understand the grade. I've spent my whole career being blamed for water going where I told it. Let me be blamed for this."

* * *

They pumped the handcar along the main line with the sound of the rising water growing behind them — not a roar yet, not yet, but a pressure, a fullness, the way a held breath fills a chest. Twice the rails ahead shone wet where no rain could reach. Once they passed a side adit streaming like a gutter, and Ansel made himself look away, because looking meant calculating, and calculating meant knowing exactly how many minutes they had, and the number kept shrinking.

The junction chamber opened around them like a throat. Their lamps found the shrine room's mouth, and inside it the tags swung gently on their nails in a draft that should not have existed — air being pushed, somewhere far above, by water finding its way down through a hundred small wounds in the mountain.

Wren stood at the threshold. Her mother's tag was in there somewhere. Ansel saw her hands curl and uncurl at her sides.

"It has to go," she said quietly. Not asking him. Telling herself.

"It has to go," he agreed.

He worked fast then, and he worked well, and there was a terrible comfort in that — in the one part of himself the creditors and the syndicate and the disgrace had never managed to repossess. The bulkhead door to the drainage gallery was a wheel-gate, iron, seized with forty years of scale. He hammered the spindle free, packed the threads with axle grease from the handcar's tin, and threw his whole weight on the wheel while Wren braced a pry bar against the frame.

"Grade drops two percent toward the south adit," he said through his teeth. "Gallery floor's lower than the chamber floor. When this opens, the water takes the low road — through here first, then out. It'll scour the chamber clean. Tags, crates, all of it. Down the gorge."

"And us?"

"We ride the high road." He nodded back along the main line, uphill, toward the third portal — the only exit Ossler hadn't sealed yet, because Ossler didn't know the map's second layer, didn't know the line continued past the reservoir on dry track through the spine of the mountain. "Handcar. Now. We open this gate and we have maybe four minutes before the adits above finish failing, and when they do—"

The mountain answered him.

It came as a sound first, deep in the rock, like a giant clearing its throat — then the north wall of the junction chamber wept suddenly from a dozen seams, and then the weeping became spurting, and Ansel stopped talking because there was nothing left to say that the water wasn't already saying.

He threw the wheel. It turned — grudging, shrieking, a half-revolution, a full one — and the gate swung inward on darkness, and the darkness exhaled cold mineral breath, and then the water arrived.

Not a trickle. A *wall*, waist-high, black with silt, carrying branches and a dead bird and forty years of settled silt, pouring through the gate into the gallery and backing up into the shrine chamber with a sound like applause. Ansel watched the lamplight catch the first of the tags lifting off their nails, spinning, gone. The crates tipped. The ore — the beautiful, terrible ore that could make a dead man remembered and a living man unmade — tumbled into the flood and dissolved into the current like sugar.

Somewhere behind them, muffled by rock and water, came the sound of Ossler's sealed doors meeting the flood. Ansel did not stay to imagine what that sounded like from the other side.

"*Go!*"

They ran. The water chased them up the main line, ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then falling behind as the grade steepened and the flood took the roads Ansel had promised it. They reached the handcar, soaked, gasping, and threw themselves onto the platform, and Wren pumped and Ansel pumped and the car lurched forward into the dark on rails that climbed, blessedly, steadily, away from the sea coming up behind them.

Once, at a curve, Ansel looked back. Far below and behind, in the black, he saw a light — a lantern, swinging, frantic — a man's voice thin and screaming, lost almost entirely under the water's voice. Ossler's men. Sealed in with their own crime.

He looked away. There was nothing his hands could do from here, and the mountain does not take requests.

* * *

The third portal opened onto grey daylight and horizontal rain, and they fell off the handcar onto the gravel shoulder of the old right-of-way and lay there breathing like landed fish while the monsoon hammered their backs. Below them, far below, the valley was vanishing. Water stood in the streets of the drowned station. The dredges on the far shore had gone dark. And out of the south face of the mountain, from an adit that hadn't run in forty years, poured a brown roaring cataract — carrying, Ansel understood, the whole buried fortune of the Vellamark seam down into the gorge, diluted past recovery, scattered past sale, gone.

Wren lay beside him, hair plastered to her face, laughing — or crying; in the rain it was impossible to tell, and perhaps she couldn't have told either.

"It's done," she said. "Ede's promise. Delivered whole."

"Not yet." Ansel pushed himself up on his elbows. His chest hurt. Everything hurt. But his mind, his treacherous, useful engineer's mind, was already running the numbers, and the numbers had gone wrong somewhere in the last hundred yards. "The flood took the chamber. It took the stockpile. But the water's got to go *somewhere*, Wren, and the south adit empties into the gorge above the old mill settlement."

She went still beneath him. "Where the squatters are."

"Where anyone downstream is." He was already hauling himself upright, already turning to look down-valley through the rain-laced grey, toward the thread of smoke that rose — still rose — from the roofs below the gorge mouth. "We aimed a river at them. We've got maybe an hour, less if the ridge lets go again, and the only way down is the old service trail."

Wren was on her feet before he finished, wringing out her coat, and there was no grief left in her face now, no fracture — only the flat bright purpose he'd first seen in a boarding house doorway, weaponized once more.

"Then don't stand there doing arithmetic," she said. "*Run.*"

They ran.
