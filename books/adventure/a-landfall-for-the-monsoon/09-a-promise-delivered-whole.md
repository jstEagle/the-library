## 9. A Promise, Delivered Whole

The water gave us up the way a dog gives up a bone it has grown tired of — reluctantly, and with something of itself still clinging.

I came to on gravel, coughing the Vellamark out of my lungs in long grey ropes. The sky above me was the color of wet slate, and rain fell into my open eyes without any opinion about whether I lived or died. Somewhere to my left, Ansel was doing the same thing I was: lying on his back, breathing like a man relearning an old trade, his hands opening and closing at nothing.

We had come through. That was all I could hold onto for the first minute. We had come through, and behind us the mountain was still swallowing itself — I could feel it through the ground, a long subterranean groan, the sound of a hundred years of tunnel giving way to the reservoir's appetite. The flood we'd ridden ahead of had found the shrine chamber and everything in it, and the mountain had taken the whole rotten inheritance back.

"Tag," Ansel rasped, before he could manage anything else. He patted his coat with hands that shook too badly to be thorough. "Wren. My tag."

"It's there." I'd seen it when we dragged each other out of the water — the small brass shape pressed flat against his chest pocket, still pinned through the cloth as if it refused to be parted from him even now. "You're still you. Or whoever that is."

He almost laughed. It turned into coughing again.

* * *

We walked because we couldn't stand still. The far portal had emptied us onto a shelf of rock above a ravine where the old maintenance trail wound down toward the valley's western shoulder, and below us — half-tumbled down the scree, folded against a boulder like a man who had simply decided to stop mid-stride — lay Ossler.

He looked smaller dead than he had alive. That was the first obscenity of it. All those months of him looming in doorways and customs sheds, his camera clicking like a patient insect, and now he was just a wet grey man with water still trickling from his cuffs, one arm bent under him at an angle no living arm would permit. His bowler hat had lodged against a stone two yards downstream and sat there filling with rain, steady as a clock.

Ansel went to him first. I watched him crouch, watch him press two fingers to the man's throat and leave them there longer than fingers need stay on a corpse. When he withdrew his hand, he wiped it on his knee without looking at what he was doing.

"The adits," Ansel said quietly. "His own men sealed them from the outside. He was running ahead of the flood to report, and the scree—" He stopped. "Or they sealed them knowing he was inside. Either way."

Either way. The syndicate did not mourn its instruments. I found I could not summon even the cheap satisfaction of hating him anymore; he looked too much like what he was, which was spent.

It was the ledger that made me kneel beside him.

It was strapped inside his coat, oilcloth-wrapped, and the water had gotten at it anyway — the edges swollen soft, the ink of the outer pages blooming into grey flowers. But Ossler had been a careful man in the way of men built carefully, and the inner leaves were dry, bound in waxed linen that had done its work.

I opened it because I told myself I was checking for routes, for names of the enforcers who might still be hunting us. That was a lie I let myself keep for exactly as long as it took to read the first page.

* * *

It was not a ledger of money. Or rather, money was the smallest column in it, tucked at the margin like an afterthought. What it held were lives, entered in Ossler's neat clerk's hand with dates and lot numbers and prices, and each entry had two names: the name that had been mined and the name that had been bought.

A magistrate on the eastern circuit, entry dated eleven years back. A minister of public works — the same ministry that had signed the order closing the Vellamark line, though the ledger didn't say so; it didn't have to. A shipping magnate. A general. Page after page, and the buyers grew more powerful as the ore grew scarcer, until the last entries were written in a hand tighter than Ossler's, hurried, as if even the clerk had begun to fear the arithmetic of what he was recording.

And there, three pages from the end, in the middle of a column of respectable names:

**Harrow & Finch, factors and lenders, Port Calloway.**

Edmund Harrow. The man who had bought my father's debts twice over, who had sent his clerks to stand in boarding house stairwells, who had a face like a closed ledger himself. He had purchased a memory. Of course he had. A man who lends money against other men's pasts would want a past of his own that could bear inspection — some honest grandfather, some grief genuinely felt, some boyhood on a farm that never existed, installed wholesale so that when he spoke of hardship he could believe he'd known it.

I sat with the ledger open on my knees and the rain coming down and understood that I was holding enough power to buy a country's conscience. Names. Dates. Proof. Any court in the territory would convulse. Every buyer on that list would pay anything — anything — to see these pages burn, and anything was a great deal when the buyers included judges and ministers and Edmund Harrow.

My father's debts. Ansel's debts. Wiped with a single anonymous letter and a photostat page. And vengeance besides — real vengeance, the kind with teeth, against the machine that had eaten my mother's name and sold it in slices.

"Wren." Ansel's voice, low. He had read over my shoulder. Of course he had. "That's—"

"I know whose name that is."

"He could clear us. Both of us. One page. We wouldn't even have to threaten him. We'd only have to show him we *could*—"

"And then what?" I heard my own voice come out flat and cold, and I let it. "Then we're Ossler. Then we're the syndicate with better manners. You hold a man's invented childhood over his head and call it justice?" I turned the pages with a thumb that wasn't quite steady. "Look at them, Ansel. Look at what this is. It isn't evidence. It's inventory. This is the stock list of everything they stole, and every name in here belonged to somebody once — somebody who died, or was broken, or was *made*, and then got sold by weight."

Ede's voice was in my head then, clear as if he stood dripping on the trail beside us: *Bring it back whole. Tell no one what it found.* I had thought, all this way, that the promise was about the map. About the mine. Standing there with the ledger in my lap I finally understood it had never been about the map at all. Ede had made his fortune in this trade and spent his last years trying to bury it, and the promise was the burial. The whole expedition — the map, the dive, the chamber, the flood — had been a funeral procession, and I had nearly arrived at the graveside planning to rob it.

I stood up. The oilcloth was soaked through where the rain hit it, but the inner pages were still dry, and I suppose some part of me wanted Ansel to argue, wanted him to give me a reason to hate him cleanly instead of the muddled way I actually felt.

Instead he said, "Burn it, then," in a voice like a man agreeing to his own sentence.

* * *

We burned it in the lee of the boulder, with matches from Ansel's tin and the bowler hat as a windbreak, which felt like the only proper use anyone would ever find for it. The waxed linen caught slowly and then all at once, and the pages curled and blackened and lifted in fragments, and I fed them to the fire one at a time because I wanted to watch each name go. The magistrate. The minister. The magnate. The general. Edmund Harrow and his purchased boyhood, his borrowed grief, gone up in a greasy yellow flame that the rain kept trying to put out and failing.

It should have felt like loss. It felt like setting down something heavy I had forgotten I was carrying.

When the last corner had gone to ash and the ash had gone to paste under the rain, Ansel spoke without looking at me.

"There's something you should know. Before you hear it worse than this."

I waited.

"The night Ede died. After you left the stairwell. He was delirious, and I went through his coat — for money, Wren. For anything I could pawn." His jaw worked. "There was a sample. A chip of the ore, wrapped in flannel, small as a coin. And I took it. I told myself I was keeping it safe. I told myself a dozen things. But I had it in my pocket when we boarded the ferry, and I had it when Ossler photographed us, and I had it in the customs shed, and I knew — I knew — what a piece that size would fetch from just one name in that ledger. Enough to bury Harrow forever. Enough to start over clean, with no past at all, which is what I wanted more than I wanted to breathe."

The rain filled the silence between us.

"Where is it now?" I asked.

"Gone. It was in my breast pocket, on the same side as the tag. The water—" He spread his hands. "The mountain has it. Or the river does. And God forgive me, but when I realized it was gone, on the gravel, ten minutes ago — the first thing I felt was relief. The second thing was shame about the relief. And I can't tell you which came first, and that frightens me more than the flood did."

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty and because I owed him some after the things I'd suspected. I had spent weeks certain his smoothness was bought, that his easy lies were the polish on a purchased soul. And here he was, half-drowned, confessing to a theft the mountain had already punished, choosing to hand me the knife instead of hiding that he'd ever carried it.

"Ede trusted you," I said at last. "A dying man, who knew exactly what that ore does, looked at you in a stairwell and handed you the map. Maybe he saw the lie in you already. Maybe he trusted you *because* you wanted the clean life so badly you could taste it — because a man that hungry will understand what it costs." I crouched and took a handful of wet ash, let it run through my fingers into the rain. "I'm not saying it's forgiven. I'm saying I believe you're sorry, and I don't believe many things lately."

He nodded, and said nothing, and the nothing between us was the first honest thing either of us had owned since Port Calloway.

* * *

We followed the maintenance trail down as the light failed, two drowned creatures walking out of a drowned country. Behind us the mountain went on settling, groaning, taking the line and the shrines and the seam down into the dark where no clerk would ever photograph them again. Ahead, somewhere past the ravine's mouth, there was a road, and past the road a town, and past the town the whole wide world of people who would never know what had been buried for their sake.

At the trail's edge Ansel stopped and touched his chest pocket once — the tag, the brass, the name that was only a name now — the way another man might touch a wound to confirm it's still there.

"Wren," he said. "When we get down. There's something I have to tell you about your mother. Something I worked out in the chamber, before the water came. Something Ede wrote on the back of the map, in the layer that only shows when it's wet."

The rain came harder. Down in the valley, far off, a train whistle sounded — one long note over the flooded country, like something calling a name across water.

"We have to get off this mountain first," he said. "But you'll want to be sitting down."
