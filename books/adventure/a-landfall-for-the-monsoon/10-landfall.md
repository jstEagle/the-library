## 10. Landfall

The letter came on a Tuesday, in an envelope too clean for the weather it had traveled through.

Ansel stood at the counter of the Harrowgate post office with rain still dripping off his coat, and read the clerk's typed notice twice: *All outstanding obligations against A. Marchetti have been satisfied in full by an undisclosed party. Please direct further correspondence to this office.*

Satisfied in full. Four words that should have felt like salvation and instead sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He knew what it was. He had known since Wren burned the ledger — or thought he'd known, watching the pages curl and blacken in her hands while Ossler's body cooled beside them and the flood roared somewhere below like a train that would never arrive. She had burned it all. Every name. Every purchased life. He had watched her do it and said nothing, because saying nothing was the promise kept whole.

But paper burns unevenly. And men like his creditor, Voss, did not keep their only copies in ledgers carried through flooded tunnels.

So someone had paid. Someone who remembered — or half-remembered, the way a man remembers a dream after waking — that Ansel Marchetti owed them money, and had reached through the fog of their own forged past to settle it anyway. Perhaps out of guilt. Perhaps out of fear. Perhaps some minister or magistrate had woken one morning with a hole where a memory used to be and decided, prudently, to close accounts.

It didn't matter. That was the strange mercy of it. The debt was gone, and he hadn't bought its erasure, and no one could hold it over him, because whoever held it no longer fully existed as the person who'd lent it.

"You'll want to sign for that," the clerk said, nodding at a second item beneath the notice — a small parcel, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with tarred twine.

"I know what it is," Ansel said. But he signed anyway, because the world ran on signatures, and he was done pretending otherwise.

* * *

The boarding house room in Harrowgate was smaller than the one in the port town where Ede had died, but it had a window that faced east, and in the weeks since his return Ansel had developed the habit of sitting by it in the grey first light with tea going cold beside him, doing nothing. Doing nothing, he had learned, was a skill. It required practice. For years his mind had been a switching yard — every thought a train demanding routing, every anxiety a signal lamp burning red. Now he let them pass through unshunted, and mostly they found their own way out of the station.

He unwrapped the parcel on the table. Inside, nested in wood shavings, was Ede's compass.

Brass, green with age at the seams, the glass scratched into a faint haze. The needle swung once, twice, and settled pointing north with the lazy confidence of an instrument that had outlived everyone who trusted it. On the reverse, worn nearly smooth, the maker's mark: a mountain over three lines of track.

Ede had made him swear to bring the map back whole and tell no one what it found. The map was gone — drowned, dissolved, along with the shrine chamber and the ore and the tunnel that had held both. But the compass had been Ede's before the map, before the selling, before any of it. It had pointed him into the mountains the first time, when he was young and honest and didn't yet know what lay under the Vellamark line. It seemed right that it should go back to the family Ede had left behind, such as that family was: one estranged daughter, one borrowed stranger, and a grave in canal country with a stone that probably spelled the name wrong.

He wrote the address from memory. *Wren Ede, care of the Lockmaster's Office, Tarn Reach.* He had looked it up twice to be sure. He had started the letter four times.

*Wren —*

*The debts are cleared. I don't know how, exactly, and I've stopped trying to find out. I think you'd tell me not to look. I think you'd say looking is how they get you.*

*I'm taking work with the Coastal & Northern. Survey crew, open line, nothing under a hundred feet of anything. They asked about my record and I told them the truth — struck off for the Kessel viaduct, reinstated pending review — and the surveyor-in-chief read my file for a long time and then said, "Can you read ground?" and I said yes, and that was the whole interview. Honest track, Wren. Track you can stand on and see the whole country around it.*

*The compass is yours. I'm sorry I never met him as he was at the end instead of at the beginning. I'm sorry for what he carried, and sorrier for what he sold. But he kept one thing whole, and it was the promise, and in the end so did we.*

*— A. Marchetti*

He read it over, added nothing, took away nothing, and sealed it. Some letters are finished the moment they're true.

* * *

The Coastal & Northern put him on the Meldrum extension: forty miles of new cut through open moorland, running from nowhere in particular toward somewhere useful, surveyed in daylight by men who ate bad sandwiches and argued about gradients and went home to wives who knew their real names.

Ansel loved it with a fierceness that embarrassed him.

There was nothing hidden in it. That was the whole of its beauty. When he sank a datum stake into the peat, the ground told him the truth — soft here, shale there, water table at four feet, drain accordingly. The land lied to no one. It had no ledger, no second layer visible only when wet, no chambers where other people's lives sat arranged like offerings. A hill was a hill. A river wanted to go downhill. His whole profession, he realized now, had always been a conversation with honesty, and he had spent six years speaking it in a whisper, ashamed, while the ground went on telling the truth whether he listened or not.

"Marchetti." The surveyor-in-chief, a squat woman named Brandt with forearms like dock rope, fell into step beside him on the third week. "You walk these cuts like a man checking for ghosts."

"Don't believe in ghosts."

"No," she agreed. "But you check anyway."

He smiled. "Habit."

"Well, break it or keep it, I don't care. You read ground better than anyone I've hired in twenty years, and I've hired men with clean records who couldn't find a valley with both hands." She spat expertly over the cut. "Whatever you did down south, the ground's already forgotten it. Ground's good that way."

He watched her stride off down the alignment, red flag snapping on her staff, and understood that she had just given him the only absolution that mattered — the kind that came without asking anything in return.

* * *

The brass tag lived in his breast pocket, over the heart, which was where he'd carried his father's watch in the years before he pawned it, and where he suspected important weights were meant to sit.

He took it out sometimes, in the evenings, in whatever lodging the survey crews had thrown up that night. It was a small thing — two inches of stamped brass, a punched hole where a cord had run through, edges gone soft with handling. His family name in capital letters: MARCHETTI. No dates. No company stamp. No serial number tying it to a seam or a shipment or a buyer.

In the shrine chamber it had hung among hundreds of others, each tagged with a name that belonged to no miner present — names collected, arranged, offered up to the dark like votive lights. Proof of lives held in reserve. Inventory of selves.

He had pocketed his own almost without deciding to. Reflex, maybe. Or the superstition of a man who finds his own name in a place where names are currency and wants, at minimum, to control the supply.

Now he turned it in the firelight and considered, not for the first time, throwing it in the nearest river.

He never did. Because the tag had changed, and he could not quite explain how, except that everything changes when it drowns and comes back up. In the chamber it had been a claim — evidence that somewhere in the syndicate's books, a Marchetti existed, purchasable, forgeable, a past in escrow waiting for some powerful stranger to buy his childhood and his father's voice and the smell of his mother's kitchen. It had been a door left open into him.

Now it was just metal with letters on it. The stockpile was silt. The buyers were amnesiacs. The ledger was ash in Wren's cupped hands on a wet rock shelf, and whatever fragments survived in the memories of powerful men were holes now, not histories — gaps they'd wake to and puzzle over and eventually, mercifully, stop noticing, the way you stop noticing a missing tooth.

The tag belonged to no one else. It couldn't. Whatever else had been written in Marchetti's name had drowned with the valley.

That was worth carrying. Not as a talisman. As a receipt.

* * *

The monsoon ended the way it began — all at once, overnight, as if a decision had been made above the clouds.

One morning the rain simply wasn't there anymore, and the sky over the moorland went hard and high and blue, and the survey crews worked with their collars open for the first time in months. Rivers dropped back inside their banks. Roads dried to rutted iron. In the low country, Ansel knew, the canals would be running high and clear, and pilots would be walking their locks again, and a woman named Wren would be reading water the way he read ground — honestly, professionally, with her father's compass in her coat pocket if she'd chosen to carry it, or not, if she'd chosen otherwise. Both choices were hers. That was the point of mailing it rather than delivering it. Some things you return without requiring a signature.

He wrote to her once more that autumn — a short note, no promises in it, just the coordinates of the Meldrum extension and a line: *If your work ever brings you north, the ground out here tells the truth, and I've gotten good at listening.* She never answered. He hadn't expected her to. But in December a parcel arrived containing a tin of canal-country tea and a single sheet of paper with a hand-drawn sketch of a lock gate and, beneath it, in her blunt drafting hand: *Still standing. So are you. Good.*

He pinned it above his bunk.

* * *

On the last day of the season, the Coastal & Northern ran the first inspection car down the completed Meldrum cut — forty miles of honest rail, ballasted and tamped and true, crossing open country under an open sky. Ansel rode in the front seat beside Brandt while the moorland unspooled, brown going gold going white at the far horizon, and the rails sang underneath them the clean, unbroken note that only well-laid track makes.

"There's talk," Brandt said, watching the country slide past, "of reopening some of the old mountain lines. Up Vellamark way. Government commission, survey permits, the whole ceremony." She glanced sideways at him. "They'll want experienced men. Men who knew the terrain."

The needle of the world swung. Settled.

"The valley's drowned," Ansel said. "Reservoir water sits forty feet over the portals. The adits collapsed in the floods — I saw it happen." All true. All verifiable. "Anything down there is beyond reach, and the ground up top won't carry a road. You'd be surveying the bottom of a lake."

Brandt shrugged. "Governments drown things and governments drain them. Nothing stays buried forever, Marchetti. Not in my experience."

"Then in your experience," he said quietly, "you'll know why some men decline the commission."

She studied him a long moment — the same long read she'd given his file, the one that ended in hiring instead of dismissal — and then nodded once and turned back to the window. "I'll put your name down as unavailable. Permanently, if you like."

"Permanently," Ansel said.

The inspection car ran on, singing, toward the coast. In his breast pocket, over his heart, a small weight of brass rode warm against his chest — a name, and nothing more, belonging to no one but the man who carried it. Behind them the mountains stood dark against the clearing sky, holding their water and their silence, keeping what was theirs.

Ahead, the landfall. Open country. Daylight all the way down.
