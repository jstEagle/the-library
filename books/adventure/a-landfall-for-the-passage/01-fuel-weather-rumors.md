## 1. Fuel, Weather, Rumors

The river told the truth before the sky did.

Sable Delacroix had learned to read it the way other pilots read instruments — the color of the water shifting from bottle-green to the brown of steeped tea, the way the current braided itself around new gravel bars overnight, the smell of the lowlands when the heat pressed down hard enough to make the reeds sweat. The Kestrel was dropping. Three weeks early, if her logbook was honest, and her logbook was always honest because it was the only thing in her life she'd never lied to.

She banked the Otter over the last ridge and Halfway Bend unfolded below her like something a child had made and loved: forty roofs of corrugated tin and thatch strung along a crescent of high ground where the river made a lazy elbow, gardens stitched bright green against the dun hills, the airstrip a brown scar mowed through elephant grass at the settlement's edge. Smoke stood up straight from a dozen cookfires. Laundry hung like signal flags. Somewhere down there, Marta Okonkwo was already standing at the strip's windsock with her hands on her hips, because Marta could hear the Otter's engine from her kitchen and had once claimed — deadpan, daring you to argue — that she could tell Sable's plane from every other aircraft in the territory by the sound of its left magneto, which ran rough and always had.

The left magneto ran rough now. Sable talked to it anyway, the way she talked to all the parts of the plane that worried her, a low one-sided conversation conducted under the engine noise. *Hold together. Just hold together.*

The strip was soft from two days of rain earlier in the week, and she came in over the trees with a touch so gentle it felt like an apology, wheels kissing grass, tail settling, the Otter rolling out past the first of the kids who materialized the way they always did, sprinting from three directions as if the airplane were a dropped coin and they'd all seen it land first. She cut the mixture and let the propeller wind down into silence, and the silence filled up immediately with voices.

"Your magneto's worse," Marta announced, arriving at the door before Sable had it open. Marta was sixty and built like the drum she played at festivals, and she had delivered more babies in Halfway Bend than the settlement had telephones, which was saying something, since Halfway Bend had four.

"Morning to you too."

"Morning. Your magneto's worse. You've been running it rich on the climb-outs again."

"I have not been—" Sable stopped, because Marta's eyebrows had gone up in the particular way that meant she was enjoying being right and would enjoy it more if Sable kept talking. "It runs rich in hot air. That's physics."

"That's stubbornness." Marta reached into the cabin and hauled out the first crate without waiting for permission, reading the stencil upside down. "Amoxicillin, good. The fever's been through the east side like a rumor. Two of Rosa's little ones." Her voice did something small and controlled around the word *fever*, and Sable understood that the medicine run had stopped being routine somewhere in the last week, back when Sable had still been three hundred miles away loading drums in Port Anselm.

"How bad?"

"Not bad. Bad-adjacent." Marta set the crate down and looked at her directly, and for a moment the banter drained out of her face and left something older and wearier underneath. "You got here quick."

"You radioed Tuesday."

"I radioed Tuesday and most pilots would've come Thursday or next week or whenever the freight schedule pleased them." Marta picked the crate back up. "Come on. Help me carry. You can count syringes while I work, you're good at counting things."

*You're good at counting things.* It was said without malice — Marta didn't deal in malice — but it landed in Sable like a splinter worked deep enough that she'd stop noticing it until she gripped something wrong. She counted things. Counted flight hours, counted seasons, counted the days remaining in any place before the leaving became necessary. She had a system for it, elegant as a fuel calculation: arrive, deliver, be useful, be pleasant, begin the slow withdrawal before anyone could form expectations with edges on them. A season, never longer. The dry season here, the wet season there, a stretch of coast between, a mining town after that. Ten years of arithmetic that always summed to gone.

She carried crates up the path to the clinic with the children orbiting her like pilot fish, asking the questions children asked — how fast, how high, had she ever seen a snake in the plane, was it true she'd once landed on a sandbar — and answering with the polished half-truths she kept for exactly this purpose. Yes, fast. Very high. Once, a tree frog, which was funnier than a snake. The sandbar story was true, mostly, though she trimmed the part where she'd bent a strut doing it.

The clinic was a long room with a tin roof that turned noon into an oven and gauze curtains that turned it into a lantern. Marta worked with her sleeves rolled and her hands moving, and Sable sat on a stool by the supply shelf and broke down crates, counting ampoules into the wooden box Marta called the pharmacy's brain. Outside, the settlement went about its business loudly. Inside, there was only the clink of glass and the small hot cries of Rosa's youngest getting an injection, quickly done, quickly comforted.

"He'll keep the fever down tonight," Marta said, coming to stand beside her, wiping her hands. "Thanks to you."

"Thanks to whoever donated the amoxicillin. I just flew it."

"You flew it through weather I wouldn't send my mother's ghost into." Marta studied her with those eyes that had looked at ten thousand faces and missed nothing in any of them. "Sable. The festival's in nine days. First rains are late this year, the river's confused, everyone's restless. Stay for it."

There it was. Every season, someone asked. It was almost a ritual now, and Sable had her responses worn smooth as river stone.

"I've got a charter out of Anselm on the twelfth."

"Move the charter."

"It's a medical charter."

"Then move it and feel guilty, guilt keeps." Marta laughed, but her hand came down on Sable's shoulder and stayed there, warm and heavy as a roof beam. "Nine days. There'll be dancing. Old Tomás is going to roast a pig the size of a canoe. The kids are building a float, God help us all, it has wings. You'd have a bed. My spare room, the one with the window that catches the morning."

The spare room with the window that caught the morning. Sable had slept in it twice, two years ago, during a week of storms that grounded everything, and she remembered it with a specificity that frightened her: the quality of the light, the sound of rain on tin, Marta's rooster conducting his idiot dawn service. She remembered it because she had let herself forget, briefly, that she was leaving. That was the danger. Not the staying — the forgetting.

"I'll try," she said, which was what she always said, and they both knew it meant nothing, and Marta's hand squeezed once and lifted away, and neither of them said anything else about it, because Marta was kind and Sable was a coward and kindness knows better than to press a coward.

* * *

The rest of the day was cargo and gossip in equal measure, and Sable had never been able to say which of them paid better.

She ferried mail sacks to the clinic porch and let people pick through them — no letters for the Vasquez family, bad news deferred; three letters for old Tomás, who held them unopened for an hour, savoring; a package of photographs from relatives downriver that got passed hand to hand through half the settlement before dark. She drank sweet, over-steeped tea with the head teacher, who wanted to know whether the government had approved the school roof money, and she told him what she'd heard in Port Anselm, which was that the money existed and was moving at the speed of government, and he sighed the sigh of a man who'd been teaching under a leaking roof for six years and would teach under it for six more. She listened to the boatmen argue about the river — dropping too fast, dropping wrong, the fish behaving strangely, the shallows at Crooked Reach already unnavigable when they should hold another month — and she filed their talk away the way she filed weather reports, because the river's mood was her road and she was superstitious enough to respect its opinions.

And she carried rumors, because rumors were ballast on the return leg. Who was courting whom. Which family was feuding over a fence line. That the Meridian Company had posted new notices at the downriver trading posts — survey permits, mineral claims, the usual paper colonizing — and that the notices covered stretches of the upper Kestrel nobody had ever bothered to claim, because everybody knew there was nothing up there but water and ghosts. She passed that along at the evening fire and watched it ripple outward, watched men frown and women shrug, and thought nothing of it. Paper claims on empty country. Companies collected empty country the way some men collected stamps.

By dusk the airfield was hers alone. This was her favorite hour and she guarded it jealously, walking the Otter's length in the gold light with a rag in one hand, checking the leading edges for dings, draining the fuel samples into a glass jar and holding them up to see what the day's tanks had collected — water, a little, sediment, less than yesterday. The left magneto. She pulled the cowl and put her hands on the warm metal and felt the engine ticking as it cooled, a language of contraction and patience.

Ten years in this plane. Longer than she'd lived anywhere, longer than she'd known anyone except the machine itself. People assumed the flying was the freedom — the altitude, the horizon, the whole world laid out like a map with no fences in it. They were wrong, or they were right in a way that shamed her. The flying was the freedom the way a locked door is privacy. What she'd actually built, bolt by bolt across a decade, was a life with no surfaces for anything to stick to. No debts. No promises with dates attached. No address that expected her. She could be gone from any place in twenty minutes, and she had been, many times, and each time the takeoff had felt like both escape and amputation, and she had never once examined which of those it truly was, because examination was the one cargo she refused to carry.

Marta found her there, of course. Marta always found her, the way water finds the low ground.

"She's a good plane," Marta said, nodding at the Otter, arms folded against the cooling air. Insects were starting to stitch the dusk together. From the settlement came the smell of woodsmoke and frying plantains and somebody's radio playing something with a lot of accordion in it.

"She's the best thing I ever did."

"That's a sad sentence, Sable."

"It's an honest one."

"Honest and sad aren't enemies." Marta came closer, lowered her voice, and the accordion music seemed to recede to make room. "I'm going to ask you something and you don't have to answer. But I'm going to ask it." She waited until Sable looked at her. "What is it you think will happen if you stay?"

The question went through her like cold fuel through a warm engine. Sable looked at the plane instead of the woman. "Nothing happens if I stay. That's the point. Nothing happens, and then it's a year, and then it's five, and then I'm somebody who lives in Halfway Bend, and then—"

"And then what? Then you'd have friends who know your birthday. Then you'd have a room that's yours. Then you'd be at the festival every year instead of hearing about it secondhand from your own cargo manifests." Marta shook her head slowly. "Child, I've buried a husband and two sons and I will tell you what I've learned: the running doesn't outrun anything. It just spreads you thin enough that the grief gets diluted. That's all it does."

"I'm not running from grief."

"No," Marta agreed mildly. "You're running from whatever it is you won't name, which is worse, because at least grief has a shape."

They stood in the insect-stitched dark for a while. Sable's hands were still on the cowling, and the metal had gone cool under her palms, and she realized she was gripping it.

"Nine days," Marta said finally. "Think about it. That's all I ask. Don't answer me tonight. Answer the rooster tomorrow, he's very persuasive." And she laughed, and touched Sable's arm, and walked back toward the lights and the accordion, leaving Sable alone with the plane and the first stars and the river muttering to itself in the dark below the strip.

She didn't sleep in the spare room. She slept in the Otter, as she often did, in the nest of blankets behind the cargo netting, with the map case under her head like a pillow stuffed with her own history. Through the windshield the stars wheeled slow and indifferent. She lay awake doing the arithmetic she always did — fuel remaining, days remaining, the twelfth of the month approaching like a shoreline — and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the altitude she hadn't flown yet.

* * *

She left on the ninth day.

Not the twelfth — the ninth, two days ahead of any schedule, because on the eighth night Marta's rooster woke her before dawn and she lay listening to the settlement breathe and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was happy. Genuinely, dangerously happy. Someone had left a plate of fried dough by the plane's wheel with a note in a child's careful hand. The schoolteacher had waved from his porch. The river lay silver and patient, and the mountains upstream stood blue in the first light, and she had caught herself thinking *next year*, and the words had tasted like a trap springing shut.

So she loaded at first light, hugged Marta hard enough to make the old woman grunt, promised nothing, and took off into a sky scrubbed clean by the night, climbing away from Halfway Bend with the settlement shrinking to a toy village, a scatter of tin and smoke beside its brown ribbon of river.

She told herself the schedule demanded it. She told herself the charter was real. Both things were true, and neither was the reason, and she knew it, and the knowing sat in the cabin with her like a passenger she couldn't charge for the seat.

An hour upriver, where the Kestrel narrowed and the hills closed in and the maps went vague, she saw the smoke.

Not cookfire smoke — cookfires were domestic, round, unhurried. This was a thin gray thread rising from the tree line near a clearing she knew only as a mark on her own chart: Wren Strip, a private cut some prospector had hacked out of the bush years ago, the kind of lonely scar in the forest that only she ever bothered servicing, because only she flew this stretch without a schedule to obey.

She circled once. Twice. The thread of smoke didn't move, didn't thicken. A signal fire, maybe. Or a dying one.

Sable Delacroix had a rule about unscheduled landings at lonely strips, and the rule was the same rule that governed everything else: don't. Don't stop, don't look, don't get involved, because involved was the first syllable of stuck.

Her hand moved the throttle anyway, and the nose came down, and the Otter descended toward the clearing like something falling in slow motion and calling it a landing.
