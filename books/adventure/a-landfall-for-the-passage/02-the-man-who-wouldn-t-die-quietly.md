## 2. The Man Who Wouldn't Die Quietly

The strip at Kettle Flats was the kind of place that existed only because Sable said it did. It wasn't on any chart worth buying — a scar of mowed grass and gravel along a terrace above the Kestrel, half a mile of flat that some homesteader had cleared forty years ago and then abandoned to the burdock. She'd found it her second year on the river, bounced twice down its length, decided it would hold, and put it in her own private atlas: a hand-inked book she kept in the Otter's map case, filled with strips like this one that the government had forgotten and the river remembered. She serviced Kettle Flats once a month, rain or shine, because a woman named Odile Trask ran a trapline out of the old homestead and traded muskrat pelts for antibiotics, shotgun shells, and news.

It was Odile who flagged her down.

Sable came in low over the terrace the way she always did, one wheel-first pass to read the wind in the grass, and saw Odile standing at the upwind end waving both arms over her head — not the lazy hello-wave of a woman expecting coffee and gossip, but the flat, urgent sweep of someone who needed a pilot to land *now*. Sable flared, touched, rolled out through the burdock smell and the drone of grasshoppers, and had the tail tied down before Odile reached her.

"You've got a man in your cabin," Odile said, breathless. "Came up the river two days ago in a leaky canoe. Been sleeping in the old smokehouse. I thought he was just a prospector resting up." She wiped her hands on her apron, and Sable saw the smear on the cloth before she saw it on the woman's fingers. Red-brown. Old. "He's not resting up, Sable. He's dying, and he won't go quiet about it."

"Did you radio ahead?"

"Radio's been dead since the lightning in June. You know that." Odile's jaw tightened; she hated being caught without the tools of the modern world, hated more that Sable knew it. "I was going to send word down with the pelt boat Thursday. He hasn't got Thursday."

Sable looked down the strip, at the smokehouse leaning against the treeline with its door ajar, and felt the particular weight settle onto her shoulders that she had spent ten years learning to set down again as fast as it landed. People in trouble along the Kestrel had a way of finding the one pilot who never stayed. It was as if they could smell it on her — the transience, the refusal — and decided she was exactly the right instrument for a job that required leaving.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

The smokehouse smelled of alder smoke and old meat and, underneath it, the sweetish stink of a body failing. A hurricane lamp hung from a rafter nail and threw a low gold light across a man stretched on a cot that had been dragged in from somewhere, his boots still on, his hat over his face like a man napping in a hayfield. He was sixty, maybe seventy — the river aged people unevenly, all weather and no years — with a beard gone white at the chin and hands that lay on his chest like two knotted roots.

When Sable's shadow crossed the lamp, the hat came off the face, and the man looked at her with eyes that were still, stubbornly, working. Clear. Hungry. The eyes of a man who had been waiting for exactly this shadow.

"You're the pilot," he said. His voice was gravel dragged uphill. "Trask said the pilot comes the first week of the month. I've been listening for you since yesterday."

"Then you've had two days to think about whatever you want to ask me," Sable said, "and I'm going to guess the answer is no."

The man laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on long enough that Odile started forward with a tin cup, and Sable stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame and did not move, because moving closer was a thing she had learned not to do. You could carry medicine. You could carry mail. You could carry a man to a hospital if there was a hospital and fuel enough to reach it — the nearest one was nine hours south, and this man had nine minutes. But you could not carry the rest of it. The rest of it stayed with you, in the plane, in the nights, in the way every settlement started to look like a place you owed something to.

The cough subsided. The man wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, considered the result, and said, "Elias Wren."

"Sable Delacroix."

"I know." He said it simply, without menace, and it landed on her anyway like a hand on the back of her neck. "You fly the whole upper river. Everybody knows the pilot who flies the whole upper river and never stays anywhere." He shifted on the cot, and Sable heard the wet rattle in him, deep down, where the water gathers when the pumps are failing. "I've got something for you to carry."

"I carry freight and mail. I'm not a taxi for last wishes."

"No." His eyes didn't leave hers. "You're the only one who goes where this needs to go."

Odile made a small sound and retreated to the doorway, and Sable understood that the two of them had already had this conversation, or the shape of it, and that Odile had decided it belonged to the pilot.

Wren's hand went under the cot blanket, moving with the deliberation of a man who had rehearsed this, and came out with a oilcloth packet, rolled and tied with fishing line. He held it out across the space between them, and Sable did not take it.

"Maps like this get pilots killed," she said.

"I haven't shown it to you yet."

"I don't need to see it. A man crawls up a river in a dying canoe to a strip nobody services, and the first thing he wants is a courier. That's not freight. That's a secret, and secrets are the heaviest cargo there is." She heard her own voice go flat and professional, the voice she used for quoting rates, and was grateful for it. "I fly medicine to Halfway Bend and flour to the Trask line and once a year I fly a coffin down to Anselm when somebody's family can pay the fuel. That's the whole of my business. You want a priest or a lawyer or a daughter. I'm none of those."

"A daughter." Something moved through his face then, quick as a fish under water, and was gone before she could name it. "That's the long and short of it, yes."

"Then send her a letter. I'll carry a letter. Letters are cheap."

"I've written her eleven letters in twenty years." He said it without self-pity, an inventory. "She's answered one. You can't fold what I've got into a letter."

"Then you've got a problem I can't solve." Sable turned for the door, and it was not a bluff, and it was not cruelty, and it was also — she knew, walking away from a dying man in a smokehouse that smelled of alder and endings — not entirely the truth either. It was the truth wearing the uniform of self-preservation, which is how most truths travel out here.

"Delacroix." The name cracked across the little room. She stopped. "Ten years you've been flying this river. You think nobody's noticed that you land everywhere and belong nowhere? You think the Company doesn't keep a file on every pilot who won't sign their fuel contract?" A pause, wet breathing. "I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you I know what running looks like. I've been doing it longer than you've been alive."

Sable turned around. The lamp light had gone amber and thick, and Wren had pushed himself up on one elbow, and the oilcloth packet was still in his fist, held out now not like a gift but like a hook.

"Show me," she said, and hated how easily she said it.

* * *

He unrolled it on his chest, because he could not sit up all the way, and made her lean over him to see it, so that she read the map down the length of a dying man's body — the river drawn in ink gone brown, the contour lines laid in with a draftsman's hand, the annotations in pencil so small and dense they looked like moss.

It was the Kestrel. She knew it the way you know your own signature: the great southward bend at Halfway Bend, the braided shallows at the Two Sisters, the canyon country above where the charts went blank and the Company's survey maps simply stopped, the paper equivalent of a locked door. Everyone on the river knew the headwaters were up there somewhere. Nobody went. The Meridian Company had held the upper Kestrel concession for sixty years and had never once filed a work plan for it, never built a camp, never cut a road — and had also, through lawyers and letters and once, famously, a floatplane that turned back two geologists at gunpoint, made it very clear that no one else would either. The river above the canyon was a blank on the map and a closed door in the world, and Sable had flown past the mouth of that canyon a hundred times and never once looked up it longer than she had to.

Wren's map looked up it. The river continued past the blank, in his fine brown ink, through the canyon and beyond it, mile by penciled mile, to a place marked at the top of the sheet in letters so small she had to bend close enough to smell the smoke in his beard. Not *X marks the spot*. Nothing so honest. Just a surveyor's neat abbreviation and a date — and the date was thirty-one years old.

"Twenty years I've been going up there," Wren said, watching her read. "Off and on. When the money allowed and the Company wasn't watching." His hand rose, trembling, and one root-knuckled finger touched the top of the sheet. "I know what's at the source. Not gold. Not the way the stories say. Something the Company wants kept under water and under silence, and I've spent twenty years making sure it isn't only under my memory anymore."

"What is it?"

"No." He almost smiled. "You don't get the what. You get the where. That's the trade."

"I'm not making a trade."

"Then I'll make it for you, since you're too honest to make it yourself." He drew a breath that whistled, and Sable realized with a cold, clinical detachment that she was now timing his breaths, that some part of her had already accepted the cargo. "You carry this to Port Anselm. To Ines Wren, my daughter. She works out of the surveyor's office there, does their charts — ironic as God. You put this in her hands and you tell her it's from her father, and you tell her the date on it, and you tell her he said: *finish it.* That's all. You don't have to read it. You don't have to believe it. You don't even have to like me." Another breath, longer, harder won. "You fly it down and you hand it over and you go back to your medicine runs, and whatever happens after that happens to her, not to you. That's the deal. That's the whole deal. I'm not asking you to land anywhere, Delacroix. I'm asking you to do the one thing you're already good at."

The smokehouse was very quiet. Outside, a heron cried once off the oxbow, that prehistoric rasping sound, and Odile's dog answered it with a single bark and thought better of it.

"And if she doesn't want it?" Sable asked. "A map to a place the Company kills to keep closed? You said it yourself — I've got a file. Maybe she's got one too. Maybe she's built a life on not hearing from you, and you're asking a stranger to walk a bomb into it because you're dying and dying men get to be selfish."

"Yes," Wren said. No flinch. "That's exactly what I'm asking."

Sable straightened up. She looked at the map a moment longer — at the fine brown river running through the blank on the world, at the small dense handwriting that reminded her, absurdly, of the private atlas in her own map case, the hand-inked record of every place the official world had forgotten. Two maps. Two people who had spent their lives writing down what wasn't supposed to exist.

"I'll think about it," she said, which was a lie, and walked out into the white afternoon light, and stood a long time by the Otter with her hand on the propeller, not spinning it.

* * *

She spent the night at Odile's table, which was against every rule she had, and told herself it was because the wind had come around and the strip was soft. Odile put coffee in front of her and didn't talk, which was the reason Sable liked her — one of the few people on the river who understood that silence could be a gift rather than a debt.

"He offered you something," Odile said, near midnight. "Men like that always offer you something. What was it?"

"A burden."

"Those are the only things anybody offers a woman like you," Odile said, and refilled the cup, and went to bed.

Sable sat with the lamp and thought about the shape of her life, which was the shape of a river seen from altitude — a line that looked like freedom from up high and, from the water, was just a thing being carried somewhere whether it consented or not. Ten years of seasons, each one abandoned just as it started to ask something of her. Ten years of being the pilot who lands everywhere and belongs nowhere, and telling herself the second half was the price of the first, when the truth was probably the reverse.

Near dawn she went out to the smokehouse. The lamp had burned low. Wren was awake — she suspected he had been awake for days, rationing his sleep like a man rationing water in a boat — and he watched her come in without hope and without despair, which was worse than either.

"No," Sable said.

He nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd known all along, and began, with enormous care, to roll the map back up.

"*But*," she said, and his hands stopped.

She crouched down so they were level, dying man and running woman, and the alder smoke hung between them like a curtain in a theater before the play.

"I'll carry it to Anselm. I'll put it in her hands, and I'll say what you told me to say. That's all. I'm not your messenger angel, I'm not your conscience, and the day after I deliver it I'm gone — downriver, out of it, whatever comes after is yours and hers. And if the Company asks me, I found it in a dead man's baggage and never looked at it. You die before you can contradict me. That's the deal, and it's the only deal, and if you don't take it I'm flying out in an hour with my medicine runs and my clean hands."

Wren looked at her for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in his chest the water gathered and shifted.

"You're a hard woman, Delacroix."

"I'm a woman who's still breathing," she said. "It's a different thing."

He took her hand then — his grip still shockingly strong, the grip of a man who had pulled himself up canyon walls on hands like that — and pressed the oilcloth packet into it, and closed her fingers over it one by one, like a man burying something.

"Tell her the date," he whispered. "Tell her *finish it*. She'll know. She's got the same hand I do. She's been drawing maps her whole life and never once drawn the one that matters."

Sable stood, the packet in her fist, heavier than its weight. Outside the sky was going grey at the edges, the color of the river in the early season, and the Otter waited on the wet grass with her tail tied down, ready to carry Sable away from this place the way she had carried her away from every place.

She was halfway across the strip when Odile came running from the homestead, apron flying, and the look on the old woman's face told Sable everything before the words arrived.

"He's going," Odile said. "Now. You'd better come."

Sable stood on the strip with the map in her hand and the morning coming up cold behind the trees, and understood that the deal she had just struck had a clause neither of them had written — that the man would not, in fact, die quietly, and that what he left behind him was going to follow her all the way down the river.
