## 4. Port Anselm, Port of Lies

The tide brought the smell first — brine and diesel and something sweeter underneath, rotting fruit from the market stalls along the seawall. Ines Wren had lived in Port Anselm for six years, long enough to stop noticing it most days, but that morning the smell arrived with unusual insistence, as if the town itself were trying to tell her something before she was ready to hear it.

She was inking a contour line when the knock came.

Three raps, spaced evenly, the knock of someone who had decided to be there rather than drifted there. Ines set down her pen and looked at the map spread across her drafting table: the eastern tributaries of the Kestrel, rendered in her own careful hand, every bend surveyed, every confluence named. She had been working on this sheet for two years. It was, she liked to say, the most complete hydrographic record of the middle Kestrel ever drawn. It was also, though she never said this part aloud, the farthest thing from her father's last known position she could manage without leaving the country.

Elias Wren had written her eleven letters in twenty years. She kept them in a tin box under the window, and she had read them so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. He wrote about weather. He wrote about river levels. He wrote, once, about a bird he couldn't identify. He never wrote about what he was looking for, or why he had chosen the search over the daughter who had waited through her whole childhood for him to come home from one more expedition, just one more, until waiting curdled into something she refused to call grief while he was still alive to contradict it.

Now he was dead. The telegram had come nine days ago, four lines of official language from a coroner's office in a town she'd never heard of, and she had read it standing at this same table, and then she had sat down and finished the contour line, because the alternative was to put her hands over her face in front of an open window on a busy street.

The knock came again.

"Coming," she said, and did not move for another ten seconds, because ten seconds of silence was all the privacy Port Anselm ever gave anyone.

* * *

The woman on her doorstep was not what Ines expected, which was nothing, because Ines had not let herself expect anything at all.

She was small and wiry, sun-browned to the color of old rope, with a pilot's squint and the particular stillness of someone accustomed to being the only person for fifty miles. Her jacket had been mended more than once, competently but without vanity. She stood like the ground might shift under her.

"You're Ines Wren." Not quite a question.

"I'm the only Wren in Port Anselm."

"Sable Delacroix." The woman didn't offer a hand. Ines noticed that; people who didn't offer hands were either arrogant or carrying something. "I flew with your father. At the end."

The word *end* landed between them and neither picked it up.

"There's coffee," Ines said, because her mother had raised her to say that even to bill collectors, and stepped aside.

Sable Delacroix moved through the house the way cats move through unfamiliar rooms — noting exits, touching nothing. Ines watched her catalog the drafting table, the maps pinned to the walls, the tin box under the window, and felt the peculiar violation of being read by a stranger. Pilots, she supposed, learned to read terrain. This one was reading her.

"My condolences," Sable said finally, holding the coffee without drinking it. "He was—" She stopped. Started again. "I knew him eleven days."

"Eleven days." Ines heard her own voice go flat and cold and did nothing to warm it. "I had him for the first fourteen years of my life, and then I had his letters. You'll forgive me if I don't know what we're condoling."

Something flickered across the pilot's face — not hurt exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone who has walked into a room and found her own furniture.

"He talked about you," Sable said. "At the end. When he could still talk."

"What did he say?"

"That you were stubborn. That you drew better than he ever could." A pause. "That he was sorry."

Ines set her cup down harder than necessary. "Sorry is a word men like my father use instead of coming home. Did he send anything? A letter? Or did he just spend his last breath making promises to a stranger?"

Sable reached into her jacket slowly, the way you reach into a cage, and brought out a roll of oilcloth tied with waxed cord. She laid it on the table between them without unrolling it.

"He traded me this," she said, "for a promise. That I'd bring it to you myself. Not mail it. Not hand it off. Myself."

Ines looked at the oilcloth and did not touch it. "What is it?"

"A map."

"I'm a cartographer, Miss Delacroix. Everything is a map. What kind?"

"The kind that gets people killed."

* * *

Later — after the coffee had gone cold, after the light through the west window had turned the color of weak tea — Ines finally untied the cord and unrolled the oilcloth on her drafting table, smoothing it flat with both palms the way she smoothed every document, out of habit, out of reverence, out of the professional reflex that had carried her through the telegram and the funeral-that-wasn't and every other ceremony of absence her father's life had required.

And there it was: her father's handwriting.

She would have known it anywhere. The cramped, forward-leaning capitals. The way he crossed his sevens twice, a habit from some survey school she'd never identified. The little annotations in the margins — *water low here, August '09*, *good gravel bar, camp 3 nights* — written in the voice she knew better than any living person's voice, the voice that had narrated her childhood absences.

But the route itself was wrong. Wrong wasn't even the word. The route was *impossible*.

"This isn't the Kestrel," she said.

"It's the headwaters," Sable said from the doorway, where she'd stationed herself like a sentry. "Above the falls. Above everything."

"There are no mapped channels above the falls. The Company surveys stopped at the gorge in—" Ines caught herself, hating how quickly the fact came. "Decades ago. They declared the upper basin impassable and struck it from the charts. Every chart since repeats the declaration. Including mine." She said the last part quietly, because it was true and because saying it aloud felt like confessing to a smaller crime than her father's.

"So where does your map say the water comes from?"

Ines traced the inked line with one finger. Up through the gorge. Past the falls. Into a basin no chart acknowledged, drawn freehand with the confidence of a man who had been there — not once, but many times, if the marginal dates were honest. And at the top of the page, circled twice, a notation she read three times before its meaning settled:

*Source confirmed. They never looked because they already knew.*

"They already knew," she repeated. "Knew what?"

Sable said nothing. It was a specific kind of nothing, weighted, deliberate, and Ines had spent enough years around silences — her father's silences, arriving by post twice a year — to know when a silence was hiding something rather than merely containing it.

"You know," Ines said. "You've seen something. A paper, a ledger, something he showed you."

"I know what he told me," Sable said carefully. "And I know what happens to people who carry knowledge like this into towns with Company offices in them."

"Port Anselm has a Company office."

"I noticed."

"And yet here you are."

"Here I am." The pilot's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "I keep promises. It's the one luxury I afford."

Ines rolled the map back up, re-tied the cord, and held it against her chest like a child holds a stolen thing. Her father's handwriting. His impossible river. Twenty years of letters about weather and birds, and all the while this — whatever this was — sitting at the end of his pen.

"Why didn't he send it to me?" she asked, and hated the crack in her own voice. "Twenty years. He could have posted it any time. Why trade it to a stranger at the end?"

"Because posted things get intercepted," Sable said. "And because he wanted you to have a choice. His words." She drained the cold coffee at last, grimacing. "He said you'd want to go. And he said I should try to talk you out of it, and that you wouldn't listen, and that I shouldn't waste the breath."

"Did you waste the breath?"

"Not yet."

* * *

The Meridian Company office in Port Anselm occupied a corner building near the customs house, white-painted, shuttered against the afternoon glare, flying a flag too crisp for a town where every other flag had faded to pastel. Ines had walked past it perhaps a thousand times without seeing it, the way you stop seeing a scar.

She saw it now.

She saw it because of the man standing outside it at dusk, talking to the harbormaster's clerk with the easy, transactional warmth of a man collecting rather than conversing. He was tall, gray at the temples, dressed in field clothes too new to have ever been muddy. When Ines passed, his eyes followed her with a professional weightlessness — the look of a surveyor triangulating.

"Miss Wren?" He said it before she'd given a name. "Tomás Herrera, Meridian Company, resource division." He offered a card she didn't take. "Terrible news about your father. Elias Wren was known to us."

"Was he."

"A fine field man. Erratic toward the end, we understood. The mind goes, upriver. Sun, isolation." He smiled with exactly the right number of teeth. "We're conducting a routine review of his recent associations. Standard procedure when a licensed prospector dies outside Company oversight. You understand."

"I understand what a review is," Ines said. "I don't understand why it requires standing outside my street at dusk."

Herrera's smile didn't move, but something behind it recalibrated. "Your father held claims. Unresolved claims. The Company simply wants to close his file properly. If he left papers — maps, notes, correspondence — we'd be glad to see they're archived respectfully. For the historical record."

"For the historical record," Ines repeated.

"We're very thorough."

So was she. That was the whole of her profession. She thought of the map rolled inside her jacket, warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and made her face into the blank expression she used for difficult clients.

"If anything turns up," she said, "I'll know where to find you."

"We'll find you," Herrera said pleasantly, "either way."

* * *

That night she locked the door, which she had never done before, and unrolled the map under lamplight, and took down the tin box, and read her father's letters against it like a scholar cross-examining a witness.

*Water low here.* The letters mentioned drought years. The map's margins matched. *Good gravel bar, camp 3 nights* — and there, in a letter dated the same August, a throwaway line: *Camped three nights on the best bar I've seen in years. Wish you were here to draw it.* He had never once wished her upriver in fourteen years of letters. It had read, always, as indifference. Now it read as an invitation he hadn't dared repeat.

She worked until the lamp burned low, matching dates, matching hands, building the case that the man who wrote her about weather had spent two decades walking toward something he couldn't name in ink that might be read by the wrong eyes.

Near midnight she found it: a letter from nine years ago, ordinary in every respect except the third paragraph, where the handwriting changed — tightened, hurried, the double-crossed sevens collapsing into single strokes.

*Some things are better lost in a fire. Don't ask which things. If anyone from the Company ever asks you what I keep, the answer is nothing, and the answer is true, because what I keep isn't mine anymore. It belongs to the river now.*

Ines sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the tide turned against the seawall, and the whole town seemed to exhale.

Her father had been afraid. Not of the river — of being *read*. And now a stranger with clean boots was asking the harbor clerk about his papers, and a pilot who touched nothing had delivered a map that got people killed, and somewhere between those two facts lay the thing her father had died guarding.

A knock at the street door — soft, twice, then nothing.

Ines went cold. She blew out the lamp and stood in the dark with the map pressed to her chest, listening to footsteps retreat unhurriedly down the cobbles, and understood that Port Anselm had stopped being a port and become a net, and that she was already caught in it, and that the only question left was whether she would cut herself free or wait politely to be collected.

In the morning, she decided, she would find the pilot again. And this time, Ines Wren would be the one asking questions.
