## 5. Two Copies of Every Truth

Ines Wren had spent eleven years learning to read other people's handwriting. Surveyors' cramped annotations, colonial clerks' looping flourishes, the careful block letters of men who wanted their measurements to look more certain than they were. She could date a map by its ink, place a cartographer's mood by the pressure of his contour lines. So when she spread her father's map across the drafting table in the back room of the Port Anselm chart house — the room where she worked past midnight because it was cheaper than heating an apartment — she expected to know him in it at once.

She did not.

The hand was his. That was the terrible part. The same slightly hooked *t*, the same habit of letting his numerals lean eastward as if hurrying somewhere. But the route was a stranger's. Elias Wren had written her forty-one letters over twenty years, and every one of them had been full of small, honest geography: a bend he'd fished, a ridge he'd climbed, the color of the water after rain. He had never once mentioned the Kestrel headwaters. He had never once drawn anything that looked like this — this obsessive, crowded thing, the river rendered not as a line but as a wound, annotated in three colors of ink with soundings and bearings and little symbols she didn't recognize, symbols that weren't surveyor's marks at all but something older, something that looked almost like a private language.

She had been up since four. The lamp burned low and yellow, and outside the shutters the harbor worked its night sounds: hawsers creaking, the last fish boats coming in, a drunk singing two streets over with the confidence of a man who believed no one could hear him. Ines traced the river's course with one finger, from the delta all the way up through the canyons to the blank space at the top of the sheet where the headwaters should have been — and wasn't. Instead there was a circle, drawn and redrawn so many times the paper had thinned, and inside it, in her father's hand, smaller than everything else on the map, as if he'd been ashamed of it or afraid of it:

*It keeps what it keeps.*

"What does that mean?" she said aloud, to the empty room, to him, to nobody.

The letters were stacked beside her in their tin box, ordered by year the way she'd ordered them a hundred times before, back when ordering them had felt like a substitute for answering them. She hadn't answered half of them. That was another thing she was only now beginning to count as a debt. She pulled the earliest letter out — dated the spring twenty years ago, when he'd first gone upriver — and read it again with new eyes, the way you reread a letter after a funeral and find it full of things you'd walked past.

*I've taken work with a survey outfit,* he'd written. *Good pay, better than prospecting. They're mapping the upper Kestrel for the Meridian Company. Don't ask me what they're looking for because I'm not sure they know themselves.*

He'd been searching for twenty years. Not prospecting — searching. She saw it now in the drift of the correspondence, the way his locations wandered upstream year after year like a man following something, the way his tone changed around the word *Company*, tightening, going flat. In the early letters he complained about them cheerfully, the way workingmen complain. By the middle years the complaints had stopped, and in their place was a new vocabulary: *I keep my own notes now. I keep them where I keep them.* And once, chillingly, in a letter she'd always taken for a joke:

*If anything happens to me, burn nothing. Burning is how they win.*

Ines sat very still.

Then she got up, went to the corner cabinet where she kept her reference folios, and took down the box of her father's papers she'd inherited along with the grief — the ones the lawyer had sent, the ones that had survived whatever fire had taken the rest. Because that was the detail she couldn't stop circling. When Elias Wren's effects had come downriver to her, they'd come in two crates, and both crates smelled of smoke. The lawyer had said a stove accident, a careless night, the kind of thing that happened to men who lived alone in the bush. Half his journals gone. Half his field notes gone. Everything from the last six years gone, except —

Except the map, which Sable Delacroix had carried out of the bush in a leather tube, unburned, unsmoked, smelling of kerosene and altitude.

Someone had burned his records. Someone had burned them *recently* — the lawyer's crates had arrived eight months ago, and the char smell had still been live in the paper, sharp enough to make her sneeze. A stove accident didn't explain why the surviving pages all predated the most interesting years. A stove accident didn't explain why the burn lines stopped clean at the edge of a page, as if the fire had been fed selectively, by hand, by someone choosing.

Ines laid the surviving fragments on the table beside the map and began, piece by piece, to build the thing her father had died building: a picture of the upper Kestrel. It took her until the gray light came up over the harbor and the gulls started their argument with the morning. What emerged was not a prospector's map. It was a case file disguised as one. Soundings that mapped not depth but access. Bearings that converged not on gold but on a basin above a drowned valley. And in the margins, in that same small, frightened hand, dates. Dates going back further than her father's time on the river. Dates from before she was born.

Beside one of them, just one, he had written a single word: *Massacre.*

The lamp guttered. Ines realized her hands were shaking, and made them stop, the way her mother had taught her to make them stop — breath, pressure, breath — because shaking hands ruined linework and she would need her linework for whatever came next.

* * *

Sable Delacroix was staying at the Anchorage Rooming House, which told Ines something already: it was the cheapest bed in Port Anselm and the pilot could clearly afford better, which meant she chose cheapness the way some people chose haircuts, as a statement. Ines found her in the narrow yard behind the house, under a straggling guava tree, doing something incomprehensible to the engine cowling of a small generator while the landlady's dog supervised from a respectful distance.

"You," Ines said.

Delacroix looked up. Up close, without the cockpit between them, she was younger than her reputation and more tired than her face let on, with grease to the second knuckle and eyes that did the arithmetic of a stranger's approach before the stranger finished walking. "You're the daughter."

"I'm the cartographer." Ines held up the map, rolled. "And you're going to tell me where you got this."

"From your father."

"That's not what I asked." She unrolled it on the flagstones, weighted the corners with four stones from the yard's border, and stood over it. "I asked where he got *this*. Because this isn't a prospector's map. This is twenty years of systematic documentation of the upper Kestrel, and the last six years of his notes are missing from his effects, and the ones that survived smell like someone set them on fire on purpose. So either you know something about that, or you know who does."

For a long moment Delacroix said nothing. She wiped her hands slowly on a rag, taking far longer than the job required, and Ines understood she was being given time to be lied to, and that the woman was deciding whether to take it.

"There was a ledger page," Delacroix said finally. "In his kit. Company stationery. Numbers, shipments, dates going back decades. I kept it."

"Show me."

"No."

"No?"

"Not yet." The pilot crouched beside the map, studying it upside down, which Ines might have found insulting if she hadn't noticed the woman was reading it fluently. "Not until I know what you'll do with it. Your father made me promise to bring it to you. He didn't make me promise to hand ammunition to whoever asks nicely."

"Whoever asks—" Ines laughed, and it came out ugly. "There was a man at the chart house yesterday. Neat suit, neat boots, boots too clean for this town. Said he was with Meridian's survey division, said they were updating regional charts, asked whether I'd ever done contract work on the upper Kestrel. Asked it twice, different ways, like he was checking my answers against each other." She watched Delacroix's face do something small and controlled. "You knew. You've known since you landed."

"I suspected."

"Suspected *what*? Say it plainly. I've spent my whole life around people who talk in contours. Give me the elevation."

Delacroix stood. She looked out past the yard wall toward the harbor, where a Meridian barge sat at the company berth with its cranes folded like sleeping insects, and when she spoke her voice had gone quiet in a way that made the dog lift its head.

"The Company doesn't spend money chasing maps, Ms. Wren. Maps are cheap. Paper is cheap. What they spend money on is making sure no one holds a piece of paper they can't buy or burn. Your father kept records for twenty years. His records burned. Do you think that was a stove?"

"I think you're telling me a story instead of answering me."

"I'm telling you the shape of the answer." Delacroix turned back to her, and there it was again, that flicker behind the eyes, some door opening a crack onto a lit room and then shutting. "People who carry documents the Company wants destroyed don't die of stove accidents. They die of weather. Of rivers. Of bandits, of bad fuel, of falling asleep drunk near a fire. The Company has never once in its history killed anyone, officially. It just has extraordinary luck."

"And you know this how?"

The question landed harder than Ines had intended, or perhaps exactly as hard as she'd intended, and she watched it strike. Delacroix's jaw tightened. Her hands, hanging loose at her sides, curled once and flattened.

"I fly into places other people won't," she said. "You hear things."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you get today."

They stood facing each other over the map, over the dead man's thinning paper and his frightened marginalia, and Ines felt something shift in her chest that she recognized with a kind of horror, because she had felt it before, in herself, in the mirror, on the nights when the lies she maintained about her own life — that she'd been too busy to write, that she didn't miss him, that the silence between them had been his choice and not hers — pressed too close to the surface. It was the look of a person standing on a floor they knew was hollow.

"You're lying to me," Ines said. "But you're bad at it, which means you're not practiced at lying to people who matter. Which means I matter, or the map does, or he did." She crouched and touched the circle at the top of the river, the worn-through place where her father had redrawn the headwaters until the paper gave out. "So here's what happens now. I'm going upstream. I'm going to find what he found, or what's left of it, and I'm going to put it on paper where no fire reaches it — a hundred copies, a thousand, in every chart house from here to the coast. You can help me or you can fly away, which is what you're good at. But if you fly away, understand that I will find out what you're hiding, and I will decide then whether it makes you a coward or something worse."

Something moved through Delacroix's expression then — not anger, not quite fear. Recognition. The look of one liar meeting another across a table and finding the furniture familiar.

"You really loved him," the pilot said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't kind; it was diagnostic.

"I really failed him," Ines said. "Which is stronger."

Delacroix looked at the map a long time. The guava tree dropped a leaf onto the delta. Somewhere beyond the wall, the town was waking up, carts and gulls and the first bell of the cannery, all of it ordinary, all of it about to become the world they were leaving.

"There's a bend sixty miles upriver," Delacroix said at last, "where the water's still deep enough to float a boat for maybe two more weeks. After that the dry season closes the river and nothing goes up or comes down until the rains." She straightened, and the shuttered look came back over her face, but it came back late, and they both knew it. "I'll take you as far as the bend. That's not a yes to anything else."

"It's a yes to everything else," Ines said. "You just haven't admitted it yet."

She rolled up the map, and as she did, her thumb passed over the margin note — *it keeps what it keeps* — and she thought of the burned crates, the selective fire, the neat-suited man asking his question twice. Her father had spent twenty years keeping a copy of the truth hidden where only the river could guard it, and someone had spent six months trying to make sure the only copy left was the one in a dead man's handwriting.

Two copies of every truth, she thought. That was the rule now. Whatever they found at the source, it would exist twice before it existed anywhere else — once in the tin, once in her hand, drawn fair and sent wide.

Behind her, she heard Sable Delacroix pick up a stone and put it, deliberately, on the fifth corner of the map, holding it flat against the wind that had started to rise off the harbor.

Neither of them remarked on it. Neither of them needed to.

But that evening, when Ines walked past the Anchorage on her way home, she saw the landlady's boy running an errand for the pilot — a telegram form, folded twice. And she saw, through the window of the Meridian office across the street, the neat-suited surveyor reading an identical form, unfolded, smiling at whatever it said.

Someone was reporting on them already. And Ines Wren, who had spent her life believing that paper was safer than people, began — for the first time — to be afraid of what hers might say.
