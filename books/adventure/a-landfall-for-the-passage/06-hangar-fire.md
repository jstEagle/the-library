## 6. Hangar Fire

The smell woke her before the light did.

Sable had slept in the Otter's shadow for ten years — in hammocks slung between struts, on cargo blankets spread across concrete, in the crook of the fuselage where the aluminum held the day's heat like a living thing. She knew the plane's smells the way other people knew their mothers': avgas and hydraulic fluid, canvas and old coffee, the green bite of river mud tracked in on her boots. So when a wrongness threaded through the dark of the hangar at Halfway Bend — acrid, chemical, sweet as burnt sugar — her body understood it before her mind could name it.

She came up off the blanket with her heart already hammering.

Smoke. Not engine smoke, not the blue haze of a cold morning start. This was black and oily, rolling along the hangar's ceiling in a slow tide, and beneath it, at the far corner where Ines had spread her father's papers out on a workbench two days ago, something was burning with a low, greedy crackle.

"Ines!" Her voice cracked against the tin roof.

A shape lurched upright from the cot by the office door — Ines, hair wild, eyes still fogged with sleep — and then the fog burned off her all at once. "The map—"

Sable was already moving. The fire hadn't reached the center of the hangar yet; it was eating its way along a spill of accelerant someone had poured in a neat, deliberate line from the fuel drums toward the workbench, patient as a fuse. Whoever set it had wanted time to leave. Had wanted the fire to find the papers slowly, thoroughly, while the whole settlement slept.

She grabbed the oilskin packet off the bench — Wren's map inside, and the ledger page she'd never told Ines about, both wrapped in the waxed cloth she'd carried through ten years of storms — and shoved it inside her jacket, against her chest, where her heartbeat could feel it. Then she turned for the extinguisher mounted by the office door and found Ines already there, hauling it down, tearing the pin free with her teeth.

They fought it together in the roaring half-dark, white foam hissing into black smoke, Sable dragging the second extinguisher from the Otter's emergency kit when the first ran dry. The fire gave ground grudgingly, chewing through a crate of spare parts, licking up one wall stud before it finally lay down and died, leaving the hangar gutted at its edges and stinking like a slaughterhouse of plastic and paint.

By the time the settlement's volunteer crew arrived with buckets and a hand pump, there was nothing left to save or lose. Marta Ochoa took one look at the accelerant line — still visible, a dark ribbon scorched into the concrete — and said nothing at all, which in Halfway Bend was a kind of testimony.

* * *

The fire marshal came up from Port Anselm on the afternoon boat, a soft-bellied man named Ferro who wore his badge like a costume he'd been handed five minutes before the play began. He walked the hangar with his hands clasped behind his back, humming, pausing over the melted junction box near the office door with the satisfaction of a man finding exactly what he'd been sent to find.

"Electrical," he announced, writing it in a little book with a little pencil. "Faulty wiring. Old building. Happens all the time out here."

Sable stood in the doorway with her arms crossed and let the silence do her talking.

Ferro glanced up, caught her expression, and had the grace to look faintly uncomfortable. "You disagree, Ms. Delacroix?"

"I disagree that wiring pours itself in a straight line across a floor." She nodded toward the scorch ribbon. "I disagree that faulty wiring starts at the far end of a building from the junction box and burns toward the papers. And I disagree that you drove four hours upriver to tell me a story you'd have charged me money to hear in Port Anselm."

The marshal's pencil stopped moving. When he looked at her again, the affability had drained out of his face, and what remained underneath was tired and careful and afraid. "Ms. Delacroix," he said quietly, "I have a report to file. The Company reads every report filed in this district. I'd advise you — as a friend of nobody, just as advice — to file your insurance claim, fix your hangar, and think hard about how much river is left between here and the headwaters this time of year."

He closed his book. He tipped his hat to Ines, who stared at him with a flatness that would have frozen a less frightened man, and he got back on his boat and went away downriver, trailing diesel smoke, a small man carrying a large lie like a stone in his pocket.

Marta watched the boat go, then turned to Sable. "Electrical," she repeated, and spat into the dust.

* * *

They sat that night in the gutted hangar because neither of them suggested anywhere else. Marta had rigged two lanterns; the Otter stood in their glow with a scorch mark high on her flank where a burning wall stud had fallen against her, a wound Sable kept touching the way you touch your own bruise to confirm it's real.

Ines had the map spread open on the one workbench that survived, weighted at the corners with wrenches. Her father's handwriting crawled across it — elevations, soundings, a route drawn in a hand that shook only near the source, as if even the pen had been afraid. She'd been staring at it for an hour without speaking.

"They didn't want the plane," she said finally. "Or they'd have burned it first. They wanted the paper."

"Paper burns faster than aluminum."

"No." Ines looked up. "They wanted the paper gone and the plane alive. A dead plane grounds you. A dead map ends the conversation. They're not trying to stop us from traveling, Sable. They're trying to stop the traveling from meaning anything." She tapped the ledger page, which lay beside the map now — Sable had finally shown her, two nights ago, in a confession that had cost them both something. "My father carried this for twenty years. Twenty years, and the week I hold it in my hands, someone tries to burn it out of existence. That's not coincidence. That's arithmetic."

Sable said nothing. Outside, the river muttered over its bars, lower than it had been a week ago, lower than it should be. The dry season was closing its hand finger by finger, and every night the Kestrel dropped another few centimeters, and every night the window at the top of the river narrowed like a pupil contracting against light.

"I'm coming upstream," Ines said.

It wasn't a question, but Sable answered it anyway. "No."

"Sable—"

"You saw what happened tonight. You saw what a *report* does, what a man like Ferro does. That was them being polite. That was a knock on the door." Sable stood, and the lantern light made her shadow enormous against the smoke-blackened wall. "Upstream is canyons and gravel bars and a guide who'll take our money and vanish at the first rapids. Upstream is no radio, no doctor, no one who knows where we are except the people we don't want knowing. I've flown that river alone for ten years because alone is the only way I can keep people safe from me. You are not getting on that boat."

Ines rose too, slowly, and Sable saw for the first time how much of her father lived in her face — the same stubborn architecture around the jaw, the same eyes that had looked at mountains for twenty years and refused to see anything but the way through.

"My father died alone on a strip only you service," Ines said, "because everyone else had forgotten he existed. He spent twenty years carrying something true through country that wanted it buried, and he died with it unfinished, and the last thing he did on this earth was trust a stranger to finish it." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "You think I'm asking permission. I'm telling you how it's going to be. You know the river. I know the map. Neither of us knows enough alone, and both of us know what happens to people who carry things alone."

"The man who set that fire will be waiting upstream."

"Then we'll arrive faster than he expects, or later than he hopes, or in a direction he hasn't thought to watch." Ines folded the map along its old creases with hands that were perfectly steady. "You can say no all night, Sable. I'll be on the morning boat to the last bend either way. The only question is whether the person flying me there has ever once in her life done the smart thing instead of the right one."

The lanterns hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, a night bird called across the water, three notes falling, like a question asked twice and abandoned.

Sable looked at the Otter — her scarred, faithful, long-suffering Otter, with its fresh burn scar and its tanks full and its engines begging for the kind of trouble she'd sworn off giving them. She looked at the map, at the route threading north through country that ate airplanes and pilots alike. She looked at Ines, standing in the wreckage of a fire meant for her, holding her father's ghost in waxed cloth and refusing, absolutely refusing, to put it down.

And Sable felt the thing she'd been outrunning for a decade shift inside her chest — fear turning over, hardening, setting like cement into something with weight and edges. Resolve. It didn't feel like courage. It felt like a decision made by someone too tired to be afraid anymore.

"One condition," she said.

Ines waited.

"We stop at Port Anselm first. There's a pilot there — Callum Reyes. He's a snake, and he'll charge us double, and I trust him further than I can throw his Cessna. But he knows the upper Kestrel better than any man alive, and if the Company comes upstream after us, I want to know whose side he's really on before they make him choose." She picked up the oilskin packet and held it out. "You carry the map from here on. If they take me, they get nothing. If they take you—"

"They won't."

"That's not a condition, that's a hope. Carry the map."

Ines took it. Their hands overlapped on the oilskin for a moment, and Sable felt the tremor in the younger woman's fingers that she'd been hiding all evening — grief and fury and terror, all of it banked down to a single steady flame.

"Marta's boys will have the hangar patched by the time we're back," Sable said, turning to the Otter, running her hand along the scorched flank like an apology. "Pre-dawn departure. We beat the heat, and we beat anyone watching the strip — they'll expect us to run at noon like guilty people."

"And if they're watching at dawn?"

Sable swung herself up into the cockpit and settled into the seat that fit her body the way a saddle fits a rider, and for the first time in ten years the answer came without running attached to it.

"Then they'll see exactly where we're going," she said, "and they'll learn something the Meridian Company seems to have forgotten."

"What's that?"

Sable hit the master switch, and the panel lights bloomed green in the dark hangar like a field of small stubborn stars.

"Some rivers," she said, "you can't burn out of the world. You can only follow them to the source."

Outside, far downstream, a boat engine started in the black water and headed for Port Anselm, carrying a marshal's false report to whoever had paid for it. And above Halfway Bend, the dry-season stars wheeled on, indifferent and bright, counting down the days until the Kestrel ran too shallow for anything but truth.
