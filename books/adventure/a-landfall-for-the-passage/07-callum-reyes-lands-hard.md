## 7. Callum Reyes Lands Hard

The Meridian Company kept its offices the way it kept its ledgers: cool, orderly, and smelling faintly of money that had never been outside. Callum Reyes noticed these things because he'd spent eleven years noticing what people tried to hide, and because the air conditioning in Port Anselm's only office tower was a luxury he associated with men who signed things.

He sat across from a man named Voss — not the surveyor himself, but a man who *represented* the surveyor's interests, which in Callum's experience meant he did the surveyor's dirty work and got none of the credit. Voss had a face like an unsigned contract. He slid a folder across the desk, and Callum felt the weight of it before he touched it. Paper was heavy when it mattered.

"Reyes," Voss said. "You've flown the Kestrel corridor for how long?"

"Nine years."

"And Delacroix?"

"Eight." He let that sit. "She flies circles around half the pilots on this coast and she does it in an aircraft older than both of us put together."

Voss smiled without warmth. "Which is precisely why we're interested in you and not her. We need someone reliable. Someone who understands that certain routes are better left unflown." He tapped the folder. "Exclusive charter rights to the upper settlements. Halfway Bend, Copper Landing, all the strips Delacroix services now. Company rates. Steady fuel contracts. No more bidding against yourself for scrap runs."

Callum looked at the folder and didn't open it. Not yet. Ambition is a strange animal; it sits very still in your chest until you feed it, and then it won't stop eating. He'd wanted this for years — not the Company's money specifically, but the *certainty*. Sable Delacroix flew on nerve and reputation and whatever gods watched over stubborn women, and every season he told himself he'd build something steadier than that, something with contracts and hangar space and a name that didn't make people say *oh, *that* Reyes* in that particular tone.

"You want me to take her routes," he said.

"We want the routes serviced. How that happens is your affair." Voss folded his hands. "There is one additional matter. A map has surfaced. Drawn by a prospector named Elias Wren. It concerns the Kestrel headwaters. If it comes through your hands — or if you learn where it's gone — the Company would consider that a professional courtesy worth remembering."

There it was. Callum kept his face still, the way he kept a plane still in crosswind: small corrections, nothing visible. *Wren.* The name went through him like a downdraft.

"Elias Wren," he said carefully, "is a rumor. Half the old-timers swear he found gold up there. Other half swear he died ten years ago."

"People say many things." Voss stood, signaling the meeting was over. "Think about our offer, Mr. Reyes. But think quickly. The dry season waits for no one, and neither do we."

* * *

He walked out into the heat of Port Anselm's waterfront and the smell of diesel and fish hit him like a wall he walked through every day of his life. His Cessna was tied down at the municipal strip, and he sat in its shade under the wing for a long time, the folder unopened on his knee, watching the river traffic crawl past.

Elias Wren.

Seven years ago, over the Kestrel rapids, Callum's engine had coughed twice and quit at nine hundred feet. He remembered it now with the terrible clarity of memory that only visits when it wants something from you: the sudden silence, the way the rapids below turned white and patient, the load of medical supplies shifting behind him as he fought for airspeed. He'd been twenty-six and certain he was going to die in the water, and then a voice had come through on the frequency — a voice he didn't know, calm as Sunday — talking him through the restart procedure, priming, mixture, the fuel line vapor lock that came with summer loads out of low strips. *Pump it three times, no more. Now leave it alone and let her catch.*

The engine caught at four hundred feet. Callum had cried, briefly and privately, and then flown home and never learned the voice's name until months later, when a bartender mentioned that Elias Wren, the prospector nobody believed in, used to sit at the end of the bar with a radio headset and talk lost pilots back to earth because nobody else would listen on that frequency anymore.

Wren had saved his life with his voice and never asked for anything. Never even introduced himself properly. And now Callum was sitting under his own wing holding a folder that smelled like money, being asked to hunt down Wren's last possession.

He opened the folder. Route schedules. Fuel contracts. And there, clipped inside, a photostat of a page — a Company ledger, decades old, with entries he didn't fully understand but understood enough. Allocations. Disbursements. A line item marked only *K.S. — final settlement,* dated the year before Callum was born. Something about the way the numbers were rounded made his skin prickle. You didn't round numbers like that unless you were making something disappear.

And in the margin, in pencil, someone had written: *Wren — monitoring continues.*

Monitoring. For twenty years. For a prospector with a mule and a radio.

Callum closed the folder. Out on the water, a barge horn sounded, long and low, and he thought about how a debt doesn't expire just because the man you owe it to can't collect. It just changes hands. It waits.

* * *

Sable Delacroix landed at the municipal strip two days later, and Callum was there, because he made sure he was there, leaning against his strut with the studied carelessness of a man who had rehearsed looking casual.

"Sable."

"Reyes." She climbed down from the Otter's strut and gave him the once-over she always did, cataloguing his aircraft, his boots, his intentions. "You're a long way from your usual haunts."

"Heard there was work in town." He shrugged. "Charter season's opening up."

"I heard the Company's been asking questions." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light. They never were. That was the thing about Sable Delacroix — she flew like a woman with somewhere to be and looked at you like a woman keeping count. "Pointed ones. About maps."

"Maps are my business. I draw them in my sleep."

"Not that kind." She shouldered past him toward the fuel office, then stopped. Turned. "Whatever they're offering you, Callum — and they're offering you something, or you wouldn't have shaved — ask them what happened to the last pilot who took their money."

"There wasn't a last pilot."

"No," she agreed. "That's rather the point, isn't it?" And she walked off toward the office, leaving him standing in the jet wash of her certainty.

That night he reported to Voss anyway. That was the part he'd turn over and over later, unable to forgive: that he went. He sat in the cool office and said, "Delacroix received a visitor recently. A prospector. Old man, sick. She flew him out to the charity ward." He paused. "I don't know if there was a map. But if there was, she'll move on it fast, and she won't fly the main corridors."

Voss wrote it down. "Good. Anything else?"

Callum thought of the ledger page. Thought of *monitoring continues.* "No," he said. "Nothing else."

Walking back to the strip in the dark, he passed the harbor lights and felt the debt settle onto his shoulders like ballast — Wren's voice in his earphones at four hundred feet, *leave it alone and let her catch* — and understood, with the clarity that comes too late to be useful, that he had just sold information about the man who saved his life, and about the woman who might be carrying that man's last wish, to the people who had been hunting that man for twenty years.

He stopped at the edge of the strip. His Cessna sat dark against the stars. Somewhere north, up a river already shrinking toward its bones, Sable Delacroix was probably loading fuel drums by lantern light, and Ines Wren — the daughter, the cartographer, the one whose grief had teeth — was probably drawing lines on paper that the Company would burn towns to erase.

*Let her catch,* Wren had said. About an engine. About a young pilot falling out of the sky.

Callum stood in the dark a long time. Then he went to his plane, opened the cowling with a mechanic's tenderness, and began, quietly, to think about fuel lines.
