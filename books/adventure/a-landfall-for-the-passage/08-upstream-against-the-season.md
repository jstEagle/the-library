## 8. Upstream Against the Season

The argument ended the way most arguments end in a hangar: with someone turning their back and starting an engine.

"You're not flying me up there," I said, hand flat on the Otter's cowling as if I could hold the whole conversation still by touching metal. "The Kestrel's dropping. You can see it from the air — the bars are showing their teeth already."

"Then we'd better hurry," Ines said.

She stood in the hangar doorway with her father's map rolled in a tin tube slung across her back like a quiver, and she had the particular stillness of people who have decided something and are merely waiting for the world to catch up. Smoke from the burned hangar still ghosted in her hair. Three days since the fire marshal had pronounced his verdict — *electrical, regrettable* — and neither of us had said aloud what we both knew: electricity doesn't smell like kerosene poured through a broken window at two in the morning.

"Ines." I made my voice the one I used for passengers who wanted to land on gravel that wouldn't hold them. "I've flown this country eleven years. The dry season isn't weather here. It's a door closing. Every tributary off the Kestrel goes to a trickle by the end of the month. The main channel drops four meters at the bend. There are rapids up there that don't exist on any chart because nobody who ran them lived to draw them."

"Then nobody will be following us," she said.

I hated her a little for that. Hated how neatly she'd turned my own caution into our advantage, the way her father must have turned arguments in his day, some stubborn gene passed down like eye color.

"I said no."

"And I heard you." She came closer, boots crunching on scorched glass. "You also said you'd carry the map to me, and then you said you'd never go upstream, and then you sat up all night reading Elias's journals with your jaw set like a woman chewing on something too big to swallow. So forgive me if I've stopped taking your first answer as your last one."

* * *

The truth was I'd been outvoted days ago and everyone knew it but me.

It happened at Halfway Bend, over fish stew in Marta Okonkwo's kitchen, the same table where I'd eaten a hundred meals without ever once staying past the season. Marta ladled second helpings nobody asked for and listened while Ines laid out the route — the last navigable bend above the canyon country, the old trading camp at Two Rivers where boats could still be hired, the headwaters beyond.

"The Company burned a hangar," Ines finished. "They'll do worse. My father spent twenty years documenting what they did at the source. If they find those journals before we do—"

"They won't need to find them," Marta said quietly. "They'll burn the riverbank down to bedrock looking." She set her spoon down and looked at me across the steam. "Sable. How many times have I asked you to stay for the winter festival?"

"Marta—"

"Eleven years. Eleven festivals." Her eyes were wet and furious at once. "And every year you're wheels-up before the drums start, because staying means being somewhere when it changes you. Well, girl. This is the change. It's standing right here asking you to stay, and this time it isn't asking politely."

The stew went cold in front of me. Outside, the river muttered against its banks, lower than it had been a week ago, exposing a shelf of red clay like a wound.

"I fly them up," I said finally, "and that's all. To the bend. Then I come back and wait."

Nobody believed me. Not even me.

* * *

We flew out at first light, before the heat built thermals tall enough to shake the wings, and the Kestrel unspooled beneath us like something dying slowly and beautifully at once.

From altitude the river was still silver, still generous. But I'd learned to read water the way other pilots read clouds, and what I saw below turned my stomach cold. Sandbars that had been drowned in June now broke the surface in long yellow crescents. Side channels that should have been running deep showed only wet mud gleaming like solder. At the confluence near Two Rivers, the water braided into a dozen thin threads, each one arguing with the others about which was the real river.

"There," Ines said beside me, pointing at nothing and everything. She had the chart on her knees, her father's map beside it, and she kept touching the junction of the two like a woman checking a pulse.

The last navigable bend was a half-moon of gray shingle where the valley pinched into the first canyon walls. I put the Otter down on the gravel strip Elias Wren had described in a letter twenty years old — and which, miraculously, was still there, because gravel doesn't rot the way piers do — and killed the engine into a silence so complete I could hear the river working its stones a quarter mile off.

Ines was out of her harness before the prop stopped.

"Sable." I caught her arm at the door. "Last chance to hear sense. I take you back to Port Anselm tonight, we let the authorities—"

"The authorities have a Meridian surveyor buying their drinks," she said. "You told me that yourself."

"I did." I looked out at the canyon mouth downstream — upstream, rather; everything that mattered now was upstream — where the rock walls rose sheer and rust-colored, and the river entered them like thread entering a needle's eye. Somewhere past that throat, in country no chart admitted to, was a spring-fed basin and a drowned valley and whatever my hands had been carrying toward for three weeks without admitting it.

I let go of her arm.

"Then we hire a boat," I said, "and we move fast, and when I say the season's beaten us, we turn around. Agreed?"

"Agreed," she said, in the tone of a person agreeing to weather.

* * *

Two Rivers wasn't a town anymore. It was a memory with three buildings attached — a collapsed trading post, a chapel with a roof like a broken wing, and a stilt house where a man named Aurelio Baptiste sold passage upstream and lies downstream, depending on the season.

He was mending a net on his porch when we found him, a small wiry man with forearms like cable and eyes the color of the river in flood. He listened to Ines describe the route with his head cocked, and I watched him do arithmetic behind his face — distance against water level, water level against calendar — and arrive at a number he didn't like.

"The Kestrel is low," he said. "Lowest I've seen in nine years. Above the first canyon there are channels that were safe in March that will gut a boat today."

"We'll pay well," Ines said.

"Money doesn't float." But he looked at us a long moment — at the tin tube on Ines's back, at the way I kept glancing at the sky, at whatever he read in the pair of us together — and something shifted. "Wren," he said suddenly. "Elias Wren. You're his girl."

Ines went very still. "You knew him?"

"He bought passage from me twice. Twenty years apart, almost to the week." Aurelio set down his net. "Both times he asked me to take him past the second canyon. Both times I refused. Both times he paid me anyway, for the part I would do, and walked the rest." He rose, joints cracking like green wood. "I always thought I'd see him a third time. Come. I have a boat that owes me nothing and a nephew who owes me everything."

* * *

We left the next dawn in a long shallow aluminum skiff with a forty-horse motor and a nephew named Tomás who handled it like an extension of his own nervous system. The first hours were almost easy — wide water, morning mist burning off the surface, kingfishers stitching blue along the banks. Ines sat in the bow with the map open across her knees, calling out features as they matched her father's ink: a leaning fig tree, a shoal marked with three careful crosses, a cliff face he'd annotated in a cramped hand — *eagle nests, left bank, good landmark, visible from far.*

She touched those annotations like braille. I pretended not to watch.

By noon the valley began to close. The walls drew in, the light went from gold to copper, and the river itself changed its voice — from a broad murmur to a tighter, faster mutter, the sound of water being squeezed. Tomás slowed the motor and began reading the current ahead the way I read weather: lips moving, eyes flicking, weight shifting before every correction.

"She drops fast here," he called back. "My uncle says run the left side until the split rock, then cross hard."

The split rock appeared like a verdict — a house-sized boulder midstream, brown water peeling around it in two unequal arms. Tomás crossed us hard to the right channel and immediately I understood why: the left arm terminated in a chute barely wider than the skiff, white and roaring, dropping into foam we couldn't see the bottom of.

But the right channel was narrowing too. I could see the gravel rising under the surface, a pale tongue reaching up toward the hull, and the sound the water made over it was wrong — a hiss instead of a hum.

"Tomás." I leaned forward. "That bar ahead. How deep?"

His answer was to gun the motor and swing wide, and even so the keel kissed bottom once, a grinding shudder through the whole boat, and Ines grabbed the gunwale and my heart climbed into my throat and then we were past, sliding into deeper water, and Tomás laughed the high laugh of a man who has cheated death so often it owes him money.

"That," he said, "was yesterday's easy water."

* * *

The first canyon took the rest of the afternoon. The walls rose until we ran in our own shadow, the sky a ribbon of violet overhead, and the river dropped through a series of steps that Tomás ran with total concentration and visible joy — ferrying across currents, threading gaps I'd have sworn were narrower than the boat, the spray coming aboard cold as confession.

Ines whooped once, actually whooped, when we cleared the third drop, and for a moment she was just a young woman in a fast boat with her hair whipping, and I felt something in my chest loosen that I hadn't known was clenched.

Then we rounded a bend and Tomás cut the motor.

Ahead, the canyon narrowed to perhaps thirty meters, and the entire width of it was rapids — not a chute, not a line, but a chaos of exposed rock and collapsing water, boulders standing in the streambed like the vertebrae of something enormous, the river tearing itself to lace around them. And between the boulders, everywhere, the pale gleam of gravel just under the surface.

"No," Tomás said simply.

"It's marked on the map," Ines said, unrolling it with shaking hands. "My father portaged here — look, he drew a trail, right side, there's a trail—"

"A trail from twenty years ago, in jungle, in the wet season's aftermath?" Tomás shook his head. "Even in flood water I might run this. Maybe. With a prayer. But this—" he gestured at the dry-season skeleton showing through the current "—this is not a rapid anymore. It is a staircase with teeth. I am sorry. My uncle's boat, my uncle's rules. No."

"Tomás," I said, and pulled the roll of bills from inside my jacket — the emergency money, the get-out-of-anywhere money I'd carried for eleven years and never spent, because spending it meant committing to something. "Whatever your uncle's rules are, this is what breaking them costs."

He looked at the money. He looked at the rapids. He looked at me, and his face did something complicated — regret and apology and a kind of pity all at once.

"It isn't the money." He said it gently, which was worse. "If I wreck the boat, my uncle loses the only thing that keeps him fed. If I die, my mother—" He spread his hands. "You are asking me to stop being a guide and become a martyr. I am not built for it."

He turned the skiff in the eddy below the rapids, and the current helped him, eager to be rid of us, and I understood with terrible clarity that the river had just made its own argument and we had lost.

* * *

He put us ashore on a gravel bar at the foot of the canyon wall, where an old landing — rotted pilings, a rusted winch, the ghost of Elias Wren's world — still clung to the bank. He unloaded our packs without meeting our eyes. He left us a jerrican of fuel he didn't have to spare and half his food, which he pretended was an accident of packing.

"The trail your father drew," he said at last, to Ines. "Right side. High. If it exists at all, it exists up there, above the flood line." He pointed at a seam of lighter stone running along the canyon wall like a scar. "Walk the scar. Don't walk the water." He hesitated, then dug in his shirt pocket and pressed something into Ines's palm: a battered compass on a lanyard, brass worn bright by decades of thumbs. "Wren left this with me on his last trip. Said if anyone ever came asking after his river, they'd need it more than I did." His voice roughened. "I kept it nine years waiting for someone to ask."

Ines closed her fingers around it. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." He pushed the skiff off the gravel, swung aboard, and looked back once, his face unreadable in the failing light. "The dry season closes in six days. After that, no boat comes up, no plane lands on that strip, no one leaves this canyon until the rains. Whatever you decide in the next six days, decide it knowing the river has already voted."

Then the motor caught, and he was gone downstream, and the sound of him faded until there was nothing left in the world but water talking to stone and the two of us standing on a gravel bar at the edge of the mapped universe.

* * *

We didn't speak for a long time.

The light drained out of the canyon in stages — copper, then ash, then a bruised purple — and the temperature fell with it, and somewhere upstream a bird cried three notes that echoed off the walls like a question repeated to an empty room. Ines sat on her pack with the compass open in her lap, and I stood at the water's edge watching the current slide past, carrying leaves and moths and one abandoned feather steadily away from us, toward the sea, toward everything easy.

Here was the arithmetic, plain as the gravel under my boots. Behind us: a plane on a strip that would be unreachable within the week, a burned hangar, a Company that knew we were coming because we'd made noise in every port between here and the coast. Ahead of us: a canyon with no road, a trail that existed only in a dead man's ink, six days of walking, and whatever waited at the source — evidence, or nothing, or men with guns.

Every instinct I had honed in eleven years of leaving said *turn around*. Say the season beat you. Fly home. Be gone before anything could hold you, before anyone could ask you to stay, before the world could make you responsible for how it ends.

Marta's voice, in my head, patient and merciless: *This time it isn't asking politely.*

"Sable." Ines hadn't moved. The compass lay in her palm, its needle swinging, settling, swinging. "You can still make the strip before dark if you go now. I mean it. I can follow his trail alone. He walked it alone twice."

I looked at her — at Wren's stubborn jaw on a younger face, at the tin tube with the map, at the compass that a dead man had mailed forward through nine years like a letter addressed to whoever cared enough to come.

I thought about a village seen from six thousand feet through a smoke haze, and a stick of cargo I'd never checked, and ten years of telling myself that distance was innocence.

"No," I said, and heard how different the word sounded than it had in the hangar. Lighter. Like setting something down. "We walk the scar. Both of us."

Ines exhaled — I hadn't realized she'd been holding it — and stood, and shouldered her pack, and clicked the compass shut.

Above us, at the top of the canyon wall where the scar of light stone met the darkening sky, something moved. Just for a second. A shape against the last of the light, upright, human-sized — there and gone, quick as a doubt.

Ines saw it too. Our eyes met.

"Tomás is long gone," she said quietly.

"Yes," I said, and picked up my pack, and started climbing toward it.
