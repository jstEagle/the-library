## 9. What the River Keeps

The canyon swallowed the light an hour before the sun actually set.

Ines noticed it the way a cartographer notices anything — as a problem of angles. The walls rose sheer on either side of the Kestrel, grey-green stone furred with moss that dripped even in the dry season, and the river ran narrow and fast between them, loud enough that she had to lean close to Sable to be heard. The water had changed color, too. Downstream it had been the brown of steeped tea, all silt and shallows. Here it ran green-black and deep, cold rising off it like breath from a cellar.

"We should stop," Sable said, and pointed.

The ledge was barely a ledge — a shelf of flat stone above the waterline, maybe six meters wide, backed by an overhang that had shed a curtain of fallen rock. But someone had been there before them. Ines saw it in pieces before she saw it whole: iron rings driven into the stone, rusted to the color of dried blood. A rope, stiff and grey with age, still knotted through one of them. A fire-blackened circle of stones. And half-hidden behind a slab, a structure that took her a moment to resolve into meaning — a low shelter built of driftwood and flattened fuel tins, roofed with sheet plastic gone brittle as dead leaves.

"Someone's camp," she said.

Sable didn't answer. She was looking at the camp the way Ines had seen her look at weather — reading it, measuring it against something she wasn't saying.

"That someone," Sable said finally, "signed his name on everything he owned. Look at the tins."

Ines looked. On the flattened fuel tins that formed the shelter's walls, someone had scratched letters into the metal with something sharp. *E. WREN. E. WREN. E. WREN.* Row after row, the way a child writes a name to make it true.

She sat down on the stone. Not gracefully. Her legs simply stopped agreeing to hold her.

"He camped here," she said. "He camped *here*."

"Looks like more than once." Sable was already moving along the ledge, careful, her boots testing each stone. "More than a few times. Look at the rope — replaced at least twice. Those rings are set at different heights, like he kept re-rigging for the water level. Twenty years of dry seasons, that rope's seen."

Ines wanted to say something. She found she had nothing. Twenty years of letters from her father, and not one of them had mentioned a canyon, a ledge, a camp with his name scratched into the walls like a prayer or a penance. He had written to her about weather. About the price of cartridges. About a dog he'd had, once, and lost to a snake. He had written her *nothing*, and she had built her whole understanding of him out of that nothing, the way you build a map of a coastline you've never sailed from the reports of people who only ever saw it in storms.

Sable crouched at the mouth of the shelter. "Ines."

There was something in her voice. Ines got up, joints aching from four days in the boat, and went to her.

The inside of the shelter was dry. Dry and dark and smelling of old smoke and kerosene and, beneath it, a faint mineral sweetness — the stone itself breathing out. Along the back wall, in a niche the overhang had carved, someone had built a shelf from a split plank, and on the shelf sat tins. Dozens of them. Biscuit tins, tea tins, tobacco tins, all of them sealed with wax that had yellowed and cracked, all of them labeled in the same sharp, slanting hand Ines knew from a single map and a lifetime of envelopes.

*E.W. — DO NOT OPEN AT WATER. E.W. — 3rd YEAR. E.W. — THE SOURCE, PART ONE. E.W. — PART TWO. E.W. — PART THREE. E.W. — PART FOUR. E.W. — PART FIVE. E.W. — PART SIX. E.W. — PART SEVEN.*

And at the end of the row, larger than the rest, a tin that had once held fifty cigars, its label scraped away, marked only: *FOR INES.*

* * *

They didn't open them right away. Sable insisted — fire first, food first, light while there was still light to make. Ines understood it was kindness dressed as practicality and let her have it. She gathered driftwood with hands that wouldn't stay steady while Sable got the little spirit stove hissing, and neither of them spoke, and the river spoke for both of them, that vast green roar filling the canyon like weather.

It was full dark when she finally cut the wax on the first tin with her knife. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were notebooks. Cheap school exercise books, the kind sold in any river-town store, their covers softened and darkened by years of handling. She opened the first one and her father's handwriting flowed up at her out of the dark, and the canyon seemed to go quieter, as if the river itself were leaning in.

*Third season at the ledge,* it began. *The water is lower than I have ever known it. I have decided to write everything down, all of it, in order, because I am fifty-one years old and I have begun to distrust my own memory, and a memory that cannot be checked is no better than a lie.*

Ines read aloud. She didn't decide to; the words simply came out of her, because some part of her understood that Sable needed to hear them too, and because her father's voice, after eleven years of silence, was a thing she could not keep to herself.

*Everyone believes I came up here for gold. I let them believe it. A prospector is a harmless animal. Nobody follows a prospector, nobody questions a prospector, and if a prospector dies in the canyon, people say: the river, the fever, the rocks. It is the best disguise I have ever worn and I have worn it for twenty years and it has cost me everything except the one thing I am trying to keep.*

She stopped. Sable said, quietly, "Keep reading."

*In the year of the flood — you will find the date in my ledger, I have it exact — I was working the bars below the falls when I saw the Company boats come up through the canyon. Six of them. Armed men, survey equipment, a man in a white suit who did not get out of the boat. I knew then that they had finally found the source, and I knew what they had come to do, because I had spent two years by then learning what the Company does when it finds something it does not want found.*

*I followed them up. I should not have. I want that written plainly: I followed armed men into a canyon alone, and I survived it, and surviving it was the worst thing that ever happened to me.*

*What they did at the spring took one day. The settlement there — the Kestrel people, the ones who had fished the headwaters since before there were maps — they had refused to move. The Company had offered them money and then threats and then nothing at all, and they had stayed, because the spring was theirs, because it had always been theirs. So the Company came up through the canyon in six boats and they burned the village and they put the dead in the spring pool itself, to hide them, and then they sealed the pool with a concrete cap and built their cache beneath the overhang above it, and the man in the white suit made a speech about progress. I heard it from the rocks. I counted the boats going down. I counted what was in them. I will not write here what I counted. It is in the ledger. The ledger is in the cache. Anyone who needs to know can know.*

Ines's voice failed on that line. She read the rest in silence, her lips moving, tears dropping onto the cheap paper and blooming there, and she didn't wipe them because her hands were busy holding the notebook the way you hold a bird — too tight and not tight enough.

Sable had gone very still. In the stove-light her face was unreadable, but her hands, Ines noticed, were not. They were pressed flat against her thighs, knuckles white, the way a person presses down on something to keep it from shaking.

"Keep going," Sable said. Her voice was rough.

Ines turned pages. The journals went on — season after season, year after year. Her father documenting everything: the Company's supply runs, the rotation of their guards, the names of the men in the white suit's employ, copied from manifests he'd bought, stolen, memorized. Photographs — she found them in the third tin, negatives and prints in waxed paper, and she made herself look: the spring pool before, the concrete cap after, the cache being built, faces of Company men at the landing below the falls. And payrolls. Actual payrolls, in the Company's own printing, with the names of men who had been paid for, the ledger's cold phrase, *site security, headwaters operation, fiscal year of the flood.*

"He wasn't prospecting," she said. It came out as an accusation, though there was no one to accuse. "He was building a case. Twenty years. He was building a case and he never — " Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "He never once told me. Not once. Eleven years of letters and he wrote me about a *dog*."

"He was protecting you." Sable said it flatly, without softness, and Ines realized that was the kindest way she could have said it. "You've seen what the Company does to people who talk. He kept you outside it. That's not nothing."

"He kept me outside *him*." Ines heard her own voice rise and echo off the canyon walls and didn't care. "I spent my whole life mapping everything except him. I mapped the coastal shoals. I mapped the plateau. I mapped places no one had ever surveyed, because — " She stopped, because the truth of it was suddenly, horribly simple. "Because he was the one place I couldn't chart. And it turns out he charted *himself*. Twenty years. He mapped his own whole life into these tins and he addressed the last one to me and he still didn't tell me. He made me come up a river in the dry season to find out who my father was."

The echo died. The river went on.

Sable fed another flame-tablet to the stove. The light jumped. "Read the last one," she said. "The one with your name."

* * *

The cigar tin was heavier than the others. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and then in a rubberized groundsheet and then in wax, was a single notebook, newer, its cover barely creased, and a folded paper Ines recognized instantly — she'd have known it anywhere, had known it since the night a dying man pressed it into a stranger's hands at a strip only one pilot serviced.

The map.

But beneath it, the notebook. And on the first page of the notebook, in handwriting shakier than the journals but still unmistakably his:

*If you are reading this, then Elias Wren is dead, and the map worked, and you are Ines, or I have badly misjudged both my daughter and my river.*

Ines laughed. It came out broken, half a sob, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth.

*You will be angry with me. Be angry. Anger is fuel; I ran on it for twenty years and it never once let me down. But before you spend it all, read the ledger page your pilot is carrying, and read what follows, and understand that I did not stay away because I did not love you. I stayed away because everything I touched after the flood was poisoned, and I had decided the only thing I could still give anyone was distance.*

*The evidence is in the cache. The cache is under the overhang above the spring pool, behind the concrete cap, in the sealed vault the Company built with its own money to hide its own crime. I have spent twenty years watching it, and the river has kept it better than I ever could, because the river is patient and the Company is not. The dry season is the only time the vault mouth stands above the water. I am writing this in the dry season. If you are reading it, so are you. You have weeks, maybe less.*

*One more thing. The pilot who brought you this — Delacroix. I chose her. I have watched her fly into Halfway Bend for years, and I know what she is running from, and I chose her anyway, because a person running that hard is a person who will understand what it costs to stop.*

Ines lowered the notebook. Sable was looking at the fire.

"He chose you," Ines said. "He watched you. He — Sable. He knew."

"I figured he might have." Sable's voice was steady, but her hands still weren't. "Dying men don't pick pilots at random."

"Why didn't you say?"

"Because it wasn't mine to say." Sable looked up then, and the stove-light made her eyes look like the river looked — dark, and moving over something deep. "He wanted you to hear it from him. Even dead, he wanted it to be his voice."

Ines looked down at the notebook, at the last page, where the handwriting gathered itself for a final effort:

*The river kept it for me. Now the river keeps it for you. And when it is done — when the Company's name is in the ground where it belongs — bring me back here. I would like to be where the water starts.*

She closed the tin. Sat with her hands on it, feeling the cold metal warm slowly under her palms, and thought about her father at this ledge, season after season, alone, scratching his name into fuel tins so that something of him would remain even if the river took him. He had been a coward in every letter and a hero in every canyon and she had never once had both of those men in the same room, and now she never would, and the grief of that was so large and so clean that she let it move through her without fighting it, the way the river moved through the canyon — around every obstacle, wearing them down, going where it was going.

When she finally spoke, her voice was her own again. "The cache. Under the overhang above the spring pool. Behind the cap."

"Two days upstream," Sable said. "Maybe three, the way the water's dropping."

"Then we go now. Tonight. By headlamp if we have to."

Sable shook her head slowly. "Not by night, not in this canyon. You've seen the water. One mistake in the dark and the river keeps us too." She fed the stove the last of its tablets and the flame shrank. "First light. We'll want the sun on the water."

Ines lay back on the stone with the cigar tin under her head like a pillow, and did not sleep, and did not mind. Above the canyon rim the stars came out in their thousands, indifferent and enormous, and the river roared its one word all night long, and somewhere upstream in the dark her father's evidence sat sealed behind a concrete cap, waiting, as it had waited through twenty dry seasons, for the water to fall far enough.

It was Sable who woke her, hours before dawn, with a hand on her shoulder and a tension in her grip that had nothing to do with the plan.

"Listen," Sable whispered.

Ines listened. Beneath the river's roar, faint and rhythmic, carried up the canyon on the cold air: an engine. A boat engine. And it was not coming from downstream.
