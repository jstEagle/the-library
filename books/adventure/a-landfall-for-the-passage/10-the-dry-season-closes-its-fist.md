## 10. The Dry Season Closes Its Fist

The river began to lie to us on the fourth morning.

It had been honest enough in the lowlands — brown, muscular, pushing us along with the indifference of a thing that has carried a thousand boats and will carry a thousand more. But here, where the canyon walls narrowed to a stone throat and the water ran green over hidden shelves of rock, it started telling us there was depth where there was none. I learned to read the lies by their color: the pale seams beneath the surface, the way the current braided and thinned like hair going gray. Shallow water is a lighter water. It doesn't hide what it's about to do to your hull.

"Gravel bar," I said, and killed the engine before Ines could ask.

We drifted the last hundred meters with the motor tilted up, the boat hissing along on a skin of water no deeper than my hand. The bar announced itself as a long pale tongue of stone breaking the surface ahead, studded with bleached driftwood that looked, in the early light, like the ribs of something enormous that had died mid-swim. We grounded with a grinding shudder that traveled up through the keel and into my teeth.

Ines didn't say anything. She'd stopped saying anything about the river two days ago, after the guide left us at the mouth of the first canyon with a shrug that managed to be both apologetic and contemptuous — *gringas, this far, alone* — and took the pay we'd promised him anyway. Since then she had learned the boat the way she learned everything: silently, obsessively, with her whole body. She was already over the side before I'd gotten the anchor line out, hauling the bow into deeper water by main strength, her boots sinking into cold gravel.

"We can't keep doing this," she said. Not complaining. Calculating. "How many bars between here and the falls?"

I looked upstream, at the water stepping down through the canyon in a series of white staircases, each one shallower than the last. The dry season had come down from the mountains like a hand closing, and every day the river gave up a little more of itself. Where Elias Wren had run this stretch twenty years ago in high water, we were picking our way through the bones of it.

"More than I want to count," I said. "We portage from here."

* * *

Portaging is not an adventure. Portaging is arithmetic done with your spine.

We carried everything: the boat, broken down into hull and frame; the fuel, decanted into drums we could barely lift; the food, the tarp, the rope, the tools, and the oilskin packet of journals we'd found in Wren's camp, wrapped now in three layers of plastic because Ines trusted nothing to weather, not even weather itself. We made trips. Four trips per crossing, maybe five, a quarter mile each way through scrub that grabbed at our sleeves and rocks that rolled underfoot like they'd been placed there by someone with a grudge. The sun came up over the canyon rim and turned the whole gorge into a kiln. Sweat soaked through my shirt and dried white with mineral crust. My hands went from soft to ruined in a single day, blisters opening and reopening until I stopped noticing them, which is the only mercy hard work ever offers.

Ines worked beside me without speaking for most of it. That was its own kind of language. On the second day of carrying, when I stumbled under the stern of the boat and would have gone down into a wash of scree, her hand shot out and caught the gunwale, taking half the weight without a word, holding it until I found my feet again. Then we kept walking.

It was on the third day, at the top of the last portage above the falls, that I finally told her.

I don't know why there and not somewhere else. Maybe because the place demanded it. From the ridge where we set the boat down to rest, you could see the whole drowned valley below — a wide basin where the river slowed and spread and went quiet, ringed by cliffs, the water so still it held the sky upside down. Ten years ago I had flown over a valley like this one. Not this one. Another one, three hundred miles east, in a country whose name I still couldn't say without tasting copper.

We sat with our backs against the hull, sharing the last of the water in the small bottle, saving the warm dregs in the big jug for the descent. Ines had her father's journal open on her knees, reading by habit even exhausted, her finger moving down the faded lines. She did that at every rest stop. Reading him back into existence, entry by entry, the way you'd feed a fire one stick at a time.

"Sable." She didn't look up. "This entry. He mentions a pilot. 'The Company's wings came twice this spring. Cargo unknown, men armed. The village at the confluence emptied before the second flight.'" Her finger stopped. "He never names them."

"No," I said. "He wouldn't."

She looked at me then. She had her father's eyes — I'd realized that somewhere around the second canyon — the same flat gray-green that gives nothing away until it decides to give everything. "You know something."

"I know a lot of things," I said. "That's the problem with me. I've spent ten years making sure I knew things nobody else wanted known."

The valley below held its breath. Even the wind seemed to wait.

"You've been circling it since Halfway Bend," she said. "Since the hangar burned. You look at me sometimes like you're rehearsing something. So say it. Whatever it costs you, say it, because I'm tired of carrying your silences on top of my father's."

So I told her.

* * *

"I flew for the Meridian Company once," I said. "Before I had my own plane. Before any of this. I was twenty-four and I owed money to people who explained interest to me with a tire iron, and the Company paid cash, weekly, no questions. You tell yourself it's just freight. Crates are crates. You don't look inside them, and if you do look, you make yourself see machine parts."

Ines said nothing. She had gone very still, the journal closed under her hand, and I understood that she was already bracing, the way you brace before a wave breaks over a boat.

"There was a village," I said. "Up on a plateau, near a concession the Company wanted cleared. They told me it was a personnel rotation. Six men, gear, a week's supplies. I'd flown muscle before — security contractors, they called themselves, though nobody contracted them but the Company. You fly them in, you fly them out, you keep your mouth shut and your logbook clean. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement."

My hands were shaking. I watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to the woman I'd been ten years ago, sitting in a cockpit smelling of avgas and someone else's cigarettes, running up the engine while six men loaded themselves into the cabin with their long guns wrapped in blankets.

"The village was called San Rafael de las Nubes. Clouds. It was named for clouds, Ines. There were maybe two hundred people. Farmers. A school with a bell the teacher rang by hand." I heard my own voice go flat and far away, the voice I used for weather reports. "I landed them on the strip at midmorning. The man who paid me — a surveyor, actually, that was his title, surveyor, same as the one who's been asking questions in Port Anselm — he shook my hand and said the operation would be concluded by nightfall and I should return in three days for pickup. Three days. I remember thinking that was a long time for a personnel rotation."

"And you came back," Ines said. Not a question. She knew. Everyone who has ever flown for bad people knows how the story goes, because the story only has one shape.

"I came back in three days," I said. "There wasn't a village. There was ash. The school bell had melted. I circled twice because I couldn't believe what I was seeing, and on the second pass I saw the strip had been graded — widened, actually, improved, like they were planning to use it again. And there was a dog. One dog, walking the perimeter of the ash, looking for—" I stopped. I hadn't told this part to anyone. My throat closed around it and I made myself push through, because she deserved all of it or none. "Looking for the people. And I put the plane down, and I walked around, and I found—"

I didn't finish. Some sentences don't survive being spoken twice.

"I filed my flight plan out of the region that afternoon and I never flew for them again. I changed my name — Delacroix was my mother's, it was clean. I sold everything I owned to buy the Otter, and I started flying medicine runs to places so small they don't appear on charts, and I made myself a rule: never stay past a season. Never let anyone know me well enough to ask. Because the people in those villages, the ones I've been supplying for ten years — they're the same people, Ines. Same rivers, same valleys, same faces. The Company burns what it can't buy, and I helped light the match, and every supply drop since has been me trying to pour water on a fire that's already burned down to the roots."

The silence afterward was enormous. Below us the drowned valley lay still as poured glass, and somewhere in it, sealed under the waterline, was whatever Elias Wren had spent twenty years guarding — evidence, Ines believed, proof of exactly the kind of work I had once ferried men and matériel toward. The river keeping what men wanted buried.

"So the ledger page," Ines said finally. "The one you admitted finding in his effects. You knew what the Company does to witnesses because you were almost one."

"Worse," I said. "I was staff."

She looked at me for a long time. The sun moved. A bird — some raptor riding the thermals off the cliff face — wheeled once overhead and was gone. I sat there and let her look, because that was the whole point of telling her: no more turning away, no more climbing to altitude where everything below becomes pattern and color instead of people.

"I'm not going to forgive you," she said at last.

"I know."

"My father spent twenty years documenting what those men did, and he died alone on a dirt strip because the Company hunted him to the edge of the map, and you — you carried them. You were the wings." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. Quieter meant every word had been weighed. "And you want absolution from me, the daughter, because I'm the closest thing to a priest this river has."

"I'm not asking for absolution," I said. "I'm asking you to know who's sitting next to you. If we're going to die in this canyon carrying his journals, you should know you're dying next to one of the people he was hiding from."

Something moved behind her eyes — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. The cartographer in her, revising a chart that had shown safe water where there was a reef.

"Here's what I know," she said. She picked up the journal and tapped its cover. "My father wrote four hundred pages about the Company's crimes and not one word about the pilots who flew for them. I used to think that was an oversight. Now I think he understood something I'm only beginning to." She stood, brushed grit from her knees, and looked down at the valley, then back at me. "He wrote that complicity is a current. Nobody swims in it on purpose. You get pulled in, and the only question — the only question, Sable — is whether you fight it or let it take you downstream. He fought it for twenty years. Alone. It killed him."

She shouldered her pack. The strap cut across the bruise she'd earned on the second portage, and she didn't flinch.

"I don't forgive you. I may never forgive you. But I'm not leaving, and not because of you — because of him, and because of that valley down there, and because the truth doesn't care who carries it as long as somebody does." She paused at the edge of the descent trail, where the path dropped steeply toward the water. "You could have lied to me. Right up to the source, you could have lied. You didn't. That's not redemption. But it's a heading."

Then she started down, and I followed, and neither of us spoke again until the boat was reassembled and sliding out onto the still water of the drowned valley, where the silence felt less like peace than like held breath.

* * *

We were halfway across the basin when I saw it.

Not with my eyes at first — with the back of my neck, the old pilot's sense that something in the sky has changed its mind about you. I turned. Far upstream, above the canyon rim, a speck crossed the gap between two peaks, catching the sun once, twice, and vanishing south.

A plane. High, fast, headed away from us — but there was only one reason a plane flew that line in dry season, and it wasn't sightseeing. Someone was mapping the approaches. Someone was working out how close the Company's aircraft could get to the headwaters, and where the canyon walls would force them to turn back, and where we would have nowhere left to run except forward.

Ines saw my face and read it instantly. "Company?"

"Or Callum," I said. "Reyes flies that route too."

"Would that be better?"

I watched the empty sky where the speck had been, and thought about Callum Reyes's easy grin and his hunger for my routes, and about the debt he owed a dead man named Wren, and about how debts and loyalties bend toward whoever pays.

"I honestly don't know," I said, and put the throttle down, and we ran for the far shore while the light lasted.
