## 11. Callum's Contrition

The order came in on a Tuesday, folded inside a weather briefing like a knife inside a newspaper.

*Intercept and confirm. Last known position, Kestrel Narrows. Do not engage. Do not reveal. Report only.*

Callum Reyes read it three times in the blue half-light of the Meridian Company's regional office, standing beneath a ceiling fan that turned with the weary patience of something that had long ago stopped believing in what it stirred. The paper was company stock, watermarked with the coiled serpent of the Meridian crest, and it weighed more in his hand than any cargo he'd ever hauled.

Voss, the surveyor, sat behind a desk too large for the room it dominated, the way a stone is too large for the stream it's dropped into. He had a way of not looking at you that was worse than looking — his eyes resting on the middle distance just over your shoulder, as though you were a landmark he'd already surveyed and found unremarkable.

"You'll want to leave before the thermals build," Voss said. "The Narrows are ugly in the afternoon."

"I've flown the Narrows."

"I know." A thin pause, the sound of a file being consulted that Voss had no need to open. "You flew them with Elias Wren, didn't you. Years ago."

The fan turned. Somewhere outside, a fuel truck clanked through its rounds, and the smell of avgas crept in under the door like a dog that had learned which houses fed it.

"Once," Callum said. "He talked me down off a mountain when my engine quit. Talked me all the way to the water."

"Sentimental story." Voss said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. Sentiment, in his mouth, was simply a defect to be noted in the logbook. "The women are carrying documents that could do the company real harm. Your job is only to find them and tell us where they are. You won't even see them again. Someone else will do the rest."

*The rest.* Callum turned the phrase over and found nothing underneath it, the way you turn a river stone and find only more river stone. He thought of the word *rest* as it was used at funerals. He thought of the rest of a journey, the rest of a life, the rest that remains when everything else has been taken away.

"Understood," he said, and heard his own voice come out level, a pilot's voice, the voice you use on the radio when the fire is behind you and the passengers can't see it yet.

* * *

He flew north into a sky the color of hammered tin.

The dry season had the country by the throat now. From altitude the Kestrel looked less like a river than a rumor of one — a brown thread stitched through a landscape gone the pale gold of old paper, the oxbows shrinking into question marks, the sandbars surfacing like the backs of sleeping animals. Every year the river did this, pulled itself inward, held its breath, and every year the people who lived along it learned again what it meant to depend on something that could leave.

Callum had made his living off that dependence. That was the honest way to put it, though he'd spent years dressing it up in finer cloth: *serving the settlements, keeping the supply lines open, being the man who showed up.* He showed up, all right. He showed up with Meridian price lists in one pocket and Meridian contracts in the other, and he'd watched, season after season, as the settlements that once owned their own boats came to own nothing but debts to the company that owned his routes.

He'd told himself the routes were earned. He'd told himself Sable Delacroix flew the same sky he did and the only difference between them was that she was too proud to sign what he'd signed.

The truth, which he was only now beginning to read like a chart he'd been holding upside down for a decade, was that she'd seen the fine print and he'd signed around it.

The Narrows opened beneath him around midday — a canyon so tight the river seemed less to flow through it than to be swallowed by it, the walls rising sheer and red-gold, striped with the shadows of clouds that couldn't reach the water. He throttled back and let the plane drift along the canyon's throat, eyes moving the way they'd been trained to move: reading the water for boats, the banks for camps, the sandbars for the bright litter of human passage.

He found what he was looking for forty minutes in, and it nearly stopped his heart, though he'd have denied that under oath.

A boat, pulled up on a gravel bar in the lee of a bend. Small. A figure moving along the shore — dark-haired, quick, bent over something at the waterline. Even from eight hundred feet he knew the set of her shoulders, that stubborn forward lean, as if Ines Wren were perpetually walking into a wind only she could feel.

*Confirm and report.* One radio call. One set of coordinates. And then, as Voss had said with such administrative gentleness, *someone else will do the rest.*

Callum flew on past the bend. He did not look back, because looking back is how you crash into mountains, and because he was afraid of what his face was doing.

* * *

There was a strip upstream, if you could call it that — a shelf of packed earth the missionaries had cut into a bench of the canyon two generations ago and then abandoned, because even God's work has a budget. He'd landed there once before, years back, with Wren in the right-hand seat hollering at him about his flare timing, and the memory of that landing came back to him now with the vividness of things remembered at moments of decision: the shelf rushing up, the canyon walls folding in on either side like hands closing, Wren's voice — *ease it on, boy, the ground's not going anywhere, but you are* — and the wheels touching so soft he'd had to look to be sure.

He landed there again alone, and the shelf held, and the canyon closed around him with a silence so complete he could hear the tick of the engine cooling, the small private sounds of his own breathing.

He sat for a long time with his hands on the yoke.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain as a fuel gauge. If he walked away now, flew home, reported *negative contact, no sign of them in the Narrows* — Voss would send someone else. Voss would always send someone else. The company was a machine for sending someone else; that was its whole genius, its whole horror. His silence would buy them a day, maybe two, and then a boat would come up the canyon with men in it, and the documents Wren had spent twenty years hiding would end up at the bottom of the Kestrel, and so, very possibly, would the women.

If he stayed — if he did the thing that was assembling itself in his chest with the terrible inevitability of weather — then he was finished. Not fired. Finished. Meridian didn't fire people who knew what Callum knew; they simply stopped renewing licenses, stopped passing along the safety bulletins, stopped, in small administrative increments, protecting you from the country you flew over. He'd seen it happen to a man in Puerto Verde who'd asked one question too loudly about a mine's water rights. The man still flew. The man flew in an aircraft with a fuel gauge that lied.

Callum got out of the plane.

The canyon light was going amber, the shadows climbing the walls like slow water in reverse. He unloaded what he'd brought, because he had known — he admitted this now, standing in the dust with his arms full — he had known what he was going to do since the moment he'd read the order, maybe since the moment he'd seen Wren's name in the company file and felt something turn over in him like a stone in a riverbed.

Fuel first: two jerry cans, five gallons each, carried down the slope and stashed in the hollow beneath a leaning slab of rock, out of the sun, out of casual sight. He marked the spot with a strip of surveyor's ribbon, red, the kind every pilot and prospector on this river read without thinking — *here, look here* — and then, on second thought, tied the ribbon low, where it would be seen by someone walking the bank and missed by anyone scanning from a boat.

Food next. He'd provisioned the flight for three days, and he emptied the locker without pretending to himself he wasn't doing exactly what he was doing: rice and tinned fish, a bag of oranges he'd bought that morning from a woman at the Port Anselm dock who'd asked him, idly, if he was flying upriver, and to whom he had lied. The lie sat in him now like a swallowed stone. He added a box of matches, a coil of fishing line, a water filter, the small mercies.

And then, at the bottom of the locker, the thing he had wrapped in a flour sack and told himself was just ballast: the spark plug.

He'd pulled it that morning before dawn, in the grey hour when the hangars were empty and even the gulls hadn't clocked in. It was a good plug, clean as a coin, and he'd replaced it with one he'd filed down himself, one that would run — would run *almost* — and then begin to miss, and then begin to fail, somewhere over open country, forcing whoever flew this route to put down and wait. It was sabotage of the gentlest, most deniable kind, and it was also, he understood, the first truly dishonest thing he'd ever done to the company, which was strange, because he'd been dishonest with the company's blessing for years. He'd lied to settlements about prices. He'd lied to himself about routes. But he'd never lied to *Meridian*, and there was a boy somewhere inside him who had wanted nothing so much as to be trusted by the people in the big office, and that boy was the one who'd filed the plug, and that boy was weeping, in whatever way grown men weep, which is to say silently and while doing manual labor.

He set the plug on a flat stone beside the fuel cans, wrapped in its flour sack, where no one would ever find it and where he would know it was.

* * *

He flew out at first light, and the engine ran rough.

Not badly — not yet — but with a stutter in it at cruise power, a hitch like a man talking who keeps losing his place in the sentence. He'd planned it that way, and hearing his own plan come true in the cylinders was a different thing from planning it. He turned south and let the miss grow, let it climb from stutter to stumble, and put down at the Company's own strip at Two Rivers with a face full of appropriate frustration and a maintenance log entry that read, in his own careful hand: *Suspect fouled plug. Grounded pending parts. No parts available locally. Estimate three days.*

Three days. He'd given them three days, and he'd given the women three days, and the arithmetic of the canyon — the dropping river, the portages, the pace of two people hauling a boat through rapids — said three days might be exactly enough, or exactly one day short, and there was nothing more he could do about it from a maintenance shed that smelled of oil and betrayal.

Voss arrived by radio within the hour. Callum could hear the suspicion in the signal itself — the extra pause before his call sign came back, the flatness of the acknowledgment, the way *copy your maintenance delay* sounded less like sympathy than like a note being made.

"Negative contact in the Narrows," Callum reported, and the lie came out steady, and he hated how steady. "Flew the full length at low altitude. No boats, no camps, no sign. If they're on the river, they're above the falls already, and nothing of ours is getting above the falls."

A long silence on the frequency. The kind of silence that has a pen in it.

"You landed somewhere," Voss said finally. Not a question.

"Two Rivers. Engine trouble. You have my log entry."

"I have your log entry." Another pause. The fan in some distant office turning. "You know, Reyes, Wren trusted you once. Saved your engine, I'm told. It would be a shame if that kind of gratitude ran in only one direction."

Callum looked out the hangar door at the strip, at the heat shimmer standing on the asphalt like something trying to rise and unable to. He thought about all the answers a man could give, and how each one was a door, and how most doors only open once.

"Gratitude doesn't fly aircraft," he said. "Bad plugs do. Send me parts or don't."

The frequency hissed. Somewhere far away, in an office with a ceiling fan and a desk too big for its room, a man was writing something down, and Callum Reyes, who had spent ten years making sure he was never the subject of anything written down, found that the feeling in his chest was not fear.

It was lighter than fear. It was the feeling of the wheels leaving the ground — that moment when the weight you've been carrying all your life is finally given somewhere else to go.

"Parts will be on Thursday's flight," Voss said, and cut the transmission, and the silence that followed was the loudest thing Callum had ever heard.

* * *

He spent the three days the way a man spends time in a cell he built himself: badly, and then, strangely, well.

The badly first. He drank one beer at the Two Rivers cantina and stared at the wall and imagined, with a precision that amounted to torture, all the ways his plan could fail — the fuel cans found by a Company patrol, the ribbon spied from the air, the filed plug discovered by some mechanic with honest eyes, the women dead in the canyon while he sat here drinking warm beer and rehearsing the word *negative contact* until it lost its meaning. He imagined Voss's voice. He imagined the coiled serpent on the watermark. He imagined Elias Wren, who had once talked him down off a mountain with nothing but a radio and his own unshakable calm, looking at him from wherever dead prospectors keep their vigil, and saying nothing at all, which was the worst thing Wren could say, because Wren had never needed to say anything twice.

And then the well, which came on the second night, walking back from the cantina under a sky so thick with stars it seemed less like weather than like evidence — proof, hung up where everyone could see it, that the world was larger than any company's claim to it. He stopped in the middle of the strip, in the dark, and let himself say the thing he'd been refusing to say since the office with the fan.

*I'm not doing this for them. I'm doing this because it's right.*

It sounded small, said aloud to no one. It sounded like the kind of thing printed on the inside covers of schoolbooks. But it settled in him with a rightness he hadn't felt since he was a young pilot with clean hands, before he'd learned that the sky could be owned, before he'd learned that a man could own a piece of it himself if he was willing to hold the leash of whoever owned the rest.

Wren had held no leash. Wren had spent twenty years alone in a canyon, writing down the truth in pencil, hiding it where only the river could guard it, and dying on a dirt strip waiting for a stranger to carry it the last hundred miles. And the stranger had carried it. And now it was in the canyon, in a boat, in the hands of Wren's daughter, moving upstream against a dying river with the whole weight of the Meridian Company sliding down the current behind it.

Three days, he'd bought them. He lay on a cot in the Company barracks, under a blanket that smelled of someone else's cigarettes, and did the arithmetic again, and again it came out the same: three days might be enough, or one day short.

Thursday's flight came in at noon with his parts — and with something else, something that made the mechanic who handed him the crate glance at him twice. A second crate, company-stenciled, addressed not to the strip but to *V. VOSS, EXPEDITION, KESTREL NARROWS — HOLD FOR ARRIVAL.* Callum helped load it into the shed without being asked, and when the freighter had gone he stood a moment with his hand flat against the crate's boards, reading it the way he read weather, and what he read there was weight. The crate was heavy in the way that only two things are heavy: engines, and weapons.

Voss wasn't waiting for parts. Voss was coming upstream himself, and he wasn't waiting three days.

Callum walked out to his plane in the white afternoon heat, opened the cowl, and began, with steady hands and an unsteady heart, to change the plug he had never needed to change — and to think, with the cold clarity of a man reading a river he is about to run, how many hours he had left, and how few of them were enough.
