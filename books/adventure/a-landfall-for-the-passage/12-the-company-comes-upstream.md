## 12. The Company Comes Upstream

Ines heard them before she saw them — a sound like the river had grown a second voice, lower and more mechanical, grinding against the current from somewhere below the bend.

She was kneeling at the water's edge, filling the dented kettle they'd carried since the guide left them, when the sound reached her. At first she mistook it for wind working through the canyon walls, that low moan the basalt made in the evenings, but the wind didn't come with rhythm. This did. A steady, patient thrumming, the sound of an engine being worked hard and not caring what it cost.

"Sable." She said it quietly, though there was no one else to hear, no one within twenty miles except the woman who was already turning toward the water, coffee tin forgotten in her hand.

Sable stood very still, head cocked, the way Ines had seen her stand on their third day out, listening to a distant storm decide whether to come over the ridge or go around. "Boats," Sable said. "More than one."

"How far?"

"Hard to say. Sound carries strange in here. Could be two bends down. Could be four." She set the tin down on a flat stone with exaggerated care, as if the small ritual of it could hold back whatever was coming up the canyon. "They're running against the current, which means motors, which means money. Nobody poles a boat upstream through this country for fun."

Ines felt something cold settle behind her ribs. Not fear, exactly. Not yet. It was older than fear — the particular dread of a cartographer who has spent her life reading terrain and has just realized the terrain is reading her back.

* * *

They climbed.

It wasn't a decision so much as a reflex, the same instinct that sent animals upslope when the valley floor turned hostile. They hauled the boat above the waterline, dragged brush over it with the numb efficiency of people who had done too much hiding in too few days, and took to the rocks on the eastern wall where the canyon narrowed to a throat of black stone. From a shelf halfway up, screened by a curtain of dead ferns, they could see the river below: a ribbon of brown-green water, shallow now, braiding itself around gravel bars that hadn't existed a month ago. The dry season had been closing its fist all week, and now Ines could see how completely it had closed. Where Wren's journals described rapids, there were stones. Where he'd written *deep channel, keep right*, there was a sandbar wide enough to land a plane on.

And up that shrunken river, around the southern bend, came the boats.

Three of them. Long, flat-bottomed, the kind built to run shallow water, each with an outboard engine and a canvas canopy stretched over a cargo bed. Even at this distance Ines could read them the way she read any map — by proportion, by load, by the way men moved through space. The boats rode low. Heavily provisioned. Fuel drums lashed amidships, crates stacked under the canopies, and standing figures at the bows holding rifles the way other men held fishing rods: casually, habitually, like tools of a trade.

At the prow of the lead boat stood a figure in a pale hat, and even before the man raised his field glasses toward the canyon walls, Ines knew him. She had seen him twice in Port Anselm — once across the street from the survey office, once outside her own door, asking her neighbor questions about cargo manifests. Mr. Voss. The Meridian Company's man. The one whose questions always sounded like courtesy and landed like audits.

"He followed us," Sable said. Her voice was flat, stripped of everything except function. "Or he never lost us. Either way—"

"He's not following the map." Ines said it before she fully understood she was going to say it, the thought arriving whole, the way her best deductions always did. "Look at them. Look at the boats. That's not a search party, Sable. Search parties fan out. They check side channels, they stop, they look. These boats are going straight upriver, straight to the source, like they've got coordinates." She watched the lead boat thread a gravel bar without hesitation, without sounding the depth, without pausing. "He knows exactly where we're going. Which means he knows exactly what's there."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Below them the engines droned, patient as digestion. "The ledger page," she said finally. "Wren's ledger page. I told you about it. Company payroll codes, disbursement records, dates going back thirty years. Dates that match nothing on any public record because there is no public record."

"There's a massacre," Ines said. "There's a massacre at the headwaters, and my father spent twenty years documenting it, and the Company has spent twenty years making sure nobody found his documentation. And now—" She stopped. Made herself say it plainly, because plainness was the only weapon she trusted. "Now they're coming to make sure nobody finds *us*. We're not witnesses to something, Sable. We're evidence. Living, breathing, walking evidence, and there are three boats full of armed men downstream who have been paid to make sure evidence doesn't testify."

The words hung in the canyon air, and Ines watched them land on Sable like weather. The pilot's face did something complicated — a flinch, a hardening, a settling, all in the space of a breath. Ines had learned to read those micro-movements the way she read contour lines: small displacements that revealed enormous underlying structure.

"You've known," Ines said. "You've known since Port Anselm what they do to people who talk."

"I've known since before Port Anselm." Sable didn't look at her. She kept her eyes on the boats, tracking them through the fern screen with the fixed attention of a woman memorizing a threat. "I told you I flew supplies. I didn't tell you everything I flew, or for whom, or what happened after. I'll tell you. I swear I'll tell you all of it. But not here, not on an open rock face with three boats of Company muscle coming up our only exit." She turned then, and her eyes were the gray of river water in flood — moving fast, carrying everything. "Right now I need you to be a cartographer and tell me something useful. Can they get past the narrows ahead?"

Ines made herself think. Made herself pull her mind out of the pit of dread and back onto terrain, because terrain was the one language she spoke fluently even when her hands shook. "The narrows above the second bend — yes, barely, if they portage the worst of it. Their boats are shallow-draft. After that..." She closed her eyes, summoned her father's handwriting, the careful annotations in the margins of the map that had started all of this. *Low water favors the walker,* Elias Wren had written. *High water favors the liar.* "After that the canyon steepens. There's a waterfall section my father marked as impassable to anything without wings. If they want to reach the basin, they have to portage. On foot. Through country that will fight them every step."

"Then we have time."

"We have a little time."

"A little is a currency," Sable said. "I've spent my whole life learning to spend it."

* * *

They moved through the last grey light, and Ines discovered that terror, properly channeled, was clarifying.

All the softness had gone out of the expedition. For days they had been explorers — reading Wren's journals by firelight, marveling at the old camp, arguing gently about which tributary the old man's notes meant by *the weeping wall*. Now every decision had teeth. How far to travel after dark. Whether to cook. Whether the smell of woodsmoke would carry downcanyon on the evening air and mark their position as precisely as a flag. Sable taught her things she had never wanted to learn: how to walk on stone without dislodging scree, how to bank a fire in a hollow so the flame showed nothing, how to listen past your own heartbeat for the sound of engines.

"They're not chasing a map," Ines said again, hours later, crouched beside a seep of spring water in the dark, drinking from cupped hands. She needed to say it aloud to believe it fully. Saying things aloud had always been how she tested truth; her father had been the same way, she realized now, filling journal after journal with words no one would ever read, testing truth against paper because paper didn't flinch. "If they wanted the map, they'd have taken it in Port Anselm. Burned the hangar with us in it, if that's what it took. They let us come upstream. They *followed* us upstream. Because we're doing their work for them — finding the cache, confirming the location, leading them through country their own surveyors couldn't crack."

"And when we find it," Sable said, "we become loose ends."

"When we find it, we become loose ends." Ines wiped her mouth. The water tasted of stone and iron. "My father understood this. Twenty years alone in this canyon, moving camp every season, burning half his own records — I used to think he was paranoid. I used to think the loneliness was failure, some inability to stay connected to people." Her voice caught, and she let it, because there was no one to perform composure for anymore. "He wasn't paranoid. He was accurate. He knew what the Company does to people who know things. He just hoped the river would guard it longer than he could."

Below them, faint but persistent, the engines droned on into the dark. They had made camp, then — she could hear the difference, the idle dropping to a putter, voices carrying up the canyon walls with that strange acoustic clarity of still desert air. Words she couldn't parse. Laughter, once. Men comfortable in their work.

"They'll reach the narrows by morning," Sable said. "We need to be past the waterfall portage by tomorrow night, or we're trapped between them and the source with nowhere to go but up a cliff face."

"And if we make the portage?"

"Then we get to the basin first, and we find what my father—" Ines corrected herself, the possessive still new in her mouth, still tender. "What *our* evidence says it holds. And then we figure out how a drowned valley full of paper becomes a public record before two dozen armed men arrive to drown it permanently."

Sable was silent. Then, in the dark, Ines heard her laugh — short, humorless, real. "You know what my father-in-everything-but-name used to say? Every pilot I ever respected had one rule: never fly into a box canyon. No way in, no way out, weather closing, engine failing — doesn't matter how good you are, the canyon wins." She shifted, and Ines heard the click of her checking the pistol she'd carried wrapped in oilcloth since Halfway Bend, the one she'd sworn she'd never need. "We flew into a box canyon, Ines. Both of us. Years ago, different directions, same canyon."

"Then we climb out," Ines said. "That's what the map is for. That's what maps are *always* for. They don't promise you'll survive the passage. They just promise the passage goes somewhere."

Above them the stars came out, hard and cold and indifferent, and below them the fires of the Meridian expedition glowed along the riverbank like a string of lit fuses burning slowly upstream. Ines lay awake between the two lights — the indifferent heavens and the approaching men — and understood, with the terrible calm of someone reading her own death notice, that the mission had changed. Discovery was over. Wonder was over. Somewhere in the dark, her father's journals pressed against her spine in their oilskin wrap, heavier than the boat they'd carried, heavier than the rifle, heavier than grief.

Tomorrow they would race armed men through a dying river to dig a massacre out of the water, and the only thing that mattered anymore was not being erased.

She closed her eyes and listened to the engines idling below, counting the seconds between their pulses like a pulse of her own.

*Somewhere out there*, she thought, *Callum Reyes owes my father a debt.* And she could only pray the man remembered it before the Company remembered him.
