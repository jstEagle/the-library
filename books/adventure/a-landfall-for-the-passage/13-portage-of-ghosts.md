## 13. Portage of Ghosts

The waterfall announced itself a full hour before we saw it — a low concussion in the rock, felt through the soles of our boots before it reached the ears, as though the river had a heartbeat and the canyon walls were its ribs. By then we'd been hauling the boat up the left bank for most of the morning, over root-laced mud and shelves of slate that flaked underfoot like old skin.

"Sounds big," Ines said. She said it the way she said everything now, clipped and practical, but I heard the question underneath it.

"It is," I said. "Wren's journals put it at forty meters. No lining around it, no running it. We go overland."

She didn't argue. She hadn't argued in two days, not since the camp with the oilcloth bundles and the twenty years of her father's handwriting, and I wasn't sure yet whether the silence was peace or just exhaustion wearing peace's clothes. Maybe both. Grief does that — hollows you out until there's room for something else to settle in.

We cached the boat's motor where the trail forked above the falls, wrapped in the tarp and weighted with stones, and divided the rest between us. The journals went into my pack. Ines had tried to take them; I'd taken them back without discussion, the way you take a hot pan from someone who doesn't know they're burning themselves. She carried the food, the rope, the water. I carried the tin boat folded over one shoulder like a coffin lid, which was exactly what it looked like, and what it would be if we failed.

The portage trail — if you could call it that — climbed away from the river through a stand of trees so old their roots had grown around boulders the way hands grow around tools. The light came down green and broken. Somewhere above us the falls kept up their drumming, close enough now that conversation meant shouting, so we stopped talking, and the silence became a third traveler, heavy as anything on our backs.

I counted my steps the way I always did when the weight got bad. Four hundred steps, rest. Four hundred more. It was a trick Callum had taught me a lifetime ago, back when we were friends instead of rivals, back when he still told me things for free. *The body quits when the mind lets it,* he used to say. *So don't let the mind do arithmetic it can lose.*

Somewhere past the thousandth step, Ines went down.

I heard it before I saw it — a cry cut short, the scrape of her pack on stone — and when I got the boat off my shoulder and turned, she was sitting against a root the size of a church pew, cradling her left ankle, her face gone the color of the slate under her.

"Don't," she said, before I could speak. "Don't say it."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say *we should have turned back* or *this is what happens* or some other thing pilots say."

"I was going to say let me look at it." I knelt and eased her boot off. Her ankle was already swelling, a hot knot rising along the outside of the joint. Not broken — I'd seen enough breaks to know the difference, had set two of them myself in settlements with no doctors — but bad enough. Bad enough that the next six kilometers of portage were suddenly a different animal entirely.

Ines watched me work with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself for ten years: fury at her own body for betraying her, layered over terror at what the betrayal cost. She was the cartographer. She was the one who measured distances. And now every meter between here and the headwaters had become a debt she couldn't pay on her own feet.

"We wrap it," I said. "We tape it tight, you keep the boot on, and we redistribute the load."

"Sable—"

"We redistribute the load," I said again, and started pulling gear out of her pack.

* * *

It took an hour to make the arrangements. The food and rope came onto my shoulders with the boat. The journals stayed with me. Ines took nothing but the water bottle and her own weight, hopping between my shoulder and whatever the forest offered — roots, trunks, my outstretched hand — in a rhythm we worked out badly and then less badly and then, by late afternoon, almost wordlessly.

The falls grew louder and then, strangely, quieter, muffled by the ridge we climbed parallel to, and in that strange hush Ines began to talk.

Not all at once. In pieces. The way her father's journals were written — fragments, observations, a man thinking out loud across twenty years. She talked because her hands were full of my shoulder and her mind needed somewhere to go, and I listened because there was nothing else to do with my ears while my legs did the arguing.

"He wrote to me every month," she said. "Did you know that? Every month, same day, like clockwork. And every letter was about rivers. Water levels, tributaries, stone formations. Twenty years of letters and I couldn't tell you a single thing about what he *felt*, because he never wrote it. I thought he didn't feel things. I thought—" Her breath caught as she hopped a gap between roots, and I steadied her, and she went on. "I thought he'd chosen the river over me."

"And now?"

"Now I've read the journals." She was quiet for three hops, four. "He wasn't choosing the river. He was hiding in it. There's a difference."

"I know the difference," I said, before I could stop myself.

She looked at me sideways. She had her father's eyes — I'd noticed it at Port Anselm and hated noticing it, the way grief keeps handing you these small cruel gifts — gray-green, patient, the eyes of someone who reads current for a living.

"Do you," she said. Not a question. An invitation, laid down gently, the way you lay down a plank across a gap.

I didn't take it. Not yet. But I carried it with me, the way I was carrying everything else.

* * *

We made camp at dusk on a shelf of rock below the ridge, close enough to the falls that their sound came back, filling the dark like weather. I got a fire going small — smoke discipline, because neither of us had said aloud what we both knew, that the surveyor's expedition was somewhere behind us in the canyon, moving at the speed of money while we moved at the speed of a sprained ankle.

Ines sat with her foot propped on a stone, ankle wrapped and iced with river water in a canvas bag, and read her father's journals by firelight. I cooked. Rice and dried fish and the last of the good tea, eked out because Callum's cache upstream was still a maybe, a rumor of kindness we couldn't count on.

"Read me some," I said.

She glanced up. "You've heard them already. You read half of them at the camp."

"You read them silently. That's different." I handed her the cup. "A journal isn't finished until someone hears it. That's what my mother used to say about letters, anyway. She wrote them to dead people. Said it still counted."

Ines took the cup. For a moment she just held it, steam climbing past her face, and then she opened the journal to a ribbon marker and read.

"'Day four hundred and eleven. Found the second marker today, the one the old man from Halfway Bend told me about, a cairn above the narrows. Under it, a tin box, rusted shut. Inside: a payroll ledger, Meridian Consolidated, dated the year of the flood. Names. Wages paid. A column headed simply *disposal.*'" She stopped. Her voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of someone carrying something fragile across ice.

"Keep reading," I said quietly.

"'I understand now why they burned the village records at San Tomás. Why the archivist drowned. They weren't hiding a crime. They were hiding the *bookkeeping.* A crime you can call a tragedy. A ledger you have to call a decision.'" Ines lowered the page. "He wrote that in his fourth year. Fourth. He knew, and he kept going for sixteen more years, collecting, copying, burying evidence in waterproof caches along the whole river like a man planting seeds he'd never see grow."

"That's not hiding in a river," I said. "That's standing guard."

"Yes." Her jaw worked. "And I spent nine years being ashamed of him."

"You spent nine years being a cartographer," I said. "You map things. It's what you do when you can't fix them."

She stared at me across the fire, and I realized what I'd said, and how much of me was in it, and it was too late to take it back. So I didn't try.

* * *

The second day of the portage was worse. The trail degraded into scree fields where every step slid, and Ines's ankle swelled despite the wrapping, and twice we had to stop while I re-taped it, her teeth gritted, sweat cutting lines through the dust on her face. The boat rode my shoulders like a penance. My pack rode my back like a second spine. By midday I had stopped counting steps and started counting breaths, and by late afternoon I had stopped counting altogether and entered that gray country beyond fatigue where the body runs on something older than will.

It was there, in that country, that I finally told her.

Not because I decided to. Because the weight rearranged something, the way a river rearranges a bank — slowly, invisibly, and then all at once.

"There was a village," I said. My voice sounded foreign, scraped thin. "Ten years ago. Up on the Rio Blanco drainage. I flew a charter for a company I didn't vet, because the pay was triple and I was twenty-four and stupid with ambition. Six men and crates. I didn't ask what was in the crates. Pilots don't ask. Asking costs you routes."

Ines said nothing. She kept pace beside me, hand on my arm, hopping her uneven rhythm, giving me the mercy of not looking at me.

"They were Company men. Meridian. I figured that out after, from the way they paid, the way they talked. I dropped them at a strip above the village and flew out, and three days later I heard what happened to the village." I stepped over a fallen trunk, my shoulders screaming under the hull. "Nobody was ever charged. Nobody was ever even named. Except me, in my own head, every night for ten years. So I started moving. One season here, one season there. Never long enough for anyone to know me well enough to ask. Never long enough for me to owe anyone the truth."

"The people at Halfway Bend," Ines said. "They covered for you. When the inquiry came through after the fire."

"They lied for me. Good people, lying for me, because I'd flown their medicine and their mail and their kids' schoolbooks, and they decided I was worth lying for." My throat closed. I pushed through it. "I never asked them to. That's the worst part. I never gave them the choice. I just let them do it and flew away before I had to watch them carry it."

We walked on. The falls thundered somewhere to our right, unseen, enormous. A bird I couldn't name called from the canopy, three notes, repeated, patient.

"Ines." I stopped. Set the boat down against a tree, my arms shaking with the relief of it. "I'm not telling you this so you'll forgive me. Your father spent twenty years documenting what those men do. I spent ten years flying supplies to the people they do it to, and one flight doing it *for* them. Those aren't the same thing. I know they're not the same thing. But they're not nothing, either."

She stood there, leaning on a root, her ruined ankle, her father's eyes, and for a long moment the only sound was the falls and my own blood in my ears.

"No," she said finally. "They're not the same thing." She lowered herself carefully onto a boulder. "But here's what I learned from the journals, Sable. Elias Wren wasn't a hero. He was a surveyor for Meridian for six years before San Tomás. He drew the maps they used. His hands helped build the road to the village. He didn't leave the Company because he was good. He left because he saw what his goodness had been used for, and he couldn't live with it, so he spent the rest of his life paying it back a page at a time." She looked up at me. "You've been paying too. Medicine runs. Mail runs. Ten years of supply flights into places the Company can't reach. You just never let anyone tell you it counted, because then you'd have to stop punishing yourself."

The words went into me like water into cracked ground — painful, and wanted, and impossibly deep.

"I don't know how to stop," I admitted.

"Neither did he," she said. "That's why he never came home." She held out her hand. "Help me up. We're not done walking."

I took her hand. It was smaller than mine, blistered, strong. Between us, for the first time since Port Anselm, there was no map, no ledger, no debt being calculated. Just two tired women and a hill to climb.

We stopped being strangers somewhere on that scree slope, though if you'd asked me later to name the exact moment, I couldn't have. Strangeness doesn't end dramatically. It ends the way fog lifts — you look up one afternoon and the far shore is simply there, and has been for some time.

* * *

We crested the ridge at golden hour and saw the falls entire for the first time: forty meters of white ruin pouring into a gorge veiled in spray, rainbows arcing through the mist like the river showing off. Below, the Kestrel gathered itself back together and ran on, deep and green and calm, as if it hadn't just been destroyed and reborn.

"Beautiful," Ines said. Then, sharper: "Sable. Look downstream."

I followed her finger. Far below, on the far bank where the river widened, a thread of smoke rose among the trees. Cookfire smoke. Deliberate, contained — the smoke of people who knew how to travel light and fast and armed.

More than one fire. Three, maybe four, strung along the bank like beads.

The surveyor's expedition. A day behind us, maybe less. Moving faster than a woman on a bad ankle and a pilot with a boat on her shoulders ever could.

Ines looked at me, and I looked at her, and the intimacy we'd built on that mountain didn't crack — it hardened, the way snow hardens into ice, into something you can walk on.

"How much farther to the cache?" she asked.

I pulled Wren's map from my jacket, careful of the damp. The headwaters lay beyond the next canyon, two days' travel if the river held. One day if it didn't hold and we didn't either.

"Close enough," I said, folding the map away. "We walk tonight."
