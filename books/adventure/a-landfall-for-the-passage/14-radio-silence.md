## 14. Radio Silence

They came for the radio at dawn.

Callum heard the truck first — the particular grind of Meridian tires on the gravel apron of the Port Anselm airfield, a sound he'd learned to read the way other men read weather. He was already at the Cessna's open door with a rag in his hand, wiping down an engine cowling that didn't need wiping, when the surveyor stepped out. Voss. The man had a name like a hinge and a face that never opened.

"Reyes." Voss didn't say good morning. Men like him considered time a resource they were spending on you, and they wanted you to feel the cost.

"Surveyor." Callum set down the rag. "You're up early."

"The river doesn't wait." Voss walked a slow half-circle around the plane, his boots crunching, his eyes doing inventory — the fuel gauges, the tie-downs, the radio rack visible through the window. "Neither do we. You're grounded."

The word landed in Callum's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He'd been expecting it for two days, ever since his handler — a soft-spoken accountant named Pell who smelled of ink and menthol — had stopped returning his calls from the Company office. Grounded. He'd flown since he was seventeen. He'd learned to land in crosswinds that made grown men pray, had threaded fog through canyon mouths where the walls rose close enough to touch with a wingtip. The sky wasn't his livelihood. It was the shape his life had taken, the way a river takes the shape of its valley.

"Grounded," he repeated. "On whose authority?"

"Meridian's. Your charter license is under review — irregularities in your flight logs." Voss said it without heat, without apology, the way a man reports the price of fuel. "We'll need your aircraft. Company business upstream. You'll be compensated at standard charter rates."

*Standard charter rates.* As if the plane were a mule. As if Callum himself were nothing but the harness.

"And my radio?"

"Confiscated pending review." Voss held out his hand, palm up, patient as a collection plate. "Company property, technically. Leased equipment. It's all in your contract, Reyes. You did read your contract."

He hadn't. Not past the pages about payment schedules and liability. He'd signed because the routes were good and the money was better than anything he'd earned running mail into Halfway Bend, and because the Meridian man who'd recruited him — over beers, back when it felt like opportunity instead of capture — had promised him Sable Delacroix's routes. *She can't fly forever,* the man had said. *A woman like that is always one bad season from burning out.* And Callum, who had spent six years watching her taillights disappear ahead of him into weather he wouldn't have attempted, had wanted to believe it.

Now here was the bill for that belief, presented at dawn by a man with a hinge for a name.

* * *

They took the radio out with a screwdriver and a canvas bag, and they took their time about it, and Callum stood on the apron in the cold gold light and watched them do it, and something in him went very quiet. Not calm. Quiet the way a river goes quiet above a falls.

Voss left two men to guard the plane — Company men, ex-military by the way they stood, wide-footed and bored. They were to refuel it, load it, have it ready by evening for the upstream run. Voss himself gave Callum a last look before climbing back into the truck.

"You'll want to stay available," he said. "There may be questions."

"There usually are," Callum said, and watched the truck go, and then stood alone on the apron with his hands in his pockets and his whole future sitting in someone else's custody.

That was when he thought of Elias Wren.

It came to him not as memory but as weight — the specific weight of a man's hand on the back of your neck in a shaking cockpit, twenty years ago, over the Kestrel rapids. Callum had been young then, younger than the engine deserved, flying a load of drill parts for a survey outfit in a plane older than both of them. The fuel line had fouled somewhere over the gorge — a clog, a vapor lock, he never learned which — and the engine had coughed once, twice, and begun to die while the rapids turned white and hungry eight hundred feet below.

He'd been too green to know what to do. Wren, riding shotgun as a passenger, some prospector hitching a lift into country no one else would fly to, had known exactly what to do. Had leaned over without asking, hands sure as a surgeon's, bled the line, primed the pump, talked Callum through the restart in a voice so level it might have been reading a grocery list. *Ease her nose down. Don't chase the RPMs. There she is. There she is.* And the engine had caught, and the rapids had fallen away beneath them, and Wren had sat back and lit a pipe he wasn't allowed to smoke and said, "You'll do, boy. Just remember: the machine will always tell you what's wrong with it, if you're humble enough to listen."

Callum had never told anyone that story. It belonged to a version of himself he'd spent years trying to outgrow — the green kid, the grateful one. And now the Company's own files had handed Wren's name back to him like a debt called in across two decades, and Callum had sat in Pell's office reading it and felt something shift in his chest that he still hadn't found a name for.

Wren was dead now. He'd learned that much from the files, along with other things — things about the headwaters, about why the Company wanted the Kestrel's source kept quiet, about the word *evidence* appearing in memos with a frequency that made Callum's skin crawl. And Wren's daughter was upriver somewhere with Sable Delacroix, in a boat, in a canyon, while an armed expedition prepared to follow them in a stolen plane.

His plane. Stolen with paperwork, which was how the Company stole everything.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day walking the town, buying things he didn't need — rope, a tin of coffee, a newspaper three days old — and letting his feet carry him past the airfield fence every hour or so, counting guards, watching the light change on the Cessna's wings. Two men. Sometimes one slept. The fuel truck came at four. By five the tanks would be full, and by evening the plane would be gone, and whatever happened in the canyon after that would happen without him, and he would live the rest of his life knowing he'd had one chance to matter and had spent it being compensated at standard charter rates.

Unless.

He thought about it the way he thought about weather systems: coldly, completely, accepting what the data said. If he touched the plane, they'd know. If they knew, he wasn't just fired — he was finished. A pilot who sabotaged aircraft got blacklisted from every strip between here and the coast, and the Company had long arms and longer memories, and men like Voss did not forgive. It might be worse than blacklisting. He'd seen what the Company did to obstacles; everyone in these rivers had seen it, in the burned-out homesteads and the vanished claim-jumpers, in the careful silence that fell over settlements when a Meridian boat went by. He might simply disappear, and the river would keep him, and no one would ask.

And if he didn't touch the plane, he'd keep everything — the license, the routes, the clean hands — and lose the only part of himself worth keeping.

He bought the coffee anyway. He drank it standing at the harbor rail, watching the water work at the pilings, and let the decision settle in him the way a landing settles: wheels touching, weight transferring, done.

* * *

Dusk came down purple and cold. The guards had built a small fire in a oil drum near the hangar and were eating from tins, and the smell of it drifted across the apron — beans, tobacco, the sour sweetness of cheap rum. One of them had a harmonica and was making a poor job of it.

Callum walked up openly, hands visible, wearing the expression he'd perfected over years of dealing with customs men and claim agents: the amiable, slightly dim professional pilot, a man interested only in his machine and his next paycheck.

"They tell me you boys are taking her up tonight," he said. "I figured I'd show you her quirks. She's temperamental about the primer."

The taller guard looked him over. "We got our orders."

"Sure you do. But you don't want to be the guy who flooded her on the first try and cost the Company an hour of daylight. Voss strikes me as a man who keeps score." He smiled. It cost him something to smile. "Come on. Thirty seconds. I'll show you the trick with the mixture lever and leave you alone."

Greed for an easier job is universal. The guard hauled himself up, and Callum climbed onto the wing root, and for thirty seconds he was home — the smell of avgas and old fabric, the familiar sag of the seat, the instrument panel glowing faintly in the dusk like a constellation he'd navigated by his whole adult life. He showed them the primer trick. He showed them the mixture lever. He laughed at the right moments.

And while the shorter guard leaned in to watch, Callum's other hand, resting casually on the cowling's edge, found the fuel line access panel he could have unbolted blindfolded, and slipped the pin loose inside it — a quarter-turn, no more, just enough that vibration would work the fitting loose over an hour of running. Not enough to fail on the ground. Enough to fail beautifully, catastrophically, halfway to the first canyon, forcing a precautionary landing, a delay, a full inspection, a day lost. Maybe more, if the mechanics were thorough. Mechanics were rarely thorough for men they didn't respect.

Then he closed the panel, patted the cowling twice — goodbye, old girl — and dropped off the wing.

"She's all yours," he said. "Treat her gentle."

The harmonica started up again behind him as he walked away. Nobody applauded. Nobody ever would. That was the thing about sacrifice that nobody told you: it didn't feel like glory, it felt like closing a door on your own house and hearing the lock click, and walking, and not looking back because looking back was the one thing you couldn't afford.

* * *

He didn't go to his room. He went to the harbor, to the fish sheds, where a man named Okonkwo kept a skiff and owed Callum for a mercy flight two winters back — a son with a burst appendix, flown through weather that should have killed them both. Debts like that don't expire. Callum laid out what he needed without explaining why, and Okonkwo looked at him for a long moment with eyes that had seen every kind of trouble this river produced, and asked only one question:

"How far?"

"Upstream. Far as she'll take me."

Okonkwo nodded slowly. "They'll come for you."

"I know."

"And you're going anyway."

Callum looked out at the black water, at the far lights of the town, at the sky overhead going hard and clear and full of stars — flying weather, cold and stable, the kind of night when the engine hummed true and the world below was all silver river and dark forest. His chest ached with wanting it. He would probably never fly legally again. This might be the last night of the only life he'd ever been any good at.

"Wren pulled me out of the rapids once," he said. "Twenty years ago. I never paid it back."

Okonkwo pushed the skiff off the mud. "River keeps accounts," he said. "Go settle yours."

* * *

The Cessna lifted off Port Anselm at midnight, heavy with fuel, climbing into a sky so clear the stars seemed close enough to foul the propeller. Callum flew without lights, without radio — the silence in the rack behind his head was enormous, a missing tooth his tongue kept finding — navigating by the river itself, by the pale ribbon of it unwinding below him through the dark, by memory and moonlight and the oldest instruments there were.

Behind him, in some hangar or office, the Company would be discovering things. The empty berth at the fish shed. The missing skiff. And tomorrow, or the day after, when their expedition plane's engine began to stutter over the gorge and the pilot's voice went tight with confusion, someone would pull the fuel line apart and find the loosened fitting, and find the pin, and understand. Voss would understand. Voss would put Callum's name at the top of a list, and the list would not be a list of debts.

One day. Maybe two. That was what he'd bought them — the women in the canyon, the dead man's evidence, whatever truth was waiting at the source. One day bought with everything he had.

He checked his watch against the riverbend below, corrected his heading, and flew on into the dark, alone with the hum of the engine and the silence where the radio used to be, and found — to his surprise, and it would be the only surprise of the night — that the silence suited him fine.

Ahead, the first canyon opened its mouth.
