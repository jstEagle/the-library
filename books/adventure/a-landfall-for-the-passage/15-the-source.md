## 15. The Source

The canyon had been narrowing for two days, and on the third morning it simply ended.

Ines felt it before she saw it — a change in the light, the way the walls fell back and the air opened like a held breath released. The river, which had spent the whole journey fighting them, shoving gravel bars into their path and stealing their depth gauge by inches a day, went suddenly quiet. They rounded one last shoulder of red stone and the water spread out beneath them, glass-still, so clear that Ines could count the pebbles forty feet down.

"God," Sable said behind her, and didn't finish the sentence.

They had come up through country that looked like the inside of a drum — layered sandstone, dry washes, the bones of things that hadn't made it through the last drought. Ines's ankle was strapped and swollen inside her boot; her hands were raw from three portages around rapids that Wren's journals called *the Staircase*, and she had stopped crying somewhere past the second one because tears were salt she couldn't afford to lose. But now they stood in the bow of the battered boat with the current gone slack beneath them, and ahead of them rose a wall of green that had no business existing in this country.

Trees. Real ones, not the scrub and cottonwood of the lower river but something older — cedars and alders crowding down to the waterline, ferns spilling from seeps in the rock, moss hanging off everything like the valley had been dipped in velvet. The air was ten degrees cooler. It smelled wet and green and mineral, like a cellar full of spring.

"The drowned valley," Ines said. Her voice came out hoarse. "It's real."

Her father had written about it in the last journal — the one whose pages she'd read by headlamp while Sable slept, her swollen ankle propped on the gear bags, reading her father's handwriting for the first time as anything other than an accusation. *The river doesn't begin,* he'd written. *It remembers.* She'd thought it was the poetry of an old man who'd spent too long alone. Now she understood it was survey notes.

Sable cut the engine and let them drift. The silence that came down over the boat was enormous — not empty silence but layered silence, water moving under water, wind in the high cedars, somewhere above them the thin silver sound of falling water that never stopped.

"There." Sable pointed with her chin.

At the head of the basin, where the green wall met the rock, the cliff face split open. Water came out of it in a dozen ribbons — not a waterfall pouring down but a spring exhaling outward, braided sheets of it sliding over stone darkened black with centuries of moss, gathering, braiding again, feeding the pool below. The basin itself was maybe four hundred meters across, ringed by cliffs except where the river exited south through the narrow throat of the canyon they'd just climbed. And the water — Ines had mapped a hundred rivers, drawn their depths from soundings and guesswork, and she had never seen water like this. It was blue the way the sky is blue at altitude: not a color laid on top but a color coming from underneath, from depth without bottom.

She got out the field notebook without deciding to. Her hand was shaking. She wrote the date, the coordinates she'd been carrying in her head since the canyon mouth, and then, because the notebook demanded honesty: *Source confirmed. Spring-fed basin, approx. 400m diameter. Outflow single channel, south.*

Underneath, smaller: *He found it. Twenty years, and he found it.*

* * *

They beached the boat on a shelf of pale stone at the basin's eastern edge, where an old slide had made a ramp of talus down to the waterline. Sable insisted on scouting first — she'd been doing that more since the canyon, checking back-trail, listening at night when she thought Ines was asleep — and Ines let her, because arguing cost energy she needed for walking.

While Sable climbed the talus to look south down the throat of the canyon, Ines stood at the waterline and let herself feel it, just for a minute. Awe. Her whole professional life had been built on the idea that every place could be known, measured, put on paper. But this place resisted paper. The light came down the cliff face in shafts and broke apart on the water. The spring roared softly, endlessly, a sound like the inside of a shell held to the ear. Somewhere in the cedars a bird she couldn't name was calling three notes, over and over, patient as a clock.

*Papa stood here.* The thought arrived whole, unbidden, and buckled her knees a little. He had stood on this stone, or stone like it, and looked at this water, and instead of coming home to tell her about it he had buried what he knew and kept walking, year after year, until the walking killed him. She pressed her palm flat against the wet rock and imagined his hand there first, twenty years ago, thirty. The rock was cold. That was all she could know for certain, and it was enough.

Sable came back down the talus fast, loose stones hissing under her boots.

"Nothing on the trail," she said. "Yet. But Ines — the water level."

"What about it?"

"Look at the old high-water mark." She pointed to a band of bleached stone along the cliff base, a meter and a half above the current surface, fringed with dead moss. "This basin drops in the dry season. Everything you see underwater right now is going to be exposed in another few weeks. Which means whatever your father hid here, he hid it to be found in the dry season — or hidden well enough to survive being found."

Ines stared at the water. The clarity that had seemed like a gift now looked like something else: an invitation. She could see the bottom shelf of rock running out from the talus slope, descending in steps into the blue. On the second step, maybe five meters down, there was a shape. Rectangular. Wrong.

"That," she said quietly. "That's not geology."

* * *

Wren's final journal had been maddeningly oblique about the cache itself — pages of tide-tables and depth calculations and one line, circled twice, that had kept Ines awake half the night: *Below the second shelf, against the north face. Sealed in '61. If the river keeps it, the river can give it up.* Now, lying on her stomach on the warm stone with her face a handspan above the water, she could see why he'd trusted the river to keep it. The shape was a steel box, maybe a meter long, bolted — she could make out the bolt heads even through the distortion — directly to the rock shelf, wedged under a natural lip of stone. The Company's own flood records would show this basin as seasonal; anyone looking for a cache would dig on land. Nobody would dive in water this cold, this remote, in a canyon that took nine days to reach by boat.

Nobody except a cartographer's daughter with something to prove and a pilot with nothing left to lose.

"It's there," Ines said. She sat up. Her heart was going hard. "Steel box, bolted to the second shelf. Papa's notes say the seal was rated for twenty years. It's been sixty-three."

"So we don't know what's inside until we open it." Sable crouched beside her, squinting down through the water. "And we don't know if it'll open at all."

"We have to try."

"I know we have to try." Sable's voice was flat, careful, the voice she used when she was managing fear. "I'm saying we try fast. We've been lucky — no smoke on our back trail, no aircraft since the Staircase. But luck isn't a plan. We dive, we get the box up, we open it, we photograph everything, and then we decide how much of it travels with us and how much stays hidden. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Then, because fairness demanded it: "You don't have to come in the water. Your shoulder—"

"My shoulder's fine." It wasn't; Ines had watched her favor it for six days. "But I grew up swimming in irrigation ditches colder than this, and you've got one good ankle. I go down, you spot me from above and mind the line."

They rigged it from the climbing rope in the gear — Sable had packed rope, Ines had learned, the way other people packed cigarettes, out of some old habit of expecting disaster. Sable stripped to her shirt, tested the cold with one foot, and swore with feeling. Then she filled her lungs and went under.

Ines lay on the shelf and paid out the line, watching the pale shape of her descend through the blue. Five meters. Seven. The water stole detail with every meter; Sable became a suggestion, then a shadow, then a smudge of motion against the darker rectangle of the box. Ines counted seconds. At ninety, the line went taut — a pull, a pause, two sharp pulls. The agreed signal: *found it, working on it.*

At four minutes, Ines started counting her own heartbeat. At six minutes, the line jerked once, hard, and she hauled before she could think, hand over hand, and Sable broke the surface gasping, hair plastered to her skull, eyes streaming.

"Cold," she managed. "God, it's — okay. Okay." She hung onto the shelf edge, breathing in great whoops. "Bolts are rusted through on two corners. I got a pry bar under the lid. It moved." A wild, exhausted grin. "Ines. It *moved.*"

"Then why did you come up?"

"Because my hands stopped working and I nearly dropped the pry bar down a hole I can't reach." She shook water out of her ears. "Your turn. You're lighter, you'll use less air, and it's your father's box. I think—" She paused, and something gentler moved through her face, there and gone. "I think it should be you."

* * *

The cold took her breath before the water did.

Ines had dived in lakes, in the sea off Port Anselm's grey harbor, but nothing like this — this was water that had been underground, water with no memory of sun, and it closed around her like a fist. She forced herself down the steps of the shelf, following the rope, ears screaming, vision tunneling. At the second shelf the bottom leveled off and there it was, close enough now to touch: a steel ammunition case, olive drab gone black with sixty years of silt, stenciled lettering almost legible — *MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED* — which meant her father had stolen the box from the Company itself, which meant he'd had access, which meant—

No time. She found the pry bar where Sable had wedged it, set her feet against the rock, and pulled.

The lid groaned. Rust flaked off in clouds. She pulled again, harder, and something gave with a shriek she felt in her teeth, and the lid came up ten centimeters and stuck. Through the gap she could see paper. Bundles of it, wrapped in oilcloth, and the corner of a photograph — faces, small and white in the gloom.

She got her fingers into the gap and heaved, and the lid tore free the rest of the way, and the oilcloth bundles shifted, and one of them began to rise — buoyant, eager after six decades — and she caught it against the shelf with both hands and held it there, kneeling on the bottom of the source of the Kestrel River, holding her father's evidence in the dark like a held breath.

She took one bundle. Just one — the smallest, the one already trying to leave. Then she kicked for the light.

* * *

They built a fire on the talus shelf, out of the wind, and dried the oilcloth wrapper over it without opening the contents, because Sable said — reasonably, infuriatingly — that wet paper torn apart is paper destroyed. So they waited, watching steam rise off the cloth, and Ines sat with her arms around her knees and tried to be patient and failed completely.

When the wrapper was dry enough, she peeled it back.

Photographs. Eleven of them, black and white, curled at the edges but intact. The first showed a valley — this valley, she realized, but different: wider, greener, with structures in it. Buildings. Fences. A church with a bell tower. The second photograph was the same valley from higher ground, and there were people in it, dozens, standing in rows outside long low buildings, and Ines's stomach began to turn before her mind caught up, because the buildings weren't houses. She'd seen layouts like that in archives. Barracks. Company labor camps — the kind Meridian's histories called *settlements* and its ledgers called *workforce housing*.

The third photograph was taken after.

She didn't want to describe it, even to herself. Smoke still standing over the valley in columns. The barracks burned to foundations. Figures in the foreground wearing Company uniforms, some of them holding things she refused to name, and in the corner of the frame, half-turned toward the camera, a man whose face she recognized because she'd spent her childhood studying photographs of him: Elias Wren, thirty years younger, hollow-eyed, holding a camera of his own.

"He was there," she whispered. "He wasn't documenting it afterward. He was *there*. He worked for them."

Sable said nothing. When Ines glanced over, the pilot was staring at the photograph with an expression Ines had seen once before, at the hangar fire, and had never been able to name. It wasn't shock. It was recognition.

"Sable."

"We should check the payroll pages," Sable said. Her voice was very controlled. "Names. Dates. Before the light goes."

Ines unfolded the typed sheets bound behind the photographs. Payroll abstracts, quarterly, 1961. Names, wages, deductions — and annotations in a clerk's hand. *Hazard duty supplement. Discretionary disbursement — special operations, Kestrel source. Do not enter in general ledger.* Page after page of men being paid, by name, for what the photographs showed. And at the bottom of the final page, in the authorization block, a signature she didn't recognize — but the printed title above it she did, because it still existed, because the man who held that title now sat in a glass office in Port Anselm and endowed scholarships and owned the newspaper.

The Company hadn't covered up the massacre. The Company had *paid* for it, on the record, and the record had been sealed in a steel box and sunk in the river, and everyone who signed those pages had gone on to run the country.

"They're all dead by now," Ines said. "Most of them."

"The company isn't." Sable fed a stick to the fire. "Companies live longer than people, Ines. That's the whole trick of them."

Ines looked at the photographs again, at her father's young face turned guiltily toward the camera, and understood the last thing she needed to understand: he hadn't hidden the evidence to protect himself. He'd hidden it because he'd tried, once, to bring it out — and someone had died for it, or he'd believed they would, and so he'd done the only thing left. He'd given it to the river and spent twenty years standing guard.

"We copy everything," she said. "Tonight. Every page, every photo, two copies, and the originals never travel together again."

"Agreed." Sable was already pulling out the camera. "We work till dark, then—"

She stopped. Her head came up, sharp, the way a bird dog lifts.

Ines heard it a half-second later: engines. Not aircraft — boats. More than one. Low, laboring, coming up the throat of the canyon from the south, from the direction of everything they'd left behind.

Sable was on her feet, kicking dirt over the fire, moving with a speed that made her bad shoulder irrelevant. "Kill the smoke. Get the tin — the metal one, everything goes in it, now." She scrambled up the talus to the overlook, and Ines, stuffing photographs into the waterproof case with hands that would not stop shaking, heard her say it aloud, quietly, to no one:

"No. No, no, no — they're early. The fuel sabotage should have bought us a *day*—"

Ines reached the top of the talus in time to see it herself. Far below, at the mouth of the canyon where the river ran brown and shallow over the gravel bars they'd cursed three days ago, three boats were coming upstream. Aluminum hulls, outboard engines, men in them — eight, ten, too many to count at this distance, and glinting among them, unmistakable even in the failing light, the long dull gleam of rifle barrels.

Behind them, impossibly, a floatplane was circling down toward the last wide bend, settling toward the water with the stiff, wounded care of a plane flying on fumes. Even at this distance Ines knew the silhouette. Everyone on the river knew it.

"Callum," she breathed.

Or Callum's plane, flown by someone else.

The surveyor's voice from Port Anselm came back to her then, mild and reasonable over the harbor bar: *Maps are just promises, Miss Wren. The question is always, promised to whom?*

The boats were an hour behind them. Maybe less. The light was dying, the box was open, and the only way out of the basin was the way they'd come in — straight through the line of men coming to erase them.

Sable turned from the overlook, and her face in the last of the sun was calm in the way of a woman who has already finished being afraid and moved on to arithmetic.

"Ines," she said. "How fast can you run on that ankle?"
