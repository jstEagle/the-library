## 16. Standoff at the Spring

The basin announced itself before we saw it — a change in the sound of the water, the roar of the falls behind us thinning into something older and quieter, like a held breath. Then the canyon walls fell away on both sides and there it was: a bowl of green water cradled in stone, fed by springs that rose from the rock itself in silver threads, so clear that forty feet down I could see the pale bones of drowned trees standing where the valley had flooded centuries ago. Or not centuries. That was the question now.

Ines stood in the bow with her father's map held open against the wind, and I watched her face do what faces do when a drawing becomes a place. Her lips moved, tracing contour lines that were suddenly real ridgelines. Twenty years of her father's handwriting, and here was the sentence he'd been writing all along.

"That's it," she said. Not a question. "That's the spring."

I brought us along the eastern shore, where a shelf of flat rock ran out into the water like a natural dock, and killed the engine. The silence that followed was so complete I could hear the springs hissing where they entered the lake, a sound like whispering from under the world.

We should have felt triumphant. For about ninety seconds, we did. Ines laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound — and pressed both hands flat against the map as if she could feel her father's pulse through the paper. I sat in the stern with my hands still on the throttle and let myself believe, briefly, that we had outrun them. That the dry season's fist had closed too late. That the canyon walls we'd portaged past had swallowed our trail.

Then I heard the engines.

Not one. Two. Low and steady, coming up through the gorge below the falls, and I knew that sound the way you know a voice in a dark room. Long-shaft outboards, the kind the Meridian Company ran on its survey boats — heavy, torquey, built for shallow water and bad intentions. They'd found the deep channel below the waterfall. They'd come up while we were hauling our boat over stone, and they'd been patient, and now they were here.

"Ines." My voice came out flat and calm, which surprised me. Ten years of flying had taught me that panic is a luxury you purchase after the emergency, never during. "Get the tin ready."

She turned, and I watched the joy drain out of her face by degrees, replaced by something harder. She'd grown up on this river's stories; she knew what engines meant as well as I did.

"How many?"

"Two boats, at least. Maybe more walking the banks." I scanned the shoreline. The shelf of rock we'd landed on backed up against a wall of granite maybe thirty feet high, seamed with ledges, a few stunted pines clinging in the cracks. Not much cover. Not none. "How long to dive for the cache?"

"Depends how deep." She was already pulling the mask and fins from the dry bag, hands moving fast but not frantic. "Wren's notes said the cache sits below the old waterline. If the trees are any measure—" she nodded at the drowned forest, its dead branches breaking the surface like the fingers of buried men— "it's twenty-five, thirty feet down. Cold. Dark. Give me ten minutes if I find it fast. Thirty if I don't."

"We may not have ten minutes."

"Then I'd better be lucky." She looked at me then, and there was no fear in her face, only a fierce, focused anger that I recognized because I'd been carrying its twin for a decade. "Sable. Whatever happens up here — whatever they offer you — remember what's in that water. Remember who died keeping it safe."

"I know who died keeping it safe," I said. "I flew some of them."

She held my eyes a moment longer than was comfortable, which was Ines's way, and then she slid over the side without a splash, and the green water took her, and I was alone on the rock with two engines getting louder and a choice I could already feel arriving like weather on the horizon.

* * *

They came around the last bend of the gorge at 4:17 in the afternoon — I know because I checked my watch, an old habit, marking time against things you can't control. Two aluminum survey boats, gunmetal gray, riding low with gear and men. Four in the lead boat, three in the second. The man standing in the bow of the first was Voss.

He'd changed since Port Anselm. The town clothes were gone, traded for canvas and boots, but the manner was identical: that unhurried, reasonable posture of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice because everyone else eventually lowers theirs. He carried no weapon that I could see, which frightened me more than rifles would have. Rifles mean fear. Voss didn't look afraid.

"Sable Delacroix," he called across the water, and the basin carried his voice cleanly off the stone walls, so that it seemed to come from everywhere. "You've made excellent time. I confess I underestimated the river."

I stayed crouched beside the boat, one hand on the mooring line, and said nothing. Behind me, thirty feet down, Ines was swimming toward the truth.

"You're wondering how we found you." He raised a hand and his boats came alongside the far shore, maybe sixty yards of open water between us. Close enough for rifles. Far enough for conversation. He'd chosen the distance deliberately; everything about the man was deliberate. "Your guide. The one who abandoned you at the first canyon. He sold your route downstream for fuel and a ride out. Men are predictable that way — not evil, just portable." A small smile. "Though I'll admit the river did most of the work. There are only so many ways upstream this late in the season, and only one person stubborn enough to take them."

Still I said nothing. Every second I kept him talking was a second Ines spent underwater.

Voss studied me across the water with the patience of a man reading a gauge. "You're not going to ask me how I know your name. That's interesting. Most people would ask." He tilted his head. "Unless you already suspect."

My stomach went cold in a way the mountain water hadn't managed. "You've got the wrong pilot," I said. "I fly medicine runs."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "And before that? Before Halfway Bend, before the Kestrel settlements, before the decade of one-season tenancies?" He let the silence stretch. "Eleven years ago there was a village called San Rafael de las Nubes, up on the Company concession north of here. It burned. Officially, squatters' cooking fire. Unofficially—" he spread his hands, a gesture of terrible openness— "well. You would know better than I, Miss Delacroix. You flew the men who did it. Three flights. We have the manifests. We have your signature."

The basin was very quiet. Somewhere beneath the surface, a spring hissed its endless whisper. I thought about lying. I thought about how good I'd gotten at lying, how it had become less a skill than a metabolism, how I could construct a false self the way Callum could rebuild a carburetor — quickly, competently, from parts on hand.

But Voss had the manifests. And a man like Voss does not show you his cards unless he's holding the whole deck.

"What do you want," I said.

"Now we're having a conversation." He settled himself on the gunwale of his boat, comfortable, unhurried. "Here is the situation as I understand it. Your friend is diving for a tin box that contains documents — photographs, payrolls, correspondence — relating to an incident the Company considers closed. Those documents, if they surface, will cause a great deal of noise. Lawsuits. Indictments, possibly. Certain careers ending. Certain very powerful people becoming very motivated." He paused. "None of which needs to happen."

"And why wouldn't it?"

"Because you're going to help me prevent it." He said it the way you'd say *the mail arrives at noon*. No threat in it. That was the horror of him — the absolute absence of threat. "Here's my offer, and I want you to hear all of it before you answer. One: full immunity. Not the informal kind — the documented kind. Signed by people whose signatures end investigations. Everything you flew for the Company, everything you knew or suspected about San Rafael, sealed forever. You walk out of this basin a woman with no past at all. Isn't that what you've wanted? Ten years of running, and I'm offering you the one thing running never gave you."

The words went into me like cold water into a wound. Because he was right. God help me, he was right. Every season I'd broken camp, every settlement I'd left before anyone could love me enough to ask questions, every night I'd slept in the Otter's shadow instead of in a house with a door someone might knock on — all of it had been a flight from exactly this moment, from exactly this name spoken aloud, and here was a man offering to make the flight unnecessary. To hand me a clean slate. To let me stop.

"Two," Voss continued, counting on his fingers with gentle irony, "you keep your license. In fact, the Company would prefer you keep flying. These routes need reliable pilots, and frankly, Miss Delacroix, you're the best I've seen work this country. We'd rather have you than fight you. Three: your friend walks away too. Ms. Wren boards a plane tomorrow with no charges, no questions, and a generous research grant if she wants one — the Company funds cartography, we always have. She can spend the rest of her life mapping rivers nobody will burn villages over."

"And the tin?"

"The tin stays in the water. Or comes up and goes into my boat. Either way, it stops existing. And Elias Wren's twenty-year obsession dies with it, quietly, the way he should have." He shrugged. "One old man's ghost versus three living people's futures. I genuinely don't think that's a hard equation."

* * *

I looked at the water where Ines had gone down and tried to calculate what she'd found, whether she was at the cache yet, whether she could hear us — sound carries strangely underwater, I knew that much, voices from above arriving muffled and enormous, like gods arguing.

And I thought about San Rafael de las Nubes.

I'd told Ines the shape of it on the portage, in fragments, the way you tell a story you've rehearsed so often it's gone smooth as river stone. But the shape isn't the thing. The thing is the smell. Kerosene and wet ash and something underneath that I have never been able to name and have smelled, once or twice since, in ordinary places — a burnt pot on a stove, a brush fire by a landing strip — and each time my whole body has stopped as if shot. The thing is the manifest in my own hand, three lines of cargo listed as *survey personnel, 6*, when I had counted seven men boarding and noticed the jerricans were heavier than survey equipment should be and had signed anyway, because the fee was triple rate and because asking questions, I told myself then, wasn't a pilot's job.

I was twenty-four years old. That's the fact I return to, the one I use like a shield, and it has never once worked. Twenty-four is old enough to count jerricans.

Voss watched me thinking. He was good at watching; it was clearly his profession, the way flying was mine. And I understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that he had done this before — stood at some other basin, some other crossroads, and made this same reasonable offer to some other cornered person, and that most of them had taken it, and that was how the Company had lasted a hundred years. Not through violence, mostly. Through offers. Through making the decent thing expensive and the cheap thing easy, one transaction at a time, until the whole country was complicit and quiet.

"Miss Delacroix." His voice softened, and somehow that was worse. "I want to say something to you, pilot to manager, off the record. What happened at San Rafael was not your doing. You were a young woman ferrying freight. The men who made the decision are the men whose signatures I'm offering you. There is a version of justice in which you are a witness, not a defendant — and that version requires those documents to stay submerged. Help me close this chapter, and you are not the woman who flew the burners anymore. You're the woman who helped bury it. It's a better story. People live happily in better stories."

And there it was. My whole life distilled into one offer, floating on green water in a hidden valley: absolution for amnesia. A clean license and a clean conscience, purchased with a tin box and a dead man's truth. All I had to do was keep running — just once more, just in a new direction, just with better shoes.

Ines surfaced.

She came up thirty feet out, silent as an otter, only her head and the strap of the dry bag crossing her chest, and I saw her eyes go from me to Voss to the boats, reading the geometry of it instantly. She didn't call out. She didn't signal. She trod water and waited, letting me finish my arithmetic in public, trusting me — or testing me — with the same ferocious stillness she'd shown on the portage, when I'd confessed and she hadn't forgiven me and hadn't left.

Elias Wren had spent twenty years documenting a massacre because he couldn't carry the weight of knowing and saying nothing. I had spent ten years running because I couldn't carry the weight of what I'd done. And Voss was offering to dissolve both weights into paperwork.

"Take your time," Voss said kindly.

"No," I said.

It came out smaller than I meant it to. So I said it again, louder, and the basin took it up and threw it against the granite walls until the whole valley was saying it back to me.

"No deal."

Voss sighed — genuinely, I think, with something like regret — and straightened off the gunwale. "I was afraid you'd say that. For what it's worth, I respect it. It's the wrong choice, but it's a principled one, and principle is rare out here." He lifted two fingers, a small unhurried gesture, and in the second boat three rifles came up off the floorboards and pointed at the rock where I crouched. "Ms. Wren! I know you can hear me. Bring up the tin, or we start shooting, and the river keeps what it keeps."

Behind me, Ines took one long breath and went under.

And that — that single deliberate dive, choosing the water over the guns, choosing her father over her life — broke something loose in me that ten years of running had kept rusted shut. I put my hand on the mooring line and started, calmly, methodically, to plan.

Because I'd finally understood what Voss never had. He thought he was offering me a way to stop running. But running was never the disease. Running was the symptom. The disease was believing I deserved nowhere.

The first rifle shot cracked across the basin, and the springs whispered on, indifferent, keeping their secret a little longer.
