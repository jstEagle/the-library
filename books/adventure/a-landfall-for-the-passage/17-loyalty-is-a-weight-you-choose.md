## 17. Loyalty Is a Weight You Choose

The water at the source was colder than grief.

Ines had known that going in — she'd felt it through her boots when she knelt at the basin's edge, felt it in the way the air above the spring hung thin and mineral-bright — but knowing was nothing against the fact of it. The cold climbed her arms like a living thing, closed over her ears, and the world above became a trembling ceiling of light where two figures stood at the rim of the drowned valley: Sable, small and sharp-edged against the sky, and the surveyor, Voss, with his men fanned out along the gravel shelf behind him.

She kicked down.

The basin was deeper than it looked from above, which was the whole trick of it, she supposed. From the rim you saw a spring-fed pool perhaps thirty meters across, blue-green and deceptively shallow near the margins, ringed by granite shoulders still streaked with last winter's snowmelt. But beneath the surface the valley dropped away — a drowned throat of rock where the Kestrel began not as a river but as a seep, a hundred small veins of water weeping from the stone, gathering themselves in silence before they found the courage to run.

Her father's journals had described it in his careful, cramped hand: *the spring sits in a cup of granite; what the Company buried there, the water keeps.* Twenty years he'd circled this place. Twenty years of letters home that said nothing and everything, of maps drawn with one route deliberately wrong, of a man teaching himself patience the way other men taught themselves to drink.

Ines swam down along the seam he'd marked, fingers trailing the rock face, counting ledges the way his journal counted them. The cold pressed in until her skull ached. Her lungs began their slow argument. And then — there. A shelf of stone below the waterline, screened by a curtain of pale algae, and set into it, exactly where Elias Wren had sworn it would be, a tin box the size of a bread loaf, its galvanized skin furred white with mineral deposit, its seal wrapped in tarred canvas and wired shut.

He had built it to last. He had built it to be found only by someone willing to go into the cold after it.

*That's the test,* Ines thought wildly, hauling at the wires, nails scrabbling for purchase on wet canvas. *That was always the test.*

The wire gave on the third try. The tin came free of its niche with a grinding shrug of stone, lighter than she expected, heavier than she could afford it to be. Her lungs were screaming now. She kicked for the light.

---

She broke the surface gasping, and the first thing she heard was Voss's voice, smooth as poured concrete.

"—a generous offer, Ms. Delacroix. I won't make it twice."

Ines trod water at the basin's edge, the tin clutched to her chest, and took stock the way her father had taught her to take stock of any terrain: quickly, honestly, without flattery. Six men besides Voss. Two rifles slung, four carried loose but ready. They held the eastern shelf, between Sable and the boat. Their own boats were pulled up below the falls — she'd heard the engines ten minutes ago, cutting across the canyon's echo, and had gone into the water knowing exactly what it meant.

Sable stood three meters from Voss, arms crossed, soaked to the thigh from wading out to meet him. Her hair was plastered flat. Her chin was up. Ines had known her barely a season of river-days now, and still she could read the set of those shoulders the way she read contour lines: everything drawn taut around something being held very still.

"You know who I am," Sable said. It wasn't a question.

"I've known since Port Anselm." Voss smiled with the bottom half of his face only. "Sable Delacroix. Pilot of record on the Corazal run, eleven years ago. The flight manifests are very thorough, Ms. Delacroix. You carried Company personnel and Company cargo to a village called Santa Rosa, and three days later Santa Rosa burned. There were inquiries. There were always inquiries. Nothing ever came of them, because nothing ever does." He spread his hands, a gesture of terrible reasonableness. "You were never charged. You were never even named. Do you know why? Because the Company protects its own. We have protected you for eleven years, whether you knew it or not. All we ask in return is the tin your friend is holding, and your silence — which, I note, you've already practiced for a decade."

The canyon held its breath. Water dripped somewhere. Snowmelt ticked off the granite.

Ines saw it hit Sable — saw the offer land the way a bullet lands, all at once, before the pain arrives. Eleven years of running, distilled into a single sentence: *you can stop now, and it costs you nothing but the truth.* Ines thought of her own years of maps that circled her father's life without ever entering it, and understood, with a lurch, that Voss had chosen his weapon perfectly. He wasn't threatening them. He was offering them absolution.

"Immunity," Sable said slowly.

"Immunity, reinstatement, whatever routes you want. The Company forgets easily. It's one of our finest qualities."

"And the people in the tin," Sable said. "The ones in the photographs. The payrolls. The ones your Company massacred at this spring before I was old enough to fly. Do they get to forget too?"

"They're dead, Ms. Delacroix. Forgetting is the one thing they do exceptionally well."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Ines, treading water, felt hope do something shameful and small inside her — felt it calculate, the way fear calculates, the cost of survival against the cost of principle. She hated herself for it. She hated Voss for building the arithmetic.

Then Sable laughed. It was not a kind sound.

"You know what my mistake was, eleven years ago?" she said. "It wasn't flying that cargo. I didn't know what was in the crates. That's the truth, and it's worth nothing, because I didn't ask. I saw the payment and I didn't ask. And every year since, I've told myself the same story — that I ran to stay free. Free." She shook her head, water flicking from her jaw. "I ran because staying meant looking at what I'd done. Every settlement I served, every medicine run, every kid I flew out with a fever — I let myself believe it balanced the ledger. It doesn't. Ledgers don't balance. They just wait." She looked at the tin in Ines's arms, then back at Voss. "You're offering me a clean page. But I finally understand that the page was never dirty. The ledger was. And the only thing that settles it is the truth coming out — all of it, mine included."

"So that's refusal."

"That's refusal."

Voss sighed, and the sigh was worse than the smile had been. He turned his head slightly, not even looking at his men, and said, almost gently:

"Then we do this the old way. Fire when—"

"*No!*"

Ines's voice cracked across the basin, and she hauled herself up onto the granite shelf with the tin locked in both arms, water sheeting off her, standing between the rifles and Sable with no plan whatsoever except the pure animal conviction that the evidence would not go back under the water while she was upright to defend it.

"Ines, get down—" Sable started.

"He killed my father." Ines's voice shook and she let it. "Maybe not with his hands. Maybe with paperwork and twenty years of pressure and a fire in a hangar he'll call electrical. But Elias Wren died carrying this, and I will not—"

The first rifle came up.

And that was when the sky tore open.

---

It was an engine sound first — wrong, ragged, a mechanical animal in its death throes — and every head in the basin turned toward the gorge mouth as Callum Reyes's plane came around the shoulder of the cliff far too low, far too slow, trailing a whisper of gray smoke and flying on what Ines would later learn was less than five minutes of fuel scavenged from a sabotaged drum.

He was trying for the water. Even then, even dying, he was trying for the water — the deep channel below the falls where a hull might survive what rocks would not. But the engine chose that moment to quit entirely, and the Otter — not Sable's Otter, but its battered twin, the one the Company had confiscated and Callum had stolen back at the cost of everything — dropped the last forty meters like a stone with opinions.

It hit the pool fifty meters from where Ines stood, port wing catching first, and the world became noise. Spray went up in a white wall. The fuselage slewed, dug in, cartwheeled once with a shriek of tearing aluminum, and came to rest canted against the gravel bar in the middle of the basin, half-sunk, smoking, its tail jutting toward the sky like a broken finger pointing at heaven.

Silence. The ringing kind, the kind that lives inside the ears.

Then Callum's head appeared over the floating wing, streaming blood from a cut above his eyebrow, and he looked directly at Voss and his six rifles, and grinned.

"Sorry about the landing," he called. "Company training. They never covered water."

"Reyes." Voss's voice had lost its concrete smoothness. "You were grounded."

"Yeah, well." Callum hauled himself up onto the wing, swaying, and Ines saw him plant his feet deliberately on the lowest point of the airframe — saw, with a cartographer's eye for load and leverage, what he was doing. "Grounded, stripped, fired, and probably arrested. So I figured I'd come up here and make it official." He patted the wing beside him. "You want to know why your fuel drums at the strip were contaminated? Why your expedition lost a full day? Me. Every hour I stole from you, these two spent getting here. Best work I've ever done."

One of the riflemen shifted his aim from Sable to Callum, and Ines moved without deciding to — grabbed Sable's sleeve, and the tin, and pulled them both down behind the granite shoulder at the basin's rim, into the seam of rock where the spring itself wept out of the mountain, cold water running over their hands.

"Callum—" Sable breathed.

"Is buying us seconds," Ines said. "Look."

Because that was the thing — the wreck was not just wreckage. It sat squarely in the channel between Voss's position on the eastern shelf and the only line of approach to the western rim where the women crouched. To reach them, Voss's men would have to either ford the outflow current or pick their way around the sinking plane, and Callum, bleeding and grinning on the wing, had positioned himself precisely so that any rifleman crossing that gravel bar would have to pass within arm's reach of a desperate pilot with nothing left to lose.

Three loyalties, Ines thought, pressing her back to the cold stone. Hers to a dead father's truth. Sable's to the people who had covered for her, and to the truth she owed them in return. Callum's — Callum's to a debt twenty years old, to a prospector who had once talked a failing engine through the Kestrel rapids, paid at last in the only coin he had left: himself, thrown into the gap.

"Delacroix!" Voss's voice again, stripped of charm now, raw with fury. "Last chance. Give me the tin and Reyes lives."

From the wing, Callum laughed — actually laughed, high and wild, the laugh of a man who has already fallen and found it liberating. "Voss, you beautiful idiot. I'm sitting on a sinking airplane between six rifles and a waterfall. What exactly are you threatening me *with?*"

But Ines had stopped listening. Because her hands, wrapped around the tin, had found the tarred canvas wrapping, and beneath it, wired to the lid, a second object her numb fingers were only now identifying: a small brass cylinder, military-stamped, with a pull-ring and a paper tag gone soft with decades underwater.

Her father's handwriting on the tag, faded but legible:

*For whoever finds this — if they come for you, light it. The smoke carries. Everything downstream sees.*

A flare. Twenty years old. Elias Wren had armed his evidence.

Ines looked at Sable. Sable looked at the flare, then at the wreck, then at the sky above the western rim — measuring wind, measuring distance, doing pilot's math with her eyes — and something passed between them that needed no words at all.

Below them, the first rifleman stepped onto the gravel bar, and Callum Reyes spread his arms wide against the smoke, and waited to see what they were made of.
