## 18. The Long Glide Down

The basin held its breath around us.

That was the first thing I understood, standing at the water's edge with the tin dripping in Ines's arms: the headwaters were a bowl, and we were at the bottom of it. Walls of granite rose on three sides, green-shouldered, streaked with the white scars of old slides. The spring itself came up through the rock like something exhaled — cold, glass-clear, feeding a pool that spilled over a lip of stone into the drowned valley below. Beautiful, if you had the leisure to be beautiful at. I didn't.

"You could take off from here," Ines said, but her voice said *please tell me I'm wrong* before I could.

"From the pool? No." I turned a slow circle, reading the walls the way I read weather. "Even if the water were long enough — and it isn't — there's no run-out. You'd climb out of here into a cliff face with maybe two hundred feet of margin. Maybe." I looked at her. "And Callum's plane is kindling on the far shore."

She set the tin down between us, careful as a sleeping child. Water sheeted off its lid. Somewhere below us — downriver, past the falls, past the portage of ghosts — engines were working their way upstream against the current, and every one of them was coming for this.

"So we don't fly," she said.

"We walk it down."

* * *

We spent ten minutes that felt like an hour deciding what to carry.

The journals went into a dry bag with the map, wrapped in oilcloth from Wren's own camp stores — twenty years old and still sound, because the man had planned for water the way other men planned for breakfast. The tin itself was the problem. It was the size of a small suitcase, sealed with solder along every seam, and heavy as sin. Photographs, payrolls, ledgers, a Company stamp pressed into wax that hadn't been broken since before either of us was born. Proof. Weight. The same thing, out here.

"Rope harness," I said. "We take turns. Flat stretches you drag it, rapids we both lift."

Ines was already cutting cordage with her belt knife. Her ankle was strapped tight — I'd bound it that morning above the falls, and she'd walked on it all day without one word of complaint, which told me more about her than any of our conversations had. "How far to deep water?"

"Six miles, maybe seven, if the channel holds. Past the second bend there's a stretch Wren marked as navigable even in dry season. If we can get the tin into moving water—" I stopped myself. "If we can get *us* into moving water, we can float it down to Halfway Bend."

"And then?"

"Then Halfway Bend does what Halfway Bend does." I cinched the last knot. "It keeps things alive."

She looked at me then — really looked, the way she'd looked at her father's handwriting on that first night in Port Anselm, like she was reading a language she'd half forgotten. "You used to say you'd never stay anywhere," she said. "You talk about that settlement like—"

"Like home?" I shouldered my pack. "Don't. Not yet. We're not out of the bowl yet."

Because we weren't. From the rim of the basin, when I climbed high enough on the talus to see downriver, I could see them: two boats, maybe three, working the shallows of the drowned valley with the patient, methodical arrogance of men who had never once been in a hurry because they had never once doubted they'd arrive. Sunlight flashed off something metallic. A rifle barrel, or a thermos, or a radio antenna. Out here it hardly mattered which. All three meant the same thing.

I scrambled back down and told Ines the truth, because we had passed the point where lying to each other cost less than fear.

"They'll reach the falls by dark. The portage will slow them till morning. That gives us the night and maybe four hours of it."

"Then we go now," Ines said, and picked up her end of the tin.

* * *

The river going down from the source was a different animal than the river coming up.

Coming up, it had been an obstacle course — gravel bars, deadfalls, channels that lied about their depth. Going down, hauling eighty pounds of sealed truth behind us, it became a gauntlet. We moved along the north bank where the granite shelved into the water, because the south bank was open ground, and open ground was where bullets lived. The light went from gold to copper to a bruised violet, and the temperature dropped fast, and our breath started showing, little ghosts that vanished as quickly as they appeared, and I thought: *there's a lesson in that, Sable. Everything shows, briefly, and then it's gone.*

Ines counted paces under her breath. She'd been doing it since the falls — some cartographer's tic, mapping distance with her body when her instruments were gone. Every hundred paces she'd murmur the total, and I began to rely on it the way you rely on a heartbeat. When she stopped counting, I knew something was wrong.

She stopped counting at four thousand six hundred.

Ahead, the shelf we'd been following dropped away into a chute of whitewater, and beyond it the bank pinched to nothing — a wall on our left, boiling water on our right, and no way through except straight through the current.

"That's not in his notes," Ines said.

"His notes were twenty years old. Rivers rewrite themselves every flood." I crouched at the edge, watching the water. It was faster than it looked, which meant it was much faster than it looked. Cold enough to stop a heart in minutes. But the far side — there, thirty yards across, the bank widened again into a gravel bench, and beyond the bench, shadowed under a leaning alder, the channel ran smooth and black and deep.

Deep water. The thing we needed. On the other side of the thing that would kill us.

"We cross here," I said.

"With the tin?"

"With everything. We rig the rope, we keep the tin lashed between us, and we go on the count. If you go down, let go of the tin. I mean it, Ines. Let go. The tin floats. You don't."

"It doesn't float," she said. "It's soldered shut and full of paper and lead seals. It sinks like a confession."

"Then neither of us goes down."

She almost smiled. In the last violet light it looked like something breaking open. "You know," she said, "my father wrote about you. In the journals. 'The pilot who won't land.' He thought you'd never come."

"He was right for ten years."

"What changed?"

I looked at the water, and the water looked back, white-lipped and indifferent. "Somebody finally asked me to carry something instead of drop it," I said. "Count of three."

* * *

The river took us the way rivers do — completely, personally, without malice or mercy.

Three steps in, the bottom vanished. The cold hit like a hand closing around my chest, squeezing the air out in one involuntary shout that the water swallowed instantly. The rope snapped taut between us. The tin swung wild, dragging us toward each other, toward the center, toward the throat of the chute, and I did the only thing there was to do, which was to turn downstream, get my boots under me, and *lean into it* — let the current carry us sideways instead of fighting it broadside, angling for the gravel bench the way you angle a plane into a crosswind, not against it, never against it.

"Ferry!" I screamed, and heard Ines understand — heard it in the change of her pull on the rope, the two of us suddenly flying the same invisible approach, the tin between us like cargo we refused to jettison.

The alder came up fast. I got a fist into its roots. Then Ines's arm locked through mine, and then her knee found the bench, and then we were crawling out onto gravel like drowned animals, heaving the tin after us, all three of us gasping in the dark.

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The river roared its indifference. My hands were cut and I couldn't feel my feet and I was laughing — actually laughing, the unhinged kind, the kind that comes out of the body when the body can't believe it's still running.

Ines flopped onto her back beside me, soaked to the soul, staring at the first stars. "Ferry," she repeated. "You taught me a flying word while we were drowning."

"Best time to learn," I said. "No time to be afraid of the vocabulary."

"I was afraid anyway."

"Good. Fear keeps your hands honest."

* * *

We made the deep-water stretch an hour after full dark, and that was when the shooting started.

It began as light — a bobbing lantern downriver, then two, sweeping the banks with the slow, greedy patience of men searching a room. Then a voice, thin with distance, calling something official-sounding across the water. Then the first shot, which cracked off the canyon wall somewhere above us and came back down as an echo, so that it sounded like the whole gorge had fired at once.

We went flat behind a fallen spruce, the tin pulled in against our chests like a shared heartbeat.

"They can't see us," Ines breathed.

"They don't need to see us. They need to make us move badly." I watched the lanterns. They were at the base of the falls portage — hours behind schedule, angry about it, sloppy with it. Sloppy was good. Sloppy was the only gift we were getting tonight. "They're driving the bank, hoping we bolt into the open."

"Will we?"

"No." And then, because the sky chose that moment to answer me, I started to laugh again — low, disbelieving, grateful. Because over the southern rim of the canyon, high and getting higher, a thread of orange smoke was unspooling against the stars.

Callum.

He'd promised nothing — couldn't promise anything, not with the Company's eyes on him, not with his career already bleeding out on the tarmac at Port Anselm. But there it was: a smoke signal climbing the night in a long lazy ribbon, marking the wind, marking *him*, drawing every lantern and every eye and every rifle barrel up and away from the water.

"He crash-landed," Ines whispered.

"He landed exactly hard enough," I said. "Come on. While they're looking up."

* * *

What followed was the longest hour of my life, and I have flown through storms that lasted longer and meant less.

We slid the tin into the black water and swam it — low, silent, chins barely above the surface, letting the current do the work while the smoke plume burned orange and beautiful above the southern ridge and the lanterns swung toward it like moths. Twice the searchlights swept back and we sank, the tin between us on the bottom, my lungs burning, Ines's hand crushing mine hard enough to leave bruises I would wear for a week and treasure longer. Once a bullet slapped the water twenty feet downstream — a flat, spatting crack — and we froze among the reeds until the shooter convinced himself he'd seen a capybara.

At the third bend the canyon opened its hands. The walls fell back. The sky went wide and generous with stars, and the river beneath us went deep and steady and sure, carrying us now instead of fighting us, and I felt the change in my bones before my brain admitted it: we were through the line. Behind us, the lanterns were small and receding and fixated on smoke. Ahead, sixty miles of dark water ran downhill toward Halfway Bend, toward lamplight in windows, toward people who covered for strangers because it was the decent thing and asked nothing back but a landing now and then.

That was when the second shot came.

Not a search shot. A real one — flat crack off the water, close, too close — and something drew a hot line across my left shoulder like a whip made of fire. I went under, came up choking, and Ines had me by the collar before I'd finished deciding to panic.

"Sable—"

"Graze." I heard my own voice, shockingly level. "Through and across. It's nothing."

"It's bleeding."

"It's *nothing*," I said, and put my hand over it, and kept swimming, because the tin was drifting ahead of us into the dark and purpose is a better painkiller than anything in my med kit.

But I'll tell you what it was, quietly, here in the telling: it was the first wound I'd ever taken that I didn't want to run from. Ten years of scars I'd collected in silence — the ones nobody could see, the village smoke I'd flown away from, the names I'd changed like shirts — and now here was a mark on my body earned moving *toward* something, carrying something, for someone, and it hurt in a way that felt almost like relief. Terror transfigured. That's the phrase that came to me, floating in a cold river under a wide sky with blood diluting off my shoulder into the Kestrel: terror transfigured into purpose. Wren would have written it in a journal. I just bled it.

* * *

Dawn found us on the last gravel bar before the long run to Halfway Bend, the tin safe between us, the smoke plume gone, the canyon empty behind us — empty, or pretending to be.

Ines dressed my shoulder with strips of her shirt and the iodine from my jacket pocket, her hands steadier than mine deserved. She worked in silence, and then, tying off the last knot, she said: "When we get there — when we get to Halfway Bend — what happens to us?"

"Us," I repeated.

"The tin. The evidence. You and me." She sat back on her heels. "My father spent twenty years keeping this river's secret. You spent ten years keeping yours. I've spent my whole life mapping coastlines I'll never see again. So I'm asking plainly, pilot: when we land, what do we do with the truth?"

I looked downriver. The mist was lifting off the water in long pale banners, and somewhere past the next bend, past the shallows and the bars and the sixty miles of current, lamps were being lit in kitchen windows by people who had no idea what was floating toward them in a soldered tin.

"We give it away," I said. "Every page. To everyone. Copies on every plane heading out of the valley until the Company drowns in its own paperwork." I flexed the shoulder; it held. "And then, Ines — then we stay and watch it work."

Her eyes went bright. "You'd stay?"

Before I could answer, the sound reached us — faint, wrong, wonderful: an engine, high and ragged, missing on at least one cylinder, limping down the eastern sky. A plane. *His* plane, flying on fumes and stubbornness, trailing a feather of gray smoke, hunting the gravel bar below us the way a tired bird hunts a branch.

Ines stood up. "Is he going to make it?"

I was already running for the water's edge, waving my shirt over my head like a fool, like a beacon, like a woman who had finally, finally decided where she belonged. "He'd better," I called back. "Because I am not hauling that tin another mile — and Halfway Bend needs to hear everything before the Company's boats get there first."

The engine coughed. The plane dropped toward the bar.
