## 19. Evidence Like a Flood

The river gave us back to Halfway Bend at dusk, and the settlement gave us back to ourselves.

I saw it before Sable did, I think — the lights along the bank, more of them than there should have been, lanterns hung on poles and porch rails and the crossbeams of the drying racks, so many that the whole bend of the river looked like a harbor of small fires. Sable sat in the bow with her arm bound and her face gray with exhaustion and blood loss, and she stared at the lights the way a drowning woman stares at a shore she doesn't believe in.

"That's too many lamps," she said. "Halfway Bend doesn't waste kerosene."

"Maybe they heard," I said.

"Heard what? We didn't radio anyone. We couldn't. Callum's—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. Callum's plane was a smear of scorched alder and torn fabric on the far side of the basin, and Callum was somewhere behind us, alive, we had to believe, because the Company men who'd dragged him from the wreck had wanted him alive. We had left him there. We had left him there and run, and Sable had not spoken his name since without her voice cracking on it.

"They heard something," I said, because I had no better answer, and because the boat was low in the water with the last of the current, and because my ankle screamed every time the hull rocked, and because I needed the lights to be what they looked like.

They were what they looked like.

The whole of Halfway Bend was on the shingle beach when we grounded — Amara Okonjo with her medicine crates stacked behind her like a barricade, the Ferreira brothers with their nets abandoned, old Tomás with a lantern held high, the schoolteacher, the radio operator's widow, children too excited to sleep, dogs too excited for anything. They waded out to the gunwales. Hands pulled the boat up the gravel as if it weighed nothing, as if it hadn't carried two women and a tin box of dead men's paper down a river that had tried to keep it.

Amara reached Sable first. She took Sable's face in both hands the way you'd take a bird's, careful of the wings.

"You're bleeding."

"Grazed," Sable said. "It's nothing."

"Nothing," Amara repeated, in the tone of a woman who has stitched a settlement's wounds for thirty years and knows every size a lie comes in. She looked past Sable to me, to the tin I was clutching against my chest like a child, to the journals wrapped in oilcloth lashed to the thwart. Her eyes went from the box to Sable's face and something passed between them that I was not yet part of — some old arithmetic, some old debt.

"Get her to my table," Amara said to the crowd. "And you—" she pointed at me, and at the tin— "you come with us. And then somebody wake the radio man."

"He's dead," said the schoolteacher. "His widow runs the set now. She's been on it all day."

"On it how?" Sable asked, and her voice had gone strange.

Amara looked down at her, and I watched her decide to tell the truth, the way I had once watched my father decide it, twenty years too late.

"Three days ago a floatplane landed at the upper bend. Company colors. Two men asked after a pilot named Delacroix and a woman with a map. Said you were thieves. Said there'd be a reward for word of you." She paused. "Rosa told them she hadn't seen a plane in a week. Every settlement on this river told them the same thing. Then Rosa got on the set and called every station from Anselm to the falls, and by nightfall the whole river knew you were coming downstream with something the Meridian Company wants buried." Her grip on Sable's face tightened, gentle and absolute. "The river decided, Sable. It decided before you did."

I felt Sable go still under Amara's hands. I had spent a week learning to read her — the pilot's stillness, the stillness of a woman who survives by never being where the blow falls — and I knew this stillness was the deepest one. Not fear. Something older.

"You covered for me," Sable said. "Ten years ago. All of you. And I never—" She stopped. Started again. "I never came back in the wet season. I never stayed. I thought that was— I thought leaving was how I kept you safe."

"Leaving was how you kept yourself safe," Amara said, not unkindly. "We kept ourselves safe too. It's called a settlement, girl. It's what settlements are for." She turned to the crowd. "Somebody carry her up before she argues with me."

* * *

They carried her, and she let them, which told me more than any confession.

Amara's table was scrubbed pine under a hanging lamp, and by the time she'd cleaned the furrow along Sable's ribs and stitched it — seven stitches, Sable counting them aloud in a flat voice to keep from feeling them — the schoolhouse was filling up. Word had gone out along the bank, lantern to lantern, and people came in from the outlying camps, poling down in dugouts through the dark, and by midnight there were forty of us packed among the desks, and the tin was open on the teacher's desk, and I was showing them what my father had died twice for.

The photographs first. I had looked at them a hundred times in the canyon, and each time the looking had cost me something, but this was different — this was looking in company, forty faces lit by one lamp, forty breath held at once. The source basin. The Company camp, 1949. The line of men from the drowned village, hands bound, kneeling at the water's edge. The payroll ledger with names in a surveyor's hand and sums beside them. My father's marginal notes in that careful cartographer's script I had known all my life without ever once reading a word of truth in it: *Witnesses relocated. Village site flooded by dam. No further inquiry recommended. No further inquiry made.*

"Meridian's story," said Rosa, the radio operator's widow, a small iron-gray woman with a voice like a river stone, "is that the upper Kestrel was empty before the Company came. Empty country, empty rights, empty graves. That's the story in every land office from here to the capital. That's the story they've been buying judges with for thirty years." She laid her palm flat on the payroll page. "This is not that story."

"It's paper," said Tomás. "Paper burns."

"Not this paper," I said. "Not all of it. Not if it's everywhere at once."

I had been turning the thought over since the canyon, turning it like a stone in the pocket, and it came out of me now half-formed and getting more solid with every mouth in the room that turned toward it.

"You can't defend a document," I said. "The Company proved that when they burned my father's records, when they burned the hangar, when they put a bullet in the water a foot from my head. Anything in one place, they can reach. Anything in one place is a thing that can be lost." I looked around the schoolhouse, at the nets and the muddy boots and the faces. "But you have planes. Every week, planes going out to every bend and camp and mission on this river, carrying medicine and flour and mail. Nobody questions a medicine plane. Nobody stops a medicine plane. What if every plane that flies out of here this week carries copies? What if there are a hundred of these by Friday, and a thousand by the end of the month, in a hundred hands from Anselm to the coast? Let them burn one. Let them burn ten. The flood's already out of the canyon."

Silence, and then Tomás said, "The mission at San Lorenzo has a duplicating machine. Church got it for the parish records. Father Ignacio owes me for two seasons of fish."

"The school has a mimeograph," said the schoolteacher, who had a name, Odile, which I learned that night and never forgot.

"Rosa's set can call ahead," said Amara. "Every station on the river. Tell them what's coming and why."

And then, from the back, from a desk by the window where she'd been sitting propped on two chairs with her arm in a sling and her color coming back: "And when the Company comes asking who copied what, and they will come — every one of you will be standing in the open with it. You understand that. They burned my hangar to get at a map. What do you think they'll do to a village that floods the country with their dead?"

The room went quiet in a way that was not empty. It was the quiet of people weighing something they had already decided.

Amara answered her. "Sable. Ten years ago you flew a man to my table with a bullet in him and the Company's men an hour behind, and you stood in this room and you said, *if they come for this village it's on me, and you should all turn me out.* And Tomás stood up and said, *the river doesn't turn out its own.*" She looked around the room, and the room looked back at her, and there was not one face turned away. "We didn't turn you out then. We're not turning out a box of paper now. The Company has money and guns and judges. We have the river, and we have each other, and we have—" she nodded at the tin— "we have the truth, which turns out to be the heaviest thing on this boat, and the only thing that floats."

Sable looked at her for a long time. I watched her try to find the shape of the argument she'd been carrying for ten years — the one about running, about how distance was safety, about how everything she touched she had to leave — and I watched the argument fail against forty faces in a schoolhouse at midnight.

"You could lose everything," she said finally, and her voice was not the pilot's voice. It was smaller. It was the voice under the voice.

"We could," Amara agreed. "Or we could lose it anyway, quietly, one season at a time, for the rest of our lives, and call that safety." She began rolling up her sleeves. "Odile. How many mimeograph masters can you cut by dawn?"

* * *

What happened over the next four days, I will tell plainly, because it deserves plain telling, though nothing about it felt plain. It felt like weather. It felt like a river rising.

Odile cut masters by lamplight, her pen scratching through the small hours, and the schoolhouse smelled of ink and methylated spirit. The Ferreira brothers built a drying rack out of net frames, and pages hung from it like a strange pale catch, forty photographs and the payroll and my father's final letter to me — the one he'd never sent, the one Sable had carried a thousand miles — copied over and over until the words *I was afraid, and then I was ashamed, and then I was only tired, and the river was the only thing I trusted to keep what I couldn't keep myself* were known by heart to people who had never met him. Father Ignacio came down from San Lorenzo with the duplicating machine in a dugout and stayed three days, printing until the machine's roller wore smooth, printing spare parts for it out of a church supply crate he'd been saving, he said, for a worthy heresy.

Rosa worked the radio in shifts. Her voice went out over the river every morning and evening, calm as a tide table: *To all stations. Copies of documentary evidence regarding Meridian Company activities, upper Kestrel, 1949, available at Halfway Bend. Distribute widely. Distribute everywhere.* And the river answered. The mission at San Lorenzo. The trading post at Two Rivers. The sawmill camp, whose foreman had a brother on the Company payroll and copied the pages anyway and mailed them to a newspaper in the capital. A doctor at the falls who had treated massacre survivors as a young man and wept, Rosa said, into the microphone, and then asked for forty copies.

Planes came in that had never come to Halfway Bend before — bush pilots I'd only heard Sable curse by name, charter men, a mission flyer, a woman who flew mail and geology samples and had a voice like a chain winch. They took their copies rolled in oilcloth, sewn into mail sacks, tucked inside crates of vaccine, hidden in the false bottoms of tackle boxes. One of them, a lean silent man who landed rough and said little, turned out to be flying for the Company's own supply line, and took his copies up to the Company's own upper camp, and slid them under the bunkhouse doors of Company laborers in the night.

"Let them read it at breakfast," he said, when Sable asked him if he was insane. "Let them read what their wages bought. Half of them didn't know. The other half suspected. Nobody's ever shown them the arithmetic before."

And Sable — Sable worked. That was the thing that undid me, watching it. She had spent ten years perfecting departure, and now she could not leave. Her Otter sat on the strip with a patched fuel line and a bullet-scarred wing, and she crawled over it and around it and through it, and she flew two runs herself, medicine up, copies down, and every time she came back there were more lanterns lit on the bank, more planes on the water, more of the river gathered into the thing we were making. She stopped sleeping in the Otter. I noticed it on the third night. She slept in Amara's spare room, on a real bed, with the door open, and I understood that she had stopped keeping her boots by the door.

On the fifth morning, the Company's answer came, and it came the only way left to it.

A floatplane in Company colors landed at the bend at mid-morning — the same one, I'd have sworn, that had come asking three days before — and a man in a good coat stepped out onto the shingle with two hard-faced escorts, and walked up through the settlement as if he owned the gravel under his feet, and stopped in front of Amara's house, where the drying rack stood in the yard hung with pages like a harvest.

He looked at the pages for a long time. He looked at the forty-odd people who had, without any signal I ever detected, quietly gathered in the yard around him. He looked at Sable, who stood on Amara's step with her arm in a sling and her pilot's stillness on like a coat.

"You understand," he said, in the reasonable voice I had learned to hate in Port Anselm, "that distributing stolen corporate documents is a crime."

"Understand it fine," said Tomás.

"And that everyone in this settlement is now an accessory."

"That's the idea," said Amara.

The man's composure flickered — the first hairline crack — because he had come to find a secret and found a town. He had come to seize a document and found a thousand, already scattered from the coast to the falls, already read by laborers and priests and newspapermen and a judge's own clerk, if Rosa's morning traffic was to be believed. There was nothing to seize. There was only everyone.

"You can't arrest a river," Sable said to him, quietly, and it was not a threat. It was a weather report.

The man in the good coat looked at her a long moment, and then at the drying rack, and then at the faces, and something in him did the arithmetic and did not like the sum. He turned and walked back to his plane. As the floats came up dripping and the drone of his engine faded downriver, nobody in the yard cheered. They went back to work. Nets were mended. Ink was mixed. Odile went on cutting masters, because there were still stations on the upper river that hadn't been reached, and a flood, once it starts, does not stop to admire itself.

I stood next to Sable in the yard, watching the wake of the Company plane flatten into the current.

"They'll convene an inquiry now," I said. "They have to. The capital paper ran the payroll this morning — Rosa got the confirmation on the set an hour ago. They can't bury it, so they'll try to own it. Public inquiry. Hearings in Anselm." I turned to her. "They'll call witnesses. They'll call you."

Sable was quiet for a while. The river ran. Somewhere behind us the mimeograph cranked and the smell of fresh ink came out of the schoolhouse door, and a child ran past with a page flapping in her hand, showing it to everybody, not understanding a word of it, understanding all of it.

"I know," Sable said. "They'll ask me what I did. All of it. Not just the Company's part." She looked down at her sling, and then out at the planes on the water, and the lanterns still hanging in the daylight because nobody had taken them down, as if the settlement had decided the dark was over and wasn't taking any chances on it coming back. "Ten years I thought the truth was a thing you carried alone or drowned with. Turns out it's a thing you hand out in copies."

She turned toward the schoolhouse, where Odile was calling her name — the Anselm station had come through, official traffic, a summons, a date.

"Come on," Sable said. "We've got a hearing to get to, and for once in my life, I'm not flying away from it."
