## 20. Reckoning

The courthouse in Port Anselm had been a cannery once, and it still smelled faintly of brine and old tin, as if the building itself remembered what it was to preserve things past their season. They had hung a new sign over the door — *Commission of Inquiry into the Meridian Company* — but the letters were already curling at the edges from the river damp, and beneath them the ghost of the old painted name showed through like a scar under new skin.

Sable stood outside it on the morning of her testimony and could not make her feet move.

The street was full. Word had traveled the way word always traveled out here — by pilot, by boatman, by the gossip she herself had ferried for ten years in the cargo nets beside the medicine and the mail. Halfway Bend had sent people downriver: Marta with her bad hip who bought insulin off every third flight; old Tomás who ran the fuel drum barge; the schoolteacher, the ferry widow, two brothers who poled produce through the shallows when the water allowed it. They stood along the railings in their good clothes, which is to say their cleanest ones, and when they saw her they did not wave or call out. They simply looked at her, steady as moorings, and that was worse and better than cheering.

Ines stood at the bottom of the steps with her father's journals stacked in a canvas satchel against her hip. She'd bound the water-swollen pages themselves, three nights of work with needle and linen thread, drying each leaf flat between boards like pressing flowers from a grave. Her ankle was still wrapped. She'd refused the crutch.

"They're ready for you," Ines said.

"I know."

"You don't have to say all of it." Ines's voice was low, meant only for her. "The Company. That's what they summoned you for. Nobody's asking about the rest."

"Nobody's asking," Sable said. "That's the problem."

She climbed the steps before she could talk herself out of it, the way you push a plane through the moment of rotation — commit, and then there is no ground beneath you, only air and whatever you've built to hold it.

* * *

The hearing room had been the cannery's floor office, and its windows overlooked the harbor where the supply boats came in. Sable knew those boats. She knew the men who loaded them and the women who kept their ledgers and the children who raced the wakes along the pilings. She had flown into this town eleven times over ten years, always in daylight, never longer than a week, and she had told herself each time that Port Anselm was just another strip on the map, another place she passed through without touching.

Now she sat in the witness chair and understood that she had been touching everything, all along, the way water touches stone — slowly, invisibly, wearing channels she couldn't see until the flood came.

The commissioners sat behind a long table draped in green felt. Three of them: a magistrate down from the capital with a face like folded paper, a river engineer who kept glancing at the harbor as though he could read the water level in his sleep, and an older woman whose name Sable didn't catch but whose eyes had the particular patience of someone who had buried a family and gone on working anyway. A stenographer's keys clicked like rain on a tin roof.

The surveyor sat at the defendants' table with his counsel. Up close, stripped of the canyon and the gunboats and the dry-season urgency, he seemed smaller — a neat man in a neat suit, hands folded, the kind of face you forgot in a crowd, which Sable supposed had always been his greatest professional asset. He did not look at her. He looked at the middle distance, at some point only he could see, and waited.

"The Commission has heard testimony regarding the events at the Kestrel headwaters," the magistrate said. "Regarding the expedition mounted by the Meridian Company, its armament, its instructions to its personnel. We have the journals of Elias Wren. We have the documents recovered from the cache." He touched the stack of evidence like a man touching something hot. "What we are asking of you, Ms. Delacroix, is simpler and harder. Tell us how you came to be there."

So she began at the beginning, because there was no other honest place to start.

She told them about Elias Wren dying at a strip only she serviced, trading a hand-drawn map for a promise. She told them about the ledger page in his effects, the numbers that didn't add up unless the headwaters held something worth killing for. She told them about the hangar fire, and the fire marshal's polite fiction of faulty wiring, and the way both she and Ines had known better and said nothing, because saying nothing was the local language and they were fluent.

And then she kept going, past the point where anyone had asked her to stop.

"In 1947," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected, "I flew for the Meridian Company. Charter contract, six months. I carried freight up the Corazal branch of the river system. On the fourth of November of that year, I carried four men from the Company's regional office to a settlement called San Rafael de los Llanos. I knew two things about those men. One, they weren't surveyors, whatever their papers said. Two, they paid me triple rate not to ask."

The stenographer's keys stopped. The room had gone very quiet, the quiet of a canyon where sound carries too far.

"I delivered them at dusk," Sable said. "I spent the night at my usual strip twenty miles downstream. At dawn the smoke was standing over the valley like a column holding up the sky. San Rafael burned that night. Eleven people died, or twelve — the count was never settled, because the survivors scattered and the Company's report called it a lightning fire in the dry season." She looked directly at the magistrate. "There was no lightning. There was me, and there was a plane, and there were four men with kerosene in their freight manifest listed as *agricultural supplies*. I have spent ten years flying away from that fact. Every short stay, every season abandoned, every rule I made myself about never putting down roots — that was the shape of it. I wasn't free. I was running, and I mistook the running for wings."

The magistrate's folded-paper face did not change, but the woman with the patient eyes leaned forward slightly, and Sable found she could speak to her instead of to the room.

"Why tell us this now?" the magistrate asked finally. "You were a minor party. An instrument. The Commission's mandate concerns the Company."

"Because instruments are how it works," Sable said. "Because somebody flew those men, and somebody fueled the planes, and somebody signed the manifests, and every one of them told themselves they were small. I'm telling you so the record shows that the machine had parts, and one of the parts is sitting in your witness chair, and she knew, and she flew anyway, and she took ten years to find the courage to say so out loud." She slid her license case across the table — the worn leather, the laminated card, the photograph of a younger woman who still believed she could outrun anything. "Pending your review, I surrender this. Whatever you decide I deserve, I'll take it standing on the ground."

The click of the stenographer's keys resumed, softer now, almost gentle.

* * *

They broke at midday, and the harbor light came slanting through the high windows, gilding the dust motes, and Sable found Ines waiting on the cannery steps with bread and hard cheese wrapped in wax paper, because Ines expressed care the way her father had — through provision, through practical things done without announcement.

"You didn't have to do that," Ines said.

"Yes, I did." Sable bit into the bread. It was good bread; Halfway Bend's flour, probably, flown down by one of the network. "It's strange. Ten years I thought the truth would be the thing that cost me everything. Turns out it's the only thing I own outright."

Ines almost smiled. Then her gaze went past Sable's shoulder and sharpened, and Sable turned to see Callum Reyes crossing the square toward them.

He looked terrible. Not injured — hollowed, the way a man looks after he's burned his own bridges and watched them go. His charter jacket was buttoned wrong. He carried a document folder under one arm like something that might bite him.

"They subpoenaed me," he said without preamble. "My flights, my contracts, my radio logs. And my handler's correspondence, which—" he exhaled through his teeth— "which I may have encouraged them to obtain. Along with certain other files I copied before the Company locked the office."

"You're testifying," Sable said.

"I'm testifying." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of stunned relief, like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. "Do you know what I gave up? The routes. All of them. Every strip you fly, Delacroix, every medicine run, every mail contract — they promised me the whole network. I wanted it so badly I could taste the avgas." He looked out at the harbor, at the boats, at the ordinary morning. "Elias Wren saved my engine over the rapids once. Talked me down through weather no sane man flies, talked me onto a sandbar the width of a church aisle, and never once mentioned it afterward. Never sent a bill. I owed him a debt I told myself I'd pay someday, when it was convenient." He shook his head. "Convenient doesn't come, Reyes. You have to land on it."

"What happens to you?" Ines asked quietly.

Callum shrugged. "Aiding and abetting, conspiracy, false statements — pick a charge, they're all in the folder. My license goes in the same bucket as yours, probably. Maybe deeper." He met Sable's eyes. "Worth it?"

Sable thought of the fuel and food left upstream like an answered prayer. Thought of the crash-landing between the guns, buying seconds with wreckage and nerve.

"Worth it," she said.

He nodded once, turned, and walked up the steps into the cannery, carrying his ruin like a man carrying tools to a job site.

* * *

His testimony took the afternoon.

Sable watched from the gallery, wedged between Marta and the schoolteacher, and listened to Callum dismantle himself piece by piece in front of the Commission. He described the arrangement with the Meridian Company in plain words: the retainer, the reports, the promise of exclusive routes. He named names. He read aloud from the handler's letters — *ensure the Wren matter is resolved before the dry season ends; the shareholders will not tolerate exposure of the source holdings* — and the words fell into the room like stones into still water, each one sending rings outward through the gallery, through the town, through whatever wires and wireless sets would carry them downriver by nightfall.

Then he testified to the sabotage. The pinched fuel line. The day's delay that had let two exhausted women reach the source ahead of the guns. He said it plainly, without drama, looking at the commissioners and not at the surveyor, and when the defense counsel rose to suggest that Mr. Reyes was a confessed criminal whose testimony should be weighed accordingly, Callum smiled for the first time all day.

"Yes," he said. "Weigh it accordingly. Everything I say is the testimony of a man who sold out to the Meridian Company and lived to regret it in detail. Ask yourself why a man would ruin himself to lie about this. Then ask the Wren journals."

The magistrate asked for the surveyor's response.

The surveyor's counsel spoke first — procedural objections, questions of jurisdiction, the careful architecture of a defense built for shareholders rather than juries. But the surveyor himself stood, adjusted his cuffs, and addressed the Commission in a voice pitched for reasonableness, and Sable recognized the tone immediately. It was the voice he'd used at the spring, offering her immunity. The voice of a man who believed every person had a price and considered it a kindness to name it.

"The Meridian Company has developed resources in this territory for thirty years," he said. "Development requires discretion. Discretion is not a crime."

"It is," said the woman with the patient eyes, "when the thing being kept discreet is a massacre."

She read the charges then, and the room did not breathe while she read them: conspiracy to conceal evidence of homicide; obstruction of a lawful inquiry; arson; assault with intent; the attempted destruction of witnesses. When she reached the counts concerning the burned records in Elias Wren's camp — twenty years of documentation, torched by Company men while Wren was still alive to watch the smoke — Ines's hand found Sable's in the gallery and gripped it hard enough to hurt, and neither of them let go.

The magistrate announced that the surveyor would be remanded pending indictment, along with the regional director and two members of the Company board named in Callum's files. Bail was denied. The surveyor did not look at Sable as the constables walked him out. He looked at the middle distance, at the point only he could see, and Sable understood suddenly that it wasn't composure. It was a man watching the entire edifice of his reasonable, discreet, well-paid life come down around him, and having nowhere left to put his eyes.

The gavel came down. The stenographer closed her notebook. Outside, the harbor bells rang the hour, ordinary and golden, and the crowd on the cannery steps parted to let the constables through, and nobody threw anything, and nobody cheered either. They just watched, the way this country watched everything — patiently, memorially, keeping accounts the way the river keeps accounts, in silt and time.

* * *

Evening found the three of them on the seawall with the last of the light turning the water to hammered copper. Ines had the journals out, all nine of them, fanned open to dry in the river breeze. Somewhere down the quay a guitar was playing, badly, joyfully.

"They'll want your license decision within the month," Callum said. He'd gotten his buttons right, finally. "Could go either way. Flying contraband evidence, harboring fugitives, lying to a federal officer—"

"Lying to a federal officer?" Sable said. "When?"

"Port Anselm, week one. You told me you weren't involved. You're a terrible liar, Delacroix. It's your most endearing quality."

"And yours?"

"Honesty at the worst possible moments." He drained his coffee, stood, stretched. "I've got a charter tomorrow. Legal one. Fishermen, up the coast." He hesitated, then stuck out his hand, and Sable shook it, and neither of them said anything else, because pilots don't, and that was enough.

When he'd gone, Ines sat down close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while they watched the fishing lights come on one by one across the darkening water.

"My father wrote something in the last journal," Ines said. "The page before the map. I haven't shown anyone yet." She opened the book carefully, to a page soft as cloth from handling, and read aloud in the gathering dusk:

*"If anyone finds this, find my daughter. Don't tell her I was brave. Tell her I was slow — that it took me twenty years to do what should have taken one. And tell her that the river was right to keep what it kept. Some things shouldn't be carried fast. They should be carried all the way."*

Sable said nothing. There was nothing to say that the river hadn't already said.

But as they walked back through the lamplit streets toward the boarding house — past the cannery, past the courthouse with its curling new sign, past the whole listening town — Sable realized she was not counting the days until she could leave. For the first time in ten years, she was not counting at all.

She was only wondering, with a feeling she had no name for yet, whether the Commission would return her license — and discovering, to her astonishment, that part of her hoped they wouldn't. Not yet. Because a grounded pilot could stay. And staying, she was beginning to suspect, might be the hardest flying she'd ever do.
