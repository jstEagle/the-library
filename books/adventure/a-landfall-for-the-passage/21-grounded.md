## 21. Grounded

The first rain of the wet season came in the night, and Ines woke to the sound of it on the tin roof like applause from a very large, very patient audience.

She lay in her cot at the Halfway Bend mission house and listened, cataloguing it the way she catalogued everything now — by what it meant for the river. This was not the fat, friendly rain of the coast that fell and forgot itself. This was the opening movement. Somewhere up in the high country, water was beginning its long argument with the land, and every drop that struck this roof would eventually pass beneath the floorboards of her father's drowned valley, over the sealed cache they had carried down out of the mountains, past the gravel bars where Sable had bled, all the way to the sea.

The river had a memory. Her father had believed that; had spent twenty years writing it down in journals that smelled of mildew and kerosene. Ines had spent four months learning to believe it too.

She got up, lit the lamp, and looked out the window into the grey drumming dark. Across the compound, in the hangar that Odile and the men of Halfway Bend had rebuilt in six furious days after the fire — *our* hangar now, though no one said so aloud — there was a light burning. It burned most nights now. She had stopped pretending she didn't know whose it was.

Sable Delacroix had been in Halfway Bend for eleven weeks.

Ines knew the number precisely because everyone in the settlement knew it, the way you know the height of a flood crest or the day the ice went out. Eleven weeks. The woman who had once told Odile, laughing, that she could feel a place start to own her if she stayed past a season — who had flown medicine and gossip and tinned peaches up and down the Kestrel for ten years without ever once letting a town learn her middle name — had been grounded since the inquiry convened in Port Anselm, and she had come *here* to wait it out. Not the coast. Not some city with anonymous rooms. Here, where the people she'd once covered for her crime still lived, and where she had finally, in the canyon, in the rain, told the truth about what that crime was.

Ines pulled on her boots and her oilskin and went out into it.

* * *

The hangar smelled of cut lumber and dope-and-fabric and, faintly, of kerosene and engine oil — a smell Ines had come to understand was, for certain people, the smell of safety itself. The Otter sat under its new skin, half-dressed, wings bare to the ribs because the covering crew wouldn't work in the damp. And under the wing, at a folding table dragged from the mission hall, sat Sable Delacroix with a lamp, a straightedge, a pot of cold coffee, and forty years of her father's field notes spread out like a patient awaiting surgery.

She didn't look up when Ines came in. "You're dripping on the triangulation."

"You're awake at three in the morning."

"I'm always awake at three in the morning." Sable turned a page with a pencil stub held delicately, the way other people held relics. "That's when the numbers are honest."

Ines hung her coat on the wing strut and came to stand behind her shoulder. The table was a battlefield of paper: Elias Wren's journals, open to pages dense with his small, slanted hand; Ines's own base maps, photostatted before they left Port Anselm; and between them, growing sheet by sheet across two months of evenings, the thing they were actually building — a map of the Kestrel from source to mouth, drawn to proper scale, with proper elevations, proper soundings, proper attribution. A public record. That had been Ines's phrase, standing in the mission hall the night after they got back, soaked and half-starved and carrying a waterproof tin that would end the Meridian Company. *It should belong to everyone. All of it. The river, the massacre, the names.* And Sable — grounded, waiting, stripped of the license that had been her whole identity for a decade — had said, in a voice Ines was still learning to hear clearly: "Then we'd better get it right."

"Show me," Ines said now, because that was the ritual, and rituals were how the two of them had learned to speak.

Sable slid the working sheet across. "Canyon section. I've plotted your father's barometer readings against the aneroid fixes I took flying — the ones I remember, anyway." A ghost of a smile. "He was good, Ines. He was better than good. His elevations agree with mine within thirty feet over a hundred miles of gorge, and he did it with a boiling-point thermometer and stubbornness."

"He was a cartographer's son," Ines said. "Nobody ever remembers that. They remember the prospector. The crazy old man at the strips."

"Well." Sable set the pencil down. "Now they'll remember the witness."

The word settled between them the way it always did — heavy, necessary, not quite comfortable. Twenty-two names. That was what the cache had held, in the end, among the payrolls and the photographs and the Company's own immaculate correspondence: a list of twenty-two workers massacred at the headwaters forty years ago and written off as fever deaths. Elias Wren had been the camp clerk. He had seen it, documented it, hidden the evidence where only the river could guard it, and then spent the rest of his life unable to go back and unable to forget — prospecting the canyon, season after season, getting closer and never arriving, until a dying man at a dirt strip traded his life's work to a stranger for a promise.

Ines picked up the pencil. "The reach below the falls is wrong. He has the channel on the east side here—" she tapped, "—but the portage logs say west. We carried it ourselves. West."

"Then we draw it west." Sable said it simply, without the flicker of defensiveness Ines had learned to expect from experts whose maps were being corrected. It was one of the quiet astonishments of these eleven weeks: watching a woman who had trusted nothing but altitude and escape learn to trust a correction. "His eyes were failing by then. Yours aren't. That's the whole point of doing this together."

Outside, the rain thickened. Inside, the lamp hissed, and two women bent over the river.

* * *

The settlement had opinions about Sable Delacroix, and Ines heard all of them, because in Halfway Bend opinions arrived with the same efficiency as everything else Odile managed — which was to say, completely.

They came to the table at the mission hall in the evenings, when the generator was running and the radio crackled with the coastal stations, and Ines would sit with her drafting board propped on crates while the room argued around them. Old Mateo, who had known Sable since before whatever it was she was running from, maintained loudly that she'd gone soft, sitting still this long, and that any day now she'd bolt for the coast like a bird off a wire. Odile said nothing, but kept assigning Sable tasks — inventory, radio schedules, teaching the Ferreira boy to read weather — with the serene authority of a woman planting a tree she intended to stay and sit under. The children called her *la piloto* and made her tell the story of the crash-landing at the spring, which she told differently every time, each version slightly more heroic for Callum and slightly less for herself.

And Sable stayed.

Ines watched it happen the way she watched anything worth mapping: slowly, then all at once. Watched her stop flinching when someone used her name in a room. Watched her learn which floorboard in the mission house creaked, which neighbor's dog hated her, which chair at Odile's table was hers now by unspoken treaty. Watched her, one grey afternoon, stand in the doorway of the rebuilt hangar for a long time, looking at the Otter with an expression Ines couldn't read — and then turn away and come to help her ink the falls section instead, as if the looking had answered something.

"She's grieving," Odile said to Ines one evening, in Portuguese, assuming the pilot couldn't follow. "A license is a self. You took hers away. Now she has to find out what's underneath."

"She can understand you," Ines said.

"I know," Odile said placidly. "That's why I said it in front of her."

Across the hall, Sable, who had absolutely understood every word, poured herself another coffee and did not look up, and Ines hid her smile in the elevation figures.

But it wasn't simple, and Ines never pretended it was. Some nights Sable woke screaming — Ines heard it through the wall, the short choked sounds of a woman fighting something in a burning hangar or a burning village, she'd never asked which, both, probably — and some mornings she found the pilot already at the table at dawn, red-eyed, redrawing a contour line for the fourth time because her hands weren't steady yet. There was a letter from the licensing board pinned above the desk, forwarded through Port Anselm, that said *review pending* in language designed to make a person feel like a case number. Sable had read it exactly once, folded it into quarters, and used it ever since as a bookmark in Wren's 1931 journal, which Ines chose to interpret as either defiance or penance and suspected was both.

"They'll give it back," Ines said once. "Callum's testimony buried them. The indictment named the surveyor and two directors. You testified to your own part and they still wrote you up as *cooperating witness*. Nobody grounds a cooperating witness forever."

"Maybe," Sable said. "Maybe not." She was inking the drowned valley, the little hatched basin above the falls where the cache had slept for forty years under the spring-fed water. When she spoke again her voice was careful, level, the voice she used for turbulence. "You know what I figured out? For ten years I thought staying anywhere was the risk. Stay a season, they find you. Put down roots, they pull them up. So I kept everything light — no lease, no debts, no winter coat I couldn't leave in a day." The pen moved. "And then the Company burned my hangar anyway, and hunted us up a river anyway, and shot at me anyway. Running didn't protect anything. It just meant nobody knew me well enough to mourn me."

Ines set down her pen. "And now?"

"Now I'm finding out whether anyone would." She said it lightly, almost joking, and Ines let her have the joke, because that too was part of the map between them — the places marked *here be dragons*, to be surveyed later, gently.

* * *

The rains deepened. The strip at Halfway Bend went to mud, then to standing water, then to a shallow brown lake that the children sailed rafts on, to Odile's despair. No plane was landing or leaving. The whole settlement turned inward like a hand closing gently around itself — roofs mended, nets dried, stories told — and Ines discovered that a grounded pilot and a cartographer with no expedition to join made an excellent pair of indoor animals.

They worked. God, how they worked.

Sheet by sheet the river assembled itself on Ines's board: the braided shallows at the delta, the tobacco-country bends, Halfway Bend itself rendered with a care no government survey had ever wasted on it — every house, every dock, the mission, the strip, the grave behind the chapel where they had buried what remained of Elias Wren after the inquiry released him, under a marker Ines had lettered herself: *ELIAS WREN, 1898–1937 AND AFTERWARD. HE KEPT THE RECORD.*

Upstream it went, into the canyon, the portage, the falls, the basin — and here Ines insisted on a second layer, an inset, done in a different hand: the massacre site, the camp layout from her father's photographs, the twenty-two names in a column, each with whatever the records gave them. *Tomás Aguirre, machinist. The brothers Okonkwo, deckhands. Ana Reyes Ferreira, cook.* Callum swore, when he saw it on one of his rare radio-check-ins from Port Anselm, that the last name wasn't kin of his, and then was quiet a long time, and then said, "Put it big, Ines. Bigger than the rest of the legend. Make them read it first."

So she did.

Some nights they worked in silence. Some nights Sable talked — fragments, offered like items from a cargo manifest: the name of the village she had flown the Company muscle to, the year, the smell of the smoke seen from altitude, the way she had told herself for a decade that she hadn't known. Ines never pushed. She had learned from her father's journals that a confession is also a kind of map, and you don't grab a map out of a man's hands; you hold the lamp steady and let him show you what he chooses.

And some nights — fewer, and then not so few — they didn't work at all. They played Odile's battered cards. They argued about whether the falls were ninety or a hundred and ten meters (Ines won; she had measured). Sable taught her the aneroid and she taught Sable the planimeter, and once, memorably, they got drunk on Odile's cashew wine and tried to reconcile Wren's 1929 compass variation with the modern charts, and ended up laughing until the lamp guttered, two liars turned archivists, keeping faith with a dead man's decimals.

It was during one of those nights, near the end of the third month, that Ines understood what was happening, and understood that Sable knew it too, and that neither of them had said it because saying it might break it.

What was happening was this: Sable Delacroix was staying past a season. Past two. Not stranded — the inquiry could have been waited out in Port Anselm, in rooms anonymous enough to vanish in. Chosen. She had chosen the mud lake and the creaking floorboard and the dog that hated her and the chair at Odile's table and the lamp in the hangar at three in the morning, and she was choosing it again every day, the way you choose a heading, and Ines — who had mapped everything except her own life, who had received her father's legacy from a stranger's hands and nearly refused it — sat with her cards fanned forgotten in her fingers and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the day a stranger walked into her office carrying a dead man's map.

"You're staring," Sable said, not looking up from her hand.

"I'm surveying," Ines said. "New territory. Very poorly charted."

"What's the terrain like?"

"Hostile. Dense vegetation. Frequent flash floods of sentiment." She laid down her cards. "Ask me in the morning."

Sable looked at her then — really looked, the way she looked at weather coming down a valley, reading it whole — and something passed over her face that Ines had no symbol for on any legend she owned. "In the morning," Sable agreed, "I'll probably still be here."

* * *

The letter came up on the supply boat that broke the rains' first pause, poling through the flooded strip road with the mail sack held high, and Odile brought it to the hangar personally, which meant everyone in Halfway Bend knew before Ines finished breaking the seal.

Sable read it standing under the Otter's bare wing. Ines watched her face do several things in quick succession — none of which she could catalogue, which frightened her more than any of them.

"Well?" she said, when the silence had gone on too long.

Sable handed her the page. Her hand was steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "Review concluded. License restored, effective immediately. Citing — listen to this — *cooperation with federal authorities and service to affected communities.* Service to affected communities." She laughed, one short note, wrong-sized. "Ten years of clean flying and it's the one season I crashed and confessed that gets me my wings back."

"So you can go," Ines said. Flat. Careful. Holding the lamp steady.

The hangar was very quiet. Rain ticked on the new roof. Out on the flood-lake, the Ferreira boy's raft bumped the dock post, and someone laughed, and the sound came in through the door like an exhibit.

"Yeah," Sable said. She looked at the Otter — at the new fabric, the fresh dope, the engine Odile's nephew had overhauled twice out of pure love. "Weather window opens in three days. Strip'll be dry enough by Thursday." She turned to Ines, and her eyes were bright and terrified and resolved, all the pilot now and all the woman. "There's a run I've been meaning to make. Ten years overdue. Upstream, to the source — there's ash in a tin in the mission safe that belongs in that basin, and I promised a dying man I'd finish his deliveries." She paused. "Come with me. First flight of the new season. We'll drop the ashes at the spring, and then—" she glanced at the letter, at the settlement beyond the door, at the chair at Odile's table that was hers by treaty, "—and then I want to talk to you about scheduled routes. Regular ones. Ones that end with a landing somewhere I'm expected."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. Then she went to the mission safe, and took out the tin, and put it in Sable's hands.

"Thursday," she said. "But we're taking the new sheets with us. If we're going to be the public record, we may as well check our own work against the original."

Above them, through the open hangar doors, the clouds were already tearing apart at the western edge, and beyond them, far upstream, the river was rising toward the drowned valley — rising, as it did every year, to keep its appointment with the source.
