## 1. Cold Work

The river gave up its dead slowly, and it never gave up its secrets for free.

Yara Eriksen learned that lesson at nine years old, the first time her father lowered her into the green-black water off the Provision's stern, his rope around her waist like a leash, like a promise. *Feel with your hands,* he'd said, his voice arriving through the regulator hose in a garble of bubbles and faith. *Your eyes are useless down there. Your hands will tell you the truth.*

Fifteen years later, she still dove by feel, and her hands had never once lied to her.

She launched before first light, when the mist lay on the water so thick that the drowned ship and the living shore were both rumors. The skiff cut through fog that smelled of cold iron and river weed, and Yara rowed without a lantern because a lantern was a confession. Anyone watching from the tree line would see only gray water moving on gray water, and then nothing at all.

The Provision sat in forty feet of silted silence two hundred meters off the northern bank, where the river widened into a brown lake that no chart bothered to name. Officially, the wreck did not exist. The charts showed clean water, unbroken current, a blank space where a naval corvette had gone down with all hands one winter seventy years ago. Officially, there were no salvage rights to claim, no permits to file, no compensation for the families of thirty-one men who had simply stopped writing home.

Unofficially, the river bottom held a fortune in brass fittings, copper sheathing, and whatever else seven decades of patience had preserved—and unofficially, Yara Eriksen was the only person alive who knew exactly how to take it.

She anchored over the stern section, checked her gauges by touch more than sight, and rolled backward off the gunwale into water cold enough to stop thought.

The cold came first, always. It closed around her like a fist, squeezed the air from her lungs in a reflexive gasp she'd trained herself not to make, and then—slowly, as her body surrendered to what could not be changed—it became something else. Something almost like clarity. Down here there was no rent due, no buyer haggling her brass down to scrap price, no neighbors in Vask village asking why a woman alone kept diving forbidden water. There was only the work, and the work asked nothing of her except everything she had.

She descended along her anchor line, hand over hand, into the dark.

At fifteen meters the last light died. At twenty-five, her gloves found the Provision's hull, and the ship spoke to her the way it always did—a long groan transmitted through steel and water and bone, the sound of a vessel settling by millimeters into its grave, patient as geology. Seventy years of sinking, and still sinking. She pressed her palm flat against the plating and felt the vibration run up her arm, through her chest, into the place behind her ribs where she kept everything she couldn't afford to say out loud.

*Good morning, old girl,* she thought, absurdly, the way she always did. *Miss me?*

Her dive light carved a yellow tunnel through the murk. The hull rose above her like a cliff face, furred with zebra mussels and trailing curtains of algae that swayed in a current she couldn't feel but they could. She worked her way aft along the familiar route—the seam at frame forty, the gash where the old girl had struck bottom or been struck, the question Yara had never been able to answer from the damage alone. Wrecks told stories if you knew how to read them, and this one told hers in a language with too many missing words.

The gash was wide enough to admit a diver carrying nothing. Wide enough to admit Yara, who carried everything: her lights, her lift bag, her crowbar, and the particular hunger that had brought her back to this hole in the river two hundred times.

Inside, the hold opened up like a cathedral built by drowning.

Her light swept across rows of collapsed shelving, crates slumped into each other like tired passengers, the skeletal ribs of the deck overhead dripping sediment in slow motion. Silt hung in the water so thick that her beam seemed to have weight, a solid thing she was pushing through rather than looking along. Her exhaled bubbles rose and flattened against the overhead, rolled along it like frightened animals seeking escape, and found none.

She had stripped this hold methodically over three years. Brass signal lamps. A captain's chronometer, sold to a collector in the capital who hadn't asked questions because collectors never do until it's too late. Copper pipe by the meter. A sea chest with someone's initials burned into the lid—*A.V.M.*—that Yara had kept instead of sold, because some things you don't put a price on even when you can't explain why.

But the strongbox she'd found only last week, wedged beneath the collapsed chart table in what had been the after cabin, and she'd needed time to prepare. Time to rig the lift gear. Time to think about whether she wanted to know what was inside badly enough to haul sixty kilos of iron up through forty feet of hostile water alone.

She'd decided yes. She always decided yes. That was the whole architecture of her life: decide yes, do the work, count the money, feel the emptiness afterward, repeat.

The box sat where she'd left it, half-buried in sediment, its lock plate green with age. She brushed it clear with her glove. Naval issue, heavy gauge, the kind designed to survive a fire, a flood, or a court-martial inquiry. Stenciled letters on the lid, mostly eaten by corrosion, but her fingers read them better than her eyes:

*PROVISION — DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT AUTHORITY OF—*

The rest was gone. Yara ran her thumb over the ghost of the missing words and felt the old question stir in her chest, the one that had nothing to do with money. *Authority of whom?* And authority to do what—to open it, or to keep it shut?

Seventy years ago, thirty-one men had sailed this river on a mission the navy had erased from every record, and the ship had come to rest in water that didn't officially exist, carrying a box that wasn't supposed to be opened. Somebody had stowed it under the chart table like a thing they didn't want to look at. Somebody had known.

Yara clipped her lift bag to the lifting eyes, connected the small air bottle she'd staged on her last dive, and cracked the valve. The bag bloomed, strained, lifted. Sixty kilos of iron and whatever slept inside it rose off the deck plates, shedding sediment in a slow brown bloom that swallowed her light.

That was when she heard the engine.

It came through the water the way all sounds came down here—not heard but felt, a pressure against her eardrums, a shiver in the mussels on the hull. Diesel. Steady. Big. Not a fishing boat; she knew every fishing engine on this stretch of river the way she knew her own heartbeat. This was something heavier, running a deliberate patrol pattern, and there was only one kind of vessel that patrolled these waters with its running lights dark and its intentions official.

Navy.

Yara killed her dive light and stood in absolute blackness, sixty kilos of forbidden history hanging from her fist, and listened with her whole body.

The engine note deepened, swung, steadied again. Coming closer. They ran this stretch maybe twice a month, the patrol boats out of the downstream station—young sailors sweeping the forbidden zone for smugglers and salvagers, finding neither, because Yara had spent three years learning their patterns the way she'd learned the wreck's. Dawn runs happened after reports of night activity. She'd been careful. She'd been invisible.

But the box was a different animal than a crate of brass fittings. It was too heavy to swim out with, too slow. If they dropped a sonar puck over the side, if they put a boat directly over her ascent point—

The engine passed almost directly overhead. She felt the wash of it, the pressure wave rolling down through the water column, stirring the silt into fresh ghosts. Above her, faint as memory, the hull of the patrol boat drew a black shadow across the last gray suggestion of dawn filtering down from the surface world.

Yara did the math she'd done a thousand times in a thousand tight spots: air remaining, distance to cover, weight to move, luck available. Air was fine. Distance was fine. Weight was the problem, and luck was never fine, luck was just arithmetic you hadn't finished yet.

She waited. Ten breaths. Twenty. The engine faded downstream, unhurried, a predator that hadn't caught a scent—or hadn't bothered to chase one yet.

Then she moved, and this was the part of the work that no one would ever see, the part that lived nowhere but in her muscles: swimming the box and herself along the riverbed in short dragging surges, away from the wreck, away from her anchored skiff, toward the drowned willow stand two hundred meters north where she kept a second cache—a spare tank, a rope ladder, a tarp. Never surface where they expect you. Never keep your treasure where you keep your boat. Her father's rules, all of them, and he'd learned them the hard way, and the hard way had eventually taken him, though not the water. The water had always loved him back.

The willows received her like conspirators. She surfaced inside their drowned canopy, under a ceiling of branches, where even a searchlight would find only tangled shadow, and she breathed—great greedy gulps of cold dawn air—while sixty kilos of sealed orders bobbed beside her, tethered to her wrist.

Through the branches she could see the river. Mist burning off in gold rags. And far downstream, rounding the bend with the smugness of all official machinery, the gray shape of the patrol boat, a figure at its bow rail scanning the water with binoculars.

Yara watched him watch the river, and felt the strange double loneliness of her trade: hidden from everyone, seeing everything, belonging nowhere. He was doing his job. She was doing hers. Between them lay forty feet of water and seventy years of silence, and only one of them was getting paid.

* * *

By full morning she had the box in the skiff, tarped, and was running downstream toward Vask with the current doing half her work. Her arms ached in the good way, the honest way. The adrenaline was draining out of her now, leaving behind the other thing, the thing that always came after—the flat gray quiet where the money should have been satisfaction and wasn't.

She'd sell the box intact. That was the plan, anyway. Buyers for naval artifacts were rare in a country that pretended the Provision had never existed, but rare wasn't the same as nonexistent, and word traveled in the salvage trade the way blood travels in water. Someone would want it. Someone always wanted what they weren't allowed to have.

She just hadn't expected the someone to find her first.

He was waiting at her mooring.

Yara saw him from fifty meters and her hands went quiet on the oars, the way they went quiet before anything important. He stood on the rotted planks of Vask's dock like he'd grown there—wrong clothes for the river, city shoes, a coat too clean for the mud, holding an umbrella against a sun that had already won. Nobody came to Vask by accident. Nobody came to Vask at all.

He raised a hand as she approached, friendly as a fisherman, and smiled with the easy confidence of a man who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered.

"Yara Eriksen," he called across the water, before she'd said a single word. "I hear you've been keeping busy."

The tarp over the strongbox suddenly weighed more than sixty kilos. It weighed everything she didn't know yet, and everything she was about to.

Yara shipped her oars and let the current carry her the last few meters to meet him.
