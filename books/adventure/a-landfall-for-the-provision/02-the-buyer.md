## 2. The Buyer

The strongbox sat on Yara's kitchen table for three days before she could bring herself to open it.

Not because it was locked—she'd had the lock off in the harbor shed the first night, working by lamplight with picks she'd bought from a retired locksmith who asked no questions and charged accordingly. Not because it was heavy, though it was, forty pounds of salt-scalded iron that had ridden the river bottom for longer than she'd been alive. She couldn't open it because opening it would mean deciding what it was. Sealed, it was salvage. A curiosity. A thing she could sell whole, unexamined, to some collector with a warehouse and no conscience. Opened, it would be whatever was inside, and whatever was inside would have weight, and weight was the one thing Yara Eriksen had spent thirty-one years learning to put down rather than pick up.

So it sat on her table, rain ticking against the tin roof of the house she rented by the month from a landlady who was mostly deaf and entirely incurious, and Yara ate her dinners around it, and slept with her bedroom door open so she could see it from her pillow, and told herself she was guarding it against theft when really she was guarding herself against it.

On the fourth morning she gave in.

The hinges shrieked like something dying. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had gone the texture of old skin, were documents. Paper, actual paper, not microfilm, not laminate—paper that had survived four decades underwater because someone had sealed it in wax and tallow before the box went down, someone who had wanted it found eventually but not easily. Yara unwrapped each piece on the table with hands she kept deliberately steady, the way she kept them steady at forty feet when the silt wanted to rise and blind her. Patience. The water taught patience or it taught you nothing.

There were orders, typed on paper gone the color of weak tea. There was a logbook fragment, the pages furred at the edges, the ink browned but legible. There was a letter, handwritten, that she read three times without fully understanding, because it was written in a shorthand of naval bureaucracy that seemed designed to say nothing while meaning everything. *Provision* appeared on every page. So did a word that made her sit back in her chair: *landfall*—not used the way a sailor would use it, but the way a conspirator would. *Landfall to be arranged. Landfall to be confirmed. No landfall on record.*

She didn't know what it meant. She knew what it was worth.

She also knew, with the flat certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading water, that she should put it all back in the box, weld it shut, and drop it off the end of the harbor pier where it could keep its secrets in the company of better swimmers. Instead she rewrapped it, poured herself two fingers of the cheap whisky she kept for cold nights, and sat watching the rain come down the window in silver ropes until the glass went dark.

That was the night she decided to sell. That was also, though she wouldn't understand this until much later, the night she decided not to.

* * *

The dealer found her, which should have been the first warning. In Yara's experience, the good buyers waited to be found; the ones who came looking had already priced what you had and priced you cheaper.

He was staying at the Anchorage Hotel, the only establishment in town with more than six rooms, and he'd sent a boy with a card the morning after she'd made her decision, which meant either coincidence or surveillance, and Yara had never once in her life benefited from coincidence. The card was heavy stock, cream-colored, embossed with a name: **AURELIO VASS**. Beneath the name, in smaller type: *Acquisitions & Appraisals. Maritime Antiquities.* Beneath that, nothing—no city, no address, no affiliation. A man who existed only where he was standing.

She made him wait a day. It was petty and she knew it was petty and she did it anyway, because the one advantage a salvage diver had over everyone else in the transaction was the knowledge that she could always put the thing back in the water, and the buyer could not.

Vass received her in the hotel's sitting room, which he had apparently annexed entire: a tea service on the sideboard, a lamp angled just so, a folding table draped in white cloth like an operating theater. He was smaller than she'd expected, neat, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that had been arranged rather than lived in. His handshake was dry and brief. His eyes did not match the rest of him—there was something unarranged in them, something that moved too fast, a current under a calm surface, and Yara recognized it because she saw it in the river every day. Still water, fast water. She'd built a life on knowing the difference.

"Miss Eriksen." He pronounced it correctly, which surprised her; most people said *Eric-son*, flattening it, and she'd long ago stopped correcting them. "Thank you for coming. Please—sit. Tea? It's decent, which is more than I can say for most of what passes for tea in this part of the world."

"I'm not staying long."

"Of course." He poured anyway, two cups, and set one at her elbow as though the matter were already settled, and she noted that too: a man who created small accomplished facts and let you argue with them at your leisure. "You've brought something up from the Provision."

Not a question. She hadn't told him what she'd found, or that she'd found anything. The wreck's location was known only in the broadest, drunkenest way—every diver in the district claimed to know where the Provision lay, and every one of them was lying or wrong, because Yara had found her by accident six years ago in a channel the charts marked as featureless, and she'd told no one, not even her landlady, not even the harbor master who took his cut of her catch and asked nothing in return.

"How would you know that?"

"Because I pay for information, Miss Eriksen, and I pay well, and someone in this town finally had something worth the money." He smiled, and the smile was warm and entirely without warmth. "Don't trouble yourself with who. It's a river town. Everyone sells something—their catch, their labor, their silence. Silence is the only commodity that appreciates."

She didn't touch the tea. "Then you know what I have. Name your price."

"I'd like to see it first."

"You'll see a description. Iron strongbox, sealed, recovered intact from a wreck in this district. Contents wrapped and waxed, apparently paper documents, apparently naval, apparently Provision-era. Condition good. Provenance—" she let the pause stretch, "—complicated."

"Complicated," Vass repeated, and something in his voice changed, a degree of temperature, the current under the surface quickening. "You know, most divers who sit in that chair tell me they found a porthole and a boot. They hedge. They're ashamed of how little they have. You're the first one to hedge because of how much. That's rare. That's—" he considered, "—valuable. To both of us."

"I said name a price."

"Triple whatever you were hoping for." He said it lightly, tossing it out like a coin, and then, before she could arrange her face around it, he named a figure.

Yara kept her hands still. It was the figure she'd been hoping for, tripled, and then some—the kind of money that retired a diver, that bought a boat with an engine that started on the first try, that paid off the harbor master and the landlady and the cold-water years all at once. She had spent her whole adult life underwater hauling up other people's lost things and selling them for enough to eat, and here was a man offering to buy her out of that life in a single afternoon.

Which was exactly why she didn't say yes. Things that came easy came from somewhere, and the somewhere was always deeper than you could see from the surface.

"Contents only," she said. "The box stays with me."

"The box is iron and rust, Miss Eriksen. The contents are the—"

"The box stays with me." She heard her own voice go flat and river-bottom hard, the voice she used on deckhands and drunks. "Take it or leave it."

Vass studied her for a long moment, and she had the sense of being weighed, measured, entered into some ledger she'd never see. Then he inclined his head, a small precise bow. "Contents only. Agreed, pending inspection. You'll bring them tomorrow?"

"I'll bring them tomorrow."

"Splendid." He rose, and she rose, and she was nearly at the door when he said it, casually, the way a man mentions the weather.

"One small thing. The coordinates."

Yara stopped. The room's warmth went out of it all at once, the way a fire dies when a door opens somewhere you can't see.

"I never gave you coordinates."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You didn't. Which is what interests me. Because my information—expensive information, reliable information—includes a set of coordinates for the Provision, and those coordinates place her in the wrong channel entirely. Too far east. Too shallow. And yet you, Miss Eriksen, are clearly diving her, because you're standing in my sitting room smelling of river silt and holding a salvage claim on documents that only exist in one place. So either my information is wrong—" he smiled, "—or the charts are. And I do so hate it when the charts are wrong. It means someone went to trouble."

The rain had started again without her noticing, ticking against the hotel window, and Yara stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame and felt, with terrible clarity, the sensation she knew from the deep dives—the one she'd never told anyone, the one the other divers would have laughed at or worse. The feeling, forty feet down in the black, that the wreck was not empty. That the Provision was not a thing she was exploring but a thing that was aware of her, patient and drowned and watching, and that everything she took from her was being taken *note of*.

She had thought that feeling was the nitrogen. She had thought it was loneliness. Standing in Vass's warm sitting room with his expensive tea going cold behind her, she understood for the first time that it had never been either. The wreck was watching back. It had been watching her for six years. And now, through this neat, dry-handed man with the fast eyes, it had sent word up the line.

"The charts are wrong," she said. "They usually are. Good day, Mr. Vass."

"Tomorrow, then," Vass said pleasantly. "And Miss Eriksen—do think about the coordinates. Not for me. For whoever comes asking after me. Because there is always a whoever, and they are rarely as polite."

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the harbor road, in the rain, because she wanted the time to think and because she didn't trust herself not to run.

The town did what river towns do in rain: shuttered, hunched, went gray. A fisherman nodded to her from under an awning. Two children raced a paper boat along the flooded gutter until it dissolved. Ordinary life, ordinary rain, and Yara walked through it feeling like a woman who had stepped off a charted channel and into water the maps didn't admit existed.

*Someone in this town finally had something worth the money.* She turned the sentence over and over, looking for the seam. The harbor master? He knew she dove the eastern channels, though not which one. The locksmith? He knew about the lock, not the box. The boy who sold her lamp oil and never met her eyes? Any of them. All of them. A river town, where silence was a commodity, and she had been rich in it for six years without knowing she was spending it.

And the coordinates. *The wrong channel entirely.* Someone had given Vass coordinates for the Provision—wrong ones, deliberately wrong ones, wrong with intent, wrong the way a false trail is wrong. Which meant someone, sometime in the last forty years, had gone to the trouble of seeding the world with a lie about where the ship lay. Navies didn't do that for ships that were merely lost. Navies did that for ships they wanted forgotten, and they didn't bother forgetting anything innocent.

She got home soaked to the skin and found herself, without deciding to, checking the strongbox. It sat where it always sat, iron and patient, and she stood over it dripping on the floorboards like a woman at a graveside.

"You're trouble," she told it. "You always were. I should have left you down there."

The box said nothing. Boxes didn't. But the documents inside had said plenty, in their tea-colored, bureaucratic shorthand: *landfall to be arranged, no landfall on record.* Orders that ordered nothing and therefore ordered everything. A ship that had gone down in the wrong channel, in waters the charts called featureless, carrying paper someone had waxed and sealed against the very water that was supposed to erase it.

Someone had wanted it found eventually. Someone had also wanted the world looking in the wrong channel while it waited to be found.

Yara dried off, ate standing up, and laid the documents out on the table one more time under the lamp. She read the letter again—the handwritten one, the only human voice among all the typed orders—and this time she noticed what she'd missed. At the bottom, where a signature should have been, there was no name. There was a single word in a careful, old-fashioned hand, pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel it with her fingertip like Braille:

*Eriksen.*

She sat down slowly. Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother, who had died before Yara was old enough to ask questions, who had come up the coast from somewhere she never spoke of, who had taught Yara to swim in cold water before she could properly walk, who had gone quiet and still-eyed whenever the navy was mentioned, and who had left her granddaughter nothing but a talent for holding her breath and a restlessness that had never once let her settle anywhere.

The lamp hissed. The rain came down. And Yara Eriksen, who had spent her whole life selling what the river gave up and asking nothing about where it had been, sat in her rented kitchen with her grandmother's name pressed into forty-year-old paper under her thumb, and understood that she had not found the Provision at all.

The Provision had found her. It had been waiting in the wrong channel, in the featureless water, patient as a drowned thing, for the one diver with the one name—and now the name was out in the world, sitting in a silver-templed dealer's ledger, attached to a price, and tomorrow she was supposed to hand the paper over and walk away rich.

She looked at the documents a long time. Then, very carefully, she took the ones that mattered—the letter, and the logbook fragment—and she did not put them back in the strongbox. She wrapped them in oilcloth, sealed them in a waterproof pouch, and stowed them in the one place she trusted, which was on her own body, against her own skin, where she kept the things she couldn't afford to lose.

The strongbox, with its decoy papers, would go to Vass in the morning. Let him buy what she chose to sell. Let him think he'd won.

But in the morning, when she carried the box through the rain to the Anchorage Hotel, Vass was waiting on the porch under the eaves, dry and neat and smiling, and the first thing he said, before good morning, before anything, was:

"I've been thinking, Miss Eriksen, about how a woman dives alone for six years in unmarked water and stays alive. It takes more than skill. It takes a reason to keep coming back. And I find myself wondering—" his fast eyes settled on her, gentle as a hook, "—what your reason is. And whether it's the same reason the ship is where it is. Because I've made inquiries about the *Eriksen* name, you see. And the navy's answer was very short, and very expensive, and very interesting."

He held the door open for her, courteous as a host, and Yara stepped past him into the warm room knowing that whatever she sold him today, she was already selling something else—herself, her name, her grandmother's silence—and that the only way out of the deep was down.
