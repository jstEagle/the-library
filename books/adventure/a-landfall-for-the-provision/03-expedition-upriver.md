## 3. Expedition Upriver

The dealer's name was Corvane, and he paid for everything in cash that smelled faintly of other people's mistakes.

Yara counted the bills twice on the scarred table of the Salt Anchor's back room while he talked, because counting gave her hands something honest to do and let her listen without seeming to. He had a voice like poured honey over gravel—warm, unhurried, designed to make you feel like a partner rather than a purchase.

"Four weeks," he said. "Provisioned generously. A riverboat chartered out of Grayharbor, crew included. Your fee is triple what we agreed, plus a completion bonus if we locate verifiable evidence of the Provision's final route upstream."

"Upstream." Yara looked up from the money. "The Provision went down in coastal water. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows what the navy told them to know." Corvane smiled with his whole face, which somehow made it worse. "I've spent eleven years and most of an inheritance chasing this wreck, Ms. Eriksen. The charts I've acquired—some of them very old, very private—suggest the ship made her last voyage on the river before she ever touched salt. I want to trace it. Every bend, every anchorage. And I want the diver who found my strongbox to be the one who reads the water for me."

*My strongbox.* She noticed that. He'd said *my*, not *the*. As if ownership transferred the moment he'd paid for its contents.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I hire someone else, and you go back to diving a dead hull alone at dawn while patrol boats hunt you like a seal." He spread his hands, apologetic. "I'd rather it was you. You have instincts. Instincts can't be bought, only borrowed."

She should have walked away. She knew that even then—knew it in the same place in her chest where the cold of deep water lived, the place that had been quietly telling her for years that she was spending her life on other people's drowned property. But three times her rate was three times her rate, and the river ran through country she'd never seen above the surface, and some traitorous part of her—the part that had stared up through forty feet of green-black water at the dawn light and thought *there has to be more than this*—leaned toward yes before her caution could weigh anchor.

"I read the water," she said. "I don't fight anyone's battles. And I keep anything I find on my own dives."

"Agreed." He shook her hand. His palm was dry and cool, like paper.

It wasn't until she was halfway home through the drizzle, money heavy against her ribs, that she realized he'd never asked how she'd found the strongbox. Not once. A man who'd chased a wreck for eleven years, who knew enough to ask about coordinates she'd never given him—and he hadn't asked the one question any real salvager would have asked first.

He already knew the answer. He just wanted to see if she'd lie.

* * *

Grayharbor smelled of tar, fish rot, and diesel, and the riverboat waiting at the coal dock was named the *Maren Lee*, which struck Yara as too gentle a name for something with a hull like a fist. She came aboard at first light with two duffel bags and her dive kit wrapped in oilcloth, and stood a moment on the gangplank looking at the three strangers who were to be her world for the next month.

The tall man was stacking crates with a soldier's economy of motion—no wasted reach, everything squared, everything where his hand would find it in the dark. He wore his hair cropped close and his silence closer. When he turned and saw her, his eyes did a quick inventory: hands, hips, eyes, exits. Ex-soldier, then, or something adjacent. He nodded once, the nod of a man reserving judgment.

"Eriksen?" His voice matched his shoulders. "Marsh. Security."

"Security," she repeated. "For a document hunt."

"For whatever documents turn out to be attached to." He didn't smile. "Corvane pays me to be pessimistic."

The second stranger was crouched at the rail with a hand lens pressed to his eye, examining something on a sliver of bark with the total absorption of a man who had forgotten other people existed. He was younger than Marsh, softer everywhere except the forearms, which were brown and corded from fieldwork. When Yara's shadow fell across him he startled, then beamed at her with unguarded delight, as though her arrival were the best thing to happen all week.

"You must be the diver! Teo Vasquez, botany." He pumped her hand with both of his, barking his shin on a crate in his enthusiasm. "Do you know there are reed beds six miles upstream that flower blue? Blue, Eriksen. No survey has ever recorded them. Nobody's botanized that stretch since—" he waved vaguely at the whole twentieth century, "—since anyone bothered. That's why I'm here. That's why any of us are here, really. The blank spaces." He said it the way other men said *the love of my life*.

The third was a woman sitting cross-legged on the deckhouse roof with a radio case open beside her, soldering iron in hand, a thin curl of smoke rising. She didn't look down when Yara came aboard, but she spoke as if they were mid-conversation already.

"If you're the guide, your boat leaves when I finish this joint, and not before. Ines." She held up the soldering iron by way of handshake. "Radio. Which means I'm the only one aboard who talks to the outside world, so be nice to me."

"She's been saying that since dawn," Marsh said flatly.

"Because it keeps being true."

Yara watched them a moment—Marsh squaring his crates, Teo apologizing to a fern he'd crushed, Ines blowing on hot metal and humming something tuneless—and felt the old familiar wall go up inside her, brick by practiced brick. Four weeks. Four weeks trapped on a boat with strangers who would want to talk, want to share watches, want to know things about her. She'd worked solo so long that company felt like a weight belt she hadn't checked the clips on.

But then Ines glanced up, caught her looking, and grinned—a quick, crooked grin with no performance in it—and something in the wall shifted half an inch.

"Coffee's in the pot," Ines said. "It's terrible. Welcome aboard."

* * *

They cast off mid-morning into a river the color of strong tea, and within two hours Yara understood why Corvane needed a guide.

The upper reaches weren't water; they were weather. Currents braided and unbraided around sandbars that migrated overnight. Deadfalls lay just under the surface, black and patient, waiting to open a hull like a tin. The charts showed a channel that had silted shut twenty years ago, and the channel that actually existed showed on no chart at all. Yara stood at the bow reading the water the way her grandmother—*someone's* grandmother, anyway, the woman whose name she carried without knowing why—had presumably once read it, watching the current's skin for the smooth glassy tongues that meant depth and the wrinkled standing waves that meant rock.

"Left of the dead cedar," she called back. "Hard left. Then trust the brown water."

"The brown water looks like it eats boats," Marsh said, but he steered where she pointed, and the *Maren Lee* slid through a gap no wider than her beam, close enough that Yara could have touched the cedar's drowned roots.

Teo hung over the rail the entire time, naming things. Sedge and willow, kingfisher and darter, a heron so gray it seemed cut from the sky itself. He kept a notebook in constant motion, sketching leaf margins in quick sure strokes, and somewhere in the second day Yara realized she'd started listening for his commentary the way you listen for rain on a roof—background comfort, proof the world was still working.

Ines ran the radio every evening at dusk, a scheduled check-in with Corvane's base camp downstream. Her reports were terse, professional, almost coded: position, condition, progress. Nothing about the blue-flowered reeds or the ruins of an old trading post they found on day four, its chimney standing alone in a meadow like a monument to someone's abandoned dinner. Yara noticed that Ines reported none of the things that made the trip feel human, only the things that made it feel like a mission.

She filed that away. Divers learned early what to do with observations: hold them, press them flat, keep them dry until needed.

* * *

On the fifth night they tied up below a bluff where the river narrowed and the current sang against stone, and Marsh produced a bottle of something harsh from the bottom of his kit and four tin cups, and nobody pretended they weren't going to drink it.

"To blank spaces," Teo said, raising his cup.

"To getting paid," Yara said.

"To nothing going wrong," said Marsh, and Ines laughed at him, a real laugh, head tipped back, and said, "You absolute funeral of a man," and after a moment—even Marsh smiled, briefly, like a door opening and shutting.

They sat around a driftwood fire while the river muttered past in the dark. Teo talked about his mother, who had catalogued orchids in the southern wetlands until the fever took her, and how he'd inherited both her notebooks and her conviction that every unnamed thing deserved a name. Ines talked about her father's shop, repairing radios in a town so small the radio was sometimes the only voice in it, and how she'd learned morse before she learned cursive. Marsh said almost nothing, but he refilled cups without being asked, and when Teo shivered he shifted the firewood closer without comment, and Yara understood that this was how the man talked—in fuel and small services instead of words.

And Yara, when they turned to her, found herself saying more than she meant to. About diving the Provision at dawn. About the way sound moved down there, the groan of the old hull settling like a big animal dreaming. About hauling up the strongbox and feeling, for one strange moment, that the wreck had *let* her take it.

"That's the nitrogen talking," Marsh said.

"I wasn't diving then."

"Then it's the whiskey."

"It's neither." She looked into the fire. "You spend enough time down there, you stop feeling like a visitor. The wreck knows you. Some nights I'd swear it's been waiting."

Nobody laughed. The fire popped. Out on the black water, something large turned over and sank again, and the river kept its own counsel.

* * *

By the eighth day they had passed the last registered settlement, a cluster of roofs and a fuel dock that Ines logged in her report, and entered water that appeared on Corvane's old charts in faded ink and on modern ones not at all. The banks closed in. The forest came right down to the current, old-growth cedar and hemlock throwing the river into permanent dusk, and the air grew cool and green-smelling, thick enough to chew.

That evening, making camp on a gravel bar, Yara stood hip-deep in the shallows rinsing her dive mask and felt it again—that sensation from the wreck, of being watched by something that was not hostile but was definitely *present*. The forest along the ridge was silent in the wrong way. No jays. No squirrels scolding. Just the river and the wind and a stillness with weight to it.

"Eriksen." Marsh had come down to the water's edge, rifle slung, and he was looking at the ridge too. "You feel that?"

"Feel what?"

"Nothing." He said it like an accusation. "There's nothing up there. There should be birds."

"Maybe the birds have better sense than we do."

He grunted, unconvinced, and scanned the treeline a moment longer before turning back. But that night Yara took the middle watch, and she sat facing the ridge instead of the river, and she kept the fire low.

Across the coals, Ines was awake too, radio headset around her neck, and their eyes met in the firelight. Ines didn't ask what she was looking at. She just reached over and turned her own camp stool to face the ridge as well, and settled into it, and began, softly, to tap out idle morse on the armrest—*nothing to report, nothing to report*—as if the rhythm itself were a kind of company.

Yara watched the dark trees and listened to the tapping and thought, with a surprise that felt almost like grief: *four weeks is going to end.*

Then, far up on the ridge, where the blackness between the trunks was deepest, something moved—not an animal's movement, not a bird-start, but a slow, deliberate shifting of weight, the way a person moves when they've been standing watch a long time and finally step behind cover.

The tapping stopped. Ines had heard—or seen—something too.

Neither of them said a word. But in the morning, when Teo found fresh-cut wood chips scattered on the gravel bar, pale and curling, still smelling of sap, and asked brightly who'd been chopping, no one answered—

—and Yara looked at the axe marks, clean and recent, no more than hours old, and understood that the blank spaces on the map had been blank for exactly as long as someone had been erasing them.
