## 4. Signs of Passage

The river had a voice, Ines decided on the fourth day, and it never stopped talking.

She heard it even over the engine's laboring cough, even through the headphones she wore half the night to keep the frequency open—a low, constant commentary of water over stone, water under root, water sliding against the hull of the *Marta B* like a hand testing the seam of a coat. It was not a comforting sound, exactly. It was an attentive one. As if the river were taking notes.

Ines understood that impulse. She took notes too.

Her logbook was a fat green thing with a cracked spine, military surplus from a surplus store in a city none of them named anymore, and by the fourth morning it had already eaten three pens. She wrote everything: fuel consumption, water depth readings off Yara's soundings, the coordinates Marsh called out at each bend, the weather, the birds. She wrote down what Teo collected and where. She wrote down the times Yara went into the water and the times she came out, because someone should, because a logbook was a promise you made to whoever came after—that this happened, that we were here, that we noticed.

Nobody had asked her to keep such a careful record. The dealer—Voss, he'd said, call me Voss, everyone does—had given her a radio and a schedule and a set of check-in times written on cream-colored stationery with a watermark too fine to photograph properly. Twice daily, at 0700 and 1900, she was to transmit a status code and nothing more. Position reports were to be verbal only, never written down.

*Never write down where we are.* That instruction had seemed reasonable in the dealer's air-conditioned office, sensible even, the kind of caution serious expeditions practiced. Out here, four days upriver with the last registered settlement two hundred kilometers behind them and the forest pressing in green and total on both banks, it had begun to feel like something else. Like being asked to help make them all disappear.

She didn't write that in the log. Not yet. But she thought it, and thinking it was a kind of writing.

"Ines." Marsh's voice came forward from the bow, pitched low under the engine noise. "Kill the motor."

She throttled down. The *Marta B* lost way with a shudder, and then the river's voice filled the whole world—the slap of current against the hull, the drip of condensation from the canopy overhead, somewhere upstream the percussion of water going over a fallen tree. The silence the engine left behind was not silence at all. It was the loudest thing she'd heard in days.

Marsh stood at the bow with his rifle slung across his back, one hand shading his eyes. He was a big man gone quietly soft around the middle, with the particular stillness of someone who had learned that moving fast got people shot. He'd told her once, in the flat tone men used for things they didn't want sympathy about, that he'd done eleven years in uniform and the last six of them listening for sounds that weren't there. Now, apparently, he listened professionally.

"What is it?" Yara came up from the stern, where she'd been coiling line. Her hair was still damp from the morning's dive at the submerged snag—she checked every deep hole they passed, compulsively, as if the river owed her something and she meant to collect. Salt-crusted, sunburned, permanently squinting: she looked like a woman carved out of the coast and dropped into the wrong landscape. "Snag?"

"Smoke," Marsh said.

Ines smelled it a moment later—or thought she did. Faint, greenish, the smell of wood burned wet. Or maybe it was just the diesel exhaust lingering in her memory, playing tricks. Up here the senses got bored and started inventing things. Yesterday she'd sworn she heard a telephone ringing in the trees, clear as anything, three trills and a pause, until Teo pointed out the bird making the sound and she'd laughed so hard she'd had to sit down.

But Marsh wasn't laughing. Marsh hadn't laughed since the second day.

"Where?" Yara asked.

"Ahead. East bank. Maybe two kilometers." He turned, and his eyes found hers with the weight of an order. "Get us a fix. I want our position exact before we go any further."

She got them a fix. Backwater eddy, right bank, bearing 041 to the split-trunk fig on the point. She read it back twice and logged it, and while her pen moved she watched Marsh watching the treeline, and she wrote down something else, small, in the margin where margins were for truths too thin to survive a full sentence:

*He smells smoke. So do I.*

* * *

They found the wood an hour later, at a place where the bank flattened into a shelf of gray clay and the forest pulled back just enough to show a curtain of hanging vine.

It was Teo who spotted it first—he was standing in the bow with his collecting case open across his knees, doing his quiet botanist's arithmetic of leaf shapes—and it was Teo who said, in his mild, accented voice, "Someone has been cutting here," as if remarking on the weather.

The stumps were fresh. Four of them, maybe five, in a rough crescent near the bank, each cut clean and low, the pale wood still bright as bone, still beading sap at the edges. Around them lay the branches—not dragged, not piled, but *arranged*, laid parallel like the ribs of some enormous animal returned to its skeleton. And the cuts themselves—

Yara crouched beside the largest stump and ran her thumb along the scar. "Not a chainsaw."

"No," Teo agreed. He had taken out one of his little knives, the good ones he kept wrapped in oilcloth, and was examining the cut face with the tenderness he usually reserved for orchids. "An axe. A very good axe. Very sharp." He looked up, and behind his wire glasses his eyes were troubled in a way Ines had not seen before. "And recent. Days. Perhaps a week."

"Logging concession?" Marsh's voice came out flat and hard, a door closing. "There are concessions up here. Paper companies. They don't file their cut maps with anybody."

"There's no road within fifty kilometers of here," Yara said. "I've been running these waters since I was nineteen. There's no road, no camp, no company stamp on any chart I've ever seen. Nobody hauls timber out of this stretch—you'd have to run the lower rapids with a barge, and nobody's stupid enough to try."

"Then somebody's stupid enough to cut trees and leave them lying here." Marsh straightened, scanning the bank, the vines, the green wall beyond. His hand had drifted, without his seeming to notice, to the sling of his rifle. "Squatters. Prospectors. Some hermit. This river's got two hundred kilometers of nowhere along it; it collects people like driftwood."

That was the official version, and Ines wrote it down, because it was plausible and because Marsh outranked everyone on this boat whether anyone had said so or not. *Fresh axe cuts, east bank, likely unauthorized logging or squatter activity. No evidence of permanent settlement.*

But her pen hesitated over the page, because the arrangement of the branches bothered her. Squatters didn't arrange. Squatters hacked and heaped and moved on. Someone had laid those branches out with care, parallel, deliberate—like a message, or like tidying. Like people who expected to come back to this place and wanted it to look respected when they did.

You didn't tidy up after yourself in a forest you were passing through. You tidied up in a forest you lived in.

She wrote that down too, in the margin, small.

* * *

They made camp that evening on a sandbar midstream, because Marsh refused to sleep on either bank now—"Water on three sides," he said, "and I'll take my chances with drowning"—and the routine assembled itself around the fire with the ease of something already old. Yara rigged the tarp. Teo pressed specimens by lantern light, murmuring Latin to himself like prayers. Marsh cleaned the rifle he had already cleaned. And Ines climbed onto the supply crates with the radio, unfolded the antenna, and waited for 1900.

The transmission itself was thirty seconds of ritual. *Marta B to Waypoint. Day four. All personnel accounted for. Progress nominal. Next contact 0700.* The reply came back in Voss's base-camp operator's flat clipped voice: *Waypoint copies. Maintain schedule. Confirm river stage rising or falling.*

"Falling," she said. "Slowly."

*Waypoint copies. Out.*

She sat with the headset half off her ear afterward, listening to the static breathe. It had a texture tonight, that static—a graininess underneath, like sand stirred into water. Interference from the storm cell building west, probably. Probably. She tuned across the band slowly, the way she did most nights, sweeping the frequencies the way a lighthouse sweeps the sea, not looking for anything in particular, just confirming that the world beyond the river still existed and was still talking to itself. Fishermen on 4 megahertz, arguing about prices. A time signal, patient and metronomic. Somewhere far south, a woman reading numbers in Portuguese, over and over, to no one who would ever admit to listening.

All of it human. All of it proof that the planet was still crowded and loud and full of people who needed to talk.

Then, at the top of the sweep, faint as breath on glass, something else.

A pattern. Three tones, ascending. A pause. Three tones again.

She froze with her hand on the dial. It lasted perhaps ten seconds, that little ascending figure, and then it folded back into static as if it had never been. She swept back down, found the frequency again, and held it, both hands on the radio now, heart tapping at her wrists.

Nothing. Static, and the static's grainy undertone, and beneath that—if she imagined hard enough—the river's endless commentary, coming through the hull, through the crate she sat on, through the bones of her own body.

Three ascending tones. She knew that interval. Every operator alive knew that interval. It was the first bar of nearly every attention signal ever devised—the little musical knock on the door that said *someone is calling, someone is calling, listen.*

But it wasn't on any assigned frequency. It wasn't a distress band, wasn't a marine channel, wasn't anything registered anywhere she knew. Which meant either it was a stray harmonic from some legitimate transmitter, bouncing off the storm front—

—or it was someone transmitting without a license, without a registration, without wanting to be found. Someone with a working transmitter, up here, in two hundred kilometers of nowhere.

She looked at the logbook. Looked at it for a long time.

Then she wrote: *1932 hrs. Brief unidentified signal, approx. 6210 kHz, three-tone attention pattern, duration ~10 sec. Likely atmospheric reflection. Monitoring.*

Likely atmospheric reflection. She stared at the words and felt them sitting in her mouth like a coin she was deciding whether to swallow.

* * *

The moccasin turned up the next morning, and after that there was no more pretending the river was empty.

It was Yara who found it—of course it was Yara, who saw everything the water touched, who read currents and debris lines the way Ines read frequencies. They'd stopped at a downstream point of gravel where the current sorted the river's leavings into neat ribbons: leaves, seeds, foam, twigs. Yara walked the line with her hands in her pockets, head down, and stopped so abruptly that Marsh, behind her, nearly walked into her back.

"Well?" he said.

She didn't answer. She crouched, and picked it up, and held it out on her open palm.

It was a child's shoe. A moccasin, soft hide, stitched at the toe with sinew in a pattern of tiny dark diamonds. It was small—smaller than Ines's hand. It was not rotted, not sun-bleached to ghost-gray, not swollen with weeks of water. The hide was still supple. The stitching was tight. One lace was missing, torn rather than untied, and inside, when Yara tipped it, a single grain of river sand ran out like a secret.

"That's not old," Ines said. Her own voice sounded strange to her, coming from outside her body.

"It's not old," Yara agreed quietly.

"Kids lose shoes." Marsh took it, turned it over, held it up against the light as if authenticity could be verified by translucence. "Kids lose shoes everywhere. Somebody camps on this river with their family—fisherman, hunter, whatever—and their kid loses a shoe in the water, and the water brings it here. That's all this is." He said it firmly, conclusively, the way he said everything he wanted to be true. "That's all this is."

"Two hundred kilometers from the last settlement," Yara said.

"People travel."

"With children. Through uncharted rapids. To cut trees with hand axes and stack the branches just so." Yara took the moccasin back. For a moment she just looked at it, turning it in her brown fingers, and Ines saw something move across her face that she couldn't name—something older than fear. Recognition, almost. The look of a woman hearing her own name called in a language she'd forgotten she spoke.

"We should turn back," Teo said softly, from the boat.

Everyone looked at him. He stood with his collecting case hugged to his chest like a shield, and his mild face was grave. "Fresh cutting. A child's shoe. Smoke that Marsh smelled and I smelled also, though I said nothing because I doubted myself." He shook his head slowly. "Gentlemen. Ladies. We are being *told* something. Whether it is a warning or a welcome, I do not know. But the river is not empty, and I think—we are not the ones who decide what happens next."

"The contract says source to delta," Marsh said. His jaw had gone tight, the muscle jumping under the stubble. "The contract says we survey the final voyage route, and we're paid, and we go home. Nobody pays us to spook at a shoe." He thrust out his hand. "Give me that. I'll log it as debris."

"You'll log it as nothing," Yara said, "and I'll keep it."

For a moment it hung there, the two of them eye to eye, the moccasin between them like a small dead bird. Then Marsh exhaled through his nose, a soldier's breath, dismissing a subordinate's insubordination because the mission mattered more, and turned away, and started giving orders about the day's run in a voice carefully stripped of everything he actually felt.

Ines opened her logbook.

*Day five, 0815 hrs. Recovered from debris line at gravel point (see position fix): one child's moccasin, hide, hand-stitched, condition consistent with loss within days, not months. No registered settlement within 200 km. M. assesses as incidental loss from transient campers. T. recommends withdrawal. Y. retains artifact.*

Her pen hovered. The morning fog was lifting off the water in long white banners, and the forest stood behind it, green and total and silent, and she felt suddenly, vividly certain that the silence was not emptiness. It was discretion. It was the silence of a household when strangers walk past the windows.

Somewhere upriver, a child had gone barefoot home.

She added one more line, in the margin, in her smallest handwriting, the handwriting she used for things she might someday have to deny:

*We are being watched. I am sure of it the way I am sure of a signal: not because I can prove it, but because the needle moves.*

At 0700 the next morning, when she keyed the mike for the scheduled check-in, the static answered her before she'd finished the call sign—three ascending tones, close now, close as the far bank, unmistakable.

And this time, beneath them, barely threaded through the interference, she could have sworn she heard breathing.
