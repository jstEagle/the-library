## 5. Static

The storm came out of the west the way Marsh had seen storms come in the Karst—without negotiation. One hour the river ran brown and sluggish under a sky like hammered tin; the next, the air pressure dropped so hard his ears popped and the birds went silent all at once, as if someone had cut a wire.

"Radio first," Ines said, already moving. She'd read the sky before any of them. That was the thing about Ines that Marsh both respected and distrusted: she was always three minutes ahead of the weather, of the current, of everyone's moods. A woman who lived her life in the gap between transmission and reply.

They made camp on a gravel shelf beneath an overhang of black slate, the kind of spot Teo called *a geological mercy* and Marsh called defensible. He got the tarp up while Yara and Teo hauled the crates above the waterline, and Ines knelt over the radio set with the tenderness other people reserved for infants.

The set was a Kolchak 9, military surplus older than any of them, its casing scarred with paint that might once have been navy grey. Ines had coaxed it back to life twice already since they'd left the dealer's base camp downstream—once with a soldering iron heated over a camp stove, once by sacrificing a length of copper from her own antenna rig. She talked to it while she worked, low and constant, a stream of endearments and curses that Marsh pretended not to hear.

"Base camp, base camp, this is Expedition Provision, do you copy." Her voice flattened into its professional register, each word spaced like stones across a creek. "Position forty-one kilometres upstream of last fix. River rising. Visibility poor. Requesting confirmation of resupply window at the confluence, over."

Static answered her—a long, oceanic hiss, the sound of the whole atmosphere rubbing its hands together.

Marsh crouched at the mouth of the overhang and watched the rain arrive. It came not as drops but as a wall, a grey curtain sweeping upriver toward them, erasing the far bank, then the near bank, then everything but the drumming on the slate and the sudden roar of the water. The river had been high for two days. Now it began to speak in a new voice, deeper, freighted with things it had torn loose somewhere above them—branches, silt, he didn't want to think about what else.

"Ines." He had to pitch his voice to carry. "How long can you hold the frequency?"

She didn't look up. "As long as the battery holds and the antenna stands. Neither of which is a promise."

"The dealer wants position reports every twelve hours. If we go dark—"

"If we go dark, he assumes the worst and sends no one, because sending anyone costs money." Ines adjusted a dial with a fingernail worn to quick. "Or he assumes opportunity and sends someone. With Herr Voss, those are usually the same calculation."

Yara appeared at Marsh's shoulder, dripping, a coil of rope over one arm. Up close she smelled of river and tar and something faintly metallic—the wreck still on her, he thought, even days away from it. "He'll send someone," she said quietly. "That's what I'd bet."

"You know him well?" Marsh asked.

"I know men who buy things they can't price." She said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "They don't send rescue parties. They send auditors."

Teo laughed from the shadows where he'd wedged himself among the specimen crates, drying his spectacles on his shirttail. "An audit! Yes. Of our bones." He seemed delighted by the idea. The botanist treated danger the way he treated weather—as an interesting variable. Marsh had served with men like that. They died smiling, and their smiles haunted you.

Lightning walked down the far ridge, three strides, close enough that the air tasted of hot metal. Then thunder rolled up the valley like a barrel going downstairs.

Ines got through at 19:40, or almost got through.

Marsh heard it happen from two metres away. The static thinned—parted, like fog—and a human voice swam up through it, thin and warped, vowels stretched into ghosts. *"—Provision, we—" A crackle. "—hold your—" And then the voice drowned again, and Ines's hand froze on the key, and for four full seconds nobody breathed.

Then the sky detonated.

The strike hit the ridge above them—he saw the flash through the rain, white on black, a tree bursting apart in a fistful of burning embers—and every hair on Marsh's arms stood up, and when his hearing came back Ines was saying a word in her mother's language, very softly, over and over, and the radio's pilot lamp was dead.

"No," she said. "No, no, come on, sweetheart, no."

She worked on it through the worst of the storm, by lamplight, sleeves pushed up, a magnifying loupe strapped over one eye like a jeweler's. Marsh watched her hands: steady, precise, patient. He'd known radio operators who wept over dead sets. Ines performed surgery.

"It's the aerial mast," she reported near midnight, when the rain had settled from fury into a long grey drizzle. "The strike came down the ridge line and induction fried the feed. I can rebuild it. But the backup antenna is in the equipment crate, and the equipment crate is—"

She stopped. Looked at the place where the equipment crate should have been, wedged between Teo's plant press and the spare fuel cans.

It wasn't there.

* * *

They searched by lamplight while the river climbed.

Marsh organized it without deciding to—it was simply what his body did, the old grammar of sweeps and sectors asserting itself. Yara took the shoreline. Teo took the overhang, calling out the Latin names of things growing there as though grief were a species he could catalogue. Ines stayed with the dead radio, because someone had to guard it, and because her hands were shaking now and she knew it.

The crate was gone. So were two days of rations—not the whole stores, just a careful selection: hardtack, smoked fish, the sealed sugar tin. Taken, not scattered. No animal did that. No flood did that, either, though the river had licked the lowest edge of their campsite and left a tideline of grit across the tarp floor.

"Somebody carried it," Yara said. She stood at the water's edge, lantern held low, reading the ground the way she read currents. "Two people, maybe. Heavy load. They went north along the shale, where it doesn't hold prints."

"Or the river took it and the rations floated." Marsh heard his own voice go flat and official, the voice he hated. "Crate was light. Buoyant."

"With the lid lashed? No." Yara turned. In the lantern light her face was all planes and shadow, hard to read, harder to trust. "Someone took it, Marsh."

"Who?" He let the word out too fast, too loud. He watched it land on all three of them like a dropped tool. "That's the question, isn't it. Who. Because I've been counting, and there are exactly four of us on this gravel bar, and none of us heard anything, and one of us was on watch."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Rain on slate. River chewing at the bank, a little closer than an hour ago.

"I was on watch until midnight," Ines said evenly. "I heard nothing but the storm. Which proves either that whoever it was moved very well, or that I'm lying, or that I'm incompetent. You'll have to pick, Sergeant."

"Don't." Marsh put his palm up. "Don't do that. Don't make it a performance."

"A performance." Ines rose slowly, lamplight catching the loupe still hooked in her collar, turning her eye into a coin. "Three weeks ago you were pulling me out of a flooded culvert in Vask and buying my coffee. Now I'm a suspect because a crate walked off in a lightning storm."

"Because things keep walking off!" He hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, gate blown open. "The marker buoys at the second portage. The spare fuel line. Teo's knife, day four. Little things, one at a time, and every time we explain them away—currents, carelessness, miscounting. Well, I've miscounted my last crate. Somebody on this expedition is reporting to somebody else. Or feeding themselves off our stores. Or leading us somewhere we haven't agreed to go."

"Somewhere," Yara repeated softly. "Like where?"

And there it was, the thing underneath, the thing he'd been circling for two days without naming: the fresh-cut stumps they'd found at the bend below the falls, pale as bone, sap still wet. The child's moccasin, sun-bleached, small as his hand, sitting in Ines's logbook between pages of coordinates. Miles from any registered settlement. Miles from anything registered at all.

"There are people up this valley," Marsh said. "People who aren't on any chart, cutting wood and losing shoes and apparently shopping from our supplies. And every one of us signed on to follow a dead ship's route into country the navy won't map. So forgive me if I keep count of the crates."

Teo emerged from the dark with his spectacles fogged, holding his notebook against his chest like a breviary. "You know what I find interesting?" he said mildly, which was how Teo prefaced everything alarming. "The cuts on those stumps. I looked at them. Clean, angled, single strokes. Not survival cutting—harvest cutting. Someone with good tools and no hurry. And the moccasin was stitched with sinew, hide-tanned, old pattern. These are not desperate people, Sergeant. Desperate people leave ragged signs. These people left almost none. Almost is the interesting part."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning they wanted us to see. Or they didn't care whether we did." Teo smiled apologetically, as if the forest's ambiguity were a personal failing of his own. "Both possibilities are very bad for our peace of mind."

* * *

Marsh took the middle watch himself, because he no longer trusted anyone else's.

He sat with his back to the slate and the rifle across his knees and watched the river rise by feel more than sight—a black breadth breathing in the dark, carrying smells of crushed green and cold mud, muttering over stones that would be submerged by morning. The rain had thinned to mist. Lightning still flickered far to the north, silent now, like artillery beyond the horizon, a war happening to someone else.

He counted crates in his head. He counted rounds. He counted the expedition's lies, starting with his own: that he'd taken this job for the money, when the money was half of it and the other half was the need to be somewhere with a mission, a route, an objective—somewhere the world still had edges you could hold. Fourteen years in uniform had taught him how to be useful and nothing about how to stop. Civilians drifted. Soldiers advanced. He needed to advance at something, even if the something was a drowned ship and a smooth-talking buyer whose questions about coordinates had never quite matched the answers Yara gave.

Yara. He turned the thought over. She dove wrecks alone at dawn. She read water like signals traffic. She'd known the dealer was trouble from the first night and signed on anyway—which was either greed, courage, or a private agenda, and Marsh had interrogated enough people to know the three wore the same coat.

And Ines, logging the moccasin in her book with neat little annotations, photographing the stumps, keeping records like a woman building a case file. For whom?

And Teo, wandering off the trail for hours, coming back with pockets full of unnamed moss and that notebook he shielded with his elbow like a schoolboy's exam.

And himself. What was he hiding? The letter from the pension board, folded in oilskin in his pack, the one that used the phrase *medically retired* and the phrase *recommended discharge* and never once the phrase *what happened at the culvert*. Everyone on this river was running from something. That was the honest arithmetic. Four fugitives in a boat, and one of them stealing bread.

Toward dawn, the mist lifted just enough to show him the far shore, and he saw—or believed he saw, and the distinction mattered less than it should have—a shape on the shingle opposite. Low, still, man-high. A stack of driftwood, probably. A stump. His eyes in the dark after fourteen years were instruments that had learned to lie to protect him.

But when he blinked, it was gone, and the shingle showed only wet stone and the first grey suggestion of morning.

He woke the others at first light. The river had come up another half-metre in the night; their gravel shelf was now an island, the outlet behind the camp a churning side-channel where there had been dry stone yesterday. The storm had passed, leaving the world rinsed and glittering and strange.

Ines went straight to the radio and had the primary set's guts spread on a tarp within minutes, working with a focus that shut them all out. By midmorning she had jury-rigged a temporary aerial from wire stripped off the fuel-line spool—there was a bitter joke in that, Marsh thought, the thief leaving them just enough copper to hang themselves—and got the set to draw power.

"Receiving only," she said grimly. "Transmit's cooked till I rebuild the feed properly. Listen."

She tuned through the bands. Static, static, a Spanish-language station ghosting in from impossibly far away, static. Then, low on the band, a carrier wave—strong, local, disciplined. A rhythm inside it. Three short, three short, three short.

SOS.

Ines's head came up. Yara, who had been mending a tear in the boat cover, went absolutely still, needle suspended.

"That's base camp's frequency," Ines said. "Somebody's transmitting distress on our own channel." She glanced at the treeline, at the rising river, at each of them in turn, and Marsh watched the same arithmetic move behind her eyes that was grinding through his own: the stolen rations, the stolen antenna, the watcher on the shingle, the SOS singing sweetly into the empty valley.

"Could it be a trap?" Yara asked quietly.

"Could it be us?" Marsh countered. "Could it be someone using our call sign, our frequency, drawing whatever Voss sends downstream straight to us?" He stood, rifle in hand, feeling the old cold clarity slide over him like a visor. "We're being managed. All of us. Every lost crate, every cut stump, every signal—we're pieces on a board, and somebody upstream is playing."

Teo closed his notebook. For once, he offered no Latin, no smile.

Downriver, faint beneath the river's roar, came a sound that did not belong to water: the flat, rhythmic cough of an outboard motor, more than one, running against the current toward them.

Ines killed the radio with one finger, as if it were listening too.
