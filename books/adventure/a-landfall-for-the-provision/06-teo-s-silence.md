## 6. Teo's Silence

The portage began where the river forgot itself—a place where the water dropped through a throat of granite and came out the other side as something younger and angrier. Yara had scouted it from the ridge at first light and pronounced it unrunnable, which was a kindness; *suicidal* was the truer word. So they carried.

They had grown good at carrying in the six days since leaving the dealer's base camp. Marsh took the heaviest loads without complaint, the rifle slung across his back like a second spine. Ines hauled the radio crate in a harness she'd rigged from pack straps, humming to herself, keeping time. Yara carried the dive gear because no one else would—eighty pounds of tank, regulator, and lead weight that clinked against her hip with every step like a patient, metallic heartbeat.

Teo carried his notebook.

He always carried the notebook. He wrote in it at dawn, at dusk, in the bottom of the boat while the rest of them bailed or cursed or slept. He pressed leaves between its pages until it swelled like something alive. Yara had caught him once, two nights ago, sketching by firelight with the intensity of a man copying down a confession, and when she'd asked what he was drawing he'd smiled—that soft, distracted smile of his—and said, "Something I remember."

*Remember*, not *imagine*. She'd thought about that word more than she should have.

* * *

The trail around the rapids was old, older than any of them expected. Not a game trail—the stride of it was wrong for animals, too even, too deliberate—and not new either. The ground beneath the leaf litter was packed hard, the way earth gets when feet have walked it for generations. Yara noticed it because her grandmother had been a walker, and Yara had spent enough childhood summers trailing behind her to know what a human path felt like underfoot: a kind of agreement between the land and everyone who'd ever crossed it.

"Someone uses this," she said, crouching to brush the litter aside.

Marsh didn't even slow. "Loggers, maybe. Surveyors. This country's got more ghosts than people." He shifted the fuel canister higher on his shoulder. "Keep moving. We want camp set before the light goes."

Ines paused beside Yara, though. Ines always paused. She looked down at the packed earth, then up at the ridgeline where the trees stood dark and close, and said nothing—but she made a small note in her logbook before shouldering the radio harness again, and Yara saw that her hands weren't quite steady.

Teo was last on the trail, as he always was. He stopped constantly—to photograph a lichen, to measure a fern against his thumb, to simply stand with his face tilted up into the green light filtering through the canopy. Marsh grumbled about it. Yara found she didn't mind. There was something steadying about Teo's slowness, his certainty that the world deserved attention. In a life spent diving into cold black water where attention kept you breathing, she recognized the discipline in it.

They were an hour into the portage when the rain started—not hard, just a fine grey sift through the canopy that turned everything soft-edged and quiet. The trail climbed away from the river along a shelf of rock, dipped through a stand of hemlock so dense the daylight went green as deep water, and then, without any transition Yara could name, Teo was gone.

Not lost. *Gone.* One moment his footsteps were behind hers, the soft crunch of his boots and the occasional scratch of his pencil against paper—he sketched while walking, which should have been impossible—and then the forest closed behind her like water over a diver, and there was only the rain and the silence where a man used to be.

She called his name. Once, level. Twice, louder. Then Marsh was pushing past her, rifle coming off his shoulder, and Ines was shouting Teo's name into the trees in three languages, and the three of them stood in the hemlock gloom calling into a silence that swallowed their voices whole.

* * *

They searched until dark.

Marsh worked the search like a soldier—grid patterns, whistle signals, marked trees. He was good at it. That was the terrible thing about Marsh: he was good at exactly the things you never wanted to need. By full dark he'd covered a half-kilometer radius and found nothing but a single boot print near a game trail heading uphill, already filling with rainwater.

"He walked," Marsh said. His voice had gone flat, the way it did when fear moved in underneath. "Nobody took him. Nobody *could* take him—we'd have heard it. He walked off this trail on his own feet."

"Why would he walk?" Ines's voice cracked on the word. She sat wrapped in her tarp by the fire they'd built too big, feeding it branches like she could burn away the dark. "He had no reason. He loved this expedition. He *named* mosses, for God's sake. You don't walk away from naming mosses."

"People walk away from things all the time." Marsh stared into the trees beyond the firelight. "Debts. Fear. Someone waiting for him somewhere we don't know about."

Yara said nothing. She was looking at the boot print, or where it had been—the rain was erasing it even now, the water rising in the depression like a slow tide. And she was thinking about the trail itself, the packed earth, the agreement under the leaves. About how Teo had stopped, that morning, at the exact place where the old path branched uphill, and stood looking at it for a long moment with an expression she hadn't been able to read.

She was thinking about the word *remember*.

* * *

His pack was still on the trail.

They found it at first light, tucked neatly against the base of a hemlock, out of the wet. Inside: his field kit, his rations, his sleeping bag rolled with military precision he'd never once displayed in camp. Everything accounted for except the notebook, which was gone, and his canteen, which was gone, and one other thing Yara noticed and didn't say aloud: the pack had been placed, not dropped. Set down carefully, deliberately, the way you'd leave something for someone to find.

Or the way someone else would arrange it after taking the man.

"Don't," Marsh said, watching her face. "Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Look at it like that. Like it's a message." He crouched, turning the pack over with two fingers. "It's a man who decided to leave. It happens. It happened in my unit twice. Good men. They just—" He stopped. Started again. "People break, Yara. Out here, in the green, it gets into your head. He'll turn up downstream somewhere, sheepish, full of apologies."

"You don't believe that."

It wasn't a question, and they both knew it. Marsh's jaw worked. "I believe what I can prove."

"Then prove it," Yara said. She was already pulling on her wetsuit. "There's a pool below the falls. Deep—one of those plunge basins the river's been cutting since the ice went out. If he slipped anywhere on that shelf of rock, that's where he'd be. Bodies sink. I've pulled enough of them to know."

Ines made a small sound and turned away. Marsh looked at Yara for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod slowly.

"Dive it," he said. "But Yara—if he's down there, we mark the spot and we radio it in. We don't play recovery. Understood?"

"Understood."

She was lying to him. She understood that too, in the part of herself that had learned to keep honest accounts. Because she didn't think Teo was in the pool. She thought Teo had seen something on that branch of the trail—something he recognized, something he'd been *remembering* all along in that notebook of his—and had followed it. But grief doesn't wait for certainty, and neither does hope, and the pool was the only door left to open. So she opened it.

* * *

The water hit her like a hand.

Cold, colder than the sea she'd grown up diving, cold enough to squeeze the air from her lungs in a white shock. Below the surface the world went from grey-green to black in the space of a body length, and Yara descended into it with the practiced calm of twenty years, equalizing, checking her gauge, letting the lead belt take her down.

The pool was deeper than she'd guessed. Thirty meters, maybe forty, a flooded cathedral walled in smooth stone, the waterfall thundering somewhere above her like weather. Her torch beam cut a cone through water so full of glacial flour it was like swimming inside milk glass. She swept it in slow arcs, the way she'd swept it through the Provision's drowned corridors a hundred times, reading the dark for shapes.

Rock. Rock. A sunken log, pale as bone. A shelf of gravel furred with something white and blind that flinched from her light.

No Teo.

She dove it methodically anyway, because grief demands procedure. Along the base of the falls where anything falling would be driven down and pinned. Along the downstream lip where the pool spilled over into shallows. Into a crevice in the north wall that opened into darkness her torch couldn't finish, and here she paused, because the crevice breathed.

That was the only word for it. Cold water moved through it in slow pulses, in and out, in and out, and the movement was too regular, too purposeful, to be current. An underwater passage. A spring-fed channel running back into the mountain. She put her glove against the stone at its mouth and felt the faint vibration of water moving somewhere far below, far within, and every instinct she owned—the ones that had kept her alive in the Provision's collapsing hull—told her to turn around.

She hovered there a long moment, torch beam swallowed by the channel's throat, and thought about Teo walking up that old trail on his own feet, following something he remembered.

Then she turned around, because she wasn't ready to die today, and surfaced into the rain.

* * *

"No body," she told them, peeling the wetsuit down to her waist, her hands shaking now that the adrenaline had room to move. "No sign of him anywhere in the pool. But there's a cave system under the north wall. Water moves through it like it's connected to something bigger. Springs, maybe. Underground channels."

"So he could have—" Ines started.

"Could have washed through, could have been pulled under, could be fifty kilometers downstream by now. Or nowhere." Yara wrung out her hair. "I'm telling you what I found. Not what it means."

Marsh stood at the edge of camp with his binoculars raised toward the ridgeline, and he'd been standing there, Yara realized, for some time. Not scanning. Watching. When he lowered the glasses his face had changed, and not in a way she liked.

"There's smoke," he said. "Northwest ridge. Maybe three kilometers. Thin—cook fire thin, not forest fire. And it's been there since yesterday evening. I thought it was our own reflection off the cloud layer." He turned. "It wasn't."

Ines went very still. "This region is uninhabited. The survey maps—"

"The survey maps are forty years old and made from aerial photos taken in winter." Marsh's voice was climbing, gaining an edge she'd never heard from him. "Three kilometers. One man missing. Smoke where nothing should be. You want to tell me those things aren't connected?"

"I want to tell you we don't *know*—"

"We don't know anything!" It burst out of him, loud enough to startle birds from the hemlocks, and then he mastered himself with visible effort, the way he always did, folding the anger back down into the flatness. "We don't know anything," he repeated, quieter. "That's the problem. That's been the problem since the strongbox. Since the questions the buyer asked. Somebody knows this river, Yara. Somebody's been using that trail for longer than any of us have been alive, and now Teo's gone, and I am not going to stand in this camp and wait to find out who's next."

Yara looked at the northwest ridge, where the smoke rose thin and silver into the rain-grey sky. She thought about the packed earth of the trail. The placed pack. The breathing cave. She thought about Teo's face in the firelight, saying *something I remember*, and about the strange, unlonely feeling she'd had for days now—the feeling of being watched, yes, but watched the way you watch family across a crowded station platform. Waiting for recognition.

She should have been afraid. Some part of her registered that she should be afraid, the way you register a pressure gauge reading.

What she actually felt, kneeling in the wet leaves with Teo's absence pressing on her chest like a weight belt, was something sharper and less familiar. Grief, yes—for the gentle man who named mosses, wherever he was. But threaded through it, hardening it, something that felt almost like direction. For years she'd salvaged other people's losses out of cold water and sold them to strangers, and told herself that was a life. Now the river had taken one of hers, and every instinct she had was pointing upstream, toward the smoke, toward the source, toward whatever truth this valley had been holding its breath around for sixty years.

"We go up," she said.

Marsh swung toward her. "Up? Toward the—"

"Toward the smoke. Toward answers. Teo didn't drown, Marsh. I'd bet my gear on it. He *walked*, like you said—walked up that old trail like a man going home." She stood, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. "And if someone up that ridge knows why, then that's where we're going. All three of us. Together. Because splitting up is how expeditions like ours end up as footnotes."

Ines was already reaching for her logbook. Marsh looked from Yara to the ridge and back, wrestling with something, and finally gave a short, hard nod.

They broke camp in the rain. And it was only when Yara lifted Teo's pack to carry it—because someone should, because he would want his specimens cared for—that she found it, wedged into the frame where no one had thought to look.

A page, torn cleanly from the notebook, folded twice, dry despite everything.

She waited until they were on the trail, until the others were ahead of her, before she opened it. It was a sketch in Teo's precise, loving hand: a hillside rendered in careful cross-hatch, stepped and terraced from waterline to treeline, every terrace planted, every row tended. Channels of water ran between the levels like veins. At the top, drawn small but unmistakable, stood a cluster of buildings with steep roofs, and beside them a dock, and beside the dock a boat.

And in the corner, in his small neat script, a caption that stopped her breath:

*Grandmother's garden. Exactly as she described it. They kept it alive.*

Yara folded the page closed with hands that would not quite obey her, and looked up the trail, toward the smoke, toward the terraced hillside that existed nowhere on any map—and walked faster.
