## 7. The Watchers

Marsh had spent eleven years learning to read ground, and the ground along this stretch of river was lying to him.

He crouched at the treeline above their camp with a tin cup of coffee going cold in his hand and watched the light die on the far ridge. The valley here narrowed to a throat of gray water and black stone, and the ridge opposite rose steep and forested, its crest still holding the last copper light of dusk. Nothing moved up there. That was what bothered him. All day he'd been counting birds—teals, herons, the small brown finches that worked the gravel bars—and all day the counts had come back wrong. Too few near the banks. Too many clustered inland, as if something on the water's edge had taught them caution.

Birds didn't lie. People did. Teo had lied, or been taken, or walked into the river on his own fool errand, and every explanation Marsh turned over felt like a stone with something rotten underneath.

Behind him, the camp murmured. Ines had the radio case open on an overturned supply crate, its guts spilled out under a hurricane lamp—circuit boards, spools of solder wire, the little blue flame of her butane pencil dancing as she worked. Yara sat apart on a log near the waterline, mending a torn strap with that patient, closed-off attention she gave everything, like a woman sewing a wound shut in someone she didn't particularly like.

"Antenna's the problem," Ines said without looking up. She said it to no one, to the radio, to the river. "The backup mast snapped clean at the base. Whoever took it didn't want us talking. Whoever snapped it wanted us listening."

"Same person," Marsh said.

"Maybe." She fed solder into the joint. The smell of it—hot metal and pine resin—cut through the damp. "Or maybe the storm did one and the thief did the other, and we're building a monster out of coincidence because monsters are easier than weather."

Marsh drank the cold coffee. It tasted like ash and old decisions. He'd told them, back at the last portage, that they should turn around. He'd said it plainly: two days of rations gone, the antenna gone, Teo gone, and a river running higher every hour with rain falling somewhere upstream where none of them could see it. Turn around, regroup, come back with proper support. Yara had looked at him with those flat diver's eyes and said the current wouldn't let them turn around for another thirty miles, which was true, and which also wasn't the whole reason she wanted to go on. He'd stopped trying to name her whole reasons. Naming them made them harder to argue with.

The light went off the ridge. Darkness came up out of the gorge like water filling a hull.

* * *

He took first watch because he always took first watch, because sleep had become a country he could visit but not live in. He walked the perimeter with the rifle slung loose across his back—he'd carried it since the army, the way other men carried guilt or rosaries—and he counted things. Trees. Stars breaking through the scud. The number of steps between the fire ring and the boats, forty-one, forty-two, forty-three. Counting kept the animal part of his brain occupied while the thinking part worked.

The thinking part kept returning to the moccasin.

A child's shoe, soft-soled, hand-stitched, sitting in fresh wood shavings three days' walk from the nearest registered settlement, which was a fuel depot with a population of nine. Fresh-cut wood meant axes. Axes meant hands. Hands meant mouths, and mouths meant a community, and a community that size left sign everywhere—trail wear, cut stumps, smoke haze, latrine trenches. They'd found almost nothing else. Which meant either the community was very small, or very careful, or both.

Careful was worse. Careful meant practiced. Practiced meant they'd hidden before.

At full dark the river sound changed. Marsh noticed it the way you notice a room going quiet when someone important enters—not a new noise but a subtraction, a gap in the water's constant speech. He stood still among the trees and listened until his ears ached.

There. Upstream, close to the far bank where the current ran slack behind a shoal: the knock of wood against wood. Once. Twice. A pause long enough to be deliberate. Then nothing.

Paddles. Or poles. Someone moving in the dark without lights, without voices, using the slack water the way a burglar uses a hallway rug.

Marsh's heart began doing what hearts do when the body understands before the mind does. He moved back toward camp low and quiet, boots finding the soft ground between roots, and touched Yara's shoulder before she could startle—she woke like a cat, all at once, a knife already half-drawn from under her bedroll, and he raised both hands and mouthed, *boats.*

She was up without a word. That was the thing about her he couldn't reconcile: she was the most frightened person on this river and the least afraid of it. She woke scared and functioned calm, and the calm never reached her eyes.

"Ines," he breathed. "Kill the lamp."

Ines looked up from her soldering, the blue flame reflected tiny in her glasses. "I'm twenty minutes from—"

"Now."

She killed the lamp. The dark came down complete, river-black, the kind of dark that has weight. For a long moment the three of them knelt in it, breathing, and Marsh heard the camp reorganize itself around him: Yara sliding the knife home and taking up the boat hook instead; Ines gathering the radio's scattered components by touch, folding them into the case with small precise clicks.

The knocking came again. Closer. On their side of the river now.

"Get to the trees," Marsh whispered. "Not the boats. If they want the boats, let them have the boats."

"They're not thieves," Yara said softly. It wasn't an argument. It was a weather report.

"You don't know that."

"I know what I've seen." Her voice was level as still water. "They've had four chances to hurt us and they've taken supplies instead. Thieves who won't hurt people aren't thieves. They're people with rules."

"People with rules shot at Teo's expedition too?" Marsh heard his own voice harden and couldn't stop it. "People with rules made him disappear?"

"We don't know what happened to Teo."

"We don't know anything, that's my whole point!" The whisper cracked at its edges. He pressed his palm flat against his thigh until the tremor in it stilled. "I'm sorry. Get in the trees. Both of you. Go."

* * *

They went. Marsh stayed at the edge of the clearing with his back against a trunk whose bark he knew by feel, and he watched the river.

The moon was a thin nail-paring behind cloud, just enough light to make the water a moving absence rather than a surface. He found them by their silence—a long canoe, maybe two, sliding down the slack channel with the current doing most of the work. Figures in it, low, wrapped in something dark. No lights. No engine. They passed the camp at perhaps sixty meters, so quietly that if he hadn't known to look he would have seen nothing but night.

Then the second canoe came, and in it, standing briefly against the sky as the craft crossed a seam of fainter black, a figure that was small. Child-small.

His breath went out of him slowly. A child, on the water, at night, in the middle of nowhere, being ferried past strangers' fires by adults who moved like soldiers and hid like ghosts. Every story he'd ever been trained to fear assembled itself in the dark: traffickers, cultists, guerrillas, the drowned dead of some private war. His hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking and hated them for it, hated the body's honesty, and gripped the rifle not to use it but to give the shaking something to hold.

The canoes were past. The moment stretched. He could let them go. He could wake the others at dawn and log it, coordinates and time and bearing, and let the grown-ups downstream decide.

But Teo was somewhere ahead of him, or under him, and the rations were gone, and the antenna was gone, and every hour this river carried them deeper into a country that did not want them, and Marsh had spent eleven years being the man who watched and reported and waited, and watching had never once saved anybody.

"No," he said aloud, to himself, to the dark. "No more watching."

He stepped into the open at the waterline and put the rifle to his shoulder and fired one round high over the river, into the black flank of the far ridge, where the bullet would bury itself in a hillside that had never done anyone harm.

The crack of it went down the gorge like the world splitting. Echoes came back from three directions, overlapping, confused. On the water, the canoes froze—then broke apart, paddles digging, sweeping hard for the far bank and the shadow of the shoal.

And then the ridge answered.

It came from above and behind the camp, upslope, close enough that Marsh felt it in his sternum before he heard it properly: a single shot, flat and heavy, passing over the clearing with a sound like a large bird's wingbeat. Not at him. Over him. Over the tents, over the fire ring, over the whole small architecture of their presence, and into the trees beyond with a whack of displaced wood.

A warning. Unmistakably, insultingly, a warning—the kind of shot a marksman makes when he wants you to know he could have made a different one.

Marsh dropped flat anyway, cheek in the mud, rifle swinging upslope, and found nothing. The ridge was a wall of darkness. Whoever had fired had already folded themselves back into it, the way the birds had folded inland all day, the way the woodsmoke never quite showed, the way everything in this valley knew how to disappear except him.

His breath came fast and shallow. He lay in the mud with the muzzle wavering and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he wanted there to be a target. That some part of him had needed the shot to be aimed, needed an enemy he could return fire at, because an enemy was a problem with a solution and everything else—the missing man, the stolen food, the child in the canoe, the slow erosion of every plan he'd made—was just weather, just loss, just the world refusing to mean anything.

Get up, he told himself. Get up, soldier.

He got up.

* * *

Yara and Ines came out of the trees before he'd finished reloading, though he hadn't called them. Of course they had. Nobody on this expedition obeyed orders; they just took turns pretending.

"One shot from the ridge," Yara said. She'd heard it from cover; her face in the starlight was pale and absolutely steady. "Over us."

"Over us," Marsh confirmed. His voice sounded wrong in his own ears, scraped raw. "They're telling us they can hit whatever they want."

"Or telling us they haven't yet." Ines set the radio case down on the crate and opened it, and her hands, in the match-flare she struck, were steadier than his. "One round over a camp is the politest message I've ever received from someone with a rifle. It says *leave*. It doesn't say *die*."

"It says they're up there right now," Marsh said, "with a firing solution on our tents, and we're arguing grammar."

Yara crouched by the waterline, looking across at the far bank where the canoes had gone. "They were evacuating someone," she said. "The small one. When your shot went off, they ran for cover like they were protecting it." She turned her head, and even in the dark he felt the weight of her look. "You fired first."

"I fired a warning."

"You fired the only gunshot tonight that nobody asked for." She said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "Now they know exactly what we have, how fast we react, and which one of us is dangerous. Congratulations. You just introduced yourself."

Marsh's jaw ached. He realized he was clenching it. "Someone has to be willing to be dangerous, or we end up like Teo."

"And how is that working for you?" Yara asked quietly. "How's the danger going? Feel safer?"

He had no answer, so he gave the river one instead, walking the perimeter again, counting steps, counting stars, counting the seconds between the river's sounds. Forty-one. Forty-two. Behind him the match flared again as Ines worked.

* * *

She got it working a little after midnight.

Marsh heard it before he understood it: a thin, rhythmic tone cutting through the rain-smell of the dark, the whistle of a carrier wave hunting for a friend. Ines had cannibalized the broken main mast's base plate, lashed the backup whip antenna to a stripped sapling with cord and solder and what appeared to be a belt, and built a transmitter out of stubbornness and spare parts. She hunched over the key now, sending by hand, and the message went out into the ionosphere in short desperate syllables.

He stood beside her while she sent it. He could read morse well enough to follow, and what she sent was not what he would have sent.

*Expedition Provision, day nine,* the key tapped. *Teo Alvarado missing, presumed lost, portage above the narrows. Supplies taken by unknown parties. Shots fired, no casualties. We are proceeding. Request guidance. Request—*

Her hand hovered. Rain ticked on the tarp she'd rigged over the radio. Then, deliberately, she added:

*Request any information on settlements, historical or otherwise, upper watershed. Any information at all.*

And then, because the atmosphere was collapsing the band and the window was closing, she signed off in a hurry, the characters tumbling over each other, and the last thing that went out before the carrier wave dissolved into static was garbled—half a phrase, truncated mid-word, *position approx—* swallowed by the noise floor like a coin dropped down a well.

"That's it?" Marsh said. "That's all we get?"

"That's all the sky's giving tonight." Ines powered down gently, the way you'd close a dying man's eyes. "Base camp will get fragments. Maybe enough. Maybe they'll hear *shots fired* and send help." She pushed her glasses up and looked at him, and in the dark her face was tired and older than it had been nine days ago. "Or maybe they'll hear *settlements, upper watershed* and decide that's worth more than we are."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means somebody's paying for this expedition, Marsh, and I've been wondering since portage two why a man who sells antiques needs a botanist, a soldier, and a diver to take a scenic cruise." She began packing the radio away, each component in its fitted slot. "I log everything. You should know that. Whatever happens next, there's a record."

"There's always a record," Marsh said. "Records don't keep people alive."

"No," Ines agreed. "But sometimes they're all that's left of them."

She rolled into her bedroll. Yara was already asleep, or performing it, curled near the dead fire with the boat hook in reach. Marsh took the watch again because sleep was still that foreign country, and he sat with the rifle across his knees and the garbled transmission echoing in his head—*request any information, any information at all*—and wondered which of them Ines thought was being watched.

Toward dawn, the rain came. And with the rain, faint through the gray curtain, from somewhere up the ridge where a marksman had folded himself back into the dark, Marsh heard—or dreamed he heard—a sound he had no training for at all.

Singing. Low, wordless, in a woman's voice. A lullaby, drifting down through the rain to a camp full of strangers, patient as the river, certain as morning, as though whoever sang it already knew exactly how long it would take them to leave—or how long it would take to stop needing them to.
