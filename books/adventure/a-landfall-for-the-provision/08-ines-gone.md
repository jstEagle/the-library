## 8. Ines Gone

Yara woke to a silence with a shape to it.

She had spent enough dawns on the water to know that silence was never truly empty. It had texture, weight, a grain you could read like current against a hull. This silence was wrong in a way she couldn't name at first—too complete, too settled, the way a pool goes still after something has already sunk.

Then she understood. The generator's evening hum was gone. And beneath it, fainter but more fundamental, the small electric tick of Ines's radio—her constant companion, the thing she touched in her sleep the way a child touches a blanket—that tick was gone too.

The camp lay under a canopy of fig and kapok, pitched on a shelf of clay above the river where the channel bent north. Mist stood waist-deep between the trees. Marsh was already up; she could hear him moving through the brush with the deliberate loudness of a man who wanted to be heard, who believed noise was a kind of armor. His voice came back to her before his body did.

"Her tent's empty."

Yara sat up in her hammock, boots half-laced, and let the cold of the morning climb her spine. Teo had been gone four days now. Four days of Marsh sweeping the banks with binoculars and Ines keeping the log in her neat, upright hand, recording times and bearings as if precision were a rope she could hold onto. *Day 9, 0612, no sign. Day 9, 1400, water level down two inches.* Ines had kept them tethered to each other by sheer documentation, and now the documenter was gone.

"Empty," Yara said, and swung out of the hammock. "Or taken?"

Marsh stood at the flap of Ines's shelter, and he did not answer right away, which was itself an answer. He was a big man gone lean from the river, jaw shadowed, eyes red-rimmed from nights he claimed he'd slept. When he stepped aside, Yara looked past him into the tent.

Ines's bedroll was folded. Not rumpled—folded, corners squared, the way she folded it every morning because order was her religion. Her tin cup sat washed and inverted on the flat stone she used as a table. Her boots were gone. Her jacket was gone. The little brass compass she wore on a lanyard around her neck: gone.

And the radio—

The radio was not gone. That was the first thing Yara's hands knew, before her mind caught up. She knelt beside the equipment case and opened it, and there the set sat, the R-108 they'd hauled through three portages, its canvas cover buttoned down. But when she unbuttoned it, her breath went shallow.

Dismantled. Not smashed, not stripped for parts. *Disassembled*, with care, by someone who understood what they were looking at. The chassis lifted free of the case. The valve board was unseated and laid beside it on a square of cloth. Coils wrapped in waxed paper. The antenna lead coiled in a figure-eight, the way spools are dressed aboard ship, the way Ines herself dressed cable—no. Not the way Ines dressed it. Better. Older. A sailor's dress, a generation older than Ines's habits, the kind of wrap Yara had seen only once before, in photographs of naval signal schools from before the war that had put the Provision on the bottom.

"Look at this," she said, and her voice came out quieter than she intended.

Marsh crouched opposite her. "They took her and left the radio."

"They took the radio apart." Yara turned the chassis toward him. "Every screw backed out clean. No scratches. Whoever did this knew exactly what they were holding—and they didn't want it transmitting again, but they also didn't want it damaged." She lifted one of the valves, cradled in its paper like an egg. "You don't handle someone's radio like this if you hate them. You don't handle it like this if you're afraid of them either. You handle it like this if—" She stopped.

"If what?"

"If you're taking it home to be repaired," Yara said. "Or if you're leaving a message."

* * *

Marsh did not want messages. Marsh wanted tracks, and he got them—or thought he did. By full light he had found what the mist had hidden: prints in the clay below the camp, four sets, maybe five, barefoot or in soft-soled shoes, coming from downstream along the waterline where the ground stayed damp and gave up its secrets. They came up from the river, circled the camp wide, and went back down to the water. No boat wake worth reading—the river had risen overnight and licked its own edges clean—but a strand of rush fiber caught on a root told Yara that a craft had been beached there, something tied with cord, something that had waited.

"They carried her out," Marsh said. He said it flat, the way men state facts they intend to act on. "Four of them. One carried the radio. They walked her down to the water and took her upstream."

"Or she walked," Yara said.

He turned on her then, and there was something new in his face, something that had been building since Teo vanished and had nowhere else to go. "Say that again."

"I said she might have walked. Her bedroll was folded, Marsh. Her cup was washed. Ines has been folding that bedroll every morning since we left the dealer's dock—I've watched her do it. If someone dragged her out of her tent in the night, that bedroll is a wreck." Yara held his gaze. "And her boots. Who abducts a woman and lets her put her boots on?"

"Someone smart."

"Someone considerate," Yara said. "There's a difference, and the difference matters."

"The difference doesn't matter to a dead woman."

"She's not dead."

"You don't know that!" It came out of him louder than he meant, cracking across the camp and startling birds out of the fig canopy, and for a moment Yara saw what was underneath the ex-soldier's calm: a man who had lost people before, whose whole discipline was built over that loss like planking over a hole in a hull, and the hole was open now and the river was coming in. He had failed Teo. He would not say so. But he had counted heads last night—he always counted heads—and there had been four, and now there were three, and the arithmetic was eating him alive.

"I don't know it," Yara said, gentler. "But I can read a scene, same as I read a wreck. And this scene doesn't read like violence. It reads like a plan. They've been watching us for days—we saw them on the ridge, they fired over us, they could have killed any of us and didn't. Last night they come down, take one person, leave two, dismantle the radio so we can't call the man funding us, and fold the bedding." She spread her hands. "What does that look like to you?"

"An ambush that got lucky."

"It looks like people with rules," Yara said. "People who decided Ines was in danger here—or that we were—and took her somewhere safe. People who are trying very hard not to hurt us."

"Then why fire on us?"

"Over us. There's a difference, and the difference matters." She heard herself using his own words back at him and saw it land, saw it land badly, and pressed on anyway. "Teo's notebook had drawings in it, Marsh. Terraces. Gardens cut into a hillside that isn't on any chart. Somebody built those gardens. Somebody's children wear moccasins and walk miles from anywhere registered. There is a settlement up this river, and it has been hiding from the world since before either of us was born, and yesterday morning it decided our radio operator was one of theirs."

"One of theirs? She's from Cádiz. She told me herself, her father repaired fishing boats—"

"And my grandmother's name was Eriksen," Yara said, and the words came out of her before she had decided to say them, surprising her with their weight, "and until six days ago I'd have told you nobody upriver had ever heard it either."

Marsh stared at her. The mist was burning off behind him, the river showing itself in long silver panels, indifferent and enormous.

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I don't know," Yara said. "I'm saying that for the first time since Teo disappeared, I'm not sure we're the ones being hunted." She looked down at the dismantled radio, at the valve resting in its nest of waxed paper, handled with more tenderness than anything in her salvage life had ever been handled with. "I'm saying somebody just took our friend away from us the way you'd take a child out of traffic. And I'm saying that frightens me more than bullets, because it means they think they know something about us that we don't know ourselves."

* * *

They searched anyway. Marsh needed to move and Yara understood that need the way she understood ballast—a thing that keeps you upright even as it drags you down. They worked the bank downstream, found the beaching spot, found the rush cord, found a single boot print of Ines's size stepping neatly between the larger prints, unhurried, evenly spaced. Unhurried. Yara knelt over that print for a long time and said nothing, because she had learned on the Provision's drowned deck that some evidence does its work best in silence.

It was near midday, heat pressing down through the canopy like a hand, when she found the other thing.

It lay in the fork of a sapling at the edge of the beach, placed, not dropped—a twist of bark cloth around something small. Inside was a strip of dried fish, two pieces, and beneath them, folded into a square no bigger than a stamp, a scrap of paper. Real paper, aged soft as cloth, covered in handwriting that had faded to the color of tea.

Yara unfolded it with fingers that had pulled coins and rings and bones out of sixty years of black water without trembling, and trembled now.

It was a page torn from a logbook. Naval hand, naval phrasing, the ink brown with age. Most of it was water-eaten, illegible, but one line survived near the margin, written larger than the rest, underlined twice:

*Eriksen stands the middle watch. Tell her mother the river was kind.*

Yara read it three times. The river talked behind her, patient, endless. Somewhere upstream, terraced gardens climbed a hillside that did not exist, and children in moccasins slept in houses that did not appear on charts, and a woman named Sanna or a man with a signalman's hands had known, somehow, that the diver with the grey eyes and the salvager's knots was an Eriksen—had known it before she knew it herself, perhaps, or had simply been waiting, all these decades, for the name to come back up the river the way names do, the way bodies of the drowned eventually do, turning gently, rising toward the light.

"Yara." Marsh's voice, from up the bank. "You found something."

She closed her fist around the paper. For one breath—one full turn of the current—she considered lying to him. Then she thought of Ines folding her bedroll, and of the radio dressed in a dead sailor's careful wraps, and of how tired she was of being a woman who kept things underwater.

"Yes," she called back. "And Marsh—it changes everything."

She stood, and slipped the paper inside her shirt, against her skin, where the river could not reach it, and started up the bank toward him, already knowing, with the cold certainty of deep water, that whatever she said next would decide whether the two of them followed Ines together—or whether she followed alone.
