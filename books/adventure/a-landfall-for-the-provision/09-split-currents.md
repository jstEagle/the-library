## 9. Split Currents

Marsh woke before dawn with his hand already closed around the rifle's stock, the way he had every morning since Ines vanished. He hadn't decided to sleep holding it. His body had simply made the decision for him, the way a body flinches from a flame, and when he opened his eyes the barrel was there against his palm, cold and familiar as a rosary.

The camp was gray and dripping. Mist lay on the river like a sheet pulled up to the chin. Across the firepit, Eriksen was already awake—of course she was, the woman seemed to run on salt water and spite—sitting on a root with her boots off, rubbing feeling back into her feet. She watched him watch her. Neither of them said anything. The silence between them had developed a texture over the past two days, a kind of grit, like sand worked into a joint.

Three of them now. That was what it came down to. Four, a week ago, when the expedition had been a joke and a paycheck: a botanist who talked to his specimens, a radio operator who hummed while she worked, a diver who wouldn't tell him half of what she knew, and himself, hired to keep the others alive. Now Teo was gone into a pool so deep the light gave up before the bottom, and Ines was gone into the trees without a sound, and the expedition had been reduced to its skeleton: a soldier, a diver, and a river that wanted them dead.

Marsh fed the fire a piece of split cedar and watched the flame climb. He was forty-one years old. He had served in three countries whose names he was contractually forbidden to say aloud, and he had learned in all of them the same first lesson: when people take someone, they leave a trail, and a trail is a promise. You follow the promise. You don't sit by the fire and philosophize about whether the people who made it might have *reasons*.

He looked at Eriksen again. She had put her boots back on. She was checking the straps of her dive bag with the unhurried competence of someone who had done ten thousand small preparations and expected to do ten thousand more. She hadn't cried for Ines. She hadn't cried for Teo. Marsh didn't know what to do with a woman who didn't cry, except distrust her, and he was doing his best.

"We're leaving in twenty minutes," he said.

Eriksen didn't look up. "We're leaving in twenty minutes," she agreed, "downriver."

The words landed in his chest like a dropped stone. "Downriver."

"The base camp. The dealer's people. We get to the sat phone, we call in proper search teams, we let professionals—"

"Professionals." He tasted the word. "You mean men with contracts. You mean the same outfit that sent us up here blind, that's been lying about this wreck since before either of us was born. You want to hand Ines to *them*."

"I want Ines *alive*." Now she looked up, and her eyes were the flat green of deep water. "Marsh. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to say it louder. There were at least six of them on that ridge. They shot over our heads—which means they could see us, which means they could have *hit* us, which means they chose not to. They took Ines and left her boots. They took her and left the food they didn't need. You know what that is? That's not a raid. That's a *correction*. Somebody upriver is telling us, in the only language they think we'll understand, *go home*."

"Or it's a hostage operation, and every hour we sit here debating their motives is an hour they're moving her deeper into country we don't know, toward a place we've never seen, where—"

"Where they've lived for sixty years!" Her voice cracked out across the water and came back off the far bank, and somewhere upstream a bird startled and went silent. She lowered it. "You've seen the gardens, Marsh. Teo drew them. Terraces, cut timber, a moccasin small enough for a six-year-old. That's not a camp. That's a *town*. And a town that's stayed hidden for sixty years doesn't survive by taking hostages and leaving witnesses. It survives by being *left alone*."

"Then it survives until the day it doesn't." He stood. His knees ached; everything ached; he had been sleeping in his boots for a week. "I don't care what they are. I don't care how many generations of *noble savages* are up there growing vegetables on a cliff. They have Ines, and I am not—"

He stopped. The sentence had a hole in it and they both knew what filled the hole. *I am not losing anyone else.* Not Teo, whose death he had ordered in the practical, bloodless language of a man managing risk: *we push the portage, we'll make the pool by dark.* Not the two men he'd lost in the service, whose names lived in a locked drawer in his head that he opened every night at three a.m. Not the wife who had finally, gently, after nine years, explained to him that loving a man who was always halfway into the next mission was like loving a man who was always halfway out the door.

Eriksen watched him stop, and something in her face shifted—not softer, exactly. More careful. Like a diver seeing a shape in the murk and deciding not to touch it.

"Sit down, Marsh."

"I'm going upriver."

"Sit *down*." She stood too, and for a moment they were squared off across the dead fire, and the mist moved between them like a third party. "You want to track six people through terrain they've hunted their whole lives, in country with no map, no extraction, and no comms, carrying a rifle and enough food for two days. You want to do it alone, because you can't trust me, because I read a scene wrong in your opinion. And you want me to just—what. Wave you off? Wish you luck?"

"I want you to stay out of my way."

"Nobody's ever come back from up that river," she said quietly. "Not the navy. Not the survey teams. Not Teo. You'll be a story they tell their children about the stupid man with the gun."

"Then I'll be a story." He heard his own voice go flat and distant, the voice he used for orders, the voice that had gotten men killed and gotten men home, and he hated it and was grateful for it. "But I'll be a story that tried."

* * *

They divided the supplies at first light, and it went the way all partings between soldiers go: with inventory, and with neither of them looking at the other's hands.

Eriksen did the dividing, because she had the better eye for weight. She was fair about it, he'd give her that—fairer than he'd have been. She pushed the rifle ammunition across to him without being asked, all eleven rounds, and he watched her do it and understood it was not a gift. It was an argument. *Take your bullets. See how far they get you.*

"You'll want the med kit," she said.

"I'll want the med kit if I find her hurt."

"You'll want the med kit when you put your foot through a rotted span in the dark, which you will, because you don't look where you're going when you're angry." She shoved it at him anyway. "Take it. I know these pools. I can bleed and patch."

He took it. He took the dried fish, half the rice, the water filter, the machete, the compass he no longer needed because he'd already memorized the bearing. She kept the flares, the tarp, most of the rope, and the boat—which was the real division, he realized, the one underneath. She kept the river. He was taking the land, and the land up here belonged to someone else.

She packed in silence, and he packed in silence, and the river ran past them, brown and muscular, going about its business, and he thought: *this is what it sounds like when a crew dies.* Not with gunfire. With a compass being passed from one hand to another.

Last, she held out the dive knife. His was lost—left in Teo's pool, in the tangle where he'd cut himself free of a snag and come up shaking with nothing in his hands. She offered it handle-first, and for a second he almost refused it on principle, and then he saw her face and understood that refusing it would be the small thing, the mean thing, and that she was giving him the chance not to be small.

He took it. "Eriksen."

"Yara." She said it tiredly. "My name's Yara. You've known me three weeks."

"Yara." The name felt strange in his mouth, too soft, too much like something you'd call across a kitchen. "When I find where they've taken her—"

"If."

"*When*—I'll signal you. Three shots, even spacing. You'll hear it off the water."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide something. He'd seen that look before, on officers weighing whether to tell a subordinate the truth that would break him or the lie that would move him. She chose the truth.

"Marsh," she said, "I've dived this river my whole life. I've found bodies. Drowners, loggers, one navy man in a dress uniform who'd been down forty years and still had his shoes on. You know what every one of them had in common?" She shouldered her bag. "Every one of them was sure. Right up until the water closed over them, they were sure. Certainty doesn't keep you alive up here. Certainty is just the last thing you think."

Then she pushed the boat out, stepped in, and took the current, and he stood on the bank with eleven rounds and a borrowed knife and watched her shrink into the mist going the wrong way, the sensible way, the way of sat phones and contracts and letting other people's people disappear into other people's rivers.

He turned north. He turned upriver.

* * *

The first day he made good distance, and he knew it was a lie.

He knew it the way he'd known, in another life, when a patrol was making time on a map but losing ground in every way the map couldn't show. The terrain was against him—deadfall stacked by winter floods, slopes that ran to scree, undergrowth that grabbed at his sleeves like hands—and the trail, when he found it, was against him worse. He found it two hours in: a broken fern, a boot print in the soft ground by a stream crossing, a thread of something pale snagged on bark. Ines's kind of thread. He told himself it was Ines's thread and moved faster, and the moving faster was the lie, because a man moving fast doesn't read ground, and ground unread is ground that kills you.

By midday he'd crossed the same stream three times without meaning to. By late afternoon he stood at the base of a ridge that matched nothing in his memory of the country behind him, and the light was going copper and long, and he understood with a cold professional clarity that he was, if not lost, then *unfound*—that the river had bent away and taken his bearings with it, and that the print he'd followed for six hours might have been a week old, might have been his own from the morning they'd portaged, might have been nobody's, might have been the forest's little joke.

He climbed anyway.

He climbed because the alternative was to sit down in the dark and admit what the climbing was for, and he was not ready to admit that. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Because the truth—the truth he kept in the locked drawer with the names—was that this had stopped being about Ines somewhere on the second day, maybe the first, maybe the moment he'd woken with the rifle in his hand. The truth was that a man can lose a wife and two soldiers and a botanist and a radio operator, and if he doesn't stop somewhere and grieve them, he will spend the rest of his life trying to lose them *back*—chasing them into rivers, into ridgelines, into the mouths of guns—because grief is unbearable and purpose is not, and any purpose will do, even a stupid one, even a fatal one.

Especially a fatal one.

From the ridgeline, in the last light, he saw it.

Smoke. Not one column—three, four, thin and domestic, rising from a valley beyond the next ridge, from a fold in the country that no chart had ever marked. Woodsmoke. Cooking smoke. The smoke of chimneys, of hearths, of *homes*.

Marsh stood in the dying light with eleven rounds and a borrowed knife and looked at the smoke of the town that wasn't supposed to exist, and his heart did a thing he didn't authorize: it lifted. It *lifted*, like a man seeing lights of a village after days in the wild, like a child seeing a window, and he had to put his hand on a tree and stand there and let the feeling pass through him and name it for what it was, because he'd felt it before, in other countries, at other ridgelines, and he knew its name.

Hope. Stupid, traitorous hope. The feeling that came just before the worst of it.

He made camp cold, no fire, back against a trunk, rifle across his knees. Below him the valley smoked its quiet smokes. Somewhere down there, Ines was alive or she wasn't. Somewhere down there, people who had never done him any harm were eating supper by fires he could see, and he was lying in the dark with a rifle, coming to take one of them back or make them pay for her, and he could not—try as he might, turning it over and over like a stone in the hand—he could not make himself feel that this was wrong.

That was the thing about going past reason, he thought, as sleep took him in increments, as the valley smoke rose into the stars. From the inside it doesn't feel like madness. It feels like finally, *finally*, being the only sane man left.

* * *

He woke to birdsong and to the smell of woodsmoke, close, too close, and lay absolutely still with his hand on the rifle.

The fire was small and built with a soldier's economy—dry fuel, no flame above knee height, no smoke to speak of now that morning was up. A figure sat on the far side of it, cross-legged, patient as furniture. A woman, old, gray-braided, with a face like a river stone and eyes that had been watching him, he understood, for some time.

She had a tin cup in her hands. Steam rose off it. There was a second cup beside her, full, set on a flat stone on his side of the fire, placed where he would find it the moment he opened his eyes. Placed, he realized, the way you set out a cup for a guest.

Or the way you set out a cup for family.

"Your grandmother," the old woman said, in English accented by sixty years of river isolation, "dived the wreck too, at the end. Did Yara ever tell you that? No." She sipped, unhurried, and looked at him with something that was not quite pity and not quite amusement. "Of course she didn't. None of you Eriksens talk. You just keep diving down after things."

Marsh did not move. The rifle was under his hand. The cup steamed between them.

"Drink," she said. "You have a long walk, and you're going to make it badly."

Below them, in the valley that was not on any map, the morning smoke of a hidden town rose straight into the windless sky, and somewhere in it, he was almost sure, a radio operator named Ines was alive, and well, and never going to want to be found.
