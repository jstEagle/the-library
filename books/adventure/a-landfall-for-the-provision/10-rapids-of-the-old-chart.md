## 10. Rapids of the Old Chart

The chart was older than her father, and it lied like a friend.

Yara had found it folded into the back of Teo's notebook, a surveyor's tracing of the upper river done in faded iron-gall ink, the rapids marked with a cluster of small anxious crosses and a notation in a clerk's hand: *Unrunnable at all seasons. Advise portage of no fewer than three days.* Someone had crossed out *unrunnable* and written beneath it, in a different, steadier script: *Not for strangers.*

She should have turned back there. She knew that even then, standing on the gravel bar with the paper trembling in the wind off the water, listening to the river make its low continuous argument against the stones. But Marsh was somewhere behind her in the hills with a rifle and a grudge, Ines was gone, Teo was gone, and the only direction left that meant anything was up. The river went up. So Yara went up.

The old chart had been right about one thing: the rapids were real. What it hadn't shown—what no honest chart could have shown—was how the gorge narrowed after the second bend, walls of wet granite leaning in until the sky became a ribbon, and the whole river funneled through a gap you could have spat across. The water there didn't flow so much as fall sideways, stacked and folding over itself, white where it broke and green where it held its breath.

She put in anyway. Her skiff was small, shallow-drafted, built for salvage work in dead water, not this. But the portage around the gorge would take three days through country where someone had already taken two days of her rations and her backup antenna, and she had eaten the last of the smoked fish that morning, and hunger makes cowards of careful people.

"Stupid," she said aloud, to hear a human voice, even her own. "This is stupid."

The river agreed enthusiastically.

* * *

The first hundred meters were almost kind. She read the water the way she read wrecks—looking for the structure beneath the surface, the hard shapes that made the soft ones dangerous. Keep left of the drowned log. Ferry across before the seam where the current split. Don't fight the river; argue with it, quietly, and let it think it's winning.

For a little while it worked. Spray came aboard in cold handfuls. The granite walls rose on either side, streaked orange and black with mineral stain, and the noise of the water filled the gorge so completely that her own heartbeat felt like an intrusion. There was a strange peace in it, she'd admit that later—the peace of a place that had never once considered whether she existed.

Then the river dropped.

Not gradually. The whole channel tilted, the way a deck tilts when a ship decides to die, and the skiff's bow lifted and the stern went light and Yara understood, in the half-second the world gave her, that the chart's clerk had been an optimist. The hole below the drop was wide as a barn door and deep as a well, and her skiff was going into it nose-first because there was nowhere else in the universe for either of them to go.

She had time for two thoughts. The first was practical: *paddle, paddle, keep the hull square.* The second arrived uninvited, from some drowned archive of her childhood: her grandmother's voice, saying something in a language Yara had never learned, a word that might have meant *hold on* or might have meant *come home.*

Then the water took the argument away from both of them.

* * *

Cold is a language with only one word, and it says it very fast.

The river rolled her the way a diver rolls a body in a net—professional, impersonal. Down. The light above her shattered into coins and then into nothing. Her boots were full of river. The skiff was somewhere, or wasn't. She kicked for what she thought was up and found more down, the current pinning her against stone with a hand like a crane's magnet, dragging her along the bottom the way she herself had dragged sledges across the Provision's deck, a hundred feet of black water overhead.

And here was the terrible familiarity of it: she knew this feeling. She had spent twenty years buying bread with this feeling. The dark press of it, the weight, the slow arithmetic of air remaining. She had always believed that if she stayed calm enough, counted long enough, the numbers would save her. Numbers had saved her in the Provision's flooded hold, in the wreck off Skerry Point, in every cold room she'd ever locked herself inside.

But rivers are not holds. A hold stays put. A hold doesn't *want* anything.

The river wanted her, and it did not count.

Her shoulder struck rock and she heard it more than felt it, a deep wooden knock. Her lungs began their mutiny. And in the last gray moment before panic took the wheel entirely, she thought—not of drowning, which seemed suddenly ordinary, but of the strongbox sitting in her cabin downstream, and the key on its cord around her neck, and how absurd it would be to come all this way carrying a dead woman's secret just to give it back to the water early.

*No,* she told the river, with the last of her breath.

The river, being a river, ignored her.

Something else didn't.

* * *

Hands. That was the first thing, afterward, that she could not stop returning to: hands. Not a rope, not a branch snagging her jacket. Hands—one set gripping her collar from behind, another catching her wrist, sure and unhurried, moving with the current instead of against it the way a swimmer fights but a fisherman doesn't. They turned her face up. They kept her face up. When the current tried to fold her under again, the hands simply declined, the way you'd decline a rude offer, and steered her sideways into an eddy behind a granite shoulder where the water stood almost still and roared about it.

She got one lungful of air and one look: a man in the water beside her, dark-haired, bare-armed despite the cold, his face composed into an expression she'd seen on divers' faces a thousand times—the mild attention of someone doing difficult work they find unremarkable. He said something. It wasn't a language she knew. It sounded like water talking to itself.

Then there were more hands, from the bank, on lines she hadn't noticed—ropes thrown with a casualness that suggested they'd been waiting there a while—and she was hauled out of the eddy and onto stone slick with spray, and the world went to pieces in the ordinary way: gray, ringing, painful, alive.

She vomited river water and coughed until her ribs filed formal complaints. Someone put a wool blanket around her shoulders that smelled of smoke and lanolin. The dark-haired man crouched in front of her, wrung out his hair, and studied her with frank, unhurried curiosity—not the wariness she'd expected, not hostility. Recognition, almost. As if he were checking her against something.

"You're a long way past the last marked landing," he said—in trade-tongue, accented, slightly archaic, like a man quoting his grandfather.

"The chart," she managed. "Said unrunnable."

"It is." He said it without judgment, the way you'd confirm the weather. "For strangers." Then, rising, calling up the bank in that liquid language, and other figures coming down through the trees—two women, an old man, all of them moving with the easy economy of people who have lived their whole lives where the ground is steep—and Yara, wrapped in someone else's blanket, shaking on the rocks, understood that she had not been rescued by chance.

They had been watching the rapids. Watching them the way you watch your own front gate.

* * *

They carried her upriver along a path that no surveyor had ever found, switchbacking through cedar shadow, and she drifted in and out of a sleep that was mostly cold leaving her body. Fragments: the smell of resin. A child's voice asking a question and being shushed. The blanket tucked closer by hands that were gentle and did not explain themselves. Once, half-waking, she saw the old man walking ahead of her glance back, and his expression was the same one the dealer wore when he looked at her salvaged documents—except inverted, warm where his had been hungry, and that frightened her worse than the rapids had.

Because she knew that look. She'd spent her whole life diving for things other people wanted. She had never once been the thing.

* * *

She woke properly in a room that smelled of woodsmoke and cedar and, underneath it, faintly, tar—ship's tar, the smell of the Provision's preserved timbers, a smell she knew better than her own mother's kitchen. For a confused moment she thought she was back inside the wreck, in some compartment the charts never showed, warmed by some impossible fire. Then her eyes focused: plank walls, hand-adzed, tight-fitted; a window of oiled cloth glowing amber with evening; a fire in a stone hearth; her own wet clothes hung on a rack by the flames, steaming gently, and her strongbox key still on its cord around her neck—someone had looked at it and left it exactly where it lay.

She was lying on a bed that was really a ship's bunk, bolted to a frame, salvaged or inherited, with a wool blanket woven in patterns she'd seen once before: on fragments raised from the Provision's crew quarters, rotted almost to nothing, their colors guessed at by museum conservators. Here the colors were not guessed. Here they were bright, and mended, and in use.

She sat up too fast and the room performed a slow rotation. A kettle murmured on the hearth. On a stool beside the bed sat Teo's notebook—her copy of the chart gone, but the notebook itself, which she'd lashed into the skiff and assumed was at the bottom of the gorge, dry, squared neatly, waiting for her like a returned library book.

The door opened without a knock, the way doors open among people who have already decided about you.

The dark-haired man from the water came in first, dry now, dressed in a canvas jacket that had once been naval issue and had been patched so many times it had become a biography. Behind him came a woman perhaps sixty, gray-braided, with forearms like dock line and eyes the color of the river in March. She carried a bowl of something that steamed and smelled of fish and barley, and she set it in Yara's hands with the authority of someone who would not be taking no for an answer.

"Eat," the woman said. "Then talk."

Yara ate. It was the best thing she had ever tasted, which she attributed to nearly dying, and which the barley probably deserved credit for too. The fire ticked. Outside, the river went on making its argument with the stones, but from in here it sounded less like a threat and more like a voice in the next room—a family, arguing comfortably, forever.

"What do I call you?" Yara asked finally.

"Bren," said the man. He nodded at the woman. "Our elder keeps her own name for when it matters." He crouched by the hearth, and for a while he watched the fire rather than her, and when he spoke again his voice had changed—careful now, testing ice. "You came up the river alone. Past the warning shots we fired at your companions. Past the empty camp. Carrying—" he nodded at the cord at her throat "—a key that opens a box we've been keeping watch on for sixty years."

The bowl stopped halfway to her mouth.

"We pulled three of your party out of the water already," Bren said. "Two chose to stay. One chose to leave." His eyes came up, and they were not unkind, but they were not soft either. "So before you say another word, diver—you should tell us whose name is on that key. Because we've been reading it since the bank. And it's a name we buried with honors a long time ago."

The fire ticked. The river argued on. And Yara, holding a warm bowl in two shaking hands in a room that smelled of her grandmother's ship, heard herself say:

"Eriksen. My name is Yara Eriksen."

The woman by the hearth closed her eyes, the way people do at a graveside—or a homecoming—and Bren exhaled slowly, and somewhere outside a bell that had no business existing this far upriver began to ring.
