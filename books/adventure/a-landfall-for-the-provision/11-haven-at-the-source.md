## 11. Haven at the Source

Yara woke to the smell of woodsmoke and bread.

For a long moment she did not open her eyes. She lay still, cataloguing sensations the way she catalogued a wreck: methodically, item by item, because surprises underwater killed you and she had never learned to stop counting. A mattress beneath her—straw-stuffed, lumpy, real. Wool blankets, washed in something herbal that clung faintly of juniper. The creak of a house settling on timber foundations. River sound, but wrong for the rapids; this was slower water, deeper, moving with the patient confidence of a river that had already done its falling.

And voices. Low, unhurried, somewhere beyond a plank door. Not the clipped exchanges of Marsh's camp or the dealer's satellite-phone patter. These were voices at ease, the way people spoke when they had spoken to each other every day of their lives.

She opened her eyes.

The room was small and clean, walls of pale planed wood, a single window with shutters folded back to let in grey-gold morning light. Her clothes hung drying on a rack by a small iron stove, and someone had set out fresh garments across a chair—trousers of heavy canvas, a shirt woven from something soft and undyed, mended at one elbow with stitches so fine they looked machined. They weren't. She could tell. Everything here was made by hand, and made well.

Her dive knife was gone from her ankle. Of course it was. But her boots sat by the door, cleaned, laces tucked inside, and beside them lay the brass strongbox key on a loop of cord—the key she'd kept against her sternum through the capsizing, through the drowning dark, through hands hauling her up into light. Someone had found it, recognized what it was, and given it back.

That told her more than any interrogation could have.

She dressed in their clothes because there was nothing else, and because refusing would have been its own kind of answer. The shirt smelled of cedar. Outside, the cold hit her lungs like clean glass.

* * *

The village—if that was the word—sat in a bowl of valley where the river widened into a slow green lake before gathering itself again for the journey down. Terraced gardens climbed the slopes in stepped ribbons, exactly as Teo had sketched them in his notebook, though no notebook had captured the color: kale gone blue-black with frost, late beans hanging fat on trellises, orchard rows pruned with an expertise that took generations. Houses of timber and stone followed the contours rather than fighting them. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys straight up into windless air. On the far shore, half-hidden by willows, a boathouse the size of a barn stood on stilts over the water.

People worked everywhere. A woman repairing a net. Two men hauling a dugout canoe up rollers. Children running a race along the top terrace, their shouts carrying across the water—and stopping, all of them at once, when they saw her.

The silence spread outward like a dropped stone. Conversations ended. Faces turned. Yara stood at the edge of a packed-earth lane with forty strangers looking at her, and understood with a diver's clarity that she was the first stranger these people had seen in a very long time—perhaps ever, in some of their lifetimes.

An older woman came toward her through the silence. She walked without hurry, hands clasped behind her back, silver hair braided in a crown. She had the kind of face that weather makes slowly: deep lines around eyes the color of shallow water over slate. When she stopped three paces away, the whole village seemed to lean in.

"You're awake," the woman said. "Good. We pulled you out of the White Teeth yesterday evening. You'd gone under twice by the time Rurik reached you." She tilted her head. "You hold your breath like someone who's been taught properly. Badly taught, but properly."

"I'm—" Yara's voice came out scraped. "I'm looking for my expedition."

"We know." Something moved in the old woman's expression—not warmth exactly, but recognition, the look of someone confirming a suspicion held for years. "Four of you came upriver. One left with a rifle. One is here. One is here. And one," she said gently, "walked into our valley two days ago and has not stopped drawing plants since."

"Teo." Relief hit Yara so hard she had to lock her knees. "He's alive."

"He is fed, dry, and insufferably happy. He says we have seventeen species in our terraces that exist in no book he owns. He asked if he could stay until spring. I told him he could stay until he stopped asking questions, which may be longer." The woman extended a hand callused like a dockworker's. "I am Sanna. I keep the council here, such as it is. Come and eat. You can be frightened after breakfast; fear goes down easier with bread."

* * *

They fed her in a long hall whose central hearth could have roasted an ox. The bread was dense and dark, the fish smoked over alder, the tea bitter with some bark she didn't know. People drifted in and out, and every one of them looked at her—openly, without hostility, the way you'd look at a photograph of someone you'd been told about your whole life.

That was the thing that began to crawl under her skin. Not suspicion. Recognition.

A boy of maybe ten stared until his mother tugged him away, whispering something that made him glance back three times. An old man by the fire watched her eat with tears standing unashamed in his eyes. When she caught Sanna watching her watch them, the elder only said, "Eat."

Afterward Sanna led her out onto a gallery overlooking the lake, where the morning fog was burning off the water in slow rags. Below, children were being taught knots on the dock—bowlines, then something more complex, a hitch Yara knew from wreck work and had never seen anywhere else. The Provision's rigging hitch. She'd tied it herself a hundred times, sixty meters down, in the dark of a dead ship.

"Where did they learn that?" she said.

Sanna leaned on the rail. "You know where."

"The Provision sank in ninety-eight. Before I was born. There were survivors—eleven confirmed, all accounted for. The navy published the list. The inquiry ran two years."

"The inquiry ran as long as it needed to," Sanna said, "to conclude what it needed to conclude." She turned those slate-water eyes on Yara. "Ask me the question you've been carrying since you woke up. The one about why we're here."

Yara's hands tightened on the rail. "Who are you?"

"We are the crew of the Provision," Sanna said simply. "Or what became of them. Ninety-one souls went into the upper river that autumn, according to the record. Forty-three of us—or our grandparents, or their grandparents—never came down off it." She let that sit in the cold air. "The ship wasn't lost in the storm they named. The ship was scuttled. By her own crew. In the shallows near the delta, where she lies now, drowned on purpose, in water chosen because it was deep enough to hide her and shallow enough to reach."

Yara felt the words land in her body before her mind, the way pressure changes announce themselves in the ears. Sixty meters down she had moved through that hull for years. She knew its bones better than her own apartment. And it had been lying to her the entire time—a corpse pretending, a grave that was actually a door.

"They faked the wreck," she said slowly. "And then?"

"And then they came upriver." Sanna's voice stayed level, but something underneath it trembled like a plumb line in current. "There were orders aboard, Yara Eriksen. Orders the officers carried sealed, and which the crew was meant to carry out and carry home and never speak of. The Provision was not a patrol vessel on her final voyage. She was an eraser. There was a village on this river, upstream of here—a settlement of people who had been in the way of something the government wanted forgotten. The Provision's mission was to see that nothing remained. No witnesses. No records. Nothing that could surface later and embarrass anyone."

The fog burned off the lake. In the clear light Yara could see the terraces fully now, and the graveyard on the hillside above them, rows of markers going back further than any of these houses.

"Some refused," Sanna continued. "Forty-three of them, mustered in the hold one night while the officers slept, and voted with their feet. They scuttled their own ship to destroy the orders themselves—sank the proof along with the vessel. Then they came up into the mountains where the maps went blank, and they built this." She gestured at the valley, the smoke, the children with their rope. "And for three generations we have kept one law above all others: no trace. No trace downstream, no trace on the ridgelines, no trace in the world. Because the families of those officers did not all forget. Some of them inherited the shame instead of the guilt, and shame curdles, given time. If proof of the Provision's true last voyage ever surfaced, it would not just embarrass a dead government. It would point a road straight to this valley."

Yara thought of the dealer's office. The triple offer. The smooth voice asking about coordinates she had never given him, and the feeling she'd had walking out—that the wreck was watching back.

"The strongbox," she said. "The documents I brought up. That's what he wanted."

"That is what they have wanted for eighty years," Sanna said. "Every few decades, someone comes looking. Surveyors. Prospectors. Last year, a man with excellent boots and a satellite phone. We watch the river the way you watch a depth gauge. We always know."

The wind moved the willows on the far shore. Somewhere below, a child laughed, and the sound went through Yara like a hook through the hand.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You don't know me. I dove your ship. I sold pieces of her. I led armed men up your river."

"You led nothing. You carried a bag and a grudge against the world, same as most of us once did." Sanna reached into her collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small tarnished disc—a mess tag, naval issue, worn nearly blank by decades of skin. "Do you know what name is stamped on this?"

Yara shook her head.

"Sanna Eriksen," the old woman said. "My grandmother. Boatswain's mate, second class. She stood in that hold the night of the vote, and she climbed this river with a child on her back, and she lived thirty-one years in the house I live in now, and she died saying the family name would come back up this river someday, because names do. Names are hooks, and the river keeps the line." She closed her hand around the tag. "Eriksen. Your grandmother was Astrid Eriksen. Am I wrong?"

The valley seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"My grandmother," Yara heard herself say, "was a stenographer in a port office. She died when I was six. My mother never talked about her family. Said there was nothing to talk about. Said they were nobody, from nowhere."

"Nobody, from nowhere," Sanna repeated softly. "Yes. That was the price of the ticket out. Your grandmother Astrid was sixteen when the Provision sailed. She was the boatswain's niece, and when the vote came, she chose the river too—but she was young, and frightened, and eight months pregnant, and when the party stopped at the delta to make the wreck look true, she slipped away in the night and went back down to the coast and buried her name so deep even she stopped digging for it." Her voice roughened. "She sent one letter, twenty years later. No return address. Four lines. We have kept it. The last line says: *If anyone of mine ever comes up the river, feed them first.*"

Yara could not speak. The bread, the tea, the clothes laid out warm from the stove—she counted them backward now, item by item, and the inventory came out to something she had no ledger for. All her life she had been competent and alone, diving other people's wrecks for other people's money, telling herself the hunger in her chest was just ambition. It had never been ambition. It had been a search pattern. She had been sweeping a grid her whole life without knowing what she was looking for, and it had been here, at the source, the whole time.

"You pulled me out of the water," she managed finally, "and you fed me first."

"We fed you first," Sanna agreed. Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "House rules."

Below on the dock, the knot lesson had ended, and the children had scattered. One of them—a girl with Sanna's slate-colored eyes—stood at the end of the boards looking up at the gallery, at Yara, with an expression of frank, unguarded curiosity. After a moment she raised one hand, not waving exactly. Testing. Seeing if the stranger was solid.

Yara raised her hand back.

"There is more you need to know," Sanna said, and the almost-smile faded, and Yara saw the weight come back into the old woman's shoulders like ballast shifting. "About your friends. About the man with the rifle. And about what happens now that you've seen this valley—because that is a door, Yara Eriksen, and doors only swing one direction for outsiders." She paused. "But first there is someone in the boathouse you should meet. She arrived two nights ago, wet, furious, and dismantling a radio with her bare hands. She's been asking for you since dawn."

Yara's chest seized. "Ines. Ines is alive?"

"She is," Sanna said, "and she has opinions. Come."

Down at the water, the girl was still watching. As Yara turned to follow the elder down the stairs, the child called out across the still air of the lake, in a voice pitched to carry the way boat-cries carry:

"What's your name? Your *whole* name?"

And Yara, standing on the gallery of a home she had known for less than an hour, gave it—all of it—for the first time in her life without flinching.
