## 13. Ines Among Them

The radio woke her before the birds did.

Not with sound—with absence. Ines had slept for six nights in a room that smelled of cedar shavings and river damp, and every morning she woke reaching for the static that had been her constant companion since she was nine years old, the white noise her father called "the ocean of everywhere." It wasn't there. Instead there was wind through the terraces above the settlement, and beneath it, faint as a pulse under skin, the sound of children laughing at dawn.

She lay still on the narrow bed and let the strangeness settle over her like a second blanket.

They had not locked her door. That was the first thing she'd checked, that first morning, when she'd woken disoriented in this room with its woven mat and its single window facing the water. The door had no lock at all—just a wooden latch, worn smooth by generations of hands, and beyond it a village going about its business as though a stranger waking among them were the most ordinary thing in the world.

*Rescue,* Yara had said, when they'd found each other two days ago by the fish racks. *I told Marsh it was rescue.* And standing here now, watching morning light climb the opposite bank through mist, Ines was beginning to understand that Yara had been right in a way none of them had language for yet.

She dressed, laced her boots—the same boots she'd been taken in, cleaned and dried beside a fire someone had tended without being asked—and went out into the blue hour of the settlement they called Haven.

* * *

It was smaller than she'd imagined from Teo's fevered descriptions before he vanished, and larger than it had any right to be. Forty structures, maybe more, climbing the valley wall in tiers: houses on stilts, drying sheds, a long low building that might have been a hall, gardens stepped into the hillside like green amphitheaters. Water moved everywhere—in channels cut along the paths, in sluices turning small wheels, in the constant conversation of the river below. The whole place hummed with it, a frequency just below hearing, and Ines thought: *of course they live here. Of course. Anyone who has ever loved the sound of a signal would build their life inside one.*

A woman passed her carrying a basket of eels, nodded, said something in a language Ines didn't know but whose cadence she recognized anyway—Scandinavian vowels folded into river rhythms, old naval English buried in it like ballast stones. Another tongue had grown here, in the dark, fed by shipwreck and secrecy. Ines wanted to record everything. She had no recorder. She had only herself, and for the first time in her professional life that felt less like loss than like permission.

She followed the laughter.

It came from the long hall—or near it, from an open-sided shelter where morning light fell in long bars across rows of benches. And there, at the front, chalk in hand and hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a length of antenna wire, stood Ines's radio operator.

Except Ines was the radio operator. Had been, anyway, back when there had been a radio, back when there had been an expedition, back when the world fit inside a schedule and a call sign. The woman at the chalkboard wore the same face as hers did every morning, and it took Ines a full three seconds to accept that she was looking at herself—not a mirror, not a ghost, but the version of herself that existed on the other side of some line she hadn't known she was walking toward.

She laughed out loud, and forty children turned to look at her, and the other Ines—no, there could only be one; the thought arrived fully formed and refused to leave—smiled with such relief that Ines's chest hurt.

"You're awake," the woman said. "Good. I've been teaching your alphabet."

*Your* alphabet. Morse. On the board behind her, drawn in a child's unsteady hand copied from the teacher's surer one, dots and dashes marched in rows: A through M, each letter paired with a word. *A is for anchor. B is for birch. C is for current.*

"I dreamed about you," Ines said, which wasn't what she meant. What she meant was: *I've been listening for you every night since the storm took our transmission apart. I've been lying in a strange bed in a strange village straining to hear whether anyone downstream was calling my name.* But the dream was true too. She had dreamed of the radio, of its guts spread across her palms, and hands—gentle, expert, unhurried—taking it apart screw by screw while she watched, and instead of grief she had felt something dangerously close to gratitude.

"Sit," the teacher said, gesturing to an empty bench at the back. "Class doesn't mind. They're used to strangers by now. You're the third."

"The third?"

"Me, then Teo, then you." She turned back to the board. "Though Teo was carried in half-drowned, so his arrival was less educational. Now—everyone, letter N. Who remembers?"

Forty voices said *dah-dah* in ragged unison, and Ines sat down on the bench as though her knees had been asked politely and had agreed.

* * *

She stayed through the lesson. She couldn't have said why, except that watching children learn code was watching children learn a way of being heard across any distance, and there was a girl in the second row—maybe eight, gap-toothed, ferociously serious—who tapped out her answers on the bench edge with two fingers, precise as a metronome, and Ines fell a little in love with her the way you fall in love with a clear frequency after weeks of storm.

When the lesson ended and the children spilled out into the light like water finding its level, the girl lingered. She looked at Ines with the frank appraisal of the young, then tapped something against her thigh. Slowly. Deliberately.

Ines read it off her fingers: *W-H-O A-R-E Y-O-U.*

"Ines," she said aloud, then caught herself, and tapped it back: *I-N-E-S. R-A-D-I-O.*

The girl's eyes went wide. She spun and ran, shouting something up the hill in the river-tongue, and Ines understood that she had just done something remarkable—that a stranger who spoke code was a rare and wonderful creature here, that the alphabet on the chalkboard was not a curiosity but an inheritance, kept alive by a woman who had learned it somewhere else and chosen to give it away.

"She'll tell everyone you tap," the teacher said, coming to sit beside her on the bench. Up close, the resemblance was almost unbearable—the same crooked incisor, the same scar on the left knuckle. "Fair warning. By tonight you'll be famous for your thumbs."

"How?" Ines asked. Not *who are you*, though the question burned. *How.* "How does this exist? How do *you* exist?"

The woman was quiet for a moment. Below them the river ran silver and enormous, and somewhere upstream, invisible around the bend, the drowned hull of the Provision held whatever it still held.

"My grandmother's grandmother jumped ship," she said finally. "Or that's the short version. There's a longer version, and Sanna tells it better than I do, and I think you should hear it from her before you hear it from me. But the how of it—" she gestured at the terraces, the sluices, the children shrieking in the shallows, "—the how is eighty years of nobody leaving and nobody arriving. Until us."

"Us," Ines repeated.

"You, me, Teo. The wreck washed us ashore, one way or another." She smiled, and it was a tired smile, an old soul's smile wearing a young face. "I'm Sanna's granddaughter. My name is Elin. I was a signals officer, once, a lifetime ago—four years ago, actually, though it feels geological. I came upriver on a survey boat. Storm took our radio. I walked out of the wreckage into this." She spread her hands. "And I stayed."

"You *stayed.*"

"I stayed." Elin looked at her steadily. "You will too, or you won't. Nobody here will make you. That's the first thing you need to understand about Haven—it runs entirely on choice. It has to. A community built by people who refused an order can't survive on orders of its own."

"And if I choose to leave?"

Elin's smile faded into something gentler and sadder. "Then you walk downriver, and we wish you well, and we never speak of this place again, because speaking of it is the one thing that kills it." She paused. "But I'll tell you what I told myself, four years ago, sitting on that dock with a broken radio and a working pair of legs. Leaving isn't complicated because anyone stops you. Leaving is complicated because you'll know what you're leaving. You can't unknow a place like this. You can spend the rest of your life trying to get the sound of it out of your ears, and you'll fail, because it's tuned exactly right. It's tuned to you."

* * *

Teo found her at midday, or perhaps she found him—there was no real difference in a place this size, where every path eventually delivered you to whoever you needed.

He was on the lowest terrace, knee-deep in a garden bed gone gloriously wild, and he looked better than Ines had ever seen him look on the expedition: sun-browned, steady-handed, his notebook replaced by a ledger bound in oiled leather with pages dense enough to be scripture. When he saw her he stood up so fast he sloshed water over the bed's edge, and then he crossed the distance between them and hugged her hard enough to crack ribs, smelling of mud and crushed mint.

"They said you were alive," he said into her shoulder. "They said it and I didn't believe it. I don't believe anything until I've counted the specimens myself."

"Teo." She pulled back and held him at arm's length. He was thinner. He was also, unmistakably, *well*—lit from inside in a way the portage-weary botanist of three weeks ago had never been. "We thought you were dead. We dragged the pool. Yara dove it twice."

"I know. I'm sorry." He said it simply, without excuse. "I slipped at the top of the falls. Hit my head. Woke up in a canoe with a woman paddling like the devil and a poultice on my skull that smelled like heaven's own compost heap. By the time I could stand, I couldn't—" he stopped, started again. "I sent no word because there was no way to send word. And because part of me, I'm ashamed to say, didn't want there to be."

"That's not shame," Ines said. "That's honesty."

He laughed—a real laugh, rusty with disuse. Then he led her along the terrace, and Ines saw why he hadn't wanted to leave.

The garden was a census. Every plant had a stake, and every stake had writing in three scripts: Latin binomials in Teo's cramped hand, the river-tongue in someone else's flowing one, and descriptions in words Ines had never encountered anywhere. *Whitewake, for the fever of childbirth. Stoneleaf, which sweetens bitter water. The blue moss that shows where the ground remembers fire.* Species that had no entry in any flora Teo had ever studied—he showed her his cross-references, pages of them, *not in Hultén, not in the provincial survey, not in the navy's botanical annex, nowhere*—growing here in cultivated abundance, bred by eighty years of hands that had needed medicine and food and beauty in a place the maps insisted was empty.

"There are plants on this terrace," Teo said quietly, "that exist nowhere else on earth. They exist here because a ship's cook smuggled seeds in the lining of his coat rather than go home to a country that had ordered him to help erase what he'd seen. Every row is a refusal. Do you understand? This entire valley is a garden of *no*."

Ines touched a leaf whose name she didn't know and felt, absurdly, like she was touching a flag.

"Yara's here," she said. "She came upriver alone, after Marsh—" She stopped. "There's a lot to tell you."

"There always is." Teo's eyes went past her shoulder, upriver, toward the deep water where a wreck lay holding its breath. "Is she all right?"

"She's deciding something. I can see it in her. She walks the dock at night like she's measuring it for a coffin or a cradle, and I honestly don't know which."

Teo nodded slowly. "Then let me tell you what I know about decisions in this valley. Everyone here made one. The crew of the Provision made theirs in one terrible night, and their children inherited it without being asked, and the grandchildren too, and somewhere in there inheritance became choice—you can see it happen generation by generation in the way they talk about the wreck. The oldest ones say *we had no choice*. The middle ones say *our parents chose*. The young ones say *we chose*, even though they chose nothing, they were born here." He crouched and pressed his palm flat against the soil, an old habit, checking its temperature, its mood. "And the ones who arrived late—me, Elin, maybe you, maybe Yara—we get to choose with our eyes open. Which is either a gift or the hardest thing that will ever be asked of us."

"Which is it?"

"Yes," Teo said, and grinned, and Ines remembered why she'd liked him from the first day, when he'd correctly identified every plant within reach of the expedition's campfire and incorrectly predicted the weather.

* * *

Evening came down the valley like a tide, gold going to rose going to violet, and the settlement gathered in the long hall for a meal that had no head table and no servers, only pots and platters and people moving among them with the ease of long practice. Ines sat between Elin and a boatman named Rurik who had lost two fingers to a snare line and told the story twice, improving it each time. The food was fish and roots and greens she couldn't name and bread that tasted of woodsmoke, and it was, without competition, the best meal of her life, and she distrusted that feeling even as she surrendered to it.

Afterward, when the hall emptied and the lamps were trimmed, she climbed to the highest terrace, where a platform overlooked the whole valley, and found Elin there with a blanket and two cups of something hot and bitter.

"I have to ask," Ines said, taking the cup. "The radio. Our radio. You dismantled it."

"We dismantled it. I led the party, if it matters. It mattered to me that it be done gently."

"It matters." Ines wrapped her hands around the cup. "I spent three days after the storm trying to bring it back. It was the closest thing I had to a voice. And then I woke up and it was gone, and instead of screaming I felt—" she searched for it, "—relieved. Like someone had finally told me I could stop transmitting. Does that make me a traitor to everything I am?"

"No," Elin said. "It makes you someone who's been shouting into the dark for a very long time and just discovered the dark was listening the whole time." She sipped her drink. "Your radio isn't gone, by the way. It's in the signals room, on the shelf, cleaned and labeled. Every component. I couldn't bring myself to cannibalize it. It felt like grave-robbing, or like robbing a church. Sanna says I'm sentimental. Sanna is right."

"Why keep it at all?"

"Because someday someone in this valley may need to shout into the dark themselves. And because—" Elin hesitated, and Ines heard in the hesitation the weight of something rehearsed and still not easy to say. "Because I remember what it cost me to stay. The first winter, I nearly went mad without a signal to tend. I used to sit by the window tapping nonsense on the sill just to feel my hands remember their work. If you stay—if any of you stay—you'll need things that are yours. Not Haven's. Yours. Your radio is yours. I only borrowed it."

Below them the river ran black and glittering, carrying the day away toward the sea and the world and the dealer's base camp and Marsh, wherever Marsh was, with his rifle and his grief and his certainty. Ines thought of the distress call she'd gotten off through the storm—one garbled burst, coordinates half-eaten by static—and wondered who had heard it, and what they'd heard, and whether somewhere downstream men were already reading tea leaves in her error.

"Elin. If I asked you to help me send a message—just one—to say we're alive, that we're safe—could you do it? Could this place survive one signal?"

Elin was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence completely.

"Sanna would say no," she said at last. "Sanna would say that one signal is a thread, and threads pull, and pulled threads unravel sweaters and shrouds. And Sanna has buried people who died of threads." She turned, and the lamplight from the hall below caught her face, and Ines saw that she was afraid—not of the question, but of her own answer. "But I'm not Sanna. And I'll tell you what I've never told anyone here, not even her. Some nights I climb to this exact spot and I key a lamp toward the south, three shorts, three longs, three shorts, over and over into the trees, knowing no one sees it. Because a voice that can't speak starts to die, Ines. I stayed. I'd stay again. But I want you to know the price before you pay it, because I paid it in the dark where no one was counting."

Ines looked down at the valley—at the terraces dimming into sleep, the last lamps, the smoke rising straight in the windless air, the whole hidden architecture of a life built inside a silence. It was beautiful. It was a trap. It was a home. All three were true, and the truth of all three at once was the complication Elin had promised, the knot she'd warned about: *you can't unknow a place like this.*

Somewhere below, a child laughed in her sleep, or dreamed she did.

And then Ines went rigid, her cup freezing halfway to her mouth, because downriver—far downriver, at the very edge of hearing, at the threshold where sound becomes imagination—she caught it. Faint. Impossible. Three shorts, three longs, three shorts, riding the wind up the valley like a ghost asking to be let in.

Not Elin's lamp. Not imagination.

A transmitter. Real, distant, and closing.

Elin heard it too. Her face went white as the foam on the rapids.

"That's not one of ours," she whispered. "Nobody transmits from downriver. Nobody *can*."

The signal pulsed again, patient as a tide, and Ines recognized the rhythm underneath the letters—not a call sign, not a distress pattern, but a greeting. An introduction. Someone down there in the dark knew exactly what Haven was, and was announcing themselves the only polite way a radio man knows how.

Someone was answering the silence.
