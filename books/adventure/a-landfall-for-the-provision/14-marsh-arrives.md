## 14. Marsh Arrives

He had been walking for two days when the forest began to lie to him.

Marsh knew the signs. He'd seen it in other men—jungle-blind soldiers in his last tour, men who'd stared too long at green until the green stared back. The trees rearranged themselves when he wasn't looking. The river, which should have been on his left, appeared on his right, then vanished entirely, then returned wider than before, brown and patient, as if it had been waiting for him to notice. Once he heard a helicopter and stood in a clearing with his arms raised for the better part of an hour before the sound resolved into wind in the high canopy, and he understood that he was alone in a way that had no bottom to it.

He had the rifle. That was the one true thing. He checked it the way other men checked a pulse—every few minutes, without deciding to, the way the heart keeps its own time. Eight rounds. He'd fired none. That fact sat in him like a stone in a boot: he had had Teo's trail, he had had the ridge, he had had figures moving in the dusk like smoke given shoulders, and he had not fired. Warning shots over their heads, and he had lain in the ferns with his cheek against the wet earth and done nothing, because the figures had been small. One of them had been small. A child, maybe, or a woman, and his finger had refused the order his brain sent down the wire, and after that the mission had come apart in his hands like wet rope.

Then Ines was gone. Then the radio was gone. Then Yara had looked at the trampled mud and the neatly coiled antenna wire—*neatly coiled*, who coiled stolen equipment?—and said, *this was rescue, Marsh. They took care of her things.* And he had heard himself shouting at her, words he couldn't fully remember, only the heat of them, only her face going still and cold the way water goes still and cold, and then they had split the supplies with a machete's fairness, half and half, and she had watched him march upstream alone and had not said *don't*. That was the worst of it, in the long gray hours. She hadn't said don't. She had let him go the way you let a man walk into weather you can already feel on your own skin.

He was a good man. He needed that to still be true. He turned the sentence over as he walked, testing it like a coin he suspected of being shaved. He had served eleven years and never struck a prisoner, never falsified a report, never left a man behind—except that he had left them all behind, hadn't he, one by one, Teo in the portage mud and Ines in the dark and Yara on the riverbank, and the mission had gone on eating people and he had kept marching because marching was the only verb he had left.

The river people had taken them. That was the sentence he built his days out of. The river people had taken Teo, had taken Ines, had fired over their heads to herd them like animals, and now Yara was—what? Dead in a pool somewhere. Or worse. Or worse, and the worse was the thing he couldn't stop imagining: Yara sitting by some fire among them, eating their food, learning their names, the way prisoners did, the way people did when they were tired and lonely and someone finally offered them a place at a table. He knew how it worked. He'd debriefed enough returned captives to know that the wire between captive and captor went soft faster than anyone wanted to admit. Kindness was a technique. A fire was a technique. He would not be fooled by fire.

By the third morning his boots had given up pretending. The right one had split along the seam and let the river in with every step, and his left ankle had swollen to the shape of a grapefruit inside its sock, and the hunger had moved past the growling stage into something quieter and more frightening, a whiteness behind his eyes, a lightness in his hands. He ate the last of his ration paste with two fingers and then, kneeling at the water's edge to drink, saw his reflection and did not know it. A bearded man with clay-colored skin and eyes like holes burned in a tarp. He put his hand up; the man put his hand up. Marsh laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, and somewhere in the laughing he heard how far gone he was, and that was the moment he understood he was not tracking anyone anymore. The trackers had become the tracked. Something in this valley had been watching him for two days, and it had simply been waiting, the way the river waited, for him to come apart on his own.

That was when he smelled the woodsmoke.

* * *

It came to him on the wind off the water, thin and domestic and impossible. Woodsmoke meant fire, fire meant people, people meant Ines or Teo or a radio or a grave, and Marsh forgot his ankle and his hunger and the whiteness in his hands and moved toward it through the trees with the rifle up, moving the way he'd been trained, heel to toe, breath shallow, the old machinery of the body running on rails the mind had long since jumped.

The trees thinned. The ground rose. And then the ground fell away and Marsh stood at the edge of a terrace—*a terrace*, cut into the hillside, stone-faced, planted in rows of something green and leafy—and below it, spread along a bend of the river where the current slowed and deepened, was a village.

He had prepared himself for a camp. A camp was what the evidence supported: fresh-cut wood, a moccasin, figures on a ridge. A camp was a handful of lean-tos, ash rings, maybe a drying rack. What lay below him was a town. Roofs of split cedar, maybe thirty of them, forty, laid in courses that shed the rain like feathers. A long building at the center with smoke rising from a ridge vent. Gardens climbing the slopes in stepped walls, green and ordered, tended. A dock—*a dock*, pilings driven into the riverbed, planked, with three dugout canoes and a skiff tied alongside and fish racks over it hung with silver bodies. Children. He could see children, small figures running between the houses, and the sound of them reached him a moment later, thin across the distance, and it was the sound of children who had never in their lives been afraid.

Marsh sank down into the ferns and put his cheek against the earth the way he had on the ridge, and the earth was cold, and he made himself look, because looking was the job, looking was the whole of the job, and what he saw broke something in him that he suspected had been cracking for a long time.

Because it was beautiful. That was the treason of it. It was beautiful the way a thing is beautiful when it has been loved for generations—there was no other way to get that look, that settledness, that sense of a place grown into its bones like a tree grown around a fence wire. No camp looked like that. No hideout. No operation. This was not people hiding. This was people *home*.

And home, Marsh thought, with the whiteness pressing in behind his eyes, home was where they had taken Ines. Home was where they had taken Teo, maybe, if Teo was anywhere at all. Home was where Yara had been carried or had walked or had—no. He shut that door. He shut it and leaned on it. They had her. They had all of them, and they had this, a whole hidden town that did not exist on any chart, and somewhere downstream a man named Voss was sitting in a base camp with a satellite phone and a budget and a buyer's patience, waiting for a signal, and Marsh was the only man on this hillside who knew the frequency, the call signs, the window. The only man. That was not a burden. That was, for the first time since the storm took the radio, a *purpose*, and purpose hit his bloodstream like a transfusion, and he got up out of the ferns and started down the slope toward the smoke.

He did not decide to walk down openly. It simply happened, because his ankle would not take the slow way and because some ragged animal part of him wanted them to see him coming, wanted them to see what they had made, what their little warnings and their thefts had walked out of the bush: this. A man with a rifle and eight rounds and nothing else.

* * *

They saw him long before he reached the bottom. Of course they did. A shout went up—not an alarm, not exactly, more like a bell rung in a language he didn't speak—and figures came out of the houses and stood in the open, looking up the slope at him, and not one of them ran. That unnerved him more than running would have. They stood in their doorways and on their garden walls, forty people, sixty, and watched a half-dead armed man descend toward their children, and they did not run, and they did not come at him either. They waited, the way the river waited.

He stopped at the edge of the first terrace, ten paces from the nearest of them, an older man with a gray beard braided into two ropes and a wooden tool in his hand, some kind of hoe, held loosely, not raised. The man looked at Marsh's rifle the way you'd look at a dog that had wandered in out of the rain. Not afraid of it. Sorry for it, almost.

"I'm looking for my people," Marsh said. His voice came out as a ruin. He tried again. "Three of them. A woman, dark hair, radio operator. A botanist. And—" his throat closed on Yara's name and he had to work it open like a knot. "And a diver. Yara. They were taken off the river."

No one answered. More people had gathered, silent, and among them he saw the small figure he'd seen on the ridge, or one like it, a girl of maybe ten, peering around her mother's legs, and Marsh felt the rifle in his hands like something obscene and did not lower it because lowering it was not a thing his body remembered how to do anymore.

"You took them," he said. "Two nights ago, at the camp. You took the woman and the radio. I tracked you as far as the second pool. I know it was you."

"They are not prisoners here," the gray-bearded man said. His accent was strange, old-fashioned, vowels rounded like a newsreel. "No one is a prisoner here."

"Then let me see her. Let me see Ines. Let me see Yara Eriksen." The name came out and hung in the smoky air, and he saw—God help him, he *saw*—a flicker pass through the crowd at it, a softening, a recognition, heads turning toward each other, someone murmuring, and Marsh felt the ground tilt under the meaning of it. They knew her. They knew her *name*. "What does that mean," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle. "Why do you know her name. Where is she."

"Yara is here," said a new voice, and the crowd opened.

She came down the path between the long house and the fish racks wearing clothes that were not hers—wool, homespun, a coat too big for her—and her hair was wet, freshly washed, and she was *whole*, unmarked, walking easily, and behind her came Ines with a child on her hip, an actual child, riding her hip like they'd done it a hundred times, and Ines was laughing at something the child had said.

Marsh's rifle came up.

It happened below the level of thought, the muzzle swinging toward the crowd, toward the smoke, toward the whole impossible lie of it, and he heard himself shouting, his voice tearing: "Put her down. Put the radio operator down. Ines—Ines, get away from them, come here, come *here*—"

"Ines is fine," Yara said, and she kept walking toward him, which was the most insane thing he had ever watched a person do, walking toward a raised rifle with her empty hands loose at her sides. "Marsh. Look at her. Look at me. Nobody's been touched. Teo's here too—he's in the plant house, he's got a whole building of plants he won't shut up about—"

"Stop." The word came out of him in a whisper and a scream at once. "Stop walking. This is—this is a technique, this is how it works, they get you comfortable, they get you *feeding* you—" and the whiteness was fully in his head now, roaring, and his hands were shaking so badly the front sight described small frantic circles against her chest, and somewhere very far away he understood that eight rounds was not enough for what he wanted to do, which was not to shoot her, which had never been to shoot her, which was to shoot the *situation*, the whole green treacherous situation that had eaten his crew and handed him nothing, and the rifle sagged in his grip like a thing exhausted of being a rifle.

Yara did not stop walking. She came all the way to him, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and river water on her, close enough that the muzzle was touching her coat, and she put one hand on the barrel and pushed it down, gently, the way you'd push down the head of a dog, and Marsh let her, and the letting was the surrender, and he felt it go through him like a blade going through wet canvas.

"They fed me," she said quietly. "They gave me dry clothes. Sanna—there's an elder here, her name is Sanna—Marsh, they know my grandmother. My grandmother was on the Provision. That's why I found the strongbox. That's why any of this—" She stopped herself. He watched her decide not to say the rest, and the deciding frightened him more than everything else, because Yara did not keep things back, Yara was the straightest current he'd ever known, and here she was, editing.

"You've been here four days," he said. "Four days and they've got you saying *Sanna* like she's family."

"Maybe she is."

"They killed Teo."

"Teo is *alive*, Marsh. Go look at him. He's arguing with a man named Eel about whether a fern has been named before."

"They took Ines in the *night*—"

"I went with them," Ines said. She had come up beside Yara, and she handed the child back to someone without looking, the way you hand off a thing that is always handed back. Her voice was steady and kind and it made Marsh want to put his fists through the nearest garden wall. "I went because they asked, and because they were right, Marsh. The radio was going to bring Voss's people here. To *this*. To the kids." She gestured, and the gesture took in the whole village, the smoke, the terraces, the children who had stopped playing and were watching the armed man in the ruined boots with frank, unafraid curiosity. "I've seen what happens when people like Voss find places like this. I wasn't going to be the map."

"You don't get to decide that," Marsh said. "None of you get to decide that. There are procedures. There's a chain of—there's *downstream*, there's a base camp, there's a man with a contract and a satellite phone, and the only thing standing between this valley and him is—" his voice failed and he dragged it back. "Is me. I'm the only one who knows the frequencies. I'm the only one who can warn him off, misdirect him, buy you time. That's the play. That's the only play. Yara." He turned to her and grabbed her sleeve, and his hand was shaking and he could not stop it. "Help me signal the base camp. You know the wreck, you know the water, you know what Voss will do when he stops getting transmissions—he'll come upriver with divers and a budget and he will not be *kind*. I can send a misdirection, I can buy this place a month, a season, I need the antenna, Ines, I need the radio you took apart—"

"They didn't take it apart," Ines said. "I did. I'm putting it back together. For weather, for a listening watch. Not for that."

"Then I'll rebuild it myself."

"With what parts?"

"With—" He had nothing. He had eight rounds and a split boot and hands that would not hold steady, and the truth of it stood in the smoky air between them all, and the gray-bearded man with the hoe had not moved, and no one had taken the rifle from him, and that was somehow the unbearable thing, that they *let him keep it*, that they looked at this broken animal of a man and trusted him with the one thing he had left, and Marsh felt his eyes go hot and hated them for it, hated the fire and the fish racks and the child's unafraid face, hated them for the kindness he could not accept because accepting it would mean that everything he had marched through, everything he had become out there in the green alone, had been for nothing.

"Yara," he said, and it came out small, the way a man's voice goes small at the end. "Help me. Please. It's the only thing I know how to do."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Behind her, at the edge of the crowd, an old woman had appeared—a straight-backed elder with white hair braided like the gray-bearded man's, and eyes the color of river water over stone—and she was looking at Marsh not with fear and not with pity but with something more terrible than either: recognition. As if she had seen men like him before. As if she knew exactly how the story went.

"Come inside," Yara said. "Eat something. Sleep. And then we talk about the radio—all of us, with Sanna, with everyone. Nobody's deciding anything while you can't stand up."

Marsh wanted to refuse. He opened his mouth to refuse. What came out instead was a sound that might have been a word, and then his ankle folded, and it was Yara's shoulder and the gray-bearded man's arm that caught him, and the last thing he saw before the whiteness took him whole was the old woman, Sanna, watching him from the edge of the crowd with an expression he would not understand until much later, when it was nearly too late:

She was watching him the way you watch a storm coming down a valley you cannot move.
