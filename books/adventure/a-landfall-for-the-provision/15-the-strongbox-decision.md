## 15. The Strongbox Decision

The key had lived against Yara's sternum for so long she'd stopped feeling it as a separate thing. It hung on a braided cord of dive line and copper wire, small as a knucklebone, brass gone green at the edges from years of salt water and her own sweat. She'd found it in the first week of her salvage work, wedged in the captain's desk drawer of the Provision's flooded chartroom, tucked inside a rotted leather pouch with a scrap of paper that read only: *for whoever is left.*

She'd assumed it opened some officer's strongbox—brass fittings, maybe a little silverware, something to sell. She'd never imagined it would open the past.

Now she stood in Sanna's longhouse while rain needled the cedar roof above them, and forty-odd faces turned toward her like flowers toward an uncertain sun.

She hadn't meant to say anything. She'd been in Haven four days. She'd eaten their fish, slept under their blankets, watched Ines laugh for the first time since the expedition began, watched Teo press leaves into his notebook with the tenderness of a man naming children. And every hour, the key had grown heavier against her chest, until last night she'd lain awake listening to the river and understood that carrying it silently was its own kind of theft.

So she'd asked Sanna for the council fire. And now, with the smoke hole drawing the woodsmoke up into the grey afternoon, she lifted the cord over her head and held the key out on her open palm.

"This opens a strongbox," she said. Her voice sounded strange in the big room—too flat, too much like the voice she used for haggling over salvage prices. "In the Provision's hold. I've known where it is since my third dive. I never opened it."

A murmur moved through the benches like wind through reeds.

"I thought it was valuables. Brass, documents worth selling. I'm not going to pretend I'm better than that. Salvage is what I do. It's how I've eaten for six years." She looked down at the key, green-edged, patient. "But there's more than a strongbox down there. There's a logbook. The real one—the one the navy never got. I found the false one three years ago, the one they planted in the wreck to close the file. This key doesn't go to the strongbox they think exists. It goes to the compartment behind the forward magazine bulkhead, where someone hid everything before they scuttled her for real."

She raised her eyes. "I didn't know what I was sitting on until I met you. Now I do. So it's yours to decide. Not mine."

Sanna sat across the fire pit from her, hands folded on her knees, and for a long moment the old woman said nothing at all. The rain filled the silence. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and went quiet.

"You could have sold it," Sanna said finally. "To the man downstream. He'd have paid you enough to stop diving forever."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Yara turned the question over honestly, because these people deserved honesty even if all she had was the unflattering kind. "At first? Because I didn't know what it was. Because the dealer's questions scared me, and scared money spends badly." She paused. "And then because Teo vanished, and Ines was taken, and Marsh went half-mad on the ridge—and somewhere in all of that I stopped thinking about what the box was worth and started thinking about who put it there. Someone wrote *for whoever is left* and sealed it in iron and sank it with a ship. That's not cargo. That's a message. And messages are for the people they're addressed to."

Sanna's eyes were the color of the river in winter, pale and deep-running. "My grandmother was eleven years old when the Provision came up this river," she said, to the room as much as to Yara. "She remembered the sailors coming ashore in the dark, wet to the ribs, carrying nothing but what they could strap to their backs. She remembered that some of them wept. Not from fear. From relief." Her voice roughened. "They had been ordered to sink their own ship and drown the truth inside her. Instead they kept the truth alive, and let the ship die in her place. Everything we are comes out of that night. And everything we've feared for eighty years is that someday the truth would be dragged back up into the light—not by us, but by people who'd use it as a blade."

She stood, slowly, joints cracking like green kindling.

"So here is what I ask, Yara Eriksen, granddaughter of Marta Eriksen, who rowed your grandmother ashore and named her daughter after this river. I ask you to sink it. Not sell it, not give it to us to hide in a drawer—we have drawers, and drawers get found. Sink it. Open the strongbox if you must, take out what needs burning or keeping, and then put the rest back into the deepest silt of that hull and collapse the passage behind it, so that no diver, no dealer, no navy man ever walks those decks again. Let the Provision finish dying. Let her keep the secret she was built to carry."

The fire popped. Rain drummed. Yara felt the weight of the key in her palm as though it had doubled.

"And if I refuse?" she asked quietly. "If I want it for myself?"

"Then you walk out of here with our blessing and our grief," Sanna said, "and we will move deeper into the hills by spring, the way we moved once before. We have done it. We can do it again." She held Yara's gaze without flinching. "But we will not fight you for it. We did not survive eighty years by fighting divers. We survived by being worth less than the trouble of finding."

Something in Yara's chest twisted at that—*worth less than the trouble*—because it was the arithmetic she'd lived by her whole life, applied to a whole people. She knew exactly what it cost to be priced that way.

"But the choice is yours," Sanna finished. "You found it. You carried it. That gives you a voice here tonight that no one else has. Even mine."

* * *

They voted after dark.

Not the way Yara had imagined votes—instant, raised hands, quick majorities—but slowly, in the old manner, each household speaking in turn around the fire while a woman named Rikka kept tally with knots in a length of twine. Yara sat near the door, half in the circle and half out of it, watching a democracy older than any government she'd ever answered to conduct its business.

Teo spoke first. He'd come in from the drying sheds with his notebook still under his arm, and he stood with the awkwardness of a man more comfortable with ferns than crowds.

"The plants I've catalogued here exist in no survey," he said. "Seventeen species that botany doesn't have names for, cultivated in terraces that appear on no map. If the truth of this place comes out, it won't just be historians who come. It'll be everyone. Collectors, developers, governments wanting to 'protect' things to death." He sat down. "Sink it. Please."

Ines spoke with the radio shack's spare parts still grease-stained on her fingers. "I've been transmitting coordinates my whole career," she said. "Coordinates are how the world finds you. I sent one garbled distress call off that ridge and look what it nearly bought us." A dry, tired laugh moved through the benches. "Sink it. Some signals should never be sent."

Old Halvard, who had been a boy hauling nets when his grandfather told him the story of the Provision, spoke of his grandfather's hands shaking whenever naval aircraft passed overhead, and how he'd taught the family a whistle-call meaning *go silent* that children still learned before they learned to swim. Sink it, he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he sat down fast.

One young man—a fisher named Dov, barely older than Yara—stood and argued the other way. "We bury it, we spend another eighty years flinching at contrails," he said hotly. "The officers' families are already hunting. The dealer already knows roughly where we are. Maybe the only safety is the truth—out loud, documented, ours to tell before someone else tells it wrong. You don't outrun a story. You claim it."

Murmurs both ways. Rikka's twine grew knots on both sides.

Marsh said nothing at all. He sat at the far edge of the firelight, arm still bound from the ridge, and watched the proceedings with the expression of a man translating everything into a language of threats and assets. When his turn came he shook his head once and passed. Yara caught Ines glancing at him, worried, and caught herself worrying too. A silent soldier was a loaded weapon waiting for a reason.

Household by household the twine filled. When Rikka finished and walked the knots through her fingers twice, she looked up with an odd expression.

"Even," she said. "Nineteen and nineteen."

The longhouse went very quiet. Rain, fire, the river below—all of it suddenly loud.

Sanna rose. All eyes came to her, and then, following hers, all eyes came to Yara.

"No," Yara said, before anyone could speak. Her heart was slamming. "No—you can't put this on me. I've been here four days. I'm not—I don't—" She heard her own voice climbing and hated it. "I'm a stranger. I dive wrecks for money. Six weeks ago I didn't know any of you existed."

"That's true," Sanna agreed mildly, and waited.

"We owe you nothing," Yara pressed on, gesturing at the room, at the fire, at the whole impossible settlement. "You owe me nothing. I pulled Ines out of a rapid and that's—it's what anyone would—"

"It is not what anyone would do," said Halvard quietly. "We've watched outsiders on this river for eighty years, girl. We know exactly what anyone would do."

Yara shut her mouth. The key lay in her palm still; she realized she hadn't put it away, hadn't been able to. Its weight had become the whole argument, condensed to two ounces of brass.

Sanna crossed the fire pit and stood before her, close enough that Yara could smell the cedar smoke in the old woman's clothes.

"When your grandmother's name came out of your mouth the first night," Sanna said, low enough that only Yara and the fire could hear, "Rikka's mother wept. Marta Eriksen pulled three children out of the flood of '61. Half this room is alive because of an Eriksen. You think you arrived four days ago." Her hand closed gently over Yara's fist, over the key. "You arrived sixty years ago. You just took the long way."

Yara's throat worked. She thought of her grandmother—the real one, dead before Yara turned ten, a woman who'd lived alone above a chandlery in a gray coastal town, who'd smelled of engine oil and salt, who'd once, drunk on aquavit, told seven-year-old Yara that their family came from *upriver, from people who chose the water*. Everyone had laughed. Yara had filed it away with the other nonsense adults said.

It hadn't been nonsense. None of it had been nonsense. Her whole drifting, solitary, competent, hungry life had been a compass needle swinging in circles, looking for north.

"If I sink it," Yara said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "it stays sunk. No one digs it up in ten years when money gets thin. No one decides the past deserves daylight. It goes into the silt and the silt keeps it. Can this room live with that? Forever?"

She looked around the fire. At Dov, who wanted the truth claimed aloud, and whose jaw tightened but who nodded once, slow. At Halvard, whose trembling hands went still in his lap. At Teo, Ines, Rikka, the mothers, the elders, the children too young to vote but old enough to understand that something enormous was being decided over their heads.

"Forever," Sanna said. "That is the word we've been living by since the ship went down. One more secret in the silt changes nothing. But it buys us another eighty years of gardens, and whistles, and children who learn to swim before they learn fear." She squeezed Yara's fist. "Decide."

Yara looked down at the key one last time. *For whoever is left,* the paper had said. She was whoever was left. That was the whole answer, and it had been waiting for her at the bottom of a cold sea for eighty years.

She closed her fingers around it.

"At dawn," she said. "I'll need rope, cutting torch, and two of your best divers who know cold water. We open the box, we burn what burns, we keep what's ours to keep—and then I bring the bulkhead down on top of it, and the Provision keeps her secret until the river takes the hull apart plank by plank. After that, nobody dives her. Not me. Not anyone."

The sound that went through the longhouse wasn't cheering. It was quieter than that and heavier—an exhale eighty years held, released all at once. Rikka cut a fresh knot into the twine, a single knot at the end, and tied it off.

Sanna stepped back and looked at her, and there was something in the old woman's face that Yara had spent her whole life seeing aimed at other people and never at herself.

"Welcome home, Marta's granddaughter," Sanna said.

* * *

They were loading the boats in the blue hour before dawn—torches, cutting gear, coils of new rope smelling of hemp—when Ines came down the dock at a run, barefoot, her face drained white.

"Yara." She grabbed her arm hard enough to bruise. "The radio. Someone worked the set last night. The log shows a transmission—short burst, high frequency, direction-finding quality. Somebody keyed our position downstream."

Behind her, at the end of the dock, Marsh stood in the mist with his pack already on his shoulders, and he would not meet anyone's eyes.
