## 16. Marsh's Bargain

Marsh had counted the doors in Haven twice before he understood what the counting meant.

Fourteen structures on the near bank—dwellings of blackened cedar and river stone, roofs pitched steep against the snowmelt season, smoke rising from chimneys in thin gray threads that bent downriver with the evening wind. A boathouse long enough for six skiffs. A drying rack hung with fish split and salted silver-pink. A schoolhouse, if the chalkboard visible through its open window meant anything, with a garden behind it where children's boots had trampled the mud into a mosaic of small prints.

He had counted them because soldiers count things. Doors, windows, sightlines, distances to cover, distances to be covered by. It was the only language his mind still spoke fluently, and it spoke it whether he wanted it to or not, all through the long golden evenings while Haven fed him venison stew and asked nothing of him except that he rest.

The counting told him something he did not want to know: that this place could not be defended. Not by him, not by anyone. Fourteen buildings strung along a quarter mile of shoreline, backed by cliffs no one could climb quickly, fronted by water too shallow for anything but the shallow-draft boats these people built by hand. If the navy came—and the navy would come, because everything came eventually, everything Marsh had ever been part of ended with men in uniform arriving to make the paperwork true—it would come up this river and find exactly what he saw: a village full of families who had never signed any treaty, never filed any claim, never existed at all except as an error someone had decided, forty years ago, not to correct.

He sat on the guest platform they'd given him—the third dwelling past the fish racks, raised on posts above the spring flood line, furnished with a woven mat and a wool blanket that smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke—and he counted doors until the light went out of the sky.

* * *

They had treated his wounds. That was the thing he kept circling back to, the fact his mind snagged on like a boot heel in brush. He'd come down out of the high country half-dead, that much he admitted freely to himself now, alone for six days with a rifle and a grudge and a map drawn from memory that led nowhere, following ridge lines that doubled back on themselves as though the forest itself were rearranging to confuse him. His feet had gone soft inside his boots. His water had run out on the fourth day and he'd drunk from a stream without boiling it, which was the kind of mistake that killed soldiers who should have known better. On the fifth day he'd fallen coming down a scree slope and opened his forearm to the bone on a rock edge, and he'd wrapped it with his shirt and kept walking because stopping felt like dying and walking felt like something else, something worse maybe, but slower about it.

And then there had been lights below him in the blue hour, lamplight on water, and he'd thought: *at last*, and then he'd thought: *they'll kill me*, and then he'd stopped thinking altogether for a while.

He woke in a warm room. A woman with gray braids and hands like tree roots had cleaned the wound with boiled water and honey and sewn it shut with a curved needle, humming something under her breath, some old song in a language he didn't recognize but that sounded like the river sounded when it ran slow. They'd fed him broth first, then stew, then bread that tasted of ash and rye. Nobody asked him questions. That was the strangest part. In six days nobody had asked him a single question—not who sent him, not what he knew, not where Yara was or why he'd left her on the river with half their supplies. Elder Sanna had looked at him once, the morning after his arrival, with eyes the color of wet slate, and said only: "You walked far to be angry. Eat while it's hot."

He had eaten. He hated himself a little for how good it tasted.

* * *

On the third night he found the radio.

He hadn't been looking for it, he told himself, and this was almost true—he'd been walking off the stew, circling the village perimeter the way his body insisted on doing, checking the paths, noting the sentry points (there were none; they posted no watch; the idea seemed genuinely foreign to them), when he'd seen the antenna wire running from the eave of the largest building down toward the riverbank. It was a good wire, professionally strung, insulated where it crossed the roofline. He followed it.

The building was the schoolhouse annex, or maybe a workshop—in daylight it held looms and a forge and a wall of tools organized with a precision that had startled him the first time he saw it, because he'd expected squalor, expected the improvised chaos of people hiding, and instead found order, industry, competence. The radio sat on a workbench beneath the window: a transceiver cobbled from three different sets, its case scarred, its dial faces replaced with hand-lettered cards, but unmistakably functional. Beside it lay a logbook. He didn't touch the logbook. He looked at the handwriting in it—Ines's handwriting, looping and quick—and read the last entry, dated two days ago:

*Weather station check. Barometer falling from the west. All quiet.*

All quiet. As if the world outside hadn't spent forty years trying to forget this place existed. As if the world outside weren't full of men like Voss—the dealer, the smooth one, the one whose name Marsh still didn't actually know, who paid in unmarked bills and smiled like a locked door—who would burn this valley to the bedrock for a box of old paper.

Marsh stood in the dark workshop for a long time, listening to the river.

Here is what he would have said, if anyone had asked him—and no one asked, that was the whole problem, no one ever asked—here is what he would have said about why he did what he did next:

The mission had to mean something.

It wasn't greed. He'd taken Voss's contract for money, yes, because money was real and pensions were not, but somewhere between the wreck site and the rapids the money had stopped being the point. There were people missing. Teo, gone at the portage, leaving nothing but a notebook full of impossible gardens. Ines, taken in the night. And Marsh had carried the arithmetic of it up every ridge and down every scree slope: *two missing, one defected, one man left*. He had failed them. He knew the shape of failure intimately; it had visited him before, in other countries, other decades, other operations that dissolved into fog and paperwork while the men who'd died in them became lines in after-action reports nobody read. He had promised himself, lying in the mud on the fifth day with his arm bleeding and his mouth full of the taste of his own limits, that this time would be different. This time the missing would be found. This time the record would be set straight. This time somebody would answer for it.

And then he'd limped into Haven and found Ines alive. Teaching children morse code. By choice. Laughing, Sanna said, laughing more than she had on the whole expedition. And Teo alive too, cataloguing plants, naming things no outsider had named, looking ten years younger than he had on the river. Nobody had been abducted. Nobody had been murdered. The mission—the mission Marsh had broken himself against like a wave against a breakwater—had never existed. There was nothing to rescue. There was nothing to avenge. There was only a village that had never hurt anyone, sitting in a valley that appeared on no map, and Marsh's six days of agony had bought him a bowl of stew and a sewing job and the slow, dawning horror of irrelevance.

That was the thing he couldn't forgive. Not them—they'd done nothing. Himself. The mission had to mean something, because if it didn't, then he had lost Teo and Ines and Yara and his own self-respect for nothing at all, and a man could not carry that. A man could not stand under that weight. Marsh had watched better men than himself go under it. He'd made a bargain with the need, there in the workshop, standing over the radio with the river talking to itself below the window: *give it meaning, and I'll do the rest.*

Voss's base camp was downstream, at the mouth of the tributary where the expedition had staged. Marsh knew the frequency they used; he'd watched Ines log it a dozen times in her neat looping hand before the storm took the first set. He knew the call signs. He knew the protocol. He had, God help him, a talent for this—the ugly, necessary craft of turning human beings into coordinates.

* * *

He waited four more nights. He wanted that recorded too, in whatever ledger a man keeps when he's deciding what kind of man he is: four nights of waiting, four nights of eating their bread and letting the gray-braided woman change his dressing and watching children race coracles in the shallows while he turned the same coin over and over in his mind.

The arguments against were not arguments. They were pictures. Yara's face when she'd argued with him at the split—*we'll die trying, Marsh, all of us, and for what?*—and the terrible calm she'd had, the calm of someone who had already grieved the outcome. Sanna's slate eyes. The sound of the children at their lessons, reciting letters in a code that had once, in another life, meant rescue to men drowning in cold water. The smell of the bread. He catalogued all of it, laid it out before himself nightly like evidence at a hearing, and nightly the verdict came back the same, delivered in the flat voice of the man he'd been before all this, the man who had survived three deployments and a divorce and the particular loneliness of men whose skills are only wanted during emergencies: *the truth will out anyway. Better it comes out controlled. Better you're the one holding the pen when the record gets written.*

Because that was the final turn of the screw, the rationalization he'd polished until he could see his face in it: Voss would find Haven eventually regardless. Men like Voss always did. Wasn't it better—wasn't it *kinder*—for the location to arrive as information rather than as discovery? For there to be someone on the inside, someone reasonable, someone who could negotiate terms, prevent the worst outcomes, keep the navy's response measured? He was not betraying them. He was getting ahead of the betrayal. He was the only person in this valley who understood what was coming, and the only one positioned to soften it.

He believed it the way you believe a rope bridge will hold: not because the belief is strong, but because the alternative is the drop.

* * *

On the fourth night, the rain came.

It arrived at dusk as a bruise-colored wall moving upriver, and by full dark it was hammering the roofs like applause, like a crowd roaring, and the river rose visibly even in the dark, a black muscle flexing against its banks. Perfect weather for it. Everyone indoors. The dogs—there were three, lazy and friendly, useless as sentries—sheltered under the boathouse. The rain covered footsteps, covered the splash of a hull, covered the small sounds a man makes when he is doing the thing he will spend the rest of his life explaining.

He went first to the boathouse. The skiffs were pulled up on rollers, and he chose the second one, the lightest, the one he'd seen two teenagers handle easily in rougher water than tonight's. He'd been watching the boats since his arrival too. Counting things. He dragged it down the rollers with his bad arm screaming and his good arm doing the work, lowered it into the flood-swollen shallows, and held it against the current with the painter while he went back for the radio.

The transceiver weighed perhaps nine kilograms. He wrapped it in an oilcloth from the workshop—another theft, add it to the ledger—and slung it across his back, and paused, one hand on the doorframe, looking out at the rain-blurred lamps of the village. Somewhere in there, Ines slept. Somewhere in there, Teo's pressed plants hung in rows in a warm room. Somewhere in there, Sanna was probably awake—old women are often awake at such hours—sitting with her memories of a ship called the Provision and a crew that chose exile over complicity.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, to no one, to the rain. It was true and insufficient and he knew both things, and he went out into the weather anyway.

The skiff took the current like it had been waiting for him. He had no paddle worth mentioning—there was a single oar lashed under the thwart, and he used it more as a rudder than a motor, steering by the sound of the water, keeping to the slack water along the eastern bank where the current ran gentler. Twice the river shoved him toward the middle, toward the white noise of the riffles, and twice he dug the oar in and hauled himself back toward the shore-side eddy, heart slamming, the radio a dead weight between his shoulder blades. Rain filled the boat faster than he could bail it. Cold soaked through his clothes within minutes. His wounded arm throbbed in time with his pulse, a red flare behind his eyes.

He rode the flood for nearly two hours. When the banks widened and the current slackened near the old logging camp—half-rotted pilings, a rusted winch, landmarks he remembered from the expedition's ascent—he nosed the skiff into a cutbank sheltered by alders and shipped the oar.

His hands shook so badly setting up the antenna that he dropped the guy-wire stake twice. The rain hammered the oilcloth. He strung the wire low, between an alder and the skiff's mast step, a compromised antenna for a compromised act, and wired the battery pack with fingers gone clumsy from cold, and keyed the set.

Nothing. Dead air, dead dial, the faint green glow of the power lamp the only color in a world of black water and gray rain.

He checked the connections. Checked them again. Smacked the case with the heel of his hand, once, gently, the way Ines used to, and the lamp flickered and steadied, and the headset filled with the ocean-hiss of an open frequency.

He pressed transmit. "Base camp, base camp, this is Marsh, actual. Do you copy?"

Static. Then, faint through the rain-static, a voice he recognized—one of Voss's technicians, a young man with a clipped accent who had once offered Marsh a cigarette on the dock and talked about his mother in Lisbon. "Marsh? Actual, confirm identity. Your signal is—" crackle "—weak. Say again your position."

Marsh closed his eyes. Behind his eyelids: the bread. The children. Yara's calm. Sanna's slate eyes. The rope bridge swaying under him, planks dropping away into the drop.

"Position," he said, and gave it. Not exact—he couldn't have been exact if he'd tried, not in this rain, not with this heart—but close. Close enough. River bend, elevation, bearing from the logging camp, the shape of the valley. He described the fourteen buildings. He described the dock. He heard the young technician's pencil scratching it all down, or imagined he did, and the imagining was worse than the sound would have been.

"Understood, actual," the voice said at last. "Stand by. We'll want to move on this."

*Move on this.* Three words, and each one landed like a stone in still water, and Marsh sat in the flooded skiff and watched the rings spread.

"There are civilians," he said. "Women. Children. Noncombatants. Whatever happens—" His voice cracked and he hated it, hated the weakness, hated the rain for giving him an excuse. "Whatever happens, that has to be accounted for."

A pause on the far end, long enough to mean something. "Copy, actual. Anything else?"

Anything else. Marsh looked downriver, into the dark where the water ran toward the sea and the sea ran toward the world, toward patrol boats and helicopters and men with clipboards making the paperwork true. He thought about saying *nothing else*. He thought about keying the mic one more time and calling the whole thing back, telling them he'd lied, drunk, concussed, send no one. The words assembled themselves in his throat and stood there, ready.

What came out instead was: "Negative. Marsh actual, out."

He broke down the antenna in the rain with hands that no longer pretended to shake for any reason but the cold. He did not go back upstream. The current was against him now, the flood was against him, and besides—some doors only swing one way. He bailed the skiff, wrapped the radio again, and let the river take him down toward the base camp, down toward Voss and his unmarked bills and his locked-door smile, down toward the meaning he had purchased at a price he refused, for one more night, to count.

Behind him, upriver, the rain kept falling on fourteen rooftops, and the river rose, and no one in Haven posted a watch, because no one there had ever needed one before.

By dawn, the message was already moving.
