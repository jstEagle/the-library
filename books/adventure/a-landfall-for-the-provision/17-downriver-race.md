## 17. Downriver Race

They left Haven before the mist had finished lifting off the water, three longboats riding low with paddlers and gear, the river running fast and green beneath them like a vein of glass. Sanna stood at the dock as they pushed off. She didn't wave. She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, then held it out toward the boats—an old gesture, Ines had told me, meaning *carry us with you*. I found I couldn't look away from it until the current took the first bend and folded the dock, the terraces, the smoke of morning fires back into the trees.

Six days ago I had never seen this place. Six days ago my whole world had been a wreck, a strongbox, a buyer's smooth voice, and a bunk that smelled of diesel and mildew. Now I was racing downriver in a boat built by hands that had never known another shore, wearing a wool sweater Sanna's daughter had knitted without measuring me—because she said Eriksens were all the same width at the shoulders—and I was fighting for it as if I'd been born to its banks.

Maybe I had been. That was the thing I couldn't stop turning over in my chest, even now, even with the spray hitting my face and Marsh's betrayal still sitting in me like swallowed stone.

He'd transmitted at dawn. We knew because Ines had rigged the repaired radio to whisper to her instead of shout—a trick of wiring she'd learned, she admitted sheepishly, from the children she'd been teaching morse code to, who had shown her how their fathers muffled signals through wet hide stretched over the speaker. The transmission had been short. Coordinates, approximate. A request for extraction and divers. And then static, because Marsh had taken the skiff downriver alone, and whatever he'd become in the night, he hadn't become cruel enough to keep transmitting.

"He thinks he's ending this," Ines said, crouched over the radio in the stern of our lead boat, one hand on the gunwale, hair whipping. "Not starting something worse."

"Then he's a fool," said Rurik.

Rurik was Haven's best boatman, which I understood within the first hour to mean best on any water anywhere. He was sixty if he was a day, built like driftwood, with hands so scarred they looked braided, and he read the river the way I read a hull—by feel, by sound, by the small lies the surface tells. He stood at the steering oar barefoot, toes gripping the planks, and when he spoke it was usually one word: *left*, *hold*, *now*.

"He's not a fool," I said. "That's what scares me."

The dealer's team had motorboats. That was the arithmetic we couldn't escape. Two inflatables with outboard engines, fuel caches already staged downstream—the dealer planned everything, that was his gift and his rot—and a head start measured in hours. We had muscle and current and knowledge. Rurik claimed the river between Haven and the wreck ran like a gauntlet for anyone who didn't know it, and that engines were a liability in the narrows, not an advantage. I wanted to believe him. Engines don't care about your beliefs.

"They'll run the main channel," Rurik shouted over the water. "Charts say it's the only way. Charts are wrong."

* * *

By midday we'd left the wide valley behind and entered country where the river forgot how to be gentle. The banks closed in, granite walls streaked white with old high-water marks, and the current gathered itself and quickened until the boat seemed less to float than to fall, gracefully, endlessly, downhill. My arms burned. The paddlers around me breathed in rhythm—not synchronized exactly, but interlocking, a human engine older and stranger than anything the dealer could bolt to a transom.

Ines sat beside me, radio wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child. Every few miles she'd crack the case open under a tarp, listen, close it again. On the fourth listening she went still.

"Base camp's moving," she said. "I can hear their traffic. They're staging at Blackfern Bend." Her eyes came up to mine. "Yara, that's four hours from the wreck site. Maybe less."

Rurik heard. His jaw tightened, and he called something forward in the river-people's dialect—low, clipped words I was only beginning to catch the edges of—and the pace changed. Not faster, exactly. *Cleaner.* Every stroke landing like it had been placed there by a carpenter. We stopped talking entirely. There was no breath to spare for it.

It was during this stretch that I saw the first marker. A gray stone on the left bank, waist-high, carved with a spiral worn nearly smooth. Then another on the right, half-submerged, humming faintly where the water broke over it. Rurik steered between them without seeming to look.

"What are they?" I asked.

"Grandmother stones." He said it the way you'd say *handrail* or *doorknob*. "My great-great-grandfather set them. The Provision's quartermaster set them before him. Same rocks, mostly. We just re-carve them."

The Provision's crew had marked this river. Had run it, learned it, loved it, buried their dead along it. Eighty years of children raised on these rapids. I put my paddle in and pulled, and pulled, and felt—for the first time since I'd surfaced from the wreck with a strongbox in my arms—that I wasn't salvaging anymore. I was defending.

* * *

The storm found us at the mouth of the Narrows.

It came down the valley behind us like a living thing, a wall of bruised cloud eating the ridgelines, and the wind arrived first, flattening the water into gray stipple and bending the pines until they hissed. Rain followed in fat, cold drops that became sheets. Rurik made his choice without hesitation: we would run the Narrows ahead of the worst of it rather than shelter and lose our lead, because the dealer's people would be sheltering, and engines and lightning don't mix, and every hour we gained was an hour the logbook stayed asleep in the silt.

"You don't have to come through," he shouted to me, nodding at a eddy behind a rock fin where Ines could wait out the worst. "Narrows eats strangers."

"I'm not a stranger," I said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back.

He grinned through the rain. "No. You're not."

The Narrows was a quarter mile of river squeezed between walls close enough to spit across, and in flood it was less a rapid than an argument the water was having with itself. Holes opened and closed. Waves stood up eight feet tall and held their shape like things with opinions. Rurik threaded us through on a line no chart would ever show—left of the standing wave, hard right past a submerged ledge he called the Sleeper, then a straight drop through a chute so narrow the boat's gunwales kissed stone on both sides with a sound like teeth.

We came through. All three boats. I was hoarse from shouting encouragement I couldn't hear, and my hands were bloodless on the paddle shaft, and when we eddied out below the Narrows into sudden relative calm, one of the young boatmen—a boy of maybe seventeen named Anders with his grandmother's spiral tattooed on his forearm—looked at me and laughed, pure joy, and I laughed too, gasping, alive.

Then Ines touched my arm and pointed upstream, and the joy went out of the day.

Behind us, far back through the rain-haze, an engine whined. High, strained, wrong-sounding—someone pushing an outboard through water it was never meant to chew. A flash of orange. One inflatable, running the Narrows blind, in a storm, on a chart channel.

"They'll die," Ines said quietly.

"Some of them," Rurik agreed. He watched the distant orange fleck with an expression I couldn't fully read—not cruelty, but the flat accounting of a man who had seen rivers collect debts his whole life. "Question is whether we let them."

I thought about the dealer's smooth voice. About triple value, and questions about coordinates I'd never given him, and the way he'd said *your grandmother's name* like a knife laid handle-first toward me, inviting me to pick it up. I thought about the drowned hold of the Provision, and the men who'd refused an order to erase themselves, and the eighty years of grandchildren who'd learned to swim in the river those men chose instead.

And I thought about the boy Anders, and about Teo, whose body I'd never found, and about the fact that some of the men in that orange boat might be hired divers who didn't know what they were carrying, only that it paid.

"We don't save them," I said. "But we don't watch them drown either. If they make it through, they make it through. If they don't—" I looked at Rurik. "Your people know the eddies. If anyone washes out alive, pull them out. Tie them up somewhere warm. That's all."

Rurik considered this, rain streaming off his chin. "Sanna's way," he said finally, approving. "Hard hands, soft heart. Move!"

* * *

The near-drowning happened an hour later, and it happened to me.

The storm's leading edge had dumped a tributary somewhere above us; we rounded a bend and the river had changed in the space of minutes, risen a foot and gone the color of creamed coffee, full of debris—whole trees traveling like battering rams. Rurik was threading us through a strainer field when a sweeper branch, invisible under the brown water, caught our port side and swung the boat sideways.

I remember the world tilting. Remember Anders' hand missing my sleeve by inches. Remember the specific, obscene calm of going under in floodwater—the roar cutting off like a door closing, the cold punching the air out of me, the brown murk lit in stuttering flashes by lightning overhead.

I've been drowning before. It comes with the trade. But the wreck is a place with rules: gravity is steady, the dark is honest, and panic is a choice you decline or die. Floodwater has no rules. It rolled me end over end, slammed me against something soft and then something very hard, and when I clawed toward what I prayed was up, I found a churning ceiling of foam instead of sky.

*This is it,* I thought, absurdly, *this is how the river keeps its Eriksen,* and the thought should have terrified me and instead it made me furious—furious at the arrogance of dying here, six days into belonging somewhere, before I'd earned the name they kept giving me.

Something hit my arm. Grabbed. Hands—two hands, strong, sure, finding my wrist and my collar in the blind water the way I'd once found a strongbox in the black of a flooded hold. Whoever held me didn't fight the current. They angled with it, let it carry us both, waited, and then kicked hard into an eddy's quiet seam, and suddenly there was air, real air, and gravel under my knees, and I was coughing the river out of myself while lightning walked the ridgeline and rain needled my scalp.

Anders. Of course it was Anders, soaked and shaking and grinning like a lunatic, holding onto me and a root simultaneously. Behind him, Rurik's voice cut the storm: counting heads, counting boats, counting losses. We'd lost a crate of food and one paddle. Nothing else. Nothing else.

When I could speak I said, "You waited for the seam."

"My grandmother's move." He hauled me upright. "She taught it to everyone. Even strangers." A pause, and then, shyly, the first time I'd seen him anything but laughing: "Even Eriksens."

I stood there in the rain with the river roaring past and something in my chest cracking open that I'd spent thirty-one years welding shut. Every dive of my life I'd been looking for something in the dark—value, salvage, proof, money, *more*—and here it was, handed to me in daylight, in a storm, by a teenager with a tattooed arm: someone had been waiting for the seam. Someone had always been planning to catch me.

"Ines!" I turned, heart lurching. "Where's—"

"Here." She emerged from behind a boulder, radio clutched to her chest, drenched and furious with relief. "Don't you ever do that. Don't you *ever*." She grabbed my shoulder hard enough to bruise, then pulled me into a hug that smelled of woodsmoke and river, and neither of us said anything else, because there wasn't anything else.

* * *

We ran the last stretch in the storm's dying tail, and it was Ines who heard it first.

She'd cracked the radio open under the tarp one more time as the rain thinned to drizzle, and her face did something complicated. "They're through," she said. "Both boats. The Narrows took one man—swept overboard, they're calling him lost. The rest made it down." She swallowed. "They're at the wreck site. Yara—they're *at the Provision*. The dealer's got divers in the water."

The river carried us around the final bend, and there it was: the grey shoulder of the point where the Provision lay drowned beneath forty feet of cold water, the shoreline I knew better than my own heartbeat, and on it—orange tarps, generator hum, moored inflatables, figures moving with the crisp purpose of people being paid. Camp established. Dive flag flying. A compressor coughing exhaust into the clean air.

We slid into a hidden eddy beneath an overhang of alder, and Rurik killed our momentum with a single stroke, and we drifted, watching, while the current whispered past us toward the sea.

"So close," Ines breathed. "An hour. Maybe two. If the storm hadn't—"

"The storm didn't beat us," I said. "It beat *them* worse. Look." I nodded at the waterline, where the dealer's crew were hauling gear, arguing, one man gesturing at the murk of the flooded river. "Their visibility down there is zero. Coffee-colored water, forty feet deep, a wreck they've never dived. They can't find a logbook in that. They can't find the *wreck* in that." I felt the plan assembling itself in me the way a dive plan used to assemble—depth, time, bottom, ascent. "They need a guide. Someone who knows the hull. Someone whose grandmother's name opens doors."

Ines went pale. "Yara. No."

"He offered," I said. "Wealth and immunity. He made the offer before he knew I'd say yes. Men like him always leave the offer standing—it costs nothing." I watched the orange camp, watched the dive flag snap in the wet wind, and underneath the fear I felt something steadier, something that had been growing in me since a pair of hands found my wrist in the flood. "So I'm going to walk into that camp and say yes. And then I'm going to take them into the Provision's black hold, and I'm going to be the last person who knows the way out."

Rurik looked at me for a long moment, rain dripping from his braided beard. Then he reached into the boat's locker and pressed something into my hand: a grandmother stone, palm-sized, the spiral freshly carved, sharp-edged, new.

"For luck," he said. "Or for leaving where you've been. Both work."

I closed my fist around it, cold stone warming against my palm, and started wading toward the shore where the dealer was waiting—and where, beyond him, beneath forty feet of murdered light, the Provision held her last secret, and held, unknowing, her last Eriksen.
