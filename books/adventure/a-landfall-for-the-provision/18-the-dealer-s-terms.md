## 18. The Dealer's Terms

They came downriver with the storm at their backs, and the storm did not forgive them for it.

Yara had thought she knew the river. She had spent half her life in its drowned corridors, reading its moods through the soles of her feet against a hull, but the water between Haven and the lowlands was a different animal—a brown, muscular thing swollen with rain, carrying whole trees like battering rams, folding the sky into itself until there was no telling where the current ended and the clouds began. Ines rode the bow of the lead skiff with her hood back and her hair plastered to her skull, one hand on the radio case lashed beside her, shouting soundings over the wind. Behind them came two of Haven's boatmen, brothers named Alder and Rusk, who steered as if they'd been poured into their boats rather than born, who read the white water the way Yara read a chart—instinctively, without apology.

They passed the first campsite at midday: cold fire ring, tire tracks in the mud already filling with rain, a scatter of compressed-air cylinders stacked under oilcloth with the care of men who expected to come back for them. The dealer's people had been here. Were here, maybe—hours ahead, no more.

"They'll have posted a watch," Marsh said. He sat amidships, gray-faced, his broken arm splinted against his chest, and he had not slept since the night of his confession. He kept looking at Yara as if checking whether she still existed. "Vex will have posted a watch."

"Then we go quiet," Yara said.

The Provision's anchorage opened before them near dusk, when the storm thinned to a gray drizzle and the light went the color of old pewter. The wreck lay where it had always lain, its broken spine just breaking the surface at low water, gulls wheeling above it in slow complaint. But the site was wrong. Yara felt it before she saw it—a barge moored over the forward hold, floodlights burning pale through the drizzle, a compressor's drone rolling across the water like a hive that would not sleep. Diver down flags snapped on three separate lines. A generator coughed somewhere behind the superstructure. Men moved on the barge deck with the unhurried economy of professionals who had all the time in the world, because they believed they did.

Too late. They were too late by hours, maybe by a day, and every hour the divers were below, they were tearing through the rooms her grandmother might have slept in.

Ines cut the engine and let them drift behind a fallen tree at the river's edge, the current nudging them gently against the bank like a hand easing a door shut. Rain ticked on the canvas. Somewhere out on the water, a diver's bubbles broke the surface in silver strings.

"Four guards I can count," Rusk murmured. "Maybe six. They're not expecting company."

"Vex is expecting *her*," Ines said quietly. She looked at Yara. "He left you alive twice. That wasn't mercy."

Yara stared at the floodlights bleeding across the black water, at the wreck beneath them wearing its crown of lights like something being interrogated. Her grandmother had been aboard this ship. Sanna had told her the name—Sigrid Eriksen, bosun's mate, twenty-six years old, who had stood on this very deck in the dark of a false night and chosen the river over the order. And now strangers were crawling through her bones with lamps.

"I'm going in," Yara said.

Marsh started to speak. She raised a hand and he stopped—not because he feared her, but because he owed her, and they both knew it, and it sat on him like ballast.

"Alone," she said. "If I'm not back by full dark, take the boats up the side channel and don't look back."

"And if he takes you?" Ines asked.

"He won't." Yara pulled her knife from her belt and considered it, then left it. "He wants something from me. That's the only reason I'm still breathing."

* * *

She swam the last hundred meters underwater, coming up inside the shadow of the wreck's listing hull, where the floodlights couldn't reach and the rain drummed on iron overhead. From there it was an easy climb—the same rusted ladder rungs she'd used a hundred dawns—and then she was standing on the deck of the Provision for the first time in her life, in the open air, with strangers' voices carrying across the water toward her.

The barge was tied off twenty meters away. A gangway connected it to the wreck's superstructure, and under the nearest floodlight, sheltering from the drizzle beneath a tarp rigged between stanchions, sat a table, two chairs, a bottle of something amber, and Vex.

He was smaller than she remembered. On land, in the dealer's office with its soft lamps, he had seemed to fill the room; here he looked like what he was—a neat, dry man in a wet country, holding a glass of whiskey he wasn't drinking. He didn't startle when she stepped into the light. He simply turned his head, the way a man turns to greet someone he's been waiting for.

"Miss Eriksen," he said. "Sit down. You're soaked through."

"You knew I'd come."

"I hoped." He gestured to the second chair. When she didn't move, he shrugged and poured a second glass anyway, setting it on the table's far edge like bait. "I'd have bet on it, actually. People who run rivers always come back to their wrecks. It's a kind of homing instinct. Like salmon."

Below them, faintly, she could hear the compressor cycling, the hiss of air lines, the muffled percussion of tools against eighty years of silt and silence. Her hands curled at her sides.

"How many divers do you have down there?"

"Three shifts, rotating." He said it pleasantly, watching her face. "We've cleared the forward compartments. Very thorough work, your navy—everything burned or pulped before the scuttling. Except, of course, the things they never found." He lifted his glass to the black water. "Which brings me to you."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Of course not." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it made her skin crawl precisely because it was genuine. "Let me tell you a story instead. A salvage story. There was a diver—competent, solitary, careful—who brought up a strongbox from this wreck. Sealed. Naval issue. Inside it, documents that should not exist: manifests, orders, a captain's private log fragment. Papers proving that the Provision never sank where the navy says she sank, doing what the navy says she did." He turned the glass slowly on the tabletop. "That diver sold those papers to a dealer. Fair price. Clean transaction. And then that dealer asked her a few questions about coordinates, and she got nervous, and she disappeared upriver with an expedition, and everyone on that expedition vanished one by one until only she was left."

The rain filled the pause.

"Here's what my employers understand," Vex continued, "and what you are only beginning to understand. Those documents aren't history. History is dead and doesn't bleed. Those documents are *leverage*. There are families—old families, respectable families, names on hospital wings and naval academy gates—who descend from the officers of the Provision. What happened on this ship, whatever your river ghosts believe happened, touches their honor. Their estates. Their peace. They have spent eighty years and considerable money making sure this wreck stays a legend. And now there's a community upriver—" he said it lightly, almost kindly, watching her flinch— "sitting on the living proof. Descendants. Testimony. A whole village of inconvenient truth."

"You don't know anything about them."

"I know they took my expedition apart like a watchmaker," Vex said. "I know your botanist is drawing flowers for them and your radio operator is teaching their children code—I recovered enough of Marsh's transmission to know the shape of it. And I know, Miss Eriksen, that you found them. Which means you can find them again. For the right buyer."

"There is no right buyer."

"There's always a right buyer." He set down his glass and leaned forward, and for the first time the warmth drained out of his face, leaving something older and hungrier underneath—the collector's face, the man who had spent his life learning that everything has a price and resenting the rare things that pretend otherwise. "But let's stop negotiating over abstractions. Let's talk about you."

He reached into his coat and produced a folder, thin, water-stained at one corner, and laid it on the table between them. He didn't open it. He just rested two fingers on it, the way a man rests his hand on a chess piece he's about to move.

"Do you know why I tripled your price for that strongbox? Not because of the documents. Documents I can get anywhere, eventually, with enough money and enough divers. I paid triple because of the name on the crew manifest inside. Sigrid Eriksen. Bosun's mate. One of eleven crew unaccounted for after the sinking—one of the eleven who walked off this ship into the jungle and were never listed as deserters, never listed as dead, never listed at all. Erased. My employers erased them once, Miss Eriksen. Paperwork is a wonderful tool." His fingers tapped the folder once. "Your grandmother was aboard this ship. Your grandmother helped build whatever is upriver. Which means you are not a salvage diver who stumbled onto a story. You are the story. Bloodline, inheritance, the whole romantic architecture of it. And my clients find that *fascinating*."

The rain came harder. Out on the barge, a man shouted something about air pressure, and the compressor note changed pitch, and Yara thought of the hold below—black and blind and full of her grandmother's ghost—and of Sanna's face in the lamplight saying *you have the deciding voice*, and of the weight of a key in her pocket that opened a box no one had opened in eighty years.

"What's in the folder?" she asked.

"Options." Vex opened it and turned it toward her. Photographs. Aerial surveys of the river system with contour lines and grid references. A page of naval correspondence, decades old, stamped and countersigned. And a single sheet on top, typed fresh, with blank spaces where figures should be. "This is an offer of employment. Salvage consultant, exclusive contract, hazard pay. This line is your fee—it's negotiable upward, considerably. This line is immunity. Whatever your friends did to my expedition, whatever you did to my divers' charts, none of it follows you. New papers, new name if you want one, a life downstream where nobody asks questions." He slid a pen across the table. "And this—" he tapped the aerial survey— "is how we find your village with or without you. Helicopters, Miss Eriksen. My clients have been patient for eighty years, but patience is not infinite, and neither, frankly, is the rainy season."

"You'd burn them out."

"I'd *buy* them out," Vex said. "I'm a dealer, not a soldier. Land, relocation, quiet compensation—my clients prefer absorption to annihilation. But absorption requires cooperation, and cooperation requires a guide, and the only guide my clients trust is a woman with Eriksen blood who has already proven she can dive this wreck better than any of my hired hands." He spread his hands. "You could save them a great deal of trouble. Or you could cost them everything. Either way, you're the hinge, and hinges don't get the luxury of staying neutral."

Yara looked at the pen. Brass barrel, heavy, expensive. It lay on the wet table like something drowned and recovered, and she thought: *this is how they do it. Not with guns. With grandmothers.* They had taken the one thing she'd never had—a family, a name, a place at a table—and turned it into currency, priced it, offered it back to her with interest. Sign, and her grandmother's people would be mapped, surveyed, absorbed, their secret converted into settlement terms and non-disclosure clauses. Refuse, and the helicopters came anyway, and her name would be on nothing, and the river would remember her as the one who led the wolves to the door or the one who wasn't strong enough to bar it.

"You keep saying *my clients*," she said. "Say their names."

Vex's smile flickered—just barely, just at the corner. "You haven't earned the names."

"No. But my grandmother did." She stepped closer to the table, close enough to see the rain beading on the photographs, close enough that he leaned back a fraction. "Eighty years ago, officers on this ship signed orders to sink evidence of a massacre and lie about it forever. Eleven sailors refused. They gave up their country, their names, their lives—all of it—to keep one promise. And now their grandchildren grow food on terraces their great-grandparents cut by hand, and they took me in without asking for a single thing except that I choose well." She picked up the pen. Vex's eyes brightened. She held it up between two fingers, turning it slowly, letting him watch. "You want to know what I inherited from Sigrid Eriksen? It isn't a name. Names are cheap—you just proved that. It's this."

She dropped the pen over the rail. It fell without a sound into the black water between the hull and the barge, and the river closed over it the way it closes over everything, patiently, permanently.

Vex exhaled through his nose. When he spoke again, the warmth was gone entirely, and what remained was worse—flat, professional, almost respectful. "Then we do this the expensive way." He raised two fingers, and lights came on along the barge rail; men she hadn't seen stepped out of the shadows, and one of them carried a rifle slung loose and easy. "Hold her boat crew at the treeline. Nobody drowns tonight—that's wasteful and it draws coroners. We put her somewhere comfortable until the weather breaks." He looked back at Yara, and for one moment the dealer surfaced again behind the flat eyes, curious, almost admiring. "You should know, before they take you below—the logbook isn't in the forward compartments. My divers have torn this ship apart for six days. So either it doesn't exist, or it's somewhere only an Eriksen would know to look. Sleep on which of those you'd rather be true. Because at dawn, Miss Eriksen, you're going to show me."

They walked her aft along the barge, past the compressor and the stacked tanks, and as they crossed the gangway she looked down once into the water churning black against the wreck's flank—down to where the old hold waited in the dark, and the true logbook lay in its silt cradle, and the key burned in her pocket like a coal.

Below her feet, faint through the steel, she heard the divers' hammers ringing against her grandmother's ship.

And far upstream, through the rain, so faint it might have been thunder—she heard the river, running toward her.
