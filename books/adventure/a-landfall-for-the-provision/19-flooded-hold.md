## 19. Flooded Hold

The Provision's hull had a voice.

Yara had known it for years—the low groan of steel remembering its death, the tick of silt settling, the way current pushed through the torpedo gash aft and sang through the ribs like breath through a flute. Divers who didn't know her called it haunted. Yara called it home the way you'd call a grave home: carefully, with your hat off.

Today she swam it with three strangers on her heels, and she hated every one of them.

* * *

They had come down from the dealer's base camp at first light—four divers, two tenders, a compressor rig bolted to a barge that squatted over the wreck like a vulture that had learned patience. The dealer himself, Voss, wore a black wetsuit tailored to his shoulders and carried himself like a man who had never once been cold. He'd smiled at Yara across the water all morning, that same smooth smile from the day he'd tripled her price, and said, "You know these passages better than anyone alive, Miss Eriksen. Lead us to the logbook and everything else falls away. The money. The questions about your grandmother. All of it."

He thought the bloodline was a hook. He didn't understand it was an anchor—and that anchors hold people in place as often as they drag them down.

So she'd nodded. She'd shown them the gash. She'd led them into the dark.

And Ines was up top with a spanner and a grudge, doing something patient and vicious to the dive tender's comm line.

* * *

Inside the hold, Yara ran her light along the bulkhead where the numbers used to be. The paint had gone the color of old tea. She counted frame stations by touch—eleven, twelve, thirteen—her gloves reading the rivet lines like braille. Behind her, the three contract divers kicked in loose formation, their bubbles rising in silver strings, their lights swinging wide and nervous. They were good. They were also strangers to this water, and the water knew it.

Voss swam last, close behind her shoulder. Even down here, even breathing off a regulator, he managed to project the sense of a man conducting a meeting.

She'd planned this for two nights, lying awake in Haven while Sanna's oil lamp burned and the community argued itself hoarse around her. *Sink it forever,* Sanna had asked, and the vote had come down to Yara's voice like a stone coming down on a scale. But sinking it meant reaching it first, and reaching it meant getting past Voss, and getting past Voss—

—meant this. Meant lying with her whole body, her whole history, her grandmother's name in her mouth like a held breath.

The hold opened ahead of them, a cathedral of collapsed decking and rusted cranes. Silt hung in the water column like weather. Her light found the forward cargo hatch, the one every chart of the wreck marked as the route to the officers' country, because every chart was wrong, and she had spent three years making sure the charts stayed wrong.

She stopped. Turned. Gave them the hand signal she'd taught them on the surface that morning: *passage narrows, single file, follow my line.*

Three nods. Three lights converging on her like moths.

She led them into the false passage.

It was real enough—a maintenance crawl between frames fourteen and sixteen, wide enough for a diver with discipline, tight enough to punish one without. She took them forty meters in, past a rusted valve wheel furred with orange bacterial bloom, past the rib where she'd once snagged her own tank and nearly joined the dead. Then she brought them to the junction where the crawl split, and there she did the thing she'd rehearsed against the inside of her own skull until it stopped feeling like betrayal and started feeling like arithmetic.

Left fork: she tied her guideline off to the cleat and paid out line, sweeping her light down the left-hand branch, letting them see the clean geometry of it, the absence of silt disturbance. A lie built from truth—she *had* cleared that branch herself, years ago, so that anyone following the published charts would find exactly what the charts promised.

Right fork: untouched. Blind. It dead-ended in a bulkhead collapse that had been waiting fifty years for someone stupid enough to test it.

She pointed left. She made the signal for *logbook storage, twenty meters.* And then, when the lead diver—Kessler, the big one with the calm hands—moved to pass her and take point, she shook her head and gestured: *I go first. Tight squeeze. My wreck.*

Kessler hesitated. Down here, faces told nothing behind masks, but she felt the hesitation anyway, a pressure change in the water, the way a room changes when a smart man starts counting.

Then Voss, behind them all, gave a thumbs-up. Trusting her cost him nothing, he thought. She was the guide. Guides led.

She went left. They followed. And thirty meters down the false branch, at the spot she'd chosen because the overhead plating was already sprung and weeping rust, she did the second thing.

She cut her own line.

Not hers—*theirs*. The reel she'd been paying out belonged to Kessler; her own safety line ran the other way, back through the right fork, spooled on a clip at her hip where no one had looked because no one looks at the guide. She let Kessler's reel drift free in the black water, a pale ghost unspooling into nothing, and she backed out of the branch on her own line, silent, while three lights ahead of her swung and searched and found only silt, only rust, only the particular darkness of a ship that had decided what it would give up and what it wouldn't.

By the time they realized the line was gone—by the time Kessler's gloved hands closed on empty water where the tether should have been—Yara was through the junction and into the right fork, and the silt she'd disturbed behind her was blooming up like smoke, erasing the junction, erasing her, erasing the difference between the passages until there was only whiteout and the sound of three men breathing too fast.

Above them, Ines was doing her work. Yara couldn't see it, but she could feel it the way you feel weather coming: the tender's surface line would be screaming static right now, or worse, transmitting beautifully to a base camp that heard nothing back. No buddy calls. No emergency surface signal. No air. Just the river, keeping its mouth shut, the way Haven had taught it to.

* * *

The true route to the strongbox room ran through the flooded wardroom flat, and Yara swam it now alone, her light narrowed to a coin, her breathing long and slow the way her grandmother's daughter's daughter should breathe in this place—as if the ship recognized her.

It did. That was the horror and the grace of it. Every time she entered the hull she felt the Provision settle around her like a dog hearing its name, and she had never told anyone, not even Sanna, because some inheritances are embarrassing. You don't tell people the dead ship likes you.

The logbook lay where the elders' map said it lay—not in the captain's safe, which Voss's people had already gutted on day one and found only ballast and barnacles, but below it, in the bilge void the crew had cut in 1943 with cold chisels and desperation, waterproofed in tarred canvas and soldered tin. The Provision's true log. Names. Dates. The order to scuttle the evidence of the massacre upriver, signed by officers whose grandchildren now sent divers into the dark to burn the past clean. Every page a wound. Every page a door.

Yara reached the void. Worked the panel loose—it came easier than it had eleven days ago, as if the ship were helping, and she hated how much comfort she took in that—and drew the tin box out into her light.

Small. Lighter than it should be. Fifty years of river pressing down on everything above it, and this little coffin of a thing floating up into her hands like it had been waiting.

She clipped it to her harness. And that was when the ship spoke.

Not the groan. Not the tick. A boom—deep, structural, felt in the teeth before it reached the ears—rolling up through the keel from somewhere aft. The silt around her shivered. A rain of rust flakes drifted down through her beam like red snow.

The false branch. The sprung overhead plating she'd chosen precisely because it was already failing. Three divers kicking hard in panic, in whiteout, in a crawl never meant for four men's adrenaline.

Yara went cold in a way the water couldn't account for.

* * *

She had planned for lost divers. She had planned for lost tempers, lost lines, lost nerve. She had planned for Voss surfacing furious and Ines talking her way through it with a dead radio and a straight face. What she had not planned—what she had refused to plan, because planning it meant admitting it was possible—was the sound she heard next: a second collapse, closer, and then silence with a shape to it. The specific silence of a space that has just stopped being a space.

Her gauge read sixty bar. Enough to leave. Enough to be gone before any of it touched her, up the line, into the grey morning, into Ines's skiff and downriver and home.

Home. The word arrived without permission and sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She turned toward the aft section instead.

* * *

Finding them took eleven minutes she did not have. The whiteout was total in the false branch, her light reduced to a rumor, and she navigated by the things the charts never recorded: the lean of the deck plates, the smell of the water—each compartment of a drowned ship has its own taste, iron and tannin and old fuel—and finally by sound. Kessler's regulator, rasping fast and shallow. A second set of bubbles, steadier. And under both, a third rhythm that was wrong—too slow, too mechanical, the cadence of a man conserving air through discipline rather than calm.

She found them at the collapse. The sprung plating had let go entirely, bringing down a section of the frame above it, and the crawl ended in a wall of twisted steel and packed sediment. Two of the divers were clear of it—Kessler and the young one, Rask, pressed against the bulkhead, lights off, breathing hard in the dark to save their air. The third was Voss.

His leg was under a deck plate the size of a door. Not crushed—pinned, the edge of it wedged across his calf where the frame had folded. His light lay beside him, still burning, illuminating a small theater of ruin: his face behind the mask, gray with effort, his hands flat against the steel as if he could hold it up by will. His eyes found her light and fixed on it, and even now—even pinned under half a century of dead warship—he managed to look like a man reviewing a contractor's delay.

Yara hung in the water six feet away, the tin box heavy on her harness, and did the arithmetic she had sworn not to do.

Leave, and the river solved everything. Voss gone, the expedition leaderless, the money scattered, the navy's interest dissolving into a tragedy no one could investigate because everyone who could testify was either complicit or drowned. Haven safe. The logbook sunk forever, as voted, as promised. Clean. Final. The wilderness forgives nothing—that was the first law of the river, older than the ship, older than the massacre, older than any of them. The river takes what it takes and keeps no ledger and owes no apology.

She looked at his hands on the steel. At the regulator hose trembling with each measured breath. At the man beneath the smoothness, which turned out to be just a man, sixty years old, cold, afraid, dying by inches in the dark of a story he had walked into believing he was its author.

*You owe him nothing,* said the part of her that had hauled scrap alone at dawn for nine years, that had learned competence as a substitute for company, that had watched a patrol boat's searchlight sweep the water and felt nothing but the hunger to be worth more than what she pulled out of it.

But that was the thing. That was the whole thing. The wilderness forgives nothing—but she wasn't the wilderness. She was Yara Eriksen, and Eriksens, it turned out, had jumped ship rather than do the unforgivable thing, seventy years before she was born. It was in the blood. Sanna had told her so, and she hadn't believed it until now, hanging in the black with her enemy's life balanced against her escape, discovering that the family she'd never met had been shaping her hands all along.

She swam in. Set her light on the deck plate. Got her shoulder under the frame edge where it had folded, found the pivot point the way she'd found a thousand salvage points, and looked at Kessler and Rask and pointed: *here, here, lift on three.*

They understood. Men who dive together learn the grammar fast.

On three, the ship groaned, resisted, and gave—not much, four inches, five, enough. Rask dragged Voss clear as the plate settled back down with a boom that rolled away through the hull like thunder looking for its storm.

Voss's gauge read ninety bar. Hers read forty-one. Four divers, one route, and a whiteout between them and the world.

Yara took Kessler's reel from where it had drifted, clipped it back to his harness, and pointed them all up her line. Then she put Voss's hand on the guideline herself, wrapped his fingers around it, and held them there one second longer than necessary—long enough for him to feel, through two layers of neoprene, that the grip guiding him out of the dark belonged to the woman he'd come to bury.

His eyes over the mask said something she chose not to translate.

* * *

They surfaced into rain.

The barge loomed out of the grey, and Ines stood on its deck with a spanner in one hand and an expression of magnificent innocence, and behind her the comm housing hung open like a dissected bird. "Lost you for a bit," Ines said cheerfully, to the rain, to no one. "Surface conditions. Terrible season."

Voss came up the ladder last, hauling his bad leg, and stood dripping on the deck with the river running off him, and looked at Yara for a long moment across the wet steel. Whatever he was going to say, whatever recalculation was grinding through behind those smooth features, he swallowed it. Men like Voss survive by knowing when a story has slipped out of their hands.

"Logbook?" he said instead. Quiet. Only for her.

"Gone," Yara said. "Or never there. Ask the river."

Something moved behind his eyes—not belief. Arithmetic again. But the rain was falling harder, and the leg was bleeding into his boot, and the nearest hospital was four days downriver, and some debts you collect later or not at all.

She turned to help Ines strike the jamming rig, and that was when she saw him.

Marsh.

Standing at the tree line above the camp, half-in and half-out of the shadow of the cedars, gaunt as a winter branch, in clothes that had been Haven's clothes two days ago. He must have come downriver along the bank, following the barge noise the way a wounded animal follows water. He was looking at the divers. At Voss. At the wreck buoy bobbing in the rain.

At her—standing over a man she'd just pulled out of the dark, on a boat full of the people Marsh had given away a home to stop.

Their eyes met across the rain, and Yara saw him understand, all at once, everything: the jammed radio, the empty-handed divers, the lie she'd built out of truth. His jaw worked. His hand twitched toward a rifle that wasn't there.

"Yara," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle like river ice going out in spring. "What did you do?"
