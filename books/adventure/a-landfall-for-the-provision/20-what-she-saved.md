## 20. What She Saved

Marsh heard the collapse before he understood it—a sound like the river itself clearing its throat, deep in the Provision's drowned ribs, followed by the flat crack of timber giving way and then a silence that pressed against his eardrums harder than any noise.

He was on the wreck's upper deck, knee-deep in water that had reclaimed the gunwales, holding the line for the dealer's second diver. The first—Voss, the one with the scarred knuckles who never spoke—had gone down twenty minutes ago with Yara, following her torchlight into the hold where she claimed the logbook lay. The dealer, Aldous Crane, had insisted on going himself after that. Pride, Marsh thought now. Or greed wearing pride's coat.

The line in his hands went taut, then slack, then taut again with a jerking rhythm that was not swimming.

"Crane." He said it to the rain, to the grey morning, to no one. Then he pulled.

The rope burned through his palms even through the gloves. Whatever was on the other end was heavy—not a man swimming up but a man buried, dragging half the ship's grave with him. Marsh braced his boots against a corroded bollard and hauled, hand over hand, the way he'd once hauled wounded men out of burning vehicles in a war nobody had thanked him for fighting. His shoulders screamed. The river fought him. The river always fought him; that was the one thing about this country he'd understood from the beginning.

A head broke the surface.

It took him a moment to recognize it as human. The face was black with silt, the hair plastered flat, the eyes wide and white-ringed above a mask pushed up on the forehead. Crane coughed—a wet, tearing sound—and vomited river water across the deck plates. His regulator hung by its hose, swinging like a hanged man.

"My leg—" Crane rasped. "Something fell. I can't—"

Marsh got an arm under the man's chest and dragged him onto the tilted deck, out of the current's main pull. Up close, Crane looked smaller than he ever had at base camp, smaller than he'd looked buying drinks and telling stories about provenance and patience. His left leg was pinned beneath a shelf of collapsed plating—not deeply, but enough. Blood threaded pink through the water pooling around them.

"The others," Crane gasped. "Yara. Voss. They were ahead of me when it came down."

Marsh looked at the black mouth of the hold hatch, maybe four meters away, exhaling cold air that smelled of iron and old wood and something older still. He thought of Ines's radio dismantled with loving care. He thought of Teo's notebook full of impossible gardens. He thought of the ridge at dusk, figures silhouetted against a bruised sky, and shots fired over their heads instead of into them—warnings, when bullets would have been easier.

He thought of the transmission he'd sent at dawn three days ago, crouched over the stolen radio while Haven slept, his fingers shaking as he keyed in coordinates he only half-knew, because some broken part of him needed the mission to mean something, needed all this loss to purchase *something*.

And here was what it had purchased: a drowned ship, a trapped man, and two divers unaccounted for in the dark below.

"I'll get you free," Marsh heard himself say. "Then we're getting off this wreck. All of us. That's the deal."

Crane laughed—or sobbed; from him it was hard to tell the difference. "You don't give the orders here, soldier."

"No," Marsh agreed, working his fingers under the plating's edge. "I gave the wrong ones already. I'm correcting the file."

* * *

The plating shifted on the third try, grinding against bone, and Crane screamed in a register Marsh hadn't known the man possessed. But it lifted. Marsh hauled him clear, splinted the leg with a boat hook lashed to the shin using the dive knife's lanyard, and got him roped to the bollard where the current couldn't argue.

"You're going down there," Crane said. It wasn't a question. His voice had recovered some of its smoothness, or maybe Marsh was just too tired to hear the difference. "For your friends. Noble. But understand—if they found the logbook, it comes up with them. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement."

"There was no arrangement," Marsh said. "There was money, and there were people stupid enough to need it. I've been both."

He clipped on the spare tank—the dealer's team kept spares topside, because Crane planned for everything except conscience—and stood at the edge of the hatch. Below, the hold was a cathedral of dark. His torch beam cut a cone through water thick with suspended silt, and in that cone moved shapes: fallen beams, drifting sediment like snow falling upward, and the ghost-gray sweep of fish scattering from his light.

He found Voss first. The big man was dead—no drama to it, just a beam across his back and the stillness of someone who had simply stopped being present. Marsh checked anyway, pressing two fingers to the throat, feeling nothing but cold water. He'd done this before too. Some skills you never lose; that was their curse.

Then the torch caught movement deeper in, near the aft bulkhead where the hull curved down toward the riverbed, and Marsh's heart did something complicated in his chest.

Yara Eriksen was digging.

She had her hands in the silt itself, shoulder-deep in the riverbed's soft floor, hauling at something wedged between a fallen girder and the ship's own keel structure. Her tanks were low—he could read the gauge from six meters away, the needle in the red. Beside her, half-buried, was a long metal case, green with age, its latch plate stamped with faded lettering: a naval archive box. The logbook. The thing every death since the expedition began had orbited like debris around a sun.

She saw his light and froze. Even through the mask, even through forty years of bad sleep and worse decisions layered on both their faces, he could read her expression: *You shouldn't be here.*

He swam down and pointed upward. *Surface. Now.*

She shook her head and went back to digging.

So Marsh did the only reasonable thing left to him, which was the unreasonable thing. He put his hands in the mud beside hers and dug.

* * *

They freed the case together. It came loose all at once, the way buried things do—surrendering without warning—and the sudden release sent them both tumbling back into the clouded water. When it cleared, Yara had the case strapped across her chest with a dive strap Marsh hadn't seen her produce, and she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't name.

Her gauge needle trembled on empty.

He offered his octopus—the spare regulator—and she took it without ceremony, which told him everything about how far past argument they both were. They rose together through the hold's black levels, past Voss's body resting in the sediment like a man finally allowed to sleep, past the collapsed section where Crane had nearly joined him, up and up until the river spat them into grey daylight and rain.

Marsh got Yara onto the deck. She rolled to her side and breathed, great racking breaths, one arm still hooked through the case's strap like a mother refusing to put down a child.

"It's heavier than it looks," she managed, when she could talk.

"They usually are," Marsh said.

Crane had watched the whole ascent from his place against the bollard, and Marsh saw the calculation move behind the man's eyes like fish under ice. "Eriksen," Crane called, and his voice had found its showroom polish again, that warm antique-shop timbre. "You have it. You know what it is—you've known since the strongbox. Naval logs, signed orders, the commission's own handwriting ordering the scuttle and the silence. Do you understand what that paper is worth? Not money—I'm talking about *leverage*. Families. Governments. There are people alive today whose entire legitimacy rests on that voyage being a tragedy instead of a crime. They will pay anything. They will bury anyone. And you—" he gestured with his chin at the strap across her chest, "—you're going to swim that to shore and hand it to me, because if you don't, my people downstream will find Haven anyway, and they won't come with offers. They'll come with manifests."

The rain ticked against the water. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered like an old man disagreeing.

Yara sat up slowly. She looked at Crane for a long moment, and Marsh watched something settle in her face—the same settling he'd seen in soldiers the moment before they did the thing that would define them.

"You want to know what I found down there?" she said. "Under the logbook. Under the case. There's a layer of silt down there, Crane, half a meter thick, fine as flour, and it's been accumulating since before either of us was born. The river's been archiving this wreck the whole time. Every year, another few millimeters. Every decade, another layer closing over it." She unclipped the strap. "The river already decided what happens to this. I'm just agreeing with it."

"No," Crane said, and the polish cracked clean through. "No, you listen to me—"

But Yara had already slid the case off the deck's edge.

Marsh saw it go. He could have caught it—maybe. It was within reach for one long suspended second, green metal turning in the brown water, bubbles streaming from its seams. He saw his own hands twitch toward it and stop. He saw Crane see them stop.

The case sank. The river took it the way the river takes everything given to it—without malice, without mercy, without comment. Down through the grey light, down past the hatch mouth, down into the hold where the silt lay waiting like a bed turned down for the night. The last Marsh saw of it was a pale rectangle fading into brown, and then not even that.

The logbook of the Provision settled into the archive of the river, and the river closed its ledger.

"You stupid—" Crane lunged, or tried to; the splinted leg betrayed him and he crashed sideways against the bollard, screaming words that dissolved into the rain. "Forty years! Forty years my family has paid for that silence! Do you know what we inherited? Debts! Shame! A name that opens doors and closes them just as fast, because everyone suspects and no one can prove—and you give it to the *mud*—"

"Yes," Yara said quietly. "That's exactly what I did."

Marsh looked at her—at this woman he'd hired, doubted, feared, followed—and understood that he was looking at the only person on this river who had never lied about what she wanted. She'd wanted salvage money and a life bigger than salvage money, and she'd found a family in a place maps refused to record, and she had just chosen it over everything the world outside could offer. Including him. Including whatever redemption he'd imagined he might buy with good behavior.

Which meant there was only one currency he had left.

* * *

"Ines jammed your surface line an hour ago," Marsh said.

Crane stopped mid-rant. The rain filled the pause.

"Your dive tender downstream—the one with the backup radio and the satellite phone and the extraction schedule. Ines killed the antenna feed before we ever launched. Your people think you're still underwater. Nobody's coming." Marsh crouched so they were eye to eye, and he made sure his voice carried the weight of confession, because that's what it was. "And the reason I know that is because I'm the one who taught her where to cut. I stole Haven's radio three nights ago. I transmitted your base camp the approximate location of the settlement. Coordinates, landmarks, the works. Everything that's happened since—that's mine. The divers coming upriver. Voss dying down there. All of it. I bought this with other people's lives because I needed the mission to mean something, and I'm telling you now because it's the only thing I have left to give anyone."

The silence stretched. Rain drummed the flooded deck. Crane's face went through several weathers—disbelief, contempt, and then, worst of all, understanding, the look of a man recognizing a tool he'd used on others now turned on himself.

"You pathetic little soldier," Crane whispered. "You think confessing fixes it? My team is already moving. Even without the radio, they run schedules. If the tender doesn't report by tomorrow evening, they assume compromise and advance on the last known position—which is *here*, which is the wreck, which is a day's walk from Haven regardless of what your girlfriend did to my antennas."

"Then we lie to them," Marsh said.

Crane blinked. Water ran off his chin. "What?"

"We lie." Marsh pulled the waterproof chart case from inside his jacket—the one he'd taken from the tender, the one marked with the dealer's own operational notes. "Your fallback protocol. Compromised position, advance to secondary rally point, await instructions via emergency frequency. I know the protocol. I helped write half of it, back when I still believed protocols protected people." He spread the chart on the deck, weighting the corners with shell casings from his pocket. "So we send the emergency signal—from Ines's repaired set, from high ground, tonight. We report the wreck site compromised, the logbook destroyed, and the operation relocating north. North, Crane. Away from Haven. Toward the old survey station at Kettle Bend, which burned down nine years ago and sits forty kilometers in the wrong direction. Your people will spend three days marching through swamp to a ruin, and by the time they figure it out, the navy response you triggered will have come and gone and found nothing but a legend."

"And why," Crane said slowly, "would I cooperate with that?"

Marsh met his eyes. "Because your leg is broken, your best diver is dead, your radio's down, and the woman holding your lifeline just fed your family's last secret to a river. Because cooperation is the only version of this story where you float home." He paused. "And because I watched her drag you out of a collapsing hold when leaving you was the smarter play. She isn't the wilderness, Crane. The wilderness forgives nothing. She does. Don't make her regret the difference."

* * *

They signaled from the ridge above the wreck at dusk, Ines's fingers steady on the key while Marsh dictated the false traffic and Yara stood watch with the rifle she'd taken from Voss's tender, though the only things moving in the valley below were herons and rain.

*WRECK SITE COMPROMISED. CARGO DESTROYED. RELOCATING NORTH TO KETTLE BEND. AWAIT EXTRACTION AT SURVEY STATION. CRANE OK. OUT.*

The radio ate the words and gave back static, and somewhere downstream men would read them and turn north, away from terraced gardens and children learning morse code, away from a dock where an elder named Sanna was waiting with hot food and harder questions.

Ines lowered the key and looked at Marsh for a long time. She didn't forgive him—her eyes said that plainly—but she nodded once, the nod you give a man who has finally reported his true position, and turned back to the set to confirm the signal had carried.

Yara sat down beside Marsh on the wet granite. Below them, the river slid past the drowned ship without a ripple of acknowledgment, keeping its new secret the way it kept all the others.

"He'll talk eventually," she said. "When he's safe. He'll tell them everything."

"Probably," Marsh said. "But by then it'll be a story about a treasure that doesn't exist, told by a man with a broken leg and a dead diver and no proof of anything. Nobody builds an expedition on that." He watched the water. "My grandmother used to say the truth is heavy. That's why most people put it down."

"What do you call what you just did?"

Marsh thought about it. The rain was easing. Far to the south, faint as a memory, he could almost imagine engines—naval boats coming upriver to answer a distress call that had already been answered, hunting a secret that now lay under half a meter of river archive, wrapped in silt, held in the dark like a sleeping thing.

"Landfall," he said.

Yara looked at him, and for the first time since the expedition began, she smiled.

Then Ines's hand closed on Marsh's shoulder, hard, and turned him toward the southern sky—where, through a tear in the storm clouds, three sets of running lights were moving upriver against the current, and the nearest one had just begun to flash a recognition signal that neither of them knew how to answer.
