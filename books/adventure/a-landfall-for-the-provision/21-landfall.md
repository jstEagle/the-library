## 21. Landfall

The navy came at noon on the third day, and they came looking for a ghost.

Yara watched them from the ridge above the river bend, flat on her belly in the ferns beside Marsh, who had not spoken a full sentence since the hold. Below them the water ran high and brown with last night's rain, and on it rode two grey-hulled boats with white ensigns snapping stiff in the wind. Helicopters had come at dawn and gone again, their rotors beating the mist into rags. Now it was just the boats, and the men in them, and the long slow business of finding nothing.

"They'll dive the wreck," Marsh murmured. His voice was gravel that hadn't been walked on in years.

"They'll dive what's left of it." Yara kept her eyes on the water. "The forward hold came down two nights ago. You were there."

"I was there." He said it like a man confirming his own sin. "I still don't—"

"You do." She said it gently, because she meant to be gentle with him now; he had earned gentleness the hard way, by confessing in front of everyone with river water still running out of his boots. "You sent coordinates for the stern section. The dealer's divers went to the stern section. The logbook was never there. It was under the collapsed plates in the forward hold, and now the forward hold is a grave with no door."

Below, a launch lowered divers over the Provision's broken back. Even from this distance Yara could read the choreography of it—the marker buoy, the shot line, the careful descending figures in black. She knew that dance. She had danced it alone for six years, before any of this, when the wreck was hers and hers only and loneliness had seemed like the price of competence rather than the absence of something better.

She wondered if the navy divers felt the cold come up through their suits the way she always had. She wondered if they would find the strongbox empty where she'd left it, lid hanging open, silt already claiming the interior. Let them catalogue an empty box. Let them write their reports.

*Nothing here but a legend,* she thought, and found that the legend had her grandmother's face.

* * *

They stayed on the ridge until the light went long and gold, until the navy boats weighed anchor and turned downstream, their wakes unspooling behind them like thread pulled loose from a seam. The helicopters did not return. Somewhere below, Ines was doing whatever Ines did—she had spent the morning with the repaired radio, transmitting weather reports on frequencies the dealer's people used, cheerful and bland and utterly misleading: *heavy fog expected through the gorge, visibility nil, recommend holding position.* By the time anyone cross-checked her forecasts against the sky, the sky would have agreed with her anyway. The river made its own weather, and the river was on their side.

Marsh shifted beside her. "I keep waiting to feel clean," he said. "I thought telling you all would do it. It didn't."

"No," Yara said. "It doesn't work like that. It's more like diving. You don't feel clean when you surface. You just feel *less deep*."

He considered that. "How long does it take?"

"Ask me in a year." She stood, brushing loam from her knees. "Come on. Sanna will want to know. And there's a boat coming for us at dusk."

"What boat?"

"Ours," she said, and heard herself say it, and did not take it back.

* * *

The descent to Haven took two hours by trail, the path dropping through cedar shadow and then opening onto terraces—Teo's terraces, though he'd only drawn them in a notebook once; the community had been building them for eighty years. Yara had stopped being surprised by them around the fourth day and started being astonished instead, which was different, and better. Astonishment could live alongside familiarity. Surprise couldn't.

Haven smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and the green crush of gardens after rain. Smoke rose from chimneys set so carefully among the trees that from the river you would see nothing but forest breathing. Children ran the dock planks barefoot, shouting to one another in a dialect that was English bent toward old Norwegian and old naval cant, words her grandmother might have used, words Yara had heard only as fragments in a dead woman's letters.

Sanna met her at the head of the dock. The elder was small and straight-backed, with hands like braided rope and eyes the grey of deep water, and when she looked at Yara she looked at her the way no one had ever looked at her—as if Yara's arrival were a fact the world had been waiting to correct.

"The boats?" Sanna asked.

"Gone downriver. They found the stern section and an empty box and each other's blame. Ines's fog report gave them an excuse to stop looking." Yara paused. "It's done, Sanna. The logbook's under forty tons of hull plate. No one's getting it. Not the navy, not the dealer, not in a hundred years."

Sanna closed her eyes. For a moment she looked every one of her years, and then some beyond them—a woman setting down a weight carried so long her spine had grown around it. When she opened her eyes again they were bright and wet and she did not apologize for it.

"Eighty-one years," she said. "My grandfather jumped ship at nineteen. He used to say the Provision sank twice—once in the water, and once in the records. We've lived our whole lives between the two sinkings." She took Yara's hand. Her grip was dry and strong. "And now the second sinking is finished too. Rest, Eriksen. Both of them can rest."

*Eriksen.* Not *Yara*. The name went through her like warm water through cold.

* * *

That evening the community gathered at the long hall, and Yara understood, standing in the doorway with river damp still in her hair, that the gathering was for her.

She had not been told. There had been no announcement, no summons—only Teo appearing at her door with a lamp and saying, "Come, you'll want to see this," in the tone of a man showing someone a rare orchid. But the hall was full, every bench, every standing place along the walls, and the tables held food that had clearly taken days: smoked fish and dark bread, preserves in colors that had no names outside these valleys, a soup that tasted of the river itself, of silt and time and patience.

Ines sat near the front and patted the bench beside her. "Sit down, diver. You're making the door nervous."

Yara sat. Marsh sat on her other side, awkward as a man at a funeral for someone he'd wronged—which, in a way, he was—but the community had fed him first and without comment, and someone's grandmother had knitted him socks that fit, and he wore them now like penance and absolution at once.

Sanna rose. The hall quieted the way water quiets—not instantly, but completely.

"We have lived hidden for three generations," Sanna said, "because hiding was the price of living. Our grandparents chose the river over the order, the truth over the mission, and the mission was to erase themselves, so they erased themselves thoroughly. They were good sailors. They were very good at disappearing." A ripple of something—not quite laughter, but its cousin—moved through the room. "We have taught our children that the world downstream is a current that pulls you under. We have taught them well. And then this woman came up the river out of that current, carrying a key and a dead woman's name, and she had every reason to sell us—every reason, and we all know what reasons look like, we've read the histories—and instead she flooded the evidence and dove into a collapsing hold to drag out a man who betrayed her."

Every eye in the hall turned to Marsh. He stared at his bowl.

"She did not do it for us," Sanna went on. "She did not know us yet. That is what I want the children to understand. Kindness before kinship—that is the rarest kind. Kinship before kindness is just survival wearing a smile." She turned to Yara, and her voice changed, dropped into something older and quieter, the register of vows. "Yara Eriksen. Your grandmother was Astrid Eriksen, daughter of Henrik, boatswain's mate of the Provision, who stood on this shore in the spring of the sinking and helped cut the first timber. Your name is written in our book. It has been written there for eighty-one years, waiting. We never believed the line had ended. Rivers don't end. They wait."

She opened a ledger then—an actual book, bound in oiled leather gone soft as cloth, its pages the color of weak tea—and Yara saw the columns of names going back and back, generations of them, births and pairings and deaths recorded in a dozen different hands, and there, in faded iron-gall ink, in a sailor's blunt capitals:

*ASTRID ERIKSEN — LOST TO THE DOWNSTREAM, 1943 — MAY THE RIVER RETURN HER*

Beneath it, in fresher ink, in Sanna's hand, dated four days ago:

*RETURNED — BY WAY OF HER GRANDDAUGHTER.*

Yara had salvaged things her whole life. Brass and china, portholes and personal effects, other people's lives hauled dripping into daylight and sold by the kilo. She had never once salvaged anything that was hers. She had not known, until this moment, standing in a smoky hall full of strangers who were not strangers, that she had been diving for it all along—six years in black water, feeling along the ribs of a drowned ship, and what she'd really been searching for was a name in a book.

"I don't—" she started, and her voice cracked, and Ines put a steadying hand between her shoulder blades, and she started again. "I don't know how to be from somewhere."

"Nobody does," said an old man near the fire, and the hall laughed, warm and easy. "You learn. Like swimming. Badly at first."

"Then teach me badly," Yara said, "and I'll get better."

Sanna smiled, and it transformed her face entirely, made her young for one impossible second. "Then stand up," she said, "and let them see your face. They've been asking about you for days, and I'm tired of answering."

Yara stood. The hall rose with her—not in ceremony, but in the loose, shuffling way of people who wanted a better look, craning and smiling, children hoisted onto shoulders. Someone began a song, low and rolling, a working song by its rhythm, a hauling chantey bent soft by eighty years of indoor singing, and the room took it up, and the sound moved through Yara like current through a hull, finding every seam she'd thought was sealed.

She had surfaced from dives before. She knew the exact sensation: the gray-green light going gold, the pressure peeling away, the world reassembling itself above her in pieces—sky, boat, hands reaching down. This was that. This was exactly that. Except that this time, when she broke the surface, there were hands.

There had always been hands. She just hadn't known which direction to swim.

* * *

Later, much later, when the song had wound down and the lamps burned low and the children had been carried home asleep across shoulders, Yara walked out onto the dock alone. The river ran silver-black under a rising moon. Mist stood on the water in columns, and somewhere upstream an owl called, and the mountains held their snowline against the stars.

Ines found her there, as Yara had known she would. The radio operator settled onto the dock boards with a sigh, legs dangling over the black water, and handed over a tin cup of something hot and alcoholic and excellent.

"He'll stay, you know," Ines said. "Marsh. Give him a season. He'll be fixing their fences and teaching the kids close-order drill and pretending he hates it."

"People change?"

"People drown a little," Ines said. "It rearranges them." She sipped. "Teo's naming a new orchid after you. Something dramatic. *Provisionia eriksenii*, probably. Botanists have no humility."

"And you? Staying?"

Ines was quiet a moment. Downstream, far away, the world she'd left behind was carrying on—frequencies and deadlines and a dealer licking his wounds in some city office, discredited, half-drowned, telling a story no one would believe about a logbook guarded by ghosts. Let him tell it. Legends were cheaper than proof, and the river had all the proof now, pressed flat under forty tons of steel.

"I fixed their radio," Ines said at last. "Did you notice? Nobody asked me to. I just... couldn't stand hearing it silent." She laughed softly at herself. "Three days ago I transmitted a fake fog report to save a settlement that officially doesn't exist. I think that answers your question."

Yara leaned her shoulder against Ines's. Below them, beneath sixty feet of moving water, the Provision lay in the dark with her secrets finally, permanently kept, her crew's great-grandchildren singing chanteys on the shore she'd died trying to reach. Some wrecks were endings. This one had been a door, held shut for eighty-one years, and Yara had been the key without knowing it.

"Morse code lesson tomorrow," Ines said. "The kids want to learn SOS properly. Teo says you're a natural teacher."

"Teo says that about everyone he wants something from."

"True. So—will you?"

Yara watched the mist move on the water, watching her, patient as family.

"Yes," she said. "I'm home. I might as well be useful."

Far upriver, at the source, the water came cold and clear out of the mountain, beginning its long journey to the sea—and for the first time in her life, Yara Eriksen was not watching the current carry things away. She was standing where it began.
