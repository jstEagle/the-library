## 22. The River Remembers

The water above the Provision was green-gold in the morning light, the color of old bottle glass, and Yara hung in it weightless while four young divers fanned out below her like startled fish.

"Slow," she said into her regulator, though they couldn't hear her yet. She'd teach them the hand signals again on the surface, and again tomorrow, and again until the signals lived in their hands the way breathing did. That was how you learned the river's language—not by memorizing it but by forgetting you'd ever spoken anything else.

Below her, the wreck of the Provision lay along the shelf where it had lain for sixty years, its hull furred with weed, its superstructure collapsed into itself like a sleeping animal curled against the cold. The navy had come twice since that last terrible spring—once with sonar sleds and once with men in civilian clothes who asked careful questions—and both times they had found what Sanna promised they would find: a legend wearing a shipwreck's bones. The logbook was silt now. The strongbox was an empty iron shell that the crayfish had claimed for a palace. The hold where a man had nearly died in the dark was just a hold, black and quiet, holding nothing but water and time.

Yara had made sure of it herself, in those first weeks, diving alone with a pry bar and a conscience. She had opened every sealed compartment, scattered every document worth scattering, let the current take the paper into the mud where no lens would ever read it again. It had felt like drowning something. It had also felt like setting it free.

Now she kicked down through the cold layer, and the children followed.

There were six of them this season—the oldest was Kess, sixteen, broad-shouldered and impatient, already better at reading current than Yara had been at twenty-five. The youngest was little Anni, eleven, who had cried the first time she put her face in the river and who now swam the flooded corridors like an otter, her braids trailing behind her in a dark rope. They were Haven's children: grandchildren of grandchildren of men and women who had jumped a dying ship rather than carry out its orders, who had built terraced gardens up the valley walls and raised families on fish and potatoes and the fierce, unspoken agreement that they had never existed.

Yara touched the hull near the breach she knew best—a tear in the plating just wide enough for two swimmers abreast—and waited for them to gather. When their masked faces ringed her in the green half-light, she began the lesson.

*This way,* she signed. *Always this way. Never the forward hold.*

Anni signed back the question Yara expected, because Anni asked it every time, the way children ask the same story to be told again: *Why?*

*Because the river changes,* Yara signed. *And the forward hold forgets.*

It was true, or true enough for teaching. The collapse three years ago had shifted the sediment; the passage that had been safe when Yara first dove the wreck as a hungry, broke salvage diver scraping copper off a dead ship was now a throat of loose silt that could swallow a diver without a burp. The river remembered everything, but it remembered by changing. That was the first thing Sanna had taught her, standing on the dock in the rain with her grandmother's name in her mouth like a stone she didn't know whether to swallow or spit out: *You don't learn the river once. You court it every day, or it stops recognizing you.*

They swam the safe passages together—the long gallery where the light came down in shafts through a split in the deck plates, the engine room where a school of silver minnows turned as one animal, the captain's cabin where nothing remained but a rusted frame of a bunk and, wedged in the wall seam, the small brass plaque Yara had left there on purpose. *PROVISION.* She could have pried it out and sold it in any port town for a month's food. Instead she let it stay, green with patina, a headstone for a ship that had never really died here at all. The real grave of the Provision was upstream, in the valley, in the gardens, in the children kicking beside her now.

Kess tapped her arm and pointed into the dark of the aft corridor. Yara followed his finger and saw it: a fresh snag, a tree limb carried down in the spring floods, wedged across the passage like a gate bar. New danger, unmarked on any map, invisible to anyone who hadn't been coming here long enough to know what shouldn't be.

She signed *good eyes*, and saw the boy flush pink even through the water, even through the mask, and thought: *this*. This was the thing she had been diving for all those years alone off the patrol routes, hauling up other people's history to sell to strangers. Not the metal. Not the money. This—the moment when your knowledge kept someone alive, when the river's memory passed through you into younger hands. She had called it competence, back then. Lonely competence, the kind you wore like a drysuit against the cold. It had taken her thirty years and one capsized boat to learn its real name.

Belonging.

*Surface,* she signed. *Lunch. And Kess—you'll tell them about the snag. Your find, your report.*

His grin, underwater, was a white flash in the green.

* * *

The radio hut sat on the high bank above the dock, a plank building Ines had built herself with help from anyone she could shame into holding a beam straight. From outside it looked like nothing—a shed, a smokehouse, a place for nets. Inside it was a cathedral of wire.

Ines had the headphones on when Yara came up the path, drying her hair in the sun, and she held up one finger: *wait*. Her lips moved silently, transcribing. On the desk beside her elbow, the set she'd rebuilt from Marsh's wreckage glowed with a soft amber patience. Three years of scavenged parts and smuggled vacuum tubes and one heroic negotiation with a supply runner from the coast, and the radio sang every evening now: weather reports, shipping bulletins, the flat cheerful voices of stations far downstream, telling the world to the world.

"—low pressure moving up the valley," Ines murmured, writing. "Expect rain by tomorrow night, heavy in the upper reaches. Fishermen advised—" She looked up, pushed the headphones down around her neck, and smiled the crooked smile Yara had first seen across a campfire on the worst night of the expedition, when they'd both still believed Teo might walk out of the trees. "Storm coming. Two days out. You'll want the young ones out of the water by tomorrow noon."

"I figured. The light went strange over the ridge."

"The light always goes strange before I hear it on the band." Ines tapped the radio affectionately. "I trust this more than the sky. The sky lies. This just repeats what other people's skies told them."

Teo was there too, of course—he was always there, drawn to the radio hut the way he was drawn to any room where information gathered. He sat in the corner with his notebooks spread across three crates, cataloguing. His hair had gone fully grey now, and he'd stopped apologizing for talking too much, which everyone agreed was an improvement. On the table lay the book he was making: drawings of plants no outsider had ever named, pressed specimens, notes in his small precise hand. Sanna said that when he finished, the community would decide whether it stayed in Haven or went out into the world under a false name and a false map reference. Teo said he didn't care anymore which. "A name is a name," he'd told Yara once, "whether the world knows where it grew or not." He had come back from the dead—or rather, he had never been dead at all, only taken home early—and whatever the watchers' warning shots had shaken loose in him on that portage, the valley had put it back together.

"Anything from downstream?" Yara asked, and heard how casually she made it sound.

Ines understood anyway. She always did. "Coast station, this morning. Routine traffic." A pause. "Nothing with our name on it. Nothing with any name on it."

Our name. There wasn't one, officially. That was the whole architecture of the peace: no name, no coordinates, no mark on any chart. The maps stayed unmarked. But inside the valley, names were everywhere—Sanna's council names, the garden rows named for the original crew, the diving spots named by the children in a language that was half the old country and half pure river invention. The deep pool below the falls was Eriksen Hole now. Yara had protested for a full season and then given up, and then, one morning, catching herself using the term, had stopped protesting in her heart too.

"Marsh sent a letter," Ines said, quieter. "Through the coast runner. It came yesterday—I meant to give it to you at supper."

She handed it over. The envelope was soft from travel, postmarked from a town Yara had never visited, addressed in a hand that had gotten steadier over the years but would never be what it was before the river broke him open.

Yara sat on the step of the radio hut and opened it in the sun.

*Yara—*

*Third autumn here. The leg aches when it rains, which the weather reports say it will, so thank Ines for the forecast and tell her she's still the only honest voice on the air. I'm teaching now. Can you believe it. Navigation and field craft, boys mostly, some girls tougher than the boys. I tell them about rivers. I tell them a river is not your enemy and not your friend, it is older than both words, and if you approach it wanting to conquer it will drown you, and if you approach it wanting to hide in it will show you every one of your own lies.*

*I don't tell them about you. Not because I'm ashamed. Because they wouldn't understand, and neither do I, most days, and that's the best thing I've learned: you can be grateful for something you still don't understand.*

*Tell Sanna the seed potatoes came through. Tell Teo I found the moss he described, third drainage west, exactly where his drawing said. Tell the kids at the dock that somewhere south a broken old soldier eats fish he caught himself and sleeps through the night, and that they did that. Whether they know it or not.*

*—M*

Yara read it twice. The second time her eyes blurred, and she blamed the sun off the water.

Three years ago, Marsh had stolen a skiff and a repaired radio and transmitted Haven's approximate location to a man who trafficked in other people's erasures. Three years ago, in the black hold of the Provision, Yara had dragged that man's employer out of a collapsing bulkhead instead of leaving him to the dark he'd earned. And three years ago, on the dock at dawn, Marsh had stood with river water still dripping off him and confessed everything—his transmission, his shame, his need for the mission to mean something, anything, after all the missions that had meant nothing—and then he had picked up the jammed radio handset and misdirected the incoming navy response with a liar's calm that probably saved forty lives, theirs included.

He had gone downstream after that. Voluntarily. "I can't live on forgiveness," he'd said, shaking Yara's hand hard enough to hurt. "But I can live toward it." Every year, a letter. Every year, a little closer to the shore of it.

"You're crying," Ines observed, not unkindly.

"It's the wind."

"There's no wind."

"There's going to be," Yara said, waving the letter toward the ridge, toward the bruising sky. "Two days. Storm."

Ines laughed, and turned back to her amber-lit cathedral of wire, and keyed the mic to pass the weather up-valley to the garden crews, her voice steady and clear, carrying nothing but rain.

* * *

That evening the whole valley smelled of woodsmoke and roasting fish, and the community gathered on the dock the way it gathered most evenings—not by rule but by gravity, the way water finds low ground.

Sanna was there, older now, her hands knotted around a cup of tea she wouldn't drink until it cooled, which meant it never got drunk. She was telling the young ones the story again—the story of the Provision, the true version, the one that existed nowhere on paper anymore except in the bodies of the people listening. How the ship had carried orders to scuttle the evidence of a massacre. How the crew had refused. How they had faked the sinking, run her aground on the shelf in the night, and walked into the wilderness carrying nothing but each other. How the officers' descendants had spent generations looking for proof to destroy, and how the proof had finally been destroyed anyway—not by them, but by a salvage diver with a pry bar and a choice.

"And then," Sanna said, in the cadence that meant the ending was coming, "the river brought us a diver. And the diver brought us a key. And the key opened nothing in the end but us."

The children groaned happily at the old joke. Yara, mending a net by the fire, pretended not to listen, the way she always did, the way Sanna always made sure to tell it loud enough for her to hear.

Later, when the fire had burned to coals and the children were asleep in a pile like puppies in the big house, Yara walked out onto the dock alone. The storm was still two days off but you could feel it gathering upstream, a pressure behind the ears of the world. The river ran black and patient under the boards. Somewhere below, sixty feet down, the Provision held its silence, its brass plaque, its school of minnows turning like a single thought.

Her grandmother had stood on a deck like that once—no, on this very deck, or what remained of it—and chosen the wilderness over the order, the living secret over the clean lie. Yara had spent the first half of her life diving wrecks for what could be sold. She had spent the second half learning that the most valuable thing in any drowned hull was the reason it sank.

Footsteps on the dock. Kess, wrapped in a blanket, fifteen-going-on-sixteen, unable to sleep the way you can't sleep before a dive day.

"Teacher," he said—the title still new, still slightly ridiculous to them both. "Tomorrow. After the shallow drills. Could you show me the deep corridor? Just to look. Anni doesn't have to come."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. He had good eyes. He'd found the snag. And the deep corridor was the one passage she had never shown anyone, the one that led to the compartment where, on her very first dive years ago, broke and hungry and alone, she had found the strongbox that started everything.

Some things you keep sealed. Some things you pass on.

"Tomorrow," she said. "After drills. Bring a spare light."

He grinned and padded back up the path. Yara stayed on the dock a while longer, watching the black water slide past, carrying leaves and rain-omens and the memory of everything it had ever held down to a sea that kept no records either.

Some maps stay unmarked, she thought. Some families are chosen by rivers.

And some doors, once opened underwater, stay open forever—waiting, patient as silt, for the next pair of hands brave enough to knock.
