## 1. Weather Coming Down

The ridgelines had been eating clouds all morning, the way they do before a monsoon decides to commit. I watched them from six thousand feet, one hand loose on the yoke of the old de Havilland, and told myself the same lie I tell every June: *it'll hold. It always holds.*

It doesn't always hold. That's the thing about the Rivergate. The river doesn't care what you've promised yourself.

"Rivergate strip, Otter Seven-Three-Bravo, inbound from the coast," I said into the radio, though nobody down there had answered a radio call in three years except Ruiz, and Ruiz only when he felt like it. The valley gave back static and the sound of my own breathing, which was fair enough. Ten years of flying medicine into the interior teaches you that most conversations are optional.

Below me the lower river ran brown and swollen already, fat with early melt, chewing at its banks like a dog working a bone it means to bury somewhere you'll never find it. Drowned willow stood in the shallows with only their crowns showing, looking like drowned men who'd given up struggling. I'd learned to read that water the way other people read weather, and what it said today was: *I'm just getting started.*

The strip at Rivergate is a gravel bar with ambitions—twelve hundred feet if the river's feeling generous, nine hundred if it isn't. Today it was feeling generous. I flared into the wind, felt the big tires kiss gravel, and rolled out past the fuel drums with room to spare. Small mercies. You learn to bank them.

Ruiz was waiting by the shed, which meant he'd heard me coming twenty minutes ago over two ridges and had taken his time walking out, because Ruiz believes punctuality is a form of anxiety and he refuses to participate.

"You're late," he said, as I shut down.

"I'm early. Everyone else is late."

He grunted, which from Ruiz is a full paragraph. He took the crate of penicillin off my cargo floor like it weighed nothing, which it didn't, and nodded upriver without looking. "Doc's got three kids with fever in Kettle Bend. And—" he paused, and I knew there was an *and* before he said it, because Ruiz's pauses are load-bearing, "—there's a telegram for you."

"A telegram." I laughed. "Who sends telegrams anymore?"

"Somebody with money," Ruiz said, and handed it over.

* * *

I read it twice, standing in the shade of the shed while Ruiz carried on toward the doc's, and the second time I read it slower, the way you reread a diagnosis you're hoping says something different than it did the first time.

**GALLOWAY STOP OFFER STOP RUN LOWER RIVER RIVERGATE TO CONFLUENCE MONSOON SEASON STOP CREW AND BOAT PROVIDED OR YOURS YOUR CHOICE STOP FIVE TIMES GOING RATE STOP REPLY BY THURSDAY STOP KESSLER**

Maren Kessler. Five times going rate. For a run down the lower river during monsoon season, when the lower river stops being a river and becomes a rumor with a current.

Nobody runs the lower river in monsoon. Not because it can't be done—almost nothing can't be done, that's the trap of this country—but because the ones who tried are mostly names on the memorial board in the Rivergate lodge, and the ones who didn't try have the good sense their mothers gave them. The water comes down off the glacier warm with rock flour and cold with attitude, and it takes boats the way the glacier takes valleys: slowly, then all at once.

So why would Maren Kessler pay five times rate for a suicide run?

Because she wasn't paying for the run. She was paying for me.

I folded the telegram along its creases, which is a habit from a life I don't talk about, and walked up to the lodge to get the gossip Ruiz had promised, because gossip in Rivergate travels faster than the flood crest and tells you more.

* * *

The lodge smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular staleness of a place where men argue about fish. Half the valley was crammed inside, sheltering from a rain that hadn't started yet but was clearly being negotiated. Doc Halloran was there, and the ferry widow Okafor, and a couple of survey boys who looked like they'd gotten lost on their way to a softer life.

"Galloway," Doc said, lifting his chin. "You bring the penicillin?"

"And the gossip. Which do you want first?"

"Gossip. The penicillin keeps."

They gave it to me in pieces, the way they always do, testing whether I'd earned each one. The ice bridge on the upper fork had gone out early—the first time in anyone's memory—which meant the glacier route might open ahead of schedule, which meant the window I fly every year might come early and leave early, like a guest who takes the silverware. There was a new man asking after deck work, some drifter named Tomas with river hands and no history, which in Rivergate either means a criminal or a monk, and the town was taking bets. And—

"Maren Kessler's been here," Okafor said, watching me over her tea. "Twice this month."

"Buying?"

"Asking." A pause. "About the lower river. About who still runs it."

"And who still runs it?"

Okafor smiled the smile of a woman delivering a bill. "You do, Gideon. Last one left. She asked after your rates. She asked after your crew. She asked—" and here she let the pause stretch until it creaked, "—after your father."

The lodge went quiet in that way rooms do when everyone's pretending they weren't listening.

"My father," I said, "has been dead fifteen years."

"Mm," said Okafor, who had lived here long enough to know that dead is a legal status, not a conversational one.

I finished my tea. I thanked Doc for the fever list. I walked back down to the strip through a rain that had finally made up its mind, and the whole time the ridgelines behind the valley were going gray, cloud stacking on cloud like a debt compounding, and I thought: *she knows about the wreck. Or she thinks she does. And she's using five thousand dollars as a hook, and the hook is shaped exactly like the thing I swore I'd never go back for.*

* * *

That evening I sat in the shed with the telegram open on my knee and the storm building outside, thunder rolling down the valley like furniture being moved upstairs. Ruiz came in, wiped his hands on a rag that had outlived several engines, and looked at the paper without reading it. He didn't need to. He'd seen me read it.

"You're not going," he said.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't say anything. That's worse." He sat down across from me, slow, joints complaining. "Gideon. Fifteen years you've kept this plane small and this name smaller. You fly medicine. You don't fly questions. That woman offers you money nobody sane would take, and you're sitting there folding that telegram like it's a map."

"It's not a map."

"No. But she wants you to think it is." He leaned forward. "Whatever's down in that lower river, whatever your father's Otter has been doing under the water all these years—it stays down there or it comes up, and either way it changes things. You know that. I know that. Does she?"

"She wouldn't be paying five times rate if she didn't think it was worth ten."

Ruiz nodded slowly, and outside the first real rain hit the tin roof like applause from an audience that wanted something from us.

"So what are you going to tell her?" he asked.

I looked at the telegram. At the word **STOP**, four times, like the sender couldn't resist the joke. At the name **KESSLER**, sitting at the bottom like a stone dropped in a well.

I thought about the night the river took my father's plane, fifteen years ago, and how I'd spent every year since keeping my wake small and my history smaller, ferrying penicillin to people who needed neither my story nor my silence. I thought about the ice window opening early this year, earlier than anyone could remember, as if the country itself had decided to stop keeping secrets.

"I'm going to tell her no," I said.

Ruiz raised an eyebrow, which for him was a standing ovation.

"And then," I said, "I'm going to figure out why saying no feels exactly like falling for it."

Thunder answered from upvalley, close now, and the rain came down harder, and somewhere south of us Maren Kessler was counting days until Thursday, patient as water.
