## 2. The Sworn-Off Water

I read the telegram four times before I let myself understand it, which is a personal record for me and bad news generally. I'm not a slow reader. I'm just very good at pretending certain sentences don't apply to me.

*GALLOWAY — REQUIRE PILOT/RUNNER FOR LOWER RIVER PASSAGE MONSOON SEASON. FLOODED CHANNELS, SALVAGE SURVEY. TRIPLE STANDARD RATE. KESSLER.*

Triple standard rate. Nobody pays triple standard rate for a salvage survey on the lower river, because nobody sane runs the lower river in monsoon season. The lower river in monsoon season is what happens when you give a mountain range permission to spit at the sea. The water comes down brown and muscular, carrying whole trees like toothpicks, and it does not care about your boat, your schedule, or your opinion of yourself.

Maren Kessler knew that better than anyone. She'd grown up on this river the same as I had, back when we were two girls racing tin boats off the Rivergate dock and she always won because she cheated first and apologized never. She was the only person in the valley with enough money to offer triple rate and enough sense to know exactly what that number would do to me.

It wasn't an offer. It was a hook, and she'd tied it herself, and we both knew the fly.

I folded the telegram into quarters and put it in my shirt pocket, over my heart, where I keep things I intend to hate later. Then I went out to check the plane, because fueling and fussing is what I do instead of thinking.

The De Havilland sat on the gravel strip looking patient, which is more than I deserved. Ten years I'd kept her small—small runs, small loads, small life. Medicine upriver, mail downriver, a passenger now and then if they didn't ask questions. The valley had mostly stopped staring at me by year five. By year eight they'd stopped whispering too, or at least gotten polite about it. That's the deal exile offers you: quiet, in exchange for everything else.

Fifteen years ago tonight—or close enough to it; the anniversary moves around like a drunk—the lower river took my father's plane, and my father, and whatever version of me might have grown up unafraid of water.

* * *

That night it rained the way it only rains here, like the sky has a grudge and a budget. I was fourteen. My father came into the hangar soaked through, moving fast, and he didn't look at me right. He looked at me the way you look at someone you're memorizing.

"Gid," he said. "Lock up after me."

"Where are you going?"

"Downriver." He was loading crates into the old Norseman, and he was loading them wrong, fast and careless, and my father was never careless with a load. "Kessler's run. Contract freight."

"You hate contract freight."

"I hate a lot of things tonight." He zipped the cargo net and stopped, one hand flat on the crate, and for a second I thought he was going to say something true. His mouth opened. Closed. What came out was: "There's chili in the icebox. Don't wait up."

He taxied out onto the black water with his running lights off, which I told myself was nothing, because pilots did odd things in weather, because he'd done a hundred night runs, because I was fourteen and the alternative to believing him was believing something else.

The storm ate the sound of his engine inside a minute. I locked the hangar. I heated chili I didn't eat. At some point I fell asleep in the office chair with my boots on.

They found the floating debris two days later, downstream of the gorge—a wheel fairing, a seat cushion, a crate stenciled with a medical supplier's name that everyone politely ignored once the manifest turned out to list nothing but bandages and saline. No wreckage. No body. The river keeps what it takes; that's the arrangement. The valley held a memorial without a grave, and afterward people started looking at me differently, the daughter of the man who'd been running something down the lower river with his lights off, and no one would say what, and eventually I stopped asking, and eventually I left, and eventually I came back smaller.

The wreck was never found. Officially.

Unofficially, every pilot on this coast knows the glacier route opens a few weeks a summer, and every pilot who's ever flown the lower gorge in that window has looked down at the meltwater pools against the ice face and wondered. You can't help it. The water there is so clear and so cold it looks like glass laid over a secret.

I spent ten years not wondering. It's the hardest work I've ever done.

* * *

Kessler found me at the dock at noon, which meant she'd timed her arrival to the mail boat, which meant theater. Maren Kessler doesn't enter a scene; she schedules it.

She looked good, damn her. Silver at the temples worn like a decoration, a field jacket worth more than my annual fuel bill, and the same quick predator's smile she'd had at nine years old when she cut my line in the rowing race and called it tactics.

"Gideon." She said my name like she was returning something I'd lent her. "You got my wire."

"I got your wire."

"And?"

"And I'm busy."

"It's monsoon season, Gideon. Nobody's busy. Everybody's boarded up drinking tea and complaining about their roofs." She stepped onto the dock beside me, unhurried, watching me coil a line I didn't need to coil. "Triple rate. Flooded channels, salvage survey. It's honest work."

"Nothing about the lower river in August is honest work."

"You used to love that river."

"I used to be fourteen." I hung the line. "Find another runner."

"There isn't one. That's rather the point." She said it lightly, but I heard the weight under it, the way you hear rock under shallow water. "No one else will fly it. No one else can. And no one else—" she paused, delicately, a surgeon choosing her instrument, "—has reason to want to see what's down there."

The dock boards creaked under both our weights and neither of us moved.

"Don't," I said.

"Don't what? I haven't said anything yet."

"You're circling."

"I'm being paid to circle." She leaned against a piling, arms crossed, entirely comfortable, which was its own kind of threat. Maren comfortable is worse than Maren angry. Angry, she makes mistakes. Comfortable means she's already won three moves ago and is enjoying the walk. "My client wants a survey of the lower channel below the gorge. Specific reach. Specific coordinates."

"What client wants a flooded sandbar surveyed in monsoon season?"

"The kind that pays triple rate and doesn't sign paperwork." She smiled. "You remember how the valley works, Gideon. Everything aboveboard floats. Everything real sinks."

I picked up my toolbag. It was empty. We both pretended otherwise.

"Refused," I said. "Tell your client."

"Mm." She didn't move. "Here's my problem, Gideon. If you refuse, I have to believe you're refusing. And I've known you since we were children stealing boats, and I know you don't refuse things. You defer them. There's a difference. Refusing means it's finished. Deferring means you're waiting for a better price."

"That's you."

"Is it?" Something flickered behind the smile—not kindness, Maren doesn't stock kindness, but recognition. Two girls from the same dock, one of whom swam away and one of whom sank. "Fine. Let's skip to the part where we're honest, since honesty's clearly painful for both of us and I like to get pain over with early."

She reached into her jacket and took out a photograph, creased once, and held it out.

I should not have taken it. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I am a grown woman with ten years of discipline and a floatplane I maintain like a religion, and I took the photograph.

Aerial shot. The glacier face, calved and raw, blue ice over black water. And there at the base, half-shadowed in the melt pool, a shape. Wing spar. Straight as a sentence. The pale geometry of an airframe that has been underwater long enough to become part of the landscape.

The Norseman's tail number was still legible. Someone had circled it in red grease pencil.

"Fifteen years," Kessler said quietly. "Ice window opens in three weeks. Closes in five days after that. My client believes there's recoverable material in that wreck. Cargo-grade material. And my client believes—you'll forgive them—that the person best positioned to find it is the person who's been flying medicine past that glacier twice a season for a decade and never once looked down."

"I look down," I said. My voice came out wrong. Flat. "Everyone looks down."

"But you know what you're looking for." She took the photograph back, gently, the way you take a knife off a child. "Triple rate, Gideon. Or I hire outside crews, and they're clumsy, and they'll tear that wreck apart looking, and whatever's in it ends up on a barge south while you're here reading about it in the paper. Those are your options. Help me do it cleanly, or watch it done badly."

The river said nothing. The river never says anything; that's its whole trick. It just holds what it holds and waits for you to come to it.

Three weeks. Fifteen years of silence, and she'd priced it at triple rate and a photograph, and the worst part—the part I'll be honest about even now—is that some cold practical corner of me had already started calculating fuel, tide windows, hull drafts. Some part of me had wanted to know since I was fourteen years old sitting in an office chair with uneaten chili going cold, and Maren Kessler had known that, because she's known me since before either of us knew ourselves, and she cheats first and apologizes never.

"I need to provision a crew," I heard myself say.

Something satisfied moved through her face and was gone. "Wire me your terms. I'll meet any reasonable figure."

"And Maren." I made myself look at her. "If I find out your client had anything to do with what my father was carrying—"

"Then you'll have your answer at last," she said, almost kindly. "Isn't that worth the trip?"

She walked back up the dock toward the mail boat, silver head high, and the whole valley watched her go and pretended not to, the way valleys do.

I stood on the planks a long time with the river pulling past underneath, brown and swollen and patient, already rising with the rain that hadn't fallen yet but would. Somewhere down that drowned channel, under forty feet of meltwater and fifteen years of my own careful not-looking, my father's plane sat with its cargo net still zipped and its secrets still sealed, and in three weeks the ice would open like a door, and I had just agreed to walk through it.

I went and bought rope. More than I needed.

Some part of me already knew I'd need every foot.
