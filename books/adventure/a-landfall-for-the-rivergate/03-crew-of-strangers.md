## 3. Crew of Strangers

The trouble with needing money is that it makes you agreeable. I'd spent thirty-one years cultivating a healthy suspicion of the world—grew up on a river that taught me early that anything floating toward you is either driftwood or something worse—and yet there I was at six in the morning on the Rivergate dock, shaking hands with a woman who paid cash and asked no questions, because my sister's hospital bill had eaten through everything I owned except my boots and my distrust.

The boots were nearly gone too.

"You're Tomas Reyes," she said. Not a question either. Maren Kessler had a way of speaking that made statements out of things you hadn't confirmed yet. She wore a field jacket worth more than my truck and looked at me the way surveyors look at land: measuring what could be extracted.

"I'm somebody's cousin of that name," I said. "Depends who's asking and why."

"Deckhand. Six weeks. Hazard pay." She named a number that would have covered the bill twice over, and I felt my suspicion do that thing it does when money walks past it—it straightened up, smoothed its shirt, pretended to be reasonable.

"Six weeks doing what?"

"Ferrying cargo downriver to the lower settlements before the monsoon closes the channel. Nobody local will run it this season. The water's wrong."

"The water's always wrong," I said. "That's what makes it water."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more like an accountant finding a column that balanced. She handed me a paper with a dock number and a time. "Boat's called the *Vespera*. Captain Galloway."

I knew the name before she said it. Everyone in the valley knew it, the way everyone knows the name of the man who fell off the church roof. Galloway. Fifteen years since the plane went into the lower river, fifteen years since the daughter packed her father's silence and flew away from a town that had already decided what happened. And now here was Kessler hiring me onto a boat captained by the ghost herself.

I should have walked. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. I stood there on the wet planks with Kessler's paper in my hand and every instinct I owned telling me the river was about to hand me something worse than driftwood, and I took the job anyway, because my sister's lungs didn't care about my instincts.

That's the thing about debts to the water. They don't drown you all at once. They just keep you treading until you're too tired to ask where you're swimming.

* * *

The *Vespera* turned out to be a river freighter gone gray at the temples, forty feet of riveted stubbornness with a shallow draft and a wheelhouse like a tin hat. She sat low in the water already, loaded, and the smell coming off her hold was diesel and canvas and something faintly antiseptic, like a clinic that had been in a fight.

Ruiz found me before I found him. He materialized out of the engine hatch the way old mechanics do, as if they've been inside the boat so long they've become part of its plumbing. He was sixty-something, built like a knuckle, with forearms that suggested a lifetime of persuading machinery to behave against its will.

"You the new hands?" he said, wiping them on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last decade.

"Tomas."

"Ruiz. Rules are simple. Don't touch what isn't yours, don't stand where the lines can take you overboard, and if the captain says jump, you ask how high only after you're already airborne." He said it without malice, the way a man recites a catechism he genuinely believes. "She's the best pilot on this river and the second-best mechanic, and the only reason she's second is she doesn't argue with engines, she just stares at them until they feel bad."

"Does that work?"

"On small engines. On big ones you need me." He jerked his chin at the deck. "Cargo went aboard yesterday. You'll be checking lashings twice a day. Water gets rough, crates walk."

I walked the deck while he talked, learning the boat the way you learn any animal—where she creaked, where she bit. The crates were stenciled with a medical supply company's name, RED CROSS-adjacent lettering, the kind of branding designed to make customs officers sleepy. Medical crates for river settlements. It made sense. It also made sense that nobody local would run them during monsoon, which meant hazard pay, which meant Kessler's number, which meant I was here.

Everything fit. That was the first thing that bothered me. In my experience, jobs that fit are like rivers that look calm—the danger is underneath, moving faster than the surface admits.

Priya came up the gangplank at seven sharp, carrying a duffel and a clipboard and the expression of someone who has already found three things wrong with your morning. Mid-twenties, quick-eyed, hair tied back like she considered loose hair a structural hazard. She looked at me exactly once, top to bottom, and I understood I had been filed somewhere.

"New deckhand?" she said.

"Apparently."

"Can you swim?"

"Better than I can float."

"Good answer. Most people lie." She clipped the clipboard to her belt and started checking lashings without waiting for me, but she slowed enough that I could fall in beside her, which I took as a kind of provisional acceptance. "Ratchet straps on the port side are chafing. Ruiz knows. Tell him again anyway, he likes being told things twice, it confirms the universe is still running."

I liked her immediately, in the wary way you like a knife that's been handed to you handle-first.

It was Priya who pointed out the captain, though I'd have known her anyway. Gideon Galloway stood at the wheelhouse rail with a mug of coffee going cold in one hand, watching the river come down from the north like it owed her an explanation. She was maybe forty, lean in the way of people who burn everything they eat just standing still, with gray starting at her temples and eyes that did most of their work at a distance. She didn't greet me. She looked at me for perhaps three seconds—long enough to inventory everything I carried, including the parts I hadn't unpacked yet—and then nodded once, the nod you give weather.

"Tomas," she said. My name in her mouth sounded pre-approved, like she'd read it off a list before I arrived. Which, I'd learn later, she probably had.

"Captain."

"Ruiz will show you the bilge pumps. If we ship water, you're on the hand pump and you don't stop until someone tells you to stop. Even then, keep pumping until you're sure."

"Sure of what?"

"That they weren't lying to make you stop." She drank the cold coffee, grimaced at it like an old acquaintance, and went inside.

* * *

We cast off at nine, and by ten I had learned three things: the *Vespera* handled like a barge with opinions; Ruiz kept the engine tuned so sweetly it was almost rude; and Priya counted everything. Crates, straps, fuel drums, minutes. She counted the way anxious people pray—continuously, and with the belief that attention itself might hold disaster off.

It was around noon, running the wide brown reach below the gorge mouth, that I saw the fourth thing.

I'd gone forward to re-tension the port lashings, and I passed the wheelhouse door, which stood open to the heat. Inside, bent over the chart table, was Priya. Except Priya was supposed to be aft, checking the fuel drums—I'd left her there not two minutes earlier, her voice still hanging in the air behind me, complaining about a bung. So the woman at the chart table, hunched over the cargo manifest with her phone held flat above the page, was either Priya or a coincidence wearing her jacket.

The shutter sound was very quiet. Twice. Three times.

Then she heard my boot on the deck plate and turned, and it was Priya after all, phone already face-down against her thigh, smooth as a card trick, her expression rearranged into mild professional boredom so fast I almost doubted what I'd seen.

"Straps okay?" she asked.

"Port side's tight now." I looked at her. She looked back with those quick counting eyes, and I realized she was counting me now—weighing whether I'd seen, calculating what I'd cost or earned. "You get the drums done?"

"Half. Finishing after lunch." She stepped past me out of the wheelhouse, close enough that I caught the smell of diesel and some cheap citrus soap, and paused. "You're staring, Tomas."

"Just admiring the paperwork. I hear manifests are fascinating reading."

"Only if you're the sort who reads other people's mail." She smiled when she said it, a real smile, easy and warm, and that bothered me more than the photographing had. A guilty person looks guilty. A person with a plan looks friendly.

I finished the lashings and thought about it through the afternoon while the river carried us down past drowned fence posts and the roofs of villages the water had swallowed decades ago and never given back. Here's what I knew: Kessler hired fast and paid cash. The manifest was medical crates, but medical crates are boxes, and boxes are honest only until you open them. Priya photographed pages she could have read anytime—unless she needed them verbatim, for someone who wasn't aboard. And Gideon Galloway had taken a job from Maren Kessler, of all the employers on earth, to run a river she'd sworn off, hauling cargo that smelled like medicine and weighed like something else.

None of it proved anything. All of it pointed somewhere.

At dusk we tied up at a shingle bar high enough to sleep on, and Ruiz cooked rice and tinned fish on a burner that he clearly loved more than most men love their children. We ate on deck under a sky going bruise-colored in the west, where the monsoon was stacking itself behind the ridgelines like something patient. Gideon ate apart, watching the water. Priya laughed at Ruiz's story about a carburetor in 1987, and her laugh was genuine, and I hated how much I wanted it to mean she was innocent.

"So," Ruiz said to me, ladling seconds I hadn't asked for, "what brings a river man onto a medical run? Wages that good in town?"

"My sister's sick," I said. "Hospital in the city. Money's short."

He nodded, no pity in it, which I appreciated. Pity is just curiosity wearing a nicer coat. "River takes care of its own," he said, "if you let it."

"River took my uncle," I said. "Different kind of care."

Gideon's head turned slightly at that—just a degree, the way a compass needle finds north—but she said nothing, and the silence she kept had weight to it, like she was holding a whole conversation underwater.

Later, when the lamp was guttering and Ruiz snored in the wheelhouse like a slow engine, I lay on deck listening to the river move against the hull. Somewhere aft, very softly, a phone screen lit the underside of a tarp for four or five seconds. Then dark. Then nothing.

I stared up at the clouds swallowing the stars and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding all day: a crew of four, one stranger with debts, one deckhand who counts everything, one captain flying from her own name, and one employer who pays cash for questions nobody asks.

Whatever this boat was carrying, it wasn't only medicine.

And tomorrow morning, when we cast off into the flooded lower river, I'd have to decide which of them to trust with what I'd seen—or whether trusting any of them was just another way of drowning slower.
