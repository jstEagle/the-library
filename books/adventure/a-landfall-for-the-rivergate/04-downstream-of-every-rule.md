## 4. Downstream of Every Rule

The river at dawn looked like something that had eaten a country and was still hungry.

I stood on the deck of the *Marta B* with a tin cup of coffee going cold in my hands and watched the water carry whole trees past us, trunk-first, like a funeral procession that had lost its mourners. The lower Rivergate wasn't supposed to run like this until the monsoon proper. It was running like this now, brown and muscular and fast as a lie, and every hour we spent upstream of the gorge was an hour the gorge got meaner.

Ruiz came up beside me and spat over the rail, which was his way of saying good morning.

"Water's up another foot since yesterday," he said. "Maybe two."

"I noticed."

"You noticed. Good. Because I've been noticing you noticing and not saying anything, which is your way of deciding something without telling anybody." He took the coffee out of my hands and drank it, because Ruiz has never met a boundary he respected. "So decide out loud, jefa."

The drowned villages started an hour later.

I'd flown over them for ten years—little grids of tin roofs and drying nets stitched along the banks—and I'd told myself they were fine, that rivers rise and rivers fall, that people who live with water learn its manners. From the air it had always looked almost gentle. From the deck of a boat pushing through what used to be somebody's main street, it was not gentle.

The first one didn't even have a name I knew. Just rooftops breaking the surface like the backs of animals, and a church bell tower standing to its eaves, and a dog on the roof of a granary watching us pass with the flat patience of a creature that had already made its peace with the arithmetic. Priya stood at the bow rail for a long time. Tomas stood next to her, not touching her, not speaking, just being there in the way some men know how to do without being taught.

"They'll come back," Priya said finally.

"Some places come back," I said. "Some places the river keeps."

She looked at me like she wanted to ask which kind I'd lived through, and I gave her nothing, because that's my gift.

* * *

We hit the standing wave at midafternoon, where the river pinches between two rock ribs and the floodwater piles up behind them like a fist cocked back. I'd run this stretch twice before, both times at half this volume, both times with my heart in a more reasonable place.

"Tomas, take the stern line. Priya, off the bow—get inside, get *down*."

"I can hold—"

"That's not a request."

She went. I put the nose into the wave the way you're supposed to, square and honest, no quarter asked, and the river still picked the *Marta B* up like a toy and showed her to the sky.

For about two seconds we flew. I've flown actual airplanes and I'm telling you the boat did it better, which is humiliating. Then the port chine caught and we went over sideways and the world became brown water and engine scream and Ruiz swearing in three languages, and I remember thinking, with a clarity I did not appreciate: *this is how he died, this is exactly the water, this is exactly the angle*—

Then the hull slapped down, half-flooded but right side up, and Tomas was hauling the stern line hand over hand like the rope owed him money, and Priya was already out on the pitching deck before anyone authorized it, grabbing the spare fuel drum that had torn loose from its lashing and was rolling toward the rail with terrible calm deliberation.

"Priya, *leave it*—"

She didn't leave it. She got both arms around the drum and rode it two feet from the edge while Tomas threw himself down the deck and got a fistful of her collar, and between the two of them and Ruiz throwing a line off the winch, we kept forty gallons of diesel that would have been the difference between making Rivergate and becoming a very well-preserved mystery.

When it was done we all sat down on the deck in a row like survivors of something, which we were, and nobody talked for a while except the river, which never stops talking.

Priya had a gash on her forearm, shallow, ugly, already going purple at the edges. I cleaned it with iodine from the med crate—the irony of ferrying medicine downriver while nearly feeding my own crew to the current was not lost on me—and she didn't flinch, which told me she'd had worse and recently.

"You should have left the drum," I said.

"You needed it."

"I said leave it."

"And I decided not to." She looked at me straight on, chin up, blood beading through the bandage. "You're the boss of the boat, Gideon. You're not the boss of whether I care if we make it."

Ruiz laughed from the wheelhouse, one short bark. Tomas didn't laugh, but something moved across his face that was adjacent to laughing, and I felt the strange, unwelcome warmth of a crew forming around me like scar tissue. Four strangers lashed together by high water. It was the closest thing to a family I'd had in fifteen years, and I hated how much I wanted it to hold.

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bar that the maps called an island, back when the maps were written by optimists. Ruiz got a fire going in a ring of stones. The *Marta B* sat aground at the bar's edge, listing slightly, and when I went aboard to check the bilge I found the first thing wrong.

The forward locker was open.

Not broken, not forced. Open, and shut again badly, the latch sitting crooked in its hasp. Inside, the spare parts and the tool roll had been shifted—not taken, shifted, the way things get moved by someone looking for space or looking for something specific and not finding it. And under the tool roll, where I kept the paper charts of the lower gorge—the ones Kessler's people hadn't provided, the ones I'd drawn myself from memory of runs I'd made before I swore the water off—one corner was creased wrong. Folded back. Refolded by a different hand.

I stood in the locker breathing through my mouth for a long time.

There are four people on this boat. One is Ruiz, who has bled for me in two countries and once punched a customs officer on my behalf, which is the purest love I know. One is Priya, who nearly went into the river today holding a fuel drum because she decided we mattered. One is Tomas, three days known, debts unknown. And one of them opened my locker.

Or the river did. Waves throw things around; latches work loose; paper creases. I could build that story. I wanted to build that story. I built it halfway and then looked at the latch again, at the way the screw heads were bright, freshly turned, and stopped building.

I climbed out and closed the hatch gently and walked back to the fire like a woman who had seen nothing, and spent the evening doing the hardest work of the trip so far, which was smiling.

Tomas was mending the torn lashing with marline splice, quick and clean, and he looked up as I sat down. "Boat took it well," he said. "Better than most boats would."

"She's held together by stubbornness and paint."

"Aren't we all." He tied off the splice and tested it, then said, quietly, not looking at me: "You checked the forward locker tonight. Twice."

My coffee cup stopped halfway to my mouth. "You watch everything, don't you."

"It's cheaper than talking." He set the splice down. "Latch was crooked this morning too. Before the rapid. I figured it was the pounding. But screws don't turn themselves." He glanced at me, and his eyes in the firelight were patient and tired and much older than the rest of him. "You want to tell me what's in there worth turning screws for?"

"The charts," I said, because lying to him felt suddenly expensive and I couldn't afford it. "Charts of the gorge. My own. Nobody else has them."

"And somebody wanted to see them."

"Somebody wanted to know what I know."

He nodded slowly, absorbing that, and I watched him decide something—I watched it happen, the small settling of his shoulders, a man signing onto a second job he hadn't applied for. "Well," he said. "River's full of secrets this year. Seems only fair the boat has some too."

Across the fire, Priya was laughing at something Ruiz said, head thrown back, arm bandaged, alive. The monsoon grumbled somewhere behind the western ridgelines like a landlord who'd be around eventually to collect. And I sat between the warmth of the fire and the cold fact of the locker and understood that the leak in the *Marta B* wasn't only in her hull.

Tomorrow we'd reach the mouth of the gorge. Tomorrow the window would start closing.

And one of the four people around this fire already knew exactly where we were going.
