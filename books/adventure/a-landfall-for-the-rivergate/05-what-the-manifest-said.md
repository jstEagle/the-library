## 5. What the Manifest Said

There are three kinds of paper on a river boat: the kind you burn, the kind you wipe your hands on, and the kind that ruins you.

I learned this from my mother, who kept a moneylender's receipt folded into her sari for eleven years until it went soft as cloth. She'd take it out at night and smooth it flat on her knee like she was ironing a shirt for a child who'd never grow. That receipt was the third kind of paper. I promised myself I'd never carry one.

So of course I was carrying three.

* * *

The manifest lived in a green clipboard wedged behind the wheelhouse seat, under a coil of rope Ruiz used as an armrest and a tin of mints he'd been "about to finish" since Manali. Gideon checked it every morning with the reverence other people reserved for prayer—counting crates against lines, initialing in pencil, saying nothing when the numbers came out right, which they always did, because Ruiz ran a tight deck even when he pretended not to know what day it was.

Which is why it bothered me, on the fourth morning downstream, that the clipboard felt wrong.

Not torn. Not obviously touched. Just—wrong, the way a room feels wrong after someone has stood in it breathing. I'd spent two years working hotels before this, checking rooms for things guests left behind and things they took, and you develop a nose for it. A room remembers hands.

Gideon had gone forward to argue with Tomas about the anchor line—he wanted to rig a second one off the bow, she thought it would drag in the shallows, and their arguments had the comfortable rhythm of people who were going to keep having them—and Ruiz was asleep in the engine bay with a wrench on his chest like a teddy bear made of iron. Nobody was watching me. Nobody ever watched me; that was my whole value on a crew, being the person nobody thinks about until the accounts come up short.

I pulled the clipboard out.

The manifest was there. Twelve pages, typed, water-spotted at the corners, each line a crate number and a contents code. MED-4 through MED-19. Antibiotics, saline, insulin in cold boxes, the ordinary cargo of mercy runs. Gideon's initials beside every line like little fences.

But the staples at the top corner had been worried loose and pressed back down, and underneath them, where the second page should have continued the sequence, the paper was a hair too thick. Two sheets where there should have been one, or rather—one sheet where there should have been two, with something tucked beneath it to fake the weight.

I looked over my shoulder. River, rainforest, rain. The monsoon had settled in overnight like a guest who intended to stay the season, drumming on the canopy roof, turning the river the color of milk tea. Somewhere ahead, drowned in all that gray, were villages we couldn't reach fast enough.

I slid the top sheet aside.

Beneath it, a carbon copy—the old kind, the kind that doesn't need a machine, just pressure and bad intentions. Someone had gone through this manifest with a sheet of carbon and a hard pencil and taken an impression of the crate codes. And whoever it was had missed one thing, or hadn't cared: the original order form was still clipped inside the back cover, the one Gideon would never have seen because it predated her contract, the freight order Kessler's office had filed when she chartered the run.

It wasn't a medical freight order.

It read, in the dry language of salvage contracts: *Recovery of specified cargo, wreck site designated RK-7, contents per attached schedule. Client requests verification of serial integrity prior to transfer.*

RK-7. I didn't know what the letters meant. But I knew what *recovery* meant, and I knew what *salvage* meant, and neither word had anything to do with insulin for the valley clinics.

And there, at the bottom, a schedule of crate numbers. Six of them. MED-11, MED-12, MED-14, MED-16, MED-17, MED-18.

Six crates on our boat that weren't medicine. Or weren't only medicine. Crates that someone, fifteen years ago, had put on a plane that went into the lower river, and that Maren Kessler had spent a decade and a small fortune trying to find.

Crates that Gideon Galloway, whether she knew it or not, was carrying straight to them.

* * *

Here is what you should understand about me, so you can judge me properly, because I intend to be judged—I've done the arithmetic and I'd rather it be honest than kind.

My family owes four hundred thousand rupees to a man named Devraj, who owns the ferry concession at the confluence and three of my father's fingers, in the sense that he holds the deed to the shop where my father lost them. My sister has a heart that beats like it's always running late. The surgery costs more than our house, our boat, and my future earnings combined, which Devraj knows, and which is why his offers arrive every few months like weather.

The last offer had come six weeks ago, in a message routed through a cousin of a cousin: *A woman named Kessler pays well for reliable eyes. Think of Anjali.*

I told myself I'd signed on with Gideon's crew to earn honest wages and nothing else. For a while that was even true. Then the stormbound night on the gravel bar happened, and Gideon sat across the lamplight from me with her sleeves rolled up and her guard down by exactly one inch, and said, "You're good at noticing things, Priya. It's a curse. Enjoy." And Ruiz taught me the card game he cheats at, badly, on purpose, so you'll like him. And Tomas stood waist-deep in freezing water for forty minutes re-rigging the anchor line without one word of complaint, and Gideon watched him do it with an expression I recognized because I've worn it: the look of someone deciding to trust again after excellent reasons not to.

That night I deleted Kessler's contact from the satellite phone's memory. Deleted it twice, to be sure.

Then I found the carbon copy, and I understood that the choice had never actually been mine to make. Kessler hadn't hired eyes hoping they'd open. She'd known what was on this boat before any of us did. She'd built the whole run around it—the flooded lower river no one else would fly, the route that passed within kilometers of a wreck site only she seemed to have charted. We weren't a mercy mission that might stumble onto salvage. We were a delivery service, and the package was us.

* * *

I stood in the wheelhouse for a long time holding two pieces of paper, doing the math that has no good answer.

Option one: give the papers to Gideon. Watch her face do whatever it does when the past reaches up out of the water and grabs her ankle. Lose the run, probably—she'd turn the boat around, or burn the crates, or do some honorable Gideon thing that would leave us all unpaid and Anjali's heart still beating late, and Devraj still holding the deed, and my mother still smoothing that soft old receipt on her knee.

Option two: photograph the schedule, send it, collect enough to clear half the debt, and spend the rest of my life being the person who did that. To a crew that taught me a card game. To a woman who called my sharpness a curse and meant it as a compliment.

There was supposed to be an option three. There usually is, in the stories. You tell the truth and find a clever way to save everyone. But option three requires the truth to be survivable, and I had just read a document proving that the woman paying our wages was using us as ballast in a game between rich people and a dead pilot's ghost. Option three required believing Gideon could forgive a betrayal she hadn't even discovered yet, and I had watched her talk about Kessler for four days, and forgiveness was not the organ that moved in her face when that name came up.

I did what I always do when the math has no good answer. I bought time.

The camera was in my duffel, a cheap one with a cracked screen, bought in another life for taking pictures of hotel lobbies. I laid the carbon copy flat on the chart table, weighted the curling corners with two mints from Ruiz's tin, and shot it frame by frame—schedule, serials, the client clause, the Kessler office stamp. My hands were steady. That surprised me. Some part of me had expected shaking, some physical protest, but the body is a coward's accomplice; it saves its trembling for afterward, when trembling is useless.

Twelve photographs. I counted them twice.

Then I put everything back exactly as I'd found it—staples pressed down, clipboard wedged behind the seat, mints returned, rope coil fluffed—and I went out into the rain and stood at the rail and let the river explain to me how far from anywhere we were.

Tomas was on the foredeck coiling line, and he nodded at me, easy, unguarded, a man with debts of his own who had decided we were in the same boat in every sense. Ruiz snored through the engine hatch. Up in the wheelhouse Gideon stood at the wheel with the rain streaming past the glass, flying a boat the way other people fly planes, and I thought: *she doesn't know yet that the water she's afraid of isn't the kind that falls from the sky.*

The satellite phone was in the galley locker, wrapped in oilcloth, for emergencies.

I told myself I wouldn't send anything tonight. I told myself I'd wait, watch, find option three, give honesty one more day to come up with a miracle. And I meant it. I want that on the record too.

But that evening, when we tied up below the drowned church spire at what the charts optimistically called Ferry Bend, I saw Gideon open the green clipboard, count her crates, initial her lines—and pause, just for a second, thumb resting on the staple corner, head tilted like a bird hearing a sound below the range of human ears.

She didn't say anything. She closed the clipboard. She looked out at the water.

And I understood that the window on option three had just closed, and that whatever I was going to do, I needed to do it before she did the math I'd already done—because a woman like Gideon Galloway, given proof that her father's ghost was riding in her hold, wouldn't hesitate.

She'd turn the boat around. And Kessler's men, wherever they were waiting, would come down that river after us like the monsoon itself.

In the galley locker, the phone waited, patient as debt.
