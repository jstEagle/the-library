## 6. Monsoon Hold

The river decided for us around four in the afternoon, the way rivers do—no negotiation, no appeal. One minute we were pushing up against a current gone the color of milk chocolate, and the next the sky simply opened its trapdoor and dropped the monsoon on us like a piano.

"Gravel bar," Gideon shouted over the roar, and Ruiz, who had been waiting for those two words since breakfast, already had the anchor line coiled. We grounded on a spine of shingle maybe forty meters long, an island by lunchtime tomorrow if the water kept climbing, and tied off to a dead spruce that leaned out over the flood like a man deciding whether to jump.

I have been stormbound before. In my experience it splits neatly into two kinds: the kind where you're alone with your own thoughts, which is punishment, and the kind where you're stuck with other people, which is worse—unless the other people happen to be interesting, and then it's the closest thing to church I've found on this earth.

This crew was going to be interesting or fatal. Possibly both.

We rigged the tarp between the spruce and the boat's cabin, got the little camp stove hissing under it, and arranged ourselves in the lamplight like survivors of something, which, give us time, we would be. Rain hammered the tarp with a sound like applause from a very large, very sarcastic audience.

Ruiz dealt cards nobody had asked for. He always does—or did, back when I knew him, years ago, on a different river with a different captain who also died badly. Some things repeat themselves in this country. Rivers, storms, card games, grief.

"Priya," Ruiz said, laying down a hand so bad it was almost artistic, "tell them about the tiger."

"There was no tiger."

"She says there was no tiger," Ruiz told me, delighted. "There was absolutely a tiger."

"It was a shadow," Priya said, studying her cards with the intensity of a woman defusing something. "On a trail. At night. In Assam."

"And what did you do, about this shadow?"

"I stood very still," she said, "and I thought about my mother, and how she'd say dying like that would be embarrassing for the whole family." A pause. The rain filled it. "Then it moved and it was a cow."

"A cow," Ruiz repeated reverently, as though she'd said *angel*.

"Cows kill more people than tigers do," Priya said. "Statistically. Look it up."

"I don't need to look it up. I believe everything now. You could tell me the moon is a cow's eye and I'd believe you."

Gideon laughed—a short, surprised sound, like she'd forgotten the machinery worked—and I found myself watching her instead of my cards, which is why I lost the hand, which is why I ended up on dish duty, which is how these things go.

* * *

Later, when Ruiz had surrendered to sleep with the suddenness of a felled tree, and Priya had rolled into her bag facing away from the lamp, Gideon and I sat at the edge of the tarp with our boots in the wet gravel and watched the river eat the far end of our island one bite at a time.

"You've done this before," she said. Not a question. "Stormbound. River work."

"Some." I turned the tin cup in my hands. It was warm and tasted of burnt coffee and aluminum, which is to say it tasted like every good night I've ever had outdoors. "My brother drowned in a river not unlike this one. Fifteen years back, give or take. Different valley, same water, more or less. Water's all related, my grandmother used to say. You insult one river, they all remember."

Gideon was quiet a moment. "How old were you?"

"Nineteen. He was seventeen. We were running a raft of timber down to the mill because the money was good and we were stupid, which is the usual combination." I said it flatly, the way you say things you've said to yourself ten thousand times until the edges wear smooth. "The river took him and gave me back. I've been trying to settle that account ever since. Working rivers. Paying what I can where I can. It doesn't balance. Nothing balances. But you keep the books anyway."

She nodded slowly, and I saw something move behind her eyes—not pity, thank God; I'd have walked into the flood before sitting through pity—but recognition. The look of someone checking a scar against their own.

"Gideon," I said, because the lamplight was low and the rain made a wall around us and some doors only open in weather like this, "why this river? Kessler's money is good, but you looked at that telegram like it was a summons. And you know this water too well to be doing it for wages."

For a long moment I thought she'd answer. Her mouth opened. Her hand did a small thing against her knee, half a gesture toward something carried a long way.

"My father flew this river," she said finally. Each word set down carefully, like stones across a creek. "Fifteen years ago. His plane went in downriver, in the lower gorge, and—" She stopped. The rain roared. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the ridgelines like a barrel escaping. "And it's complicated."

"Complicated," I repeated. "That's a word men use about women and rivers use about everything else."

That earned me another of the short laughs. But the door had closed—I watched it close, polite as anything, no anger in it, just a woman putting something heavy back down because the floor wasn't ready. She looked out at the black water instead of at me.

"He was a good pilot," she said. "Better than anyone gave him credit for, after. That's all I'll say tonight. Maybe that's more than I should've said."

"It's enough," I told her, and meant it, and understood suddenly that it was true: whatever she was carrying, she'd shown me the shape of it through the wrapping, and a man who presses further in weather like this deserves what he gets.

We sat there while the river rose. Once, her shoulder came to rest against mine—not leaning, exactly, just ceasing to hold itself apart—and neither of us made anything of it, which is sometimes the most a thing can be and still survive being noticed.

"Get some sleep, Tomas," she said eventually. "Tomorrow the window opens or it doesn't, and either way we run."

I crawled into my bag and slept like the dead, which is a phrase I use carefully these days.

* * *

What woke me wasn't sound. It was absence—the stove ticking as it cooled, the rain thinned to a whisper, and under all of it a wrongness I felt in my teeth before I understood it in my ears.

I lay still and counted the breathing around the camp. Ruiz, snoring in his key of E-flat. Priya, silent in her bag. Gideon, out on watch rotation, or so the schedule said.

But the lamplight threw shadows past the tarp's edge, and out beyond the dead spruce, at the treeline where the gravel gave way to alder and dark, there was a light. Small. Cold. The blue-white of a screen, cupped in a palm.

A figure stood among the trees with its back to us, hunched over something held close to its face, and even at forty meters, even in the dark, I knew the posture. Everyone knows that posture now. It's the posture of a person telling someone far away where they are.

I heard nothing of the words. The wind took them, or the phone was held too close, or the speaker knew enough to keep his voice below the water-sound. What I heard was the wind shift, and the creak of the spruce, and my own pulse, loud and stupid in my ears.

The screen-light went out. The figure stood a moment longer, head tilted, listening the way I was listening—two animals in the same dark, each pretending to be part of the landscape. Then it moved, unhurried, back along the treeline, and by the time I'd worked up the nerve to sit up and look properly, there was nothing to see but alder and rain and the last gray suggestion of a shape dissolving into the trees.

I didn't call out. I want that on the record, whatever record there is. I lay in my bag with my heart going like a paddle wheel and I did not call out, because I didn't know yet who it was, and because a name spoken into the dark can't be taken back, and because some cowardly animal part of me wanted one more hour of not knowing.

I got it. Then dawn came up the color of dishwater, and the river had eaten ten more feet of our island in the night, and Gideon stood at the bow with her binoculars fixed on the gorge downstream, and her voice, when it came, was flat and bright as ice.

"Window's opening early. We run today—all of us, together, no stragglers." She lowered the binoculars and looked at each of us in turn, longer than she needed to, longer than was comfortable. "Everyone sleep well?"

Nobody answered fast enough. Nobody answered slow enough, either. And I stood there on the shrinking gravel with the secret burning a hole through my pocket, watching four faces and finding the traitor in none of them and all of them, thinking: *whoever you are, I stood thirty meters from you in the dark, and I let you walk away.*

Upstream, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of an engine that wasn't ours.
