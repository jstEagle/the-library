## 7. The Leaking Boat

The bilge pump woke me before the birds did, which is a distinction no boat owner ever wants to make.

I'd been sleeping in the wheelhouse with my boots on, a habit I'd picked up somewhere between the drowned church at Kilbride and the third night of listening to the river eat its banks, and when I came up out of a dream about my father's hangar — the good dream, the one where the doors are open and the light comes in — I heard it: that wet, arrhythmic *clunk-gurgle* of a pump doing more work than it was built for. I lay still for a moment and did the math every skipper does at three in the morning, the arithmetic of how much water is too much, and how fast, and how far we were from anywhere a boat could die with dignity.

The answer to the last question was: right here.

I went below with the flashlight in my teeth. The bilge was holding maybe four inches where it should have held none, and the water was coming in from the garboard seam on the starboard side in a thin, patient silver thread, the way water always is — never dramatic, never honest, just persistent. I pressed my thumb against the seam and felt the hull breathe. Somewhere back in the drowned forest, or the rapid above Kilbride where we'd shipped that green water over the rails, something had started to let go.

"Ruiz," I said, when I found him folded into the engine bay like a man who'd been built there from spare parts. "We're taking water."

He didn't even open his eyes all the way. "We've been taking water since Natchez Point. I've been pumping it since Natchez Point. You've been sleeping on top of it since Natchez Point."

"How long can you keep up with it?"

He considered this with the gravity of a man being asked about the afterlife. "Depends on the river. Depends on the load. Depends on whether the pump decides it's retired." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last week. "I'd say we're good for the gorge."

"And if we don't run the gorge?"

"Then we're good for a very slow funeral."

* * *

The problem — because there is always a problem, and the river keeps a full calendar of them — was the window.

I'd been watching the glacier all week the way you watch a debt collector's car in your rearview. The meltwater gauge at the old survey station had been climbing, and the ice face upstream had started shedding its skirts in the afternoons, white thunder rolling down the valley like the mountain clearing its throat. Every year the calving opened a channel through the icefields for a few weeks — three, maybe four if the season was kind — and every year the wreck of the *Meridian* sat under that moving ice like a secret under a floorboard, waiting for the light to change.

Kessler had said three weeks. She'd said it with that flat certainty she has, the certainty of a woman who has read the same reports I have and paid better analysts to interpret them.

Except the window was opening early. I could feel it in the water — the river had come up six inches overnight, cold and mineral-bright, the signature of a glacier letting go somewhere above us. And I could see it in the light on the ice when we rounded the bend at Gray Narrows: a seam of open water where there should have been white, a black thread unspooling through the frozen country, widening by the hour.

Three weeks had become, by my rough count, four days. Maybe less.

"We run the gorge tomorrow," I told them that evening, on the gravel bar where we'd tied up. Priya was mending the tarp over the cargo with the focus of a woman stitching her own future shut. Tomas sat on a crate, sharpening a pike pole with a whetstone, and looked at me the way he always did — like he was reading a document and being polite about the redactions.

"The gorge at this water level," Ruiz said, not looking up from the engine he was pretending to fix, "is a coin flip."

"I've flipped worse coins."

"That's not comforting, Gideon."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be a schedule." I looked at the three of them — my mechanic, my deckhand, my stranger — and felt the strange weight of the word *crew*, which I had spent ten years avoiding and which had somehow attached itself to me again like a burr. "We run it at first light, before the melt peaks. We stay light, we stay centered, and we don't stop. Once we're in, we're in."

Tomas spoke without looking up. "And the boat?"

"The boat holds."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure the boat holds until we're through the gorge. After that it can do whatever it likes." I meant it as a joke. Nobody laughed, including me.

* * *

First light came up gray and green, the monsoon light I knew too well, the whole valley lit like the inside of a bottle. The gorge at Gray Narrows is a mile of river that the mapmakers gave up on — they just drew a jagged line and wrote *caution* in a handwriting that shakes. At low water it's a technical run, a slot-car track of rock and boil. At this level it was something else. It was the river deciding, for one mile, whether it was a river or a weapon.

I took the wheel myself. Ruiz was below at the pump, one hand on the throttle linkage, shouting depths at me like a man calling a horse race. Priya and Tomas were on the bow with the pike poles, reading the water ahead of us, and I'll say this for whatever else happened later: in that mile, the three of them moved like they'd been a crew for years. When Tomas called *left channel, rock under the boil*, Priya was already shifting her weight to balance him, and when the first big hole opened up under our starboard quarter I had the wheel over before my conscious mind caught up, running on their voices the way you run on a rope.

We took water over the rails twice. The pump clunked and gurgled and complained. The gorge walls closed in, black stone streaked with meltwater, and the noise was enormous, the river shouting the whole time, and somewhere in the middle of it — halfway, at the worst bend, where the channel kinked and the current stacked up into a standing wave the color of wet slate — Ruiz came up the ladder two steps at a time and said a word he never says.

"Gideon. Boats. Behind us."

I risked the half-second look back, which is a half-second the gorge will bill you for.

Two boats. Aluminum-hulled, outboard-braced, running our wake with the casual competence of people who knew exactly where the channel went — which nobody knows, which is the point of the gorge, which is why Kessler's money was supposed to be the only money crazy enough to be here. They were maybe four hundred meters back and closing, and the lead boat had a crane arm folded flat along its gunwale and men in drysuits, and on the side of the hull, stenciled in letters that caught the gray light, a name I knew.

*Kessler Marine Recovery.*

"They can't be," Priya said. "Nobody runs the gorge blind. Nobody—"

"They're not running it blind." The words came out of me flat and cold, and I heard them the way you hear someone else say something true about you in another room. "They're running it behind us. We're the icebreaker. We're the depth sounder." I looked at the wake we were leaving, the clean line of it drawn through the worst water in the valley, and I understood — finally, completely, in the place where understanding feels like a bruise — that we had not been hired to run the river.

We had been hired to prove it could be run.

"Who told them?" Tomas asked, and his voice was very quiet, and he was looking at me, but Priya was looking at the gunwale, at her own hands, at the water, at anything.

The bilge pump clunked. The gorge roared. And I stood there at the wheel with the standing wave coming at us and did the arithmetic again, the three-in-the-morning arithmetic, except this time the water coming in wasn't under the floorboards.

The supplies disturbed at Natchez Point. The manifest pages I'd noticed missing and told myself were river damage. Ruiz's fuel lines that someone had been into. The satellite call Tomas heard from the treeline at Monsoon Hold — I'd filed that under *trappers*, because I wanted to, because filing it anywhere else would have meant admitting that the small warm thing I'd been building out here, this crew of strays, had a hole in it.

The leak was never just in the boat.

"Later," I said, because the wave was here. I put the wheel hard over, Ruiz screamed the throttle to the stops, and the *Meridian* — my father's old girl, or at least her granddaughter, carrying his name and my stubbornness and whatever it was Kessler wanted badly enough to buy a traitor for — climbed the standing wave, hung there for one long gray moment with the gorge walls tilting on either side, and came down the other side still pointed downstream.

The Kessler boats followed. Of course they did. They had a paid map of everything I'd charted the hard way, and they were spending it like water.

* * *

We came out of the gorge at 0740 with the hull still holding, the pump still clunking, and nobody saying anything, which is the loudest a boat can be. The valley opened up ahead of us, and there it was: the glacier, three miles upstream of the wreck site, its face split by a seam of open black water that had not existed two days ago. The window. Open early, and already wide enough for a boat.

Wide enough for four boats.

I put us in against the willow bank at the first eddy and killed the engine, and in the sudden quiet I could hear the glacier calving, a long white report rolling down the valley like artillery on the far side of a war.

"Nobody goes anywhere," I said. "Nobody touches the radio. Nobody touches the sat phone." I looked at each of them in turn, and I hated how much I wanted one of them to look back at me clean. "One of you has been talking to Kessler since before we left Rivergate. I don't know who. I'm going to find out, because in about an hour there are going to be four boats at that ice and one crate under it, and I am not losing my father twice."

Priya's face did something complicated and broke apart at the edges. Tomas set down the pike pole with the care of a man laying down a weapon he might need again. And Ruiz came up out of the engine bay with bilge water on his sleeves and said, quietly, in the voice he uses for terminal diagnoses:

"Gideon. You need to hear this. The pump's not going to make the wreck site and back."

Behind us, around the gorge's last bend, the first Kessler boat slid out into the open water, its crane arm unfolding like something waking up.
