## 8. Confrontation at the Confluence

I have done a lot of arithmetic in my life—fuel loads, cargo weights, the exact number of rupees that stood between my family and the bank taking the house in Kolhapur—but I had never once calculated what a photograph was worth until the morning it cost me everything.

The confluence announced itself before we saw it: a brown seam of silt braiding into the glacier-green of the main channel, like two arguments meeting and neither winning. Gideon cut the engine to half and stood at the wheel with her binoculars up, and I watched her shoulders change shape.

"Boats," she said. "Two of them. Anchored off the gravel spit."

Ruiz came up from the engine bay wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since before I met him. "Salvage rigs," he said. "Kessler's paint job. I'd know that ugly green anywhere."

Nobody said the thing we were all thinking, which was: *nobody finds anybody on this river by accident.* The monsoon had closed every approach but one, and Kessler's boats were sitting on it like they'd been mailed an invitation.

Because someone had mailed them one.

Me. I'd mailed it. Three taps of a satellite phone from the treeline during the storm—no, not even mine, I'd only borrowed the moment; the call itself had gone out while we sat around the lamp telling stories, and I had lain awake after hearing it and told myself it wasn't connected, told myself the world didn't work that neatly. But the world worked exactly that neatly, and I had helped it along, because there was a number attached to those torn manifest pages and the number had a house inside it and my mother's name on the deed.

Gideon turned from the water and looked at me. Not at Ruiz, not at Tomas crouched low by the gunwale. At me. She had the kind of face that made you feel audited.

"Priya," she said. "You want to tell me why Kessler's salvage team is parked at the only fork in the river between here and the ice?"

There were perhaps four seconds in which I could have lied. I want it noted, for the record of my own conscience, that I did not take them.

"Because I sold you," I said.

The words came out smaller than I'd planned. On the river everything echoes—the canyon walls pick up your voice and hand it back to you twice—and so my confession came back at me from both banks, *sold you, sold you,* as if the landscape itself wanted witnesses.

Tomas went very still. Ruiz's rag stopped moving.

"I photographed pages from the manifest," I said. "The ones that were torn out. The crates that weren't medicine. There's a buyer—a client of Kessler's—who's been looking for what your father hid for fifteen years, and they're paying for its location." My voice cracked on the next part and I let it. "I sent coordinates. During the storm. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'll give you everything back, the photos, the pages, all of it—"

I had the phone out of my jacket before I finished the sentence, holding it out to her like evidence at a trial where I was also the defendant, the prosecutor, and, God help me, the star witness.

Gideon looked at the phone. She didn't take it.

"You could have just asked for money," she said finally, and there was something in her voice that wasn't anger. It took me a moment to identify it, because I'd never heard it aimed at me before. It was grief. "I'd have found a way to say no kindly."

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because it was exactly that. She would have. That was the worst part. She would have refused me gently, and I would have walked away poorer and cleaner, and my mother would have lost the house, and I would have hated her a little for it forever. Instead I'd chosen the ugly version, and now here we all were, drifting toward two boatloads of men who got paid to be ugly professionally.

"They're coming about," Tomas said quietly.

He was right. One of the green rigs had raised anchor and was swinging its bow toward us, and a man at the rail had something long and dark resting across it that might have been a rifle or might have been a very optimistic fishing rod. On this river, I'd learned, the distinction was mostly academic.

"Gideon," Ruiz said. "We're running low on everything except trouble."

She still hadn't taken the phone. She looked at me another long moment, and then she said, "After. Give it to me after we're not dead. Right now I need your eyes and your hands and your guilt can ride along in the bilge with the rest of the water."

It was not forgiveness. I knew forgiveness when I saw it; my mother dispensed it like tea. This was something more practical—an accounting entry marked *pending*—and somehow that was worse and better at once. I pocketed the phone and took my position at the bow line, and if my hands shook, the river was rough enough that nobody could prove it.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do, without any of the courtesy of slow motion.

The lead rig closed the gap and a man with a bullhorn—which is a device invented specifically so that cowardice can be amplified—announced that we were operating in a restricted salvage zone and should prepare to be boarded. Gideon answered him with a gesture that required no amplification whatsoever.

Then the second rig moved to cut off our downstream line, and Ruiz made his decision.

He'd been below deck, and he came up carrying the spare fuel drum—the last of our reserve—over one shoulder like it was a bag of laundry, and he heaved it into the water on the upstream side. It was either madness or genius; with Kessler's men it turned out to be both. They veered to avoid the drum, expecting debris, maybe expecting worse, and in those three seconds of their hesitation Ruiz swung our stern toward the near bank, toward the wall of willow and alder that lined the channel like a green fence.

"There," Tomas said suddenly, pointing. "There's a channel."

I saw nothing. I want to be honest about that: I saw a solid wall of vegetation and certain death. But Tomas was already moving to the bow, and when he talked about water he had a way of sounding like he was reading from a document the rest of us hadn't been issued. "My uncle ran timber down this side when I was a boy. It cuts behind the gravel bar and rejoins below the rapids. Two meters wide in dry season." He glanced at the swollen brown water. "Maybe five today. Maybe."

"Maybe is my favorite word," Gideon said, and drove us at the willows.

The branches took the windshield first—I heard it go with a sound like the river applauding badly—and then we were through, into a corridor of water so narrow the alders brushed both rails at once, and behind us the bullhorn was screaming coordinates to the second rig, and ahead of us was Tomas, standing at the bow like a figurehead, calling depth and bend in a low, flat voice that never once rose.

They followed. Of course they followed. But their boats drew thirty centimeters more than ours, and the river knew it, and about four hundred meters in, we heard the first one grind to a stop on a submerged bar with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and the swearing that floated downstream after us was, I have to admit, deeply satisfying.

The second one made it further. Long enough to matter.

* * *

They caught us at the pinch point, where the channel narrowed between two boulders and the water stacked up white and mean. Their rig slid in behind us with its engine idling high, and three men stood on the deck, and the one in front had Kessler's foreman's build—you don't forget a man built like a load-bearing wall—and a voice like gravel in a bucket.

"Vasquez! End of the line. Hand over the girl and the manifest pages and you can keep your boat and your teeth."

*Hand over the girl.* As though I were cargo. As though I hadn't spent six years on this river earning my place in a crew that had, forty minutes ago, every reason to throw me overboard themselves.

Gideon put the boat in neutral and turned to face them, and I watched her do the math—three men, one rifle, a channel too narrow to run—and I watched her arrive at the answer I'd already reached, which was that there wasn't one.

And then Ruiz stepped in front of me.

Not dramatically. That's the thing I keep returning to. He didn't make a speech. He didn't look noble. He just shifted his weight, the way he did when positioning himself under an engine about to drop, and said, "The girl stays," in the tone of a man stating the boiling point of water.

The rifle came up. I remember thinking, absurdly, that it was a very clean rifle for a salvage man, and then the world became noise and motion. The first blow caught Ruiz across the shoulder and spun him; the second he partially blocked with his forearm and I heard something in it go wrong, a sound like green wood breaking. He went down onto the deck plates and got up again—he *got up again*, this sixty-year-old man with hands like old rope—and planted himself in front of me a second time, bleeding from the ear now, saying nothing at all, which was somehow louder than anything he could have said.

Something in my chest tore loose and reassembled itself into a different shape.

"No," I heard myself say, and pushed past Ruiz's good arm, and held up the phone. "You want the pages? Here. Here's everything." And before anyone could stop me—including, briefly, physics—I hurled the phone over the side, into the brown water, where it vanished without ceremony, along with the photographs, the coordinates, the payout, and the house in Kolhapur.

The silence that followed lasted about two seconds.

Then Gideon opened the throttle.

The boat lunged through the pinch with maybe a hand's width on either side—I felt willow leaves slap my face like the river slapping me personally—and behind us the rig couldn't follow through that gap at speed, and their shouting fell away, and then fell away entirely, swallowed by the channel bending east into country none of their maps had ever bothered with.

* * *

We ran for twenty minutes before Gideon let us drift, in a wide slow eddy behind a fallen cottonwood, engine ticking, everyone breathing like the air rationed.

Ruiz sat against the rail with his arm bound in a strip of sailcloth and Priya's—my—spare shirt wrapped around his ear, refusing help with each individual bandage as a matter of principle. His forearm was broken. He described it as "a hairline, probably, the river will set it," which was medically insane and emotionally correct.

I knelt in front of him. "Ruiz. I'm—"

"If you apologize," he said, "I will make you listen to the story of my gallbladder, and it has diagrams."

So I didn't. I just stayed kneeling there, and after a while he put his good hand on top of my head, briefly, the way you'd steady something wobbling on a shelf, and that was the whole conversation.

Gideon stood at the wheel with her back to us, watching the water. When she finally spoke, she didn't turn around.

"The window opens tomorrow," she said. "Early, like everything else this season. We make the wreck site by nightfall or we lose fifteen years and my father both." A pause. The current muttered against the hull. "Priya. You're on the bow lines. If you're going to owe this crew, you'll pay it in rope."

It wasn't absolution. It was better—it was a job. I picked up the lines and went forward, past Tomas, who looked at me once and nodded, a small nod, the kind you give someone who has finally read the same page of the map you're on.

Ahead, past the last bend, the valley walls fell open and the glacier came into view for the first time—white and vast and groaning, a wall of old time holding its breath above the river. And beneath it, somewhere in that green-black water, a plane had been waiting fifteen years for us to arrive.

I meant what I threw away. Every bit of it.

But as the ice boomed once, deep, like a door unlocking, I noticed Tomas watching the glacier face with an expression I couldn't file anywhere—not fear, not awe. Recognition.

"Tomas," I said. "What is it?"

He didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was very quiet.

"I've seen that wreck before," he said. "When I was nine years old. And I know what else was in the water with it."
