## 9. Calving

The glacier told you when it was in a mood. You learned that the way you learned a horse, or a river, or a man with a temper—by listening past the noise to what the noise was doing. All morning it had been talking. Not the ordinary creak and settle of a living ice mass doing its slow arithmetic, but a deeper conversation, groans that came up through the water and into the hull of Tomas's borrowed skiff like the mountain clearing its throat before it said something unforgivable.

I kept my hands on the tiller and my eyes on the face.

It was bigger than I remembered. Everything about that night fifteen years ago had been dark and sideways and screaming, so my memory held only fragments—the white wall lit by our running lights, the black water full of floating debris, my father's plane already gone under. Now, in flat grey daylight, I could see the whole cathedral of it: two hundred feet of blue-green ice streaked with moraine, hanging over the lake at the river's head like a verdict nobody had gotten around to reading aloud.

"Depth's right," Ruiz called from the bow, where he'd spent an hour swinging the sonar puck off the side like a man dowsing for water. "Forty-one feet. Bottom shelves up from there. If your father put her down where you think he did, she's sitting on that shelf."

"She's sitting where he put her," I said.

Priya didn't say anything. She hadn't said much since the confluence, since she'd stood in front of me with her phone out and her whole face gone the color of wet paper and confessed everything—the torn manifest pages, the photographs, the money Kessler's people had promised her family. I hadn't forgiven her. I want to be honest about that, because this story doesn't work if I pretend I'm better than I am. What I'd done was worse than forgive her, maybe. I'd decided I needed every pair of hands on the line today, and grief makes a strange kind of accountant. There would be time later to decide what she owed and what I could afford to collect.

Tomas shipped the oars and let us drift. "The window," he said quietly. "Look at the waterline."

I looked. The meltwater mark on the ice ran higher than it should have, three days early, a dirty band showing where the summer sun had been eating at the base all season. The calving season wasn't coming. It was here.

"We raise the crate fast or we don't raise it at all," I said. "Ruiz, hooks. Priya, watch the face—if anything above the waterline starts to move, you scream like the devil himself is after you. Tomas—"

"I know how to dive a wreck," he said. "Badly."

"Badly is fine. Fast and badly."

* * *

The water was the kind of cold that isn't cold so much as opinionated—it took hold of you and informed you, thoroughly, that you were not welcome. Tomas went down first with the lift bag and the hook line, and I followed, because there was no version of this where I watched from the surface. Some doors you have to open yourself or they stay shut forever, and some of them stay shut even then.

Visibility underwater was maybe ten feet, milk-grey with rock flour, and the wreck materialized out of it the way ghosts do in stories—first a shadow, then a shape, then the thing itself, undeniable.

Her tail was still on. That was the part that undid me. Fifteen years in forty-one degrees of glacier-fed water and the tail assembly stood up off the shelf intact, fabric-covered rudder gone soft as old leather but the frame true, the registration numbers still legible under a skin of silt. NC-something-something. My father's handwriting was on that plane somewhere, in maintenance logs, in grease pencil on the instrument panel—*check the door latch, G., stop flying with your boots off*—and I hung there in the freezing dark with my regulator chattering and looked at the numbers and felt fifteen years collapse into the length of my own body.

The crate was lashed into what remained of the cabin, still strapped down under the cargo netting, which meant one thing and I knew it before I knew why I knew it: he'd secured it. A man whose plane is going down doesn't strap his cargo in unless the cargo matters more than his exit. Unless he went into the water *because* of the cargo.

Tomas worked the hook line around the crate while I held the netting clear, and we lifted it together, the lift bag blooming overhead like a pale lung, and the crate rose off the shelf trailing silt, and behind it, half-buried, I saw the corner of something else—a metal case, small, and I made a decision I'd carry out of that water with me: not today. Today was for the crate. Today was for staying alive.

We broke the surface gasping, and the glacier screamed.

Not groaned. Screamed—a long rising shriek of tortured ice that went through my chest and came out my back teeth, and above us, high on the face, a crack opened like a lightning strike frozen mid-fall.

"Priya!" Ruiz bellowed.

"I SEE IT! MOVE!"

* * *

We got the crate into the skiff and ourselves with it, and Tomas rowed us away from the face with the kind of desperate, graceless power that doesn't look like anything in the movies and looks exactly like staying alive in real life. Behind us the crack propagated with a sound like the whole valley tearing a sheet, and then a section of the serac the size of a church let go.

The wave it threw hit us broadside and turned the world into white noise and green water. I remember holding the crate. That's all. Whatever else happened in those ninety seconds—and there was swimming, and there was Ruiz hauling me over the gunwale by my collar, and there was Priya bailing with a fuel can cut in half—I remember my arms locked around sodden pine boards while the lake tried to take everything back.

Then it was over, and we were drifting in a litter of brash ice, and the new iceberg sat in the middle of the lake looking smug, and nobody spoke for a long time.

Ruiz finally broke the silence by prying at the crate lid with his pry bar. "Well? Are we going to look, or are we going to sit here admiring the scenery?"

The boards came away swollen and reluctant. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job across fifteen years, was a wooden box, and inside the box—

Logbooks. Three of them, leather-bound, water-stained at the corners but dry at the heart. And beneath them, wrapped separately, a bundle of documents in a rubber sleeve.

My father's logbooks. His actual hand. I'd forgotten what it looked like—slanted, impatient, the letters leaning forward like they were trying to get somewhere faster than the words would allow. I opened the last one to the final entries and read, there in the bottom of the boat with glacial melt dripping off my chin, the truth I had flown ten years of medicine runs trying not to ask for.

He hadn't crashed. He'd scuttled her. The last entry laid it out in his clipped pilot's shorthand: the cargo Kessler's family had paid him to run—contraband, artifacts stripped from the upper valley sites, things that belonged in the ground and in the museums of the people who made them—he'd loaded it, flown to the lake, and put the plane down on purpose rather than deliver it. *Rather than let it be used,* he'd written. *Let them call it a crash. Let them call me careless. Better careless than complicit.* And below that, names. Dates. Payments received and payments refused. The Kessler name sat in that ledger like a stone in a boot—old man Kessler, Maren's father, and Maren's signature countersigning the final contract, dated nine days before the crash.

She'd been twenty-two years old. She'd known exactly what her father was buying, and she'd signed for it anyway.

I read it twice. Then I read it aloud, because Ruiz deserved to hear it, and Priya, and Tomas, who had debts to this water of his own and listened with his jaw tight and his eyes closed.

"So she didn't hire us to salvage it," Priya said slowly. "She hired us to find it. To find out if it was still down there. If the proof was still down there."

"And now we've told her," Ruiz said grimly, "by diving it."

The satellite phone was in the dry bag. It had been quiet since the confluence—or I'd assumed it had been quiet. Priya pulled it out, checked it, and went white again in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

"Gideon. There's a message. Sent an hour ago." She looked up at me. "From Kessler's number. One line."

"Read it."

"'I know you found it. The window closes tonight. Be on the water at the lower landing by dusk, or I tell the valley what I've always told them—that Gideon Galloway helped her father steal from it.'"

I looked up at the glacier. The sun was already sliding behind the western ridge, and the meltwater mark gleamed like a countdown, and somewhere downstream, at the lower landing, Maren Kessler was waiting with whatever she thought truth was worth.

I closed the logbook and held it against my chest, oilcloth and all, and felt something I hadn't felt in fifteen years, which was not peace and not fear but the strange third thing underneath both of them—the feeling of a weight you've carried so long you forgot it was there, suddenly set down.

"She can tell them," I said. "Or I can. But either way, I'm flying out through that window tonight, and I'm landing at Rivergate, and everybody's going to hear it from me first."

Ruiz grinned at me through the dripping ruin of us all. "Then we'd better get you to a plane, boss."

Behind us, the glacier calved again—smaller this time, almost conversational, like it was done with us. Like it had been waiting fifteen years to hand this over, and was glad to be rid of the weight too.

Tomas was already rowing.
