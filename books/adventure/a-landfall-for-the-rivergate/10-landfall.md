## 10. Landfall

The window was open. That was the joke of it, the glacier's idea of humor—three weeks of waiting, a leaking boat, Ruiz's hands stitched like a baseball, Priya's confession still sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone—and now the ice gave us forty minutes of clear air and said, *well?*

I stood on the gravel bar with the logbook in a dry bag against my ribs and looked up at the slot of sky between the ridgelines. The Beaver sat where we'd dragged her above the flood line, one float patched with epoxy and prayer, her engine cowl still bearing the dent from the day my father taught me to preflight by feel.

"Fuel's enough," Ruiz said beside me. He'd wrapped his ribs that morning without being asked and moved like a man negotiating with his own body. "Not enough to sightsee. Enough to get there."

"Where's there?" Tomas asked. He'd been asking it gently for two days, ever since the crate came up out of the green water with the glacier groaning overhead like something waking.

I'd almost answered him twice. Both times I'd stopped, because the answer had always been *nowhere*, because the answer had always been *away*, because ten years of flying medicine into this valley had been built on the promise that I would never again be the Galloway girl, never be the daughter of the man who took the money and the plane and the story down into the river with him.

But my father hadn't taken anything down into the river. He'd put it there. On purpose. And he'd written down why, in a hand I recognized the way you recognize your own reflection, and he'd named the Kesslers as the men who wanted what he refused to carry, and every year since, Maren Kessler had built her salvage company and her respectability on top of a lie my father died keeping.

The logbook weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"Gideon," Tomas said. "Whatever you're going to do—do it before the weather does."

I looked at Rivergate's direction, southwest, past the shoulder of the ridge. Somewhere down there was the strip, the town, the people who had looked through me for a decade with a politeness that was worse than hatred. Somewhere down there Maren Kessler was waiting for word from her salvage team, expecting me to come back empty or not come back at all, either way useful to her.

And somewhere down there was a truth that had been underwater long enough.

"Priya," I said.

She came over slowly. She'd barely slept since the confluence; she looked like she'd aged a season in three days. "Boss."

"I'm going to need a witness who can hold a camera steady."

She understood immediately. I watched her decide all over again—the same decision she'd made when she handed me the torn manifest pages, made once more, cheaper this time, easier. "I'm coming."

"Tomas. Ruiz." I turned to face them both. "You can stay here with the boat. There's no reason—"

"There's every reason," Ruiz said. "I've been flying with Galloways since before you could reach the rudder pedals. Get in the plane."

Tomas just looked at me, and then at the river, and then back. "You owe me a story," he said. "I figure I should hear it from the source."

* * *

We were wheels-up at 11:40. The window was holding—high broken cloud, wind out of the northwest at fifteen knots, the kind of conditions the interior grants maybe four days a season and grants grudgingly, like a landlord who resents the tenant.

The glacier route is twenty minutes of flying that takes years off you even in good weather. The icefall below us was calving as we crossed the first lateral moraine—a house-sized block sheared off the face and went down into the meltwater with a sound I felt in the floorboards before I heard it, a deep white concussion rolling up through the airframe. Priya braced a hand on the seat frame. Ruiz didn't look up from watching my gauges.

"Left float's weeping a little," he said. "Nothing a landing won't fix."

"Comforting."

"I'm an old man. Comforting is my job now."

Tomas was in the back, quiet, watching the ice come apart beneath us. I caught his eye in the mirror once and he nodded, like we'd agreed on something neither of us had said aloud.

The valley opened ahead and there was Rivergate, small and brown and stubborn on its bench above the flood plain, the strip cut into the terrace grass, the hangars, the church steeple doing its best impression of a control tower. Smoke from cookstoves standing straight up in the calm. A place I had landed two hundred times and never once belonged to.

Maren Kessler's Cessna 206 was parked at the fuel drums. Of course it was. She'd have flown up the moment her salvage team radioed that we'd reached the wreck site. She'd be standing on the apron right now, tidy and patient, ready to explain to anyone who'd listen that poor unhinged Gideon Galloway had gone treasure hunting in her father's old crash site, dredging up family grief for no good purpose, and wasn't it sad, and didn't someone ought to ground her.

I set up for the approach with my heart going like a loose propeller.

"You could still turn south," Ruiz said quietly. "Anchorage. Lawyers. Do it the clean way."

"The clean way takes two years. Kessler's got permits pending on half this drainage. Two years and she owns the river." I flattened out over the tree line. "No. This ends where it started."

I flared onto the strip harder than I usually would—deliberately, so the whole town would hear it—and rolled out and taxied to the apron and shut down in front of the general store, where Saturday morning had produced maybe thirty people and a dog, and Maren Kessler standing among them with her arms folded and her face already arranged.

I climbed down with the dry bag under my arm. My legs felt borrowed.

"Gideon." Maren's voice carried beautifully; it always had. Concerned. Reasonable. "People are saying you've been up at the old site. Whatever you think you found—"

"You want to know what I found?" I pulled the logbook out and held it up. "Dad's flight logs. Fifteen years of them, sealed in the crate he sank himself. Want to know why the cargo manifest said medical supplies and the crates said otherwise? Because your father contracted him to move contraband upriver, and when Dad read the manifest close enough to understand what he was carrying, he scuttled it. Deliberately. Wrote it down. Dated it. Signed it." I let that sit. "He chose the river over your family's money. That's what killed him—not bad weather, not bad luck. A decision. His last one, and it was the right one."

The silence on the apron was total. Even the dog had stopped moving.

Maren's composure held for about four seconds, which for Maren was a geological era. Then: "That's a document of unknown provenance. Anyone could have—"

"It's in his hand," said old Marta Ellingssen, from the front row, in the voice of a woman who had once paid my father in eggs for freight runs. She stepped forward and took the logbook from me and opened it and looked at the page for a long moment. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious. "It's in his hand. And it names your father, Maren. I remember the summer he bought that new boat. We all wondered."

Priya stepped up beside me and held out her camera. "I photographed the manifest pages too," she said clearly. "The originals and the copies. In case provenance comes up."

Ruiz limped forward, which for Ruiz constituted a dramatic entrance. "And I'll testify I watched Hal Galloway load that crate into the river site myself, the week before he went down. He told me if anything happened to him, the truth was in the water and it would keep."

Maren looked around at the faces turning away from hers, one by one, the whole valley performing a slow arithmetic it had apparently been waiting fifteen years to finish. "This doesn't prove—" she started, but her voice had lost the room, and she knew it, and everyone knew she knew it.

"Marta," I said, "the valley council still meets in the schoolhouse?"

"Thursdays."

"It's Thursday."

* * *

They convened by two o'clock. I won't give you the whole afternoon—there are lawyers involved now, and depositions, and a permit review that will grind along like everything official in this country—but the shape of it was simple. The logbook went into evidence. The Kessler salvage permits were suspended pending investigation. Maren walked out of the schoolhouse alone while the room was still talking, and nobody followed her, and that was the verdict that mattered most.

Afterward, Marta found me on the store porch, where I was drinking coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration.

"Hangar three's been empty since Oskar died," she said. "Council voted while you were still in the air, actually. Figured you'd say yes." She paused. "Figured you'd earned being asked."

I looked out at the strip, at my Beaver sitting in the late light with her dented cowl and her patched float, and I thought about ten years of landing here and leaving before anyone could learn my name properly.

"Yeah," I said. "I'll take it."

* * *

**Tomas**

Here is what I know about debts to the water: they don't cancel. They only change hands.

I signed onto that boat for wages, and I figured I'd work the season, send money downriver to my sister's kids, and disappear the way I always do. Instead I ran a gorge in a sinking boat, hid in a side channel I learned from a smuggler who's probably dead now, helped raise a crate off a drowned airplane while a glacier tried to eat us, and ended up standing in a schoolhouse in Rivergate watching a woman tell the truth in front of everyone who ever hurt her.

Gideon found me on the dock at dusk, after the planes were tied down and the weather had finally, mercifully, begun to close in behind the ridgelines like a door shutting on the whole season.

"So," she said. "You staying?"

"Winter's coming. Nothing to fly to."

"There's work. Firewood, at least. And Ruiz wants help rebuilding the boat's transom—he says you've got hands."

"He says that about everyone he's drunk with."

"He's not drunk. That's how you know he means it."

I looked out at the water. The river was dropping already, the flood falling off it, the drowned villages giving their roofs back to the air one at a time. Somewhere upstream the glacier was calving in the dark, spending itself, opening and closing its one window a year whether anyone flew through it or not.

"My sister's kids," I said. "Downriver. I send what I can."

"Then you'll need wages," she said. "Hangar three opens Monday."

And that was it. No speech, no handshake even—she just bumped my shoulder with hers on the way past, the way you do with crew, with family, with people you expect to see tomorrow, and went up the dock toward the lights of the town that had finally, after fifteen years and one sunken truth, made room for her.

I stayed on the dock a while longer. The water was quiet. For once, so was I.
