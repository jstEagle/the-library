## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The river gives up its dead at dawn, or that's what the old men at the landing like to say over their coffee, and like most things old men say over coffee it's only half true. What the river actually gives up at dawn is light—thin and green and full of silt—and if you're already down at forty feet when it comes through, you get about ten minutes of working visibility before the current stirs the bottom back into soup. So you learn to be down there before dawn. You learn to work in the dark and let the light find you.

I was down there before dawn. I'm always down there before dawn. It's the one appointment in my life I've never missed, which says something about me I'd rather not examine too closely.

The wreck sits on her starboard side in a bend of the channel where the current runs lazy and deep, keel toward the surface, hull furred with a decade of zebra mussels so thick she looks less like a ship than a whale that died mid-thought. Officially, she doesn't exist. Unofficially, she doesn't exist either—the navy's response to every inquiry ever filed about a vessel of her tonnage going missing from these waters has been a form letter and a silence you could park a barge in. She is the wreck nobody names, and I have spent six years diving her, and I have never once said her name out loud, partly because I don't know it and mostly because names are how things get found.

My name isn't Paloma Voss, by the way. That's just the name I answer to. The boat I came in on is registered to a man named Aldous Greer, who was born in 1941, sold bait and diesel off a dock two counties downstream, and died in 2011 of a heart that finally quit arguing. Aldous had no family, no enemies, and a paperwork trail thin enough to read a newspaper through, and before he died he did me the single greatest kindness of my life: he got drunk, looked at me across his bait cooler, and said, "Boy, you want to disappear, disappear into something boring." Then he signed some papers. I buried him properly, because he deserved it, and I've been Aldous Greer's silent partner ever since. The tax assessor thinks Aldous is ninety-one years old and frugal. Let him think it. Frugal men don't get audited.

I ran my hand along the hull plates, counting by touch. Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen. Plate sixteen had been my project for three weeks—a seam split wide enough to admit a slim diver with a good knife and no claustrophobia, which described me on both counts, though the claustrophobia question gets more complicated every year. Inside was cargo hold two, and inside cargo hold two was the reason I kept coming back instead of doing what any sensible criminal would do, which is take the money and forget the coordinates.

The sensible thing. Right. I've heard of it.

* * *

Let me back up, because diving a classified wreck at first light makes more sense if you understand the economics, and the economics are simple: I am paid in cash by people who do not exist to pay me, to retrieve things that were never officially lost, from a ship that was never officially here. There's a man in the county seat—I'll call him the Notary, because that's what he calls himself, and because calling him what he probably actually is would require vocabulary I save for special occasions—who brokers these jobs. A collector wants a brass fitting. An insurance adjuster wants a manifest "misplaced." Once, memorably, a woman in very good shoes wanted a footlocker's worth of personal effects belonging to an officer whose widow was still drawing a pension based on his heroic death somewhere he never was. I brought her the footlocker. She paid triple. I didn't ask what she told the widow, because asking questions is how you end up with a name, and names are how things get found.

Cash jobs. No records. No receipts, no invoices, no paper with my handwriting on it anywhere between the mountains and the sea. I buy fuel with cash from three different stations on rotation. I sell salvage through a fence in the next county who thinks I'm Aldous's nephew and slightly slow, which suits everyone. My dive gear is secondhand and mismatched. My rifle—the one wrapped in oilcloth under the false floor of the bait well—is bought from a man who does not believe in bills of sale on religious grounds. If you made a map of my life, it would be a river with no banks: everything flows, nothing holds, nothing can be traced back to its source.

That last phrase is going to matter later. It matters now, honestly, but I didn't know it yet, standing—or rather floating—in cargo hold two with my dive light cutting a cone through water the color of strong tea.

The crates were still stacked where they'd settled when she went down, lashed in rows with military precision even after all this time, because whatever else the navy denies, they build a crate that stays lashed. Stenciled markings, mostly eaten by rust and mussel, but legible in patches. Lot numbers. Weight classes. And on maybe one crate in twenty, when the light hit it right and the growth had flaked away, a stamp I had photographed exactly once, developed the film myself in a bucket in my galley, looked at for a long time, and then burned in the same bucket.

You don't put that kind of ordnance on a river. Rivers are shallow, narrow, crowded with civilians who fish and swim and run barges full of grain. That kind of ordnance goes to sea, or it goes to war, or it goes nowhere at all. It certainly doesn't travel upriver on a naval vessel to a stretch of water where the navy maintains, in writing, in triplicate, that it has no operations, no assets, and no interest.

And yet. Forty feet down, in the dark, I counted crates.

* * *

Here's the thing about cataloging contraband: it gets into your hands. Not literally—though yes, also literally, my logbook lives in a waterproof bag taped inside the head liner of my cabin, and if you're reading this, Paloma, burn it—but into your hands the way a splinter works in. You stop noticing it until you grip something wrong, and then it's all you feel.

Six years of dives. I knew this wreck better than I've known most people. I knew that plate sixteen sang a low note when the current swung around from the northeast, and that the engine room flooded last and cleanest, and that someone had tried to scuttle her fast—seacocks opened, hatches dogged from outside—and failed, because she'd settled on the shelf instead of sliding into the channel's throat where they'd wanted her. Somebody had meant for this ship to go deep and mean, into the silt, into the forgetting. Instead she'd caught on a ledge like a drunk catching a doorframe, and lay there at forty feet, shallow enough to find, deep enough to argue about.

Shallow enough for me.

I worked the row of crates aft, running my fingers along stencil ghosts. Most of it was mundane once you got past the ordnance—crates of administrative material, filing cabinets bolted to the deck (who bolts filing cabinets to a deck unless the contents matter more than the ship?), a pallet of typewriters wrapped in waxed paper that had somehow stayed dry for a decade, which struck me as the single most bureaucratic miracle I'd ever witnessed. Typewriters. On a warship. Inland. Sunk on purpose.

I'd taken pieces of this puzzle out over the years and turned them over and put them back, the way you worry a tooth. A manifest page, water-swollen, listing cargo weights that didn't match the lot numbers stamped on the crates themselves—as if somebody had loaded one thing and written down another. A name tag off a uniform, the thread rotted, the letters gone. A mess tray. A pair of boots, size eleven, standing upright on the deck as if their owner had stepped out of them mid-thought and walked on into the dark.

That one I didn't photograph. Some things you leave.

The guilt, since we're being honest, and since nobody's listening except the mussels: I left legitimate work once. Had it, held it, was reasonably good at it—commercial recovery, insurance dives, the dull honest business of pulling other people's mistakes off the bottom for a paycheck and a W-2. I had a name then. A real one. I had a contract, four years back, on this same river, upstream section, a recovery job that ended the way contracts end when somebody's paying you to find something they'd really rather stayed found. I surfaced with what they'd hired me to find, and I saw the faces on the boat, and I understood—not slowly, not over time, but in the space between one breath and the next—that I had just become a loose end wearing a wetsuit.

So I took the money. All of it. And I took Aldous's advice, and I disappeared into something boring, and I have been boring ever since, diligently, professionally, with the discipline of a man who knows exactly what happens to interesting people on this river.

Except I keep coming back here. Six years. Every week, weather and current permitting. Cataloging. Counting crates. Photographing stamps and burning the photographs. If you asked me why—and Mara Quist will ask me why, eventually, though I don't know that yet, and wouldn't believe it if I did—I'd tell you it's about leverage. Information is currency; a man with no money needs some currency; therefore I keep a ledger of the navy's sins in a waterproof bag above my bunk. That's the story I tell myself, and it's even true, the way a skeleton is a body. But skeletons used to walk somewhere. There was a version of this that wasn't strategy. There was a night, early on, third or fourth dive, when I came up out of the black water into the gray morning and floated on my back looking at the sky and thought: somebody's son is down there next to those boots. Somebody's son, and a shipload of lies, and the river keeping all of it, keeping it the way rivers keep things, patiently, without malice, forever.

I dove again the next week. And the week after. Leverage, I told myself. Sure.

* * *

The light came through around five-forty, green-gold shafts slanting down through the channel like something being poured, and I gave it my usual ten minutes—enough to finish the row, not enough to be seen finishing it. Visibility opened up just long enough for me to see the whole hold at once, the way you occasionally see a room you've lived in for years as if for the first time: the crates in their patient rows, the filing cabinets standing at attention, the boots, the dark at the far end where the corridor ran forward toward holds I hadn't opened yet.

Holds I hadn't opened yet. Six years, and there were still holds I hadn't opened. A man could ask himself why he was rationing out a wreck, saving sections the way you ration cigarettes, making it last. A man could ask himself that and not enjoy the answer.

I checked my gauge, thumbed the release on the lift bag for the day's take—one crate of the waxed-paper typewriters, harmless, salable, the Notary's buyer in the city had a thing for obsolete office equipment, God help us all—and started up.

That's when the sound came through the water. Sound carries down here like nowhere else; you learn to read the river's acoustics the way farmers read clouds. Engines, yes—there were always engines, barges, bass boats, the ordinary traffic of an ordinary morning. But this was different. This was a single engine, big and low-revving, moving against the current at a steady crawl, the way you move when you're looking for something. Survey speed. Search pattern speed.

I killed my light and hung in the green dimness beside the hull, one glove resting on the mussels, and listened to the boat pass overhead. Close. Closer than anyone uninvited had come in six years. Through the hull I felt the thrum of it, and beneath the thrum, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, voices. Two of them. One young. One older, clipped, the cadence of a man giving orders he expected obeyed.

Then a radio crackle, thin and distorted through forty feet of water, and a fragment of sentence that reached me whole:

"—Meridian team's gone quiet. Nine days now. Nobody's answering on nine."

A pause. The older voice again, flat: "Keep looking."

They moved on upriver, toward the source country, and I hung there in the cold beside the wreck nobody names, holding a lift bag full of typewriters, feeling the splinter work deeper.

Nine days. Channel nine. I knew that voice—the older one—even filtered through steel and silt and a decade of my own careful forgetting. Knowing it was impossible. Knowing it was the one thing on this river I had arranged my entire life to avoid knowing.

I stayed down longer than the light allowed, which is a diver's way of hiding, and when I finally came up the river was empty and gold and lying beautifully, the way rivers do.

I should have gone home. I want that on the record, wherever records go when you've spent your life avoiding them: I knew, and I should have gone home.

Instead I hauled the crate aboard, fired up Aldous Greer's ancient diesel, and pointed the bow upstream—toward the landing where, as it happened, a supply runner named Ferro owed me money and was, at that exact moment, drinking coffee and rehearsing a proposition he thought I'd refuse.

He was right. I refused it.

But that's the next part of the story, and the river, patient as always, was willing to wait for me to catch up.
