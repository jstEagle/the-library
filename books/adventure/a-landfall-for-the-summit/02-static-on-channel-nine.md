## 2. Static on Channel Nine

The man who came looking for me at the fuel dock in Puerto Hondo wore the particular expression of someone who had already been told no by four other people and was hoping the fifth would be too tired to say it again. His name, he said, was Delgado, and he ran supplies upriver for outfits that needed supplies run. His boat was bigger than mine, his engine newer, and his hands were the soft hands of a man who paid other people to lift things.

I was elbow-deep in a bilge pump that had developed opinions, and I did not look up.

"You're Voss," he said.

"Nobody here is Voss."

"Nobody here is anything, I've noticed." He crouched beside me on the dock, careful of the oil, which told me he was a man who respected other people's messes even if he didn't make his own. "The woman at the cantina said the same thing. The man who sells diesel said the same thing. You all rehearse it together, or does it come naturally?"

"It comes with the rent," I said, and gave the pump a twist that made it cough. "What do you want?"

"I want to hire you. Not for salvage. For a run."

"I don't do runs."

"I know. That's why I'm here instead of at the marina, talking to people who do runs and charge accordingly." He took off his hat, which in this heat was either a sign of respect or heatstroke. "There's an expedition upriver. Scientific outfit. Meridian Foundation — you may have heard of them, they have a website."

"I don't have internet."

"Nobody up here has internet. That's sort of the point." He settled onto a crate with the sigh of a man preparing to tell a story he'd told too many times. "Nine days ago they went quiet. Radio silence. Not the usual kind, either — not the kind where they're busy or the batteries are low or the satellite's being temperamental. The kind where you call every six hours on schedule and nothing answers."

"Radios die."

"Theirs had a backup. The backup has a backup. These people were funded by universities, Voss. They had redundancy the way other people have regrets." He turned his hat in his hands. "And the last transmission — the scheduled check-in, nine days ago — it wasn't the usual weather report and headcount. The leader came on, and he sounded..."

He stopped, and I made the mistake of looking up at him, because a man like Delgado doesn't stop unless the thing he's about to say is the actual reason he's sweating through his shirt.

"He sounded what?"

"Wrong," Delgado said. "Not scared, exactly. Like a man reading something he'd just decided to read. He said they'd reached the source." He watched my face when he said it, the way you watch a wall for cracks. "He said, *it's here, it was here the whole time* — and then he said the word *source* again, or started to, and the transmission cut. Mid-word. Not faded. Cut. Like someone reached over and turned it off from his end."

The pump in my hands had stopped being interesting. I made myself keep working it anyway, because a man who stops moving when he hears a word like *source* is a man who tells the whole dock everything.

"Upriver's closed," I said. "Has been since before I was running it. You know that. Everyone knows that. It's why your scientific outfit with its redundant radios had to sneak past the barriers in the first place, and it's why nobody official is going to lift a finger to find them."

"Nobody official is going to lift a finger," Delgado agreed. "Which is why I'm talking to nobody official. You know the water above the gorge better than anyone who'll admit to knowing it. I'm offering three months of what you make in a year to go up, look, and come back and tell me what you find. Or don't find. I'm not asking you to rescue anyone. I'm asking you to look."

"And if I look and find nothing?"

"Then I've bought peace of mind at a fair price."

"And if I look and find something?"

Delgado put his hat back on. "Then I'd think carefully about what you do next. Because whatever it is, someone upriver didn't want that transmission to finish."

* * *

I told him no.

I want it on the record that I told him no, and that I told him no well — with the flat, unhurried finality of a man who has said no to better offers and worse men. I told him the river above the gorge was nobody's business, that expeditions that go where they shouldn't get what expeditions get, and that my boat was a salvage boat and my business was dead things that stay dead. I even gave him the name of a runner two landings down who might do it for half the money, knowing full well the runner would refuse, but it made my no look like generosity instead of fear, and a no that looks like generosity is a no that doesn't get examined.

Delgado took it the way I expected. He wrote a number on the back of a fuel receipt, set it under a stone on my dock, and said, "It stays good for a day," and left, and I watched his wake flatten out against the current and thought, good, that's done.

Then I sat on my deck with a beer I didn't taste and listened to the river, and the river did what it always does, which is nothing, which is everything.

Here's what nobody tells you about building a life out of silence: it holds. For years it holds. You get good at it the way you get good at anything — repetition, discipline, the slow accumulation of small refusals. No runs. No names. No radio, because a radio is a mouth and mouths get you found. My boat is registered to a man who died in 2011, and I keep his death certificate in a plastic sleeve because once, in a fuel stop up the coast, a bored harbormaster asked for papers and the certificate was the most interesting thing I owned. I pay cash. I tip well. I am, in every ledger that matters, a rumor with a boat.

And it holds, right up until it doesn't.

* * *

The thing about Delgado's story that kept snagging me wasn't the silence or the cut transmission. Cut transmissions happen. Rivers eat radios the way they eat everything else. What kept snagging me was the shape of it — a scientific expedition, funded, redundant, careful, going up a river that has been closed for forty years, and finding something at the source worth turning off a man's voice for.

Because I knew what was at the source. Or I knew what was near it. I had spent six years diving a wreck that no chart admits to and no navy claims, forty kilometers below the headwaters, in water cold enough to keep a secret. A hull with no name. Cargo that had no business being cargo. I'd catalogued it the way you catalogue anything you can't talk about — in my head, at night, over and over, until the list wore grooves in me.

And I knew — in the way you know a thing you've decided not to know — that the wreck was not the reason the river was closed. The wreck was the *evidence*. The river was closed because of what the wreck was carrying, and who had put it there, and why.

So when a funded expedition goes upriver and finds something at the source and goes silent, a man in my position has exactly one correct response, and it is the response I gave Delgado, and I gave it for free.

I finished the beer. I went to bed. The river ran all night, saying nothing, the way it has always said nothing, and I lay there listening to it not say it and thought about how a man can spend a decade being no one and how easy it is, how *restful*, how the silence fits like water fits a diver, all around you, pressing you gently into the shape of the dark.

* * *

In the morning Delgado was gone but the receipt was still under the stone, and I threw it in the river, which felt correct and ceremonial and solved nothing.

I worked. There's always work. The wreck doesn't dive itself, though God knows it's tried to drown me often enough to suggest it has opinions on the matter. I went down at slack tide with the light just coming gray through the surface, and I worked the hold where I've been slowly, methodically, over years, mapping what's there — crates with stenciling I've memorized and will not write down, a bulkhead that doesn't match the rest of the hull, the place where the metal is torn *outward*, which is a fact I have carried for six years like a stone in my boot.

And floating in the gray-green quiet, hanging on the line, waiting out my decompression with nothing to do but think — that's where it happened. That's where Delgado's story finally gave up its secret, because the mind under water, in the cold, with the wreck below you like a sleeping animal, is an honest mind, and mine finally asked the question it had been avoiding:

What was the expedition leader's name?

Delgado hadn't said it. Delgado had said *the leader* the way you say *the weather*. But Delgado had played the transmission. He'd had a recording — he told me he did, told me the foundation kept logs of every check-in, and he'd played me thirty seconds of it on a little speaker, tinny and hissing, mostly to prove the silence wasn't equipment failure. I'd barely listened. I'd been watching the dock, watching the road, watching for anyone who might be watching me. Professional habit. The voice had gone in one ear and been filed under *not my problem*.

But the voice doesn't stay filed. Voices never do.

I went up. I broke my own decompression schedule doing it, which is the kind of thing a careful man doesn't do, and I sat on the deck dripping and pulled the little receiver I keep for weather bands and river traffic — the one radio I own, because a man with no radio is a man who can't hear what's coming for him — and I sat with it in my lap and did not turn it on.

Because here is the thing I had remembered, hanging on the line with the cold pressing in: the voice on Delgado's speaker hadn't been a stranger's voice. It had been a voice with a particular flatness to it, a way of clipping the ends of words, a rhythm I knew. A voice I had heard before, years ago, across a table, in a life I don't talk about — a voice that had once told me, with that same careful flatness, *you can walk away from this, Voss, but you'll always be walking*.

I had told myself it was a coincidence. Voices are common. Accents travel. Nine days of silence and a cut transmission and a closed river and a wreck with a torn-open hold — those are just facts, and facts don't have to know each other.

But the voice had said *it was here the whole time*, and I knew — the way you know a current under a calm surface — that it had been talking about my wreck. About the thing I had spent six years keeping. And I knew the voice, and knowing the voice meant the expedition wasn't science, and the silence wasn't accident, and my no — my careful, generous, rehearsed no — had been exactly what someone upriver was counting on.

I turned the radio on. Found the band. The river hissed the way it always hisses, static on every channel, the sound of a wilderness declining to comment.

And then, faint, on the emergency frequency, in that flat, clipped voice I would know drowned — a voice I had last heard in a room where I signed things I shouldn't have signed — a voice said:

"...anyone receiving, this is Quist, Meridian expedition, if you're hearing this—"

And cut.

I sat there a long time. Long enough for the sun to move. Long enough for the static to become the only sound in the world.

Daniel Quist. The man who had told me I'd always be walking.

I went below, and I started pulling the dive gear out of the salvage crates where I hide it, and I told myself it was for the money, and I told myself it was for the silence, and I told myself every lie a man tells when he's already three lies past the truth — which is that some part of me has been waiting nine years for a reason, any reason, to point the bow upriver.
