## 3. What the River Keeps

The boat's name is *Delia Mae*, painted on the transom in letters that have faded to the color of old teeth. Delia Mae was never a real person. I bought the registration off a probate clerk in a river town two hundred miles downstream, a dead woman whose family wanted the paperwork gone more than they wanted it accurate. She has been my legal identity on the water for six years now, and in all that time she has never once complained about the company.

I spent the morning loading her like a man packing for a funeral he'd rather not attend.

That's the thing about prepping for a run upriver — everything you put aboard is a decision you're making about how you might die. Water jugs mean you expect to be out past easy resupply. Spare prop means you expect to hit something. A rifle wrapped in an oilcloth and slid into the hollow of a salvage winch housing means you expect company you didn't invite.

I did all three.

* * *

The dive gear went in last, because I kept hoping I'd change my mind about bringing it.

Two tanks, regulator serviced by a man in Port Anselm who asks no questions because I pay him triple and once fixed his roof during a storm without being asked. Wetsuit rolled tight as a sleeping snake. Mask, fins, the little brass knife I've carried since before any of this — since back when I had a name people said out loud in offices with air conditioning.

I stowed the lot under the false floor of the forward locker, beneath forty pounds of chain and a coil of anchor line that would take any casual searcher ten minutes to move and give them every reason to stop looking. Chain is honest work. Nobody suspects a man who hauls chain.

"You're doing it again," I told myself, cinching the strap.

Doing what? Well. Talking to myself, for one. But also: cataloging. I've got a habit, professional deformation maybe, of inventorying my own motives the way I'd inventory a hold. Item one: one (1) expedition, silent nine days. Item two: one (1) voice on a radio recording that I have now listened to eleven times. Item three: one (1) reason to stay home, weight unknown, depth unknown, still down there after all these years.

The voice was Elias Vann. That's what kept me awake through the small hours, staring at the ceiling beams of my shack while the river muttered its one long sentence against the pilings. Elias Vann, who taught a younger, stupider version of me how to read current from the color of the water. Elias Vann, who signed off his transmissions with *clear and calm* the way other men sign off with *over and out*, because he believed — genuinely, insufferably believed — that the river rewarded composure.

Nine days of static where his voice should be.

I could have stayed home. Let me be clear about that, since I'll be lying to other people later and I'd like at least one account of this to be true. The supply runner, Okafor, offered good money and I turned it down flat, and I meant it when I turned it down, and then I lay in the dark listening to that recording one more time and heard something I'd missed the first ten passes.

At the very end of the transmission, under the cut-off word — *the sour*— there's a second sound. Faint. Underwater, or near water. A boom, muffled, the kind of sound a river makes when something big gives way somewhere upstream. Or the kind of sound a charge makes when somebody wants a cave mouth closed.

Elias Vann didn't go quiet. He was made quiet.

So here I am, loading a rifle into a winch housing.

* * *

There's a version of my life where none of this happens, and it starts with a contract I took eleven years ago, and I've done a decent job of not thinking about it for roughly ten years and eleven months, which is a personal record I was hoping to extend indefinitely.

But the river keeps things. That's the first thing anyone who works water learns. Rivers keep silt and engines and wedding rings and the occasional car with a story attached. And this particular river keeps a particular thing, at a particular bend, in eighty feet of cold green murk, and eleven years ago a man with a government letterhead and a briefcase full of cash asked me to go down and look at it and tell him what I saw.

I was thirty-one. I had a reputation, a partner named Corliss whom I trusted more than God, and a mortgage I'd been stupid enough to take out on dry land. The contract came through a broker who dealt in recovery jobs that legitimate firms wouldn't touch. Salvage, they called it. Location survey, they called it. What it actually was, I understood within four minutes of my first descent.

A ship. Navy gray under the silt, though no navy admitted ever putting hull in this water. Ninety meters of her, broken amidships, sitting in a gorge so narrow the sonar had missed her for decades because the walls confused the return. And in her hold — I want to say cargo, but cargo implies commerce — crates. Stenciled crates. And paperwork in waterproof cases, and things I didn't have the classification clearance to understand and didn't need it either, because some things explain themselves.

Corliss dove with me. We came up the second day with our lights dying and our heads full of questions we were already learning to regret.

"They sank her," Corliss said on the surface, spitting his regulator taste into the current. "You don't scuttle your own ship in a river nobody patrols unless you're burying something."

"Maybe she ran aground."

"Paloma." He looked at me the way you look at a friend who's reaching for a comforting lie. "There are blast marks on the keel."

There were. I'd seen them. I'd touched them, ran my glove along the torn steel, felt the petals of metal bent outward — outward, mind, from inside — and I had come up and said *maybe she ran aground* because the man with the briefcase was watching from the tender and paying by the day.

That's the part I keep weighted down. Not the wreck. The wreck was just steel and bad history. It was the moment on the surface, water streaming off my hood, when I chose the mortgage over the truth, and said nothing, and shook the man's hand, and watched him pay Corliss double what he paid me because Corliss had asked one question too many and needed to be made comfortable.

Three months later Corliss drowned in a lake two hundred miles away. Single diver, no buddy, experienced man, calm conditions. The report said equipment failure. I read it four times and every time it said the same thing, in the same clean bureaucratic grammar, and I understood that the report wasn't describing an accident. It was closing a file.

I sold the house. I let my certifications lapse. I became a ghost with a boat registered to a dead woman, and I told myself the retreat was prudence, was wisdom, was a man finally learning the water's first lesson: don't fight the current.

It wasn't. It was a bribe I paid myself with silence, and I've been living on the proceeds ever since.

* * *

By late afternoon the *Delia Mae* sat low on her lines, loaded for a week upstream. Fuel drums lashed amidships. Food in sealed pails. Tools distributed with a paranoid's logic — nothing essential in any single place, so that losing any one compartment wouldn't end me. The rifle slept in the winch housing. The dive gear slept under the chain.

I stood on the dock with a cup of coffee going cold and did the arithmetic I always do before a run. Draft: eighteen inches light, twenty-six loaded; the upper shallows above Kiln Bend run thinner than that in a dry season, and this had been a dry season. Current: moderate now, murderous after rain. Distance to the Meridian's last coordinates: three days' hard running if the gorges behaved, five if they didn't.

And the fourth number, the one that doesn't fit on any chart: eleven years since I'd pointed my bow at that bend, and every year the river between here and there getting longer, the way rivers do in memory.

A kingfisher worked the far bank, blue bolt stitching the shadows. Somewhere behind me a truck door slammed at the fuel shed, and I did the thing I always do — turned my head slow, marked the vehicle, marked the man, filed him under *known* before I let my shoulders drop. Six years of practice. You can unmake a name faster than you can make one, but staying unmade takes daily labor.

The man was just the fuel man. For now.

Here's what I know about ghosts, though, standing there with my coffee and my dead woman's boat: they don't get to choose their hauntings. I spent a decade building a life with no handles on it — no records, no debts, no attachments a subpoena could find. Smooth all over. Nothing to grab. And it worked, right up until a supply runner played me nine seconds of a dead-silent channel and a voice I knew said *the source* and stopped mid-word, and every handle I'd spent years removing grew back at once.

Because it was Elias Vann. Because it was that bend of river. Because whatever's up there went silent with a boom underneath it, and I am possibly the only man on this water who knows what a buried thing sounds like when someone decides it needs burying deeper.

* * *

I cast off at dusk, which is when I do everything, darkness being the ghost's native element. The *Delia Mae* slid off the dock and the current took her bow the way it takes everything, patiently, without malice, and I stood at the wheel and let it.

Upriver, the hills close in. That's the direction I'm headed, into the narrowing, toward the place the maps start apologizing for themselves. Behind me the landing lights shrank to a smudge, then a rumor, then nothing, and the river opened ahead black and moving and keeping its secrets the way it always has.

Somewhere up there, nine days deep into silence, was a scientific expedition that had gone looking for answers. Somewhere deeper than that, under eighty feet of green murk at a bend I could navigate blind, was the answer they'd found — or its twin, or its cause, or its corpse. And somewhere in the middle distance, riding down that same current toward me whether I liked it or not, was everything I'd sunk in 2013 and paid good money to leave there.

I thought I'd weighted it down properly. Professional job. Clean drop.

But bodies come up, eventually. They always come up. The river keeps things, yes — but only until it decides otherwise, and it has never once consulted me on the schedule.

Around the first bend, where the channel narrows and the current picks up its pace like a man late for an appointment, I saw a light on the far bank. Small. Stationary. The kind of light a person uses when they're waiting for a boat and don't care who knows it.

I hadn't told anyone I was coming.
