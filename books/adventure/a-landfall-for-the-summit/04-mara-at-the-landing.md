## 4. Mara at the Landing

The landing at Puerto Ceniza was less a town than an apology—a strip of mud and corrugated tin where the road gave up and the river took over. I'd been coming here for six years, long enough to know which of the three fuel pumps worked, which of the two bars would water my beer, and which of the locals would sell me ice without asking what it was for. It was the last place on the map with a name, and even that name was a lie. Ceniza means ash. There had never been a fire here worth the word.

I tied off the *Salacia* at the downstream mooring, away from the traffic, because that's how you do things when your whole life is built on not being noticed. The morning was thick with river heat already, the kind that makes your shirt a second skin by eight o'clock, and the air smelled of diesel, wet rope, and the green rot of the floodplain. Somewhere upstream, nine days' run through country no map bothered to detail accurately, a man whose voice I hadn't heard in eleven years was either dead or wishing he were.

I told myself I'd come to Puerto Ceniza for fuel. That was true in the way that most of my lies are true—they just leave out the load-bearing parts.

* * *

The fuel took twenty minutes and cost me three conversations I didn't want. Old Ferro at the pump wanted to know if I'd heard about the expedition—the scientists, the ones who went up past the markers and stopped answering their radio. Everyone at the landing wanted to talk about it. It was the only news the river had produced in months, and rivers are jealous mistresses; they don't like sharing the stage.

"Nine days," Ferro said, watching the gauge instead of me, which is his version of tact. "Nine days of nothing. The company that hired them sent a man down last week. He asked around, paid some people, left again." He shrugged the shrug of a man who has outlived every consequence he was ever promised. "Nobody here will take anyone up there now. Not for money."

"Smart," I said.

"You going up?"

The question hung there the way questions do when everyone already knows the answer is none of their business. I fed the pump another bill. "I'm going diving," I said. "South channel. Wreck nobody's claimed."

Ferro looked at me then, briefly, with the flat patience of a man who has watched liars come and go for forty years and found most of them harmless. "There's a woman at the Cantina Azul," he said. "Been there three days. Asking after boats. Offering stupid money." He handed back my change. "She's asking about you specifically."

The world did one of those slow tilts it does sometimes, like a boat taking water you haven't found yet. "Who told her about me?"

"This is a landing, Paloma. Everything here tells everything else." He wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than I'd been alive. "She said her name was Quist."

* * *

I should have walked back to the boat, cast off, and pointed the bow south until the whole business was someone else's problem. That was the plan. That had always been the plan—six years of plans shaped exactly like that one, plans with silence as their keel and invisibility as their sail. I'd gotten good at them. Good enough to almost believe them.

Instead I found myself standing outside the Cantina Azul at nine in the morning, looking through its open front wall—it wasn't so much a wall as a suggestion of one, mosquito mesh and open shutters—at a woman sitting alone at a table with her back to the corner, facing the door, a habit that marks people who've learned something about the world they didn't want to learn.

She was maybe thirty, sunburned across the cheekbones in a way that said she'd been outdoors for weeks without the sense to stop, dark hair pulled back in a tail that had given up on elegance sometime last week. She wore field clothes gone soft with washing, boots that had seen actual work, and she sat with the particular stillness of someone who has been waiting so long that waiting has become a discipline rather than a state. A satellite phone sat on the table in front of her like a piece of evidence. Next to it, a battered laptop in a case scarred with airline stickers.

She saw me before I decided whether to be seen. Her eyes did a quick inventory—boat shoes salt-bleached, dive knife at my belt, the particular tan line of a man who wears a wetsuit top more often than a shirt—and whatever conclusion she reached, she reached it fast.

"You're Paloma Voss." Not a question. She said my name the way you'd read it off a manifest.

"I'm nobody," I said. "You've got the wrong landing."

"I don't think I do." She didn't stand. Smart. Standing makes it a confrontation; staying seated keeps it a conversation, and conversations can be ended. "Sit down or don't. But hear me out first, because I've spent three days and most of my cash learning that every boatman on this river would rather lie to me than earn from me, and I'm done being polite about it."

Against every instinct I own—which is a short list, but well-curated—I sat down.

"Mara Quist," she said, though we both knew Ferro had already sold her name cheaper than a beer. "My brother Daniel led the Meridian expedition. Nine days ago they went silent. The last transmission—" she tapped the satellite phone without picking it up, careful, the way you're careful with a wound, "—was forty-one seconds. He named coordinates. He said the words 'the source,' and then the transmission cut mid-word. Mid-word, Mr. Voss. Daniel never left a sentence unfinished in his life. He finished other people's sentences. He finished crossword puzzles in pen."

"What do you want from me?"

"A ride." She leaned forward. "That's all. I'm not asking you to search, to rescue, to play hero. I'm asking for transport to the last set of coordinates, and I'll pay—"

"No."

"—whatever—"

"No." I said it again, flatter this time, the way you'd set down something heavy. "Whatever you think this river is, it isn't. Whatever you think happened to your brother's expedition, the answer isn't upriver, and even if it were, upriver is not a place you get to by hiring the first drunk with a hull. Every boatman here refused you because every boatman here is smarter than you are. I'm just honest about it."

Something moved behind her eyes—not hurt. Calculation. She was re-racking me, the way you rack a rifle, checking where I actually stood against where I'd claimed to.

"Ferro says you know the upper river better than anyone alive," she said. "Says you ran the Gorge of Bells solo once, in flood season, and came back down with a dent in your hull and no explanation."

"Ferro also waters his beer. We all have our fictions."

"He says you dove a wreck up there once. Years ago. Under contract."

The cantina noise seemed to drop away, the way sound does underwater. "Ferro talks too much."

"He does," she agreed. "But he doesn't invent. I checked him against three other sources." She turned the satellite phone around and woke the screen. A coordinate pair glowed there, seven digits deep into country that officially did not exist. "Daniel sent these to me four hours before the last transmission. No message attached. Just coordinates. He knew something was wrong, and he trusted me with the location and nothing else, because Daniel believed information was like medicine—you give the dose, not the bottle." Her voice stayed level, but I heard the thing under it, the thing she was holding down with both hands. "He's alive. I'd know—I mean that as a fact of evidence, not sentiment. If he were dead, whoever killed him wouldn't have left the camp burned and the cache untouched. They'd have cleaned it. Someone wants us to think nature got them. Nature doesn't burn tents."

I looked at the coordinates. I looked at her hands—steady, chapped, nails cut short like a climber's or a surgeon's. I looked at the door, which was right there, which was always right there.

"You've been refused by how many boats?" I asked.

"Eleven."

"And you're still sitting here."

"I'm still sitting here," she said, "because twelve seemed like a bad number to stop on."

Despite everything—the dread, the old guilt doing its familiar crawl up the back of my neck, the certain knowledge that every hour I spent near this woman was an hour spent walking toward the thing I'd spent six years walking away from—I laughed. It came out rusty, like a winch that hadn't turned in a season.

"That's not courage," I told her. "That's stubbornness with a budget."

"They're the same thing past a certain point," Mara Quist said. "Ask any river."

* * *

I told her I'd think about it, which was true, and left, which was necessary, and walked the mooring line south along the bank with my thoughts moving faster than my feet. The *Salacia* sat low and patient on her lines, registered to a dead man named Efraim Costa, insured by no one, taxed by no government that could find her, carrying below her deck plates a cargo hold full of equipment that answered no useful questions about how a salvage diver comes to own a recompression chamber and a rifle locker. Six years of careful architecture. One woman with a satellite phone, and the whole structure creaked.

Here is what I didn't tell her, and what I couldn't stop telling myself: I knew that voice on the relayed recording the supply runner had played me. Nine days of static on Channel Nine, and then a supply runner named Ovidio plays me thirty seconds of the expedition's final transmission, hoping I'll take the contract, and the voice on it—clipped, tired, precise even while frightened—was a voice I'd heard across a chart table in another life, giving me a heading I should never have followed.

Daniel Quist had been my handler's second choice, back when I had a handler. Or maybe his first. The memory was a body I'd weighted down years ago, and it was surfacing now the way bodies do—slowly, and always feet first.

If Daniel Quist was dead, then part of my past had died with him, and part of my debt had died uncollected, and I could go on being nobody until nobody was all that was left of me.

And if he was alive—

If he was alive, then somewhere upriver a man who knew my real name was waiting to be found, and finding him meant becoming findable myself.

I stood at the rail a long time. The river went about its business below me, brown and patient, keeping everything it kept.

* * *

She was on my dock when I came back. Of course she was. She had a duffel bag at her feet and the laptop case over her shoulder and the expression of a person who has already won an argument that hasn't started yet.

"I thought about it," I said.

"I know. I watched you think about it from the bar window. You paced."

"Pacing is thinking."

"Pacing is deciding slowly." She picked up the duffel. "I talked to Ferro while you were brooding. He says you're the best pilot on this stretch and the worst liar, and that you'll say no twice and yes on the third ask, because you're a decent man pretending very hard otherwise."

"Ferro," I said, "is going to die of honesty someday, and it will serve him right."

"So this is the third ask." She held my gaze, and hers was the kind of gaze that doesn't look away first, doesn't soften, doesn't negotiate—it just stands there like terrain. "Take me upriver, Paloma Voss. To the coordinates. That's the whole contract. What happens after that is my problem, not yours."

"It'll be your problem for about four minutes," I said, "and then it'll be mine, because nothing on this river stays anybody's own problem for longer than that."

"Then we'll share it." She stepped onto the deck without being invited, and the *Salacia* rocked once under her weight, accepting her the way the river accepts everything—without comment, without permission, permanently. "Two stubborn wills, one current. Ferro's phrase, actually. He's got a poet buried under all that beer-watering."

I looked at her for a long moment. She looked back. Behind her, the landing went on with its small commerce of diesel and rumor; ahead of us, upriver, waited the gorge, the burned camp, the source, and every weighted-down thing I had ever hoped to leave sunk.

"One condition," I said finally. "You follow my rules on the water. When I say run the rapids, we run them. When I say hide, we hide. When I say turn back—"

"When you say turn back," she said, "I'll consider it carefully and probably refuse."

"That's not how conditions work."

"That's how conditions work between equals." She dropped her duffel on the deck plates with a thump that sounded, to my ear, exactly like a hatch closing on six quiet years. "You know the river. I know my brother. Neither of us knows what's up there. Which makes us even, and even is the only fair place to start."

I cast off the lines because arguing with her on the dock would have taken longer than arguing with her underway, and underway I'd at least have the engine noise to hide behind. The *Salacia* swung her bow upstream, into the current, and the current, as it always does, had opinions.

We made the first bend together, and that's when I saw it—far up the channel, where the brown water braided around a gravel bar, a boat wake. Wide, deliberate, running parallel to ours, half a mile off and holding pace.

Somebody else was headed for the source.

Mara saw it too. She raised the satellite phone, checked the coordinates against the shoreline, and said, quietly, "Tell me that's a fisherman."

I throttled down and watched the wake hold its line, patient as a signature, and I knew—with the certainty of a man who has spent years learning what pursuit looks like—that whatever waited for us upriver had already begun.
