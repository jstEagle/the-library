## 5. Rapids and Reckonings

The gorge announced itself an hour before we saw it, the way trouble always does. First the water changed its mind about being a river and started rehearsing to be something else entirely—a long, low thunder rolling up through the hull like the boat had swallowed a train. Then the banks began to close in, granite shoulders leaning over us, and the current picked up its pace from *walking* to *running* to *why are we still in this conversation.*

Mara felt it before I said anything. She'd been sitting on the cooler since dawn, working her way through my coffee supply with the entitlement of someone who'd never once considered that coffee might be rationed, and when the sound came she set down her cup and looked at me with the expression of a woman doing math she didn't like.

"That's not a waterfall," she said.

"No."

"Good. Because I've seen what you do with waterfalls."

"You haven't seen anything yet," I said, which was true in more ways than one.

I killed the engine and let us drift while I read the water. This was the part people don't understand about running rapids—the actual running is maybe ten percent of it, and it's the ten percent where you're too busy to be scared. The other ninety percent is standing still, looking at chaos until your eyes find the order hiding inside it. Every rapid is a sentence. Most of them are lying to you, but they all have one true line buried somewhere: the seam of green water, the tongue of smooth current threading between the white, the one path where the river, for whatever reason, decided to be merciful.

This one was a long sentence. The gorge ran better than two kilometers through rock that had been folding itself for a hundred million years and hadn't finished yet. Standing waves stacked up mid-channel like the river was trying to build stairs to nowhere. And at the bottom, where the walls pinched tightest, there was a hole—a keeper, the kind of hydraulic that takes a boat, holds it underwater, and thinks about it for a while.

But there was a seam. Left of center, hugging the north wall so close you could shave with it, a ribbon of green wound down through all that violence like a lie told well enough to pass inspection.

"There," I said, pointing. "We thread that."

Mara followed my finger. Her face did something complicated—fear and arithmetic fighting for control—and then settled into a flat calm that I recognized, because I'd worn it myself in another life. It's the face of a person who has decided that panic is a luxury item and they can't afford it this quarter.

"That's six inches from the wall," she said.

"Four, probably."

"Wonderful."

"If it helps, the alternative is the hole at the bottom, and the hole doesn't kill you right away. It keeps you. Like a subscription."

"It doesn't help."

"It rarely does." I went forward to check the lashings—dive gear under the tarp, the rifle wrapped in a salvage net nobody would look twice at, fuel cans wedged and double-strapped. Everything cinched down for the kind of day where the river gets a vote. "Here's what happens now. You sit behind me, low, hands inside the boat, and you hold onto that cleat like it owes you money. When I say lean, you lean. When I say nothing, that means everything's fine even if it doesn't look fine."

"And if we capsize?"

"We won't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." I turned the engine over and let it idle, listening to it the way you'd listen to a horse before asking it for something unreasonable. The old Evinrude had opinions but no grudges, which put it ahead of most people I'd known. "Last chance to swim back to civilization."

"I can't swim back to civilization. Civilization is forty kilometers downstream and I don't swim."

"Then I guess we're committed."

She sat. She took the cleat. And she didn't say another word, which was the moment I first thought—way down under the adrenaline, in some cellar of my mind I wasn't ready to open—that Mara Quist might be exactly as stubborn as her brother, and that this might turn out to matter.

I put us into the current.

The first kilometer was loud but honest. Big water, big waves, nothing trying to trick us—I drove straight into the troughs and let the boat climb them like a dog shaking off bathwater, and each time we came down the hull slammed flat and took it, and Mara made a sound that was half gasp and half laugh and entirely involuntary. The spray came over the bow in sheets. The canyon walls threw our own noise back at us doubled, so it sounded like a fleet running the gorge instead of one rotten boat and two people who should have known better.

Then the walls pinched, and the river stopped being honest.

Everything narrowed at once. The seam I'd marked was still there—green and smooth and narrow as a hallway—but the approach to it was guarded by a lateral wave coming off the south bank, a wall of water traveling sideways across the channel like a bouncer who'd decided we weren't on the list. Hit it wrong and it would flip us; hit it right and it would throw us toward the north wall faster than I could correct.

There was no third option. There's never a third option. That's the thing the river teaches you if you live with it long enough: choice is mostly just deciding which force you'd rather be handled by.

"Lateral coming from the right," I shouted. "When it hits, we go left. Don't fight it."

"When it hits? How will I know when it—"

It hit.

The world tilted. For one full second we were a sailboat without a sail, heeled over far enough that I could see the riverbed through the clear strip along the gunwale, stones the size of fists rolling past underneath us like the contents of some enormous pocket. Then the wave let go and dumped us hard to port, exactly where I wanted to be, and I gunned the engine and swung the nose into the seam with maybe a hand's width of daylight between the hull and the granite.

Rock screamed along the starboard side—a long, grinding shriek that went through my teeth and out the back of my skull. Not a hit. A *kiss.* The difference between the two, in shallow water, is about four knots and your entire future.

And then the keeper rose up ahead of us, that hungry fold in the river at the bottom of the gorge, and the seam delivered us directly past its lip close enough that I could see down into it—see the boil, the recirculating foam turning over on itself like something digesting—and then we were through, and the gorge opened its hands, and the river went calm so fast it felt like being forgiven.

We drifted in the pool below the rapids, engine ticking, both of us soaked to the skin and breathing like we'd run the distance ourselves.

Mara looked at me. Water ran off her chin. Her hair was plastered to her head in a way that would have been funny if my heart weren't still trying to exit through my ears.

"You kissed the wall," she said.

"I said hello."

"You *screamed* along the wall for eleven seconds."

"That was the boat screaming. Boats are dramatic."

Something happened to her face then—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile would build later, once she'd had time to pour the foundation. She wrung out her sleeve and looked away at the canyon walls, and I heard her say, quietly, almost to herself:

"Danny would have loved that."

I didn't answer. Some doors you leave shut until you know what's behind them.

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bar below the gorge, and the next morning the river gave us the cache.

I'd have missed it if Mara hadn't caught the color. We were running a slow bend, the water glassy and brown-green, the banks thick with ceiba and strangler fig, when she stood up so fast the boat rocked and said, "Stop. Stop—go back."

Tucked into a cut in the bank, half-hidden by a curtain of hanging roots, was an orange dry bag lashed to a tree at chest height. Expedition orange. The kind of gear government science budgets buy and grad students carry. Below it, half-swallowed by the undergrowth, a stack of plastic cases sat in the mud with a tarp thrown over them—neatly, deliberately, corners aligned.

I backed us in against the current, nosing the bow into the cut, and Mara was over the side before I'd gotten the throttle to neutral, wading thigh-deep with her satellite phone already out like a weapon.

The cache was intact. That was the first wrong thing.

Rations—still sealed, still stacked by meal type, the way organized people organize. Water filters in their boxes. A medical kit with the cross unbroken. Two tents in compression sacks. A solar panel folded with its cable coiled on top. Somebody had packed this camp out—or packed it *in*—with care, with method, with the particular tidiness of people who expected to come back for it.

Nobody comes back for it. That's the second wrong thing. Nine days silent, and every calorie they'd cached was still here, untouched, rotting politely in its vacuum seals.

"They walked away," Mara said. She was crouched over the cases, not touching them, reading them like a diver reads a wreck. "Danny was—God, he was *fanatical* about caches. You stage them heavy, you stage them often, because if something goes wrong upstream, this is what keeps you alive. He wouldn't leave this. He couldn't. Unless—"

"Unless leaving it was the plan."

"Who walks away from their food?"

"People who aren't planning to need it." I pulled the tarp back the rest of the way. Underneath, stenciled on the top case: MERIDIAN EXPEDITION — Q.D. QUIST, LEAD. And beneath the stencil, in marker, in handwriting that slanted hard to the right, someone had written a single word:

*UPSTREAM.*

No date. No direction beyond the obvious. No note about numbers or condition or intent. Just the word, urgent enough to underline twice, and then nothing.

Mara photographed everything. Her hands were steady on the phone, but her voice had gone thin. "If they moved fast, they'd travel light. If they moved *scared*, they'd move fast." She looked up at me. "Paloma. If they found something—if whoever burned that camp upstream came after them—they'd run light and leave the weight. But Danny would have taken the drive. He'd have taken the data. Where's the data?"

"Maybe upstream."

"Maybe *taken.*"

That possibility sat between us in the boat for a long moment, wearing its boots.

I repacked the tarp the way I'd found it—no sense advertising that anyone had been here—and took only what wouldn't be missed: a handful of ration bars, a spare filter cartridge, a coil of cord. Mara watched me do it and didn't object, which told me she was learning how the river works: you take small, you leave small, you stay a rumor.

We pushed back out into the current, and for the next hour neither of us said much. The unease had changed shape overnight. Yesterday it had been a stone in the shoe—uncomfortable, ignorable. Today it was the feeling you get on a dive when the visibility drops and you realize the thing breathing beside you in the dark might not be your dive partner.

Because here's what kept circling me: the cache was neat. Burned camps aren't neat. Panicked flights aren't neat. Whatever had happened to Daniel Quist's team had happened in a way that left time to tidy up—or happened to people who believed, right up until the end, that they were coming back.

People who trust the wrong thing completely are the ones who leave the neatest scenes. I've stood in a few.

Late afternoon, the river bent west and the light came down the channel flat and gold, and Mara finally spoke again.

"The word. *Upstream.* He wrote it like a warning."

"He wrote it like a destination."

"Those can be the same thing out here."

"Yeah," I said. "They usually are."

Ahead of us, the channel narrowed toward a stand of dead snags rising out of the water like the masts of a fleet that had sunk standing up, and beyond them, faint under the smell of wet wood and river mud, I caught something else on the air.

Smoke.

Not our smoke. Old smoke, cold smoke, the ghost of a fire that had done its work and been put out on purpose.

Mara smelled it a beat after I did. She went very still, phone in her hand, and when she looked at me her eyes had gone from fear to something harder and more useful.

"How far?" she asked.

"Around the next bend," I said, and throttled down, and we drifted toward it together.
