## 6. The Burned Camp

The river told us before we saw it.

Smoke has a signature, if you've spent enough years reading water and the air above it. Woodsmoke drifts lazy, domestic, a supper smell. This was different—acrid, chemical, the ghost of something that had been encouraged to burn rather than allowed to. It hung in the gorge like a rumor neither of us wanted to repeat out loud.

Mara smelled it too. She'd been quiet since the cache—the neat, untouched, abandoned cache—and quiet from her was worse than talking. Talking meant she still believed words could fix things. Silence meant she'd started doing arithmetic.

"That's them," she said finally. "That's Daniel's second camp. The coordinates put it just past this bend."

"Could be anyone's fire," I said, because it's my job to say stupid things in a hopeful voice.

"It's their smoke," she said, and I didn't argue, because she was right.

I brought us around the bend slow, engine at an idle, hugging the shadowed bank where the current ran slack under an overhang of alder. The camp sat on a gravel shelf maybe forty meters above the waterline—a good site, dry, defensible, with sightlines down both channels of the river. Daniel Quist had chosen it well. Whoever burned it had chosen it better.

The tents were gone. Not collapsed, not abandoned—*gone*, reduced to ash circles and the skeletal ribs of aluminum poles that had melted into modern art. The fire had been thorough in a way forest fires aren't. Forest fires are opportunists; they take what's dry and skip what's wet. This fire had been fed. Piled. Tended. Someone had stacked every scrap of nylon and paper and plastic in the center of the camp and given it fuel and time.

"They burned the food too?" Mara said, standing in the bow with her binoculars up. "Who burns food?"

"Somebody who doesn't want you eating it later," I said. "Or somebody making a point."

"What kind of point?"

"The kind that says *we were here, we took our time, and we're not worried about who sees.*"

I nosed the boat into the shallows below the shelf and killed the engine. The silence came down like a lid. Up here the river ran quieter, wide and brown-green over gravel bars, and the only sounds were water and birdsong and, faintly, the tick of cooling nothing, because there was nothing left to cool.

"I'm going up," Mara said.

"We're both going up. Stay behind me and step where I step."

"You're not my bodyguard, Voss."

"No," I said. "I'm the guy who knows which ground is lying to him. Bodyguarding is a hobby."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked at the ash again and the smile died without ever being born, and we climbed.

* * *

The gravel crunched underfoot with the particular loudness of gravel that knows it's being walked on by people who shouldn't be there. I kept us off the main path—there was a main path, worn by boots, and I didn't like how fresh it looked—and circled through the scrub until we came at the camp from above, from the rocks, the way I'd want to come at any place that might have teeth.

The teeth were all gone. Just the burn, and the ash, and the wrongness.

Up close, the fire told its story plainly. There'd been three tents—I counted stake holes, four per tent, twelve holes in the packed dirt, plus a cook fly. A table made of planks on barrels, now charred at one end where someone had set the fire against it deliberately, feeding the flame with the table itself. A fire ring with weeks of use in it, coffee tins, a tin of condensed milk punctured neatly with two holes, the way field men do it. And everywhere, the fine gray lace of paper ash, curled and weightless, drifting when we moved. Documents. Notebooks. Maps. All of it fed to the flames, and the flames had done honest work.

Except whoever built this fire had missed one thing, or left one thing, or—one thing, anyway.

The tent stake stood dead center in the largest ash circle, driven straight down into the earth with a rock, or a boot heel, or a great deal of conviction. It wasn't holding anything down. There was nothing left for it to hold. It was just standing there, upright, absurd, like a flagpole for a country that no longer existed.

And impaled on it, folded twice, weatherproofed under a scrap of clear plastic weighted with stones—

A chart.

Mara saw it before I did. She crossed the ash in four strides, and I let her, because some things a person has to walk to themselves. She knelt, lifted the plastic, unfolded the chart with hands that shook and then, visibly, decided not to.

It was a hydrographic survey chart of the middle river. Official issue, the kind her brother would have carried as expedition lead. Someone had marked it in grease pencil—a position fix, precise, annotated with depth soundings and a notation in a tight engineer's hand: *hull section consistent w/ naval construction, length est. 60m, cargo hold anomaly, see photos.*

The wreck. My wreck. Marked and measured and written down in her brother's handwriting, and then driven onto a stake in the middle of his own burned camp like a trophy, or a message, or a receipt.

"He found it," Mara said. Her voice was very level, which is how you can tell when a person is one degree from not being level at all. "He actually found it. He surveyed it. He—" She stopped. Started again. "Paloma. Why is this the one thing they didn't burn?"

I crouched beside her and looked at the stake, really looked at it. Galvanized steel, standard expedition issue, same as the twelve empty holes. Taken from their own kit. Driven into the ground through the chart, pinning it to the earth the way you'd pin a butterfly, or a threat.

"Because burning it would be getting rid of evidence," I said slowly, working it through as I said it, the way you work a splinter toward the surface. "This isn't getting rid of evidence. This is *displaying* it. Somebody wanted the next people who came through—you, a search party, anybody—to find this exact thing, exactly like this."

"Why? What does it tell them?"

"It tells them he knew." I stood up. My knees cracked, middle-aged and unheroic. "It tells them Daniel had located the wreck, documented it, and understood what it was. And it tells them that whoever did this *knew what he knew.* You don't burn a man's camp and leave a marked chart unless the chart means something to you. Unless finding it scared you enough to come all the way up this river and do this."

Mara looked up at me. "You're saying they weren't looking for the expedition."

"I'm saying the expedition was never the point. The *wreck* was the point. Daniel found the thing somebody sank thirty years ago and never wanted found again, and somebody found out, and this—" I gestured at the ash, the melted poles, the punctured milk tin, the whole tidy theater of it— "this is what happens to people who find it."

The wind moved through the alders. Somewhere downslope, the river went on minding its own business, which struck me as deeply irresponsible of it.

Mara refolded the chart along its original creases, careful as a surgeon, and put it inside her jacket, against her body, where a person keeps the things they've decided to bleed for.

"Then we keep going," she said.

"Mara—"

"Don't." She stood, and there it was, the thing I'd seen at the landing and seen again in the rapids: that stubbornness with bedrock under it. "Don't tell me it's dangerous. Don't tell me to turn back. My brother drove a stake through his own map to leave me a message, and the message is *keep going,* and I am going to keep going, and you can drop me at the next landing if that's a problem for you."

I looked at her for a long moment. Ash drifted between us like gray snow.

"It's not a problem," I said. "But if we're doing this, we do it awake. From here on, we anchor midstream, not at the banks. We run the boat at first light and last light, when the shadows are long and hard to read across. We don't build fires. We don't talk above a murmur after dark. And if I say *down,* you get down before you finish wondering why. Agreed?"

She nodded once. "Agreed."

"Good. Because there's one more thing this camp is telling us, and you're not going to like it."

Her eyes narrowed. "What?"

"How long do you think it takes to burn a camp like this? Thoroughly? Feeding the fire, stacking the fuel, making sure the metal warps and the paper goes to lace?" I tipped my head at the ash. "Hours. Minimum. Somebody stood right here, in the open, on a river with traffic on it, and spent hours making sure this job was done properly. That's not a hit-and-run. That's confidence."

"Confidence in what?"

"In nobody coming. In nobody asking. In the river keeping its mouth shut, the way it always has." I turned to head back down to the boat, and that was when I saw it.

* * *

The far channel runs maybe six hundred meters across from the camp shelf, curving away behind a long island of boulders and deadfall before it rejoins the main stem downstream. I'd glanced at it on the way up—habit, the same habit that makes a man check his mirrors on an empty road—and it had been empty. Brown water, white rock, shadow. Nothing.

Now there was a wake.

It was small, at that distance. A long pale V drawn on the dark water, moving upstream, against the current, which meant power, which meant purpose. No boat visible yet—it was still tucked behind the island—but the wake preceded it the way smoke precedes fire, unwinding across the channel in two clean lines that no log, no bird, no trick of current makes.

I put my hand on Mara's shoulder and pressed down, gently, and she got low without a word, which I noted and filed away under things I liked about her.

We watched from the rocks. The wake traveled. Steady speed, steady line—not searching, not sweeping. A boat that knows where it's going. It slid out from behind the island's tail, and even at six hundred meters I could read the silhouette: low profile, dark hull, center console, the flat patient stance of a working boat running light. No cabin. No flag. No registration numbers I could see, though at that distance I wouldn't have expected to see any.

Unmarked. Unhurried. Headed upstream.

Toward the source. Toward whatever was left. Toward, eventually, us.

"They followed us," Mara breathed. "From the cache. Or the landing. They've been behind us the whole—"

"Maybe," I said. "Or they're ahead of schedule and we're behind ours. Either way, standing on an open gravel shelf admiring the view is a way to die of vanity."

We went down to the boat fast but not panicked, because panic is loud and loud is fatal. I pushed off silent, oars first, letting the current take us before I risked even the idle, and swung us into the deep channel with the sun falling behind the western ridge so that our wake ran in shadow. Behind us the burned camp shrank to a smudge of black on the bank, and the stake with its pinned chart stayed in my mind at full size, sharp as a nail through the palm.

Mara crouched in the bow, watching the far channel until the island swallowed it. When she turned around, her face had changed. Not frightened, exactly. Recalibrated. A woman who had been hunting answers discovering, with some surprise, that she was also prey.

"How many boats do you think they have?" she asked.

"More than one. Always more than one. One boat is a mistake. Two boats is a plan."

"And we have one boat, half a tank of fuel, a rifle, and a dead man's coordinates."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"It *is* bad, Paloma."

"It's bad," I agreed, easing the throttle forward just enough to make way without making noise. "But bad has a shape. Bad has a direction. They're upstream, we're upstream, and Daniel's coordinates are further upstream than either of us. Which means for once, everybody in this river wants the same thing."

She looked at me. "That's supposed to be comforting?"

"No. It's supposed to be true. Comfort's your department." I checked the fuel gauge, the sky, the water ahead, and the water behind, in that order, because that's the order things kill you in. "Hold on. We've got maybe three hours of light, forty kilometers of gorge, and a wake back there that's going to notice our wake eventually. I intend to be a rumor by the time it does."

The boat rose onto plane, soft as a held breath, and we ran north into the narrowing gold of evening, hunted, committed, and—for the first time since the landing—entirely certain of what we were chasing.

Someone had burned the truth once already.

We were going upstream to dig it out of the ashes.
