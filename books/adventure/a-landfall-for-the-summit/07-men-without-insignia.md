## 7. Men Without Insignia

The fuel stop at Caño Hondo was the kind of place that existed on maps only as a smudge—a concrete apron bleeding into the river, a pump older than I was, and a tin-roofed cantina where the beer came cold only if you asked nicely and paid in cash. Which suited me fine. Cash was my native language.

I'd been running the river long enough to know that Caño Hondo had exactly one rule: nobody asks anybody anything. The fuel man, Ernesto, took my money without counting it because he knew I didn't cheat, and I left his till alone because I knew he'd notice. It was a working arrangement between two people who preferred to remain rumors.

Mara stayed on the boat. We'd agreed on that before we tied up—her sitting belowdecks with Tomas's coordinates spread out like tarot cards, me handling the pump and the small talk. She'd argued about it for exactly four minutes before I pointed out that a woman asking questions at Caño Hondo would be remembered for a year, and being remembered was the one thing I'd built my whole life around avoiding. That shut her up. Not happily. Nothing shut Mara Quist up happily. But it shut her up.

I was screwing the fuel cap back on when the truck came down the track.

It was a white pickup, new enough to be wrong. Nobody drove a new truck to Caño Hondo unless they were lost or lying, and men like this were never lost. Two of them got out, then a third from the passenger side, and I did what I always do when the world sends me a problem: I kept my hands busy and my face stupid.

They were dressed like fishermen. That was the first tell. The vests were too clean, the boots too unbroken, and not one of them smelled like fish or sunscreen or the particular despair of a man who's been skunked all day. The second tell was how they moved—spread out without discussing it, one drifting toward the bow of my boat, one toward the cantina door, one coming straight at me with a smile that had been issued by the same government that issued their sunglasses.

"Morning," the straight-at-me one said. "Beautiful water."

"It's a river," I said. "It does all right."

He laughed like I'd said something charming instead of something true. Up close he had the kind of tan that comes from ranges, not rivers, and a handshake that tested your grip the way a vet tests a horse's leg. "Name's Corrigan." No card. Men who hand out cards want to be found again. "We're doing a survey. Environmental impact, upstream. You been up that way?"

"A little."

"Fishing?"

"Salvage." I let him look at the boat—the rust streaks, the winch, the crates of gear that looked like junk because half of it was. "There's money in what other people lose."

"There is," he agreed, warmly, as if we were two businessmen discovering a shared enthusiasm. "You find much?"

"Enough to eat. Not enough to retire." I lit a cigarette, mostly so my hands would have an alibi. "You boys are a long way from any environmental impact I've heard about. The survey office is in the capital. This is—" I gestured at the general squalor of Caño Hondo, the dog asleep in the shade of the pump, Ernesto watching us through the flyscreen with the stillness of a man memorizing faces for later. "This is nowhere."

"Nowhere's where the river starts," Corrigan said. "And the river's what we're studying." He turned, casual as a man admiring scenery, and looked at my boat the way a customs officer looks at a hull. His eyes snagged on the dive gear bag. On the tarp covering the long crate I hadn't bothered to disguise, because a rifle wrapped in oilcloth under salvage tools is just another piece of salvage to anyone not looking for it. He didn't ask. That was the third tell. Curious men ask. Careful men note.

"You run charters?" he asked.

"Sometimes."

"We might need a boat. A local man who knows the upper water." He named a figure that would have covered six months of my living, said it lightly, the way you'd mention a lunch order. "Just ferry work. Survey equipment's heavy. The current up there's no joke, we hear."

"The current up there has killed better boatmen than me," I said. "I don't go past the last landing this time of year. Water's wrong."

"Water's wrong," he repeated, tasting it. "That's a funny way to put it."

"I'm a funny man."

The one by the cantina had drifted closer while we talked, close enough now that I could see the bulge under his vest that no fishing license explains. He wasn't even pretending to browse the dusty shelf of lures. The one near my bow had leaned over the rail once—just once—and come away again. They'd seen Mara's bag. Maybe her shadow through the hatch. Maybe her.

Corrigan took a slow turn along the apron, hands in his pockets, a tourist with nowhere to be. "Nine days ago," he said, conversational, "a research team went missing upriver. Meridian Foundation. Sad business. Families are worried. If you'd seen anything—flares, smoke, a camp abandoned—"

"I don't see much of anything," I said. "That's sort of my specialty."

"Mm." He stopped at the edge of the concrete, where the water slid green and indifferent past the pilings. "You know what I've noticed about this stretch? Everybody's polite. Everybody's vague. It's almost like people here have been told to forget things." He smiled at me again, and for a moment the smile went off duty and something else looked out from behind it—something patient and administrative. "Of course, memory's a funny thing. Sometimes it comes back on its own. Sometimes it needs help. And sometimes—" he shrugged, expansive, reasonable, "—sometimes a man decides his time is worth more spent closer to home. Safer investments. Happier family. Whatever a man like you has."

"I don't have a family."

"No," he said softly. "I noticed that too."

There it was. Beneath the courtesy, laid out neat as instruments on a tray: the offer, the warning, and the proof they'd already opened every drawer in my life. A registered boat belonging to a dead man. A bank account that didn't exist. A name I used the way other men used cologne. They couldn't prove anything—that was the point of how I lived—but they could make everything harder, and harder was a currency I understood.

I flicked the cigarette into the water and gave him my best honest face, the one I'd worn through two depositions and a funeral. "Look, friend. I dive wrecks and pull engines out of the mud. If a science boat went missing, that's above my pay grade and below my interest. I'm taking my fuel and going downstream, because downstream is where the work is." I stuck out my hand. "Good luck with your survey."

He shook it. His grip said *we'll see*. Mine said *sure*.

"Downstream," Corrigan repeated. "Smart man." Then, as I turned away: "Voss."

My spine went cold in a way the morning heat couldn't touch. I didn't stop walking. Stopping is how you tell them it landed.

"That's the name on the boat registration," he called after me, pleasant as a weather report. "Funny thing, registrations. They can be checked."

I waved without turning around, a little backward salute, the universal gesture of a man with nothing to hide and less to say, and I finished fueling, and I paid Ernesto, and I cast off like a man with an afternoon of easy work ahead of him. Only when the bend took Caño Hondo out of sight did I let my hands shake.

Not much. Just enough to remind me they remembered how.

* * *

We ran until the light went copper, then tucked into a side slough I've used before—a cut behind a gravel bar where the willows grow thick enough to swallow a boat and the current hums past the entrance like a guard walking his rounds. I backed us in, killed the engine, and set the anchor by hand so the chain wouldn't talk.

Mara watched me do all of it without helping, arms crossed, which was her way of helping.

"They weren't fishermen," she said. Not a question. She'd seen them from the hatch; I'd counted on that.

"No."

"And they knew your name."

"The name on the boat," I said. "Which is more than most people know."

She waited. Mara Quist's great weapon wasn't volume—it was the silence after, the space she left open like a door, standing there with her brother's disappearance in her eyes until filling it became easier than enduring it. I'd watched her use it on Ernesto, on the fuel line, on me. It worked on me every time, and I resented it professionally.

So I told her some of it. The part that was true and safe, which is to say the part that was true and useless.

"I worked contracts," I said. "Recovery work. Salvage with paperwork, insurance investigations, that kind of thing. Legitimate. Boring. The kind of diving where somebody files a report when you come up." I fed a stick into the little stove and watched the flame take. "Then one job went sideways. Badly. People got hurt, and the report that got filed wasn't the report that happened, and I signed off on it anyway."

"Why?" Flat. No judgment in it yet. Judgment was coming; she was just loading it.

"Because they offered me money, and because the alternative was becoming a person who asks questions in a country where questions get answered with boats that burn at anchor." I shrugged. "I took the money. I bought the boat. I bought a dead man's name to put on it. And I've been invisible ever since. It turns out invisibility isn't free. It's just that the payments are quiet."

"What happened on the job?"

"Does it matter?"

"Yes," she said simply. "Because those men back there talked to you like a colleague. Not like a stranger. Like someone they'd read a file on and liked what they read. Paloma—I've spent three weeks talking to bureaucrats about my brother, and I know the difference between a man being stonewalled and a man being handled. They were handling you. Gently. The way you handle an asset."

The fire popped. Out in the main channel, the river kept up its low monologue, the sound it makes whether anyone listens or not.

"I dove this river before," I said. "Years ago. Before your brother ever thought about it. There's a wreck up there that isn't on any registry, and I was hired to look at it and say nothing, and I looked at it, and I said nothing, and they paid me very well for the nothing." I kept my eyes on the flame because looking at her while I said it would have cost more than I had on hand. "That's the truth. Part of it. The rest of it I'll tell you when I understand it myself, and if that sounds like a coward's answer, it's because it is one."

Mara was quiet a long time. Long enough for me to catalog everything she might say, ranked by how much each version would hurt.

What she finally said was: "You could have lied to me completely. You didn't. That's either a good sign or a very sophisticated bad one."

"It's usually both."

A ghost of a laugh escaped her, quickly strangled, like she'd caught herself enjoying the company of a man she hadn't decided to trust. She pulled her knees up and stared at the fire. "Daniel would have liked you," she said, and her voice cracked on the tense, and she hated that it cracked, and I pretended not to notice because noticing out loud is its own kind of cruelty. "He trusted the wrong people easily. Trusted institutions. Believed that if you brought the truth to the right desk, the right desk would do the right thing." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, brisk, furious at the tears for wasting time. "Those men today—they're why he's gone, aren't they. Whoever sent them. Whatever he found."

"I don't know," I said. And then, because she deserved one thing tonight that wasn't hedged: "But I think he found what I found. And I think that's why the river's closed."

She looked at me across the fire, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod slowly, once, like a woman signing something.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we go get it."

I should have argued. Every instinct I'd polished over ten years of staying alive said argue, deflect, turn the boat downstream while she slept. Instead I lay back against the gunwale and listened to the river, and somewhere upstream, faint under the water-sound, I thought I heard an engine—not close, not threatening, just *there*, moving the way we were moving, patient as arithmetic.

Somebody else had decided to go get it too.

I didn't sleep much after that. Neither did she. But around midnight, without a word, Mara shifted her bedroll closer to mine—not touching, just nearer, the way you moor a second boat alongside in weather. Trust built on incomplete foundations, load-bearing anyway.

I kept watch until dawn, and the engine sound never came closer, and never went away.
