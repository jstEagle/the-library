## 8. Dead Man's Coordinates

The drive was the size of a cigarette pack and about as forthcoming.

Mara had been working on it since we tied up in a bend of deadwater two hours past the burned camp—anchored under overhanging alder, engine cold, coffee reheated twice on the single-burner because neither of us wanted to say out loud that we weren't sleeping. She'd spread her brother's things across the deck like a surgeon laying out instruments: laptop, solar panel, cable adapters, a tin of what I'd assumed were biscuits until she opened it and it turned out to be encryption hardware soldered by hand.

"You built this?" I asked.

"Daniel did. I just keep it charged." She didn't look up. "He never trusted anything with an antenna."

"He wasn't wrong." I said it before I thought about it, and she glanced at me with that look she'd been sharpening all week—the one that filed everything I said into a growing folder labeled *later*.

The river moved past us without opinion. Somewhere downstream, maybe ten miles, maybe twenty, men in an unmarked boat were deciding whether we'd turned back or pushed on. I'd taken us through water shallow enough to read the gravel through it, then poled a half mile of reed shallows no chart would bother to lie about. If they had thermal or air support, none of it would matter. If they had boats and patience, we'd bought ourselves a night.

Patience was the one thing I had plenty of and hated spending.

"Mara," I said, for the fourth time since dark. "Whatever's on there—"

"It's encrypted with a passphrase," she said, "and Daniel was sentimental in ways he thought nobody noticed. I've got maybe forty guesses left before the drive does something unfriendly to itself."

"What kind of unfriendly?"

"The kind he learned about from a man who used to dive government contracts."

I let that sit. The tin of biscuits—hardware—hummed faintly, which I chose to interpret as encouragement.

* * *

I'll say this for Mara Quist: she didn't fill silence with noise. Most people who've lost someone want the world talking at all times, a wall of sound between them and the shape of the absence. Mara worked in quiet the way I dove in it—methodically, breathing slow, trusting her hands more than her eyes.

She tried names first. Our mother's maiden name. The dog. A girl named Sable whom I gathered had broken his heart at nineteen. The drive rejected each one with the same dumb patience of a locked door.

Then she sat back on her heels and looked at the river instead of the screen, and I watched her stop guessing and start thinking, which is the most dangerous thing a person can do on my boat.

"He called me in March," she said. "Before the expedition launched. He was excited, but it was a strange excitement. Not 'we found funding' excited. More like—" she searched—"like someone who'd stopped being afraid of something."

"Scientists get brave when the grant clears."

"This wasn't grant talk. He said, *if anything happens, don't let them make it look like weather.*" Her voice stayed level. That was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "I laughed. I told him he'd been reading too many thrillers. He laughed too. And then he said, *ask the diver.*"

The coffee in my cup had gone cold enough to taste like the bottom of the river. "Ask the diver what?"

"That's the phrase. *Ask the diver.* I thought he meant their logistics guy. There was a diver on the team roster—somebody for sampling work." She turned the laptop toward me, screen glowing against the dark. "Except I checked the roster again after the camp burned. There's no diver listed. There never was."

The alder leaves ticked against each other overhead. Downriver, a night bird made its one complaint and quit.

"So," Mara said, very quietly, "who are you, Paloma Voss? Because my brother knew you before he ever met you, and he sent me to you like you were the only door out of a burning building."

I could have lied. I want that on the record, wherever records go: I had a lie ready. It was a good one, worn smooth from use, about salvage work and navy contracts that dried up and a man who liked his own company. I'd told it to customs officers and barkeeps and once, memorably, to a woman in Puerto Anselmo who deserved better.

Instead I heard myself say, "Ask me again tomorrow. Let me see what your brother left first. If I'm going to tell you who I am, you should know it while you're holding proof of what I am too."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she nodded once, and went back to the keyboard, and I understood I'd just made a promise I couldn't take back—and that some part of me had been waiting years to make it.

* * *

The passphrase, when she found it, wasn't a name or a date. It was coordinates.

She tried it almost as a joke—a string of numbers from the corner of one of his old charts, a spot on the middle river where nothing was supposed to be. The drive clicked, considered, and opened like a fist unclenching.

"His wreck site," she breathed. "He used his wreck site as the key."

"Sentimental," I said, and my voice came out wrong, because I knew those coordinates too. I'd held them in my head for eleven years, the way you hold a stone you swallowed.

The files loaded in a list. Photographs first—hundreds of them, timestamped across nine days of the expedition. Mara scrolled and I watched over her shoulder, and the river might as well have stopped moving for all the attention either of us paid it.

They'd found the wreck. Of course they'd found the wreck; I'd given the river fifteen years of keeping its mouth shut and Daniel Quist had needed nine days. The photos showed the hull from the outside, silt-streaked, ribs exposed where the current had done its slow surgery. Then inside. The hold.

I know that hold better than I know my own hands. I've measured it in exhaled breaths, mapped it by touch in water so black my light only made the darkness argue back. But I'd never seen it lit properly. In Daniel's photographs, laid bare under expedition floods, it looked almost ordinary—cargo netting rotted to lace, crates slumped like tired passengers.

And stenciled on the crates, crisp as the day some dockhand sprayed them: lot numbers. Manifest codes. And on three of them, small and unmistakable, the stamp that had ended my legitimate life and started this one.

CLASSIFIED. HANDLE UNDER AUTHORITY OF—

The authority line was blacked out in the photographs. Redacted before the crates ever sank. Somebody had censored cargo destined for the bottom of a river nobody was supposed to know about.

"They're not science samples," Mara said. Her finger hovered over the screen without touching it, the way you hover over a body. "This is ordnance storage configuration. I catalogued munitions handling for two years at the institute—I know what crating looks like." She pulled up another image, zoomed. "Live fuzes. Paloma, these are live fuzes in a civilian river system, sitting in a hull that's been leaking for decades."

"There's more," I said. "Keep going."

Manifests next. Scanned documents, high resolution, the paper yellowed but legible. Shipping manifests for a vessel whose name had been ground off every plate on the hull—I'd checked, early on, with a wire brush and a growing sickness—but which the paperwork still remembered. Tonnage figures. Cargo declarations. And stamped diagonally across each page in ink that had outlived the ship:

DESTROY AFTER RECEIPT.

"Somebody kept copies," Mara whispered. "You don't destroy-after-receipt unless the receipt itself is the crime."

"Or you destroy them and somebody keeps one anyway." I nodded at the screen. "Look at the dates."

She looked. I watched her do the arithmetic, watched it land on her face in stages—confusion first, then the cold recalculation, then the thing underneath both, which was grief putting on armor.

"The sinking predates the disaster report," she said slowly. "The official record says the *Vireo* ran aground in the flood season. Natural loss. But these manifests are dated six weeks *after* the last confirmed sighting of the ship anywhere on the river. It was still operating. Still loading cargo. And then—" she scrolled, faster now—"then there's a gap. Six weeks of nothing, and then a single entry. Tow authorization. Night movement. No destination logged."

"And then it's at the bottom of the river," I said, "in eighty feet of water, four miles from any channel deep enough to run aground in. You don't drift that far sideways while drowning."

The silence that followed had weight. The river filled it, patient, indifferent, carrying whatever it carries.

Mara opened the last file. A text document, short. Daniel's handwriting converted to type—he'd typed it himself, which meant he'd sat somewhere with a signal or a power source and chosen his words, which meant it was meant to be found.

It read:

*If you're reading this, I didn't come back. The hold confirms it. They scuttled her—charges along the portside frames, placed by hand, professional work. You don't sink a ship that neatly by accident. They sank it on purpose, with everything aboard, because of what the crew saw upstream. Find the sister section. Everything they buried is still buried together. Don't trust the river to keep the secret. Rivers only keep what nobody pays to dredge.*

Below it, a single line of coordinates. Not the wreck. Something upstream. Something higher than any point the expedition's public itinerary admitted to.

The source.

* * *

Mara closed the laptop. Opened it. Closed it again, as if checking the grief would behave differently on the second pass. It didn't.

"My brother wasn't doing hydrology," she said. Her voice had gone flat and precise, the voice of someone walking a plank carefully. "Nine days of sampling equipment in the manifest, and it was cover. Every core sample, every flow gauge—it was theater. He went up that river to build a legal case, and he dressed it as science because he knew—" she stopped. Started again. "Because he knew what happened to people who asked plainly."

"Yes," I said.

"Don't trust the river to keep the secret." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "He even wrote like you. Terse. Practical. Like a man leaving instructions for someone who already knows half the story."

"He wrote it for whoever found the drive. Could've been anyone."

"No." She shook her head. "It's addressed in the metadata. I just saw it. The recipient field." She turned the screen, and there it was, in the file properties, in a field most people never think to look at:

*For V.*

The alder leaves ticked. My heart did something complicated and unwelcome.

"I never met him," I said. It was true, and it was insufficient, and we both knew it. "I need you to understand that much tonight. Whatever else I tell you tomorrow—Daniel and I never stood in the same room."

"But he knew you."

"He knew *of* me. There's a difference, and right now it's the width of a knife blade."

"Is it?" Mara stood, and in the low glow of the laptop she looked older than she had at the landing, and harder, and more like her brother. "Because here's what I can piece together, Paloma, and you can stop me whenever I'm wrong. Eleven, twelve years ago, somebody dove that wreck on a private contract. Found the same things Daniel found. And that diver took money—or took fear, or took both—and went silent, and the river stayed closed, and the families of thirty-one people got a flood story and a closed case." Her eyes didn't leave mine. "And then, years later, a scientist starts pulling threads, and one of the threads leads him to a ghost of a diver living off the books on exactly this stretch of water, and he tells his sister: *if anything happens, find him.*"

The night bird complained again, farther off. Or maybe it wasn't a bird. I turned my head toward the downstream dark and listened until I was sure it was feathers and not an engine.

"Thirty-one," I said quietly.

"What?"

"The number. You said thirty-one." I looked at the river. "It's thirty-four. Three were never on the public list. Deckhands, seasonal workers, people whose paperwork didn't survive the flood story any better than they did."

Mara went very still. "How do you know that?"

"Tomorrow," I said. "I promised you tomorrow. But I'll give you this much tonight, free of charge, because you've earned it and because I'm tired of carrying it alone: your brother was right about all of it. The charges. The cover-up. The reason the headwaters were sealed. He was right, and it's the same reason I've spent eleven years being no one, and the men who burned his camp are the same kind of men who paid me to forget." I met her eyes. "The mission changed tonight, Mara. This stopped being a rescue the day we found that stake through the chart. What's left is testimony. Yours, mine, and his."

She sat back down slowly, the laptop cradled in her arms like something rescued from a fire.

"The coordinates," she said. "Upstream. The sister section."

"If it exists, it's above the last survey marker. Past where the maps give up."

"Then we go there."

"We?" I raised an eyebrow. "You've seen what they did to a camp full of trained scientists."

"I've also seen what you did running the gorge with a hole in the transom and a passenger who wouldn't shut up." Something crossed her face—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might someday build. "You could put me ashore. First landing, first fuel stop. Nobody would blame you."

Every practical bone in my body voted yes. Put her off, run light, run fast, be a rumor again by Thursday. It was the play that had kept me alive for a decade, and I could feel it waiting, patient as the wreck, offering me the old bargain one more time.

"No," I said. "I couldn't."

She held my gaze for a moment longer than the sentence required, then nodded and began packing the drive away with the care of someone handling remains.

That was when the radio, tuned to Channel Nine out of habit, crackled once—a burst of static with a shape to it, the acoustic signature of a transmitter somewhere downriver switching on. Then off.

Not a voice. Just a boat, somewhere behind us in the dark, checking whether anyone was listening.

Mara froze. I put my hand over the volume knob and eased it to zero, and in the sudden silence we listened to the river, and the river, for the first time since I'd known it, sounded like it was holding its breath.
