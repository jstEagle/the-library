## 9. Night Runners

There's an old rule among river people: never anchor in the current. Anchor in the slack water behind a point bar, tie off to something that grew there on purpose, and sleep with one ear open. I'd followed that rule for eleven years without incident, which is either proof the rule works or proof that my luck had been sitting in an account somewhere, drawing interest, waiting for the night it all came due.

We anchored at dusk below a bend called Sow's Tooth, where the river split around a gravel island and reassembled itself downstream in a hurry. The spot was textbook—deep eddy, willows for cover, high ground between us and anything coming down the far channel. I cooked rice with the last of the dried onions. Mara cleaned Tomas's wound by lantern light while he sweated through a fever and argued, semi-coherently, about torque specifications for a pump nobody was building.

"You should eat," Mara said, when she finally sat down across from me.

"I am eating."

"You're performing eating. There's a difference." She took the pot anyway, scooped a portion, handed it back like a toll collector. "You've been checking the treeline every four minutes since sunset."

"Three minutes," I said. "I'd hate for you to think I'm sloppy."

She almost smiled. Almost. The last two days had sanded most of the smile off her—the burned camp, the chart with the tent stake through it, the wake we'd seen sliding across the far channel like a knife drawn slowly through silk. Whoever they were, they weren't in a rush. People who are in a rush make mistakes. These ones were letting us do their scouting for them.

Tomas slept, if you could call it that—fever dreams with his hands twitching at invisible machinery. Mara banked the lantern low. I took first watch in the bow with the rifle across my knees and the river talking its endless talk against the hull.

Here's what nobody tells you about waiting for trouble: your body knows before you do. It's not sound or sight, exactly. It's the absence of things. Frogs going quiet in a wedge behind you. The willows holding still when everything else is moving. At some point past midnight I realized the night had gone polite, the way a room goes polite when the wrong man walks into it.

I reached back and put my hand over Mara's mouth before her eyes were fully open. Her whole body went rigid, then—credit where it's due—deliberately loose. I pointed. She looked. She understood.

An engine. Not running loud, the way honest engines run. Running *quiet*, throttled down to a murmur, upstream of us and closing. Then a second engine answering it from the far channel, and I understood the shape of the thing: they'd split, and we were the filling.

"Mara," I breathed. "Cut the anchor line. Don't drop it—cut it, we may need to move again fast. Wake Tomas. Tell him he's cargo tonight, nothing more."

"What are you—"

"Going to do something stupid," I said. "It's the only thing I'm good at after dark."

The first boat came around the point with no lights, which told me everything. Honest fishermen run lights because rocks don't care who they sink. These men wanted darkness the way I wanted silence—we spoke the same language, and that was the worst part. I knew their grammar. I'd written sentences in it once.

They swept a spotlight across the willows, casual as a man checking his watch. The beam crossed the bow of my boat maybe a foot above the gunwale while Mara flattened herself over Tomas and I lay in the bilge water smelling of diesel and old rope and thought, very clearly, *this is the life you built, Paloma. This exact moment. You just didn't know the address.*

The light moved on. The engine slowed further. They'd seen the empty anchorage and they were confused, and confusion would only last until someone noticed the cut anchor line drifting or the warm ashes in my stove.

"Paloma," Mara whispered. "They're stopping."

"They're boarding." I was already at the tiller. "Which means we're leaving."

I turned us out of the eddy and into the main current with the throttle barely above idle, letting the river carry us the way it carries leaves—silent, unpowered, a piece of the night instead of an intrusion on it. Behind us the spotlight snapped back to our wake, and a voice cracked across the water, amplified and flat: "Vessel ahead. Cut your engine and stand off."

The nice version, I thought. That's the courtesy call. There wouldn't be a second courtesy call.

"Paloma—" Mara said.

"Hold on to him." And I opened the throttle all the way.

* * *

Running rapids at night is not bravery. Let me be precise about that, because Mara called it brave later and I want the record straight. Running rapids blind by moonlight is what happens when staying put has become the more dangerous option and your body makes the decision your brain is still drafting objections to. Bravery implies a choice among reasonable alternatives. There were no reasonable alternatives. There was the gorge, and there was the men with the spotlight, and the gorge at least was honest about wanting to kill us.

Sow's Tooth Rapids ran half a mile of broken water—boulders like the backs of sleeping animals, channels that forked and forked again, drops that sounded like applause from an audience you didn't want to meet. By day, with the sun high, I could read the water: the V of smooth green that meant the safe line, the boils and standing waves that meant trouble, the porcelain-smooth tongue above a hole that meant *do not*. By moonlight, all of that became rumor. I read by shadow and by sound, by the way the spray blew off a wave crest, by the blackness of water versus the blackness of rock—a distinction that matters very much and is visible approximately never.

The first drop hit us like a fist under the ribs. Tomas cried out; Mara made a sound that wasn't quite a word and held him down with both arms and her whole weight. Something in the stern crashed—I heard my spare fuel can go over the side and felt the boat lighten by its absence, twenty liters of range gone into the dark like a coin dropped from a bridge.

"Left!" I shouted, though I couldn't have told you why except that the left channel smelled like spray and the right one smelled like rock. We threaded it. A boulder the size of a chapel scraped past the hull close enough that I could have touched it, and did, briefly, with the tiller's edge, and the shudder that went up my arm went all the way down my spine.

The second boat entered the rapids behind us.

That was their mistake, and I want it noted that even mid-terror, some cold accountant in the back of my skull logged it: they were good, but they were strangers to this water. I had run Sow's Tooth four times, twice by day, twice at dusk, and I had spent three years diving the river below it. Every rock in this stretch owed me money. Their pilot drove the way men drive when they trust horsepower more than reading—straight at the V, fast, confident—and the V lied to him. It does that. There's a lateral wave at the bottom of the second drop that shoves you sideways into a hole the locals call the Molar, and I'd watched it eat a log once, rolling it like a dog with a stick.

Their spotlight strobed wild across the water, caught us, lost us, caught us again. Then it swung skyward, hung there a moment like a dying star, and went out. The sound that followed was not an explosion. It was worse—an engine note rising into a scream and then simply stopping, replaced by the river's own noise, indifferent and enormous.

Mara twisted around. "Did they—"

"Don't look," I said. "Look forward. Help me read."

Because here's the arithmetic I was doing, and hating myself for doing it: men in that water, in the dark, in current like that, had minutes. Maybe less. And every second I spent thinking about it was a second I wasn't reading the next drop, and three people's lives were riding on my reading. The cold accountant said *you can't help them anyway.* Some other voice, older and less convenient, said *you could try.*

I tried. I yelled it back over my shoulder into the wind—"They're in the water! Light! Light everything!"—and Mara, God bless her, didn't ask questions. She got the lantern up and lit, swinging it in a wide arc astern, shouting into the dark in a voice I hadn't heard from her yet, a carrying voice, a search-party voice. Whether anyone heard, whether anyone was alive to hear, I never learned. The river closed behind us like a book.

* * *

We came out of the gorge two miles down, into water gone suddenly gentle, and I let us drift a long time before I dared the engine again—low, just enough for steerage, nosing into a flooded side slough where the willows closed over us like a curtain.

Damage report, taken by lantern light with numb hands: fuel can gone. One oar gone. The stove had torn loose and dented the hull strake near the transom—not leaking, but complaining. My dive bag had shifted and soaked half our dry clothing. Tomas had been thrown against the gunwale and would have a bruise to add to his collection. Mara had a rope burn across one palm from holding the lantern line, and she hadn't mentioned it, and I found it while checking her hands for something else entirely, and the finding of it did something strange to my chest that I decided not to examine.

"We lost the food?" she asked.

"Some. The waterproof panniers held. Rice, beans, the medical kit, most of the ammunition." I paused. "Lost the coffee."

She stared at me. "The coffee."

"There's a hierarchy of tragedy, Mara."

And she laughed. It burst out of her sideways, ragged, half hysteria, the laugh of a person whose body needed to discharge something that wasn't tears. She pressed her burned hand over her mouth and laughed into it until her shoulders shook, and Tomas stirred and mumbled something about pressure valves, and I sat there in the wet bilge watching her come apart and reassemble, and I understood that I was in trouble of a kind no rapid had ever put me in.

Because here's the thing I'd learned about fear, eleven years of it, professionally: fear strips you down to load-bearing structure. Everything decorative burns off. What's left is what you actually are. And what Mara Quist was, stripped down, was steady. Steadier than me. When the spotlight found us I'd seen her cover Tomas with her body without hesitation, without calculation, the way you flinch from a spark. When I'd shouted for the light she'd had it burning inside three seconds. She didn't freeze, didn't argue, didn't need the situation explained. She just *acted*, correctly, and then afterward she shook and laughed and bled quietly and asked about the coffee.

I had spent years mistaking invisibility for safety and solitude for strength. Sitting in a flooded slough with a woman whose hand I was still holding longer than the medical inspection required, I began to suspect I'd gotten the equation wrong.

"You held," I said. Stupidly. It was the best I had.

"So did you." She looked at our hands, then at me, and didn't pull away. "Where did you learn to read water like that? In the dark?"

"Same place I learned everything useful," I said. "Somewhere I shouldn't have been."

She let that sit. She was getting good at letting things sit—waiting me out the way the river waits out a bank. "Those men," she said. "The ones in the water. You tried to save them."

"I made noise."

"You lit them a path out of a gorge at midnight, knowing they'd come to kill us an hour earlier." Her voice was quiet. "Don't make it smaller than it was."

"It's not virtue," I said. "It's a defect. I've been trying to have it surgically removed for years. The river keeps reattaching it."

That earned me another laugh, softer this time, and then she leaned her head back against the gunwale and closed her eyes, and neither of us moved our hands, and the willows whispered their gossip overhead, and somewhere upstream the river carried whatever it carried toward the sea without telling us.

* * *

We took turns sleeping. Real turns, enforced, because adrenaline is a loan and the interest comes due around dawn. I slept second and woke to gray light and the smell of rice heating and Mara at the tiller with Tomas propped beside her, both of them studying the far bank like it owed them an apology.

"Boat traffic?" I asked.

"Nothing since before dawn. Either we lost them or they lost us." She handed me a cup of the rice water they use when there's no coffee, which is to say punishment in liquid form, and I drank it like a loyal citizen.

Tomas surfaced from his fever enough to fix me with a look. His voice came out gravel. "You ran Sow's Tooth at night."

"Badly."

"No." He shook his head, wincing. "Nobody runs it at night. Daniel used to say—" He stopped. The name hit the air between us and stayed there.

"Daniel used to say what?" Mara asked gently.

"That the river doesn't forgive anything," Tomas said. "But it forgets faster than men do." He looked at me. "He'd have liked you. He liked people who broke rules for reasons."

I took the cup of punishment-water and walked to the bow, because my face was doing something I didn't have permission to show anyone yet. Daniel Quist. The man whose voice on that nine-day-old recording had pulled me out of eleven years of careful hiding. The man whose coordinates were burning a hole in Mara's satellite phone. Somewhere ahead of us, upstream, past the burned camp and the men without insignia and the wreck I'd sworn never to name, Daniel's trail ended—or didn't.

Behind me I heard Mara say, quietly, to Tomas: "He'll get us there."

And the terrible thing, the thing I turned over in my chest like a stone with a warm side, was that I believed her. Not because I was good. Because for the first time in eleven years, I had something to be good *for*—and the men who'd burned that camp had no idea yet what they'd created when they pushed two stubborn people onto one current.

I raised the throttle. The slough gave us back to the river, and the river pointed uphill, toward the source, and we went.
