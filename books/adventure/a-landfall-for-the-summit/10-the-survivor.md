## 10. The Survivor

The smell found us before the man did.

I'd been reading the river the way I always do—by ear, by the lean of the current against the hull, by the particular silence a jungle makes when it's hiding something. Mara was up front with the satellite phone cradled in both hands like a bird she was afraid to startle. We'd been following her brother's last coordinates for two days, and the coordinates had gone strange overnight, drifting off the channel into a side slough where the water went tea-dark and the trees leaned in close enough to comb your hair.

Then the smell. Sweet and wrong, like fruit rotting in a closed room.

"Stop," Mara said. She didn't shout it. She said it the way you say *amen*—quiet, final, already braced for what it means.

I killed the engine and let us drift. The slough narrowed ahead into a wall of buttress roots, kapok trees standing on their own lifted skirts, and wedged down in the hollow between two of them—half in the water, half out—was a shape that had once been a man.

My first thought, and I'm not proud of it, was *good, an answer*. Because a body tells you one story cleanly. A missing man tells you forty stories, all of them worse.

Then the shape coughed.

* * *

He'd crawled, that much was clear. There was a drag mark through the mud behind him, ten meters maybe, ending at the root hollow where he'd wedged himself like driftwood. He was expedition gear head to toe—Meridian logo on a shirt that had been white in another life—and he'd done something clever and desperate with a tarp and river clay, smearing himself gray-green, packing mud into his hair. Hiding from men who hunted by eye.

The clever part was how long it had kept him alive. The terrible part was everything else.

"Tomas Reyes," Mara said, kneeling in the muck before I'd even got the boat tied off. "Tomas. It's Mara. Mara Quist. Daniel's sister."

His eyes opened. They took a long time focusing, and when they did they fixed on her face with an expression I recognized from mirrors—the look of a man checking whether what he's seeing is a hallucination, and deciding he doesn't care either way.

"You're real," he rasped. His voice sounded like a hinge nobody had oiled since the war. "You're—no. No, you can't be here. You have to go. You have to go *now*."

"We're not going anywhere," Mara said, and there it was again, that stubbornness of hers, the same iron she'd used on me at the landing. "We came for answers."

"They're coming back." His hand came up out of the mud and grabbed her wrist with a strength that surprised me, given the rest of him. "They come back every day. They walk the bank. They're looking for—" He stopped. His eyes slid past her shoulder to me, and something in them changed. Calculated. "Who's this?"

"Paloma Voss," I said. "Salvage diver. Boat driver. Currently unemployed."

"He's the one who knows the river," Mara said, which was generous, because what I'd told her was closer to *the river knows me and we've agreed to avoid each other*.

Reyes looked at me for a long moment. Feverish men do a lot of looking, and most of it means nothing, but this meant something. He was placing me. Or trying to.

"The diver," he said finally. "Daniel talked about you. Before we left. Said if anything happened, find the man who dives the wreck nobody names." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been discontinued in this part of the world. "I thought he was being dramatic. Daniel was dramatic about rivers."

"What happened?" Mara asked. Her voice stayed level. Her hands didn't. "Tomas. What happened to them?"

* * *

Getting him into the boat was ugly work. He weighed half what a man his size should weigh, and the half that remained screamed at me through gritted teeth when I lifted him—there was a wound high on his right thigh, wrapped in strips of expedition shirt, soaked through and re-soaked, the fabric stiff as bark. Not a gunshot. A machete cut, or a fall onto something sharp, badly closed, badly infected. The smell I'd caught from the water was coming off it as much as off him.

I ran us downstream a kilometer to a bend I knew—a gravel bar backed by deadfall, sightlines open on both channels, water deep enough to leave fast without lights. Old habits. Even now, carrying a dying man, my hands picked the spot the way other men pick a parking space.

Mara built a shelter of tarps while I cleaned the wound with the good alcohol, the bottle I'd been saving for exactly this kind of arithmetic: one bottle of grain spirits versus one engineer's leg. The leg was winning so far, but narrowly.

He passed out twice during it. Both times he came back fighting, grabbing whatever was nearest—which, the second time, was my forearm—with those fever-strength fingers and saying, clear as a bell, "*Don't let them take the drive.*"

"There is no drive," I said. "There's just you and us and a boat."

"Then don't let them take *me*." And then he was under again, and Mara and I looked at each other across him, and neither of us said what we were both thinking, which was that we had no idea yet what we'd be willing to trade to keep him.

He surfaced near dusk, lucid in the way the dying sometimes are—all the fog burned off, just the hard bright core left. Mara fed him water and rehydration salts in sips, and he talked. Maybe because she asked. Maybe because a man carries a thing that long, nine days alone in a root hollow listening to armed men walk the bank, and eventually the thing has to come out or crush him.

"It started fine," he said. "That's the joke. Best field season of my life. Daniel had us running bathymetry up past the old closure markers, and everyone kept asking why the government would fund science above the forbidden section, and Daniel kept saying *because they finally declassified it*, and we believed him. Why wouldn't we? He'd fought two years for those permits."

I kept my face still. Permits for that stretch of river didn't exist. I knew that the way you know the load rating of a bridge you're standing under.

"Day six, the sonar found the second structure." Reyes stared up through the tarp at nothing. "Everyone assumed it was rock. Caverns, sinkholes, whatever. But the returns were wrong. Too regular. Daniel sent me down with a line and a camera—just a look, thirty meters, in and out." His breath hitched. "It wasn't rock."

"The sister section," I said.

His eyes came around to me slowly. "You know it."

"I know the wreck," I said. "I've been diving it for years. I never found the second section. It's sealed."

"Dynamited," Reyes said. "Sealed from the inside, if you understand me. Charges set after she went down. Somebody wanted that part of the ship to stay part of the river forever." He drank, grimaced, kept going, because he was an engineer and engineers finish their reports. "I got the camera running along the seam and there was a gap—blast damage, a meter wide, collapsed plate. I put the camera through."

He stopped there for a long time. Mara touched his hand. He didn't seem to feel it.

"Bunks," he said finally. "Dozens of them. And personal effects—wedding rings in a mess tin, someone's rosary, children's drawings, laminated, like somebody's family had mailed them aboard. And crates. Crates stamped with a civilian shipping line that went bankrupt twenty years ago, the one that ran the ferry routes." His voice cracked down the middle. "It was the ferry, do you understand? Part of it. They told everyone the *Santa Elena* broke up in a storm with all hands. There were memorials. My aunt lost friends on that crossing. And here's the stern section, dynamited shut, sitting ninety kilometers from where the storm supposedly sank her, full of people's *drawings*."

The fire crackled. Somewhere downriver a heron said one rude word and thought better of it.

"Daniel pulled the manifests from the main wreck two days later," Reyes went on, quieter now, the fury banked down to coals. "You've seen them?"

"I've seen enough," I said, and didn't say the rest—that I'd seen them eight years ago, photographed them, taken money for forgetting them. That confession was still sitting in my chest like a swallowed anchor, scheduled for delivery on some night I hadn't chosen yet.

"Live ordnance too," Reyes said. "In the forward hold. Marked, catalogued, serviceable. A navy ship, sunk in a civilian ferry disaster zone, loaded with live munitions the navy has no record of ever deploying. Daniel sat up all night with the documents and in the morning he was a different person. He said—we were going to be witnesses. That was the word he used. *Witnesses.* He said evidence dies in the dark unless you carry it into the light personally." Reyes laughed, that broken-hinge sound again. "Poetic. He was a scientist, not a poet. But that morning he was both."

"And then?" Mara asked. Her voice was very controlled. I'd learned in the past week that her control was a load-bearing structure. You didn't touch it.

"Then the men came." Reyes's jaw tightened. "Night of day eight. Four boats, no lights, no markings, no insignia. They didn't announce themselves. They came up the bank shooting. Priya went first—she was on watch, she never even got her rifle unslung. Then Okonkwo tried to reach the satellite phone and—" He shut his eyes. "They burned the camp with people in it. Some of them. The ones who were slow."

"How did you get out?" I asked.

"I didn't fight fair." He said it flat, no pride in it. "I'm the engineer. I'd rigged our fuel drums with quick-release valves for fire safety. When the shooting started I opened every valve and threw a flare. Whole camp went up like a refinery. In the confusion I got into the water. Took a blade across the thigh from one of them wading in after me—I put a rock into his face and swam. Came up in the slough. Crawled into the hollow." He gestured vaguely at his own ruin. "And then I stayed alive. Nine days. Because Daniel's last order was *somebody has to testify*, and I'm the somebody."

Mara made a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life. Not a sob. Something smaller and worse, a single syllable of grief bitten in half before it could become words.

"Your brother died well," Reyes said to her, taking her hand with both of his ruined ones. "He stood up. He walked toward them, holding the documents over his head, shouting that they were killing witnesses of a civilian disaster, that the world would know. He made them do it in front of each other. Men like that hate witnesses. It's the one thing they can't salvage."

* * *

Later, when Reyes slept—not a peaceful sleep, but the honest unconsciousness of a body that has spent its whole budget—Mara sat beside me at the fire with her knees drawn up and the satellite phone dark in her lap.

"Every day," she said. "He said they come back every day and walk the bank."

"They're looking for the drive," I said. "Or the journals. Or him. Doesn't matter which. They'll keep coming until they're sure there's nothing left to find."

"Then we should move him."

"Moving him kills him faster than staying does. Look at that leg." I fed a stick to the fire. "What he needs is a hospital, antibiotics, surgery. What he wants—" I nodded toward the sleeping man, toward the coordinates still glowing faintly on the phone in Mara's lap, the ones that pointed upstream, past the last survey marker, toward the source. "What he wants is to finish his report."

Mara was quiet a long time. The firelight moved on her face and I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd been doing since the landing, the one where every answer costs more than the question.

"Paloma," she said. "He said the ordnance is still down there. Live munitions, in the forward hold, on a wreck I've read about my whole life in accident reports that called it a storm." She turned to look at me, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "They didn't just kill my brother to hide papers. They're keeping a mass grave sealed with explosives next to it. And every day that site stays buried is a day the lie stays true."

I fed the fire another stick and said nothing, because the true reply was *I know, I've known for eight years, I took their money and dove away from it*, and that reply was going to cost me her, and I wasn't ready to pay tonight.

Downstream, far off, an engine note crossed the water. One boat, running quiet, no lights. It faded east and didn't come back—but Reyes stirred in his sleep and said, clearly, "*Don't let them take the drive*," and Mara's hand found mine in the dark and held on, and I understood that the decision had already been made, by both of us, somewhere in the last hour, without anyone saying it aloud.

Upstream lay the source. Downstream lay hospitals and honesty and a leg that might still be saved. Tomas Reyes had voted with his nine days in the mud.

At dawn, we'd have to ask the river to choose.
