## 11. Triage on the Water

Tomas Reyes started talking to people who weren't there somewhere around the third bend above the root hollow, and by the time I nosed the boat into a side slough to wait out the light, he'd moved on to arguing with them.

I'd heard fever talk before. On the *Corvina*, years back, when a deckhand took a chunk of propeller through the calf and the infection climbed into his blood before we could get him to a hospital. He'd spent his last lucid hours apologizing to his mother for a bicycle he'd stolen when he was nine. So I knew the sound of it—the way a man's voice goes from arguing to pleading to a kind of cheerful, terrible calm—and I knew what it meant when the calm arrived.

Tomas wasn't there yet. But he was walking toward it, and the current was faster than I liked.

Mara had him propped against the gunwale on the deck, her jacket folded under his head, feeding him water in sips small enough that he wouldn't choke. She'd gotten antibiotics into him from the expedition med kit—cracked, water-stained, but the blister packs had held—and she'd cleaned the wound in his side with the last of our potable water, which meant we were now drinking river water boiled in shifts, and I was doing the boiling.

"He says the generator's flooding," Mara said, without looking up. "He keeps saying it. The generator's flooding, someone has to shut the intake."

"Tell him it's shut."

"I did. He looked at me like I was an idiot." She wrung out the rag she'd been using on his forehead into a bucket. "He's an engineer. He doesn't believe anything he can't see the schematics for."

"Then show him a schematic."

"He's hallucinating, Paloma."

"Then lie better." I came over and crouched, taking Tomas's wrist the way I'd take a mooring line in a chop—firm, quick, reading the pulse the way you read a strain gauge. Fast. Thin. The skin under my fingers hot as an engine block left in the sun. "How's the wound look to you?"

"Red. Spreading. There's—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Mara Quist did not like saying things she couldn't take back. "There's a line. Going up toward his chest. I've seen it in first-aid manuals. I know what a line like that means."

I did too. I'd just been hoping she didn't.

I sat back on my heels and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one language I'd never been fluent enough in to stop speaking. We were, by my best dead reckoning, a little under two days' hard run from the last inhabited landing—the one where I'd taken on fuel and been interviewed by men whose politeness had the tensile strength of piano wire. Two days downstream, if I ran the gorges at full throttle and the river cooperated, which the river had shown no interest in doing since the day I was born. Call it thirty hours if everything went right. Thirty hours to a clinic with a real doctor, real antibiotics, real IV lines.

And upstream, past the last survey marker, past the rusted barriers and the shot-through warning plaques, past everything the maps pretended ended at a dotted line—was the source. The reason Daniel Quist had come up this river with a team of scientists and a cover story and a backup drive full of classified manifests. The reason men without insignia had burned their camps and hunted us through two nights of rapids. The reason Tomas had a hole in him and a fever climbing his spine like water finding a crack.

One current. Two directions. And a man on my deck who could only be spent in one of them.

* * *

Mara and I had the argument on the bow, in voices kept low because Tomas had finally gone under—real sleep or fever sleep, hard to say, and I'd stopped pretending I could tell the difference.

"We go upstream," she said. "That's not even a discussion."

"Then let's not discuss it. Let's do the math instead." I kept my tone flat, because I'd learned long ago that the angrier I got, the flatter I had to sound, or the river heard it and gave me something to be angry about. "Thirty hours downstream, best case. He's got sepsis starting. You know what that line on his chest means. Every hour we spend going the wrong way is an hour off his clock."

"Then we go faster."

"Upstream? Against current, through the Gorge of Bells, with a boat that's already taken damage and a fuel load I wouldn't bet a fish on?" I shook my head. "I've run this river my whole life, Mara. I'm telling you what it can do. It doesn't care what we're carrying."

"Then we carry him. Portage."

"With a septic wound, over a portage that took us a full day coming down, with two people to do the carrying?" I looked at her, and she looked back, and I could see her doing her own arithmetic—the same numbers, arriving at the same terrible total, and refusing to sign for it anyway. "You want him to die at the source instead of in a hospital bed. At least be honest with yourself about which one that is."

"That's not—"

"It is. It's exactly that. You're asking a dying man to spend his last hours as a piece of evidence."

The words came out harder than I'd meant them, and I watched them land, watched her flinch and then set her jaw, and I thought: there it is. The Quist family jaw. I'd seen it in photographs of Daniel, that same stubborn architecture, and I was starting to understand that everything I liked about Mara and everything that made her impossible came out of the same bone.

"He's not dying," she said. "People survive sepsis."

"Some do. When they get to hospitals."

"He survived the ambush. He survived nine days in a root hollow. He's the toughest man on this river and you know it."

"I do know it. That's why I'm arguing the way I am." I rubbed my face. My hands smelled of two-stroke fuel and boiled water and, underneath it, the river itself, which never washed off. "Mara. Listen to me. I have spent years learning exactly one skill: knowing which losses to accept. It's why I'm still alive. It's why nobody knows my name. You take the loss you have to take, and you keep the rest of yourself intact, and someday—maybe—you get to spend what you saved. I'm telling you the loss we can afford is the source. Not him."

"And I'm telling you," she said, very quietly, which was worse than shouting, "that my brother died up there. That six other people died up there. That they died because somebody decided the price of silence was worth their lives, and every single person who could have said something chose instead to accept the loss and keep the rest of themselves intact." Her eyes didn't leave mine. "Does that sound like anybody you know?"

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't a confession, and I wasn't ready to give the whole one yet. She knew pieces. She knew I'd been on this river before, on contract, that it had ended badly, that my invisibility had a price tag on it somewhere. She didn't know the amount. I'd tell her. I'd already decided I would, when the river gave us a night long enough to survive the telling. But not with Tomas burning on my deck and pursuers somewhere behind us and the Gorge of Bells between us and everything that mattered.

"Paloma," she said, and her voice cracked, just once, just a hairline fracture, "he's all I have left of him."

And there it was. Not the argument. The thing underneath the argument, which was the only thing that had ever really been underneath any argument I'd ever been part of.

"I know," I said.

"Then help me."

* * *

We might have gone on circling each other until dark, two stubborn wills sharing one current, except that Tomas saved us the trouble of deciding.

He woke at dusk. Not the fever-waking of the afternoon, all schematics and flooding generators, but something clearer, something that had pushed up through the heat like a swimmer through chop. Mara was at his side in an instant, water ready, and I hung back at the rail, because some conversations you're not part of until you're invited.

"Hey," he said. His voice was gravel and rust. "Hey. Where—"

"Side slough, above the second gorge," Mara said. "You're on Paloma's boat. You're safe. Drink."

He drank. Then his eyes found me, and they were too clear for a man with his numbers, and I understood that he knew his numbers too. Engineers always do. It's the ones who do the load calculations on their own bodies who are the worst, because they never get to be pleasantly surprised.

"How long have I got," he said. Not a question. He'd have put a question mark on it if he'd wanted one.

"You'll have as long as it takes to get you downstream," I said.

"Downstream." He tried a laugh and it came out as a cough. "The hospital. Right." He looked at Mara, and something passed between them, some sibling shorthand I wasn't issued the key to, and then he looked back at me. "You two have been arguing about me. Don't bother denying it. I could hear the shape of it through the fever. One of you wants to go up. One of you wants to go back."

Neither of us said anything. The river said plenty. It was doing its evening work against the hull, patient, indifferent, the same sound it made over every drowned thing it had ever kept.

"Let me save you the fuel," Tomas said. "I'm the engineer on this crew. I know what my readings look like. That line on my chest means the infection's in the blood, and blood goes everywhere, and there's no intake valve I can shut." He took a breath, and I watched the cost of it move through him. "So here's my vote, and it's the only vote that matters, because it's my life we're spending."

"Tomas—" Mara started.

"No. Listen. All of you." His hand found hers and gripped it, and even from the rail I could see the effort it cost, and I could see that he'd pay it. "I spent nine days in a root hollow watching ants carry things. You have a lot of time to think, in a root hollow. And what I thought about was this: six people went into that gorge and didn't come out. Daniel. Elena. The Okafor twins. Priya. Marcus. I know their names. I say them every morning like a rosary, because if I stop saying them, the river wins." His eyes were wet now, or the fever was, and it didn't matter which. "And the thing that kept me alive wasn't the water in the roots or the ants. It was the drive. It was knowing that what Daniel found was still real, still sitting in a dry bag, still true. They can burn the camps. They can shoot us in a gorge and call it nature. But they can't burn the truth unless we let them, and letting them means going home and letting the story die with us."

He turned his head on the folded jacket, and looked at the upstream dark, where the river came from, where the maps ended, where the wreck's sister section sat dynamited shut behind whatever wall the navy had built out of silence.

"I'm not asking you to take me to the source," he said. "I'm telling you where I'm spending what's left. If there's a hospital at the end of it, fine. If there's a graveyard, also fine. But I am not going to lie in some clean white room downstream while the men who did this get to keep their names off the paperwork. I've been a piece of cargo my whole life. Boats, planes, trucks. I'm done being cargo." His grip on Mara's hand tightened. "Mara. Little sister. I'm done being cargo."

She made a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life. Not a sob. Something smaller and worse, a sound like a mooring line parting under load—the specific note of a thing that held for a long time and then, all at once, didn't have to anymore.

* * *

I walked to the stern and stood there for a while, because somebody had to make a decision and I was the one with the boat.

The truth—the whole truth, the one I hadn't given Mara yet—was that I'd stood in this exact place before, on this exact river, years ago, with a different dying thing on my deck. Not a man. A decision. I'd been young, and legitimate, and I'd taken a recovery contract that was supposed to be about a wreck and turned out to be about a burial, and when I found what was in the hold, I was offered money instead of a choice, and I took the money, and I told myself the arithmetic worked. One man, one silence, one life kept intact for someday. I'd been spending that someday ever since, and the account had never once gone up.

Tomas was offering me the trade in reverse. Spend everything now. Buy nothing. Witness.

I thought about the deckhand on the *Corvina*, apologizing to his mother for a bicycle. I thought about how the last thing a man says is the thing he was carrying all along, and how Tomas, with his fever and his ants and his rosary of names, had already said his: *they can't burn the truth unless we let them.*

I came back to the bow. Mara was still holding her brother's hand, and her eyes were red, and her jaw was set, and I loved her so hard in that moment it felt like a hull breach—sudden, catastrophic, below the waterline where you can't patch it.

"Here's how it goes," I said. "We run upstream. Nights, when the patrols can't see us. Days, hidden, and Mara boils water and keeps him clean, and I sleep in shifts because I'm no good to anyone dead of exhaustion. We go as far as his readings let us. And the first time that line on his chest reaches his throat—" I looked at Tomas. "You tell me. No heroics, no arguing. You tell me, and I point this boat downstream, and we run for the hospital at full throttle, and whatever we lose to the current, we lose. That's the deal. That's the only deal I'll captain."

Tomas smiled. It cost him, and he paid. "Engineer's terms," he said. "I accept."

Mara looked at me across him, and she didn't say thank you, and I was glad, because thank you would have made it a favor, and it wasn't a favor. It was a reckoning. Mine, finally, coming due.

"One more thing," I said, and I crouched so I was level with Tomas, and I told him the thing I'd been carrying up this river like ballast. Not all of it. The shape of it. That I'd been on this water before, on contract. That I'd found what Daniel found. That I'd taken the money and buried it, and that every mile of my invisibility had been purchased with that silence, and that I was done paying for it. "So when we get to the source," I said, "I'm not going as your pilot. I'm going as a witness. Same as you. Same as Mara. The dead up there deserve three of us, not two."

Tomas studied me for a long moment with those too-clear eyes, and then he nodded, slow, like a man approving a load plan.

"Then we'd better get you there," he said, "before I'm too feverish to remember your confession."

* * *

We cast off into the last of the light, running upstream against a current that had never once done anyone a favor, and I had the throttle open and the bow trimmed and my eyes on the darkening water when Mara, at the rail behind me, went very still.

"Paloma," she said. "Look at the bank."

I looked. On the eastern shore, back from the water in the shadow of the tree line, a fire had just been lit. Small. Deliberate. The kind of fire you build when you want to be seen—or when you want whoever's watching to think you're the one who can't see them.

And below it, just visible where the brush opened to the water, the pale shape of a boat hull. Unmarked. Riding light.

Between us and the source.
