## 12. The Gorge of Bells

The locals had a name for it, and like most names given to dangerous places by people who'd lost someone there, it was meant as a warning dressed up as a story. The Gorge of Bells. Old Ferro at the landing had told me about it my first season on the river, back when I still asked questions instead of avoiding them.

"You'll hear it before you see it," he'd said, tapping his ear with a scarred finger. "The water goes through stone so narrow the river screams. Sounds like church bells drowning. That's how you know. When you hear the bells, you get out of the water, because the water's already decided it doesn't want you."

I'd laughed then. I was younger, and the river hadn't yet shown me its full inventory of ways to kill a man.

Now, three days upstream from the burned camp, with Tomas Reyes burning fever in the bow and two boats' worth of pursuit somewhere behind us, I heard the bells.

It started as a change in the air pressure more than a sound—a low thrumming in my chest, in the boat's hull, in the fillings of my teeth. Then it climbed. The river ahead bent around a shoulder of black rock, and the sound coming around that bend was unlike anything water should make. Not a roar. Roars are simple. This was layered, ringing, almost musical—the river forced through a throat of stone no wider than a wagon road, compressing all its fury into frequencies that didn't so much hit your ears as take them hostage.

Mara stood up in the stern, one hand on Tomas's shoulder to steady him, and her face did something complicated.

"That's not natural," she said.

"Neither is anything else we've seen this week."

"I mean it literally doesn't sound possible. The acoustics—"

"Mara." I cut the engine and let us drift. "It means we're close, and it means we're done boating. Nobody runs the Gorge of Bells. Not because it can't be done. Because the men who tried aren't available for interviews."

Tomas stirred under the tarp I'd rigged against the sun. His fever had a rhythm now—raging at night, grudging remission by day—and in the mornings he surfaced like a diver coming up too fast, gasping, disoriented, asking questions about things that hadn't happened yet. This morning he'd gripped my wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, "Don't let them bury it again," before sliding back under.

The evidence rode with us in a waterproof case lashed under the thwart: Daniel Quist's journals, the drive Mara had decrypted, photographs of manifests stamped with classifications that shouldn't have existed on a ship the navy swore it never operated. And beside it, wrapped in oilcloth, my dive charges—the reason I'd stopped at every wrecking yard between the delta and the headwaters, trading salvage gear for primers and gel, lying to men who were used to being lied to.

We beached on a gravel shelf below the gorge's southern wall and started unloading.

* * *

Portaging a boat is an argument between optimism and physics, and physics has better lawyers. The Aluna was shallow-draft aluminum, maybe four hundred pounds dry, closer to six hundred loaded with what remained of our supplies after the night runners took their toll. The trail along the south bank—if you could call a goat path a trail—climbed forty feet above the water and hugged the cliff face for the length of the gorge. Maybe two kilometers of carrying everything twice.

I went first with the heaviest loads, cutting brush, testing footing. Mara organized with the ruthless efficiency I'd come to expect from her—she'd been a logistics coordinator for the expedition, she told me once, which apparently meant she could turn chaos into inventory lists. She'd divided our remaining world into three categories: essential, heavy-essential, and things we'd burn if pursuit got close.

Tomas couldn't walk. We both knew it and neither of us said it. He weighed ninety pounds less than he had at the landing, and his skin had gone the color of old candle wax. We made him a shelter under an overhang near the boat, within sight of the water, and left him with the rifle, the pistol, and instructions that amounted to *shoot anything that isn't us.*

"If they come while you're in the gorge," he said, catching my arm as I turned to go. His voice was gravel and static. "You don't come back for me. You hear? You keep going. The evidence is the mission now. I'm just—" He coughed, wet and deep. "I'm just the man who carried it this far."

"You're the only witness who survived," I said. "That makes you the most valuable thing on this river. More than the documents. Documents can be forged. A living man who saw what they did—that's harder to disappear."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I understood that he was trying to decide whether I was telling him the truth or just telling him something kind. Fever makes philosophers of everyone.

"Go," he said finally. "Before I say something sentimental."

I went.

* * *

The portage took six hours and cost us everything it was going to cost.

The trail was worse than it looked from the water, which is a special achievement for a trail that looks terrible from the water. In places it narrowed to eighteen inches of crumbling shale with the gorge dropping away sheer on the left, the river visible far below as a white vein hammering through black rock. The bells never stopped. After an hour they stopped being sound and became weather—you breathed them, sweated them, felt them in the architecture of your skull. Conversation was impossible. We developed a system of hand signals, and Mara's were crisp and impatient, like everything else about her.

On the fourth trip—I'd lost count of the total loads; my shoulders had filed a formal complaint and gone on strike—Mara insisted on carrying the evidence case herself.

"It's my brother's work," she said, when I reached for it. "I carry it."

There was no arguing with that voice. I'd learned that around day two. So she took the case, slung across her chest like a bandolier, and I took the fuel drums, and we moved along the cliff path single file, me leading, the roar of the bells swallowing even our footfalls.

She was ten feet behind me when the rock went.

No warning. No crack, no groan—the bells ate every sound the mountain made. One moment the ledge was there, holding her weight the way it had held mine a dozen times that day. The next moment it was a slide of shale and Mara was going down with it, sideways, toward the drop, and I spun and threw myself flat and caught her wrist as the world tilted.

For one long second we were both suspended in the noise—her weight pulling me toward the edge, my boots scrabbling for purchase on stone that had decided, today, to stop being reliable. Below us the river churned white through its stone throat, fifty feet down, maybe sixty, churning with enough force to strip a body to the bone before it ever reached the sea.

I got my other hand on her forearm. Got a knee wedged against a root. Pulled.

She came up over the lip of the trail gasping, and we lay there together on our backs, chests heaving, while the bells rang on above us, indifferent as God.

"Ankle," she managed, when she could talk. "When I slid. I think—"

I checked it. Swollen already, darkening at the joint. Not broken—I'd seen enough breaks to know—but a bad sprain, the kind that swells to the size of a grapefruit by morning and turns walking into a theological debate.

She stared up at the canopy, jaw tight, and I watched her do the arithmetic. Two kilometers of trail left. Six hundred pounds of boat. A man dying of infection at the far end. Pursuit behind us, hours not days.

"I can hop," she said.

"You can't hop two kilometers of cliff path carrying classified evidence."

"Then what's your plan, Paloma?"

I looked at her—at this woman who'd forced her way onto my boat with nothing but a satellite phone and a refusal to accept silence as an answer, who'd held steady through gunfire and rapids and the slow-motion death of her brother's whole team, who was lying on a cliff ledge with a ruined ankle and already calculating how to make it someone else's problem instead of hers.

"My plan," I said, "is that I carried dive gear up mountainsides for a living before I got smart. Get on my back."

"Absolutely not."

"It wasn't a request. It was a load rating."

She fought me for exactly as long as it took her to try putting weight on the ankle. Then she swore—a long, inventive, multilingual oath that I later suspected she'd learned from engineers—and let me haul her up.

So I carried her. Evidence case and all, her arms looped over my shoulders, her breath hot against my neck, the weight of her and the weight of Daniel Quist's truth riding together on my spine while the gorge rang its drowned bells and the trail crumbled inch by inch beneath my boots.

Somewhere in the second kilometer, she said, quietly, into my ear: "You could have left me at the landing. Everyone else did."

"Yeah, well." I shifted her weight, found new footing. "Everyone else didn't owe your brother a debt."

"You never told me what the debt was."

"No," I said. "I haven't."

And she didn't push, because she was learning my silences the way I was learning her stubbornness—one concession at a time, both of us keeping accounts neither of us intended to settle until we absolutely had to.

* * *

We came off the trail at dusk, into the pool below the gorge where the river widened and caught its breath. The upper water was different here—faster, colder, tasting of stone and snowmelt. The last survey marker had been a day behind us. From here on, the maps were guesses.

Tomas was alive. Barely. The fever had climbed while we were gone, and he'd fired the rifle once at nothing—or at something he'd seen in the trees that we never found. His eyes tracked wrong when I knelt beside him.

"Bells," he whispered. "I heard the bells. Daniel loved that story. He wanted to—" Coughing fit. "He wanted to record them. For the archive. Said no one would believe—"

"Save it," I said gently. "Tell it to the inquiry. All of it. That's the job now."

Mara made camp with her ankle propped on a log, directing operations by pointing and glaring, refusing painkillers she felt she hadn't earned. I cooked the last of the rice with river water and condensed milk and pretended it was food. We ate in the blue half-light, listening to the bells fade behind us as the evening wind turned, and for twenty minutes the world was almost quiet.

Then I heard the engine.

Not ours. Upstream, around the bend above the gorge—two strokes, badly tuned, running fast. I killed our fire with a boot heel and put Mara and Tomas into the rocks, and I took the rifle and moved toward the waterline, because whoever was running that engine at night, above the Gorge of Bells, was either suicidal or following us, and I'd stopped believing in coincidence somewhere around the burned camp.

They came around the bend at speed, running the upper water with more confidence than sense—a low-slung skiff, black-hulled, no lights, no registration, two men aboard. Men without insignia. My favorite kind of company.

They were running too fast for the water they were in. I saw it before they did—the channel above the gorge's mouth funnels hard to starboard, and there's a seam in the current where the fast water meets the slack, and if you hit that seam at speed in the dark, the river takes the wheel.

They hit the seam.

The skiff slewed sideways. I heard shouting—two voices, sharp with sudden understanding—and then the hull struck the rocks at the gorge's mouth and folded around them like paper, and both men went into the water.

One of them surfaced immediately, downstream of the wreck, clawing for the slack eddy near the shore. The other didn't surface at all.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the whole point of what's left of my life. My first instinct was to stand very still in the shadows and let the river finish its work. These were the men who'd burned Daniel Quist's camp. Who'd shot at us in the dark. Who'd put a bullet hole in the hull four inches from where Mara slept. Every survival instinct I'd spent seven years sharpening told me to watch, and wait, and count this as the river settling one of its own accounts.

But the man in the water wasn't swimming anymore. He'd gone under, come up, gone under again, and when he came up the third time he wasn't making progress toward anything—he was just fighting, and losing, and the gorge's mouth was dragging him backward toward the bells.

I dropped the rifle and went in.

The cold hit like a fist. I swam the eddy line, got a hand on his jacket as he went down again, and hauled him up. He was young—younger than me by ten years, maybe more, a kid in a black tactical vest with a face I couldn't see and hands that grabbed me with the mindless strength of drowning. We went under together, twice, the roar of the gorge sucking at us like a drain, and I got an arm around his chest and kicked for the shallows and the river argued every inch.

I got him to the gravel bar. Got him breathing—water and vomit and then air, ragged, wonderful air. I rolled him onto his side and knelt there in the shallows, soaked to the soul, and said, "You're okay. Breathe. You're okay."

And that's when the second man came out of the trees.

He had a pistol. Of course he had a pistol. He stood at the edge of the gravel bar with the weapon leveled at us, and in the moonlight I could see his face for the first time—hard, wet, furious, and underneath the fury something rattled, because his partner was coughing river water at my feet and the man holding that partner's life together was me.

"Step away from him," the man said.

"He's aspirating. If I step away, he drowns in his own lungs on dry land."

"Step. Away."

Behind him, up the slope, I heard movement—Mara, dragging herself through the rocks on a ruined ankle, doing God knew what, probably preparing to do something heroic and catastrophic. I raised my free hand, kept the other on the kid's back.

"You want to shoot me, shoot me," I said. "But ask yourself who pulls your partner out of the water after. Because I'm the only thing standing between him and the rest of that river, and you've got maybe thirty seconds before he stops breathing again, and every second you spend pointing that gun at me is a second you're spending on him."

The muzzle wavered.

The kid under my hands convulsed, coughed out another cupful of gorge, and sucked in air with a sound like a torn sail.

The man with the gun looked at his partner. Looked at me. And I watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes—the same arithmetic I'd watched run behind a hundred faces in another life, the cold professional calculus of men whose jobs ran on deniability. A dead witness was clean. A dead rescuer was messy. A partner who owed his life to the target was a complication that would follow them both for the rest of their careers.

"Who are you?" he said.

"Nobody," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

He backed up the slope without lowering the pistol entirely, and then he was gone into the trees, and I was alone on the gravel bar with a half-drowned man who worked for the people who'd killed Daniel Quist's team, holding that man's life in my hands like something I'd fished out of the river that I couldn't put back.

The kid looked up at me, gray-faced, shaking, and said the last thing I expected.

"They're not taking orders from the navy anymore," he rasped. "You understand? The commander's gone off the map. Whatever he tells you at the source—it's not sanctioned. It's just him."

And then he passed out, and I sat in the freezing shallows holding a stranger's secret along with everything else, and up the slope Mara's voice came low through the dark:

"Paloma. Tell me you didn't just save one of them."

I looked at the unconscious man, at the gorge roaring its bells behind us, at the black water that had tried to keep us both.

"Mara," I said, "I need you to help me tie him up. And then I need to tell you something I should have told you at the landing."

Because the kid had said *the commander*, and I knew that name, and I knew that voice, and the past I'd weighted down and sunk seven years ago had just surfaced again—breathing, this time, and pointed straight at the source.
