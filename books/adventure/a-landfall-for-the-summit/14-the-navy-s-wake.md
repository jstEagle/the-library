## 14. The Navy's Wake

I heard the diesel before I saw anything—two engines, running in that lazy, throttled-back way boats run when they're not going anywhere fast and don't need to be. That's the sound of a search, if you've spent enough years listening to rivers the way I have. A boat in a hurry makes a different noise. A boat hunting makes this one: patient, wide, sweeping.

I killed our engine mid-channel and let us drift, and I put my hand up before Mara could say anything, because she was already halfway to questions.

"Listen," I said.

She listened. Tomas lay in the bow under a tarp among the salvage crates, feverish, breathing in that shallow scraped way that told me the infection in his leg had stopped negotiating and started making demands. Three days since we'd pulled him out of the root hollow. Two days since we'd made the choice at the fork—Mara arguing for the source, me arguing for mercy, Tomas settling it himself with a voice like gravel poured slow: *the truth.* So here we were, committed, hauling a dying man up a dead river toward a place that officially did not exist, and now something official was coming down it.

The bend above us held its water flat and green. Around it came the wake first—a long V drawn clean across the surface—and then the hull.

It was gray. Not navy-gray exactly, not the bright honest gray of a patrol cutter on postcard duty, but a working gray, the color of something that didn't want to be photographed. No number on the bow that I could see from this distance. No flag. A .50 mount forward, canvas-covered, which is how you cover a gun when you want people to know there's one under the canvas.

"Friends of yours?" Mara whispered.

"I don't have friends," I said. "That's the whole point of me."

* * *

We got off the main channel inside ninety seconds. There's a side slough about two hundred meters below the bend—I'd marked it years ago and never had reason to use it, a slot between drowned timber where the water went shallow and brown and the overgrowth closed overhead like a hand folding shut. I backed us in with the pole, quiet as I know how to be, which is quieter than most people would believe and less quiet than I'd like. The tarp went over everything. Mara knelt by Tomas with her hand light on his shoulder, keeping him still, keeping him down, and he looked up at her with eyes gone glassy and bright and said nothing, because he understood better than either of us what being found would mean.

The patrol came around the bend doing maybe eight knots, and it slowed further as it passed our slough. Slowed, and hung there a moment, engines idling, the wash slapping against the drowned trees. Through the leaves I could see shapes on deck. Four, maybe five men. Binoculars. One of them talking into a handset. None of them wearing anything you could call a uniform, which is its own kind of uniform, if you follow me.

They moved on. But they were moving upstream, and we were going upstream, and a river only has so many directions in it. Sooner or later the math catches you.

"We can't outrun them," Mara said when the sound had faded to nothing. "Not with Tomas like this. Not loaded the way we are."

"No," I agreed. "We can't."

"So what do we do?"

I looked at the water around us, brown and patient, and then at the bank, and an old memory surfaced—the kind of memory I usually keep weighted down. Years ago, diving a recovery contract on this same river, I'd taken shelter from a storm in a flooded cave a few miles up. Limestone country. The river had eaten its way into the rock over centuries, and somewhere along that stretch there was a cave mouth below the waterline, big enough to swim a boat's worth of gear into, dry shelf rock inside where a man could sit out a war if he had to.

"There's a hole in the world about six miles up," I said. "Water goes in but doesn't come back out, far as anyone knows. We put Tomas in there, dry and hidden, and then—"

"And then what?"

"And then I give them something else to look at."

Mara stared at me. She'd been staring at me a lot lately, and each time it lasted a little longer, and each time I minded it a little less, which should have worried me more than it did.

"You're going to bait a gunboat," she said. "In a skiff. Alone."

"When you say it like that it sounds stupid."

"How else would you like me to say it?"

* * *

We ran the six miles in the last of the light, hugging the shadowed bank, and found the cave mouth exactly where my memory said it would be—a black seam in the limestone, waist-deep at the entrance, opening into a chamber where the water went glass-still and the air smelled of wet stone and old time. I poled us in stern-first. Inside, a shelf of dry rock sloped up out of the water, big enough for a man to lie on, big enough for supplies.

Getting Tomas in was the worst work I've done on this river, and I've done some bad work on this river. He tried to help. That was the worst part—he kept trying to help, gripping the rocks with hands that shook, biting down on his own sounds until I thought he'd crack a tooth. Mara took his head in her lap once we had him on the shelf, and fed him watered-down ration broth spoonful by spoonful, and talked to him low and steady about nothing—about their grandmother's kitchen, about a dog they'd had as children, about anything that wasn't here.

I unloaded half our medical kit, all the antibiotics, the last of the clean water, the rifle's spare magazine because I'm not a complete fool even when I'm being a reckless one. Then I crouched by Tomas, and he caught my wrist with surprising strength.

"They'll come back," he said. "They always come back."

"I'm counting on it," I said.

He almost smiled. Fever does strange favors sometimes.

Mara walked me to the water's edge, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because we'd gotten good at that—saying nothing together, letting the river do the talking. Then she grabbed the front of my shirt with both fists, hard, and pulled me close enough that I could see the exact color of her fear.

"You come back," she said. "I'm not asking. I'm telling you how this ends. You come back, Paloma."

"Yes," I said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that's true."

She let go of my shirt. She smoothed it flat where she'd crushed it, which was somehow worse than the grabbing, and then she turned away and went back to Tomas, and I slid into the water and swam out into the dusk.

* * *

Here's the thing about baiting a patrol boat: you don't run from them. Running tells them you're the thing they're looking for. You have to be something else entirely—something boring, something explainable, something that makes them feel clever for dismissing you.

So I became a smuggler.

There's a stretch of river twenty miles downstream where the real small-time traffic runs—fuel, cigarettes, outboard parts, the ordinary gray economy that keeps every remote waterway alive. I knew their routes, their timings, their habits, because I'd spent years being careful never to be part of them while knowing everything about them anyway. Invisibility isn't absence. It's choreography.

I waited until full dark, then ran downstream with no lights, which is normal behavior for that trade, and positioned myself crossing the channel at the narrows where the patrol boat would have to pass on its evening sweep. I let them see me—let my running lights flicker on just long enough, let them catch the silhouette of a shallow-draft skiff moving heavy and furtive, the universal signature of somebody moving something they shouldn't.

Then I ran.

Not toward the cave. Never toward the cave. Downstream, toward the narrows, into the worst water on that stretch—the S-turn rapids where the channel kinked twice around rock shelves that ate propellers for breakfast. I've run those rapids maybe forty times, and every run I've made has been in daylight with a fresh motor and a clear conscience. This was none of those things.

The patrol boat lit me up at the top of the turn. A searchlight, white and absolute, pinning me to the water like a specimen. A loudhailer cracked the night: instructions to heave to, in that flat official voice that pretends it isn't a threat.

I heaved to the throttle instead.

The first turn I took blind, reading the water by the white of it, by the sound of it, by thirty years of listening to rivers tell me where their bones are. The skiff slammed off a wave crest and landed hard enough to knock the wind out of me, and something in the stern cracked—not structural, I prayed, please not structural—and the second turn came up faster than the first and I took it wider than I wanted, scraping shale, sparks of pain through the hull, and behind me the searchlight swung wild as the patrol boat hesitated at the top of the rapids.

That hesitation was the whole plan. A gray boat with a covered gun doesn't chase a smuggler into bad water at night. Too much risk to the equipment. Too many questions if things go wrong. They'd mark my position, note my heading, and file me under *nuisance, criminal, minor.*

Except they didn't hesitate quite long enough.

They came in after me. Cautious, guns out, searchlight bouncing off the spray—but they came in, and the second turn caught them the way it catches everyone who doesn't live on this river. I heard the impact before I saw it, a deep grinding crunch of hull on shelf rock, and then the searchlight swung up at the sky and stayed there, and the loudhailer said something that dissolved into shouting, and I did the professional thing, which was to stop watching and start surviving.

Because I'd miscalculated one thing. Just one. In my beautiful little plan, I'd assumed they'd break off and I'd circle back through back channels to the cave by midnight. Instead they'd followed me into the rapids, which meant the only exit from the rapids was past them, which meant I had to keep running downstream—twenty miles of dark water, a cracked hull taking on water, and a fuel gauge I hadn't checked since afternoon because I'd been busy being brave.

The gauge, when I finally checked it, gave me the answer I deserved.

* * *

I ran her dry eleven miles later, in a flooded timber stand off the true channel, and I'll tell you honestly that I sat there in the dark with water sloshing over the transom and felt something very close to laughter building in my chest. Thirty years of careful, invisible, meticulous survival, and I'd ended up marooned in a swamp because I'd gotten clever. The river forgives nothing. It especially forgives nothing in men who think they've figured it out.

But I'd grown up poor on water like this, before I ever grew up careful, and poor teaches you things careful never does. I stripped the skiff's fuel line, siphoned the dregs from the reserve tank, found a half-jerrican wedged under a crate that I'd forgotten loading—which is the kind of luck you don't question, you just accept with your hat off. Enough for maybe eight miles at idle. The cave was twelve away, upstream, against current.

So I rowed. Or near enough—trolling motor at lowest draw, paddle when the battery sagged, poling through the shallows, dragging through the deadfalls. Six hours. The sky went from black to bruise to the pale gray that isn't dawn yet but is thinking about it. My hands split open along old rope scars. Somewhere in the fourth hour I fell asleep for a second or two at the paddle and woke with my face an inch off the water, and after that I bit the inside of my cheek bloody to stay awake.

All the while, the thought that kept me pulling was simple and enormous: *she's in there in the dark with a dying man, and she believes I'm coming back.*

You can survive a lot on a thought like that. I recommend it, if you ever find one.

* * *

The cave mouth showed gray-black ahead of me just as the light came up enough to trust my eyes. I swam the skiff in, grounded it on the inner shelf, and stood there dripping, swaying, half-dead, and Mara rose from where she'd been sitting against the wall—rose the way people rise when they haven't slept, all at once and unsteadily—and looked at me for a long moment.

Then she hit me. Open palm, across the arm, not hard, once.

"You were gone nine hours," she said. "Nine. And the radio's dead and I couldn't—" Her voice did something complicated and came back down. "Tomas spiked to forty degrees at three in the morning. I thought he was dying. I sat here holding his hand in the dark thinking, *when Paloma comes back, if Paloma comes back,* and I hated you for making me think the word *if.*"

"I'm sorry," I said.

"Don't apologize." She was crying now, quietly, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like the tears were a mechanical fault she intended to fix herself. "Do you know what I did? While you were out there being heroic? I boiled water. All night. Over a spirit stove, in a cave, so it would be clean when he needed it. I changed his dressing twice. I kept the lamp hooded so nobody outside would see glow off the water. I wrote down every hour, his temperature, what I gave him, because Daniel was the same, Daniel kept logs, and if I'm going to lose someone I'm going to lose him *documented.*"

She stopped. Breathed.

"I kept him alive," she said. "You kept yourself alive. Between us, we might manage."

I looked past her at Tomas, asleep on the shelf, sweat-soaked but breathing deeper than yesterday, the infection's advance visibly stalled—held, not beaten, but held. At the log book open beside him in her precise, furious handwriting. At the rows of cooled boiled water in cleaned cans, ranked like ammunition.

Thirty years I'd survived alone, and I had told myself it was discipline. Standing in that cave, looking at what devotion looks like when it stops declaring itself and starts boiling water at three in the morning—it occurred to me that everything I'd called self-sufficiency was just loneliness with good posture.

"Mara," I said.

"Don't," she said. "Not yet. When we're out of this. When Tomas is safe. When it's finished—you can say whatever it is then. Say it to me in daylight, somewhere with a horizon. Until then, save your breath for the poles."

She handed me a can of boiled water, and I drank it, and it was the best thing I'd ever tasted, and I understood that this was her answer, given in her language: *I kept the fire going. Now keep rowing.*

* * *

We left the cave an hour later, Tomas stabilized on the shelf of the skiff, Mara at the tiller while I slept in the bilge like cargo—which she refused to allow for exactly four minutes before practicality beat pride, another thing I was learning about her. I woke at noon to the smell of river and the sound of her humming, very quietly, some tuneless thing, and I lay there a moment longer than I needed to, memorizing it.

Then I saw why she'd woken me.

Ahead, where the river bent north toward the high country, the valley walls closed in and the water went strange—faster, greener, threaded with mist that didn't move with the wind. On the left bank, half-swallowed by vines, stood a rusted barrier boom, and beside it a concrete post bearing a plaque shot through so many times the lettering survived only in fragments:

...CLOSED BY ORDER... DANGER TO NAVIGATION... BY ORDER OF...

And beyond it, past the barrier, the river ran clear as glass, spring-fed, impossibly blue-green, the clearest water I had ever seen in my life—water so transparent it looked less like water than like a wound in the world, a window laid flat over the depths.

Past the last survey marker. Past the maps. Past everything.

"The source," Mara said softly. "Twelve miles."

Behind us, far downstream, faint as memory, a diesel engine changed pitch.

Two of them, this time.
