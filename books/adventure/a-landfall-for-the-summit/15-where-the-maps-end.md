## 15. Where the Maps End

The last survey marker was a brass disc bolted to a granite shoulder of rock, and it was the loneliest thing I'd ever seen that wasn't dead.

SURVEY STATION 47 — RIO ALTO BASIN — 1974, it said, though the river had eaten most of the letters down to suggestions. Somebody had pried at it with a knife at some point—old gouges around the rim—but they'd given up before they got it loose, or maybe they'd only wanted to prove they could. Forty years of moss had grown over the vandalism like skin over a scar.

I idled past it slow enough to touch it if I'd wanted to, which I didn't. Touching things is how you leave fingerprints.

"Is that really it?" Mara asked from the bow. She had her brother's coordinates open on the satellite phone, cross-checked against a paper chart she'd annotated in pencil until the margins looked like a madman's diary. "Past this point there's nothing? No data?"

"There's data," I said. "It's just forty years stale and classified. The river doesn't care what's on the map. It never has."

She looked back at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing for two days now—the same arithmetic I did every time we rounded a bend and didn't die. Tomas lay under the tarp in the stern, breathing in shallow sips, his fever climbing by degrees I could measure against my own dread.

We went through.

* * *

I've spent most of my life in water you can't see through. Silt water, tea-colored water, water the color of old regret. You dive it by feel, by memory, by faith in your own hands. It teaches you a certain humility. It also teaches you not to trust anything you can't touch.

So when the river changed an hour past Station 47, it unsettled me more than any rapid had.

It happened between one bend and the next. The current steadied, the brown gave way to green, and then the green gave way to nothing at all—water so clear it stopped looking like water. The bottom appeared thirty feet down as if there were no medium between us and it: boulders furred with moss, a fallen tree silvered by decades, fish hanging in the current like ornaments somebody had forgotten to take down. My wake spread across the surface and I could watch it all the way out, every ring, like the river was showing me its own math.

Mara leaned over the gunwale and said something, and I heard her perfectly at twenty feet, which shouldn't be possible on moving water.

"It's spring-fed," I said. "All of it. From here up, the river isn't running off the mountains—it's coming up out of the ground. Cold, filtered, no sediment." I kept my eyes forward because looking at her looking at the water was doing something to my chest I didn't have time for. "Nobody fishes it. Nobody swims it. That's why."

"That's why what?"

"Why the closure held." I throttled down and let us drift. "You can close a muddy river with a fence and a sign and men with guns, and people will still sneak in, because muddy rivers hide things. But this—" I gestured at the impossible clarity below us. "This looks like a church. People feel watched in water like this. Even the ones who come to do wrong."

Especially the ones who come to do wrong. I knew that from the inside.

The cold came off the surface in a thin white breath. Upstream, the valley narrowed, and the walls rose gray and wet, and somewhere ahead—I couldn't hear it yet, but I could feel it in the way the current leaned, patient and insistent—the river was pulling toward its source like a rope paying out.

"Paloma." Mara's voice had gone flat, careful. "Look at the bank. Left side."

I looked.

Rusted barriers ran along the shoreline where the valley first pinched—posts sunk into the rock, connected once by chain or cable, long since surrendered to rust. Behind them, half-swallowed by brush, stood a concrete blockhouse the size of an outhouse, its roof caved in, its door gone. And mounted beside where the door used to be, a plaque.

I brought us alongside without being asked. Some things you don't decide; your hands decide for you.

The plaque was bronze, and someone had shot it. Not once—a dozen times, methodically, the way you'd shoot something if you hated it but couldn't afford to remove it. The lettering survived in fragments:

**RESTRICTED WATERS — BY ORDER OF —**
**NO ENTRY BEYOND THIS POINT**
**BY AUTHORITY OF THE MINISTRY OF —**

And beneath, smaller, the line that made Mara make a sound low in her throat:

**DANGER — SUBMERGED HAZARDS — NO DIVING**

"They warned people away from diving," she said. "At the source of the river. Why would you—" She stopped. I watched her get there, watched it land on her face the way it had landed on mine three days ago and hadn't left since. "Because there's something down there they don't want found."

"Or something down there that already found them," I said.

Tomas coughed from the stern. It was a bad sound, wet and deep, the kind of sound a chest makes when it's filing complaints it intends to pursue. We moved.

* * *

We ran the clear water through the afternoon, and the signs kept coming, each one older and more honest than the last.

A second barrier line, higher up, its posts bent outward—not rusted apart but *bent*, forced, by something heavy coming through in a hurry. A stretch of bank where the vegetation still hadn't recovered right, growing in a different texture than everything around it, the way ground does after it's been dug up and packed back down by machinery. A mile of shoreline studded with old rebar stubs, spaced too regularly to be natural, marking something that had been built and then un-built, leaving only the teeth behind.

Mara catalogued it all—photographs, notes, coordinates—working with a focus that frightened me a little, because I recognized it. It was the focus of a woman converting grief into fuel, and fuel burns whatever container you put it in.

"You're quiet," she said at one point, catching me watching her.

"I'm navigating."

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Navigators look at the water. You keep looking at me like I'm going to dissolve."

"I'm weighing odds," I said, which was true, just not the whole truth. The whole truth was that somewhere in the last two days—somewhere between the burned camp and the gorge and the night she'd sat up with Tomas, holding his hand and talking to him steadily about nothing, about their childhood, about a dog they'd had—she had become the fixed point my boat swung around. And fixed points are dangerous for a man whose entire survival strategy has been drift.

Ahead, the valley opened into a long lake-like reach, and there, on a gravel shelf at the far end, stood the third barrier—and this one told the whole story in a single image.

They'd tried to dam it. Not the river—the *road*. A steel gate, industrial, wide enough for vehicles, anchored into the rock on both sides of the narrowest neck of shore. It hung open now, sagging on one hinge, seized with forty years of rust. But the gate wasn't the story. The story was the sign mounted on it, and the state of the sign.

Someone had shot this one too, but differently. Not hating it. *Silencing* it. Every word had been targeted individually, each line of text obliterated with tight clusters, leaving only the border and the date stamp legible: **1974**.

"They shot the words out," Mara said quietly. "Not the sign. The words."

"Somebody wanted the sign to stay," I said. "As a warning to whoever maintained it. *We can reach your signs.*"

Tomas woke while we were passing the gate. He surfaced from the fever like a man coming up from depth too fast—eyes wild, hands grabbing, words tumbling out in Spanish first and then English, half of it about pressure gauges and valves and *they're still down there, don't let them vent it, don't let them—*

Mara held him down and talked him back, and I watched the river run clear and cold past my gunwale and thought about how much of my life I'd spent pretending water was just water.

* * *

We camped that night on a gravel bar in mid-channel, because mid-channel meant nobody could approach without us hearing water change against a hull, and I've slept to that alarm system longer than some of the men hunting us have been alive.

Tomas was worse. There was no dressing it up anymore, and I'd stopped trying. The wound in his side had gone angry red at the edges, and the fever had a rhythm now—climbing through the evening, breaking sweat around midnight, then climbing again from a higher floor. He was lucid in windows, maybe an hour at a time, and in those windows he drank water and answered questions and sometimes, unprompted, gave us pieces of what he'd seen, as if his subconscious understood he was testifying and might not get another session.

"The hold," he said, in one of the windows, staring up at stars so thick they looked like spillage. "Daniel got into the sealed section. Forward hold, behind the false bulkhead. Documents in waterproof cases—hundreds of them. Manifests. A casualty list." His voice caught on the word. "Civilians. Forty-one names. They scuttled her on top of it. The wreck isn't an accident site. It's a *lid*."

"We know," Mara said softly. "We know, Tomás. Rest."

"And the source—" His hand found her wrist with surprising strength. "The source is the other lid. You'll see. When the maps end, that's where they put the rest of it."

Then the fever took him back, and the night got very long.

Mara sat beside him, and I sat at the fire line with the rifle across my knees, and neither of us spoke for a long time, because some silences are conversations.

"He's dying," she finally said. Not a question. She'd earned the flatness of it.

"He's fighting," I said. "But yes. Without a hospital, without antibiotics, without—" I stopped myself from listing the whole inventory of things we didn't have. "Hours matter now. Maybe a day. Two if he's stubborn, and he's shown me stubborn."

"So we're close enough." She turned to look at me, firelight on one side of her face, starlight on the other, and I thought: *this is what it costs, standing in the open. This is what it buys.* "How far to the source?"

"By water, half a day. Less if the channel's clean." I fed a stick to the fire. "But the source isn't a place you arrive at, Mara. It's a place you go *under*. Everything Daniel found says the record's in the flooded caverns. Which means one of us dives it while the other tends a dying man in country full of men with guns."

"One of us dives it," she said. "Both of us go. We're not splitting up. Splitting up is how expeditions die—that's practically the first thing I learned from my brother's field notes, and he learned it the hard way nine days ago."

I almost smiled. Almost. "You've been reading his notes for tactics."

"I've been reading his notes for *him*." Her voice cracked, just once, and she pressed it flat again with visible effort. "He wrote that the river forgives nothing. That you have to earn every mile. So we'll earn it."

Across the fire, Tomas stirred and murmured something that might have been a name or might have been a depth reading. The clear water slid past our gravel bar, black now under the stars but still somehow transparent, still somehow honest, carrying its cold breath up from whatever vast dark throat it rose from.

And that was when I heard it—or felt it, first, in the soles of my feet through the gravel, before my ears caught up.

Engines. More than one. Far upstream, beyond the sagging gate, beyond the shot-out words, coming *down* toward us—or holding position, patrolling, waiting. In the silence of this valley, sound carried like a confession.

I killed the fire with a sweep of sand and put my hand on Mara's shoulder in the dark.

"Don't move," I whispered. "They're already past the place where maps end. Which means they know exactly where they're going."

She went very still beside me. Below us, the clear water ran on toward its source, patient as testimony, and upstream, in the dark, the engines idled like held breath.
