## 16. The Forbidden Source

The river changed its mind about itself two bends past the last survey marker.

I'd been reading water for thirty years, and I'd never seen anything like it. One moment we were pushing through the usual brown-green broth the river had worn like a coat since the landing, and the next the bottom simply *arrived*—every stone, every sunken log, every pale fish hanging in the current, lit up like the water had been poured in that morning and nobody had gotten around to dirtying it yet.

"Spring-fed," I said, mostly to myself. "We're past the aquifer line."

Mara leaned over the gunwale, watching a school of something silver scatter beneath us. "It looks like glass."

"It looks like a lie," I said. "Rivers this clear are rivers nobody's allowed to use."

Behind us, Tomas made a sound that wasn't quite a word. He was wedged in the bow on a bed of salvage tarps I'd arranged into something like a stretcher, and he'd been fading since dawn in the way men fade when fever stops being a visitor and starts paying rent. His skin had gone the color of the old charts in my cabin, yellowed and brittle-looking, and every bump of the hull against a rock drew a breath through his teeth that I felt in my own chest.

"Close?" Mara asked him.

"Close," he managed, and then, because engineers apparently can't help themselves even while dying: "The water table's feeding it from the left bank. Feel the temperature drop?"

I felt it. The current had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with altitude, a deep cold, cellar cold, the cold of water that had been sitting in the dark for a long time thinking about things.

The signs started a mile later, and they were the kind of signs you shoot through.

The first barrier was a line of rusted pilings across the river, chained once, the chains long since cut or rotted, the pilings themselves furred with forty years of moss. A plaque had been bolted to the largest of them. I pulled the boat alongside and read it, or read what was left of it. Most of the lettering had been obliterated by bullet holes—someone had stood in a boat, probably in this exact spot, and emptied a magazine into a government sign until the government's words stopped being legible.

What survived read: *—BY ORDER OF ——— RESTRICTED WATERS ——— AUTHORIZED PER—*

"Somebody felt strongly about this," Mara said.

"Somebody was told to feel strongly about this," I said. "There's a difference. The first kind signs their work."

The second barrier was newer, or had been maintained longer—concrete posts, steel cable, a padlock the size of my fist still hanging from a hasp, green with age. Beyond it the river narrowed into a canyon of gray stone, and the water coming out of that canyon was so cold it steamed faintly in the morning air, a ghost of a ghost.

I put the nose of the boat against the cable and looked back at Mara. She looked at Tomas. Tomas, eyes half-shut, said, "Cut it. I'll take the blame. I'm not going to be alive long enough for the paperwork."

"Cheerful man," I said, and got out the bolt cutters.

* * *

The canyon swallowed sound the way I'd been told the Gorge of Bells did, but this was a different kind of swallowing—quieter, more deliberate, like a man closing a door behind him. The walls rose sheer on both sides, sixty, eighty feet, and the river ran deep and slow between them, deep enough that my depth sounder gave up and started displaying apologies.

Then the sounder found the bottom, and the bottom was wrong.

"Paloma." Mara was watching the screen. "Is that—"

"I see it."

The riverbed didn't slope. It *dropped*—a vertical wall of stone falling away beneath us, the reading going from forty feet to nothing, no bottom at all, the sounder pinging into a void. I killed the engine and let us drift, and we drifted over the edge of something enormous.

I could see it. That was the thing that made my hands go cold on the wheel. In water that clear, at that depth, with the morning sun slanting down the canyon, I could see the shape of it—a cavern mouth below the river, vast, a cathedral doorway of dark stone, and the river pouring into it without a ripple, sliding over the lip and down into the earth like water going over the edge of a tablecloth.

"The source," Mara said quietly.

"Part of it." I'd spent years diving the wreck downstream. I knew this river's plumbing better than any man alive, and I'd always known the wreck sat over a fissure, a crack in the world that breathed cold water. I'd told myself it was just geology. "The river goes *in* here. Somewhere downstream, it comes back out. And in between—"

"In between is a cavern system."

"In between is the biggest dive of my life, and I've done stupid dives for money."

Tomas stirred. "The team's sonar," he said, each word costing him. "Daniel mapped it. Before. Before the second camp." His eyes found Mara's, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with sonar. "He said it was like the river had a basement. He said—" A cough took him, wet and long, and when it released him he finished in a whisper: "He said somebody had been in the basement already."

* * *

We moored in a slot of shale where the canyon wall stepped down to the water, and I went down first, alone, because some habits don't die even when they should.

The water at the lip was cold enough to ache. I rolled in, checked my regulator, checked my gauges, and dropped over the edge into the dark.

The cavern opened beneath me like a held breath. My light found walls ribbed and fluted, stone carved by ten thousand years of patient water, and the space went on—chambers branching off chambers, the river threading through them in slow silver ropes. I swam the main passage with the discipline I'd learned in the navy and abandoned everywhere else: line, air check, turn pressure. The line of the passage bent, doubled back, descended.

And then my light caught steel.

Not rusted piling steel. Ship steel. Riveted plate, a curve of hull, and my heart did something complicated in my chest because I knew that curve. I'd run my hands over that exact curve of plating forty feet of river downstream, on the wreck that didn't officially exist, in a hold full of cargo that shouldn't have been there.

Except this was different. This section was *shut*.

The sister section—I knew the wreck's class, knew its layout the way you know a face you've studied in photographs—should have opened here into an engine compartment, a companionway, the guts of the ship. Instead the opening had been sealed. Not with a collision bulkhead. Not with anything a shipyard built. The edges of the opening were blown outward, jagged, the plate curled back like a flower made of shrapnel, and the cavity behind it had been packed—deliberately, methodically, with rock and rubble and poured concrete—until the compartment was a tomb with a mason's signature on it.

They hadn't sunk the ship and hoped.

They'd sunk the ship, and then they'd come down into the source cavern with dynamite and cement and buried the part that mattered, the part that would have told the story, and sealed it inside the one place on earth no salvage crew was ever going to be licensed to reach.

I hung in the black water with my light on that curled, blasted steel, and I thought about the hush money in its envelope, and the years I'd spent being no one, and how carefully I'd never asked the question whose answer was floating right in front of me, wearing concrete.

I'd found the evidence once. I'd found a fraction of it. The rest had been down here the whole time, in the dark, under a padlock and a bullet-riddled sign, waiting for someone with a reason to come.

I went back up because my air told me to and because there was nothing more I could do down there alone with a tank and a light. But my hands were shaking on the ladder, and it wasn't the cold.

* * *

Daniel Quist's final camp sat on a shelf of stone forty feet above the water, tucked under an overhang that would keep off everything but the memory of rain. We climbed to it with Tomas between us, him refusing to be carried and paying for the refusal with every step, and when we crested the shelf I stopped, because the camp was *neat*.

That was the thing the burned camp downstream hadn't prepared me for. This one wasn't burned. Wasn't scattered. Wasn't torn apart by men looking for something. The tents were struck and lashed. The gear was racked. The cook kit was clean and stacked. It looked less like an abandoned camp than a finished one—like men who had known they were about to be interrupted had taken twenty minutes to make sure the interruption would find nothing, and then walked into the canyon to meet it.

Or like one man had done it. Alone. After.

Mara stood very still in the middle of it, turning in a slow circle, and I watched her do the arithmetic I was trying not to do: five men had gone up this river, and one camp had been closed by one pair of hands.

"Here," Tomas said. He'd lowered himself onto a supply crate, gray-faced, and was pointing at a flat stone near the back wall, under the overhang's driest reach. "He always cached data separate. Separate from the computers, separate from himself. He said—" a breath— "he said paper doesn't have a password to give up."

Under the stone, wrapped in a dry bag, sealed with tape and a second bag and more tape, we found the journals.

Three of them. Waterproof notebooks, the kind surveyors use, filled edge to edge with a small, furious, precise handwriting. Daniel Quist had written the way engineers build—every load calculated, every joint accounted for. The earliest entries were science: water samples, sonar traces, the cavern system sketched in cross-section with dimensions in a neat column. Then the science started finding things it hadn't gone looking for.

Mara read aloud at first, and then stopped reading aloud, and I read over her shoulder, and the camp got very quiet except for the river below and Tomas's breathing, which had developed a rattle I didn't like.

*Day 9. Sonar confirms second hull section in the lower chamber, sealed with explosive. Charge patterns consistent with military demolition. This was not a sinking. This was an interment.*

*Day 11. Recovered manifest fragment from the downstream wreck (V. assisted—local diver, knows the site better than the charts do, will not give a name). Manifest stamped in three places. Cross-referenced with the passenger list from the ferry disaster, '89. The numbers are the same. The cargo manifest and the civilian dead are the same document.*

*Day 12. I understand now. They didn't sink the evidence. They sank the witnesses. The ship was carrying the ferry's own investigation team, the survey records, the recovery divers' reports—everything the navy had seized after the ferry went down and everything it never intended to release. Then they scuttled it over the fissure and blasted the section into the cavern, and the river has been keeping their secret at the source ever since. Forty-one civilians died on the ferry. The navy knew the ferry was unseaworthy because the navy had sold it the ferry. The investigation said weather. The investigation was written by the men who sold the boat.*

*Day 14. Contacted V. again through the old channel. He hasn't answered. Mara, if you're reading this, his name is—*

Mara's finger stopped on the page. Not because the writing stopped. Because she had.

"That's you," she said. "The V."

"That's me," I said.

"He wrote to you. He tried to—"

"He wrote to a man who didn't exist anymore," I said. "I told you I got a letter through an old channel, once, that I never opened. I told you I burned it." I looked at the journals, at the neat furious handwriting, at the entry that had a name in it—*his name is*—and I understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the lungs, that the letter I'd burned had been an offer. An alliance. A man upstream, doing for free and for the dead what I'd once been paid to bury, reaching out a hand across the dark, and me, feeding it to a stove because the dark was warm and the stove was easy.

"Keep reading," I said. My voice came out sanded flat.

She kept reading.

*Day 15. Men on the river. No insignia, good boats, bad manners. We are moving the evidence to the source camp and sealing the data. If anything happens to us, the journals are under the stone. The numbers are the numbers. Forty-one. Say them out loud. They only work if someone says them out loud.*

The last entry was dated nine days ago. Below it, in fresher ink, a single line, added in a different hand, shakier:

*They came at dusk. I ran. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

Tomas, from his crate, in a voice like paper: "He made it out. He made it downriver. The root hollow where you found me—I was following his trail. He'd been through the hollow a day ahead of me." He looked at Mara, and his eyes were burning with fever and something else, something that had kept a dying man moving through a canyon for nine days. "Your brother was alive three days ago. Heading downriver. Toward the landing."

Mara closed the journal with both hands, carefully, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

"Then we go down," she said. "We take the journals, we take the manifests, we take the photographs of that sealed section, and we go down after him."

"Down is where they are," I said.

"Down is where *everyone* is," Mara said. "The dead, the living, and the men who made both." She looked at me, and there was no forgiveness in her face yet, but there was something better—there was a job, and my name on it, next to hers. "You burned his letter, Paloma. You don't get to burn the rest of it."

Below us, far down the canyon, an engine started. One engine. Then a second. Then, after a pause long enough to be deliberate, a third.

They weren't even trying to be quiet anymore.
