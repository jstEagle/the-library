## 17. What Daniel Found

The journals were wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was wrapped in a man's stubbornness.

Mara found them in the last of Daniel's caches, tucked beneath the overturned aluminum boat where he'd camped above the source, and she carried them back to the fire the way you carry a sleeping child—carefully, like any sudden motion might break whatever was left of him. Three notebooks, waterproof paper, a pencil stub worn to the length of a fingernail. Everything else the expedition had carried was scattered or burned or sunk. But Daniel had always been a man who trusted paper. He used to say data could be deleted, but handwriting had to be burned, and burning took a decision.

He'd been right. Whoever came for his team burned the tents, sank the drives, took the satellite phones. They hadn't found the journals.

I sat across the fire from her and watched her open the first one, and I'll be honest with you—I was afraid of what was in them. Not for me. I'd made my peace, or something like it, years ago. I was afraid for her. There's a difference between losing a brother and learning that he died because of something you didn't know, and I'd have spared her the second if the river had given me the option.

The river doesn't give options. That's the first thing it teaches you, and the last.

* * *

She read in silence for a long time. The fire cracked and settled. Tomas slept under the tarp we'd rigged over the boat, his fever finally broken enough that his breathing had a rhythm to it again instead of that shallow, scraping sound that had kept us both awake for two days. Above us the source cavern breathed its cold air out of the mountain, in and out, like something enormous sleeping.

"Paloma." Mara's voice was strange. Flat in a way I recognized, because I'd used it myself—the voice you find when the feeling is too big to fit through your throat all at once. "When did you last talk to a man named Aldous Crane?"

The name went through me like a hook through the gill plate.

"Crane." I said it slowly, buying time, the way you do when the bottom drops out and you're feeling for it with your feet. "You know that name?"

"I'm asking you."

"Then I'm asking you to tell me what's in the book first, because I have a feeling one of us is about to be surprised, and I'd rather it was me."

She turned the journal around and held it toward the firelight. Daniel's handwriting was small and precise, an engineer's hand, every letter built to specification. She'd found the entry from early on—the expedition had been upstream eleven days when he wrote it, which meant it was written maybe three weeks before the ambush.

*Made contact today with A.C. Took some doing. He runs private recovery work out of Puerto Viejo, does contract diving the navy farms out when it wants clean hands. He knew the wreck. He knew it before I told him anything. That's the interesting part.*

I read it twice. Then I read it a third time, and the third time my hands weren't quite steady, so I gave the journal back to her.

"Keep reading," she said.

*I offered him a place on the team. He declined. But he didn't hang up on me, and a man like that doesn't leave a line in the water unless he's thinking about taking it. I think he's the diver. I think he's the one who found the hold years ago and took money to forget it. If he'll talk, he's not just a witness, he's the witness. The one who can put a face and a signature on the whole operation.*

Mara looked up at me. The firelight did something complicated with her face—grief and anger and something underneath both that I didn't have a name for yet.

"You never told me he contacted you."

"Because he didn't." I heard how it sounded even as I said it. "Mara, I swear on the water, nobody from that expedition ever called me. I found out about the Meridian going silent from a supply runner on channel nine, same as I told you. I didn't know your brother from Adam."

"Then why does my brother's journal describe you?" She read aloud: *'Ex-navy salvage. Left the service under a cloud nobody will name. Dives the unnamed wreck at dawn like a man visiting a grave. Takes cash. Registered boat to a dead man.'* She looked at me over the page. "Does that sound like anyone you know?"

It sounded like me. It sounded like me the way a mirror sounds.

"Crane," I said. "Crane described me to him."

"Or Crane *is* you." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. She was too tired and too full of dead brother to raise her voice. "Paloma, I've spent three weeks on this river with a man I know almost nothing about. I know you dive well. I know you lie well. I know you recognized the name of the expedition leader before the supply runner finished saying it—don't tell me you didn't, I watched your face. And now I find out my brother was trying to recruit a diver with your exact history, and you're telling me the universe just happened to hand me the one other man on this river who fits."

"Crane brokered the original job." The words came out of me like ballast off a sinking deck, one at a time, whether I wanted them or not. "Years ago. He was the middleman between the navy's dirty-hands office and people like me. He found divers, made offers, made problems disappear. When I took the hush money, Crane was the one who counted it into my hand. He knew my boat. He knew my habits. If your brother went looking for the diver who found the hold, Crane is exactly who he'd have found first—because Crane is the one who'd have told the navy the diver existed in the first place."

Mara sat with that. I watched her turn it over the way she turned everything, all the way around, looking for the flaw.

"So Crane sold your name to my brother."

"Or sold my brother to the navy." I nodded at the journal. "Keep reading. What happened after the contact?"

She read. Her lips moved slightly, the way people read when they're reading out loud in their heads, and I watched the color come and go in her face with the turn of each page.

* * *

*A.C. came back to me today. Says the diver won't talk, but he'll think about it. Says for the right number the diver might provide what he calls "orientation." I don't like the smell of it. A man who took hush money once will take it twice. I told A.C. I want the diver, not his orientation. A.C. laughed. I don't like the way he laughs either.*

*Bad feeling tonight. Radio traffic on the band we shouldn't be hearing traffic on. Engine sounds upriver after dark. Rourke thinks it's a patrol. I think patrols announce themselves. I've moved the journals.*

*They came at dawn. Not soldiers—I want to be clear about that in case this is the last thing I write. Men without insignia, but they moved like soldiers, and they had a boat I'd swear on my life was navy surplus with the numbers ground off. Rourke is dead. They took Sela and the others upriver. I don't know why they left me. I think they think I'm dead. I think they think the river will finish the job.*

*If anyone finds this: it wasn't the gorge. It wasn't the weather. It wasn't nature. We found what they sank the ship to hide, and they came for us the way you'd come for a fire you didn't want spreading. Check the manifests. Check the hold. Check the sister section at the source. And find the diver. If the diver ever decides to talk, he ends this. He's the only one who was there before.*

Mara closed the journal. For a while the only sound was the fire and the cavern breathing and Tomas muttering something in his fever that might have been a prayer or a parts list.

"He tried to recruit me," I said. It came out barely above the fire's noise. "Through Crane. And Crane—"

"Crane took your brother's money, or the navy's money, or both, and sold the expedition out." Mara's voice had changed. The flatness was gone. What was under it was worse. "Paloma, my brother spent his last weeks trying to build an alliance with you. He was one phone call away from the one man who could have corroborated everything, and the man in the middle—the man my brother *trusted* to make that call—was the man who'd buried the evidence the first time."

"I didn't know." I heard how thin it sounded. I said it anyway, because it was true, and on this river true was all I had left. "I never knew the offer existed. Crane never—"

"Of course he never told you." She stood up. She was holding the journal against her chest like a shield, or a wound. "That was the whole design, don't you see it? Crane keeps you ignorant so you stay quiet. Crane keeps my brother hopeful so he keeps digging. And when the digging gets close, Crane hands the navy a map and a passenger list. Nobody has to know anything except Crane. He's the lock on both doors."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the water a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"I spent nine days thinking my brother was lost," she said, and now her voice did break, just once, and she let it, and kept talking through it, which took more strength than anything she'd done on the rapids. "Nine days thinking the river took him. And the river didn't take him. A man I've never met took him, using a diver's name as bait, and that diver was *you*—not because you did anything, but because of what you didn't do. Because you took the money and went invisible and left your name sitting in Crane's files like a loaded gun he could hand to anyone."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that would survive contact with her. She was right in every particular, and being right in every particular is the cruelest thing a person can be to you when there's nothing left to fix.

* * *

She walked down to the water's edge and I let her go, because following her would have been the wrong move and staying put was only slightly less wrong. I fed the fire. I checked Tomas, who opened his eyes, saw it was me, and closed them again with the trust of a man too sick to be choosy.

It was full dark when she came back. She sat down across from me and put the journals between us, all three, stacked like a small altar.

"I'm not forgiving you," she said. "I want that on the record. You didn't kill my brother. But you built the silence he died inside of, and I'm not going to pretend that's nothing."

"Okay," I said.

"But." She turned the top journal around and opened it to the last written page, and there was something in her face now that hadn't been there before—something that had burned off the grief and left the ore underneath. "Listen to this. Last entry. He wrote it the night before they came."

She read: *'If the diver won't come to us, we'll go make the case so complete he can't refuse. The sister section. The manifests. The names. I'm going to build something no one can buy, bury, or burn. The dead have been quiet long enough. They're due a witness.'*

Mara looked up at me across the fire.

"He wasn't just gathering proof, Paloma. He was building it *for you*. He thought if he made the case airtight, you'd finally talk. He spent his last weeks trying to give you a way out of the silence." Her jaw tightened. "So here's how it's going to be. We finish what he started. We get the sister section open, we get the manifests, we get the names of the civilians they drowned, and we put all of it in front of the world with your name attached—your *real* name, the one you've been hiding behind a dead man's boat registration. The dead have been quiet long enough. They're due a witness. And so are they due *him*."

I looked at the journals. I looked at the cavern mouth breathing its cold breath above us, where Daniel Quist had died trying to build a case with my name in its foundations.

And that was when Tomas, fever-bright and half-conscious under the tarp, said clearly, as if he'd been listening the whole time:

"They're coming upriver. I heard engines. An hour ago. Maybe two."

The fire suddenly seemed very small.
