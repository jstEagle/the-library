## 18. Ambush at the Headwaters

They came up the river the way weather comes—first a pressure you feel in your teeth, then the sky goes wrong, then it's on top of you.

I heard them before I saw anything. The Gorge of Bells does something to sound, swallows it whole and gives back nothing but its own roar, and for two days we'd been listening to that roar like men listening to a locked door. Then, upstream of the gorge, where the river widened into the clear spring-fed water past the last survey marker, the sound changed. Underneath the bells there was an engine note that didn't belong. Diesel, big displacement, more than one.

Mara heard it too. She was crouched over Tomas, wringing a cloth into the basin we'd been using to cool his fever, and her hands went still the way a bird goes still when a hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

"How many?" she asked.

"Two boats, maybe three." I'd climbed the bank with the binoculars while the light was still gray, and what I'd seen had rearranged my morning entirely: three hulls running dark, no lights, no insignia, riding low with the weight of armed men. They'd anchored at the mouth of the gorge overnight rather than run it in the dark. Careful. Professional. The kind of careful I used to sell by the hour.

"They'll wait for full light," I said. "Then they'll come up the last reach, land everyone, and burn everything that burns. And everything that doesn't burn, they'll drop in the water."

Tomas, feverish on the ground beneath the tarp we'd rigged, opened his eyes. He'd been drifting in and out for a day, the wound in his side doing its slow arithmetic, and when he spoke his voice came out sanded down to the grain. "The cavern," he said. "They can't have the cavern."

"The cavern is the problem," Mara said. "It's sealed. It's been sealed for thirty years."

"It's sealed *badly*." I looked at her. She didn't understand yet, and I watched the moment arrive in her face the way dawn arrives—slowly, then all at once. "A sealed site gets cleaned quietly. A collapsed site gets excavated publicly. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"You want to blow it up." Her voice was flat. Not accusing. Weighing.

"I want to make it *theirs* to open. In front of witnesses, with permits, with news cameras if we can manage it. A cave-in they caused stays buried forever. A cave-in *we* caused becomes a question they can't answer without digging." I turned toward the water, where the source pool lay glass-clear over the drowned entrance, the sister section of the wreck below it, dynamited shut decades ago by men who thought closure was the same thing as silence. "Right now it's a secret. By tonight I want it to be a crime scene. Nobody cleans up a crime scene they've already denied exists."

* * *

We spent the morning preparing, and I will tell you honestly that part of me—the part that had spent years perfecting the art of being nowhere—kept whispering that we could still run. Downriver, into the maze, gone. But Tomas couldn't run. And Daniel Quist's journals were sitting in that final camp above the pool, along with the manifests, the photographs, the drive with everything on it, and every hour we delayed was an hour the men coming upriver got closer to making all of it ash.

So we worked. I had dive charges left from the salvage kit—three of them, meant for cutting a fouled propeller shaft off a wreck, sized for steel, which meant they were more than enough for limestone. I spliced detonating cord and waterproofed the connections with the same care I'd once given to staying invisible, and it occurred to me somewhere in the middle of it that this was the first time in nine years my skills had pointed at something instead of away from it.

Mara rigged the beacon. That was her project, and she wouldn't show me the details, only that she'd cannibalized the satellite phone's transmitter and one of the strobes from the dive kit, and that she kept checking the sky with an expression I recognized from church funerals—hope and grief wearing the same face. I didn't ask. Some things you let people carry alone until they're ready to set them down.

Tomas insisted on moving himself to the rocks above the pool. We argued; he won, the way dying men often win arguments, simply by refusing to lose them. He propped himself against a granite shoulder with the rifle across his lap and said, "I held a line before. Badly. Let me hold one properly."

"That's not—" Mara started.

"It's exactly what it is," he said. "Go do your demolition, Paloma. I'll watch the water."

* * *

The dive itself was the strangest of my life. The water at the source is so clear it feels like a lie—you descend through light instead of out of it—and forty feet down the drowned entrance of the sister section gaped in the limestone like a throat. I'd seen it two days ago with Mara's light playing over the dynamited bulkhead, the torn steel, the records behind it that proved thirty civilians had died in a disaster the navy had solved by sinking the evidence.

Now I placed the charges against the rock above the entrance, not the wreck itself. That mattered to me. Whatever else happened, I wasn't going to put another hole in that ship. The charges went into the limestone crown of the cavern, set to bring the ceiling down across the mouth in a plug of rubble no backhoe would mistake for geology. Rubble with detonating cord in it. Rubble with *fingerprints*.

A sealed site can be quietly cleaned. A collapsed site demands excavation, and excavation demands explanation, and explanation is the one thing those men upstream could not survive.

I knotted off the last connection, checked the firing line twice, and hung there in the impossible blue water looking at the wreck through the haze of my own breath, and I said—I'll admit it—I said, "Hold on. Help is ugly, but it's coming."

Then I went up.

* * *

They came at mid-afternoon, and they came in force.

Three boats fanned out across the last reach, maybe fourteen men, and they didn't bother pretending anymore. No questions about fuel, no offers of money, none of the courteous menace from the fuel stop weeks ago. This was the erasure crew. They beached at the gravel bar below our position and came up the bank in a spread line, rifles slung until they weren't, moving with the unhurried competence of men who had done this before and expected to do it again.

"Paloma." Mara was beside me behind the rock, voice low. "The beacon. It has to go up now or never."

"Now," I agreed. "On my mark. And Mara—" I caught her arm. "Whatever happens in the next ten minutes, the buoy goes up, the camera sees it, and this stops being a story nobody tells. That's the win. Us getting off this river clean—that was always a bonus, never the plan."

She looked at me for a long second, and then she did something I didn't expect: she kissed me, quick and hard, like sealing a hatch. "For after," she said. "You owe me an after."

"Mark," I said, and hit the firing switch.

The charges went off as one breath. The river above the pool stood up—a white column, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat—and when the water settled, the entrance to the sister section was gone under fifty tons of fresh-fallen limestone. Even from the bank you could see it: new rock, raw rock, rock that had no business being where it was.

Every man on that gravel bar stopped and stared at the water. And I understood, watching them, that I'd just made their mission impossible. You can't quietly clean a collapse. You have to explain it. And explaining it means admitting what was sealed there.

They came for us angry.

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have fronts and flanks and some shape to them. What followed was fourteen men trying to take a ridge from three people who knew every rock on it, and knowing the rocks counted for more than numbers.

Tomas fired first. From his granite shoulder he'd spent an hour learning the angles, and his first shot dropped a rifle out of a man's hands at two hundred yards—not the man, the rifle; even half-dead he was choosing his targets like an engineer chooses loads, precisely, minimally. His second shot was less merciful, because by then the men had stopped being careful.

Mara and I fell back along the ridge, trading shots to keep their line honest, giving ground the way you give line to a fish—slowly, with intent, always keeping the bend. They pushed up the slope in two groups, and the terrain funneled them exactly where the terrain always funnels people who don't know it, into the gully below our fallback position, and for four or five minutes we made that gully a very expensive place to stand.

Then a round cracked off the rock beside my head and took most of my hearing with it, and in the ringing aftermath I saw one of them flank wide through the trees—good man, patient, the kind I'd have hired once—and come up behind Tomas's position.

"Tomas!" My voice came back to me as a stranger's shout. "Left! Tree line!"

Tomas didn't turn around. He'd known, I think. Maybe he'd heard him; maybe he'd just done the math the way engineers do. He rolled off the granite shoulder onto his wounded side, which should have put him out of the fight entirely and instead put his rifle on the flanker's line, and he fired twice.

The flanker went down. So did Tomas.

* * *

I reached him through fire that seemed to come from everywhere, dragging him behind the granite while Mara laid down enough rounds to make the tree line reconsider its ambitions. He was conscious. That was the good news. The bad news was everything else—the wound reopened, blood in a way that blood shouldn't be, his breath coming in short wet pulls.

"Held it," he said.

"You held it." I was pressing cloth into him and lying through my teeth about how bad it wasn't. "You held it beautiful."

Above the pool, the men were regrouping, and I could hear their radio traffic now—they'd gotten close enough that voices carried—and what I heard changed the taste of the whole afternoon. They weren't calling for reinforcements to press the attack. They were calling for a helicopter. Because the collapse had done what collapses do: it had made noise that satellites notice, created a site that required official response, forced the very machinery of record-keeping to turn its eyes toward a place that had spent thirty years paying to stay blind.

"You did it," Mara said beside me. She was reloading with steady hands and tears running freely down her face, both at once, no contradiction between them. "You miserable, stubborn man. You actually did it. They have to dig now. In the open. With cameras."

"Not yet." I looked at the buoy marker we'd staged at the pool's edge, the beacon Mara had built, still waiting. "Not until it's floating in open water where a lens can find it. They'll try to control the excavation story unless—"

The radio chatter sharpened. One of the boats was pushing off from the gravel bar, running for the lower river—running for help, or running to intercept whatever Mara's beacon might summon. And the rest were coming up the ridge again, angrier now, because the helicopter they'd called was theirs, and they intended to own this site before anyone else's eyes arrived.

Tomas gripped my wrist with a hand that had gone cold. "The buoy," he rasped. "Get it in the water. I'll keep them busy."

"You've kept them busy enough—"

"*Get it in the water.*" His eyes were clear for the first time in a day, fever burned off by adrenaline or by something beyond it. "This is what I'm for, Paloma. Let me be what I'm for."

Behind us, the first helicopter was already a throb on the southern horizon. Mara met my eyes over Tomas's body, and I saw it in her face: the after she'd kissed me for was arriving faster than either of us had planned, and whether we got to keep it depended entirely on the next hundred yards of open water between us and the sky.

I picked up the beacon.
