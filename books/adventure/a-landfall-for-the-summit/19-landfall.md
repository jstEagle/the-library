## 19. Landfall

The charges went off at 4:47 in the morning, and the mountain swallowed them the way it swallowed everything—without comment.

I felt it through my boots before I heard it, a deep structural shudder running up through the rock of the headwaters shelf like the earth clearing its throat. Then the sound arrived, rolled down the gorge and came back off the far walls twice, three times, until it seemed less like an explosion than an argument the valley was having with itself. Dust boiled out of the cavern mouth in a gray plume that caught the first light and turned briefly, absurdly, gold.

Mara stood beside me on the ledge with her arms crossed against the cold, watching the entrance to the source collapse in on itself. Tomas lay behind us under a tarp, propped against my dive bag, gray as river stone but conscious—conscious enough to say, when the last echo died:

"You just buried the truth."

"No," I said. "I buried the *door*. There's a difference, and in about six hours a lot of very important people are going to learn it."

She looked at me then, and I gave her what she needed, which wasn't reassurance. It was arithmetic.

"A sealed site gets cleaned quietly," I said. "A collapsed site gets excavated loudly. They can't send divers into a rubble-choked cavern without permits, without engineers, without a paper trail a mile long. Every ton of rock they move is a question they have to answer in writing." I nodded at the settling dust. "I didn't hide the evidence, Mara. I made hiding it expensive."

Tomas coughed—a wet, bad sound—and said, "The navy hates expensive."

"The navy *hates* witnesses," I corrected him. "We're about to introduce the two concepts."

* * *

Getting down from the headwaters was its own kind of violence. The portage trail Daniel Quist's team had cut was still there, half-grown-over, and we carried Tomas down it in a litter made from a tent fly and two paddles while he did his best not to scream and mostly succeeded. His fever had broken sometime in the night, which was either the antibiotics finally working or his body deciding it would rather be angry than dead. With Tomas, I'd learned, those were often the same thing.

By midmorning we had the boat loaded and pointed downstream, and here is where I should explain the plan, because plans deserve explaining even when they're insane. Especially then.

The evidence—the manifests, the documents, the journals, the photographs Daniel had risked everything to gather—was sealed in four waterproof cases lashed inside a salvage buoy I'd been carrying for years and never once used. It was a deep-water marker, orange and white, rated to survive being dragged across a reef by a trawler. Inside it, along with the paper proof, I'd packed a GPS logger and a radio beacon transmitting on a frequency Mara had chosen nine days ago, back when I still thought her satellite phone was just a lifeline and not a tripwire.

Because that was the part I hadn't known. That was the part she'd kept in her pocket like a stone.

"There's a helicopter," she told me, somewhere around the second set of rapids, shouting over the water. "There's been a helicopter since yesterday."

I looked at her. The boat bucked under us. She looked back with the particular steadiness of a woman who has decided you are going to hear this whether or not you both die first.

"Channel Nine has been dark for weeks," she said. "But Daniel's backup drive had a second thing on it—not files. A contact. A producer named Ade Okafor, who ran a story three years ago about munitions dumping off the coast, got sued into silence, never stopped digging. I called him from the burned camp. From the *first* night, Paloma."

"You—" The bow slapped a wave. I steered. "You've had a news crew on standby since the burned camp, and you're telling me now?"

"I'm telling you now because if I'd told you at the burned camp, you'd have turned the boat around." Her jaw was set. "Wouldn't you."

I wanted to lie. It's a skill; I've kept it sharp. But there was no percentage in lying to a woman who'd just out-planned me by a week, so I said, "Yes," and she said, "That's why," and we ran the next rapid in something that was almost companionship.

Okafor couldn't get close by road—the upper river had been closed to civilian traffic since before I was born, another quiet courtesy of the men without insignia. But helicopters don't need roads. He'd been holding at a fuel strip two valleys east, waiting for one word: *landfall*. And the beacon in the buoy would give him that word, plus coordinates, plus a live feed of exactly where to point his camera.

"Assuming," I said, "the navy doesn't shoot it down."

"They won't," Mara said. Then, quieter, honest: "They might. But they'll do it on camera, and that's a different war than the one they've been fighting."

* * *

We hit open water at two in the afternoon.

After days in the gorges—after walls of stone so close you could read the grain of them, after a river that spent its whole life shouting—it was almost shocking, the width of it. The lower reach spread out flat and brown-green under a high white sky, a mile bank to bank, lazy-looking the way big rivers always are right up until they kill you. Somewhere behind us, upstream past the barriers and the shot-through warning plaques and the gorge where a man had fallen and I had failed to catch him, the source sat sealed under a hundred tons of fresh rock, keeping its dead.

I killed the engine and let us drift. The buoy came up over the transom, orange and scuffed, lighter than it had any right to be given what it held. Four cases of paper. Twenty-three names. Everything the drowned had been waiting decades to say.

"Mara," I said. "Last chance. We could run. Both of us, Tomas, the evidence—we could be at a federal courthouse in forty hours, and this stays a legal matter instead of a public one."

"And the site gets sealed pending investigation," she said. "And the investigation gets classified pending review. And the review gets lost pending budget. I've read how this ends, Paloma. My brother read how this ends. That's why he's dead." She put her hand flat on the buoy. "Light it up."

I keyed the beacon.

For about ninety seconds, nothing happened, and I had time to remember every reason this was a mistake. Then the GPS logger chirped acquisition, the transmitter locked, and somewhere over the eastern ridgeline a rented helicopter with a camera mount and a producer who'd been waiting nine days turned its nose toward the water.

And so, I have to assume, did everyone else.

* * *

They came out of the downstream haze at 2:40, and they were not subtle.

Two rigid-hulls, military gray, running fast enough to throw rooster tails, and behind them the patrol boat—the same one that had swept the upper river, the one I'd baited away from a flooded cave at the cost of nearly drowning—coming on slower and heavier, like a verdict. No flags. No hull numbers worth reading. Men on the decks in gear that had been carefully stripped of anything that might later be entered into a record.

"Tomas," I said. "Down."

"He's been down for three days," Mara said, but she pulled the tarp over him anyway, and he let her, which told me more about his condition than the fever chart did.

I put the buoy back in the water. It rode high and bright, tethered to the boat by thirty feet of line, bobbing on the long slow swell like a piece of the sun that had fallen in. Then I sat down at the helm, put my hands where they could be seen, and waited.

The rigid-hulls split wide to take us from both quarters. Loudspeakers crackled: *Cut your engine. Remain aboard. This is a restricted security zone.* All the words of law, none of the authority—I'd checked, back when checking was how I stayed alive, and there was no restriction on this water that any court had ever seen. They knew it. I knew it. The gap between those two facts was where people like me usually disappeared.

The patrol boat closed to fifty meters and held there, and a man stepped to its rail with a megaphone, and even at that distance I recognized the posture. Commander Voss—no relation, thank God—was built like a filing cabinet and had the same warmth. I'd seen his type across contract tables in another life. Men whose signatures ended things.

"Paloma Voss," the megaphone said. My real name, spoken aloud on open water for the first time in years, and it landed strangely—like a coat that no longer fit but still weighed the same. "You are in possession of stolen government materials. Surrender the buoy and come aboard, and this remains an administrative matter."

"Administrative," Mara repeated softly, beside me. "Like the expedition."

On the horizon, low and getting lower, a speck resolved into rotor blades.

The commander saw it too. I watched him see it—watched the small recalibration move through his shoulders, the way a man's weight shifts when the ground turns out to be thinner than advertised. The helicopter was still five miles out, but five miles at airspeed is nothing, and whatever Okafor had told him on the phone, the camera was already warm.

"Turn that aircraft away," the commander said into his radio, not the megaphone, and I heard the order go out, and I heard the answer come back, and the answer must have been unsatisfying, because his jaw did something complicated.

The rigid-hulls kept coming. Thirty meters. Twenty. On the nearer one, a man raised a weapon—not aiming, not yet, but *presenting*, the way you present a document, except the document was a machine gun and the venue was my boat.

"Mara," I said. "When they fire—or if they board—get behind the console."

"They're not going to fire," she said. "Look at the sky."

I looked. The helicopter was close enough now to be a shape rather than a speck, and hanging off its side was a camera lens the size of a dinner plate, and I swear to God the pilot flew a slow, deliberate orbit around the patrol boat first, framing it, cataloguing it, giving the men on deck their close-ups whether they wanted them or not. The loudspeaker on the aircraft clicked on, and a voice with a producer's calm said, "Commander, this is Ade Okafor, Meridian Broadcasting. You're live. You have been for four minutes. Perhaps introduce yourself and your vessel for the record?"

Silence on the water. Real silence—the kind a mile-wide river shouldn't be able to hold.

The gunner on the rigid-hull looked at his officer. The officer looked at the patrol boat. The commander looked at the helicopter, and I watched twenty years of institutional confidence arrive at a simple mathematical problem: *fire on civilians, on camera, with names, or stand down.*

He was a careful man. Careful men do the math.

"Stand down," he said. Not into the megaphone. Into the radio, quietly, the way you say a thing you hope history won't hear. But the helicopter heard it. The boom mic heard it. And somewhere in a broadcast truck two valleys east, a signal light turned red and a story began to exist that could never again be un-existed.

* * *

What happened after happened slowly, the way ice goes out of a river—not all at once but in groans and shifts, one structure failing into the next.

The rigid-hulls backed off to a hundred meters and idled there, suddenly very interested in appearing to be fishing vessels. The commander's megaphone made one more attempt at procedure—*this zone remains closed pending clarification*—and Okafor's voice came back pleasant as Sunday: *Happy to clarify, Commander. Name spelling?*

And then the helicopter came to us.

It hovered off our beam, close enough that the rotor wash flattened the water, and Okafor's voice said, "Ms. Quist? If you can confirm the beacon contents, we have a document specialist standing by."

Mara stood up in the boat. I want to record that accurately, because it mattered: she stood up in an open boat between two armed vessels and a patrol ship, in front of a camera that was putting her image into several million living rooms, and she did not look at any of the guns. She looked at the buoy.

"My name is Mara Quist," she said, clear and carrying, the way you speak across water. "My brother was Daniel Quist. He led the Meridian expedition. Eleven days ago he and seven colleagues were killed upstream by men without insignia, because they found proof that a naval vessel was deliberately scuttled at the river's source to bury evidence of a disaster that killed civilians—civilians whose names were classified, whose families were lied to, and whose bodies are still down there." She put her hand on the buoy, on the camera's side of it, deliberately. "This contains the manifests. The orders. The names. It's going up now, and it isn't coming back down."

The buoy's data link was already streaming—had been since the beacon lit, because Mara had never planned to hand the evidence to anyone, including the helicopter. It was going straight to three servers in three countries, which meant that by the time any lawyer anywhere said the word *injunction*, the manifests were already common property. I hadn't known that either. Standing next to her, I was beginning to suspect there was quite a lot I hadn't known, and that the pattern would continue.

The commander's boat hung there for a long moment. Then, faintly across the water, I heard him make the call that ended his career: "Nobody fires. Nobody boards. Log the time and note that we are maintaining distance pending instructions." Instructions that were not coming, because every office he might have called was watching the same broadcast he was.

Men without insignia become men with names. That's the whole trick of it. Not courts, not courage—cameras. A uniform only works if it's anonymous, and anonymity dies the moment a lens finds you and a voice says *and you are?*

The gunner on the near rigid-hull took off his helmet. Just took it off, and sat there bareheaded in the sun, looking young, looking like a kid from somewhere with a river of his own, and I thought: *there it is.* The first crack. By nightfall there would be others; by week's end, hearings.

Tomas pushed the tarp off his head, squinted at the helicopter, at the retreating hulls, at Mara standing tall with her hand on the evidence, and said, hoarsely, "Daniel would have hated missing this," and then, because he was Tomas, "also he would have insisted on doing it himself, badly, in a kayak."

Mara laughed. It cracked in the middle and became something else, and I put my arm around her shoulders and let the river carry the sound away, and the helicopter orbited us once more, patient, thorough, making sure the world got its money's worth.

* * *

It was dusk before the first legitimate authority showed up—an actual Coast Guard cutter, actual flag, actual hull numbers, arriving with the sheepish energy of an institution that has just realized which side of the story it's late for. Okafor set down on a gravel bar a mile downstream to refuel and file, and sent word asking us to come in, and we did, towing the buoy like a lantern behind us.

While the cutter's officers photographed everything with hands that weren't quite steady, Mara sat on the gravel beside me, mud to the knees, hair wrecked, watching the sun go down behind the gorge that had tried to kill us twice.

"They'll investigate," she said. "Slowly. Badly. Some of them will get promoted for it."

"Some," I agreed. "Not him." I nodded toward the patrol boat, sitting dark and humiliated out in the channel, under new orders to hold station and touch nothing. "Careful men fall hardest. Everyone below him is already rehearsing what they'll testify."

She was quiet a while. Then: "You said your name today. Out loud. To them."

"I did."

"How did it feel?"

I thought about it honestly, because she'd earned honesty and I was apparently out of the business of rationing it. "Like surfacing," I said. "Too fast. Ears screaming. Light everywhere." I looked at her. "But air."

She leaned her shoulder into mine, and we sat like that while the cutter's floodlights came on and the paperwork of justice began its long, unglamorous migration upriver.

Then one of the Coast Guard officers came down the gravel toward us, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a man delivering news he didn't write.

"Ms. Quist. Mr. Voss." He glanced back at his colleagues, lowered his voice. "There's a lawyer at the landing, flown in tonight. Federal. Says he needs to talk to whoever recovered the Meridian evidence." A pause. "And ma'am—he says he's been trying to reach your brother for three weeks. Says Daniel contacted him first. Back in the spring."

The river kept moving past us in the dark, indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea.

Mara looked at me, and I looked at her, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: *Daniel had a plan B. And we've never heard of it.*

"Send him down," she said.
