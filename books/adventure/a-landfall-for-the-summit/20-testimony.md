## 20. Testimony

The landing looked different with people on it.

For eleven years I'd known that stretch of gravel and rotted pilings as a place you passed through quickly, eyes down, engine idling. Now there were vehicles parked along the levee road—two navy sedans, a coroner's van, a news truck with its mast cranked up like a heron swallowing. There was yellow tape, which struck me as funny in a way I didn't share with anyone. The river had been off-limits for thirty years without a single ribbon to show for it, and now that it was finally open, they'd strung tape everywhere.

Mara stood at the edge of the crowd with her arms crossed, watching the helicopter pad the way a woman watches a dog that bit someone she loves. She hadn't slept more than an hour since Landfall. Neither had I, but sleep and I had a long history of polite estrangement.

"They're saying the commander's been suspended," she said as I came up beside her. "Not charged. Suspended. Pending inquiry."

"Pending is how they say *we're hoping you'll get bored*."

"I know." She turned to look at me, and up close I could see what the last three days had cost her—the raw rims of her eyes, the way she held herself like something load-bearing. "I also know it's not how it ends this time. There are cameras. There's a buoy full of documents sitting in an evidence locker with three separate agencies fighting over who gets to catalog it. My brother's journals are already photocopied in four places." A thin smile. "Daniel would have appreciated the redundancy. He backed everything up twice. Drove me crazy."

"He sounds like he'd have driven me crazy too."

"He would have liked you," she said. "That's worse."

* * *

They took Tomas out of the regional hospital's third-floor window room only once that week—for the statement, and even then under guard, because there were still men whose names we didn't know walking the halls with visitor badges and flat expressions. But Tomas talked. That was the thing nobody had counted on, least of all the men without insignia: Tomas Reyes, half-dead from fever and a bullet graze, propped up in a hospital bed with an IV in one arm, had decided he was done being quiet, and he talked the way water talks going over a ledge—continuously, and with no intention of stopping.

He gave them the timeline. The camp at the second bend. The burned tents. The hold photos, the manifests stamped with a classification code that shouldn't have existed on a civilian wreck. The night the armed men came, and how Daniel Quist had sent him into the root hollow with the backup drive and told him, in so many words, *live to be boring*. Tomas had been boring for nine days. Then he'd been very interesting indeed.

By Thursday, his account was in the record. By Friday, so were mine.

* * *

The hearing room wasn't a courtroom. It was a community hall above the fire station with folding chairs and a portable stenographer and a flag that had been borrowed from somewhere and hung slightly crooked, which felt right. Inquiries like this one liked to dress down. It made the men in uniform look less like defendants and more like neighbors, and neighbors don't hang each other.

I wore the only shirt I owned without salt stains. Mara sat behind me in the second row, close enough that when my name was called I could hear her breathing go deliberate and slow, the way it did before a dive.

"State your name for the record," said the man running the inquiry—a civilian counsel named Okafor, gray at the temples, with the patient voice of someone who'd spent twenty years asking questions he already knew the answers to.

And here was the moment. Eleven years of aliases. A boat registered to a dead man. Cash jobs, no receipts, a life folded small enough to fit through the gaps in every ledger on the coast. I'd rehearsed this part on the drive in, telling myself it would be easy, just four words, just air.

It wasn't easy. Your name is a door you've kept locked so long you forget there's anything behind it, and then one day you put the key in and your hand shakes.

"Paloma Voss," I said. "Full name. Paloma Anselmo Voss."

The stenographer's keys clattered. Somewhere in the second row, Mara exhaled.

"You've operated in this region under other names," Okafor said. Not accusing. Establishing.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because eleven years ago I found things in that river that powerful people wanted found by no one. And because they paid me to forget them, and I took the money, and then I made myself into a man who couldn't be traced back to what I knew." I looked at the folding chairs, at the officers in their pressed uniforms sitting very still, at the two in the back row who hadn't written a single note all morning. "That's the whole confession, counselor. Everything else is detail."

Okafor let the silence sit for a moment, the way a good diver lets the silt settle before looking for anything.

"Take us through the dive," he said. "Eleven years ago. Start at the descent."

So I did. I told them about the contract that came through a broker with no letterhead, the fee that was triple market rate, the instructions to survey the wreck's forward section and photograph nothing. About the hold, and the crates that weren't cargo—civilian effects, luggage tags, a child's shoe fused to the deck plating by whatever burned that ship before the river took it. About the manifest page I lifted against orders, stamped with a classification block, listing souls that appeared on no passenger roll anywhere. About the envelope of cash and the phone call afterward, a voice like dry paper telling me the site was closed, the matter resolved, and that a man with my talents should consider a career change.

"And you accepted those terms," Okafor said.

"For eleven years, yes."

"What changed?"

I could have said *Mara*. It would have been true and it would have been generous and it would have shifted weight onto her shoulders that belonged on mine. Instead I said what was actually true, which was harder and cleaner:

"A man named Daniel Quist died ten meters from where I once swam, holding evidence I could have surfaced a decade ago. Whatever changed, counselor, changed too late for him. I'm here to make sure it isn't too late for the record."

* * *

They cross-examined for six hours. The officers' counsel—a smooth man named Brandt with cufflinks worth more than my boat—did most of it, and he was good, I'll give him that. He walked me through my criminal exposure like a man laying out silverware. Unregistered vessel. Tax irregularities. The hush money, itemized, year by year, until the number sat on the table between us like a dead fish.

"Mr. Voss," he said, "you're asking this inquiry to accept the testimony of a self-described criminal who profited from his silence."

"No," I said. "I'm asking you to accept the testimony of a man who's testifying anyway. You can charge me with all of it. Every count. I'll plead to the lot before lunch. But my statement doesn't change, and neither does the manifest, and neither does the shoe in the hold that belonged to a nine-year-old girl your client's predecessors sank a ship to forget."

Brandt smiled the smile of a man who'd been told the weather was turning and didn't believe in weather. "You understand that cooperation doesn't purchase absolution."

"I'm not shopping for absolution," I said. "I'm buying a witness stand. Different store entirely."

Behind me, quietly, Mara laughed—one short breath through the nose—and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs and stay there.

* * *

The tide didn't turn all at once. Tides never do; they turn the way a big ship turns, slow and inevitable and mostly invisible from the deck. But you could see the signs if you knew water.

The two officers in the back row stopped coming after the second day. One of them, we learned later, had requested reassignment and been denied, then requested retirement and been granted with unusual speed. Commander Aldous—the man who'd ordered fire on us at the headwaters and stood down only when the helicopter's camera caught his own guns in frame—gave testimony that lasted forty minutes and consisted largely of the phrase *to the best of my recollection*. His career was a hull with the seams opening. Everyone in the room could hear the water coming in. He heard it too. On the fourth day he asked for a recess, and by the following week his name was on a suspension order, and the men who'd once visited him without insignia were visiting him with subpoenas instead.

Some circled wagons, of course. There was a rear admiral who called the whole affair *an environmental survey mischaracterized by bad actors*, and a senator's office that issued a statement expressing confidence in the chain of command, and a retired captain on the evening news who suggested, with a straight face, that Mara and I had staged the entire expedition as a grift. Mara watched that segment standing in the hospital cafeteria with a coffee she wasn't drinking, and when it ended she set the cup down and said, "Good. Let them talk. Every word makes the archives look better by comparison."

Tomas, meanwhile, had become something of a local institution. The nurses adored him. The press adored him. He gave interviews from his bed with the blanket pulled up to his chest like a man holding court, and he told the story the same way every time, in the same order, with the same details, because he understood—better than any of us—that consistency was armor. When a reporter asked him why he'd survived when the others hadn't, he was quiet for a long moment and then said, "Because Daniel told me to live to be boring. So now I intend to be extremely boring, publicly, for the rest of my life." The clip ran everywhere. *Live to be boring* went on a t-shirt within the month, which would have appalled Daniel and delighted Tomas.

* * *

On the last afternoon of my testimony, Okafor asked me one question off-script. He waited until the stenographer had the record caught up and the room had thinned to the essential personnel, and then he leaned back in his chair and studied me the way you study a chart before committing to a channel.

"Mr. Voss. Eleven years of invisibility. You built it carefully—I've read the file, and it's careful work. You could have stayed invisible. The money's still buried, metaphorically speaking. No one was looking for you until Ms. Quist found you. Why give it up?"

I thought about lying, or at least decorating. Old habit. But the room was nearly empty, and the light through the high windows was going gold the way it does over water at the end of a long day, and I was tired in the specific way a man gets tired when he's finally set something heavy down.

"Because invisibility isn't free," I said. "You pay for it in installments. A name here, a truth there, a decade of watching the sun come up over a wreck full of dead civilians and saying nothing. And the interest compounds. Eventually you owe more than you have." I shrugged. "I paid it off. That's all. The price was the only thing I had left that was actually mine, and it turns out it was worth spending."

Okafor nodded slowly, and wrote something on his legal pad that I suspect was not for the record.

"The inquiry will reconvene Monday," he said. "Thank you, Mr. Voss. For what it's worth—" and here he paused, choosing, a careful man even off the clock, "—the record is better with you in it than out of it."

* * *

Mara was waiting outside on the steps, in the last of the light, and she didn't ask how it went because she'd been in the room and she knew exactly how it went. We walked down toward the landing together, past the tape and the trucks, and the river was doing its evening trick, going bronze and slow, keeping its secrets the way it always had—but differently now. Loosely. Like a fist deciding, finger by finger, to open.

"They're releasing the wreck site next month," she said. "Protected status. Dive permits, memorial plaques, the works. There's a list." She glanced sideways at me. "Of names. They recovered them from the manifests. Forty-one. They're going to read them aloud at the surface, on the anniversary."

Forty-one. I'd seen maybe a dozen of them down there, in the dark, over the years. The child's shoe. A wristwatch stopped at 4:17. I'd cataloged cargo that shouldn't exist and never once said their names, because I didn't know them, because knowing them would have made it real, and real was the one thing I'd sold the rights to.

"Paloma." Mara had stopped walking. She was looking at me with that expression she had—not softness exactly, but the clear-eyed attention of a woman who'd learned to read water and had extended the courtesy to me. "You did the hard part. The part nobody else could do. Don't you dare spend the peace on penance."

"It's not penance," I said. "It's ballast. There's a difference. Penance drags you down. Ballast keeps you upright."

She considered that, and then, because she was Mara and because the evening was warm and the river was kind for once, she took my hand—which she'd been doing more often lately, casually, like it was a thing we'd always done and had simply gotten around to mentioning—and we walked the rest of the way to the boat in a silence that didn't need filling.

My boat sat at the mooring with her new registration papers in a plastic sleeve tucked under the wheel. Real name, real owner, current fees paid. It had taken the harbormaster four tries to stop calling me by the alias out of habit. The dead man whose name she'd worn for a decade was formally, legally, released from service.

Tomorrow, Okafor's office had told us, there would be a preliminary finding announced—*evidence sufficient to support referral*, the lawyers said, which in lawyer meant *someone is finally going to answer for this*. And the week after that, the first dive permits.

But tonight there was just the landing, and the light going out of it, and Mara's hand in mine, and the river running past us toward the sea with forty-one names it had carried for thirty years, finally, on their way home.

I stood at the rail a while after she turned in, listening to the water, counting the price one last time. Eleven years of silence. One brother. One team. A life lived in the gaps of the world.

Paid. All of it, paid.

And for the first time since I'd gone under, I slept like a man with nothing left to hide—though the river, being the river, had one more thing to show me before the season turned.
