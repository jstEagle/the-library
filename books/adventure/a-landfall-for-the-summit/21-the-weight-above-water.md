## 21. The Weight Above Water

The morning of my last dive on the wreck nobody names anymore—because now everybody names it—I woke before the alarm and lay in the bunk listening to the river do its housekeeping against the hull.

Old habits. For years the dawn was when I worked, when the water was glass and the patrol boats were still asleep and a man could slip under the surface like a debt slipping off a ledger. Now the dawn was just the dawn. There were three other boats anchored in the bay below the headland, dive flags up, coffee smells drifting across the water like we were all at some church social instead of floating over a grave.

I'd have found it obscene six months ago. I mostly didn't now. That was Mara's doing, though she'd deny it with that particular tilt of her chin that meant she absolutely wouldn't.

"You're up," she said from the cockpit, not looking up from the thermos she was wrestling. She'd been aboard two weeks while the inquiry ground through its motions in the provincial capital, sleeping in the forward berth she'd never once called anything but "the forward berth," as if naming it something warmer would cost her something she wasn't ready to pay. Or maybe I was the one who thought that way. We'd gotten good at lending each other our silences.

"Couldn't sleep," I said. "Too quiet."

"That's the first time in recorded history anyone's said that about this river."

"The river's fine. It's everything else." I climbed out into the gray light. The site buoy sat a hundred meters off the bow, yellow and official, with a plaque bolted to it that had already been photographed by four newspapers and vandalized once by somebody who apparently felt civilians killed by their own navy deserved less than a memorial. The plaque had been replaced within a day. That was new too. Things used to stay broken here.

Mara handed me coffee. "You don't have to go down."

"I know."

"It's not your job anymore. The site's mapped. Documented. There are government divers coming Thursday with a crane and a budget."

"I know." I drank the coffee. It was terrible. She made it the same way every morning, too strong, left too long, and I'd decided somewhere along the line that complaining about it would mean admitting I planned to keep drinking it indefinitely, which was a conversation neither of us had authorized yet. "I want to see it in the open. Once. With the sun on it."

She studied me the way she did—like reading a gauge she suspected of lying. "You're not going to take anything?"

"Nothing down there is mine to take."

"And you're sure about that?" Not accusatory. Genuinely asking. That was the thing about Mara Quist: she could put a question through your ribs gentle as a hypodermic.

"I'm sure," I said, and found that I was.

* * *

The water at the source had always been impossibly clear—spring-fed, cold as a held breath—but down here at the wreck, forty meters below where the river widened into the drowned valley, visibility ran maybe fifteen on a good day. Today was a good day. Sunlight came down in columns, actual columns, like the water had finally agreed to stop keeping secrets either.

The *Aurelia Vane* lay on her starboard side across the old channel, exactly where she'd settled thirty-one years ago when men in uniforms she'd trusted opened her seacocks and let her die with eighty-seven souls aboard, most of them refugees, all of them written off as weather. Her hull was furred white with decades of silt-life. The survey team had strung guide lines in bright orange, and someone—somebody with a sense of humor or a sense of justice, possibly both—had fixed small brass tags at intervals, each stamped with a name. Eighty-seven names. You could follow them down the deck like a litany.

I'd been diving this wreck for nine years. Nine years of dawn runs, of cataloging cargo that shouldn't exist, of photographing sealed crates and classified manifests and the scuttle charges' telltale placement, and filing none of it anywhere except the locked box under my bunk and the locked room behind my eyes. Nine years of being paid hush money by a man named Corliss whose name was now in the newspapers next to the word *arraigned*. Nine years of telling myself invisibility was survival, when the truth—the one I'd finally said out loud to Mara in the rain under that ledge, the confession that should have ended whatever was growing between us and instead, God help me, hadn't—was that I'd sold the dead for enough cash to disappear, and disappearing was just another word for hiding.

Now I swam the deck in full daylight with other divers' bubbles rising silver around me, and the brass tags caught the sun.

There's a thing that happens down there, at depth, that surface people don't believe: the dead feel present. Not haunted-movie present. More like neighbors. For nine years they'd been my neighbors, and I'd kept my fence high and my visits transactional. Take the photo, log the crate, get out before the feeling gets big enough to drown you. Guilt is heavy gear. You can dive with it if you're careful, but it eats your air.

Today I swam slowly and let it be big.

I found the hold where Daniel Quist's photographs had been taken—the ones his sister decrypted on a backup drive while Tomas Reyes fevered in the bow of my boat, the manifests stamped CLASSIFIED, the note in his blocky engineer's handwriting: *they sank it on purpose.* The hatch stood open now, officially, forensically open, evidence markers fluttering on little lines like festival flags. Inside, the crates were empty. Everything needed had already come up—every document, every charge casing, every bone the recovery teams could find, each one tagged and carried into the light and given back a name.

I'd helped carry some of them myself. Testimony does that. You give a sworn statement under your real name for the first time in years, and then they ask if you'll assist the recovery divers, because you know the wreck better than any living person, and you say yes, because saying no would be going back to the fence, and the fence doesn't exist anymore. I'd burned it myself. Collapsed a cavern entrance with dive charges to force the navy to excavate in public view—that was the whole trick of it, the only leverage a man like me ever had: make the truth too expensive to bury twice.

My hand drifted toward a rusted fitting near the hold coaming—a valve wheel, half-buried, the kind of thing I'd have brought up without thinking in the old days, sold to a collector who liked his relics morally unexamined. I wrapped my fingers around it. Cold iron. Eighty-seven names on brass tags above me, sunlight in columns.

I let go.

That was the whole dive, really. That was the ceremony nobody had scheduled: a man touching the thing he would have taken, and leaving it. Everything needed has already come up. What stayed down belonged to the dead, and the dead, for the first time since I'd known them, were not mine to traffic in. They were the country's now. They were history's. They were Mara's brother's life's work, completed posthumously by strangers with clipper boards and government credentials, and if that wasn't justice exactly, it was at least the end of the lie, which is where justice has to start.

Near the stern, at the tag marked *R. OKONKWO*, age 34, I stopped. I didn't know him. Nobody living knew him; he'd been a ferry mechanic, married eleven days when the ship went down. But his tag was at the end of the litany, and somebody has to be last, and I figured I could be the one to keep him company while the light moved.

I stayed until my computer grumbled about no-deco limits. Then I hung my flag on the ascent line and went up slow, watching the wreck shrink beneath me—not disappearing, the way things used to disappear here, but receding, the way things do when they've finally been seen enough.

* * *

Mara had the coffee ready when I broke the surface, which told me she'd been watching my bubbles the whole time and pretending to read. I came up the ladder dripping, and she looked at me with that gauge-reading expression and said, "Well?"

"Well what?"

"Did you find peace down there, or whatever it is people write in the brochures?"

"Found a valve wheel," I said. "Left it there."

Something moved through her face—understanding, maybe, arriving ahead of language, the way it usually did with her. She handed me a towel instead of a speech. That was another thing we'd learned about each other: when to hand over the towel instead of the words.

We sat in the cockpit while I steamed dry in the strengthening sun. Below us the bay did its morning business—dive boats, a fisheries launch, two kids in a canoe paddling out to read the plaque aloud, because that was a thing people did now. Read the names at the surface. All eighty-seven. Some of them couldn't get through it without stopping. Nobody minded the stopping.

"Tomas called," Mara said after a while. "From the hospital. He's walking without the cane most days. He says the inquiry lawyers want him back on the stand Thursday, and he wants us there."

"Us," I said.

"Us." She turned the thermos cap in her hands. "He said—and I quote—'Tell Whitlock the dead don't need a bodyguard anymore. They need a witness who shows up.' He's very smug for a man who nearly died in a root hollow."

"He earned the smugness."

"There's more." She hesitated, and I braced, because Mara hesitating was rarer than calm water in the gorge. "The survey team finished the sonar mapping yesterday. The whole valley floor." She looked at me. "Paloma, there's a second wreck. Upstream. Older. Pre-dates the *Vane* by twenty years, at least. Same signature—scuttle charges, sealed cargo, the works. It's not in any record. It's not in *any* record."

I sat with that. Somewhere downstream a fish jumped, indifferent to the arc of human history.

"They sank more than one," I said.

"They sank more than one." She said it flatly, but her hand found mine on the armrest, and her grip said everything the flatness wouldn't. "The inquiry can pretend it's an anomaly. One bad commander, one bad night, case closed. Or—" She stopped. Looked at me. And I knew what she was going to say before she said it, because I'd been feeling it building in my chest since the moment I let go of that valve wheel, the strange lightness of a man whose hiding place has been demolished and who has discovered, to his considerable surprise, that he likes the exposure.

"Or we go look," I said.

"Or we go look." A breath. "Together. Openly. On the record this time."

I looked out at the bay—at the dive flags, the kids reading names, the yellow buoy standing guard over eighty-seven people who were finally, permanently, above water. Nine years I'd spent learning how to vanish. It had taken one river, one stubborn woman, one dying engineer, and eighty-seven reasons to learn the opposite trick.

"Give me a week to service the boat," I said.

Mara smiled—the real one, the rare one, the one worth every fathom I'd ever sunk. "Take two," she said. "We're going to need the fuel."
