## 22. A Landfall for the Summit

The river looked different when you weren't lying to it.

That was the thought I kept circling as we loaded the boat at the landing in the gray half-light of a morning in late spring, a year and some change after everything. Same water. Same brown-green current sliding past the pilings with that patient indifference rivers have, like they've seen every kind of fool float down them and expect to see every kind again. But I was different, or at least differently arranged. For eleven years I'd come to this landing the way a man visits a mistress—before dawn, no witnesses, cash for fuel, gone before anyone could attach my face to my boat. This morning I stood at the fuel dock in full daylight, paid with a card that had my actual name on it, and let the kid running the pump read it off out loud.

"Voss," he said, squinting at me. "Like the guy from the inquiry? The diver?"

"The same."

"Huh." He handed back the card like it might be warm. "My uncle said you were dead."

"Your uncle," I said, "is one of many."

Mara laughed from the boat—that low, unguarded laugh she still hadn't quite gotten used to having, like a woman who'd spent years rationing her own joy and was only now learning the supply was renewable. She was coiling lines with the economy of someone who'd learned knots from her brother and then improved on them out of spite. Daniel had taught her well. She'd taught herself better.

Tomas was already aboard, settled into the helm chair they'd practically built around him during his recovery, one hand resting on the wheel like an old dog's chin on a familiar knee. He'd put on thirty pounds since the root hollow, most of it muscle, all of it stubborn. The doctors at the hospital had told him he'd be lucky to walk without a limp by winter. It was spring now, and he walked fine, and he'd told them so in writing, which if you knew Tomas was the most violent thing he'd ever done.

"You're staring," he said without looking up.

"I'm assessing my crew."

"Your crew is assessing your fuel bill. You let the marina kid charge you dockside prices." He tapped the gauge. "Eleven years off the grid and you still can't haggle."

"Some skills atrophy," I said. "Others get replaced by a conscience. It's a bad trade, honestly."

* * *

We were going upriver. That was the whole of the plan, and the whole of the plan was the point.

Not fleeing this time. Not slipping upstream under moonlight with dive gear hidden under tarps and a rifle wrapped in oilcloth among the salvage, the way I'd done it the first time, when the river was a corridor and every mile of it a held breath. This trip we carried survey equipment, cameras, a depth sounder, and a stack of official-looking permits that were actually official—the first such documents ever issued for the upper reaches, signed by people whose signatures would have meant nothing a year ago because those people hadn't existed yet either. The inquiry had created jobs. Cartographers. Archivists. A commission with a name long enough to need its own acronym, which everyone now just called the Source Authority, and which Mara served on with the particular ferocity of a woman who intended to personally correct every lie on every chart.

"We're mapping," she'd said when she proposed it, six months back, over coffee in a kitchen that was technically mine and functionally ours, a distinction I'd stopped fighting sometime around the second month. "The whole system. Every channel, every cave entrance, every barrier the navy sank to keep people out. We put it all on public record. Every sounding, every photograph. If it's on a map, it can't be disappeared."

"That's not how maps work," I'd said. "Maps disappear all the time. I've watched it happen."

"Then we make too many copies." She'd set down her cup with a click that ended the discussion. "You know the water better than any survey team they could hire. And you owe the river a debt you can't pay in testimony alone."

She was right, which was annoying, and she knew she was right, which was more annoying, and I loved her, which made the whole arrangement unbearable in the way that only true things are unbearable.

So: mapping. Openly. In daylight. With our names on the manifest and a satellite transponder broadcasting our position to anyone who cared to look, which was the entire point. The wilderness didn't care. The wilderness had never cared whether we came honest or crooked—it forgave nothing either way, took its tolls in blood and hull plating regardless of your intentions. But we'd stopped asking it to forget. That was the difference. That was the whole difference, and it had cost two years and more than I could itemize, and I'd pay it again before I'd go back to being a rumor.

* * *

The first day was easy water, the broad lower reaches where the river spread out and forgot it was headed somewhere dramatic. Tomas ran the helm while Mara worked the sounder and I sat on the bow doing the thing I was actually best at, which was looking at water and seeing what wasn't obvious.

The old landmarks were still there, wearing new meanings. The fuel stop where the men without insignia had questioned me—where a polite man with a flat voice had offered me money to turn around and then, beneath the courtesy, offered me the alternative—I steered wide of it on principle. There was a new dock there now, and a sign advertising cold drinks, and a family was eating lunch on it. Children. On that dock. A year ago the idea would have been obscene, like flowers growing out of a gun barrel.

"They're building a footpath up to the gorge overlook," Mara said, coming forward with the chart tablet. "Funding came through last month. They want to call it something. There's a committee."

"A committee for naming a path."

"There are committees for everything now. It's what accountability looks like when it gets bored." She dropped down beside me, shoulder against mine, and studied the water like she was learning to read it. Two years of living with a diver and she'd picked up the habit—reading surface texture for what lay beneath. She was getting good at it. "They want to name part of it after Daniel."

I said nothing for a while. The river said plenty.

"What do you think?" I finally asked.

"I think he'd have hated it. He hated having his name on things. He used to submit papers under initials so reviewers couldn't discount him for his age." She smiled at the memory, and it hurt to watch, the way good memories of dead people always hurt—a clean pain, useful pain. "But I think the path should exist. People should be able to stand above the Gorge of Bells and hear it. You can't understand what happened here if you've never heard how loud the quiet places get."

The Gorge of Bells. I'd portaged around it once carrying her on my back with her ankle ballooning and the evidence strapped to my chest and a man dying in the water behind us. I did not say any of this. She knew. She knew all of it now—every piece, including the pieces I'd spent eleven years hoping would rot quietly at the bottom of me. Confession in the rain, under the ledge, the whole ugly architecture of it: the secret contract, the hold full of ordnance and paper, the hush money I'd taken and buried myself under along with the truth. She knew that when the expedition went silent and the supply runner asked me to go look, I'd said no twice before saying yes, and that the yes had been bought not by courage but by recognition—one voice on a corrupted radio transmission, a man I'd wronged reaching up out of the dark asking, without knowing it, for the one person who owed him everything.

She'd taken it the way she took everything—not with forgiveness, which she considered cheap, but with understanding, which she considered expensive and therefore worth paying for. "You took the money because you were young and scared and they made sure you were both," she'd said. "And then you spent eleven years paying it back in the only currency you had left, which was silence. I'm not going to thank you for the silence. But I'm not going to pretend I don't know what it cost you."

That was Mara. She didn't absolve. She accounted.

* * *

*

We camped the second night at the site of the burned camp, because Mara wanted to fix its position precisely and because I wanted—I admitted this only to myself—to see it standing in daylight without dread.

It was just a clearing now. The river had been working on it, softening the ash into soil, pushing grass up through the old fire scar. The tent stake driven through the chart marking the wreck site was long gone; I'd pulled it out myself, that first terrible morning, and given it to the inquiry along with everything else. But I could still find the spot where it had stood, the way you can find a healed wound if you press.

"Coordinates confirmed," Mara called from the bank, tablet glowing. "Cross-referenced with Daniel's backup drive. It matches."

"It'll match forever now," I said. "That's the point of you."

She looked up at that, and something passed between us across the clearing—across the place where her brother's camp had burned and where, forty meters under the water beyond it, the wreck sat in its new legal dignity, buoyed and protected and dived by researchers who read the names of the drowned aloud before descending. Names read aloud. That had been Mara's insistence at the hearings, and she'd won it, and now it was procedure: no dive on the site begins until someone says the names. Forty-one civilians. Nine crew who'd refused the scuttling order and gone down arguing. My old handler, whose name turned out to be real after all, though nothing else about him had been.

Tomas joined us at the fire that night, moving slowly, favoring the leg on cold evenings the way he denied favoring it. He looked at the clearing for a long time.

"I crawled through there," he said. "After. That direction—" he pointed with his chin, "—toward the root hollow. I remember thinking if I could just reach the water, the water would hide me. Because that's what you learn about rivers growing up. They keep things."

"Do they?" Mara asked softly.

"No." He fed a stick to the fire. "That's what I learned up here instead. Rivers don't keep things. They deliver them. Slowly, maybe. Downstream, eventually. Everything comes down the river sooner or later." He glanced at me. "Even you."

* * *

The third day we entered the clear water.

Past the last of the old survey markers—the ones the original charts acknowledged, before the closure—the river changed character the way it always did, going suddenly, impossibly transparent, spring-fed and cold and lit from below. The rusted barriers were still there, the shot-through warning plaques, and we photographed all of it, catalogued it, GPS-tagged every corroded chain and bullet-pocked sign. Evidence of the effort that had gone into making a place unplaceable. Somebody had spent real money ensuring this water stayed empty. Now we were spending real money ensuring it stayed full—full of light, of record, of visitors, eventually, when the trails opened and the world remembered there was a source.

At the cavern system we anchored and went down together, Mara and I, on open-circuit with lights and line reels, through the entrance that I had collapsed with dive charges two years ago and that the recovery teams had since re-opened properly, engineered, shored, mapped. The sister section was exactly where the dynamite had hidden it: the flooded galleries holding the full record of the civilian deaths, manifests and photographs and the ship's own logs, preserved by cold and darkness and deliberate secrecy. It was a library now. There was a rack at the entrance for dive lights and a laminated card listing the names, and you were supposed to read them before entering, and we did, all forty-one plus nine, standing chest-deep in the shallows while Tomas held the boat and read aloud in his careful, accented voice, giving each name its full weight, no rushing.

Inside, the water was so clear it felt like flying. Mara swam the galleries with her camera, documenting yet again, though the documentation had been done and done—but she'd explained her reasoning once, and I'd never forgotten it: "Archives die of neglect, not destruction. You have to keep visiting. A witness isn't a moment. It's a practice."

A witness isn't a moment. It's a practice. I'd started thinking of my whole life that way—the eleven wasted years, the two hard ones, whatever came next. Not a story with an ending. A practice. Something you got up and did.

Daniel's final camp still sat above the cavern mouth, preserved now under a shelter the Authority had built, his journals under glass. Mara visited the way some people visit graves—with coffee, with talk, with updates. She told him about the mapping project. About the footpath committee. About me, sometimes, in a tone I pretended not to overhear and treasured anyway.

* * *

*

On the fifth day we reached the headwaters proper, and I understood, finally, why the book needed this title.

The river didn't begin dramatically. That was the joke of it, the beautiful anticlimax of it. After all the gorges and bells and drowned secrets, the source was a modest upwelling—a hillside seeping clean out of limestone, gathering itself in pools, deciding to become a river the way a decision gets made: gradually, and then all at once. The summit of a river is not a peak. It's a landfall in reverse. A place where water arrives out of the earth and turns, and commits, and goes.

We stood on the bank above it. All three of us. Tomas filled a bottle, ceremonially, for his mother. Mara took photographs until she ran out of reasons to hide behind the camera, and then she just stood there with her hands in her pockets, crying without drama, letting the source do what sources do.

I thought about the man I'd been at the start of this—the one registered to a dead man, paid in cash, diving a wreck nobody named, so carefully invisible that I'd nearly succeeded in convincing even myself I didn't exist. I'd thought invisibility was safety. It was just a slower drowning. The river had known it all along. Rivers deliver.

"So," Mara said, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, businesslike again, which was her version of composure and I loved it. "Survey runs start tomorrow. Upper channels first. Tomas has the helm, I have the sounder, you have—" she gestured at all of me, "—whatever it is you have."

"Cynicism and boat sense."

"Don't undersell the boat sense."

We launched downstream the next morning, which felt right. You come home by coming back to the water, not away from it. The current took us gently at first, then less gently, the river remembering its intentions, and Tomas handled the helm with the quiet pleasure of a man who'd been given back a thing he loved, and Mara called depths and hazards from the bow, and I sat where I always sat, reading the water, seeing what wasn't obvious—which these days was mostly just the future, arriving like weather, unwritten and unmapped and entirely ours to chart.

The wilderness still forgives nothing. Fine. We weren't asking anymore.

We were just telling it where we'd be, and going anyway.
