## 1. Miles, Not Years

Vesper Joubert kept his life in a leather notebook the way other men kept theirs in calendars. Not dates—distances. *Port Sable to the Tern shoals, eleven miles by skiff. Tern shoals to the government pier, six. Pier to boarding house, two on foot.* Fourteen years of it, column after column, the sums climbing like a debt he could never pay down. He was thirty-four years old and had walked, rowed, and ridden roughly the circumference of a small moon, and he could not have told you a single year of it that had mattered more than any other.

The coast survey was honest work, which was to say it was dull. He spent his mornings wading the tidal flats with a sounding rod, driving it into the gray silt until it refused, marking the depth in pencil on a slate strapped to his wrist. His afternoons he spent triangulating from a tripod on the dunes while the wind tried to peel his hat off and the gulls screamed their opinions of him. The government paid by the mile surveyed, not by the hour endured, and so Vesper had taught himself to think of the world as something to be crossed rather than inhabited. A marsh was a distance. A headland was a distance. People, when he thought of them at all, were also distances—his mother, dead six years, four hundred miles and a lifetime away; his father, dead three, an unmeasurable distance that no instrument he owned could sound.

He did not think of his father. He had gotten very good at not thinking of his father, the way a man gets good at not favoring an old wound, until the not-thinking becomes its own kind of labor, its own full-time employment.

The boarding house at Port Sable stood on stilts above the tideline, and his room in it was eight feet by ten, which he knew precisely because he had measured it the first night, out of habit. He had lived there eleven months, the longest he had stayed anywhere since he was nineteen, and he told himself this meant nothing. The landlady, a widow named Mrs. Calloway with forearms like a stevedore, left a lamp burning in the hall for him because she had noticed he came in late and had never once asked why. He had noticed her noticing. He had not thanked her. Gratitude was a distance too, and he had never learned the measure of it.

On the evening the telegram came, he was cleaning his sounding rod with an oiled rag, working the cloth along the brass in slow strokes, when Mrs. Calloway's knock came—two raps, no more, her way.

"Boy's brought something for you." She set a tray on his desk: a plate of bread and salt fish she hadn't been asked to bring, and on top of it, folded twice, a yellow flimsy. "From the capital. Boy says the man who sent it paid double for same-day delivery and looked like he hadn't slept."

Vesper set down the rod. Telegrams, in his experience, came in two varieties: extensions of contract, and news of death. He had received three of the second kind in his life and could still recall the weight of each one in his hand, the way paper could be heavier than stone.

He unfolded it.

*JOUbert V. SURVEYOR. PORT SABLE. STOP. HIGHLAND SURVEY TEAM SEVEN UNACCOUNTED FOR UPSTREAM SOURCE DISTRICT RIVER KESS. STOP. GEAR RECOVERED INTACT NO PERSONNEL. STOP. MINISTRY REQUIRES EXPERIENCED FIELD MAN FOR RECOVERY AND ASSESSMENT. STOP. YOU WERE RECOMMENDED. STOP. DEPART IMMEDIATELY. STOP. TRANSPORT PROVIDED. STOP. DIRECTOR HALE.*

He read it twice. The bread went cold on the tray. Outside, the tide was coming in, filling the pilings' shadows with a sound like slow breathing.

*No personnel.* Four words doing the work of a whole cemetery. Teams did not vanish. Teams drowned, and left boots on the bank. Teams fell, and left ropes. Teams went mad and wandered, and were found, eventually, or were not. But a team of six trained men did not simply stop, all at once, like a clock with the pin pulled.

And then there was the other thing, the thing that sat in the telegram like a fishhook in soft flesh: *You were recommended.*

Recommended by whom? He had spent fourteen years making himself small and mobile and unmemorable, a man whose references were distances. The Ministry's highland division was a closed world of career men and family names. Nobody in that world knew Vesper Joubert existed—except the people who had known his father, and his father had been dead three years, and dead men did not make recommendations.

Except that dead men, in Vesper's experience, were the only ones who ever did.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat at the desk with the lamp turned low and the notebook open in front of him, and he found himself, without deciding to, turning back through the years. *Port Sable, eleven months. Before that, the Karst survey, seven months. Before that, the dredge camps, five.* Back and back, the columns marching, and somewhere behind all of it, at the very beginning of the book, the first entry, made in a hand younger and angrier than his own: *Left home. Nineteen miles to the rail junction. Walked it.*

He had written that entry the day after his father had told him, in the flat voice the old man used for facts he considered self-evident, that a man's work was the only monument worth raising. His father had spent thirty years in the highlands digging into the earth's memory—valley surveys, river sources, strata and sediment—and had come home each season with his fingernails permanently dark and his eyes full of some country Vesper had never been invited to see. *The better digger*, his father's colleagues had called him, half in admiration and half in complaint, because Aldous Joubert could read ground the way priests read scripture and had no patience for anyone who couldn't.

Vesper had inherited the gift and hated it. He had used it to measure coastlines instead, flat honest places where the ground kept no secrets worth having, where the only thing buried was shell and the occasional drowned boat. He had told himself for fourteen years that this was a career. He had known, on some unlit level, that it was an exile.

The tide turned sometime after midnight. He heard it in the pilings, the long exhale of the ebb. He got up and stood at the window. Below, the flats lay exposed to the moon, silver and ribbed like the palm of an enormous hand, and his sounding rods stood planted in them in their rows, little stakes marking depths that would be gone by morning. That was the whole of his work, he thought. Marking depths that would be gone by morning.

In the notebook, he wrote: *Port Sable to the rail junction, by government transport, distance unknown.* He looked at the line a long time. It was the first entry in fourteen years he could not complete, because he did not know where he was going, only that it was upstream—up the Kess, toward the source district, toward country where the maps went blank and the ground kept everything. Toward, though he made himself write no such thing, the last coordinates his father had ever surveyed.

He packed by lamplight. He was quick at it; he had packed for departure more times than he could count, and his hands knew the ritual without him: instruments wrapped in oilcloth, boots, the brass compass his father had given him at fourteen and that he had carried ever since without ever once admitting it was a keepsake. At the bottom of the bag, wrapped separately, went the notebook.

Mrs. Calloway was in the kitchen before dawn, as always, and she pressed a paper of bread and salt fish into his hands at the door and looked at him with an expression he pretended not to understand.

"You'll come back through," she said. It was not quite a question.

"Contracts end," Vesper said.

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman who had buried a husband and raised two sons and knew precisely what a man meant when he answered a question he hadn't been asked. "The lamp stays lit."

* * *

The government transport was a coastal steamer with a chartered cabin and a crew that would not meet his eye, which told him the Ministry's money was aboard and the Ministry's business was not to be discussed. He spent the first day on deck watching the coast he had spent eleven months measuring slide away westward, every headland a number in his notebook, every number suddenly meaningless, the whole coastline resolving into what it had always been: a place he had been hiding.

The telegram rode in his breast pocket. He had read it so many times the creases had gone soft. On the second morning, alone at the rail with gray water sliding below, he took it out again and made himself look at the part he had been not-looking at since the first reading.

*You were recommended.*

He turned it over. On the reverse, in a different hand—clerical, hurried, the pencil pressed hard—someone had added a line that the telegraph clerk at Port Sable had not transmitted, or had not noticed, or had been paid not to notice:

*They are sending you because your father knew the source. Do not discuss the team. Do not discuss the valley. Sign nothing. — A friend of A.J.*

Vesper stood at the rail a long time, the flimsy trembling faintly in the wind off the water, though his hands were steady and always had been. Somewhere upstream, six men had stopped existing between one footfall and the next. Somewhere upstream, his father had spent thirty years reading ground that the government now wanted read by his son. And somewhere in the world there was a person who called themselves a friend of Aldous Joubert, which was a country Vesper had believed uninhabited since he was nineteen years old.

He took out the notebook, opened it to the fresh page, and under the unfinished entry he wrote, in his small even hand:

*Distance to my father: pending.*

Then he closed the book, and watched the river's mouth open ahead of them in the gray morning like a door.
