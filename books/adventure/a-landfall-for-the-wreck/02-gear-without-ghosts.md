## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The river told me where the camp was before I ever saw it.

Rivers are honest in a way people aren't. They carry what they're given—silt, leaves, the occasional dead bird—and they deliver it downstream without editorializing. Three kilometers below the last known position of Survey Team Nine, the water had started carrying things that didn't belong to it: a strip of waxed canvas, a pencil stub with a surveyor's hexagonal cross-section, and finally, snagged in the roots of a fallen fig tree, a single bootlace, coiled neat as a question mark.

I followed the river up because the river was the only thing that knew where it was going.

The valley narrowed as I climbed, walls of black basalt pressing close enough that the light came down in ribbons instead of sheets. Orchids grew out of nothing—out of bare stone, out of shadow, pale as drowned fingers. The air smelled of wet iron and crushed fern. Somewhere above me, unseen, water fell from one shelf of rock to another, a sound like slow applause.

I had been walking since before dawn, and I had not stopped thinking about the telegram. *Team missing. Equipment intact at last camp. Requesting Joubert specifically. You're the better digger.*

*The better digger.* As if there had been two of us measured on some scale, father and son, and the government had kept score all these years without telling me. As if my father's ghost were a reference I'd listed on an application.

I found the camp at midafternoon, in a shelf of flat ground where the river bent away from the cliff and left a beach of gray pebbles. And I stopped walking, because my body understood something before my mind did: nothing here was wrong, and everything here was wrong.

The tents stood. All four of them, guy-lines taut, rainflies drawn, corners staked with the even tension of hands that knew their work. A tarp stretched between two trees over the mess area, still shedding the morning's condensation in a fine silver drip. A kettle sat on a cold fire-ring, upright, lid on. A line of laundry hung between two saplings—a shirt, two pairs of socks, a towel—all dry now, all still hanging, patient as laundry in a painting.

Six men had walked into this valley. Six men had lived here, eaten here, argued about card games and bad coffee here. And then they had stepped out of existence so cleanly that the camp hadn't even noticed.

I stood at the edge of the pebble beach for a long time, letting the silence have its way with me. Then I did what I'd been hired to do. I began to read the ground.

That was the first wrongness, and it was the worst one. There were no tracks leading away.

There were plenty leading in—the team's own arrivals, weeks old, softened by rain but legible to anyone who'd spent his life reading dirt like scripture. Boot prints in the mud at the river's edge, the crisscross of daily traffic between tents and waterline, the churned patch where someone had dragged a supply crate. All of it pointed one direction: *in*. They had come here. They had lived here.

But from the camp itself—from its center, from the fire-ring, from the front flaps of every tent—there was nothing. No trail out. No prints toward the ridge, toward the gorge, toward the treeline. It was as if six men had been lifted off the earth by the collar, like figures cleared from a board game.

Rain should have erased them, I told myself. Rain erases everything. But rain doesn't erase selectively. Rain would have chewed the *incoming* tracks just as thoroughly, and those remained, faded but certain, a fossil record of arrival without departure.

I circled the camp twice, widening each ring, reading the leaf litter, the moss, the soft silt along the streamlet that trickled behind the cook area. Nothing. Birds moved through the trees overhead. The river kept its applause. I was alone with six absences.

Then I went through their things, and the wrongness changed flavor—from eerie to deliberate.

The boots were paired. Every man's boots stood beside his tent flap in matched pairs, laces tucked inside, toes pointing toward the river. Not scattered, not kicked off in haste, not lost in a flood or a landslide. *Arranged.* Beside them, in most cases, other small offerings of order: a folded jacket, a hat set crown-down, a mess kit stacked with its cup nested inside the bowl.

Inside the first tent, a sleeping bag rolled tight as a sausage. Inside the second, the same. In the third, a man's shaving kit laid open on his cot, razor dry, mirror angled at the ceiling—as if he expected to come back and find his own face waiting.

Nobody packs like that when they're leaving in a hurry. Nobody packs like that when they're leaving at all. You pack like that when you're tidying up after someone else. When you want the place to look like no one was ever here—or like everyone who was here meant to return, and simply hasn't yet.

Both readings frightened me. I couldn't decide which frightened me more.

The equipment crates were the strangest part. Government survey gear is expensive, serialized, inventoried within an inch of its life. Theodolites, levels, range finders, sealed drums of film, a shortwave radio with its handset coiled on top. All of it present. All of it *functional*, as far as I could tell—nothing smashed, nothing soaked, nothing scavenged by weather or animals. Whoever arranged this camp had taken care to preserve it. Which meant whoever arranged this camp had wanted it found.

Or wanted *me* to find it. The thought arrived uninvited and sat down heavily beside me while I worked.

It was in the fourth tent that I found the journals.

Three of them, standard government issue, oilcloth covers, brass corner caps. They lay in the center of the tent floor in a neat row, and on top of each one rested a stone. River stones, smooth and gray, each about the size of a fist, each placed with care, the way you'd weight a tablecloth against wind.

Journals weighted shut. I crouched over them for a long moment before touching anything, because some part of me—the part my father had trained without either of us admitting it—understood that I was looking at a message. Stones hold things down. Stones keep things from blowing away. Stones say: *this matters; don't lose it.*

I lifted the first stone. Opened the first journal.

Survey log, routine as communion. Grid references, barometric readings, notes on vegetation and drainage. Team Nine had been mapping the upper watershed for the Water Resources Directorate, the same directorate whose letterhead sat in my breast pocket. The handwriting belonged to a junior surveyor named Okafor—neat, tireless, faintly pedantic. He complained about the leeches. He praised the cook. On the twelfth day he wrote that they'd reached the head of navigation and would proceed on foot toward the source country, per orders.

The second journal was the team leader's. Captain E. Vasquez-Rey. Harder hand, slanted forward like a runner leaning into wind. His entries shortened as they climbed. Day fourteen: *Local guides refuse to pass the falls. Paid them off. Continuing with five.* Day sixteen: *Found cut stone in the riverbank. Much older than anything on record for this region. Photographed.* Day seventeen, a single line: *We are being watched from the far bank. Do not feel threatened. Feel observed.*

And then the third journal, which was also Vasquez-Rey's, though the hand had begun to fray at the edges, letters losing their discipline the way men do. The final entries were dated nine days ago.

Day eighteen: *Okafor swears the stones near the pool are dressed. Cut faces under the moss. This valley was not empty before the dam. Someone made sure the maps said otherwise.*

Day nineteen: *Radioed the Directorate with our position and preliminary findings. Response came back inside the hour, which frightens me more than the watchers did. They told us to hold position. They told us a retrieval party was coming. Retrieval is an odd word for it.*

Day twenty, the last page, written fast, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*Men gone from camp in the night. No sound. No struggle. Found their beds empty and their boots standing paired outside, which is impossible, which means someone came in and staged this after. I am writing this in the daylight with the radio dead and the stones weighted on the logs so whoever comes looking will know we did not wander. We did not wander. If you are reading this, go to the coordinates. The old dig. J-7. He was right. He was right about all of it and they buried him for it, and now they've come back to bury us too.*

*Coordinates follow. God forgive me for leading them here.*

Below that, in figures I knew better than my own birthday, was a string of numbers.

My father's dig.

I sat back on my heels in the doorway of the dead men's tent, and the valley seemed to lean in around me. J-7. Julian Joubert. Sector seven of the excavation he'd run, alone and unfunded and officially disgraced, in the years before the dam filled the lower valley and the government's maps redrew history over the top of it. The years when he stopped coming home. The years when I learned to measure my life in miles precisely so I wouldn't have to measure it in him.

He'd died eleven years ago, alone, upriver, in country the maps went blank past. An accident, the coroner wrote. Exposure. A man of his age, alone in high country—it happens.

They'd sent me his effects in a box. I'd never opened half of it.

Now a stranger's dying hand had written his coordinates in a journal weighted shut with river stones, and somewhere upstream, in the country where the guides refused to walk, whatever my father had found was still waiting—and whatever had taken six surveyors in a single silent night was waiting with it.

I should have gone back down. That was the sane arithmetic: one man, no backup, monsoon season stacking its black battlements over the western ridges, a valley that swallowed teams of six without leaving a print. Every professional instinct I owned voted for retreat.

But dread and compulsion are twin currents in the same river, and I have never once in my life managed to swim against the second one.

I took the journals. All three, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped inside my pack against my spine like a second backbone. I doused their cold fire, out of respect or superstition, I couldn't say. At the edge of the camp I paused and looked back once—at the taut tents, the paired boots, the laundry drying for men who would never wear it again.

"You didn't wander," I said aloud, to no one, to all of them. "I know."

The river applauded. The clouds over the source country had gone the color of gunmetal, and beneath them, three days' climb upstream, a waterfall I had seen only once, as a boy, from very far away, was hiding the last work of Julian Joubert.

I turned upstream and started walking.

By nightfall I'd reached the ferry crossing at the gorge mouth, where the river ran wide and brown and the far bank was a wall of unlit green. There was a lamp burning on the water—a flat-bottomed boat sliding out of the dusk with a woman at the tiller, silver-haired, unhurried, reading the current the way priests read scripture.

She brought the boat alongside the shingle and looked at me, at my pack, at the direction I'd come from, and her face did something complicated.

"Upstream," she said. Not a question.

"I need to get to the head of navigation."

"No one goes past the falls." She said it the way you'd recite a law older than any government's.

"I know," I said. "I'm going anyway. I'll pay."

She studied me a moment longer, and something in my face must have answered a question of hers, because she sighed and swung the tiller over.

"Get in," said Maren. "And tell me who you are, that you're walking into the one place this river keeps its dead."
