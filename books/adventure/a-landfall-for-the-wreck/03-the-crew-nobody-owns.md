## 3. The Crew Nobody Owns

The harbor at Port Callas smelled of diesel and salt and the particular rot of fish left too long in the sun, and Vesper Joubert walked its length twice before he let himself believe what he was looking for would be there. It always was, if you knew where to look. Every port had one—the boat that ran when no other boat would run, captained by someone who had stopped asking passengers why they needed to go.

He found her at the third berth past the customs shed: a flat-bottomed river ferry with a patched hull and a name scraped half away. *Kindly*. The letters that remained were painted a blue that had once been bright.

The woman scrubbing her deck looked up before he spoke. She was fifty, maybe older, with forearms like mooring rope and gray shot through her black hair in a way that suggested weather rather than age. Her eyes did the thing he'd learned to watch for in his years of contract work—she read him in one pass, boots to face, and filed him somewhere.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm hiring."

"Most people say good morning first."

"Good morning." He held out the telegram. "Vesper Joubert. I need to go upstream on the Serrano. Past the last marked bend."

She took the paper but didn't read it right away. She looked at it the way a person looks at a snake they've found in their kitchen—not with fear exactly, but with the calculation of whether it's worth the trouble of killing.

"The Serrano above the falls," she said. "That's not water anymore. That's country."

"I know."

"Do you." She finally unfolded the telegram, read it twice, and handed it back. "Government's offering this much for a man to go look for men they probably don't want found?"

"They want them found quietly. I intend to find them loudly."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. A door opening an inch onto a lit room.

"Maren Oduya," she said. "I own the boat and the boat owns me. What's your rate?"

They haggled the way people haggle who both expect to be lied to, and settled somewhere that cost Vesper most of what the government had offered him, which suited him fine. Money was distance. Distance was all he'd ever saved.

* * *

Maren

The boy came to the dock at dusk, the way he always did, carrying nothing and knowing everything. Maren had fished Ivo out of the flood season three years back—a skinny river-rat with no surname anyone could pronounce and a way of reading water that made grown men nervous. He wasn't hers. Nobody's, really. But he slept aboard *Kindly* more nights than not, and she'd stopped pretending that meant nothing.

"You're going upriver," he said. Not a question. He'd seen the crates being loaded. He saw everything.

"Business."

"Past the falls?"

"Who told you about the falls?"

Ivo shrugged, which was its own answer. The river talked to him. She'd never decided whether that was a gift or a sickness.

"There's pay in it," she said, because the boy took offense at charity the way priests take offense at heresy. "Real pay. And you're the only one who can read the shallows above Kettle Bend since old Samo died."

He considered her for a long moment, thirteen going on ancient, and then said the thing he always said when she tried to give him something:

"I'm not staying on your boat, Maren. I'm just here while the water's high."

"Sure," she said. "While the water's high."

He slept in the wheelhouse that night. He'd been sleeping there for three years.

* * *

Tull found them, or perhaps they found Tull; afterward no one could say which. Vesper located him in the back room of a dockside bar called the Anchor Line, where ex-soldiers went to drink themselves into forgetting which wars had been theirs. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a sergeant's posture ruined by a civilian slouch, and when Vesper laid the map on the table between them, Tull looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"Serrano headwaters," he said at last. "That's blank country. Government maps stop at the gorge."

"That's why I need you."

"You need a soldier for blank country?" Tull's laugh had gravel in it. "Soldiers are for countries somebody drew."

"I need someone who knows how to hold a trail when the trail doesn't want holding. Someone who's done garrison duty in places where the locals decide who walks through."

Tull turned his glass in a slow circle. "Who else you got?"

"A ferry-woman. A kid."

"A kid."

"He reads water better than any man I've hired."

Tull looked at the map again, and Vesper watched something pass over his face—not greed, not adventure, but the particular hunger of a man who had come home from a war to discover home was the thing that didn't fit anymore. Vesper knew that hunger. He wore it himself, under the surveyor's calm, like a coat lining.

"Half up front," Tull said. "And I keep my own rifle."

"Done."

* * *

Maren

They cast off at first light, and Maren stood at the wheel watching her crew arrange itself around her boat like strangers in a waiting room. The surveyor sat amidships with his instruments, already measuring things, because that was how he held the world at arm's length—she'd figured that out inside a day. The soldier checked the cargo lashings twice, then a third time, hands busy so his mind wouldn't have to be. And the boy perched on the rail with his feet over the water, trailing his fingers in the current as though taking its pulse.

Nobody owned these people. That was why she'd signed on herself, if she was honest, though she'd told Vesper it was the money. A crew nobody owned was the only kind worth belonging to.

"Sandbar," Ivo said, without lifting his head from the water.

Maren saw it two seconds later, a pale seam in the brown current. She swung wide. Behind her, Tull grunted—the closest thing to approval he'd issued yet—and Vesper wrote something in his notebook.

By noon the boy had called four bars, a sunken log, and a change in current that meant rain in the hills within two days. By evening, Tull had reorganized the entire cargo deck without being asked, and Vesper had stopped writing in his notebook and started just watching them.

* * *

The first night they made camp on a shingle bank below Kettle Bend, and the fire did what fires do—it pulled the truth out of people by inches, the way tide pulls a crab from under a stone.

Tull cooked, because soldiers cook the way they do everything else, methodically and without complaint. Rice and tinned fish and chili oil he produced from some bottomless pocket. They ate in a triangle around the flames, and the silence had a different texture than the day's silences. Companionable. Almost.

"Kettle Bend," Vesper said. "You know it well?"

"Everyone on this river knows Kettle Bend," Maren said. "Ferry run crosses there. Has for thirty years. My mother ran it before me."

"What happened to her?"

The question landed harder than he'd intended; Vesper heard it land. Maren fed a stick to the fire instead of answering, and for a moment he thought she'd leave it buried.

"Fever year," she said finally. "Whole stretch of the river lost people. I was twenty-two. Took over the run because nobody else would cross during the quarantine." She glanced at him across the flames. "You want to know why I'm going past the falls, surveyor? Because my mother ferried this river her whole life and never once saw where it started. Somebody should see where it starts."

Ivo, curled against a crate with his knees drawn up, said nothing. But his eyes had come open, and they stayed on Maren with an expression the boy probably thought was hidden.

"And you, Tull?" Vesper asked. "Why does an ex-soldier sign on for blank country?"

Tull's jaw worked. "Garrison posting, six years, up on the northern line. You know what I guarded the whole time? Nothing. A border nobody crossed, guarding a line nobody drew for any reason anybody ever told me." He poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "I figure if I'm going to stand in empty country again, I'd like it to at least have a reason."

* * *

Vesper

Later, when the others had turned in, Vesper sat awake with the telegram and the journal page he'd copied from the abandoned camp, and felt the strange weight of the day settling on him.

He had spent eleven years measuring his life in kilometers. Contract to contract, coast to coast, each job a distance between himself and whatever it was his father had wanted him to be. He'd gotten good at it. Good at arriving, good at leaving, good at the particular loneliness of a man whose belongings fit in two cases.

And now here were three strangers on a shingle bank, sleeping in a rough triangle around a fire he hadn't built, and he realized he could not remember the last time he'd spent a night among people who weren't paid to be near him.

Except—they were paid to be near him. That was the arrangement. That was all it was.

He looked at Tull's sleeping bulk, at Maren's silhouette in the bow where she kept a watch she didn't delegate even to the boy, at Ivo's small form curled like a comma against the crate. Paid help. A crew assembled in four days from whoever the harbor had to offer.

So why did it feel like something being returned to him?

He thought of his father then—not the estrangement, not the years of silence, but the man himself, kneeling in some trench with a brush in his hand, saying the thing Vesper had hated him for saying: *A dig is just people, Vesper. The ground keeps. People don't.*

He'd argued with that for a decade. The ground kept; that was the whole point of it. Ground didn't leave. Ground didn't lie to you about coming back.

But sitting in the firelight with these three, Vesper felt the argument shifting under him like a sandbar in flood. Maybe his father had meant something else. Maybe keeping wasn't the same as staying.

Upstream, somewhere past the falls, in country the maps refused to name, his father's initials waited carved in stone. And between here and there lay three days of river that none of them had traveled, guarded by people none of them had met, for reasons the government telegram had been very careful not to state.

Ivo stirred in his sleep and murmured something—water-talk, river-words, the language he fell into when dreaming. Maren's head turned toward the sound and settled again.

Three days, Vesper thought. Then the blank country. Then whatever it was that had swallowed seven trained surveyors and left their boots paired neatly side by side.

He banked the fire and lay down facing the water, and the last thing he heard before sleep was the Serrano moving in the dark, patient and enormous, carrying them all upstream toward the place where it began.
