## 4. Upstream Rules

The river told you when you'd crossed into country that didn't want you.

Tull had learned to read water the way other men read faces—twenty-two years of soldiering will do that, most of it in places where the maps were lies and the rivers were the only honest witnesses left. So when the current tightened between walls of black stone and the water went from brown to a green so deep it looked like it had opinions, he called a halt without asking anyone's permission.

"From here," he said, "we're guests."

Vesper looked up from his charts—the government-issued kind, all confident lines and invented contours—and Tull watched him understand. The river narrowed ahead into a gorge where the survey paper simply gave up, the contour lines dissolving into blank cream space marked with a single printed word: *UNSURVEYED*. Not unexplored. Unsurveyed. As if the land had been asked and had declined to answer.

"Maps go blank," Ivo said, delighted, hanging off the gunwale of Maren's flat-bottomed boat like the teenager he was and the mountain goat he clearly believed himself to be. "Actual blank."

"Blank means somebody made it blank," Maren said. She poled them forward with an economy of motion Tull envied; he'd carried rifles heavier than her whole life, probably. "Paper doesn't forget on its own. Somebody burned it out."

Tull said nothing. He was thinking about the last time he'd stood at the edge of a blank place, and about what the army had paid him to do there, and about how some debts don't show on any ledger but follow a man upstream anyway.

* * *

They made the first rapids by noon, running the boat light while Tull and Vesper walked the gear along the portage path—a track so old and so precise it embarrassed every road Tull had ever helped build. Stones set like teeth. Switchbacks cut with patience rather than machinery. Whoever lived up here had been maintaining this path since before either of their governments existed, and would be maintaining it long after both were footnotes.

At the top of the gorge the valley opened like a held breath finally released. Terraces climbed the slopes in green steps. Smoke rose from rooflines hidden under thatch. And everywhere, the sound of water—not just the river but seeps and springs and threads of runoff, the whole mountainside weeping gently toward its source.

A boy saw them first. He didn't run or shout. He simply turned and walked uphill at a pace that managed to communicate, without one word, *they're coming, and they're not welcome, but they already know that.*

"They know we're here," Tull said.

"They knew before we launched," Maren said. "Probably before we signed contracts."

An hour later, as the light went long and copper, the elder came down to meet them.

He was small and upright, wrapped in wool the color of wet slate, and he walked with a stick he plainly did not need for balance—he used it the way a magistrate uses a gavel, tapping stone at intervals as though confirming the ground was still in order. Behind him came six men carrying nothing visible, which frightened Tull more than rifles would have. Men who carry nothing are men who don't expect to fight. They expect to win before fighting starts.

"You are going to the source," the elder said. Not a question. His accent was thick but his grammar was exact, the grammar of a man who'd learned a language from books and then decided which parts of it to keep.

"We're looking for people who went missing," Vesper said. "A government team. Four of them."

"The paper people." The elder's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "They also said they were looking. They said many words. Words are cheap up here. We have too many already; the wind brings them and won't take them back."

"We'll pay for passage," Vesper said. "We'll respect your law."

"Your law ends where the water begins." The elder tapped his stick twice against a boulder, and Tull noticed the boulder was carved—old marks, worn shallow, older than any alphabet Tull could name. "You may sleep tonight in the shelter house. One night. You may draw water, but not from the source itself. You may walk the low path, never the high one. And when the sun comes up tomorrow, you go back downstream."

"And if we don't?" Ivo asked, because he was seventeen and the question was genetically mandatory.

The elder looked at him for a long moment, and something in the look made even Ivo's shoulders come down.

"Then the mountain keeps you," he said, "the way it keeps everything else it is given." He turned and led them up toward the shelter house, and over his shoulder, almost as an afterthought: "Your government sent others once. A long time ago. A man with a beard and bad boots and eyes like a man reading something written inside his own skull."

Tull felt Vesper go still beside him, stillness like a struck bell.

"What happened to him?" Vesper asked.

"He stayed longer than one night," the elder said. "That is all I will say tonight. Tonight is for shelter, not for history."

* * *

The shelter house was a single room of timber and stone, swept clean, cold hearth, sleeping platforms along the walls. Someone had left firewood stacked with the same terrible precision as everything else in this valley. On the wall hung a rack of dried fish and a coil of rope, both offered, both clearly not gifts—loans against good behavior.

Maren got a fire going. Ivo went out to check the boat lines three times, which was his way of being nervous without admitting nerves existed. Tull sat with his back to the wall where he could see the door and the single window, an old habit he'd stopped apologizing for around the time he'd stopped being able to sleep any other way.

Vesper wasn't there.

Tull found him twenty minutes later, fifty meters behind the shelter, standing at the base of an outcrop where the low path bent around a shoulder of rock. He was holding a lantern low against the stone, and the light was shaking—not his hand, Tull realized, but his breathing.

Carved into the rock, weathered to the color of old bone, were two letters and a date:

**V.J. — 1971**

The strokes were deep, cut by someone with strong hands and no training in lettering, re-cut at least once where lichen had tried to eat them. Below the initials, smaller, nearly erased: a line of symbols Tull couldn't read, but which matched, exactly, the notation system from the journals in the missing team's camp. The ones weighted shut. The last page naming coordinates above the source.

"He was here," Vesper said. His voice came out sanded flat. "Nineteen seventy-one. I was—" He stopped. Did arithmetic Tull could see him refusing to finish. "I was four years old. He told my mother he was doing coastal work. Coastal. Three hundred miles from any coast."

Tull leaned against the opposite wall of the path and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't cheaper than silence.

"All those years," Vesper said. "All those years I thought he chose the digging over us. Over me. And he was—" His jaw worked. "He was forty meters from the thing that killed him, carving his name into a rock like a kid, like a man marking a trail home."

"Or marking a warning," Tull said.

Vesper looked at him.

"Men carve two things," Tull said. "What they found, and what they want found after them. Sometimes it's the same thing. Usually it isn't." He nodded at the smaller line of symbols below the initials. "That's not sentiment. That's a key. Your father wasn't leaving his name. He was leaving instructions."

The wind moved through the valley, carrying woodsmoke and water-sound and something underneath both—a low vibration, almost subsonic, that Tull felt in his back teeth more than heard. The source, somewhere above them in the dark, doing whatever sources do.

"One night," Tull said quietly. "That's what he gave us. Come morning, these people put us on the water whether we agree or not. So whatever you're going to decide about following those instructions—you decide it before sunrise, and you decide it knowing that if we stay past dawn, we're not trespassing anymore. We're trespassers who were warned. There's a difference, and up here I suspect it's the only difference that matters."

Vesper pressed his palm flat against the carved letters, the way a man touches a grave or a door.

"He stayed longer than one night," Vesper repeated. The elder's words, turned over like a stone in the hand. "And the mountain kept him."

Below them, lamplight moved in the shelter house window. Maren's silhouette crossed it, steady, unhurried, a woman securing a boat she expected to need again. Above them the ridge stood black against a sky already bruising with cloud—too early in the season for monsoon, everyone had said, but the clouds hadn't read anyone's calendar.

Tull had spent his life around men who lied with maps. He knew what blank spaces meant. They meant someone, somewhere, had decided the truth cost too much to print.

Tomorrow at dawn, the elder would send them downstream.

Tull watched Vesper's hand on the stone and understood, with the calm certainty of a man watching a fuse burn, that none of them were going anywhere.
