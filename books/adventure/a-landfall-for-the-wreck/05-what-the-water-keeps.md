## 5. What the Water Keeps

Ivo found the pool by accident, which was how he found most things worth finding.

The others had gone ahead along the ridge line—Vesper and Tull breaking trail through the bamboo, Maren holding back at the boats with the gear—and Ivo had slipped off the path the way he always did, following a thread of cool air that came down out of the hills like something breathing. Air meant water. Water meant game trails, game trails meant food, and food meant he'd eat tonight without having to pretend he wasn't hungry. That was the arithmetic of his whole life, learned young and learned hard: everything could be counted if you knew what to count.

But this air was wrong. It smelled of stone and deep cold, not of mud and fish. So he followed it anyway.

The trail dropped through a stand of fig trees so old their roots had become architecture, buttressed walls of wood taller than Tull, and then the forest simply stopped, as if it had been told to. Beyond the last tree lay a bowl of grass, and in the center of the bowl lay the water.

It was the clearest water Ivo had ever seen. Not river-clear, not rain-barrel clear—clear the way glass is clear, clear the way a held breath is clear. He walked to the edge and looked down and forgot, for a long moment, that he was supposed to be counting anything at all.

The bottom of the pool was a village.

He saw the roof beams first—great dark rafters lying in ordered collapse, black with age but still squared by human hands, still joined at angles that meant someone had raised them against the sky once. Then the walls: courses of dressed stone furred with pale silt, doorways standing open to nothing, a street running down the middle of the drowned town like a spine. And near the center, half-buried in the silt of what must have been its square, hung a church bell on its fallen frame, green with bronze rot, its mouth open toward the surface as if it had been ringing when the water took it and had never been allowed to stop.

Ivo crouched at the edge and did not move for a long time. His grandmother had taught him the old stories—the valley that went under the water, the people who were moved out in a single season, the government men with their papers and their promises—but she had told them the way you tell children stories, soft and far away, set in a time before time. This was not far away. This was here. The bell was *here*, close enough that if he lay flat and reached, he might have touched the tip of its yoke.

He lay flat on the stones and reached anyway. He didn't touch it. But looking down past his own wavering reflection, he saw something else, and the wonder in his chest turned over like a fish going belly-up.

Rope.

A coil of modern rope lay on the ancient street below, white nylon gone gray-green with algae, weighted down with a stone. It lay among roof beams older than his grandmother's grandmother like a lie told in church. And beside it, half-hidden in the silt, the glint of a carabiner. Steel. New steel doesn't grow green; it grows dull, and this was only slightly dulled. Weeks, not years. Maybe days.

Someone had been diving here. Recently. With good equipment.

Ivo sat back on his heels and made himself think the way Vesper had taught him to think—not the way trackers think, which is fast and sideways, but the way diggers think, which is slow and downward. *What do I know? What can I prove?*

He knew the missing survey team had come up this river. He knew they had vanished without tracks, which had bothered him from the first day, because everything leaves tracks—everything except things that go into water. He knew the government wanted the source of the river kept quiet, and now he was looking at a drowned village the government's own maps said had never existed. And he knew that nobody dives into a pool in forbidden country, alone, with nylon rope and steel hardware, unless they are looking for something or hiding something.

Or both.

* * *

He marked the place the way his mother had taught him—a broken branch angled against the fig root, pointing downstream—and went after the others at a run, low through the bamboo, feet finding the quiet places. He found them at the ridge's shoulder where the land opened onto a view of the upper gorge, and he heard them before he saw them: Vesper's voice, low and tight, and Tull's answering rumble, and under both the sound of wind moving up the valley like a long inhale.

They were arguing about whether to go on. He could tell because Vesper had his father's map out, the one with the initials carved in stone two valleys back, and Tull had his arms crossed, which was Tull's way of saying no without spending the words.

"There's a village," Ivo said.

They both turned. "A living one?" Tull asked.

"A dead one." Ivo crouched and drew it in the dirt with a stick: the bowl, the pool, the street below the water, the bell. His hand was steadier than his voice. "Underwater. Whole streets. A church bell. And rope." He looked up at Vesper. "New rope. And a carabiner. Someone's been diving there."

The silence that followed had weight. Wind filled it, and somewhere downslope a bird called three notes and got no answer.

"Show me," Vesper said.

* * *

They went down to the pool together in the last hour of light, and Ivo watched Vesper kneel at the edge exactly where he had knelt, and watched the man's face do something complicated—grief and recognition fighting each other behind his eyes like animals in a snare.

"My father knew," Vesper said quietly. "That's why the coordinates were in the journal. Not the source. *This.*"

"Government flooded it," Tull said. "Moved everyone out, put the water over it, wrote it out of the record. Happened before, other valleys." He said it flatly, the way he said most true things. "Question isn't the village. Question's the rope."

"I'll go down," Ivo said.

"No," said Vesper and Tull, together.

"It's shallow enough. I've dived deeper in the flood season for nets." He kept his voice even, because begging made adults say no faster. "You're too big to move quiet down there, and Maren shouldn't get wet with her hands the way they are. It has to be me."

Nobody liked it. He could see that nobody liked it, and he understood suddenly that this was what it felt like to be someone whose absence would matter—that these two hard, damaged men were afraid for him, and that the fear was a kind of inheritance too. It made him braver, and it made him more careful, and he offered them both things at once.

"Rope around my chest. You hold the other end. Two pulls means I'm coming up, three means trouble."

Tull rigged it himself, checking the knots twice, which for Tull was practically an embrace.

* * *

The water was colder than it looked. Cold poured down through him like a second skin, and then the noise of the world above—the wind, the voices, his own heartbeat in his ears—fell away, and there was only the green-gold light and the silence of the drowned street beneath him.

He swam down the middle of the road. It was different from below than from above; from above it had been a curiosity, a picture, but from inside it was a *place*, and the place had been left in a hurry. Doors stood open. A cooking pot lay on its side in a doorway, silted to the rim. A child's sandal rested on a windowsill, placed there deliberately, the way you place a shoe you mean to come back for. Ivo's chest tightened around the cold. These people had been told they'd return. He understood being promised things by people with paper. He understood what it was to be moved.

At the bell he stopped, treading water above the green bronze, and pulled the rope free of its stone. Nylon. Good quality, government-issue weave—he'd seen coils of it in the abandoned camp, in the survey team's stores. His blood went slow and loud.

Then he saw the rest.

Beyond the bell, where the street bent toward what had been the church, the silt had been disturbed—recently, deliberately, in a pattern no current makes. A trench dug by hand. And at the end of the trench, wedged beneath a fallen lintel, a waterproof case, olive-drab, lashed shut with the same nylon. Government stenciling on its side, half effaced by algae but legible to anyone who'd spent a week handling the missing team's crates: a survey designation. A number.

And beside the case, caught in the silt like a signature, a boot.

Not old. Not rotted. A modern survey boot, sole up, laces still tied.

Ivo did not touch the boot. Three pulls, he thought, three pulls means trouble, and his hand was already making them before his mind finished the sentence, hauling him up hand over hand through the cold while the drowned village watched him go with all its open doors.

* * *

He broke the surface gasping, and Vesper and Tull hauled him out onto the stones, and he lay there shaking—not from the cold, or not only from the cold—while the light failed in the trees and the pool went from gold to iron.

"A case," he said. "Waterproof. Survey markings. And a boot. Laces tied." He made himself look at Vesper. "Laces tied, Vesper. Nobody takes a boot off underwater. Somebody put that boot there. Somebody *arranged* that case under the stone, same way the camp was arranged. Boots paired. Journals weighted." His voice cracked and he hated it and pushed on. "The team didn't vanish. They were *staged*. All of it—the camp, this—it's a display. Someone wants us to find some things and never find others."

Tull was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than Ivo had ever heard it. "Then whoever staged it knows someone's coming. Knows *we're* coming."

"And they wanted us to look here," Vesper said slowly, staring out across the darkening water, at the place where the bell hung silent under the surface, keeping whatever else it kept. "Which means everything we've found so far—the camp, the journal page, this—" He stopped. Ivo watched him arrive at the end of the thought and saw it land like a blow.

"Everything we've found," Vesper said, "was left for us to find."

Above the pool, high on the black shoulder of the ridge, a light blinked once—twice—and went out.
