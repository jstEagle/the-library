## 6. The Father's Vault

The source of the river was not a spring. It was a wound.

Vesper had expected—what? A seep in the rock, a moss-dark cleft, water rising shy as breath. Instead the mountain simply opened, a mouth of black stone thirty feet across, and the river poured out of it whole, already old, already heavy with the silt of somewhere no map admitted to. The sound of it filled the gorge like a congregation. He stood at its edge and felt the spray settle on his face, cold and mineral, and understood that everything downstream—the drowned village, the vanished team, his father's long silence—all of it had come out of this dark.

Ivo found him there. The boy came down the goat track without a word and stood beside him, watching the water boil up from the earth.

"It doesn't end," Ivo said finally. "I went in as far as the light goes. There's a passage. Dry stone for maybe forty meters, then it turns and the water comes from deeper than that."

"Deeper than the mountain?"

"Deeper than anything." Ivo wiped his nose. "Tull says don't go in."

"Tull says a lot of things."

"He says them because he means them." But the boy was already looking up, past Vesper's shoulder, at the cliff wall where the river emerged—and Vesper followed his gaze and saw what the boy had seen.

A seam. Barely visible behind the falling mist where the outflow struck a shelf of rock and shattered into permanent rain—a vertical line in the stone too straight to be geology. And below it, half-erased by a century of spray, a mark chiseled by hand: two letters, crossed by a date.

*E.J. — 1961.*

His father had been twenty-three years old.

---

They climbed to it in the last hour of light, Maren anchoring the rope from above because her hands were the steadiest, Tull below calling holds that the water noise kept swallowing. The seam was a door. Not a clever one—it had been meant to be found eventually, or perhaps only meant to be found by someone who already knew to look—but it swung on hinges his father had oiled within living memory, and the air that came out of it was dry and still and smelled of lamp oil and paper and time.

Vesper went in first. He did not ask himself why. Some inheritances you walk into the way you walk into a burning house: because it is yours, and because no one else will.

He lit the lamp they'd brought for exactly this and held it up.

The chamber was not large. Twenty paces by ten, cut into the living rock, shored with timber beams gone silver-grey with age. And it was full—not of treasure, not of gold, but of *paper*. Crates of it. Ledgers bound in oilcloth. Survey maps rolled and sleeved in waxed canvas. Photographs sleeved against damp, hundreds of them, each one numbered in his father's small, upright hand. Along the far wall, wrapped in tarpaulin and laid out like patients in a ward, were the artifacts themselves: roof beams charred at one end, a church bell green with age but stamped with a foundry mark and a date, pottery, tools, a child's shoe. Things pulled from mud. Things pulled from water.

Things pulled from the valley before the dam.

"Oh," Maren said softly, behind him. Just that. One syllable carrying the whole weight of the room.

Vesper set the lamp on a crate and opened the nearest ledger with hands that did not feel entirely like his own.

His father's writing. He knew it the way you know a voice through a wall—had seen it on school permission slips, on grocery lists, on the single postcard sent from a dig site when Vesper was nine. *Thinking of you. The light here is good.* That was all the man had ever been able to say. But here the same hand ran on for pages, dense and urgent, and it said everything.

*March 4th. Cross-referenced the ministry survey against my own elevations. The reservoir projection is falsified. They know the village stands. They have known since '58. The final report lists the site as "seasonal habitation, no permanent structures, no historical significance." I have walked those streets. I have counted the graves in the churchyard. There are ninety-one of them.*

*April 19th. Met with Deputy Minister Corrane today. He was very kind. He offered me a position. He said history is a long game and some chapters are better left unprinted. I told him the dead do not sign releases. He smiled as though I had confirmed something about myself he had already written down.*

*June 2nd. They have moved up the flooding schedule. Corrane says it is for engineering reasons. The engineering reasons are that every week the valley remains dry is a week someone might look.*

Vesper read standing, then kneeling, then sitting on a crate because his legs had opinions of their own. Behind him the others moved quietly, unpacking lanterns, cataloguing without being asked—Ivo photographing page after page with the battered camera from their kit, Maren reading dates aloud in her ferry-woman's flat, unhurried voice, as if she were announcing crossings.

It was Tull who found the last ledger. Or rather, Tull found the tin box beneath the last ledger, army-green, dented, locked with a small brass padlock whose key hung from a nail beside it, exactly where a man would leave it for a son he hoped would come.

Inside: a pistol, wrapped in cloth and still greased. A bundle of letters, unopened, addressed to *V. Joubert* in care of a coastal postal office Vesper had lived three streets from for four years. And a single sheet of paper, folded once, dated eleven days before his father's death.

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead or I am gone, and either way the room has done its work. Everything here proves what they did—the false surveys, the moved schedule, the ninety-one graves they put under sixty feet of water and called progress. Corrane knows I have copies. That is why the accident report will say what it says. Do not trust the government's grief, boy. Grief is the cheapest thing they manufacture.*

*I am sorry for the miles between us. I told myself I was keeping you away from this to keep you safe, and that was true, and it was also a coward's arithmetic. You deserved a father, not a fortress. If you have come this far upstream, then you are the better digger after all, and I am glad of it, and I am sorry for it too, because this knowledge is a heavy inheritance and I would have spared you the weight if sparing you hadn't cost me everything else.*

*Seal the room when you're done. The waterfall keeps it better than I ever could.*

Vesper read it twice. Then he sat down on the cold stone floor of his father's life's work and wept—not gracefully, not briefly, but the way a man weeps when twelve years of anger discover, all at once, that they have been aimed at a dead man who was trying to hand him the truth the whole time.

No one spoke. Maren put a blanket over his shoulders and went back to the ledgers. Ivo kept photographing, the shutter clicking soft and steady as a heartbeat. Tull stood at the entrance with his back to the room, watching the dark beyond the falls, doing the thing soldiers do instead of comforting people: guarding them.

When it was done—when the grief had burned down to something harder and more useful—Vesper stood, wiped his face, and felt the transformation complete itself in his chest like a key turning. This was not sadness anymore. Sadness was a luxury the schedule no longer permitted. What remained was fury, clean and cold as the water outside, and a purpose to aim it at.

"They killed him," he said. His voice sounded strange in the sealed room, flattened by stone. "Corrane signed the order and called it an accident, and they've spent twelve years letting me believe my father died alone in a ravine because he was careless." He looked around at them—at the ex-soldier, the ferry-woman, the boy who should have been in school. "They flooded ninety-one graves and forged the paperwork, and when one man refused to bury the evidence with the village, they buried him instead."

"And now they know we're here," Tull said quietly. He didn't turn around. "The survivor talked. Someone's been talking longer than that. Which means Corrane's people aren't wondering where we've gone. They know."

Maren looked up from the ledger in her lap. "Then we copy fast and move faster."

"We don't move," Vesper said. He heard his own voice and barely recognized it—steady, certain, his father's cadence coming up through him like water through stone. "We document. Every page, every artifact, every date. Three copies, separated. Because a vault in a mountain can be sealed, and a witness can be silenced, but paper in three directions—" he tapped the ledger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping thing— "paper is the one thing they never learned to drown."

Outside, the light behind the waterfall was going copper, and far off—so far it might have been thunder, except the sky was clear—came a sound none of them wanted to name.

Rotor blades, riding the wind up the gorge.

* * *

They worked through the night by lamplight while the mountain listened.
