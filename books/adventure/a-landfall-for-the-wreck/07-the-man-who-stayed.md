## 7. The Man Who Stayed

I have carried the dying before. Tull, when the fever took him on the Kess run, burning three days in my cabin while I spooned water between his cracked lips and prayed to a river god I don't believe in. Ivo's mother, at the end, when she weighed less than her own blankets. I know the weight of a body that has decided to stop.

The man we pulled from the shallows below the falls was not that kind of weight. He was heavy with living, the awful heavy of a man who wants to die and whose body refuses him the courtesy. We found him snagged against a deadfall, half in the current, his hands still gripping a leather map case like it was an infant he'd been ordered not to drop. Even unconscious, even blue-lipped and shivering through whatever fever had already begun its work, he would not let go of it. Tull had to pry one finger at a time.

"Cartographer," Vesper said, reading the stencil inside the case's lid. *R. Osei, Gov't Survey Division.* His voice did something strange when he said it—went flat as a sounding line dropped into deep water. "He was on the team."

"He was on the bottom of the river," Tull said.

"And now he's on my deck." I looked at them both, at Ivo crouched at the rail with his eyes going from the man to the treeline and back, reading the forest the way the rest of us read weather. "And now he's our problem."

Nobody argued. That should have told me something, right there. A crew that doesn't argue is a crew keeping secrets, and I'd spent enough years ferrying strangers across dark water to know what secrets do below decks. They ballast you wrong. You list without knowing why.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the bluff, upriver of the falls but downwind of the chamber, because Vesper had become particular about who breathed near that place. We rigged the survivor under a tarp with a fire close enough to warm him without cooking him, and for six hours he did nothing but shake and mutter coordinates in his sleep. Numbers. Always numbers. Elevations, bearings, grid references—the litany of a man whose whole life had been reduced to points on paper.

It was Ivo who noticed the boots first.

"His boots are dry," the boy said, crouched at the edge of the tarp. "River took everything else. Watch, coat, pack—all gone or ruined. But his boots are dry inside."

Tull grunted. "Maybe he kept them above water."

"Maybe," Ivo said, in the tone of a boy who does not believe a thing and wants you to know he checked anyway. "Or maybe he walked here after the others went under. Maybe he walked a long way."

I thought about that while the fire popped and the mist off the falls drifted through camp like something searching. A man who survives a drowning that killed seven others. A man who walks out of forbidden country alone, carrying nothing but a map case he won't release even unconscious. A man whose boots are dry.

Either the river loved him, or someone let him go.

* * *

He woke at dusk on the second day, all at once, the way soldiers wake—no drifting, no confusion, just eyes open and fixed on me across the fire. Grey eyes, river-pale. He looked at me for a long moment and then said, in a voice sanded down to gravel:

"You're not military."

"No," I said. "Ferry."

"The Joubert boat." Something moved behind his eyes—not fear exactly. Recognition, and what recognition costs. "You're Maren. The old woman who runs the lower crossing."

"I'm the woman who runs the lower crossing," I agreed. Old I let stand; arguing with a fevered man about adjectives is a fool's economy. "And you're the reason a government telegram went looking for diggers. Seven men walked upstream and didn't walk back. You're the eighth."

"There were nine of us." He said it flatly, correcting the record the way cartographers correct coastlines—one small mark that redraws everything. Then he coughed, long and wet, and when he could speak again he said: "Bring Joubert. What I have to say, he needs to hear. And keep the boy away from the treeline. Please. Just—for tonight, keep everyone away from the treeline."

* * *

Vesper sat across the fire from Osei with his father's journal open on his knees, though he never once looked down at it. I've watched Vesper Joubert hold things the way other men hold weapons, and that journal is the worst of them—he carries it like a debt he intends to collect.

Osei drank the broth I gave him in small, disciplined swallows, a man rationing himself even now. When he finally spoke, his voice had steadied into something worse than the gravel: precision. A report. He'd clearly rehearsed this, maybe to us, maybe to himself, in whatever dark hours he'd walked out of the valley.

"We found the chamber on the eleventh day. Behind the falls, same as your father left it. Halvard—our expedition lead—radioed it in. Confirmation of site, request for instructions." Osei stared into the fire. "You have to understand what Halvard was. Twenty-two years in the Survey. Best field man I ever worked under. When the reply came, I read it over his shoulder. It was two words."

He stopped. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the bluff, water fell forever.

"*Seal it,*" Vesper said quietly.

"*Erase it.*" Osei shook his head. "Same thing, different verbs. And then a third instruction, separate message, coded channel—the kind that isn't supposed to exist on a civilian survey. It named names. It said anyone exposed to the contents was a liability to be managed. Halvard read it twice, folded it into his pocket, and smiled at me. That smile. I've been walking away from that smile for nineteen days."

"What were the contents?" Vesper asked. Not gently. I understood why. That chamber held his father's last years, his father's death, every answer the man had died without passing on—and here was a stranger rationing them out like medicine.

"The valley wasn't flooded by accident," Osei said. "That's the start of it. The dam project was approved with surveys that showed no significant settlement upstream. Your father proved otherwise. Proved there was a town down there—whole streets, a church, records, graves. Proved the government knew before they flooded it, and falsified the record afterward, and drowned the proof along with the people." He looked up, and his grey eyes were terribly clear. "Your father didn't die protecting artifacts, Joubert. He died holding receipts."

Vesper didn't move. I've seen men take bullets with less stillness. But his hands, on the journal, went white at the knuckles, and I thought: *there it is. Grief turning over, the way a log turns in a current, showing you its underside.*

"Halvard followed the erase order?" I asked, because someone had to keep the current moving.

"For four days he tried. Set charges at the falls entrance. The rock was older and harder than their models—did more damage to his schedule than to the door. So he sent a coded sitrep requesting extraction and heavy equipment." Osei's voice thinned. "The reply authorized extraction for *one*. One set of hands to carry the documentation of erasure downstream. The rest of us were reclassified."

"Reclassified," Tull repeated, from the shadows where he'd been sitting sharpening a knife that didn't need it.

"As liabilities." Osei said it without drama, which was somehow the most terrible thing about him. "There was an accident on the crossing at the narrows. Rope failed. Seven men, fast water. I went in last, on purpose, because I'd seen Halvard check the knots. I'm a strong swimmer. I let the river decide whether I was a liability too." He almost smiled. "The river ruled in my favor. Barely."

"And the map case," Vesper said.

"Everything. Every bearing, every depth reading, the original settlement surveys versus the filed ones, the correspondence copies Halvard thought he'd burned." Osei finally loosened his grip on the case and set it down between himself and Vesper like a man laying down a loaded weapon. "Halvard thinks it's at the bottom of the narrows. Which means somewhere downstream, men are being paid to make sure it stays there. And when they finish with the river, they'll come up it."

* * *

Ivo came back into the firelight just then, silent as he always is, and I saw Osei track him the way a man tracks movement in brush. The boy sat, took bread, ate. Normal. Everything normal.

Except.

Except that three days ago, Ivo had gone scouting the lower trail alone and come back saying it was clear. Except that twice now our campsites had felt—wrong to me, in the way a river feels wrong before it rises, some pressure change you can't name. Except that Vesper had mentioned, laughing, that the boy seemed to know the government's patrol schedules better than the government did.

I am an old woman who runs a ferry. My whole trade is reading currents, and I am telling you that a current changed in that firelight. I watched Ivo watch Osei, and I watched Osei not-watch Ivo back, and neither of them knew I was watching both.

No. That's a lie, and I promised myself I'd stop lying somewhere around the second night of this man's fever. Here is the truth: I had suspected for days. I had suspected and said nothing, because Ivo was sixteen and hungry and had a mother's grave to visit and no money for flowers, and because I did not want it to be true. That is what loyalty does when it goes rotten—it makes you complicit in your own blindness.

So I did the only thing left. While Vesper pored over the map case and Tull stood watch and Osei slept his second sleep, I crossed the camp to where Ivo sat apart, mending a net he never used, and I sat down beside him and said, quietly, so quiet the fire barely heard it:

"The radio burst, boy. Three nights ago. From the ridge. Someone keyed a transmitter up there for eleven seconds, and it wasn't any of us, and it wasn't the dead."

Ivo's hands went still on the netting. He didn't look at me. In the firelight I saw his jaw work once, twice, the way it does before he says something true, which is rare, which is why we keep him.

"They have my brother," he said.

Three words, and the whole camp tilted.

"Who has him?"

"The people Halvard works for. They picked him up in Port Sable weeks ago—before we ever signed on. Said he'd stay healthy as long as their friend on the river stayed useful." Now he looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and sixteen years old, hating himself more than any of us ever could. "Eleven seconds, Maren. Coordinates and a count of heads. That's all I ever gave them. That's all."

I put my hand over his on the net, and we sat there in the smell of woodsmoke and falling water, and I felt the betrayal go through our little crew like ice water through a hull—not loud, not dramatic, just cold, finding every seam.

Because here is what I knew, sitting in the dark with a boy who sold us to save his brother, and here is what I did not yet say aloud: the coordinates he'd transmitted three nights ago were the coordinates of *this* camp. Of the chamber. Of Vesper's father's vault, with its receipts and its proofs and its dead man's initials carved in stone.

They weren't coming up the river anymore.

They were coming by air, and they were already close, and above the bluff the sky had gone the color of a bruise, and the first far-off grumble of the monsoon rolled down the valley like something clearing its throat.

I squeezed Ivo's hand once—forgiveness, or as close to it as I could manage with a storm coming—and went to wake Vesper.

Some truths you deliver gently. This one had a helicopter behind it.
