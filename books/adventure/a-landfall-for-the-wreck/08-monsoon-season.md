## 8. Monsoon Season

The clouds came for the ridge the way debt collectors come for a debtor—slowly at first, all patience and shadow, then all at once, with no intention of negotiating.

Vesper felt them before he saw them. The air in the chamber behind the waterfall had gone strange overnight, thick with a pressure that made his ears ache and his father's artifacts seem to hum against their wrappings. He had spent half the night cataloguing by lamplight, photographing each piece twice, once in situ and once against the grey cloth Ivo had laid out like a shroud. Now he climbed out through the falling water into a sky that had turned the color of old bruises.

Below him, the valley lay green and innocent under a light that no longer belonged to the sun. The river that had led him here—the river his father had followed, the river the government had dammed and drowned a village to tame—ran brown now, swollen with rain that hadn't even started yet. It was only the vanguard. Somewhere over the western ranges, the real monsoon was gathering itself like a fist.

He had perhaps two days. Maybe less.

* * *

He worked because work was the only prayer he knew.

Back inside the chamber, he laid out his father's letters on a flat stone and photographed them page by page, his hands steady in the way hands go steady when there is nothing left to lose. The correspondence told the whole rotten story in his father's careful draftsman's hand: the survey reports from thirty years ago marking the village of San Isidro for inundation; the internal memos acknowledging the archaeological significance of what lay beneath the floodplain; the decision, initialed and stamped, to proceed anyway and reclassify the site as barren rock. His father had been hired to make the past disappear and had refused—and had spent the rest of his life hiding the proof of it up here, behind the waterfall, where the water kept his secret the way it kept everything else.

Vesper copied every document twice. One set went into oilcloth, into the pack Tull would carry downriver. The second set he sealed in the steel case Maren had brought aboard her ferry without ever asking why—a woman who asked fewer questions than any person Vesper had ever met, and answered even fewer.

"You keep looking at the sky," Ivo said from the chamber mouth. The boy stood silhouetted against the waterfall's white curtain, thin as a blade, water beading in his dark hair. "You keep looking at it like it owes you money."

"It does," Vesper said. "Everyone up here owes somebody something."

Ivo came inside and crouched beside the flat stone, studying the photographs of his father's letters with an expression too old for his face. "These people," he said. "The ones who drowned the village. They're coming for this."

"They're already here."

* * *

Tull heard the helicopter before any of them did—not the sound of it but the absence of sound beneath it, the sudden hush in the birdsong along the ridge where the ex-soldier had spent the morning watching the trail. Vesper found him lying flat among the ferns below the treeline, binoculars pressed to his eyes, his breathing slow and deliberate in the way of a man conserving himself for something.

"There." Tull passed the glasses without looking away from the ridgeline himself. "Coming up the valley. Low. They think the storm cover makes them invisible."

Through the lenses Vesper watched the helicopter rise out of the southern haze, a black insect against the bruised sky, riding the leading edge of the monsoon the way carrion birds ride a fire. It banked once, twice, and settled toward the saddle of the ridge three miles downstream—the same ground where their own camp had stood a week ago, where someone had left signs cut into bark and stones stacked in patterns no surveyor would use.

Someone who had walked with them. Eaten with them. Shared their firelight.

"Who?" Vesper asked, though he wasn't sure he wanted the answer.

Tull took the binoculars back. His jaw worked once. "Could be anyone," he said, which was a soldier's lie, kind and useless. Then, because Tull had never in his life let a kindness stand: "It's not me. And it's not the boy. You know that already or you wouldn't have said it out loud where we could hear."

"I don't know anything," Vesper said. "That's the problem. I've spent my whole life knowing distances and nothing else."

Tull grunted and began checking his rifle with the unhurried tenderness other men reserved for children. "Then know this much," he said. "They'll send men up the trail tonight, ahead of the rain, because after the rain starts nobody moves in this country and they know it. Four, maybe six. Professionals. Which means I hold the narrows until I can't, and you finish your work, and when I come off that hill—" he snapped the magazine home with a click that sounded final as a headstone—"we are on that boat and gone before the water takes the trail. Understood?"

"And if you don't come off the hill?"

Tull looked at him then, and something in the old soldier's face was almost gentle. "Then you'll know exactly how long I held," he said. "Now get back to your digging, Joubert. You're the better digger. Act like it."

* * *

Maren was readying the boat.

She had run the ferry long enough to read weather in her bones, and she had spent the afternoon doing the things ferry-women do before a flood: double-lashing the cargo, sealing the engine housing, walking the hull with her palms flat against the planks as if taking its pulse. When Vesper came down to the pool below the falls, she looked up from coiling rope and read his face in one glance.

"They're here," she said. Not a question.

"One helicopter so far. Men on foot by nightfall."

"Mm." She tested a knot, found it wanting, retied it. "And you still don't know which one of us is theirs."

"No."

"Good," Maren said, and Vesper stared at her.

"Good?"

"Because it means you haven't decided yet." She rose, wiping her hands on her thighs, and looked at him with those flat grey eyes that had seen more rivers than most people see years. "A man who knows who betrayed him has already chosen what to do about it. A man who doesn't know still has to choose something bigger. So choose, Vesper Joubert. Tonight, before the rain. Because the rain won't wait for you, and neither will they, and I will only ask you once: what is this boat carrying downstream? Evidence? Or us?"

Vesper looked at the steel case lashed amidships, at the oilcloth bundle beneath the thwart, at the drowned village visible in fragments through the clear brown water—roof beams, the tip of the church bell, the bones of a town that official history insisted had never existed. His father had died alone protecting this truth. That was the inheritance Vesper had spent his whole life refusing: not the dig, not the coordinates, but the loneliness of being the only one who knew.

Except he wasn't the only one anymore. That was the thing his father had never had. There was a boy who dove into flooded villages out of pure wonder. An old soldier holding a pass in the rain. A ferry-woman who asked one question and waited for the answer like it was a contract.

"The evidence goes downstream," Vesper said. "All of it. Copies in three directions, in case any one boat gets stopped. And we go with it."

Maren nodded slowly, once, the way she acknowledged a current she'd already charted. "Then we leave at first rain," she said. "Not before. Rain covers the engine noise. And Vesper—" she caught his sleeve as he turned, her grip brief and hard as a mooring line—"whatever you saw in that man's face when you learned one of us was theirs, put it away. You'll need both hands for the storm."

* * *

The first drops fell at dusk.

Fat, warm, sporadic—the monsoon clearing its throat. They struck the pool around Ivo like small detonations as the boy surfaced from his last dive, hauling up the final item: a waterproof document tube from the drowned camp of the missing survey team, recovered from beneath a roof beam where the survivor had hidden it before his companions were taken. Inside were the team's own photographs of the chamber, their own copies of the falsified reports, and a list of names—the officials who had ordered the expedition silenced—written in a cartographer's shaking hand.

Vesper spread the papers under the lamplight in the chamber and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that the choice had never really been his to defer. It had been made thirty years ago by a man who chose truth over safety and paid for it in solitude. All Vesper could decide was whether to pay the same price the same way—or differently.

Differently, then. With company.

Outside, the rain thickened from punctuation into prose, from prose into a roar. Downstream, three miles through the drowning dark, he imagined the helicopter's handlers watching their instruments, waiting for the storm to bury the valley the way it buried everything, assuming the water would do their work for them a second time.

Let it come, Vesper thought, pressing the last photograph into its oilcloth sleeve. We were here. It happened. The water keeps what it keeps—but it gives up its dead to anyone stubborn enough to dive.

Thunder rolled down the valley like a door slamming. And through the roar of the falls, Vesper heard the thing he had been dreading and waiting for all day: a shot, single and deliberate, from the direction of the narrows where Tull held the trail.

The monsoon had made landfall. So had the men who wanted them dead.
