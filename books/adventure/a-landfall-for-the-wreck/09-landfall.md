## 9. Landfall

**Vesper**

The first drop hit the waterfall like a gunshot.

Vesper felt it through the stone—a percussion in his boots, in his teeth, in the sealed chamber behind him where his father's life lay arranged in trays and oilcloth. Then another drop. Then a hundred, then the sky simply gave way, and the world beyond the falls turned to gray rope and roaring white.

He had perhaps an hour. Maybe less.

The chamber breathed cold air against his face as he worked by lamplight, hands steady in the way they only ever were when the stakes stopped being abstract. Survey photographs. The falsified geological report, stamped with a ministry seal twenty-three years old. His father's field journal, its final pages written in a hand gone shaky, describing men in government trucks arriving at night to tell him the valley was already gone on paper, so it might as well be gone in fact—and that if Aldous Joubert knew what was good for his family, he'd agree.

*He didn't agree,* Vesper thought, turning a page. *And it cost us everything. And he was right anyway.*

He photographed everything twice. He copied the ledger of names—the officials who had signed off on the flooding, the compensation that never reached the drowned village, the order designating the site for "administrative erasure." Into the waterproof case went negatives, journals, the seal-stamped originals. Three copies. One would go downriver with Maren. One was already cached in the elder's village, wrapped in wax cloth beneath the meeting-house floor where no government man would think to look. The third rode against Vesper's own ribs, tied there with cord, warm as a second heartbeat.

The water found him before he expected it. A thin tongue of it slid across the chamber floor, cold around his ankles, and he understood with sudden clarity that the mountain itself had voted. The monsoon wasn't coming to the source. It was coming *through* it—the whole watershed above the valley unloading itself into rock that had been waiting thirty years to give back what it held.

His father had built the sluice gate for this. Vesper had found it two days ago, iron-sick and crusted, jammed with silt, and had spent a day and a half clearing it without letting himself name what he was doing. Now he named it.

He stood at the gate wheel with the lamp set on a dry ledge and looked back once at the chamber: the pottery, the stelae, the evidence of a town that had never officially existed, preserved in the dark by a dead man's stubbornness.

"I'm sorry," he said. "It's the only way to keep you."

Then he threw his weight against the wheel.

Iron groaned. Something below shifted—a sound like a throat clearing after decades—and the water came in honest now, ankle-deep, knee-deep, climbing the legs of the worktable where his father had sat. Vesper retreated up the passage, watching black water take the room the way it had taken the village below: patiently, totally, without malice. By the time he reached daylight the chamber was drowning, and the valley's secret was going home to the river that had first tried to keep it.

Above the falls, the sky was a bruise. Somewhere south, a helicopter would be fighting this weather and losing. Somewhere north, Tull was alone on the pass.

Vesper ran.

* * *

**Maren**

The river had become a living thing with opinions.

Maren had run the ferry through spring floods, through logjams, through the year the bridge ice took old Piet's barge with him aboard. She had never seen water like this. It came down the gorge not as current but as freight—whole trees cartwheeling end over end, brown hills of foam, a smell of torn earth so thick she could taste the mountain's insides.

"Tell me you've got it rigged," she shouted to Ivo, who crouched at the stern lashing the signal buoy to the strongest line they owned.

"It'll hold," Ivo shouted back, "if the river lets it."

Below them, at the last navigable bend before the gorges went vertical, the relay station waited: a battered transmitter salvaged from the survey team's own camp, wired by Ivo's clever hands to a battery bank and an antenna strung up a lightning-scarred pine. Downstream, forty miles on, sat the provincial capital, the newspapers, the inquiry office that a certain ferry-woman had spent three nights convincing would listen. Copies of everything. Photographs, documents, sworn statements dictated in Maren's flat river-accent while Vesper's voice crackled over the wire from upstream.

The plan was simple and terrible: when the flood crested, the buoy would carry a duplicate cache downriver on the current, past every checkpoint, past every hand that wanted it buried, into the one place too public to silence. The river that had been paid to forget would be made to remember.

Maren checked the lashings herself, then checked them again, because she had buried one husband to this river's moods and did not intend to bury anything else today. Rain hammered her hood. Lightning walked the ridgelines like something searching.

She thought about Tull, up on the pass, and her jaw tightened. She had known. God help her, some part of her had known since the second week—the way he read the horizon, the little notebook he thought nobody saw, the nights he walked the perimeter when walking it served no purpose. She had said nothing because she had her own reasons for running, and a woman who runs doesn't ask questions about other people's running.

But he had stood between them and the soldiers' advance party two days ago with his body doing the talking his mouth wouldn't. Whatever game he'd been playing, he'd changed sides somewhere on that trail. Or he was playing a deeper game still. Either way, he was up there alone, and the weather was coming down like a verdict.

"Hold together," she told the buoy, the line, the river, the boy beside her. "All of you. Hold."

The flood crest came at dusk—a wall of sound moving up the gorge, and then the water itself, rising faster than a man could climb. Maren cut the buoy loose. It leapt from her hands into the torrent like something glad to go, and the river took it south, carrying the valley's testimony toward the light.

* * *

**Ivo**

Ivo had never been afraid of storms. Storms were just weather having feelings, his mother used to say, before the fever took her and left him alone with a tracker's eyes and nothing worth tracking. But this storm had a shape, and the shape was wrong. It moved against the wind sometimes. It paused. It listened.

He felt it listening now, hunched under the pine beside the transmitter, feeding the battery bank the last of their charge while Maren watched the river. Through the static, the capital's operator was confirming receipt—*documents received, statement logged, hold your position, help is—* and then the frequency filled with a new voice, clipped and official, ordering the channel cleared, claiming interference, claiming authority.

"They're jamming," Ivo said. "They're already jamming."

"Too late," Maren said, and there was iron under the rain in her voice. "It's received. Logged. They can shout at the sky all they want."

But Ivo wasn't listening anymore. He was looking up-trail, at the black seam of the pass where the ridge notched against the sky, and what he saw made his stomach drop through the wet ground.

Lights. Descending. Not the erratic scatter of a helicopter in distress—steady, spaced, disciplined. Men on foot, coming down the soldiers' route, and ahead of them a single point of light swinging side to side in a pattern Ivo recognized because he'd seen Tull do it a hundred times on night marches.

Signal code. *Come ahead. Path clear.*

"No," Ivo whispered. Then louder, standing, rain streaming off his chin: "No, no—he wouldn't—"

"He would," Maren said quietly. "If they gave him reason. If they threatened something he loves more than himself." She was already moving, hauling the transmitter case onto her shoulder. "Question is whether he still does. Get the spare batteries. We're going up."

"The pass is a hour's climb in this—"

"Then we'd best not waste it arguing." She fixed him with the look she used on drunks and debtors. "You're the only one of us who can find footing in the dark, boy. So find it."

Ivo found it. He climbed with the storm screaming in his ears and the lights bobbing above him, and he did not let himself think about what they would see at the top, because thinking was a luxury the weather no longer allowed.

* * *

**Tull**

Tull had held worse ground. That was the lie he told himself, standing alone on the saddle of the pass with the monsoon trying to peel the mountain open, and it almost worked.

The advance party had come at noon, four men out of uniform carrying government rifles, and their captain had laid it out with the courtesy of a man discussing freight rates. The chamber was to be destroyed. The evidence surrendered. The ferry-woman, the boy, and Joubert were to be detained as trespassers—no charges necessary, no paperwork, the ministry would handle everything. And Sergeant Tull, late of the border regiment, dishonorably discharged for a massacre he'd refused to order, could have his name restored. His pension. His country's forgiveness. All he had to do was keep standing where he was standing, and let the men coming behind him pass.

They'd given him until landfall to decide.

Now the landfall had come, and the decision was made, and it had been made hours ago in truth—maybe years ago, in a border prison cell, when he'd learned exactly how much a government's forgiveness weighed. Nothing. It weighed nothing. He'd spent six years running from one set of orders, and these men had offered him a fresh set with a ribbon on it.

The captain's lights were descending through the rain behind him, following the path he'd signaled clear—because he *had* signaled it clear, because a good soldier finishes the deception even when he's the only one who knows it's a deception. Let them come down confident. Confident men walked badly.

Ahead of Tull, at the narrowest point of the trail, a shelf of granite hung over a drop into the flooded gorge. One man with a crowbar and knowledge of the rock could bring it down across the path. It would trap the party on the wrong side of the slide—not forever, but long enough. Long enough for the river to carry its cargo south. Long enough for a chamber to drown and a truth to surface somewhere no one could push it back under.

Long enough, probably, to kill him. The same rock that blocked the trail would block his retreat, and below him the gorge was a brown riot, and the cold was already in his bones the way it had been in the prison, in the war, in every winter since.

He heard them coming. Boots on wet stone, unhurried, sure of their ground.

Tull set the crowbar against the fault line he'd chosen and thought about Maren's face when she'd caught him with the notebook and said nothing—just looked at him, and kept looking, until he'd had to walk away. She'd given him a chance to be better without ever saying a word about it. The boy Ivo shared his rations like it was nothing. Joubert carried his dead father like a lantern and never once asked Tull what he was carrying.

"You can still change your mind," the captain called through the rain, close now, twenty meters, fifteen. "Your name, Sergeant. Your country. Everything back."

Tull gripped the crowbar and smiled at the dark.

"I had a country," he said. "These people are better."

He pulled.

* * *

**Vesper**

Vesper met them at the tree line, Maren and Ivo soaked through and wild-eyed, and above them the pass spoke with a sound like the end of the world—rock and thunder indistinguishable, a white scar of falling stone crossing the trail, and then silence except for the rain.

"TULL!" Ivo was already scrambling upward. Maren caught his collar and held on, both of them staring at the slope where the path had been, where the lights had been, where nothing moved now but water finding its thousand ways downhill.

Vesper stood in the deluge with the waterproof case against his ribs and his father's valley drowning behind him, and he felt it leave him—not the grief, which would keep—but the weight of it, the solitary weight, the miles-not-years weight he had carried since he was old enough to carry anything. The evidence was southbound. The chamber was underwater. The pass was closed. Whatever happened next would happen to all of them or none of them.

The rain began, very slowly, to soften.

And out of the settling mist below the slide, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that made Ivo cry out and start running again before anyone could stop him:

Someone down there, weak and furious and alive, whistling the ferry-woman's docking call.
