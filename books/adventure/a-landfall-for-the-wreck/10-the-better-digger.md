## 10. The Better Digger

The inquiry took eleven weeks, and Vesper measured every one of them in the wrong currency.

He sat in a government hearing room in the capital that smelled of floor wax and old paper, his hands folded on a table scarred by decades of other men's nervous fingers, while men in good coats read aloud from documents he had carried out of the valley in oilcloth. The chamber's records lay between them like a body on a slab: survey ledgers with two sets of figures, one for the public archive and one true; correspondence about the reservoir project dated years before any "natural" flooding was announced; photographs of the drowned village taken *before* it drowned, its church bell still hanging, its gardens still green.

A tall man with a barrister's voice asked him to state, for the record, how he had come into possession of these materials.

"I dug them up," Vesper said.

"And your father? Aldous Joubert?"

"Dead eleven years. He died keeping them safe."

The room did something he hadn't expected. It went quiet—not the hostile quiet of men protecting themselves, but something older and more uncomfortable. Grief, maybe, or shame. The tall man looked down at the ledger in front of him for a long moment before speaking again.

"The valley," he said, "was not lost to natural causes."

"No."

"And the village of Sainte-Anne-des-Hauts—"

"Was drowned on purpose. With people who wouldn't leave still in it, if my father's notes are right." He kept his voice level. It cost him. "Two hundred and six names. I can give you the list."

They took the list. They took everything—the copies Maren had broadcast downstream from the ferry's radio during the landfall, the originals Tull had held at gunpoint through the worst night of all their lives, the testimony of a broken cartographer named Emeric Vale who wept twice on the stand and once in the corridor where no one official could see him. They took the traitor too, though that word had stopped fitting Halvard Somers somewhere around the moment he'd chosen to hold the pass against the helicopter men with nothing but a rifle and a storm at his back. He'd survived. Barely. He testified from a wheelchair, his leg pinned and plated, and when they asked him why he'd turned on his handlers he said only: "I stayed too long already."

The inquiry's findings ran to four hundred pages. Vesper read none of them. He didn't need to. What mattered arrived in a plain envelope weeks later, on government letterhead with too many signatures: the valley would be opened to investigation. The drowned village would be raised—or as much of it as the water allowed—and memorialized. A stone marker would stand above the source, bearing two hundred and six names, and beneath them, in smaller letters, a single line:

*Found and preserved by A. Joubert, who kept faith with the dead.*

His father's name, in government ink. Vesper read the letter three times in Maren's galley, sitting across from her while she mended netting, and when he finally looked up she was watching him with that flat river-woman patience that never asked questions it knew you weren't ready to answer.

"Well," she said.

"Yeah," he said.

"That's your father's name on official paper."

"It is."

"Took them long enough."

Vesper laughed—a short, cracked sound that surprised him more than anything the inquiry had produced. Maren went back to her netting. Outside, the harbor light swung its slow arc across the water, and the ferry rocked at her moorings like a dog dreaming of running.

* * *

The offer came a week after the letter, delivered in person by a deputy minister who wore his ambition like cheap cologne. He met Vesper at the harbormaster's office, all handshakes and regret for the past unpleasantness, and laid it out over coffee neither of them drank: a permanent post. Director of Historical Surveys. Staff, funding, authority over every dig in the territory. His name on the door.

"You're the better digger, Joubert," the deputy said, and smiled like the phrase was a compliment. "Everyone says so. That telegram we sent you—we chose you deliberately. We needed someone who could find what others couldn't."

Vesper set down his cup. "You mean someone expendable."

The smile flickered. "I mean someone capable."

"There's a difference," Vesper said, "and I've spent my whole life being the first thing while people called me the second." He stood. The office window looked out on the harbor, on Maren's ferry riding at anchor, on Ivo up in the rigging doing something Tull was loudly forbidding him to do. "I'll tell you what I learned upstream. My father spent thirty years digging alone because he trusted institutions to bury the truth instead of him. He was right to dig and wrong to be alone. I'm not making his mistake twice."

"You'd give up a directorship? For a ferry run?"

"For the people on it." Vesper paused at the door. "Keep the post. Offer it to someone who measures their life in miles of corridor. I'm done measuring mine that way."

* * *

He signed on with Maren the same afternoon, which surprised no one except perhaps himself. She made him pay for his berth like any other hand—one share of the season's take, half the maintenance, all of the watch rotations nobody wanted—and when he tried to argue she looked at him until he stopped.

"This isn't charity," she said. "Charity sinks ships. You work, you eat, you stay. Those are the rules."

"I know the rules."

"Do you?" She coiled rope with quick, economical hands, not looking at him. "You've been leaving your whole life, Joubert. Every job, every town, every contract—you finish it and you're gone before anyone learns how you take your coffee. Staying is a skill. Most people never learn it."

"I'm learning."

She tied off the coil and finally met his eyes. Something in her face had softened by a degree so small most people would have missed it. He almost missed it himself.

"Then start small," she said. "Unpack your bag. All the way. Hang things up."

It sounded like nothing. It took him three days. There was something in the act of setting out his few possessions in a locker that belonged to a boat that belonged to a life that wasn't going anywhere without him—something that felt more like surrender than any flood or firefight ever had. His father's brass compass went on the shelf. The last journal page, the one naming coordinates that had started everything, he burned in the galley stove, and Maren watched him do it and said nothing, and the smoke went out the vent and was gone.

* * *

Ivo apprenticed himself without asking anyone's permission, which was, Tull observed, the most Vesper-like thing the boy had ever done. He appeared at the survey sites with his own notebook, copied Vesper's measurements in a jagged teenage hand, asked questions in bursts of three and five, and fell silent for hours at a stretch when the work demanded it—which was more discipline than Vesper had managed at seventeen, or twenty-seven, or possibly ever.

They worked the shallows of the drowned valley together in the clear weeks after the monsoon, when the water ran low and glass-still. The memorial crews came with cranes and dive teams, raising the church bell first—it hung now in a frame on the ridge above the source, and rang once each dusk for the two hundred and six. Vesper taught Ivo to read sediment layers, to feel with closed eyes where a wall's footing ran under silt, to know the difference between stone that had fallen and stone that had been placed.

"Why does it matter," Ivo asked one evening, mud to his elbows, the bell's single note fading behind them, "to get it exactly right? They're dead either way."

Vesper considered the question longer than the boy expected.

"Because the lie was exact," he said finally. "Somebody sat at a desk and got every false figure precisely right, and it killed two hundred and six people and erased a village and broke my family in half. Exactness isn't neutral, Ivo. It's a tool. You can build a memorial with it or a grave. Somebody has to be willing to be exact on the right side."

Ivo chewed on that, staring at the water where roof beams ghosted up through the shallows like the ribs of something sleeping.

"My mother used to say the river keeps what people throw away," he said at last. "I used to think that meant bad things."

"It means everything," Vesper said. "That's why somebody has to go look."

* * *

Tull stayed too, though he claimed it was for the wages and everyone let him keep the fiction. He rebuilt the ferry's engine with a patience none of them had seen in him before, swore at it in three languages, and slept, for the first time since any of them had known him, without a rifle within reach. Some nights Vesper found him on deck watching the ridge lights of the valley—now strung with work lamps for the memorial crews—and his face in that light was younger than it had any right to be.

Halvard wrote once from the prison hospital, a single page, no apology offered and none wanted. *I held the pass,* the letter said. *It was the first thing I ever finished.* Maren read it aloud at supper and then put it in the drawer with the ship's papers, filed among things that were part of the vessel now.

* * *

On the last evening of the season, they ran the ferry downstream through the reopened valley, carrying the first official visitors—families of the drowned, old now, some of them children then—who stood at the rail in silence as the water that had stolen their village carried them gently over its roof. The bell rang at dusk. Two hundred and six names. One of the old women touched Vesper's arm as she disembarked and said, "Your father's face, you know. Everyone says it." And then, before he could answer: "But kinder eyes."

He stood at the mooring a long time after the visitors had gone, watching the water go gold and then gray, and realized he was doing something he had not done in twenty years: standing still, wanting nothing, going nowhere.

Maren came to stand beside him. Below them, Ivo was showing Tull how to gut a fish properly, loudly, and Tull was letting him, which was its own kind of miracle.

"So," she said. "Where next?"

"Nowhere," Vesper said, and heard how strange and easy the word was in his mouth. "Nowhere next. This is the place."

She nodded slowly, as if confirming a reading she'd taken long ago. "Took you long enough."

"I measure slow."

"No," Maren said, turning back toward the galley, where the lamp was lit and supper waited and the whole small bright world of the boat hummed with the sounds of people who had stayed. "You just finally learned to measure the right thing."

Behind them the bell rang again, once, over the water that kept everything—and Vesper Joubert, the better digger, stood on the deck of the only home he'd ever chosen, and did not count the miles.
