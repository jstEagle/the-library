## 1. Twelve Years of Miles

The tide was going out and so, apparently, was my will to live dramatically.

I hung forty feet down in green-gray water off the coast, working a rusted valve on the *Cormorant's* old bilge line, and I thought—not for the first time—that if you added up every mile I'd traveled since I was nineteen, it would circle the world twice. If you added up the years, they'd come to thirty-one. The miles were winning. They'd been winning for twelve years now, ever since the year I stopped counting years and started counting distance, because distance doesn't ask questions. Distance doesn't look at you across a galley table and say *Mara, when are you coming home?*

Home was a word other people used. I had berths.

The valve gave with a shriek of corroded metal that I felt in my teeth through the regulator. I braced my boots against the hull plating—soft with rot, the kind of soft that means the whole ship is one bad season from being a reef—and got the packing nut loose on the second try. Below me, the seabed was a plain of pale silt and old rope, the kind of bottom that swallows things politely and never mentions them again. Somewhere under that silt, probably, were things people had paid good money to lose. That was the trade. We didn't find treasure. We found what grief had dropped overboard.

"Reyes." Suri's voice crackled in my ear, thin and tinny through the hardline. "You've been quiet for six minutes. Either you're dead or you're thinking, and both worry me."

"I'm working."

"You're brooding with tools. There's a difference. The valve?"

"Valve's free. Packing's shot. Tell the harbormaster his bilge system is held together with prayer and barnacles."

"He knows. He's hoping you won't notice."

I noticed everything. That was the problem with this life—it left you nothing to do but notice. Notice the way the light came down in columns through the cold water. Notice the small crab making its living in the seam of the plating. Notice that I'd done this exact job, or some version of it, four hundred times, and that my hands could do it without me, and that some mornings I wasn't entirely sure I showed up at all except as a pair of hands and a debt ledger.

I worked the packing out by feel. Cold seeped through the suit at the wrists, the usual slow leak of warmth that no amount of money in salvage ever fully sealed. Twelve years of miles, and every one of them cold at the cuffs.

"Mara," Suri said, quieter now. "Radio picked up something while you were down. Coast station rebroadcasting a notice. You'll want to hear it up top."

"Notices are weather and fish quotas."

"This one isn't."

I finished the job because finishing is the only discipline I've ever kept, then made the slow ascent, watching my bubbles climb ahead of me like small white thoughts leaving without permission.

* * *

The deck of the survey skiff smelled of diesel and kelp, which is to say it smelled like every morning of my adult life. Suri handed me coffee in the dented tin mug—the mug that had survived three sinkings and one marriage proposal, neither of which she'd accepted—and pointed her chin at the radio set bolted under the wheelhouse awning.

The notice was playing on a loop. Official voice, official cadence, the particular flatness that governments use when flatness is doing heavy lifting:

*"By order of the Coastal Naval Authority, all vessels are prohibited from approaching, anchoring near, or conducting salvage operations upon the wreck designated Site K-9, situated in the upper reach of the Vell River gorge. This restriction remains in force indefinitely. Violators will be subject to seizure of vessel and cargo, and prosecution under maritime statute—"*

Suri clicked it off. "K-9," she said. "That's the gorge wreck. The one from twelve years ago."

"I know which one it is."

"You know more than which one it is."

I drank my coffee. It was terrible, which meant it was mine. "It's a navy wreck in a navy river behind a navy fence of ice. Not our problem. Our problem is a bilge valve and a harbormaster who pays late."

"Mara."

"Suri."

She looked at me the way she always did when she was deciding whether to push—head tilted, eyes narrow, a woman who'd spent enough years on decks to know that some hatches you don't open at sea. She was twenty-six and had come to me at nineteen with forged papers and no history, which meant we understood each other perfectly. People without histories don't dig in each other's holds.

"The survey team went upriver last month," she said. "Three boats. You heard them provisioning at Calloway Dock. And now there's a notice forbidding approach to a site nobody's touched in twelve years, issued the same week those boats stopped answering their schedule calls."

"That's coincidence."

"That's a pattern wearing coincidence's coat."

I looked out at the water. From the deck, the sea was all surface, all shine, an honest lie. Underneath it was where I lived, mostly. Underneath it was where everything true about me had happened, and none of it had stayed down there the way I'd wanted.

Twelve years ago, my brother Teo had taken an expedition up the Vell gorge. Nine crew, two boats, a navy contract he never explained and I never asked about, because we were young and proud and I figured whatever he was doing, he'd tell me over beers after. The expedition went in during the last navigable window before calving season. It did not come out. The navy said ice. The navy said current. The navy said, with great patience, *accidents happen in gorges*, and they said it to our mother until she died of saying-it-to-her, and they said it to me until I stopped asking and started traveling, because a man in motion can't be cornered by a question.

They recovered two bodies. Neither was Teo's. They closed the file anyway. Files close easier than rivers.

"You're doing the thing," Suri said.

"What thing."

"Where your face goes somewhere and leaves the rest of you standing here holding a mug."

"My face has seniority. It goes where it wants."

She almost smiled. Then didn't. "There's more. Dispatcher at Calloway—Ilo Venn, you know him, the one with the handwriting like a spider fell in ink—he flagged the notice for us specifically. Said he tried the survey team's frequencies for nine days. Nothing. And then yesterday, one transmission came through. Garbled. Four seconds. He played it for me before I left the dock."

My hands went still around the mug. I hadn't told them to. Hands have their own ledgers too.

"And?" I said. My voice came out level, which cost me something.

"It's not the words that matter. The words are just coordinates and 'hold position.' Static most of it. But Mara—" She hesitated, and Suri does not hesitate; hesitation in Suri is like a fire alarm in a lighthouse, it means something structural. "The voice. Ilo ran it twice. He says he knows it. He says it's Rennick. Ossie Rennick. From Teo's crew."

The gorge wind came down off the headland and moved the awning canvas with a sound like a page turning.

Rennick. Big Ossie Rennick, who'd taught me to splice line when I was eleven because Teo was busy and Rennick was bored, who'd carried me on his shoulders through the Calloway festival when I was small enough for shoulders to be a country. Who was listed, officially, among the drowned of the Vell expedition, twelve years ago, along with my brother, along with seven others, in a file the navy sealed and the river kept.

A dead man's voice, transmitting coordinates from a river that eats expeditions, the same week the government tells everyone to stay away.

"That's impossible," I said.

"Obviously," Suri agreed. "So. What do we do about the impossible?"

"We do our jobs. We finish the Cormorant job, we take the next job, we keep moving. Miles, Suri. That's the whole trick. You keep moving and the past stays upstream."

"You don't believe that. You've never believed that. You just say it in a wry voice so it sounds like philosophy instead of fear."

I looked at her. Twenty-six, forged papers, no history, and a talent for finding the load-bearing wall in any conversation and leaning on it.

"Ilo still has the recording?" I asked.

"He's keeping it for you. He said—" She stopped, replaying it, getting the phrasing right. "He said, *Tell Mara the river's been quiet too long, and quiet rivers are either sleeping or hiding.*"

I set the mug down on the chart table. My reflection looked back at me from the dark glass of the radio's dead face—thirty-one years old, salt-cracked, gray coming in early at the temples, a face that had learned to be a mask so well it sometimes fooled its owner.

Twelve years of miles. Two times around the world, measured honestly, in cold water and paid invoices and ports where nobody knew my brother's name. And all of it, every mile, had been a long way of walking toward this one moment: a four-second transmission, a dead man's voice, and a question I'd buried so deep even I'd stopped hearing it knocking.

Teo Reyes went up the Vell gorge and did not come down. The navy said ice.

The navy had just told the entire coast, in writing, to forget the place existed.

People don't post guards around accidents. They post guards around answers.

"Get the Cormorant invoice settled," I said. "Then provision the skiff for upriver. Quietly."

Suri studied me for a long moment, and whatever she saw—whether it was the diver or the brother or just a man finally running out of miles to hide behind—she nodded once and went forward to make it happen, because that's what Suri did: she turned my worst decisions into functioning plans.

I stood at the rail and watched the tide turn. Out past the breakwater, the water lay flat and patient, holding everything it had ever been given.

Upstream, the glacier waited. And for the first time in twelve years, I was going to stop measuring my life in miles.

Tides would do. Six of them, if the rumors about calving season held true.

I'd spent half a lifetime learning how to hold my breath.

It was time to find out what for.
