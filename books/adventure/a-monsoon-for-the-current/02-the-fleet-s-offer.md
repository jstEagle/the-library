## 2. The Fleet's Offer

The notice was nailed to the harbor board between a missing-cat flyer and an advertisement for engine degreaser, which felt about right. Official paper always looks smaller than the trouble it carries.

*By order of the Coastal Authority: all vessel traffic is prohibited from approaching, surveying, or salvaging within three nautical miles of the Kestrel Narrows above Marker Eleven. Violators will be fined, impounded, and prosecuted.*

No reason given. No date of expiry. Just the gray stamp and the gray language, the way the navy says *forget this exists* without ever having to admit there's anything to forget.

I read it twice, mostly out of habit. Twelve years of reading notices like that teaches you the grammar of silence. When they tell you what you can't do without telling you why, it means somebody already knows why, and that somebody would rather you didn't.

I went back to my coffee. It had gone the temperature of harbor water, which in this town is a flavor you acquire or you leave.

"You're staring at that like it owes you money," said the harbormaster's clerk, a young man named Pell who filed things with the enthusiasm of a man who had never lost anything important to a filing cabinet.

"It doesn't owe me anything," I said. "That's the problem."

He laughed like I'd made a joke. I let him.

* * *

I'd been back from the Coyle wreck four days. Four days of scrubbing salt off gear, of selling the recovered brass by weight because nobody pays for provenance anymore, of sleeping in a bunk that didn't move and finding I couldn't. My life, measured honestly, was a ledger of miles. Two hundred miles down-coast for the Coyle. Sixty upriver once, years ago, on a job I don't talk about. Twelve thousand miles of water between me and the last time I saw my brother's boat leave this same harbor with nine men and a cargo manifest nobody would show me.

People ask divers how long we've been diving. We answer in distance. It keeps the arithmetic honest. Years lie to you — they stack up soft and make you feel like you've accumulated something. Miles just go. You go with them.

The morning Vessna Kade walked into the Saltline Tavern, I was measuring my life in the bottom of a glass and pretending it was lunch.

She came in the way weather comes in off the water — no announcement, just a change in pressure that everyone feels before they see. The tavern quieted in stages, the way it does when a person enters who owns boats. Not one boat. Boats. Plural, working, crewed. In a salvage town, that's not wealth, exactly. It's gravity.

Vessna Kade was maybe fifty, built like she'd been assembled from spare parts of better ships, with a face that had been sunburned so many times it had stopped arguing about it. She wore a fleet jacket with the insignia cut off, which told me more than the insignia would have. You only cut off your colors if you're between allegiances, or hiding one.

"Reyes." She said it like a confirmation, not a greeting. She'd checked before coming. That was worth noting. People who check are people who intend to spend money.

"Captain Kade." I didn't get up. Curiosity is cheaper seated.

She took the stool across from me anyway, uninvited, and signaled the barkeep with two fingers. He brought whatever she drank without asking her what. That told me she'd been in this town before, or that the barkeep was smarter than he looked, which was possible but unlikely.

"I have a job," she said.

"Everyone has a job."

"I have *this* job." She set a folded paper on the bar between us. Not the navy notice — a chart. Hand-annotated, current, expensive. The Kestrel Narrows ran up its middle like a scar, and at the head of it, circled twice in red grease pencil, sat a depth reading that made my teeth ache. Forty-one fathoms. In a gorge. With ice upstream.

"That's inside the exclusion line," I said.

"Yes."

"That's a navy-sealed wreck."

"Yes."

"And you're showing me this in a tavern full of men who'd sell their mothers' teeth for a look at it."

She glanced around the room with the mild contempt of a woman counting exits. "Half these men can't read a chart. The other half work for me already. Who are they going to tell?"

I looked at the chart again. The red circle. The depth. And beneath the circle, in her own handwriting, small and precise: *six tides.*

Six tides. Anyone in the trade knew what that meant this time of year. Calving season. When the glacier at the river's head started shedding house-sized pieces of itself into the current, and the Narrows — already narrow, already mean — turned into a chute of moving ice that no hull survived and no insurer would touch. Six tides was the window. After that, the route closed until the melt eased, and whatever was sitting at forty-one fathoms behind the exclusion line would sit there behind a wall of grinding ice, unreachable, unclaimed, unbothered, until next season. If the navy didn't get to it first. If the navy hadn't already.

"Why me?" I asked.

"Because you're good."

"Flattery's free. Try again."

"Because you're good," she repeated, "and because you're broke, and because you don't work for Doss Harrow anymore, and because—" she paused, and here was the thing about Vessna Kade: she paused like a chess player, not like someone searching for words — "because you have history with that stretch of river, and history makes men careful, and careful men come back."

There it was. Under the flattery, under the arithmetic, the hook. She knew about my brother. Of course she did. Everyone in the trade knew; grief travels faster than gossip in a town this size, and mine had been traveling twelve years.

"I don't dive graves," I said.

"This isn't a grave." She turned the chart toward herself and tapped the circle. "It's a wreck. The *Meridian Star*, navy transport, went down eleven years ago with a cargo hold sealed under naval order. Manifest classified. Crew lists classified. Cause of sinking — and guess — classified."

"The navy classifies everything."

"The navy classifies paperwork," she said. "They don't classify wrecks. They seal wrecks when there's something in them worth sealing. There's a difference, and the difference has a market value, and I am prepared to pay you triple your going rate to find out what it is before anyone else does."

Triple. I did the math the way a drowning man does math — fast, greedy, and badly. Triple rate through six tides, plus salvage shares if we lifted anything, plus the simple fact that my gear needed replacing, my boat needed a new bilge pump, and my pride needed something it wasn't going to find at the bottom of a tavern glass.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, the thing I didn't say: *Kestrel Narrows.* The same water. The same gray gorge. The last place my brother's expedition transmitted from before the transmissions stopped and the navy arrived and the notices went up and the whole river went quiet as a held breath.

Twelve years ago, they told us the *Cormorant* — Teo's boat — was lost to ice. No wreckage recovered. No bodies. A compensation letter signed by someone whose name I've forgotten on purpose. And now here was another sealed wreck, up the same river, behind the same kind of silence, and a woman with cut-off colors offering me money to open it.

Coincidence is a word sailors use when they haven't finished being suspicious yet.

"Who else knows about this?" I asked.

"My competitors know a wreck exists. They don't know where. The navy knows where. The navy also knows the calving schedule better than I do, which means either they'll beat us to it or they've decided it's not worth the trip. I'm betting on the second, because navies are lazy about cold water." She finished her drink in one motion. "But betting isn't knowing, which is why I want a diver who treats unknowns like enemies instead of puzzles."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I hire the second-best diver on this coast, and he gets rich or drowns, and either way I never think about you again." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. Cruelty would have been easier to dislike.

She left the chart. Folded it once, precisely, and set it in front of me like a bill.

"Six tides, Reyes. Tide one starts tomorrow at slack water. My tender, the *Ferryman*, leaves from the north quay at dawn. Triple rate, half up front, and I don't ask questions about your past and you don't ask questions about my buyers." She stood, dropped coins on the bar that covered both drinks and then some, and added, almost as an afterthought, the way you'd add a knife to a gift: "One more thing. The survey team that went up two weeks ago — Harrow's people, some of them — stopped answering their radio Tuesday. If you've got any sentiment about that, leave it ashore. Sentiment sinks divers."

She walked out. The tavern resumed its noise around the space she'd occupied, like water closing over a stone.

* * *

I sat with the chart for a long time after she left. Long enough that the barkeep refilled my glass without being asked and then pretended he hadn't, which is the highest form of kindness a barkeep offers.

Harrow's survey team. Doss Harrow. The man who taught me to read bottom contours the way priests read scripture. The man who pulled me out of the water the first time I blacked out at depth, slapped me awake on the deck of his boat, and said, *the sea doesn't take what you love, boy, it takes what you reach for.* The man who had been more father to me than my actual father, who was a rumor with a drinking problem.

His team, silent since Tuesday. Up the same river. Near the same narrows.

I unfolded the chart again. Studied the annotations. Her handwriting was tight and economical — soundings, current speeds, a note about a submerged shelf at thirty fathoms that would shred a carelessly anchored line. This was a professional's chart. Which meant somewhere, someone had surveyed that stretch recently enough to map it, inside an exclusion zone where surveying was illegal.

Somebody had been up there already. Somebody besides the navy. Somebody besides Harrow's vanished team.

I thought about saying no. I want to be clear about that — I genuinely considered it, turned it over, weighed it the way you weigh anything that might cost you your life against anything that might cost you your mind. Saying no meant keeping my miles small and my sleep dreamless and my brother's ghost where I kept him, folded up in the dry storage of my chest, taken out only on the bad nights.

Saying yes meant going back up that river. Back into the gorge where the water runs green-gray and cold enough to stop a heart that's already hesitating. Back toward whatever the navy had sealed and whatever Kade wanted and whatever had swallowed nine men and a boat and every answer I'd ever asked for.

Triple pay buys a lot of gear. It doesn't buy back twelve years.

But it might buy a look inside the silence. And I'd learned — slowly, expensively — that silence doesn't age well. It rots. It fills with worse things than truth.

I folded the chart into my jacket. Finished the drink. Left coins of my own on the bar, because I don't like owing taverns any more than I like owing captains.

Outside, the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of old zinc. Somewhere north, up past Marker Eleven, a glacier was calving its first ice of the season, right on schedule, indifferent as a clock. Six tides. The water would rise and fall six times, and then the door would close for a year.

I walked the length of the quay to where my boat sat low on her mooring, needing paint, needing a pump, needing what I needed. Halfway there, I passed the harbor board again, and the navy notice, and I stopped.

Someone had already pinned a second notice over it. Fresh ink. Same gray stamp:

*Amended: exclusion extended to five nautical miles. Effective immediately.*

The navy was drawing the circle tighter. Today. The same morning Vessna Kade showed me her chart.

Either she'd known that was coming — known it before the navy posted it — or she'd made them post it. And I couldn't decide which possibility frightened me more, so I did what I always do with fear too big to carry whole.

I broke off a piece and dove with it.

Dawn, north quay, the *Ferryman*. I'd be there. Not for the money — I'd tell myself that later, and almost believe it — but because somewhere up that green-gray river, Doss Harrow's radio had gone quiet, and my brother's grave had never been marked, and I had six tides to learn whether the sea takes what you reach for, or whether, sometimes, if you reach hard enough, it gives something back.

I untied my lines and started the engine. The tide was already turning.
