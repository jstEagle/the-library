## 3. Radio Silence

The notice came the way navy notices always come: too late to matter and in language designed to make you feel stupid for reading it.

*By order of the Coastal Authority, all vessel traffic is prohibited within two nautical miles of the Kestrel Narrows. Survey operations in progress. Violators subject to seizure.*

I read it twice, standing in the wheelhouse of the *Marrow*, with rain doing its usual impression of a monsoon against the glass. Two miles off the Kestrel Narrows. Upriver. Twelve years ago that stretch of water had been open to anyone with a boat and a death wish, which described my brother's crew exactly.

"You're making that face," Suri said from the galley doorway, holding two mugs like offerings.

"What face?"

"The one where you pretend you're not thinking about Tomas."

"I'm always not thinking about Tomas. It's my best skill."

She set one mug on the chart table anyway, close enough to my hand that refusing it would take effort, which was her whole strategy. Suri was twenty-three and had learned everything she knew about me by watching, which made her more dangerous than any rival fleet.

The radio crackled. Not the weather band, not the fleet chatter—something thinner, farther up the spectrum, the kind of transmission you only caught if your antenna was old and stubborn like the rest of the boat.

"—repeat, we've got movement on the lower shelf, over."

A survey team. Upriver. In the narrows the navy had just sealed.

"Movement on what shelf?" I said, mostly to myself.

Suri leaned toward the speaker. "That's a long way inland for shelf talk."

"That's a long way inland for anyone still breathing." I turned the gain up. The voice dissolved into static, then reassembled itself, waterlogged and patient, the way voices do when they've been talking into a dead channel for hours.

"*Marrow*, if anyone's listening, this is Venn at Gray Harbor dispatch relaying for the upriver survey. Team Corvid. Last confirmed position above the second buoy line. They're overdue by nine hours and their emergency beacon is—" static ate the rest.

Nine hours overdue. Beacon status unknown. I'd heard this exact sentence before, twelve years ago, delivered by a different dispatcher with the same careful flatness people use when they already know how the story ends and are just waiting for you to catch up.

"Suri," I said. "Get me the harbor frequency."

"You're going to answer."

"I'm going to ask a question. Answering implies commitment."

She gave me the look she reserved for my worst lies—the ones I told myself—and keyed the mic before I could stop her.

"This is the *Marrow*. Go ahead, Gray Harbor."

* * *

Ilo Venn had been a dispatcher for eleven years, which meant she had developed the professional habit of treating every voice on the radio as a person who might soon stop being one. She kept a ledger—not official, never official—of the calls she couldn't forget. The fisherman who'd gone quiet mid-sentence about his daughter's wedding. The ferry pilot whose last words were an apology to no one in particular. And now, apparently, Team Corvid, whose beacon had blinked twice at 0340 and then decided, like everyone else in this story, to stop answering questions.

"Marrow," she said, when the call came through. "You're coastal. What's your interest in an upriver survey?"

There was a pause on the other end. She knew that pause. It was the sound of a man deciding how much truth he could afford.

"My brother ran surveys up there," the voice said finally. "Twelve years back."

Venn pulled the file she'd already pulled twice that morning. Reyes expedition, lost with all hands, six souls, cause listed as ice collapse, investigation closed by naval authority before it opened. She'd read enough closed-by-the-navy files to know they were less investigations than burials.

"And your name?"

Another pause. Longer.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if I'm going to spend my shift wondering why a coastal salvager cares about a survey team he has no contract with."

"Mara Reyes."

She wrote it down even though she didn't need to. Some names you write down so the day can't pretend it didn't happen.

"Mr. Reyes. I'll be honest with you, because the alternative is the script, and you strike me as a man who's heard the script." She glanced at the relay log. "Team Corvid went dark nine hours ago. Their last clear transmission mentioned salvage cuts on a hull. On a wreck that isn't supposed to have been found."

Silence. Then, very quietly, the kind of laugh that isn't a laugh at all.

"Salvage cuts," Mara Reyes repeated. "On the navy wreck."

"You know the wreck."

"Everyone knows the wreck. That's the problem. Knowing it and saying so are different crimes."

Venn looked out the dispatch window at Gray Harbor's gray morning doing its gray work. Somewhere upriver, past the buoys nobody maintained and the gorge that narrowed like a throat, six people—or five, or four, the numbers were the first thing to go soft in these situations—were either dead or wishing they were.

"There's a boat going up tomorrow," she heard herself say. "Small expedition. They asked for a relay point and someone to hold their schedule. I volunteered because nobody else wanted the night shift." She hadn't volunteered. She'd been assigned. But the lie came out smooth, and she let it. "If you were the kind of man who ignored navy notices—which obviously you're not—I'd tell you they could use a diver who knows the water."

"I'm not that kind of man," Reyes said.

"No," Venn agreed. "Of course not."

They both sat with that for a moment, two professionals appreciating each other's craftsmanship.

"One more thing," she said. "Corvid's last transmissions. There's a voice on them the team leader flagged as unfamiliar. Older man. He wasn't on the roster. He told them to pull out, that the wreck was already spoken for." She checked the transcript again, though she had the words memorized by then. "He signed off with a name. Harrow."

The silence on the other end lasted long enough that she checked her connection light.

"Say again?" Reyes said, and his voice had changed. It had gone somewhere cold and young.

"Doss Harrow. H-A-R—"

"I heard you." A breath. "That's impossible. Doss Harrow doesn't do upriver work. Doss Harrow does coastal salvage and drinks his profits and tells the same four stories until you can recite them."

"People change."

"Not him. He's the most permanent thing I know." Another breath, and now she could hear something underneath the words, some old machinery grinding back to life after years of rust. "Play me the transmission."

"It's not—"

"Play me the transmission, Ms. Venn. Please."

She played it. The old man's voice filled the dispatch room, gravel and river-water: *"...whatever they told you, the hull's been opened. Get out while the tide's yours. This water keeps what it's given."*

And then, quieter, almost to himself, almost as if he knew who might someday listen: *"Tell the boy the ledger's still open."*

Venn muted the channel. "Mr. Reyes? Are you there?"

For a long moment, nothing. Then Mara Reyes, in a voice she would remember for years—flat, precise, the voice of a man setting down something heavy he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten its weight:

"He hasn't called me the boy since I was nineteen."

* * *

Suri found me on the aft deck at midnight, doing the thing I do instead of sleeping, which is standing very still and letting the rain make decisions for me.

"So we're going upriver," she said.

"We're not going upriver."

"You've got the charts out. You've got the dive compressor serviced. You re-spooled the tether line, which you only do when you're scared, and you only get scared about one thing." She handed me tea I hadn't asked for. "Also you said 'we're not going upriver' in the tone you use when we are."

I drank the tea. It was terrible. She makes it terrible on purpose; she says it keeps me honest, because a man will lie about anything except bad tea.

"Twelve years ago," I said, "a survey team went into those narrows and stopped answering the radio. Six people. One of them was my brother. The navy closed the file in nine days and opened a shipping lane restriction in ten, and everybody agreed that was fast, efficient work, and nobody asked why a wreck full of drowned men needed guarding." I watched the harbor lights smear in the rain. "Today another survey team went into those narrows and stopped answering the radio. And the last voice on their channel belonged to the man who taught me to dive."

"Doss was on Corvid's channel?"

"Doss was *with* Corvid, or near them, or watching them drown, and either way he signed off with a message for me. 'The ledger's still open.'" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Doss keeps ledgers. Debts, favors, who owes whom. He used to say every salvage is a debt the sea's collecting. I thought he meant it as philosophy."

"And now?"

"Now I think he meant it as a confession I wasn't ready to hear."

Suri was quiet for a while. Rain on the wheelhouse roof, the harbor bell doing its slow arithmetic.

"Tomas," she said. "Your brother. If there's anything left of him up there—"

"There isn't. Twelve years of current, Suri. There's nothing left of anybody after twelve years." I said it firmly, the way you drive a stake into ground you don't trust. "This isn't about him."

"Then what's it about?"

It was a fair question and I hated it. I looked at the chart table through the glass, at the notice still lying there with its neat navy prohibitions, at the route penciled upriver in my own hand—when had I done that? Sometime between the tea and the midnight, apparently, my hands had voted without me.

"A man I love like family is involved in whatever killed my actual family," I said. "And there's a wreck up there that somebody's been cutting open in secret for years, and a navy that seals it instead of salvaging it, and a survey team that stopped talking nine hours after finding cuts on the hull." I drained the tea and grimaced at it, grateful for something honest. "I don't know what it's about yet. But I know the shape of it. I've known the shape since I was seventeen, and I spent twelve years swimming away from it, and it turns out the shape is longer than twelve years of miles."

Suri nodded slowly. "So we're going upriver."

"We're going upriver."

"I'll fuel the Marrow."

She went inside. I stayed on the deck a while longer, because the rain was steady and the harbor was quiet and somewhere upstream, past the warning buoys and the narrowing gorge, a glacier was holding its breath around a secret, and a man who taught me everything was keeping a ledger with my name in it.

The radio hissed behind me. Just static. But for a second—one clean, cold second—I swear it sounded like breathing.

* * *

At 0500, Ilo Venn logged the *Marrow*'s departure from Gray Harbor, destination listed as "coastal grounds," heading listed as due east, which was true for approximately forty minutes and then became a lie with a wake behind it.

She noted it in her private ledger, under the entry she'd started the night before. Then she picked up the microphone, considered the empty channel, and did the small illegal thing she'd been turning over all night: she copied Corvid's final coordinates onto a scrap of paper, sealed it in an envelope, and addressed it to the *Marrow*, care of the Gray Harbor pilot office, where a certain captain owed her a favor and asked no questions.

Some transmissions you relay. Some you just pass along, hand to hand, before the water closes over them.

She wrote one line on the envelope and underlined it twice:

*In case the river keeps what it's given.*
