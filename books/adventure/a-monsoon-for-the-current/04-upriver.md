## 4. Upriver

The boat that would take me upriver was called the *Honest Return*, which told me everything I needed to know about its owners' relationship with optimism. She was forty feet of welded steel and stubbornness, patched in places with plates that had clearly come from three different vessels and possibly a bridge. Her skipper was a woman named Ferren Oduya, built like a capstan and about as talkative, and she looked me over at the dock the way you'd look over a rope someone handed you right before you trusted it with your life.

"You're the diver," she said.

"I'm a diver."

"Venn said you were the diver. The one who knows the gorge." She spat over the rail with the precision of long practice. "I don't care what Venn said. I care what you know. So: what do you know?"

"That nobody comes back from up there with good news."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Load your gear."

Ilo Venn had earned me this berth, in the way that dispatchers earn things—with phone calls, favors, and a tone of voice that made it clear she was calling in a debt nobody remembered agreeing to. She'd met me at the survey office the morning after the silence started, and she hadn't wasted a second on pleasantries.

"The *Kestrel's Petal* went dark at 0400 yesterday," she'd said, sliding a printout across the desk. "Survey contract, navy-adjacent, mapping the approach channel above the second narrows. Six crew. Last transmission was routine—depth readings, current speed, a joke about the coffee." Her finger tapped the transcript. "Then this."

I read it. *Contact on the hull. Not ours. Repeat, not ours. Going down to look.*

"Going down to look," I repeated.

"Forty meters of water in a gorge with a current like a fist. In diving suits rated for survey work, not salvage." Venn had a face like a folded map—all creases, hard to read, but you knew there was a country in there somewhere. "And here's the part I didn't put in the official log."

She turned the transcript over. There was a name written on the back in her own handwriting, small and careful, like it might bite.

*Teodor Vasch.*

I knew the name the way you know a scar. Teodor Vasch had been second diver on my brother's expedition, twelve years ago, the one that went up the same gorge and came back as a navy press release and a closed file. The one that came back as nothing.

"He's alive," Venn said. "He's been working survey contracts upriver for years. Quiet man. Keeps to himself. And now he's on a boat that stopped answering its radio after saying the words *not ours* about a wreck the navy swears doesn't exist."

"You think he's alive."

"I think a man who survived that river once knows things about it the rest of us don't. And I think you're the only diver stupid enough and angry enough to go find out what." She leaned back. "I can't authorize an expedition. Nobody can. The navy's sealed the whole approach. But I can lose some paperwork, and Ferren Oduya owes me a favor from a bad year neither of us talks about. You'd be going as ballast. Officially, the *Honest Return* is running a cargo survey for an agricultural concern."

"Agricultural," I said.

"Up a gorge. In calving season." For the first time, something moved behind the creases—tired humor, or maybe just tiredness. "The navy doesn't read fiction, Mara. That's always been their weakness."

So now I was ballast, standing on the deck of a boat named after a lie, watching the gorge swallow the morning.

* * *

The first thirty miles were almost kind. The river ran wide and brown-green, lazy in the way big rivers are lazy, the way a sleeping animal is lazy. We passed fishing platforms on stilts and a floating chapel that someone had painted a cheerful, improbable blue, and I let myself pretend, for about an hour, that this was just work. Another job. Another mile on the pile.

Then the banks started closing in.

It happens gradually, the way a hand closes. First the trees lean closer, then the rock shows through, gray and wet and older than any language I speak. The river narrows from half a mile to a quarter to a channel barely wider than the boat, and the current picks up from a murmur to a voice to a shout, and the walls rise on either side until the sky is a strip of white at the top, like the gorge is a wound and we're traveling down inside it.

"Second narrows by dusk," Ferren said, hands steady on the wheel. "You'll want to see the buoys before dark."

The buoys.

The navy had sealed the river—official notice, no approach, all vessels subject to detention—but sealing a river and maintaining it are different professions. The warning buoys that marked the safe channel through the narrows had been placed, according to the charts, eleven years ago. Nobody had touched them since. They hung in the current like drowned men who'd been left there on purpose, paint gone, chains furred with weed, some listing so far over that their lights pointed at the bank instead of the sky. One was missing entirely, and where it should have been, the chart said *SHOAL—DO NOT CROSS*, and the river said nothing at all, which was worse.

"They never came back," I said. "After the incident. They posted the notice and never came back to maintain a single buoy."

"Navy doesn't maintain," Ferren said. "Navy forbids. Maintenance is for people who expect to return." She steered us past a listing buoy close enough that I could have touched it. Someone had scratched marks into its flank, long ago, under the peeling paint—tally marks, or a name, or just the record of a man counting days. I didn't look long. Looking long at things like that, up here, was how the gorge got into you.

We passed the second narrows at dusk, engines at half, Ferren reading the water by eye and by a lifetime of it, calling the channel to herself in a low voice like a prayer she'd stopped believing in but kept up out of habit. The walls squeezed in. The current shoved. Somewhere below us, the river ran over rock shelves that the charts marked with the naval equivalent of *here be dragons*, and above us the first stars came out in that strip of sky like coins dropped into a well.

I stood at the bow with my gear checked twice and checked again, because checking gear was a thing my hands knew how to do when my head wouldn't shut up.

Twelve years ago, a boat not much bigger than this one had come up this same channel. My brother Elio had stood at its bow—I knew because there was a photograph, the last one, him grinning with his hair full of river wind, pointing up at the walls like they were a wonder instead of a warning. Teodor Vasch had been aboard. Doss Harrow had signed the manifest as expedition master, back when Doss still put his name on things that could be traced.

And the river had taken all of them, and the navy had taken the story, and I had spent twelve years taking miles—salvage miles, contract miles, any miles that kept me moving and kept me from coming back here.

Now here I was. And the gorge didn't feel like a place. It felt like a throat, and we were going down it, and everything in me that was still a nineteen-year-old kid waiting at a dock for a boat that never came back was screaming at the rest of me to turn around.

I didn't turn around. I'd learned that much about myself, at least. Whatever else the miles had bought, they'd bought that.

* * *

We anchored in a side eddy below the third narrows, in a slot of slack water Ferren knew from "a previous life," which was all she'd say about it. Suri—the deckhand, nineteen, sharp-eyed, all elbows and opinions—cooked something involving rice and a fish she'd caught off the stern with the smugness of a magician revealing a trick. I ate it. It was good. I told her so, and she looked at me like I'd complimented the tide.

"You've been up here before," she said. Not a question.

"Once. Twelve years ago. As far as the second narrows. We waited for a clearance that never came."

"The brother," she said, and then, seeing my face, had the grace to look like she wished the river would take her. "Venn told me. Sorry. I do that. I say the thing."

"It's fine."

"It's not fine. Nobody comes up this river for fine." She poked the fire. "Ferren says the water's dropped eight centimeters since the charts. Glacier's retreating faster. Means the calving season's going to be early and mean. Means we've got maybe six good tides before the flood surge turns the lower gorge into a washing machine."

"Venn said the same."

"Venn's usually right. It's her worst quality." Suri looked up at the walls, black against the stars. "You feel it, don't you. The gorge."

"What do you mean?"

"Like it's not empty." She said it lightly, but her hand had gone still on the poker. "Ferren won't say it. Old-timers say the river keeps what it takes. That you can feel them. The drowned." She shrugged, too casual by half. "Superstition."

"Sure," I said.

But that night, lying in my bunk with the anchor chain ticking against the hull like a slow clock, I listened to the river go by in the dark, and I thought: it's not superstition. It's just accurate. The gorge did keep them. Elio was down there, or what the river had left of him, somewhere in the black water under the black rock, twelve years and forty meters and a navy seal deep. And tomorrow I was going to dive into the same water, past the same walls, to a wreck the navy had forbidden and then abandoned and then forbidden again, looking for a man who'd survived it once and might not have survived it twice.

The river feels like a grave, Suri had said, in her way. She was right. It was worse than that.

Graves are for the people who come back to visit.

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow, the way it does at the bottom of a gorge, arriving last and leaving first. I was on deck before Ferren, gear on, checking my gauges by headlamp while the walls around us were still just darker shapes against the dark.

"Survey wreck's two miles up," Ferren said, appearing beside me with two cups of something that claimed to be coffee. "Past the third narrows. The *Kestrel's Petal* anchored in the lee of the north wall—Venn had their last position. You dive from here, swim the eddy line up, drop where the current slackens. Forty meters to the hull, per their soundings." She handed me a cup. "Current's the killer. Mid-tide slack gives you maybe twenty minutes at depth. Miss your window and the river decides for you."

"The river usually does."

"You've got that look," Ferren said. "The look of a man who's already decided the river's wrong about something."

I drank the coffee. It was terrible. It was perfect. "There's a man up there," I said. "Or was. Teodor Vasch. He was on my brother's boat. He's the one who sent the last transmission."

Ferren was quiet a moment. "The one who said *not ours*."

"The one who said *not ours*." I looked up the channel, where the third narrows waited like a held breath. "Twelve years ago the navy told us there was nothing up here worth the risk. No wreck, no cargo, no reason. They told my mother that at the funeral that wasn't a funeral, because there wasn't a body. And now there's a survey team forty meters down saying *not ours* about something the navy swears isn't there." I set the cup down. "Somebody's been lying for twelve years, Ferren. I'm going to find out who, and I'm going to find out why, and I'm going to do it before the ice closes the door."

"And if the river wants you instead?"

"Then the river and the navy can fight over me," I said. "I've got no plans to make it easy for either of them."

Ferren looked at me for a long moment, and then she did the thing I was starting to understand was her version of a blessing: she nodded once, spat over the rail, and went to start the engine.

Suri appeared at my elbow with my fins and my mask, and her face was doing something it hadn't done since the dock—something careful and young.

"For what it's worth," she said, "if the gorge keeps you, I'll come find you. Ferren thinks I'm joking when I say that kind of thing. I'm not."

"I know," I said. "That's your worst quality."

She laughed, once, sharp, and then the *Honest Return* was moving, and the third narrows were coming, and the river was saying what it always says, which is nothing, which is everything.

The water at the drop point was black and flat and patient. I stood on the gunwale with the gorge walls rising on either side of me, and twelve years fell away like ballast, and I thought about my brother pointing up at these same walls, grinning, calling them a wonder.

"Hold on, Elio," I said, to no one, to the river, to the dark under the dark.

Then the current slackened, and I went in.

The cold hit me like a hand. And forty feet down, through the green-black murk, where the survey charts said a hull should be—

Something was already shining a light at me.
