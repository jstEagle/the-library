## 5. What the Ice Keeps

The water upriver was a different animal than the sea I'd made my living in. The sea at least had the decency to be honest about wanting to kill you. This water just wanted to keep you—cold enough to stop your heart from arguing, dark enough that your torch beam seemed to apologize for intruding. I hung in it forty feet above the wreck and let the river decide how I'd drift, which was the first lesson of diving current: you don't fight the water, you negotiate with it, and the water always wins the long game.

"Comms check," Suri's voice crackled in my ear, thin and tinny through the hardline. "Reading you at depth forty, drift two knots. You're coming up on her now."

"I see her." My breath rasped in my own ears. "She's big."

That was an understatement wearing a bow tie. The *Corvane*—that was the name the navy's notice had given, though the hull below me had long since surrendered its paint to the river—lay heeled over on the channel floor like a drunk sleeping off a century. A coastal freighter, maybe two hundred feet of her, masts gone, superstructure furred with a gray silt-moss that made her look grown rather than sunk. The gorge walls rose on either side of her, close enough that I could have swum to either bank in a minute. The ice above us, the glacier's tongue that made this whole run navigable only six tides out of the year, filtered the light down to a green murk, like diving inside a bottle of old glass.

"Any thermal?" I asked.

"Nothing active. River's quiet. You've got your window, Mara. Make it count."

I made my descent slow. Twelve years of diving had taught me that the first descent on any wreck is a courtship—you don't grab, you don't pry, you look. Wrecks punish greed. They hold grudges. And this one, I already knew, held more than grudges. It held my brother, or had, once, in whatever way a man is held by the place that killed him.

The navy's notice had sealed this site for twelve years. Salvage restricted. Approach prohibited. The kind of language that governments use when they mean *forget this exists*. And I had spent twelve years obeying it from a distance, the way you obey a locked door in a house where something died—gladly, at first, and then with a resentment I told myself was just professional curiosity.

My boots touched silt beside the hull and a small cloud bloomed around me and settled. I put my glove against the steel. Cold came through the glove like a message.

"Contact," I said. "Hull's intact along the port side. Silt's heavy. I'm going to run the seam."

"Copy. Slow and methodical. Kade wants a map, not a hero."

Kade wanted a lot of things. Triple pay, my silence, my name on her manifest, and the *Corvane*'s cargo before her competitors or the calving season or the navy itself got there first. I'd taken her money because I'd told myself it was just work, and because the alternative was spending another season diving other people's losses for other people's profit, measuring my life in miles. But hanging there in the green dark with my brother's grave under my gloves, "just work" felt like a joke I'd been telling too long.

I swam the port seam, torch sweeping. Rivet lines, plate laps, a forest of river growth. At the bow the anchor chain still ran out and down into the muck, and something about that snagged at me—the *Corvane* hadn't gone down in a storm, then. She'd anchored. She'd sat here, in this gorge, on purpose, and then she'd filled.

"Anchor's down," I reported. "She was moored when she went."

A pause on the line. "That's not in any of the survey notes," Suri said. "Kade's going to want—"

"I know what Kade's going to want."

I kept swimming. The current pushed at my back, patient, insistent, a hand between my shoulder blades reminding me the river had somewhere to be. I came around the bow, crossed the hawse, and started down the starboard side, and that was where the courtship ended.

The first cut was a gash in the plating four feet long, edges bright even under the silt film. Not torn. Not corroded through. *Cut*—burned, by the look of the bead, the way a cutting torch leaves a signature no explosion and no reef can fake. I swept my light along it and my stomach did something slow and cold that had nothing to do with the water temperature.

"Say again, Mara?" Suri said, because I'd gone quiet and divers who go quiet make dispatchers nervous.

"Nothing." I put my glove on the cut's edge. Smooth. Old enough to have grown a skin of river life over it, thin but there—years of it. "Hold on."

I swam on. Twenty feet, another cut. Thirty feet, a whole panel removed, plate and frame both, lifted out clean and carried away. The openings were dark mouths leading into the ship's interior, and every one of them had been made by men with tools and time and the confidence of people who did not expect to be interrupted.

"Mara, your heart rate just spiked. Talk to me."

"Suri." I made myself say it flat. "Somebody's already been in this wreck."

Silence on the line, just the hiss of my own regulator and, beneath it, the river's long murmur through the hull.

"That's not possible," she said finally. "The navy's had this site sealed since the incident. Nobody's had access. That's the whole—"

"That's the whole point, yeah." I swept the torch across the opened panel again. The growth on the cut edges was the kind that takes seasons, not weeks. "This wasn't done last month. This is years old. Multiple openings, planned sequence—they went in through the cargo deck, here, and here, and there's a third access aft. Somebody ran a full salvage operation on this wreck and nobody wrote it down."

"Kade's fleet didn't—"

"Kade's fleet didn't know about it. You don't spend six tides and triple pay on a wreck you've already emptied." Though even as I said it, I heard the hole in my own logic. *Kade's fleet.* Her fleet. And Doss Harrow, who had raised me in this trade, who had taught me that a wreck tells the truth even when men don't—who had, the night before I left, bought my drinks and asked careful questions about my route and my tides and my depth windows, with the easy affection of an old man and the precision of a man taking inventory.

I put that thought down. Not yet. Not in forty feet of green dark with my hands on a dead ship.

"Keep mapping," I said. "I want the full pattern of these openings before I surface. Every cut, every removed panel, positions relative to the hull numbers."

"Copy. Mara—be careful in there. If somebody cut their way in, the structure's compromised. Those openings change how she's loaded."

"I noticed."

That was the other thing, the thing I hadn't said yet because saying it would make it real. The cuts weren't random. They weren't a scavenger tearing at a hull for whatever metal was handy. They followed the ship's logic—cargo access points, the strongrooms, the line of the central corridor. Whoever had done this had had the *Corvane*'s plans. Not a navy that seals a wreck and forgets it. A navy that seals a wreck and then quietly comes back for what it doesn't want found.

* * *

I worked the starboard side for an hour, and the pattern only got worse.

The removed panels clustered amidships, over what the old plans said was the secure cargo hold—the reason the navy had cared about this ship, the reason Kade was paying triple, the reason six tides mattered. And here was the thing that turned my cold shock into something colder: the panels over the *crew* sections were untouched. The cuts went to the cargo and stopped. Whatever these men had come for, they'd taken it and left the dead their privacy.

Or what they'd thought was the dead's privacy.

"Coming up on the aft access," I said. The third opening, a neat rectangle burned through the plating just forward of the engine room. "Going in for a look at the corridor line."

"Negative," Suri said, sharp. "Kade said outer hull only on dive one. No penetration until the structure's mapped."

"I'm not penetrating. I'm looking through a door somebody else already opened."

"That's penetration with extra steps and you know it."

I hung at the opening. My torch beam went through into a corridor furred with silt-moss, deck plates visible, the ship's spine running forward into dark. Nothing moved in it. Nothing ever moved in a wreck; that was the lie we told ourselves. Things moved in wrecks all the time—silt, current, gas, the slow tectonics of a ship settling into the river's grip. And sometimes, if you were very unlucky, other things.

The beam caught something on the corridor floor, ten feet in, and my heart stopped being a professional organ and became just a muscle hammering in my chest.

A tank. A dive bottle, lying against the bulkhead, and beside it a weight belt, and the silt had drifted over both of them the way silt drifts over everything, patiently, completely, the way it drifts over things that have lain undisturbed for a very long time.

"Suri," I said. My voice came out wrong and I cleared my mask and tried again. "There's dive gear inside. Old gear. Somebody left a tank and a belt on the corridor floor, and it's been there years."

More silence. When she came back, her voice had changed—the dispatcher's calm gone thin over something human. "Mara. Get out. Come up. Now."

"Suri—"

"That's not salvage gear and you know it. That's a body's worth of equipment lying in a sealed wreck the navy says nobody ever entered. Get out. Surface. Kade needs to hear this from you, not from me reading your telemetry."

I looked down the corridor one more time. The dark went forward, toward the cargo hold, toward the strongrooms, toward whatever the navy had cut out of this ship and carried away while the river kept its mouth shut. And somewhere in that dark, or somewhere in the twelve years since, was the answer to a question I had carried since I was twenty-three years old: what happened to the *Meridian*'s crew, and why did the navy's report have more pages redacted than written?

My brother had gone up this river on a survey contract and come home as a name on a memorial plaque. The navy had called it a navigation accident. I had spent twelve years not asking questions, because the men who raised me had asked me not to, and I had loved them enough to obey.

But the river, it turned out, had not been asked to keep quiet. The river had just been keeping things.

"Mara. Your air. Come on."

"Yeah." I backed away from the opening, giving the wreck its privacy back, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "Coming up."

I rose through the green dark with the current pushing at me and the cold inside me that had nothing to do with the water. Above me the ice ceiling filtered the daylight down, and I thought about what else that ice had filtered, what it had watched, how long it had been the only witness up here that couldn't be paid off.

* * *

They helped me over the gunwale and Suri had coffee going, which on the *Tern* meant she'd known I'd want it before I knew it myself. I sat on the deck plates dripping and told them—Kade, Suri, old Petch at the helm—about the cuts. All of them. The sequence. The plans they implied. The gear on the corridor floor.

Kade listened the way she did everything, with her arms crossed and her jaw set at an angle that meant her mind was three moves ahead and none of them generous.

"Years," she said at last. "You're certain."

"River growth on the cut edges. Silt depth on the gear. I'd stake my ticket on five years minimum. Could be more."

"Five years." She turned and looked upriver, toward the glacier, toward the *Corvane* lying in the dark under our keel. "The navy sealed this wreck the week it went down. There was a patrol boat on this stretch for two years after. Nobody dives a sealed site under a navy patrol."

"Then somebody did," I said. "Somebody with the ship's plans, a cutting rig, and enough clearance to work under the navy's nose. This wasn't scavengers, Kade. This was an operation."

"And they took the cargo."

"They took what they wanted of the cargo. And they left a diver's gear on the corridor floor." I wrapped my hands around the coffee Suri put in them. It was too hot and I was grateful. "Which means either somebody died in there and they left him, or somebody surfaced and reported that the job was done, and either way the navy's version of events has a hole in it you could drive a freighter through."

Kade was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into the register she probably used on her own fleet, the one that made men twice her size recheck their rigging. "Twelve years ago, the *Meridian* survey goes missing up this river. The navy calls it an accident, seals the *Corvane*, and pays off every family that asks questions. Now we find out somebody's been inside the sealed wreck since. You want to know what I think, Reyes?"

"I expect you'll tell me."

"I think your navy didn't seal this wreck to keep people out." Her eyes came around to mine, gray and flat as the river. "I think they sealed it to keep something *in*—and then went back for it when they thought no one was watching. And whatever they went back for, your brother's crew found it first."

The coffee had gone cold in my hands. I hadn't noticed drinking it.

"Second dive is yours," Kade said. "Inside the hull. You found the doors; you go through them. And Reyes—" she paused at the companionway, "—whatever you find in there, I want it logged, photographed, and in my safe before you so much as breathe a word of it on the radio. If the navy comes back for a third visit, I want leverage."

"And if what's in there is just bodies?" Suri asked quietly. "Just dead men and old gear?"

Kade looked at her, then at me, and for one unguarded second something moved behind the flat gray—something that might, in a better woman, have been called regret.

"Then it's still leverage," she said, and went below.

Suri sat down next to me on the deck plates. The river ran past the hull, brown and patient, carrying its silt and its secrets down toward the sea. Upstream, the glacier sat in its gorge, white and silent, holding its twelve years of witness.

"My brother dove wrecks like this," I said. I hadn't meant to say it. "Before. He used to say the river keeps what men can't afford to. I thought it was just something divers say."

"Is it?"

I looked upriver, at the ice. "No," I said. "It's an inventory."

The tide would turn in four hours. And somewhere down in the dark, through a door somebody else had already opened, my brother's last days were waiting to be counted.
