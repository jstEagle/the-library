## 6. The First Tide

The tide came up the gorge like a slow argument nobody could win.

I felt it before I saw it—the water under our anchor line going slack, then turning, the whole brown weight of the river hesitating against the sea pushing back into it. Twelve years of diving had taught me to read water the way other men read faces, and this water was saying: *you have six hours, and then we'll see about you.*

"Bottom time starts now," I said, and went over the side.

The *Cormorant's Rest* sat on the shelf where the charts said she would, though "sat" was generous. She lay on her port side at forty-one meters, half-buried in silt that the river had been laying down over her like a man covering something he was ashamed of. A navy corvette, decommissioned and scuttled—or so the notice said. Scuttled ships go down clean. This one had gone down fighting something, or running from it. Her superstructure was crumpled inward like a crushed beer can, and there was a tear along her starboard flank you could have driven a skiff through.

I swam the length of her hull with my slate and my measuring tape, doing the unglamorous arithmetic of salvage. Length overall. Depth of burial. Angle of list. The location of every opening big enough to put a diver through, because that was the real work—not the wreck itself but the question of how you got inside her without becoming part of the collection.

That was when I found the cuts.

I want to be honest about what happened in my chest when I saw them, because I've told this story enough times now that I know how easy it is to skip past the moment that mattered. The first cut was a plasma line, clean-edged, maybe thirty centimeters wide, punched through the hull plating just aft of the engine room intake. Modern. Not navy-modern—navy cutting is neat, ceremonial, done by men who file reports. This was working-man's cutting, fast and slightly crooked, the kind of line you burn when you're cold and rushed and don't care what the metal thinks about it.

There were four of them. Four entry points, burned through a sealed wreck that no civilian was supposed to know existed, in a channel the navy pretended wasn't navigable.

I hung there in the green dark with my tape measure dangling forgotten from my wrist and did the math I didn't want to do. Corrosion on the cut edges. Silt drift patterns. The way the river had already started healing over the wounds. Years. Not months. *Years* since someone had stood exactly where I was standing, breathing compressed air out of a tank, looking at the same forbidden hull.

Someone had beaten me here by years, and whoever they were, they hadn't filed a report either.

My gauge read eighteen minutes. I marked all four cuts on the slate, took photographs with the little camera in its housing, and made myself finish the survey properly, because panic is just information arriving faster than you can spend it. Then I went up.

* * *

Suri met me at the ladder with a thermos and a face full of questions she was too disciplined to ask all at once.

"You were down long," she said.

"I was down thorough." I took the thermos. The coffee inside tasted like it had been brewed sometime during the previous administration, which meant Suri had made it, which meant I drank it anyway. "Run the photos to the tablet. Don't look at them."

She looked at them immediately, because Suri Onda has never once in her life obeyed an instruction that began with *don't*. I watched her face while she scrolled. She was twenty-six, built like a capstan, with a shaved head she claimed was hydrodynamic and I suspected was stubbornness. She'd come up from the coastal yards three seasons ago, and in that time I'd learned two things about her: she could fix anything with a hammer and an opinion, and she was incapable of lying except about small things, like whose turn it was to pump the bilge.

"That's not right," she said finally.

"No."

"These are—" She turned the tablet toward me, zoomed on the plasma lines. "Somebody cut into a navy-sealed hull. Somebody with commercial gear."

"Somebody with commercial gear and a lot of nerve."

"When?"

"Years ago. The corrosion says four, five. Maybe more."

Suri set the tablet down on the chart table with exaggerated care, the way you put down something that might bite. "So the navy's secret isn't a secret."

"The navy's secret has been shopped around," I said. "Or robbed. Or both. And whoever did it didn't tell Kade, because if Kade knew there was already a way in, she wouldn't have needed me."

"Unless she knows and wanted you to find it fresh."

I looked at her. She shrugged, entirely unbothered by the conspiracy she'd just invented.

"Kade doesn't play games that cost money," I said. "Games are for people who can afford them. But somebody played one here." I pulled the chart of the gorge across the table and studied the channel below us, the way it doglegged around a rock spine the locals called the Deacon's Finger. "Here's what matters. Those cuts are on the starboard side, facing open water. Anyone coming up the channel with a sonar rig and half an education will find them in an afternoon. We need to make sure 'anyone' isn't here for another five tides."

"And how do we arrange that?"

"We lie," I said. "To the river, mostly. Come on."

* * *

We spent the rest of the flood working the deception, and I'll grant it was Suri's idea in the end, because I'm generous with credit when the credit is deserved and she'd deny it anyway.

The trick was simple: make the wreck look found from the wrong direction. We ran the skiff down-channel with a grapple and a marker buoy and spent two noisy hours dragging the hook across the silt slope on the *port* side, the buried side, the side with nothing on it but mud and disappointment. We dropped a second buoy there. We chipped paint off the skiff's gunwale and left it on rocks near the false site. Suri even cracked a spare dive cylinder valve so it hissed down on the bottom like a dying man's last breath, which was theatrical, and I told her so, and she said theater was cheaper than gunfire.

"If they come up that channel," she said, hauling the grapple in, "they'll see fresh marks on the wrong side of the wreck and waste their first tide digging mud."

"And if they come up the other channel?"

"Then we're swimming."

It was good work, honest work, the kind of work Doss Harrow would have called *a day's thinking*, and thinking about Doss put a taste in my mouth like river silt. He'd taught me the grapple drag, years ago, on a different river, with different lies. I pushed him out of my head and checked the tide board instead. Three hours of ebb left. Plenty of time to be paranoid in comfort.

Paranoia, as it turned out, didn't need the invitation.

* * *

Suri saw them first. She had the eyes for it—yard-trained, always scanning horizons for things that shouldn't move.

"Smoke," she said, from the bow. "Downstream. Past the Finger."

I came up beside her and looked where she pointed, and there it was: a smudge of diesel haze hanging over the water where the gorge bent, low and gray, the kind of smoke a working vessel makes when it's pushing hard against current. Then the shape under it—a fleet tender, heavy-built, riding deep. And behind it, in echelon, two more.

I knew those silhouettes. I'd drunk coffee on the deck of the lead ship two weeks ago while Vessna Kade explained, in her flat, patient way, exactly how much triple pay bought and exactly how little of her patience it bought with it. The *Ledger's Mercy*. Fleet flagship. Sixty meters of salvaged ambition with a crane arm like a gallows.

"They're early," Suri said.

"They're not early. They let us lead." I felt it settle over me, cold and familiar, the old diver's certainty that the surface world was lying to you. "We run ahead, we mark the channel, we take the risk, and they follow the wake. That was the deal I thought I signed. Apparently the deal included an escort I didn't ask for."

"Maybe they're just anchoring for the night."

"At the mouth of the gorge? With the light going?" I watched the tenders grow. They weren't slowing. They were coming up the channel like they owned it, and maybe they did, and maybe the paper I'd signed in Kade's cabin was worth exactly the ink it was printed with. "Get the skiff secured. Kill the generator. I want us quiet and dark in twenty minutes."

"What about the buoys?"

"What about them?"

"The real ones." She nodded toward the marker we'd left over the actual starboard-side cuts, the one piece of evidence that we'd found the true entry. It bobbed out there in the failing light, orange and cheerful and damning. "If they've got night optics—"

"They'll see it." My stomach did something slow and unpleasant. "Can we move it?"

"Not quietly. Not before they round the Finger."

"Then we don't move it." I stared at the buoy, at the tenders, at the arithmetic closing around us like a fist. "We make it boring. Buoys are everywhere on this river—half of them are navy leftovers that haven't been serviced since the war. If ours looks abandoned, it's scenery. If it looks maintained, it's a confession."

Suri was already moving, because that was the other thing about her: she argued with her hands. She came back from the locker with a bucket of rust-stained grease and a handful of dead gull feathers she'd collected off the foredeck for reasons I'd never asked about, and together we made our own marker look like ten years of neglect. Grease in the seams. Feathers snagged on the ring. A gash in the float skin, patched badly with tape gone gray. She even climbed partway up the mooring line and hung off it until the weed and silt smeared convincingly.

"There," she said, when we'd hauled clear. "One derelict buoy, owner unknown, probably a hazard to navigation, definitely not worth boarding."

"And if they board it anyway?"

"Then we're swimming," she said again, and this time neither of us laughed.

* * *

We doused the lights and sat in the dark of the wheelhouse while the *Ledger's Mercy* came up the channel.

I'll say this for Vessna Kade: she handled her ship beautifully. The tender came around the Deacon's Finger with barely a wash, engines throttled down to a murmur, and slid past our anchorage close enough that I could see lamplight moving behind her wheelhouse glass. Men on her foredeck. A crane crew coiling wire. Someone with binoculars sweeping the banks—and, for one long breath, sweeping us.

We were a small expedition boat at anchor with no lights, which in a working gorge at dusk was either invisible or suspicious, depending entirely on what the man with the binoculars was looking for. The beam crossed our wheelhouse. Held. Moved on.

"She saw us," Suri whispered.

"Everyone sees us. Nobody sees anything until someone tells them to." I kept my voice flat. "Kade knows where we are. She put us here. The question is whether she knows what we know."

"About the cuts?"

"About everything. About why she really wants this wreck. About what's in her hold besides dive gear." I thought about the survey team that had stopped answering its radio, about the voice on that last transmission that I still hadn't said out loud to anyone, because saying it would make it a fact and facts have weight and I was carrying enough. "Twelve years ago somebody sold my brother's expedition to the river. I used to think it was the navy being the navy. Now I think the navy was just the biggest buyer in the market."

Suri was quiet for a while. Through the glass, the *Mercy*'s anchor chain rattled out astern of us, half a mile down-channel, squarely between us and the sea. She'd anchored across our exit. Whether that was caution or a door closing, I couldn't yet say, and the not-saying was its own kind of answer.

"So what do we do?" Suri asked. "Five tides left. They're sitting on our front porch. You want to dive tomorrow anyway?"

"I want to dive tonight."

Her head came around. "The ebb's almost done. Slack's in forty minutes, then it turns and runs hard all night. Night diving in a gorge current, alone—"

"Not alone. You're coming."

"I'm a deckhand."

"You're a deckhand who fixed a compressor with a hammer and an opinion, and you're the only person on this boat I'm sure isn't selling information to anybody." I held her eyes. "Here's the truth, Suri, since we're apparently trading in it. Those cuts on the hull mean someone got inside years ago. Which means whatever the navy sealed in that ship, someone else already knows about it—and they didn't tell Kade, and Kade didn't tell me, and everyone in this river is lying to everyone else at market rates. I am not spending five tides waiting politely in line. Tonight, on the slack, we go down through the third cut—the small one, aft—and we find out what's worth all this lying."

"It's a corvette," Suri said. "Corvettes don't have treasure."

"Corvettes have records. Compartments. Safes." I looked out at the black water where the wreck lay under forty-one meters of river, patient as a held breath. "And somewhere in her, twelve years ago, my brother's expedition ended. Whatever's in that hold, it's the same thing that killed them. I'm done letting other people decide what that was."

Suri looked at me for a long moment. Then she started checking tanks, because arguing with her hands was how she said yes.

Behind us, down-channel, a launch detached itself from the *Ledger's Mercy* and began moving upriver through the dark—slow, unlit, and heading directly for the derelict buoy that wasn't supposed to be interesting to anyone.
