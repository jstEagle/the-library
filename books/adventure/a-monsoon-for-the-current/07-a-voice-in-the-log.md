## 7. A Voice in the Log

The hold of the *Corvane* was a drowned cathedral.

That's the only honest way to describe it. My headlamp swept across cargo cradles the size of church pews, ribs of steel arcing overhead into blackness, and silt hanging in the water like incense that had never quite finished burning. Twelve years of river had settled through every seam and turned the interior into a slow-motion snowfall that never touched ground—or rather, never finished touching it. Everything down here was mid-fall, forever.

I checked my gauges out of habit more than need. Forty-one meters. Bottom time twenty-two minutes. Water temperature a number I preferred not to read twice, because reading it twice made it feel like an opinion I could argue with.

"Talk to me, Mara." Suri's voice came through the comm line, thin and tinny, stretched over the hardline reel like thread pulled too tight. She'd insisted on running comms from the entry point rather than topside—said she didn't trust the navy's old cable splices, and honestly, neither did I. But her voice was the difference between diving a wreck and being buried in one, so I took it where I could get it.

"I'm in," I said. "Main hold. Visibility about three meters and falling."

"Falling how?"

"The way everything falls around here. Slowly, and without apology."

A pause on the line. Suri had learned my humor the way you learn a dog's moods—not by enjoying them, exactly, but by respecting what they meant. When I got funny, I was fine. When I went quiet, that was when to worry.

The hold plan I'd built from the exterior survey said the crew quarters were aft and above, behind a bulkhead door that should have been dogged shut when the ship went down. But ships don't die politely. The *Corvane* had gone down stern-first, according to the navy's sealed report—the one document anyone would admit existed—and the flooding sequence would have driven air pockets forward and up. Which meant anything the crew had been carrying, holding, or trying to save in their last minutes would have drifted toward the bow. Toward me.

I swam the center aisle with my light low, reading the silt like handwriting. That's a skill nobody teaches in certification courses; you learn it from wrecks themselves. Fresh disturbance looks different from old. Old silt lies down like it's tired. New silt stands up on edge, confused, still deciding which way to settle.

There were tracks in the hold.

Not animal. Not current. Boot-fins, wide gauge, commercial pattern—the kind Kade's fleet used, the kind half the coast used, if we're being fair. But they crossed the main aisle in a purposeful line toward the aft bulkhead, and they were old enough to be furred over with the fine gray bloom of years, and new enough that my stomach dropped anyway.

Someone had been here since the cuts on the hull. Someone who knew what they wanted and where to look for it.

"Mara," Suri said. "Your breathing rate just spiked."

"You can hear that?"

"I can hear everything. It's the whole job."

"Then hear this: there's been visitors."

"How long ago?"

"Ask me again when I'm not standing in their footprints."

* * *

The bulkhead door stood open.

Not broken—open. Dogging levers thrown back clean, hinges working, the whole mechanism oiled recently enough that there was no corrosion bloom on the moving parts. Somebody had come through here with tools and patience, and patience is the thing that scared me most. Wreck divers with greed move fast. Wreck divers with something to hide move slowly.

Beyond the bulkhead, the corridor ran aft into darkness my lamp couldn't finish. The deck pitched maybe fifteen degrees, and the overhead had partially collapsed at the far end—a shelf of plating and pipe that had come down sometime in the last decade and now blocked the passage like a fist closed around the throat of the ship.

But before the collapse, along the port side, the cabin doors stood in a row. Officers' country. And one of them—third from the forward end—was different from the others. The others were swollen shut with twelve years of water and rust. This one had been opened, and then, deliberately, wedged. A length of pipe braced from deck to handle, keeping it from swinging closed.

Somebody had wanted to come back to this room.

I hovered at the threshold and let myself feel the thing I'd been not-feeling since the hull cuts. Hope. Ugly, treacherous hope, the kind that gets divers killed because it makes them stay past their bottom time, makes them push past their reserves, makes them believe that whatever they're looking for is worth becoming part of the wreck themselves. Every salvage man I knew had a story about someone who died of hope. Usually the story ended with "and he was right, there was treasure down there"—which somehow made it worse.

"Bottom time thirty," Suri said. "You've got maybe twenty minutes of working margin before I start lying to you about your air."

"Lying how?"

"Generously."

I went through the door.

* * *

The cabin had belonged to somebody junior. Bunk, locker, fold-down desk bolted to the wall—the desk still folded down, which told me the last person to use it hadn't bothered to stow for heavy weather, or hadn't had time. A chair hung from its tether, turning very slightly in the microcurrents, patient as a metronome.

The desk drawers had been searched. I could tell because they were open, and because whoever had done the searching had left things behind—clothing buttons, a comb, a tin cup—all furred gray, all arranged by nothing, scattered by the small physics of flooded rooms. The searchers had taken what they wanted and left the rest to the river.

Except.

Under the desk lip, wedged into the junction where the fold-down hinge met the frame, there was a shape my light almost skipped over. Almost. But it was the wrong kind of wrong—too rectangular, too deliberate—and I'd spent enough years reading wrecks to know that the river doesn't make rectangles.

I worked it free. It came out stiff, reluctant, trailing a ghost of sediment.

A waterproof case. Navy issue, the old pattern, rubberized canvas with a zip seal and a pressure clasp. The kind they issued for charts and documents that were supposed to survive exactly this. The clasp was green with age but the seal looked intact, and my hands—gloved, clumsy, suddenly shaking in a way I resented deeply—took three tries to work the latch.

Inside: a logbook.

Standard ship's auxiliary log, waterproof stock, the pages stiff with their own coating. And on the cover, in grease pencil, faded but legible, a name written in a hand I would have known blindfolded, underwater, dead, or dreaming.

*E. Reyes.*

My brother's name. My brother's handwriting. The particular slant of his E's, the way he crossed his t's low and fast like he was always in a hurry to get to the next word.

The river roared in my ears, except rivers don't roar at forty-one meters—they whisper, they press, they murmur against your hood like something trying to get your attention politely before it stops being polite. My regulator hissed. My own breath sounded like a stranger's.

"Mara." Suri's voice, careful now. "Heart rate's through the roof. Talk."

I couldn't talk. Talking required language and language required believing in time, and time had just come apart in my hands.

Because here was the arithmetic that broke me: the *Corvane*'s crew had died twelve years ago. Officially. Certifiably. There had been a memorial service—I'd stood at it, seventeen years old, in a borrowed jacket, while men in uniform said words like *lost with all hands* and *the sea keeps what it takes*. Elian Reyes, diver second class, lost with all hands. The date was carved into a stone in the harbor cemetery, and I had measured every mile I'd ever traveled against that stone.

I got the book open under my lamp with fingers that wouldn't obey.

Waterproof stock doesn't blur. That's the whole point of it. The ink sat on the page crisp and immediate, as fresh as yesterday, and the first entry I found was dated—

Eight days after the death date.

Eight days after my brother had been declared dead, someone with his handwriting had written, in the flat procedural voice of a man logging routine observations:

*Day 9 post-incident. Air recycler holding. Morale poor but stable. V. says the patrol boats aren't looking. V. says nobody is looking.*

V. Vessna. Vessna Kade.

The vertigo hit me like a blow to the inner ear—that sick tilting sensation divers call the leans, except mine wasn't in my balance, it was in my history. Because either my brother had survived the sinking and lived hidden in this wreck, writing entries while the world mourned him—

Or someone had kept him alive long enough to write them, and then decided he shouldn't anymore.

And either way, Vessna Kade had known.

* * *

I did the stupid thing. Of course I did. I'm telling you this honestly because the rest of the story won't make sense otherwise: I stayed past my margin. Suri started lying to me about my air, generously, exactly as promised, and I ignored her, because the logbook was talking and I had spent twelve years learning to listen for exactly this voice.

I couldn't read everything—not down there, not with my hands shaking and my light guttering and my brain refusing to process text at any speed. So I did the professional thing, finally, the one piece of competence I could still locate: I sealed the case, clipped it inside my harness against my chest, and started up the line.

The entries I'd glimpsed kept unspooling behind my eyes on the ascent. *Day 11. Rations divided four ways instead of six.* Four ways instead of six. Six men had gone into that air pocket. Two had stopped needing rations. *Day 14. V. promised extraction before the ice. V. promised.* The repetition of that name, day after day, promise after promise, like a prayer wearing itself smooth.

*Day 17. No boat. Doss says wait. Doss always says wait.*

Doss Harrow. My mentor. The man who taught me to read silt, to respect the water, to measure a life in miles. In my brother's log, waiting.

I broke the surface in the black pool of the entry chamber, and Suri's face above me, lit by her helmet lamp, went through three expressions in two seconds—relief, fury, and then a third thing she didn't have a word for yet.

"You're eleven minutes past margin," she said.

"I found something."

"I can see that. You're hugging it."

I looked down. I was, in fact, holding the case against my chest with both arms, the way you hold a child, or a bomb.

"Suri." My voice came out wrecked, and I let it. "This is my brother's log. He was alive in here. After they buried him. He was *alive*, and somebody knew, and—" I stopped, because the next sentence was the one I couldn't say yet, the one that would make it real: *and the woman paying me triple rates might be the reason he isn't.*

Suri crouched at the pool's edge and put her hand on the case, over my hands. She didn't try to take it. She just held on, briefly, the way you steady a man on a ladder.

"Surface interval," she said. "Sixty minutes minimum. Then you tell me everything, in order, with names. And Mara?"

"Yeah."

"When we go back down—we do it smart. Whatever's in that book, it already killed people once. It doesn't get to kill you too."

Above us, faint through the rock and the water, I heard it and felt it before I understood it: engines. More than one set. Deep-throated, working-hard engines, the sound of a fleet making way against the current.

Kade's competitors had found the river.

Or Kade had lied about how much time we had.

Either way, the tide was coming in, and I had eight days of my brother's life in a waterproof case pressed against my heart, and every single one of those days had a name in the margins that I knew personally.

The first of them was Vessna Kade.

The second was Doss Harrow.
