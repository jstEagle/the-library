## 8. Second Tide, Rising Cost

I have buried four divers in my career as a fleet captain, and I have learned that grief at sea is a logistics problem. You can't stop the work — the dead don't pay for fuel, and the living still eat — so you fold the mourning into the schedule like any other cargo. You say a few words over the rail, you log the time and position, you pour something decent into the water if you have something decent aboard, and then you turn back to the manifest.

I was twenty-six when I folded my first one in. His name was Teodor Brac, and he died of a bad valve on a routine inspection dive off the Sable Keys, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that has never left me: *this is what it costs to be the one who signs the papers.* Everyone else gets to feel the loss. The captain gets to feel the arithmetic underneath it.

His name was Pell Omari, this time. Twenty-nine years old. Good lungs, better hands, a laugh that carried across two decks. He'd been with my fleet three seasons, and he had a girl in Corren Harbor who thought he worked honest wrecks, which is to say he'd told her nothing true about anything, the way divers do.

And he died in a corridor that Mara Reyes knew was collapsed, because I am now certain of it — certain the way you're certain about a knife you find in your own back, by the shape of the wound.

* * *

Let me walk it backward, because that's how I've been walking through everything since they brought him up.

Second tide. We'd anchored the *Kestrel's Debt* below the wreck site overnight, running dark, no lights except the working lamps on the dive deck, because Reyes's little expedition boat had gone quiet upstream of us and I didn't trust quiet. Quiet on a river means one of two things: either nobody's there, or somebody doesn't want to be found. On a river with a navy-sealed wreck sitting in it, I'd have bet my hull on the second.

My plan for the day was simple and, I want that on the record, simple in the way good plans are simple. Two teams. First team — Pell and his swim partner, a broad silent man called Jute — goes in through the breach Reyes used yesterday, maps the forward hold, tags anything worth lifting. Second team stands by on the line. Four hours bottom time, hard ceiling, everyone back before slack water turns against us.

Reyes came aboard at first light. That should have been my first warning — a man who won't share a campfire sharing his morning coffee. He stood at my chart table with his hands wrapped around the mug like it was the only warm thing in the estuary, and he said, "The interior's shifted since my dive. The corridor past the breach is unstable. If you're going in, go slow, probe everything, and don't trust the deck plating aft of frame forty."

That's what he said. And here is what he didn't say, and what I have turned over maybe nine hundred times since: he didn't say *the corridor's collapsed.*

He said *unstable.* He said *shifted.* A man who spends his life underwater chooses his words like he chooses his weights — exactly, or not at all. And "unstable" is a word that says *be careful,* and "collapsed" is a word that says *there is no way through, and if your man goes in anyway, he will die in the dark with silt in his regulator and thirty meters of twisted steel between him and the sky.*

I asked him directly. I want that on the record too, because I know how these stories get told afterward, and I will not be remembered as a captain who sent a man into a hole without asking. I said: "Reyes. Is there a way through that corridor or isn't there?"

He looked at me over the rim of the mug. He has one of those faces that gives up nothing — river-brown, scarred along the jaw, eyes the flat gray of water under cloud — and he looked at me for a long three seconds and said, "Probe everything."

Not yes. Not no. *Probe everything.*

And I took it, God help me, I took it as an answer. Because I wanted the wreck more than I wanted certainty, and because a part of me — the part that grew up poor on a salvage scow watching other captains take the rich marks — read his evasiveness as hoarding. As a man protecting his claim. I thought: *he found something in there yesterday and he doesn't want us following his line to it.* So I told Pell to map the forward hold and treat the aft corridor as secondary, and I put extra probes on the gear list, and I felt clever about it.

Pell went down at the top of the tide, happy as a man going to market. I heard him on the wire — we run hardline comms on interior dives, always, no exceptions — and his voice was bright and easy, calling out depths and headings, and Jute answering in his half-words, and for forty minutes it was the most ordinary dive of the season.

Then it wasn't.

* * *

You learn to hear a dive go wrong before anyone says so. The rhythm changes. Divers talk in a kind of music — call, answer, call, answer — and when one voice starts arriving late, or arrives too fast, or stops arriving, every ear on the deck hears it even if nobody names it. At minute forty-three, Pell's voice stopped arriving.

Jute's came instead, clipped and wrong: "Kade. Kade. Silt event, aft corridor. Pell went in. Line went slack. Kade."

Line went slack. Three words that mean the tether is no longer attached to a living man pulling against it, or attached to anything at all.

We ran the standby team in six minutes after that, which is fast — I'll say that for my people, we are fast — and it didn't matter. It never matters. That's the joke the sea tells, over and over, century after century, and every diver laughs at it right up until the punchline lands on them. Six minutes. In clear tropical water, six minutes is survivable. In a flooded steel corridor full of displaced silt, at depth, in current, six minutes is a geological age.

They found him at the collapse itself. The corridor didn't just narrow — it ended, a pancaked section of hull plating and framing crushed down to a gap you couldn't pass a body through, and the gap was packed with the fine gray silt that lives inside old wrecks like smoke lives in a burned house. He'd gone in headfirst, probing, exactly as trained, and the plating above him had let go — whether from his touch or from the current working it, no one will ever say — and it came down on him and drove him into the floor and pinned him in the dark, and the silt closed over the space like a hand closing over a candle.

Jute got one arm through. Got a grip on Pell's harness. Pulled until his own shoulder tore, and pulled, and pulled, and the wreck did not care. That's the thing about wrecks — people think of them as dead ships, but they're not dead, they're just slow. They move the way glaciers move, the way grief moves, settling and shifting and closing, and they have all the patience in the world and none of the mercy.

We got Pell out at minute fifty-one. The fleet surgeon worked him on the deck for nineteen minutes, in front of everyone, because hiding it would have been worse. Then we wrapped him in canvas and laid him under the awning aft, and I made the announcement myself, because that's the captain's job, and I watched twenty-two people become twenty-two kinds of broken at once.

* * *

Now. The arithmetic.

Here is what I know, and what I assembled in the hours after, sitting in my cabin with the log open and a bottle I didn't open, because I needed my head cold:

One. Reyes dove this wreck alone, yesterday, through the same breach. He spent nearly two hours inside. A man does not spend two hours inside a wreck without learning its geography. He would have found that corridor blocked within twenty minutes, because the blockage is total — not narrow, not risky, *total*. There is no version of a careful diver reaching that collapse and describing it as "unstable."

Two. When I asked him point-blank, he gave me a phrase instead of an answer. And I let him, because I was greedy.

Three. This morning, while my teams were rigging, one of my spotters saw movement on Reyes's boat — the young deckhand, the girl, Suri, hauling something small and dark aboard from their tender, quick, like contraband. I don't know what it was. But I know what it looked like.

So the story writes itself, doesn't it? The rival salvager finds the wreck first, finds the way in, finds whatever's worth finding, and then lets the competition walk into a meat grinder while he keeps his mouth shut and his hands clean. It's an old story. It's probably being told right now in the mess of my own ship, in the low voices men use when they think the captain can't hear them but hope she can.

Except.

Except that I've watched Mara Reyes work for eleven years from the other side of the industry, and I have never once heard a credible story of him cutting corners on another diver's safety. He's ruthless about claims and stingy with information and he lies the way fish swim — naturally, constantly, without apparent effort — but the man has pulled competitors out of bad water at cost to himself twice that I can document. There's a diver in Corren Harbor who owes him a lung. So either I'm wrong about what he knew, or eleven years of reputation is a mask worn by a man patient enough to wait a decade for one perfect murder.

And there's a third possibility, the one I keep circling back to at three in the morning with the river slapping the hull: that he knew the corridor was gone, and he told me in the only language he trusts — indirection — and I heard what my greed wanted to hear. *Unstable. Probe everything.* Maybe that was a warning shouted as clearly as a man like him knows how to shout. Maybe the knife in my back is my own hand.

But here's the thing about guilt, whether it's his or mine: it doesn't matter whose it is. Guilt is ballast. It sits where you put it and it weighs the same either way, and right now I have a dead diver under canvas and a crew that needs somewhere to put the weight, and I am the captain, and the weight goes where I say it goes.

* * *

I went to him at dusk.

His boat — the *Lark*, someone called her, an ugly little expedition tub with a patched rail — sat fifty meters upstream of us, and I crossed in the skiff with Jute beside me, because Jute had asked, in his half-words, to come, and I understood why. Some things need witnesses.

Reyes was on his aft deck mending a lamp cable, of all the domestic things, and he looked up when the skiff bumped his hull, and I'll give him this: he read the situation off my face before I said a word. His hands went still on the cable.

"Pell Omari," I said. "Died at fourteen hundred today. Your corridor."

Something moved behind those flat gray eyes — I saw it, some flicker, though I couldn't tell you if it was guilt or grief or just the reflex of a man doing sums. "The aft corridor," he said. "Past frame forty."

"You told me it was unstable."

"I told you—"

"You told me to probe everything." I stepped onto his deck without invitation, because invitation had left the conversation somewhere around the words *your corridor.* "A man who spends his life underwater chooses his words like weights, Reyes. You know that better than anyone. Unstable isn't collapsed. Unstable is a caution. Collapsed is a wall. My diver went looking for a caution and found a wall, and the wall fell on him, and he died in the dark with his partner's hand on his harness, close enough to touch, far enough to lose."

The deckhand, Suri, had come up from below and stood at the companionway, watchful, a wrench in one hand like she'd planned to be holding it. The other one — Venn, the dispatcher, soft-bodied and sharp-eyed — hovered behind her.

Reyes set down the cable. When he spoke, his voice was level, almost gentle, which enraged me more than shouting would have. "Captain Kade. I have watched this trade kill people my whole life, and I have learned that the sea doesn't take sides and neither do wrecks. But I will say this once." He stepped closer. Close enough that I could see the salt dried white in the seams of his jacket. "If I had known a man of yours would die in that corridor, I would have chained myself to your anchor chain rather than let the dive happen. Whatever else you believe about me — and you're welcome to believe the worst, everyone eventually does — believe that."

"And what did you know?"

"I knew the corridor was bad." No flinch. "I didn't know it was fatal. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole ocean."

"There's a difference," I agreed, "and my diver is on the wrong side of it."

For a moment neither of us spoke, and the river filled the silence, slapping both our hulls impartially, and I hated it — hated the water, hated the wreck sitting fat and secret under it, hated the whole gray economy of salvage that turns dead men into line items.

"Your fleet stays off the wreck," I said finally. "Until I decide otherwise. That's not a negotiation."

"It's also not yours to order."

"My diver is dead in your wreck, Reyes. As of this afternoon, everything about that wreck is mine to order."

He held my stare, and I watched him weigh something — I could actually see the scales moving, this man who shows nothing, showing the weighing — and then he nodded, once, slow. "Off the wreck," he said. "Until you decide otherwise."

It was too easy. I want that on the record too. A man who fights for every scrap of advantage folding that fast means he's already gotten what he needed from the site, or he knows something about it that makes it worthless to anyone but him, or — and this is the thought I carried back across the water in the dark, Jute silent beside me, Pell's empty bunk waiting aboard —

Or he's protecting something inside it that matters more than the cargo. More than the claim. More, maybe, than a dead man's name.

* * *

We buried Pell at slack water, midnight, because the river allows no other hour. Canvas, chain, the deep spot off the channel marker. I poured my decent bottle into the water and said the words, the old ones, the ones that fit any sea and therefore fit none of them perfectly, and my crew stood along the rail in the lamplight with their hats off, and Jute cried without sound, which was somehow worse than sound.

And then, because I am what the trade made me, I went below and opened the chart table and started planning the next dive.

Because here is the last entry in tonight's ledger, the one I haven't written anywhere yet, the one that's been forming since Reyes's deck: while I stood at Pell's rail, my second officer brought me a signal copy. Our downstream picket boat, the one watching the channel approaches, logged a contact at dusk. Gray hull. Naval lines. Running dark, riding high, no registry broadcast.

A patrol vessel, anchored eight miles downstream of us, in the middle of nowhere, at the exact moment a navy-sealed wreck started giving up its secrets.

Reyes thinks this is a story about salvage. Let him. I've been in this trade long enough to know that when the navy shows up uninvited, the wreck isn't the treasure — the wreck is the evidence. And somebody, somewhere upriver of all of us, has decided that evidence is worth more than any of our lives.

Including, I suspect, the life of whoever they sent to make sure we never reach it.

Tomorrow, third tide. And I am done diving blind.
