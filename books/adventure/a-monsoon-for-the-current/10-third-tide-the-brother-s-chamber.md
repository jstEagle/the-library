## 10. Third Tide: The Brother's Chamber

The third tide came in gray and mean, the way tides do when they've decided not to be your friend anymore.

I'd slept maybe two hours in the bunk Suri had bullied me into taking, and I dreamed of my brother's hands. Not his face—his hands, turning a wrench, tapping out some rhythm on a rail, the way he did when he was thinking. In the dream he was trying to tell me something with those taps and I couldn't read it, which was accurate, because I hadn't been able to read anything he tried to tell me for twelve years, including the last time, when he said *come with us, Mara* and I said *next season* and next season turned out to be a word that only applied to people who were still around to have one.

So I woke up angry, which is a kind of fuel if you're careful with it, and a kind of poison if you're not. I made coffee on the little burner and drank it standing at the rail watching the gorge walls drip meltwater, and I told myself the same thing I'd been telling myself since the logbook: *get the rest of it first. Feel it later. Feeling it now gets you killed down there.*

Suri came up rubbing her eyes. "Tide's turning in forty minutes."

"I know."

"You've got your line rigged?"

"I know, Suri."

"And your reserve bottle's full, and you checked the regulator twice, because last time—"

"*I know.*" Then, because she was right and we both knew she was right and being sharp with her about it was just me being sharp with myself by proxy: "Thank you. For checking."

She nodded like a woman accepting tribute from a difficult king, and went forward to watch the water.

* * *

The wreck sat in its notch of riverbed like a thing the mountain had swallowed and never digested. Two tides ago I'd mapped her outer skin and found the salvage cuts—clean, modern, professional work done years before anyone official admitted anyone had been here. One tide ago I'd gone through the gash in the hold and found the logbook in a dead man's coat pocket, in my brother's handwriting, dated three weeks after the date the navy had carved onto his memorial stone. Three weeks after they told us there was nothing left to find and no reason to look.

Today I was going deeper. Today I was going to the cabin.

Kade stood at the rail with her arms crossed, watching me suit up. She hadn't slept either—I could see it in the seams around her eyes—but she wore her tiredness like armor instead of admitting it was a wound. "You come up with anything less than everything," she said, "and this whole run was a waste of six divers' nerve."

"Comforting. Thank you."

"I'm not in the comfort business." She handed me the slate herself, which was as close as she got to tenderness. "Third tide. Currents turn at slack plus ninety. You're back on this deck or you're part of the wreck. Understood?"

"Loud and clear."

"Mara." She caught my arm before I stepped off. Her grip was stronger than it looked, which is true of most dangerous things. "Whatever's in that cabin—it's been down there twelve years. It'll keep another ten minutes while you think. Don't let it rush you. The men who put it there wanted it rushed."

That was the closest thing to wisdom anyone had offered me on this whole river, and it came from a woman who'd spent the last two days threatening to bill me for air. I nodded, and went over the side.

* * *

The water took me the way it always does—cold first, then pressure, then quiet. Down here the river stopped being a river and became a country, dim and green-brown, lit in shafts where the surface cracked enough light through to make moving shadows on the hull. My lamp cut a cone through the murk and the murk pushed back, patient, indifferent. Twelve years of silt hung suspended like the dust in an abandoned house.

I went in through the salvage cut. Of course I did—the people who cut it had known exactly where to cut, and I'd stopped pretending I didn't understand what that meant. Somebody had come here with charts. Somebody had come here with permission. Navies don't hand out permission; they sell it, quietly, to whoever asks the right way, and then they seal the file and call the whole thing closed.

Past the cut, the corridor narrowed. Past the corridor, the stairwell down. And past the stairwell, the passage I'd marked on my slate two tides ago and hadn't been able to make myself enter yet: the officers' country, where the cabins lined up like teeth.

My brother wouldn't have been quartered there. He was crew—a diver, a wrench-hand, a man whose name would appear in a manifest and nowhere else. But manifests get filed, and files get moved, and papers travel toward whatever cabin holds the man who signs for them. If there was a record of what happened to the *Corriander*'s cargo—and there was, because cargo doesn't vanish, it changes hands—if there was a record, it would be in the captain's cabin or the purser's locker, and both of those were down here behind a door that had been dogged shut from the inside.

Dogged shut from the inside. I want you to sit with that the way I had to sit with it, hanging in the dark with my lamp shaking slightly because my hands were shaking slightly. A door dogged shut from the inside means someone chose to close it. Someone stood on this side of it, in rising water, and spun the handle home, and either they were sealing something precious in—or sealing themselves in, because outside was worse.

The corridor beyond the dogged door had partially collapsed. Not recently—the collapse was old, the steel rusted soft at the joints, furred with twelve years of river growth. I could see why the navy's survey team had stopped answering their radio, if they'd gotten this far. This wasn't a place you entered so much as a place you negotiated with.

I went in sideways, tank-first, the way you do, exhaling slow to keep my buoyancy honest. The passage was barely wide enough for me and my bottle together, and every meter of it I thought about the arithmetic of entrapment: how long a bottle lasts at panic breathing rates versus calm ones, how far it is back to the cut, how many times I'd promised Suri I'd come back up. Panic is just math done badly. I did the math well. Mostly.

The cabin door was steel, painted once, the paint gone to blister and bloom. The stencil above it had survived better than the paint: *PURSER.* Below that, smaller, someone had chalked—chalk, actual chalk, which meant someone had stood here in air, in dry clothes, within the last decade—two tally marks and a date I couldn't quite read through the silt film.

I wiped the porthole glass with my glove. Behind it, the cabin was black. But the door itself—

The door was dogged shut, but the dogs were old, and the frame had racked when the ship settled. There was a gap at the bottom, four fingers wide, where the deck had warped away from the wall. I lay horizontal in the corridor, cheek against the cold steel, and put my lamp into the gap.

Papers. Dozens of them, floating in the trapped air pocket like pale fish, turning slowly whenever the current breathed through the gap. A strongbox, open, empty. And a ledger—bound, oilcloth-wrapped, wedged under the frame of the bunk where somebody had put it deliberately, the way you put things you intend to come back for.

Somebody had intended to come back.

* * *

Getting the door open without bringing the corridor down on my head took eleven minutes that felt like a career. I worked the dogs with a pry bar, easing each one, listening to the hull groan and complain, waiting between movements for the sediment to settle so I could see what I'd disturbed. Twice I backed out entirely and hovered in the stairwell while the passage shed a skin of silt and rust flakes. Kade's voice was in my head the whole time: *it'll keep another ten minutes while you think.*

It kept. I thought. I went back in.

On the fourth dog, the door swung inward all at once, and the trapped air pocket exhaled through the gap in a long silver belch of bubbles, and the papers swirled, and for one stupid second I thought: *he's in there, he's going to swim out.* Twelve years, and some animal part of my brain still expected my brother to walk out of any dark opening like he'd just been waiting for me to show up and stop being late.

The cabin was empty. The cabin had always been going to be empty. I went in anyway.

The ledger was where the light had shown it, wedged under the bunk frame, oilcloth tied with wire. I brought it out into the open water and unwrapped it there, because if the river wanted it back, I wanted the river to know I'd be watching. Waterproof paper. Navy stock, the good kind, the kind that costs more per page than a deckhand makes in a week. The ink had held.

The first pages were inventory. Ordinary. Crates numbered, weights recorded, signatures at the bottom of each page. I almost skimmed them—but I've learned, mostly the hard way, that the devil in naval paperwork lives in the margins, in the small annotations, in the initials where initials shouldn't be. So I read every page, holding the ledger in the cone of my lamp, gloved fingers careful as a surgeon's.

Page nine. That's where it started.

*Crates 41–88 offloaded at secondary anchorage, night of the 14th. No manifest entry. Receiving party civilian-contracted, three lighters, no running lights. Cargo transferred under direction of Cdr. ——— (name blotted, but the rank was clear). Crew informed cargo is "sensitive ordnance, restricted handling." Crew NOT informed of destination.*

Offloaded at a secondary anchorage. By lighters with no lights. While the crew—my brother among them—was told it was ordnance too sensitive to log.

I turned the page and my heart was doing something I didn't authorize.

*14th, continued. Cdr. ——— instructs that offload occur during crew mess to minimize witnesses. Diver R. Reyes questions absence of manifest entry. Told to log hours only, contents not his concern.*

*R. Reyes.*

Rowan. Rowan Reyes, who tapped rhythms on rails when he was thinking, who asked questions even when—especially when—he'd been told not to. They'd written him down. Somewhere in a sealed file, in a cabin at the bottom of a river, my brother existed as a problem to be managed: *questions absence of manifest entry.*

Keep reading. Get the rest of it first. Feel it later.

*17th. Storm building NE. Cdr. ——— orders remaining cargo moved despite weather. Second lighter swamps at the anchorage; two tons lost overboard. Cdr. ——— declines to report loss. States loss will be covered by "final disposition of vessel."*

Final disposition of vessel. I read that phrase four times before it finished detonating. Final disposition. As in: the ship herself. As in: the rest of the cargo, the crates still aboard, the whole remaining weight of it—written off. Not lost. Written off. You write off a thing you expect to cease existing.

And a ship ceases existing with her crew aboard.

*19th. Vessel ordered to transit the gorge ahead of weather window. Pilot advises against. Order stands. Crew complement: twenty-two.*

Twenty-two. I knew the number. I'd grown up knowing the number, the way you grow up knowing a scar's shape. Twenty-two names on the stone, twenty-two families paid out of a fund that officially didn't exist, twenty-two men explained to the world as weather. And here it was in advance, in ink, in a purser's hand: *pilot advises against. Order stands.* They sent the ship into the gorge in a storm on purpose, with the cargo already ashore and sold, because a wreck needed no explaining and a survivor might.

The last entry was dated the morning of the loss. Just four lines.

*Crew mustered. Morale poor. R. Reyes again raised question of manifest. Confined to quarters for remainder of transit.*

Confined to quarters. He'd been locked in—locked in, on a ship being steered into a storm on purpose—and when the water came, he hadn't even had a corridor to run down. He'd died in a box, asking the right question, and the answer had been a door.

I don't remember deciding to be angry. Anger isn't a decision; it's a tide. It came up through me the way the real tide comes up the gorge, filling every low place, lifting loose things off the bottom. I hung there in that drowned cabin with a dead man's ledger in my hands and I was furious in a way I hadn't known I could still be—furious at the commander with the blotted name, furious at the fund that paid the families hush money dressed as sympathy, furious at myself for twelve years of *next season*, furious at the whole gray machinery that had taken twenty-two men and filed them under *weather*.

But rage underwater is a luxury. Rage spends air. I made myself do the last thing, the important thing: I folded the ledger flat against my chest, wired it into the mesh bag with the logbook, and I searched the cabin properly. The strongbox was empty except for a receipt stub—civilian buyer, coastal registry, a name I memorized and a number I memorized harder. And taped inside the bunk frame, flat and patient, a single mimeographed sheet: the transfer order. Signed. Countersigned. The countersignature belonged to an office I recognized, because every diver on this coast recognizes it—the office that had issued the notice forbidding approach to this very wreck. The office that had sealed the site. Sealing the evidence of its own signature into the one place it had forbidden anyone to look.

They hadn't hidden the proof. They'd drowned it. Same method, same arithmetic: water needs no explaining.

I clipped the sheet into the bag. I looked around the cabin one last time—at the empty strongbox, the chalk tallies on the door, the bunk where a purser had once lain awake writing down what his superiors would kill twenty-two men to keep unwritten—and I thought: *you did the one brave thing available to you. You wrote it down and you hid it where your own people would never dredge. Good. I'm here to finish the transaction.*

Then the current shifted.

Not gently. The slack was ending early—the melt coming down off the glacier was shoving the schedule around, Suri had said so, everyone had said so, and here it was proving it. The corridor behind me began to breathe hard, silt boiling up in brown ropes, and somewhere aft a section of hull let go with a sound like a giant clearing its throat. The papers still floating in the cabin swirled toward the gap in the door and vanished into the dark, every one I hadn't taken, gone to join the rest of the archive the river was keeping.

I kicked for the corridor. The math was suddenly urgent: distance to the cut, current against me, air at a third. I went through the collapsed passage fast and rough, shoulder kissing steel, and the wreck groaned around me like something waking up annoyed, and I thought with perfect clarity: *she knows I've got it. She wants it back.*

I came up the stairwell into the hold, into the cut, into the green-gray open water, and kicked for the light.

* * *

Hands hit me at the ladder—Suri's, then Kade's—and they hauled me over the rail like a landed fish, which I was in no condition to resent. I lay on the deck coughing river out of my lungs and laughing, actually laughing, which worried Suri more than the coughing did.

"What," Kade said. "What did you find."

I sat up. The mesh bag was still wired to my harness. I lifted it, water streaming off the oilcloth, and set it on the deck between us with a care that made both of them go still.

"The cargo," I said. "Sold. Before the ship ever came up the gorge. Offloaded at night, no manifest, civilian buyers, receipt signed. And then—" my voice did something I hated, cracked like a boy's, and I made myself finish— "then they sailed her into the storm anyway. On purpose. Twenty-two men, written off as *final disposition*. Rowan questioned the missing manifest twice. The second time they confined him to quarters. He died locked in a cabin, asking the right question."

Nobody spoke. Upstream, faint, the glacier made its sound—that deep grinding groan, ice talking to ice, a sound like the world clearing its throat before it says something terrible.

Kade looked at the bag, then at me, and whatever was behind her eyes had stopped being commerce. "That's not salvage," she said quietly. "That's a case."

"That's a trial," I said. "If we live long enough to hold one."

Which was when Suri, who'd been scanning downstream with the glasses, went rigid and said, in a voice I'd learn to dread: "Mara. You need to see who's on that boat."

I took the glasses. Downriver, rounding the bend into our stretch of gray water, came a familiar fleet shape—and standing at her rail, unmistakable even at this distance, square-shouldered and silver-bearded and wearing the face of every hard lesson I'd ever been taught—

Doss Harrow. Standing beside Vessna Kade's rivals, come up my river, wearing the expression of a man arriving to collect a debt.

The mentor of my whole life, sailing straight at the truth I'd just pulled out of the deep.
