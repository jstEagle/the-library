## 11. Loyalty's Ledger

There are two kinds of men who come back to a river they swore off. The kind who want to prove something, and the kind who owe something. I've been the second kind so long I'd forgotten there was a first.

I stood at the rail of Kade's lead barge as it nosed up the gorge, watching the water go from green to gray to that particular bruised color the river takes on when it runs out of patience with the land. Forty years in the salvage trade and I still couldn't tell you why water does that. I could tell you what it pays, though. That I knew to the cent.

"You're quiet," Kade said beside me.

"I'm old," I said. "Quiet comes free with the package."

She didn't laugh. Vessna Kade never laughed when she was counting, and she was always counting. She had that ledger look — eyes moving over the gorge walls like they were columns of figures, adding up what the ice would cost her and subtracting what Mara Reyes was going to earn her. Triple pay, she'd promised him. I'd been the one who told her he'd take it. I'd been right, which is the most expensive thing a man can be.

"Your boy's already inside," she said.

"He's not my boy."

"No?" She turned the word over like a coin of doubtful weight. "You taught him to dive. You taught him to read current. You taught him—" she paused, savoring it, "—to keep his mouth shut about navy work. Who does that sound like?"

"It sounds like you've been reading my mail."

"I've been reading your ledgers, Doss. For six years." She said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "Every survey line, every sealed notice, every wreck the fleet ever touched before anyone else knew it existed. You think I pay you for charm?"

I looked at the water instead of her. The truth was I didn't know what I thought she paid me for anymore. In the beginning it had been simple: I owed money to men whose collection methods made drowning look merciful, and Kade had information needs, and I had access. A clean transaction, if you didn't look too hard at what was being sold. And what was being sold, mostly, was paper. Survey coordinates. Tide tables. The occasional name of a diver who might be bought or warned off.

Then it stopped being mostly paper.

* * *

Twelve years ago I sat across a table from a naval officer named Corren and listened to him explain, in the flat voice men use when they've rehearsed a sentence until it stops meaning anything, that the *Meridian Star* — the expedition boat that went upriver with Tomas Reyes and nine others aboard — had been lost to ice, that the loss was regrettable, that the matter was closed, and that any man who suggested otherwise would find his licenses closed along with it.

Tomas Reyes. Twenty-six years old, all elbows and certainty, the kind of diver who checked his gear twice not from fear but from respect. I'd taught him alongside his brother. I'd watched them grow from boys hauling lines on other men's decks into the best pair of hands on the coast. When the *Star* went missing, I drank myself stupid for a week and then got up and did what the trade does: I worked. Grief is a luxury in this business. The river doesn't stop charging rent because you're mourning.

What I didn't do was ask questions. That was the transaction Corren offered, though he dressed it as a warning. Silence, and my licenses stayed. Silence, and the debt collectors found their interest rates mysteriously halved. Silence, and I kept working while better men than me stayed drowned.

I told myself for years that I'd been threatened into it. That's the story a man tells himself at night, the one with him standing brave against the dark. The truer story is smaller and uglier: nobody held a gun to my head. They held a ledger. And I looked at the ledger, and I signed.

The worst part — the part I can barely write even in my own head — is that Mara never suspected. Twelve years, and the boy came to me every season, sat in my kitchen, ate my terrible cooking, asked me about tides and wrecks and whether his brother had been afraid at the end. And I'd pour him something strong and say, "No, lad. Ice took them quick. No fear in it." A kindness, I called it. A mercy. It was neither. It was maintenance. You maintain a lie the way you maintain a hull — patch it every season or the sea finds the seam.

And every season, Kade's fleet seemed to know things. Which wrecks were worth chasing. Which notices were real and which were theater. Mara noticed too — he was sharp, always had been — but he filed it under rivalry, under the ordinary graft of the trade. He never filed it under *Doss*. Why would he? I was the one constant. I was the man who'd pulled him out of the water when he froze on his first deep dive, sixteen years old, lungs full of panic. I was the hand on the back of his neck when they buried an empty coffin for Tomas.

That's the thing about love in this trade. It becomes a tool, and then it becomes a habit, and then one day you're using it against the very person it was for, and you can't remember when the exchange rate changed.

* * *

The gorge narrowed around us like a throat deciding whether to swallow. Suri — Kade's deckhand, a wiry girl with rope-scarred palms and no patience for old men — threw me a coil without looking. "Buoys ahead," she said. "Navy ones. Dead as doorposts."

I looked. Warning buoys, the kind the service used to mark restricted channels, rusted down to skeletons, their lights long gone, their chains furred with weed. The navy never maintained them because the navy never expected anyone to come this far. Or because the navy preferred its warnings decorative. Either way, we slid past them in silence, and I felt each one go by like a name read at a funeral.

Kade had brought four boats and thirty-one souls upriver on my information. Six tides, I'd told her. Six tides before calving opens the upper channel and turns this gorge into something no sane captain enters. She'd planned her whole run around my numbers. If I was wrong, thirty-one people would learn about it the hard way, and I'd have another set of names to carry.

"You're doing the face," Suri said, appearing at my elbow with coffee that smelled like it had been brewed twice and resented both times.

"What face?"

"The one where you're sorry about something but haven't decided what yet." She handed me the cup. "Old man wisdom says drink it anyway."

I drank it. It was terrible. There was comfort in that; some things stay honestly bad. "You know Reyes long?" I asked.

"Three seasons. He pulled me out of a collapsed hold on the *Cormorant*." She shrugged, the way young people shrug when describing the moment someone saved their life. "He counts his dives before he counts his money. Weird habit. Kade hates it."

"He learned it somewhere," I said, and Suri gave me a look — quick, sideways, too knowing — and went forward, and I wondered how much of what I kept hidden was actually hidden, or just unspoken, waiting in the hold like ballast.

* * *

We anchored below the bend where Kade's scouts said the wreck lay, and I did what I'd done a hundred times: I gave the fleet everything. Depth soundings from surveys I'd copied years ago. The likely entry points on the hull. The corridor layout of a vessel class I knew by heart because I'd helped design the salvage plan for her sister ship, back when the plans were public and the secrets were only cargo.

It wasn't until the second day, when the first reports came back — modern cuts in the hull, clean ones, professional ones, years old — that I understood how far down the ledger went. Someone had been here before. Someone with navy-grade equipment and navy-grade permission. The wreck hadn't just been sealed. It had been harvested. And whatever was left inside, left behind deliberately or carelessly, was the part they wanted buried under the calving flood.

Kade stood over the charts that evening, tapping the entry point. "Your sources," she said. "Did they mention prior salvage?"

"My sources mentioned nothing," I said. "My sources are paid to mention what serves them."

"Then serve yourself some honesty, Harrow. What's in that hold that a navy would cut its own hull open and leave the rest to drown?"

I could have lied. God knows I'd had practice. But lying to Kade was like shorting a tide table — eventually the water came in anyway, and it came in over you. "Names," I said. "Paper. The kind of paper that turns a lost expedition into a murder."

She went very still, the way she did when the arithmetic finally balanced. "Whose names?"

"Tomas Reyes'. Nine others'. And everyone who signed off on letting them drown with the evidence in the hold."

"And you've known this—"

"I've known pieces of it for twelve years," I said. "I've known enough to be ashamed since the beginning. Whether that's the same thing, you can bill me later."

For once, Kade didn't count. She just looked at me with something that in a better woman might have been pity. "Reyes is down there right now," she said. "In that hold. Finding whatever you spent twelve years helping bury. What do you suppose he'll think, when he surfaces and learns his mentor's fingerprints are on the seal?"

"That depends," I said, "on whether he learns it from me or from you."

"That," said Vessna Kade, "is going to cost you."

Everything costs, in this trade. That's the first lesson I ever taught Mara, back when teaching him was the best thing I did with my days. Everything costs. The question I'd managed to avoid for twelve years was the only one that mattered: *paid by whom?*

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat in my bunk with the little tin box I'd carried upriver like a coward carries a confession — inside it, the originals. Corren's letter. The payment records, laundered through three shell companies until they looked like legitimate consulting fees. Twelve years of receipts for the price of nine lives, and mine among the buyers, because silence purchased with blood is still a purchase.

I'd told myself I kept them as leverage. Insurance. Every man in this trade keeps insurance. But sitting there in the dark with the glacier muttering somewhere upstream — a low grinding groan, like the mountain clearing its throat — I admitted what the box really was. It was a door. A way out I'd been keeping oiled for twelve years and never once had the guts to walk through, because walking through meant telling Mara Reyes that the man who raised him had helped bury his brother twice. Once in the water. Once in the lie.

The glacier groaned again, longer this time, and the hull ticked around me as the river answered.

In the morning, Kade's lookout reported a small expedition boat anchored above us — Mara's people, gone quiet, running dark. And downstream, right on schedule, a gray shape riding at anchor with its lights off and its intentions showing: a navy patrol, come to sweep the board before the flood erased it.

They'd move soon. Patrols always move at dawn or at tide change, and both were coming. And I understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the damage is done, that whichever way I moved next — toward the boy or toward the men who owned me — I'd be arriving late. Twelve years late, plus one tide.

I put the tin box in my coat, checked the charge on the old service pistol I hadn't fired in a decade, and went up on deck to watch the water rise.

Sixth tide coming, I thought. Time to settle the ledger.

But the river, being the river, had already started settling it for me — because out of the gray morning mist, moving fast and running dark, came a boat I knew the way you know a scar: Mara Reyes, headed straight for the wreck, with the patrol's searchlight sweeping the wrong channel behind him and only minutes left before the water and the navy and the truth all arrived at once.
