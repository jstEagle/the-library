## 12. Fourth Tide: Confrontation at Anchor

Kade's launch came alongside at slack water, which was the only polite thing about the approach. Two boats, engines cut, drifting together in the gorge like two knives deciding whose throat went first.

I stood on the deck of the *Harrow's Mercy*—Doss had named her before I could walk, and the name sat differently on my tongue now—and watched Vessna Kade climb our ladder with the unhurried confidence of a woman who had never once been told no by anything smaller than a glacier.

She came over the rail and looked around our deck the way an assessor looks at a drowning man's estate. Suri by the winch, arms crossed, doing her best impression of a woman who wasn't scared. Ilo half-hidden behind the wheelhouse glass with the radio unplugged, because we'd learned that lesson already. And Doss leaning against the mast with a mug of coffee he wasn't drinking, wearing the face of a man with nothing on his mind but the weather.

Which should have been my first warning. Doss always had weather on his mind.

"Reyes." Kade stopped three paces off, close enough to talk, far enough that neither of us would have to pretend an apology. "You hid your entry point from me."

"You found it anyway."

"Took me two tides and a drowned man." Her voice didn't rise. That was worse. "Tomas Okafor. Twenty-six years old. He went into your corridor because you wouldn't tell us where the safe one was."

"The corridor didn't kill him," I said. "The wreck did. The wreck kills everyone who goes in without knowing what it wants. I've spent four tides learning what it wants. He spent eleven minutes."

"Eleven minutes I paid for."

"Then bill the navy. They're the ones who sealed it."

Something moved behind her eyes—not softening, Kade didn't soften, but a recalculation, the way she recalculated everything, constantly, like a ledger that never closed. She glanced at Doss. Doss shrugged with one shoulder, a small economical motion, and I thought: *he's done this before.* Fed her something. Given her a read on me before I'd opened my mouth. The realization landed cold in my gut and I made myself keep looking at her instead of him.

"You've been down four times," she said. "What's in there?"

"Steel and silt."

"Don't insult both of us."

I looked out at the water. The river ran green-gray between the walls of the gorge, carrying its freight of melt, and somewhere under that skin of current lay my brother's handwriting, dated after the day the world said he died. Four tides I'd known. Four tides I'd told no one, not even Suri, not entirely, because a thing spoken becomes a thing owned, and I had watched enough men own pieces of my family to last the rest of my life.

But six tides is six tides. And tide five would be the deep dive, alone, through wreckage that shifted with every hour of meltwater. If something went wrong down there, I wanted—no. That wasn't honest either. What I wanted was for someone else to know where I'd be. Someone with boats and winches and a reason to pull me out.

"I found a logbook," I said.

Suri's head turned. Doss's coffee mug stopped halfway to his mouth—a fraction of a second, then resumed, smooth as ever. Smooth as ever. God, I'd loved that smoothness my whole life. It had raised me. It had also, apparently, been reporting to Vessna Kade.

"A logbook," Kade repeated.

"Paper. Waterproof case. In the hold, under the collapsed cargo netting." I kept my voice flat, salvage-report flat, the voice I used for insurance assessors. "It belongs to the *Corvane*. My brother was aboard when she went down. Officially."

"Officially," Kade said, tasting the word.

"The official record says he drowned with the rest of them, twelve years ago, when the navy scuttled her for navigational hazard." I turned back to face her. "The log is dated nine days after that."

The gorge held the silence. Water slapped hulls. Somewhere upstream the glacier ticked and groaned, the sound carrying down the rock like a bone settling.

Kade didn't ask if I was sure. She was too good a salvager to waste breath on questions the answer to which was written all over me. Instead she said, "And the navy doesn't know you have it."

"The navy doesn't know anyone's been inside since they welded the hatches."

"They will." She stepped closer. "They always do. You know how I know? Because twelve years ago somebody got here first. Modern cuts on the hull, right? Plasma, clean lines, navy-grade torch work. They came back for something after they sank her, and whatever they took, they left the rest to rot behind a paper seal nobody enforces until someone like you or me gets curious." She let that sit. "Curiosity has a body count, Reyes. Tomas Okafor is mine to carry. Yours is a brother. Let's not add to either ledger."

"And what are you proposing?"

"A truce." She said it plainly, no ceremony, which from Kade was the highest form of ceremony. "My fleet holds the downstream anchor and the surface cover. Your crew knows the wreck. We split what comes up—cargo value split even, the documents—" and here she paused, and I watched her choose her words with the care of a woman laying charges, "—the documents go public. Both our names on it. Both our fleets' names. If the navy comes for whoever tells this story, they come for two stories at once, and it's harder to drown two."

"Why?" Suri said from the winch. Flat, suspicious, seventeen kinds of tired. "Why would you burn the navy over some old paper? There's cargo in that hold worth more than both our boats."

Kade looked at her, and for a moment the captain's mask slipped enough that I saw the arithmetic underneath, and the arithmetic was ugly. "Because I put a boy in that corridor blind," she said, "and the only way that stops being my fault is if what killed him turns out to matter. Salvage I can write off. A cover-up I can't." She turned back to me. "That's the whole pitch, Reyes. Take it or run out the clock."

* * *

We drew up terms on the deck of the *Mercy* with the tide turning beneath us, and I want to say I negotiated hard, but the truth is I mostly listened and watched Doss.

He'd drifted closer during the talk, close enough to hear everything, and he contributed exactly twice—once to confirm the load ratings on our lift bags, once to say "the girl's right about the current window" when Kade proposed running the deep dive on tide five. Helpful. Measured. The voice of forty years in the trade, the voice that had taught me knots and decompression tables and how to read a river's mood off the color of its foam.

And underneath it, I caught it—the small motions. The way his thumb tapped the rail in little groups of three. The way his eyes went downstream every time a gull crossed the water. I'd seen those taps my whole life and called it a habit, a tic, Doss being Doss. Standing there with a truce half-signed, I finally asked myself who he might be counting for.

There was a signal lamp mounted at the stern of the *Mercy*, standard salvage rig, shuttered. Doss had checked it that morning. I hadn't thought anything of it. Doss checked everything; that was the religion he'd raised me in.

Now I watched him drift aft, casual as a man going to spit, and stand with his back to the negotiation, and I watched the shuttered lamp swing—just slightly, just twice—toward the gray line of the headland downstream, where the channel widened toward the sea and where, two nights ago, Ilo had picked up a patrol transmission on the emergency band before we killed the radio entirely: *gray hull, anchored position, awaiting instructions.*

Maybe it was nothing. Maybe an old man stretching his back.

Maybe.

"Reyes." Kade's voice pulled me back. She'd produced a battered notebook and was writing terms in pencil, her hand steady despite the deck's motion. "Terms as agreed. One: joint operations from tide five onward. Two: your dive plan governs inside the wreck; my safety officer has veto on surface conditions. Three: any documents recovered are copied in duplicate before either boat moves. Four—" she looked up, pencil hovering, "—nobody goes into the water alone again. Not you, not my people. Not for anything."

"That last one costs time."

"It cost me Tomas. Sign it anyway."

I signed. She signed. Suri witnessed, writing her name small and fierce like she was stabbing the page. Then Kade tore the sheet in half and gave me one piece, which is old fleet custom—each party holding half a truth, neither able to forge the whole—and the gesture struck me sideways, because that was exactly what we were doing with everything else now. Half a truth each. Trust assembled out of mutual damage.

"My diver Priya runs point with you on five," Kade said, pocketing her half. "She's good. Better than good. She also knows I'll fire her body out of a torpedo tube if she touches your logbook without witnesses."

"Charming management style."

"It keeps people alive." She paused at the rail, and for a moment the wind off the glacier flattened her voice so only I could hear it. "Your brother's log. When you read it—if there's names in it, Reyes, names of men who were bought or men who were abandoned—you say them out loud. All of them. Even the ones that hurt."

"You don't know what's in it."

"No," she said. "But I know what's in people. And I know that when you find a list like that, the temptation is to trim it down to the parts that keep your friends clean." Her eyes flicked, briefly, deliberately, toward Doss, and back. "Don't trim it."

Then she was over the side and down her ladder and gone, engines coughing awake, her launch swinging wide back toward the downstream anchor with the afternoon light going copper on her wake.

* * *

Dusk came up the gorge like water filling a bowl. We ate standing, hard bread and smoked fish, nobody sitting because sitting meant relaxing and relaxing meant talking and talking meant saying things we couldn't take back.

Ilo broke first. She'd been quiet all day, quieter than her nature, and finally she set down her cup and said, "The patrol boat moved."

Everyone stopped chewing.

"Moved how?" Suri asked. "Radio's dead."

"Binoculars. From the ridge before dark." Ilo's hands were wrapped around the cup like it was warm, though it wasn't. "It weighed anchor this afternoon. It's not at the downstream anchorage anymore. It's coming up the secondary channel—the shallow one, the one the charts say can't take its draft."

"The charts are twelve years old," Doss said mildly. "River's changed."

"Yes," Ilo said. "It has."

Nobody looked at Doss. Everybody looked at Doss, in the particular way of people carefully not looking. He met it square, that old calm face, that weather-worn patience, and I felt the truce paper in my jacket pocket go heavy as a stone.

"Doss," I said. "Walk with me."

We walked to the bow. The glacier muttered upstream, a long grinding syllable repeated like a man talking in his sleep. Below us the fourth tide turned, black water sliding seaward, taking the last of the light with it.

"Tide five tomorrow," I said. "Priya dives with me. We go for the final pages—the ones past the bulkhead, in the flooded cabin. After that, we surface, we copy everything, and we make for the coast ahead of the calving. That's the plan. Whole plan. Nothing hidden."

"Good plan," Doss said.

"There's one thing in it I don't understand."

"Ask, then."

I turned and looked at him, really looked, the way you look at a wreck before you commit to the descent—reading the structure, checking for what's rotted through. "The signal lamp. This morning. Twice, toward the headland."

The pause lasted maybe two seconds. In forty years of knowing him I'd never seen Doss Harrow need two seconds to answer anything, and those two seconds told me more than a confession would have.

"Lamp check," he said. "Routine."

"On a shuttered lamp."

"Routine includes shutters, boy. Always did. I taught you that."

"You taught me everything," I said. "That's what makes this hard to say. So I'll say it fast. If you're signaling anyone—if there's a debt, or a habit, or a man downstream holding paper over you—I'm asking you once, tonight, to tell me. Because tomorrow we go into that cabin and come out with names, and if one of those names is yours, Doss, I would rather hear it from you at anchor than from a boarding party at gunpoint."

The river filled the silence. He looked at me and something moved in his face, quick as a fish under ice, there and gone—grief, maybe, or the memory of grief, or just the weight of a choice he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Mara," he said, and used my name, which he almost never did, "you dive tomorrow. Dive well. Come up slow. And when you're on the surface with that paper in your hand—" he put his hand on my shoulder, and his grip was steady and warm and I hated how much it still steadied me, "—remember that the truth is a cargo like any other. It matters less what's in the hold than who's waiting topside to receive it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got tonight." He squeezed once and let go. "Get your sleep, boy. Tide waits for no one, and neither does the ice."

He walked aft, unhurried, and I heard him stop by the stern, and I heard—faint, unmistakable, twice—the click of the lamp shutter.

I stood at the bow in the dark and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. The patrol moving to a channel it shouldn't be able to navigate. The lamp clicks. Two nights ago, the emergency-band transmission, *awaiting instructions.*

Tomorrow, tide five. Tomorrow, the cabin, the pages, the names.

And somewhere downstream, in the dark, a gray hull was already answering someone's signal—moving upriver toward us through water that was supposed to be too shallow to hold it.

Behind me, Suri's voice, low: "Mara. Tell me that wasn't what I think it was."

I couldn't. So I said the only true thing left.

"Get the dinghy ready. Whatever happens at tide five—we may need to be gone before tide six."
