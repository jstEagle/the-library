## 13. The Glacier Groans

I learned to read ice before I learned to read letters. That's not poetry—it's just what happens when your mother runs a fish camp at the mouth of a glacial river and your alphabet is a series of cracks, booms, and silences. A letter tells you what someone wants you to know. Ice tells you what's true whether you want it or not.

So when I woke in the dark of the third night and heard the river talking in a voice I didn't like, I didn't need a chart or a tide table to know we were in trouble.

* * *

The expedition boat—Kade's old survey hull, the *Petrel*, borrowed, patched, and held together more by stubbornness than welds—lay at anchor in the lee of a granite shelf two miles below the wreck. Above us, the gorge narrowed and climbed until it ended in the pale blue wall of the Vaskar glacier, eight miles upstream as the water ran but close enough at night that its voice carried down the rock like a rumor through a sleeping house.

For three days that voice had been a low, patient grinding. The sound of a thing doing its work on its own schedule. I'd grown up with it the way some kids grow up with a snoring father—annoying, constant, and only alarming when it stopped.

It hadn't stopped. It had *changed*.

I climbed out of my bunk without waking the deckhand asleep across from me—Ilo, who'd come upriver with us after the radio silence and who slept like a man expecting to be arrested at any moment, which, given recent events, was reasonable—and went up through the hatch into a night so cold it felt personal.

The sky was doing that thing it does up here in the weeks before calving season: no stars worth counting, just a low ceiling of cloud the color of dirty wool, holding the heat hostage. The water below me was black and moving in that slow, muscular way it does when the melt has already started feeding it from above. Our anchor light swung. Everything else was still.

Except the glacier.

The grinding had developed a rhythm. A groan, then a silence, then a groan a half-tone lower, like a hull settling in a storm, like a door in a house where nobody's supposed to be home. And under it—this was the part that made the hair on my arms stand up, and I'm a woman who has been underwater with things that wanted to eat me—a sound like a long exhale. Air moving through ice. Pressure finding somewhere to go.

"Ah," I said, out loud, to no one. "You're early."

Glaciers don't care about your plans. That's the first thing, the middle thing, and the last thing anyone should know about them. Kade had built her whole schedule on the almanac calving dates—the same dates the navy used, the same dates the fleet brokers used, the dates every salvage contract in the territory was written around. Six tides. That had been the arithmetic. Four tides left, as of this evening.

The glacier had not read the almanac.

* * *

I got the coffee going because it was that or stand in the cold listening to my own heartbeat, and I'd done enough of both for one lifetime. By the time the pot was hot, Mara was awake. I don't know how he knew—maybe the same way I knew, some animal thing the trade beats into you, or maybe he'd never really been asleep. He came up the companionway with his hair flattened on one side and his eyes already fully awake, which is a trick I've never managed.

"You heard it," he said. Not a question.

"Whole gorge heard it. Probably the navy heard it." I poured him a cup. "Your ice is waking up early."

He took the cup and looked upstream, into the dark, the way he always does—like the river owes him something and he's come to collect. Twelve years of that look, Doss told me once. Twelve years of a man measuring himself against a current.

"How early?" he asked.

I shrugged, which was a lie, because I had a number. "Early enough. That groan isn't meltwater finding its channels. That's the face letting go in places. When the tongue starts talking in sentences instead of words—" I made a small gesture with my cup, the universal gesture for *things fall down*. "Almanac says we have four tides. My ears say two. Maybe three if we're lucky, and the glacier has never once cared whether I'm lucky."

Mara drank his coffee and said nothing for a while. That's one of the things I respect about him. Most men, you hand them a deadline cut in half, they start talking—plans, bargains, blame. Mara goes quiet the way deep water goes quiet, and you get the feeling that somewhere down in him a decision is already made and the rest of him is just catching up to it.

"The fifth-tide dive," he said finally. "The deep run. I can't do it in two."

"Then you'll do it in two or you won't do it at all."

He looked at me then, and I saw the thing under the calm, the thing I'd been seeing since we found the logbook: a man who had spent twelve years diving for other people's lost property and had finally, finally found his own, and the river was trying to take it back before he could get it all up.

"We'll make it fit," he said.

"Sure," I said. "And if we don't, we'll make a very interesting story fit instead."

* * *

Kade came on deck at first gray light, and she'd heard it too—I could tell because she didn't ask what woke us. She stood at the rail with her hands in her coat pockets and listened to the glacier the way a creditor listens to a debtor, and when the long groan came again, lower than the one before, her jaw did that small tight thing it does when she's recalculating money in her head.

"Say it," she said to me.

"Two tides. Maybe three. The face is going early."

"The almanac says—"

"The almanac," I said, "is a guess with good manners. The ice doesn't read. I told you that when you hired me on for this run."

She had hired me on for this run—me, Suri Oyelaran, ice-reader and general-purpose pessimist, at a rate I'd have been a fool to refuse and a bigger fool to feel good about. Kade's fleet doesn't hire pessimists as a rule. Kade hires optimists because optimists dive deeper and ask fewer questions. But after the survey team went silent upriver, after the navy's gray boat appeared downstream like a verdict, after the drowned man from her own crew came up out of the wreck's collapsed corridor looking like a question nobody wanted to answer—after all that, she'd started wanting pessimists. Pessimists are just optimists who've read to the end of the book.

"Two tides," she repeated. She said it the way you'd say a debt aloud, to make it real. "We still have a navy patrol downriver, a wreck that's eating divers, and a partner—" here she glanced at Mara, and I watched her decide not to finish the sentence about Doss, which was its own kind of news— "and now the calendar itself is against us."

"The calendar was never for us," Mara said. "It just hadn't said so out loud yet."

I liked him for that. I've liked him for a lot of things over the years, most of them bad for my peace of mind.

Kade turned to the matter of logistics, which is where she goes when the world frightens her: air, lines, the state of the *Petrel*'s engine, whether the skiff could make the run to the wreck site and back inside a single tide window with the current already fattening. I answered what I could and made up the rest with a confidence I didn't feel, and by full light we had a plan, which is to say we had a list of things that could go wrong, arranged in an order that flattered us.

* * *

Midmorning, the glacier gave us a demonstration.

I was on the foredeck splicing line when the sound came—not the groan this time but a crack, sharp and total, like the sky snapping a finger, and then a rumble that rolled down the gorge and through the hull and up through my boots and my teeth and into the place where I keep my oldest fears. Eight miles upstream, a piece of the Vaskar's face the size of a chapel had let go. We couldn't see it. We didn't need to. The river told us everything: the water at our anchor line shivered, then rose—just a hand's width, just a slow swell, but wrong, wrong the way a heartbeat is wrong when it skips—and then a low gray line of disturbed water came sliding down the gorge toward us, the leading edge of the surge, carrying with it a scatter of brash ice that hadn't been there an hour ago.

"Anchor watch!" Kade's voice, from the wheelhouse. "Suri, the line!"

I was already moving. The swell took the *Petrel* and lifted her, gentle as a hand under a sleeping child, and set her down again, and the anchor line came taut with a sound like a plucked string, and for about ten seconds we were a toy in the grip of something that did not know we were there and would not have cared if it did.

That's the thing I want to write down, in case anyone ever reads this: it wasn't malice. It wasn't even indifference, really, because indifference implies it noticed us enough to disregard us. The glacier doesn't disregard us. It has never once thought about us. We are the smallest possible thing in its life—smaller than the meltwater, smaller than the grit it carries, smaller than the stones it will grind to flour over ten thousand years. Our six tides, our contracts, our navy, our dead, our truths—we are less than weather to it. We are a scratch on a stone it is using to sharpen itself.

The swell passed. The brash ice rattled against the hull and drifted on downstream. The *Petrel* swung back on her line and settled, and the gorge went quiet in that particular way quiet goes after a loud thing, ringing with the absence.

Ilo came up on deck looking gray. He'd been below with the radio, trying to raise the coastal station through the mountains, and I could tell by his face he'd gotten nothing but static and his own worry echoed back at him.

"Was that—" he started.

"Calving," I said. "A small one. An appetizer."

"An appetizer," he repeated. He sat down on the hatch coaming rather heavily. Ilo is not a river man. Ilo is a dispatcher, a man whose entire professional life has been built on the assumption that if you send a message, someone receives it, and that the world is fundamentally a system of orderly communications. The glacier had just sent him a message, and it was not orderly, and I watched him take that in.

"The navy's schedule," he said. "The patrol downstream. They're working off the almanac too. If the calving's early—"

"Then their window's early, and they know it, and they'll get desperate," I finished for him. "And desperate men with gray boats do what they do."

Mara had come up during all this, and he stood at the rail watching the brash ice recede downstream, and I saw him do the same arithmetic Ilo was doing, and then some further arithmetic on top of it, some private column of figures I couldn't read. His brother's log was aboard, wrapped in oilcloth in the chart drawer, the pages we'd already recovered. The pages still down there—the final entries, the ones that named names—were in the deep cabin, behind the collapsed corridor, in the part of the wreck the river was about to take back forever.

"Two tides," Mara said again, to the ice going downstream.

"Two tides," I agreed. "And the second one's going to be ugly."

* * *

Afternoon brought wind, which I could have done without, and with the wind came the sound again, closer now—or not closer, but more frequent, the groans stacking up like a man climbing stairs. I took the skiff up the gorge a half mile with the sounding lead and a stopwatch, because Kade wanted numbers and numbers are how she prays, and what I found I didn't much like reporting.

The current had come up a full knot since morning. The melt was on in earnest, and the river was rising, and rising water in a narrowing gorge means one thing for divers: it turns the wreck's flooded interior from a still, dark library into a washing machine. Every hour, the currents inside that hull were getting meaner. The corridor that had already killed one of Kade's divers was getting meaner faster.

I sat in the skiff with the engine off, drifting back down on the current, and let myself do something I almost never let myself do, which is feel the whole weight of the thing at once.

Because here's what I hadn't said out loud, to Kade or Mara or anyone: I'd been on the survey team's last known heading. I'd helped chart the warning buoys the navy never maintained—had rowed out in a borrowed boat and set them, years back, as a contract job, and been paid late and poorly, and thought nothing of it. And somewhere upstream of those buoys, the survey team had gone silent, and before them Mara's brother's crew had gone silent, and the navy had written both silences off with the same flat bureaucratic pen, and now here we all were, sailing into the same silence with our eyes open, because the truth was worth more to us than the lesson.

The glacier didn't think any of this was interesting. The glacier was going to calve whether we found the log or not, whether the navy got its way or not, whether Doss—Doss, whose name nobody was saying, whose signal to the patrol had put us all in this narrowing box—lived or died or drowned or turned king's evidence. The ice would grind the wreck to nothing and grind the nothing smaller, and in ten thousand years a stone that had once been part of a navy hull would be flour in a riverbed, and there would be no one to testify and nothing to testify about, and the glacier would not have noticed.

I found that, oddly, comforting. Not the destruction—the scale. There's a mercy in being small. It means the outcome isn't all on you. It means you can do the work in front of you and let the ice be the ice.

I started the engine and ran back down to the *Petrel* to report my numbers, which were bad, and my recommendation, which was worse: dive the fifth-tide run on the fourth tide. Tonight. Early. Before the interior currents finished turning the wreck into a trap with a library in it.

* * *

They took it the way I knew they would. Kade argued for an hour—about air reserves, about the light, about the navy patrol and what a night dive would look like from downstream if the gray boat had a glass on us. Mara argued for nothing. He just checked his gear, piece by piece, with the patience of a man dressing for his own wedding, and when Kade finally ran out of objections, he looked at her and said, "The river's already voted."

Which left the vote to the rest of us, and I cast mine by checking his lines twice and marking his depth gauge with paint where his eyes would find it in the dark.

Evening came down the gorge like a lid closing. The glacier groaned its stair-climbing groan, and this time, at the end of it, there was a new sound underneath—a high thin note, like wire under tension, that I had heard exactly once before, the year the fish camp went under, the year the ice took the whole face off in a single night and the river stood up and walked.

I stood at the rail and listened to it, and I thought about my mother, who taught me the alphabet of cracks, and I thought about what she'd say if she were standing here. She'd say what she always said, which was: *the ice doesn't hate you, girl. It doesn't love you either. Get your work done inside its patience, and when the patience runs out, be somewhere else.*

The patience was running out. You could hear it going, note by note, up the dark gorge.

Below, in the lamplight of the deck, Mara finished his final checks and shouldered his tank, and Ilo killed the running lights, and the *Petrel* went dark against the black water, and the skiff's engine turned over with a cough and caught.

"Two tides," Kade said at the rail beside me, though it wasn't two anymore and we all knew it. It was one, and half of one, and the half was spending itself while we talked.

"Two," I said, because there was no percentage in the truth, and I watched the skiff pull away upstream toward the wreck, toward the deep cabin, toward the pages that named names, and the last thing I heard before the current took the sound was the glacier, high and thin and patient, tuning its wire.

Then, from downstream—far off, but not far enough—a gray shape at the mouth of the gorge put out a light.
