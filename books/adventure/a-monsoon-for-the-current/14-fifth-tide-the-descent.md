## 14. Fifth Tide: The Descent

The fifth tide came in gray and mean, the way the river had been all week—like it knew something we didn't and was in no hurry to share.

I sat on the deck of Kade's borrowed tender with my suit half on, one leg in, one leg out, staring at the water as if it might blink first. It didn't. Rivers never do. They just keep moving, patient as debt collectors.

"You're stalling," Suri said. She was coiling line beside me with the brisk efficiency of someone who'd decided that if she stopped working she'd have to start feeling things, and she'd rather not.

"I'm checking my gauges."

"Your gauges are on your wrist. You're looking at the water."

"The water is a gauge."

She snorted and kept coiling. Fair enough. I'd been looking at that water for twenty minutes and it hadn't told me anything except *stay out*, which was advice I'd been ignoring professionally for most of my adult life.

The truth was simpler and uglier: I didn't want to go back down. Not because of the depth—the wreck sat at forty meters at slack water, well inside my range—or the cold, or even the collapsed corridor where Kade's diver had died two tides ago. I didn't want to go down because down there, in the dark between the bulkheads, my brother had been alive after the date they'd carved into his memorial stone. Twelve years of grief, and somewhere in the last three days it had turned into something worse than grief. Grief you can carry. But hope—that thing had teeth.

The final pages of the log were still in the aft cabin. Ilo's reconstruction of the wreck's layout said so, cross-checked against the pages I'd already recovered, which ended mid-sentence on the night of the twelfth of some month twelve years ago, in my brother's cramped handwriting: *If anyone finds this, the rest is in the strongbox under—*

Under what, Teo? Under what?

* * *

Kade came up from the wheelhouse with two mugs of something that smelled like tar sweetened by an optimist. She handed me one without asking if I wanted it. Captains don't ask.

"Tide turns in forty minutes," she said. "Current profile says you'll have maybe fifty minutes of workable bottom time before it starts pulling. After that, the gorge does what it wants."

"I read the current tables."

"You read them. I don't trust you to *believe* them." She looked out at the water, arms crossed. The wind pushed her collar against her jaw. "Mara. The corridor that took Ovett—it's shifted since yesterday. Suri heard it groaning through the hull on the hydrophone. Whole sections of that ship are deciding right now whether they want to keep standing."

"That's why I'm going today and not tomorrow."

"That's why I'm asking if you've lost your mind."

I drank the tar-coffee. It tasted like an apology from the coffee. "My brother wrote those pages. The navy killed him and eleven other men and sold their cargo out from under their bodies, and every government office from here to the capital has a file that says otherwise. Those pages are the only version of events that isn't bought and paid for. So yes. Probably."

Kade studied me for a long moment. There was something in her face I hadn't seen before—not the fleet-captain arithmetic she usually wore, but something older and tireder. "Ovett had a sister," she said finally. "He dove for me eight years. She told me once that salvage men die twice—once when the water takes them, and once when the people who loved them stop telling stories about them." She set her empty mug on the rail. "Bring back the story, Reyes."

Then she walked away before I could say anything, which was probably wise, because I'd have said something sincere and we'd both have hated it.

* * *

The descent started clean.

Forty meters of green-black water, my lamp cutting a cone through silt that hung like weather. I went down the guide line hand over hand, breathing slow, counting my exhales the way Doss taught me when I was fourteen and all elbows and fear: *the river doesn't care how fast you fall, boy, only how fast you breathe.* Doss. I pushed the thought away. That was a problem for the surface.

At thirty meters the wreck rose out of the murk, and even now, fifth dive, it got me the way it always did—a cargo steamer the length of a city block lying on its port side in the throat of the gorge, masts snapped, funnel collapsed, wearing twelve years of silt like a shroud it had grown into. The navy charts called it a hazard. The navy notices called it sealed. My brother had called it home for the last nine days of his life.

I swam the guide line to the breach point—hull plating peeled back near the engine room, a wound we'd widened ourselves—and paused at the threshold. Standard discipline. Check air: fine. Check line: running free. Check nerve:

The nerve check was interrupted by the river.

It came as a sound first, felt more than heard—a deep bass note through the water, through my chest, through the fillings in my teeth. Somewhere upstream, the glacier was talking. Then the current arrived, not the lazy push of the last four dives but a shove, a hand flat against my back, pressing me toward the breach whether I'd finished my checklist or not.

Fifty minutes, Kade had said. The glacier had apparently decided to renegotiate.

I went in.

* * *

Inside, the world became a throat.

That's the honest description, and I've had time to think about honest descriptions. A ship flooded and settled for twelve years is a maze of corridors that used to be horizontal and now aren't, doorways that open into decks instead of rooms, and everywhere the soft brown fog of silt waiting for any disturbance to turn it into blindness. My lamp beam shrank to arm's length. My bubbles went up along surfaces that weren't quite vertical, found gaps, escaped into places I couldn't follow. Every exhalation sounded enormous. Every creak of settling metal sounded personal.

Aft cabin. Two hundred feet of corridor, one collapse point at the midships ladder—I'd cleared it on the third tide, stacked plates into a crawl-through—and then the cabin itself, where the captain's strongbox had been bolted to the deck before the deck became a wall.

I moved through the dark counting kick cycles. Twenty kicks past the galley. Thirty past the crew berths, where I made myself look, briefly, at the rack where they'd found the first bodies twelve years ago, and made myself not think about which bunk had been my brother's. Forty kicks. The ladder collapse loomed out of the silt, a jagged staircase to nowhere, and I began the crawl-through—regulator first, tank turned sideways, pushing with fingertips, the gap barely wide enough for me and my ego, and my ego was the smaller of us.

Halfway through, the river hit again.

Not a push this time. A pull. The whole wreck groaned around me, a long metallic complaint like a whale with opinions, and the current reversed through the corridor and sucked at my fins, dragging silt off the collapse pile above me. Something shifted. Something small, a plate edge, a pipe—but small things in a collapse are just the opening argument.

I froze in the gap, cheek against cold steel, regulator hissing, and listened to the ship decide whether I lived there now.

It decided no. The groan faded. The silt rained down around me, turning my lamp beam into a snow globe, and I breathed, four counts in, six counts out, until my hands stopped shaking enough to lie to myself about it.

*Keep moving, Reyes.* My own voice in my head, but it came out in Doss's gravel. Traitor even in memory.

* * *

The aft cabin was exactly as Ilo's drawings promised and nothing like I wanted it to be.

The deck was the port bulkhead now; the strongbox hung off it at a drunken angle, bolted flanges bent, lid sprung. Empty. Of course empty—Teo would have emptied it, transferred everything waterproof into something portable, something he could take with him when he tried to swim out. Which meant the final pages weren't in a box. They were wherever he'd put them to *carry*.

I swept my lamp across the cabin. Bunk frame. Locker, door hanging. Desk bolted to what had been the starboard bulkhead and was now the overhead, drawers hanging open like tongues. And there—wedged behind the desk's knee-space, tucked into the angle where frame met steel—a canvas map case, naval issue, the kind survey officers carried. Silt had half-buried it. Someone had wedged it deliberately, tightly, in the one place in the cabin a current couldn't easily reach.

My brother had known this ship. Had known currents. Had known that whoever came looking might come in a hurry.

I pulled the case free. It was heavier than paper should be. Inside: oilskin wrapping, and inside that, pages—dozens of them, logbook leaves torn out and bundled, plus a slim ledger with a navy requisition stamp, plus photographs in a wax sleeve. My hands wanted to shake. I made them be professional hands. Stow the bundle in the chest pouch, seal it, check the seal. The reading was for the surface. Down here you carried, you didn't grieve.

I was turning for the exit when the glacier spoke again.

This time it wasn't a note. It was a sentence—a long, grinding roar transmitted through miles of water and rock, and the wreck answered it with movement. Not settling. *Shifting*. The deck under my knees tilted a degree, two degrees, and the corridor behind me filled with a sound like a drawer full of knives closing.

The current came through the doorway hard enough to tumble a tool across the floor. My guide line—which had run free on the way in—snapped taut and then went slack in a way lines shouldn't, and I knew before I touched it: parted, or pinned under something that had just moved. Either way, it wasn't a road anymore.

Okay. Okay. New plan. The plan was: don't die.

I'd like to tell you I was calm. What I actually was, was very busy. I clipped the pouch double, checked my air—half, which had been comfortable ten minutes ago and was now a countdown—and pulled myself out of the cabin into the corridor, into silt so thick my lamp showed me nothing but a brown sun with no edges.

Navigation by feel. Left hand on the bulkhead, counting kicks. The galley's stove should be a pipe bump at hip height. Twenty kicks. The pipe wasn't there. Thirty kicks. Still no pipe. The deck had shifted; the geometry had changed; I was navigating a building that was being demolished while I stood in it.

Panic is a diver's real enemy, everyone says so, and everyone's wrong. Panic is loud and easy to spot. Despair is quiet. Despair sits on your chest at forty meters in the dark and whispers that this is fitting, actually, that you'll die in your brother's grave, that the river has been trying to collect you for twelve years and today it closes the account.

I told despair to wait its turn and kept swimming.

* * *

I found the galley by smell—old fuel oil seeping from a ruptured line, sharp enough to cut through the regulator's rubber taste—and corrected left. Found the ladder collapse by hearing it: the new pile had grown, plates interlocked at fresh angles, and the crawl-through I'd made on the third tide was gone. In its place, a gap near the overhead, maybe eighteen inches, maybe less, leading up and through into the forward corridor.

Eighteen inches, with a tank on my back, in zero visibility, with the current pulling me backward into the wreck and my air ticking down like a rude clock.

There's a moment in every bad dive—I've had four of them now, and I remember each one the way you remember car crashes—where the animal part of your brain takes the controls and screams *out, out, get OUT*, and the trained part of your brain has to physically wrestle it, hand over hand, saying *no, out is that way, and out is through.*

I went through.

Shoulders first, exhaling to shrink, dragging myself up and over the fresh-fallen plates. The gap narrowed behind me as I passed—I heard metal settle into the space my body had vacated, polite as a door closing—and then I was through, into the forward corridor, and the current here was a living thing, hauling me toward the breach, and I let it. No more dignity. No more technique. I rode the river's fist out through the peeled hull plating and into open water, tumbling, lamp spinning, and for one long disorienting moment I didn't know which way was up, which is the one question a diver must always be able to answer.

Then the light changed. Green-gray to pale silver. Up. I kicked, and kicked, and the surface came down to meet me like a decision.

* * *

I broke into rain.

The storm had arrived while I was below, fat drops hammering the river into static, and the tender was a hundred meters downstream—dragged, or drifted, or both. I raised my arm, lamp flashing, and screamed into my mask until Suri's answering flash stabbed back through the rain.

They pulled me over the rail more than helped me aboard. Suri stripped my mask and hood while Kade knelt on the deck and put her face level with mine, close enough that I could see the rain running off her nose.

"Air," she demanded.

I showed her the gauge. Eleven percent. Enough for five more minutes of sitting still and nothing else.

"Pouch," she said.

I fumbled the chest seals with fingers that wouldn't obey, and Suri finished the job, and the oilskin bundle came out into the cockpit light, wet and intact and heavier than any cargo I'd ever lifted. Kade looked at it, then at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod slowly, like a verdict coming in.

"That's all of it?"

"That's all of him," I said.

Suri wrapped the bundle in a dry blanket as if it were a survivor, which it was. I sat on the deck plates shaking—the good shaking, the after-shaking, the body presenting its invoice—and watched the upstream dark, where the glacier was still talking, longer and louder now, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat before it speaks.

Kade stood, braced against the tender's roll, and shaded her eyes downriver. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a captain reading bad weather on the horizon.

"Mara. You need to see this."

I got my legs under me and looked. Through the rain, downriver where the gorge widened, a shape was coming up the channel against the current. Gray hull. Angular superstructure. Running lights in a formation I'd known since childhood, the way you know a police siren.

The navy had found us.

And anchored off our stern, riding the swell with its own lights burning, was a boat I knew better than my own hands—Doss Harrow's old salvage tug, the *Kindly Tide*, flying no colors at all.
