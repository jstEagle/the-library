## 15. Betrayal Surfacing

I have kept a radio for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned one iron law of the trade: the worst news never comes over the airwaves. It comes on the water, silent, running dark, with its engines throttled down to a whisper so you hear it only when it is already alongside.

The gray boat came at slack water before the fifth tide, which was either coincidence or contempt. Navy boats don't do coincidence. They do schedules, and someone had given them ours.

I was on deck with a mug of tea going cold in my hands, because that's what dispatchers do between crises — we hold warm things and wait for them to stop being warm. Suri was below, sleeping off the fourth tide. Kade stood at the rail doing her captain's stare at the middle distance, the one that makes junior crew members confess to things they haven't done yet. Mara was still wet from his last dive, sitting on the engine housing with the logbook wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a man holding a bomb he'd decided to love.

That's when I heard it. Not an engine. A wake. The particular flat slap of a hull moving without lights, upriver, against the current, patient as arithmetic.

"Kill the lantern," I said.

Kade didn't ask why. That's the thing about Vessna Kade — she'll argue with you for an hour about salvage rights, but she never argues with dread. The deck went dark. We all stood there listening to the river, and the river told us what it always tells you eventually: something big was coming and it wasn't in any hurry because it already knew where we were.

"Patrol," Mara said quietly. Not a question. He'd been expecting it since the third tide, maybe since twelve years ago. Some men carry grief; Mara carried grief with a forecast attached.

I got below to the radio room, which is my church, my bunker, and my confession booth all in one, and I did what I always do: I listened. The patrol wasn't transmitting. Of course it wasn't. You don't announce yourself when your whole purpose is to arrive unannounced. But I swept the bands anyway, and on a frequency the navy uses for harbor traffic — a frequency they were supposed to have retired years ago — I caught two syllables of a relayed order, clipped and cold, and a callsign I recognized from the survey team that had gone quiet upstream.

The same callsign. The same voice, if voices can be said to belong to callsigns. The navy hadn't lost that survey team. It had *reassigned* them.

I want to tell you I was surprised. I want to tell you my stomach dropped, that betrayal hit me like cold water. But here's the bitter truth of spending three decades behind a transmitter: you spend years suspecting everyone, and then one day you're proven right, and it doesn't feel like vindication. It feels like the bill finally arriving for all those years of suspicion. Interest included.

* *

*

They came aboard at 0340 by my watch, which I checked out of habit and then resented checking, because now I would always know exactly what time the bottom fell out.

There were six of them, in gray foul-weather gear with no insignia, which is its own kind of insignia. No flags, no names, no warrant read aloud. A man who identifies himself has to answer for himself. These ones intended to be nobody, and they were very good at it.

They came up the port side while their boat held position off our starboard quarter, floodlights dark, and the first I knew of the boarding party proper was boots on my deck and a voice saying, "Nobody moves," in the tone of a man who has said it many times and been obeyed every time.

Kade moved. Naturally. She took one step toward the wheelhouse and a hand the size of a hatch cover closed on her shoulder, not hard, just final, the way you'd stop a child from walking into traffic. I watched her weigh the situation in about a second and a half — six of them, two more visible on the patrol boat, probably four more aboard it, and us: a dispatcher past fifty, a deckhand asleep, a diver wrung out from the deep, and a captain with excellent judgment and no weapons worth the name.

"Evening," Kade said, pleasant as poison. "You're anchored in a salvage lane."

"We're anchored wherever we're anchored." The speaker was tall, weathered, with the kind of face that had been deliberately made forgettable. A lieutenant once, I'd have guessed, or whatever the navy promotes instead of lieutenants when it wants deniability. "This vessel is detained pending inspection. Anyone who interferes will be charged under maritime security statutes."

"Which ones?"

"All of them."

You had to admire the economy.

They went through us with the efficiency of men who'd done this before and would do it again and had been promised no one would ever write it down. They woke Suri by shaking her bunk frame, and she came up swinging before she was fully conscious, caught a wrist, and then thought better of it when she saw how many wrists were available. They put the four of us in the saloon — not tied, not locked, just *contained*, the way you contain evidence — and then they started on the boat.

And I sat there with my hands flat on the table thinking one thought, over and over, in the rhythm of the bilge pump: *someone told them.* Someone gave them our position, our schedule, our intentions. And there was exactly one person outside this hull who had all three.

Doss Harrow.

I'd known Doss since before Mara could swim. I'd logged his check-ins for twenty years — *Harrow, position such-and-such, all well, out* — thousands of transmissions, each one a small promise kept: *I am where I say I am, I am doing what I said I'd do.* That's what a radio man's friendship is made of, really. Confirmed positions. All wells. Outs.

And now some part of me — the part that had spent thirty-one years sweeping dead frequencies and finding nothing but static and lies — whispered that Doss had been giving the navy confirmed positions on *us*, too. That every *all well* had run both directions along a wire we couldn't see.

I hated myself for thinking it. I thought it anyway. That's the job. You think it anyway.

* *

*

They found the logbook in eleven minutes.

I know because I counted, and I know because I watched through the saloon's salt-fogged window as the forgettable-faced officer held the oilcloth bundle up to the light of a work lamp, turned it over twice, and said something to the man beside him. I saw Mara come off the engine housing like a spring released. I saw two of them take his arms, and I saw him stop — actually stop, plant his feet, go still in a way that frightened me more than struggling would have, because struggling is hope and stillness is arithmetic, and Mara Reyes had clearly finished his sum.

Then Kade moved again, and this time she was magnificent.

She didn't lunge. She *strolled*. Out of the saloon, across the deck, hands loose at her sides, talking the whole way — about salvage law, about admiralty jurisdiction, about how the wreck lay in disputed water and how anything recovered from it belonged, provisionally, to whoever held the original claim, and did they have the original claim, because she'd be happy to produce hers, it ran to forty pages and cited three treaties. It was nonsense stacked on nonsense with a cherry of nonsense on top, and it worked, because bureaucratic men are helpless before a confident woman citing documents.

The officer holding the logbook hesitated. One second. Two.

Mara tore free.

I don't know where he found the strength — he'd made two deep dives inside eighteen hours and should have been horizontal for a week — but grief and adrenaline make a fuel that burns hotter than sense. He crossed the deck in four strides, and he didn't fight the officers; he fought *past* them, straight at the logbook, and his hand closed on the oilcloth at the same moment the officer's did, and for one suspended second the two of them stood there in the work lamp light, pulling in opposite directions, the whole story of the last twelve years hanging on the grip strength of two men and a stitch of waterproof cloth.

The stitching held. Mara's grip held better.

He wrenched it free, staggered back two steps, and then — and this is the part I'll remember until they scatter my own ashes somewhere with poor reception — he didn't run. Running would have meant losing it. Instead he pressed the logbook flat against his chest with both arms, dropped to his knees on his own deck, and wrapped himself around it like a man sheltering a child from hail.

You can't wrestle a man who's protecting something without hurting him badly, and hurting him badly means paperwork, and paperwork means names. The officers looked at their superior. Their superior looked at Mara kneeling on the deck, and I swear I saw him do the math: *a dead or broken diver draws questions; a stubborn one just draws delay.*

"Bag everything else," he said. "Leave him his paper. It's worthless without corroboration."

Worthless. I filed that word away. Men only call a thing worthless when they're afraid of its value.

They took the rest. Charts with our dive marks penciled in. The sonar prints. Kade's claim folder — she protested with genuine theatrical anguish, which fooled nobody and was clearly meant to. The survey photographs from the outer hull, the ones showing the old salvage cuts. Two of Mara's spare tanks, which made no sense until I realized they weren't confiscating equipment, they were *delaying* it, buying hours, keeping us pinned while the tide clock ran down.

And then the forgettable-faced officer paused at the saloon door, looked back at the four of us — Kade rigid, Suri seething, Mara still on his knees around his brother's handwriting, and me, sitting with my tea long cold — and said, almost gently:

"You were never boarded. There was no inspection. This vessel was never here. Understood?"

We understood. That's the thing about men like him. He wasn't asking whether we agreed. He was telling us what story would be official tomorrow, and letting us know, in the same breath, that our version would sound like the ravings of people who'd been at sea too long.

He was right, too. That's the bitterest part. He was right, unless we brought back something that made our story heavier than theirs.

* *

*

They were gone by 0430, running dark downriver, and the river closed over their wake like a page turning.

For a long while nobody spoke. The bilge pump kept its idiot rhythm. Somewhere upstream the glacier groaned — a long, grinding complaint, ice settling into the new warmth, the route closing hour by hour whether we breathed or not.

Suri spoke first, because Suri always speaks first; tact is a skill she plans to acquire someday. "Somebody signaled them. Position, timing, all of it. Nobody finds a boat this size in the dark by luck."

"I know," Kade said.

"Doss knew our anchor point. Doss knew the tide plan. Doss—"

"*I know,*" Kade said, sharper, and then, quieter: "I know."

Mara finally unfolded himself from the deck. He sat back on his heels with the logbook in his lap, and he looked older than I'd ever seen him, and calmer, which was worse. There's a kind of peace that isn't peace at all — it's just a man who has stopped hoping a thing isn't true.

"He raised me," Mara said, to no one in particular. To the deck, maybe. To the river. "Twenty years. Every knot I know. Every dive. He taught me how to read current off the surface." His thumb moved along the oilcloth seam, back and forth, back and forth. "And he sold the route to the men who buried my brother."

"You don't know it was him," Suri said, but her heart wasn't in it. Hers rarely is when she's wrong on purpose.

"It was him." Mara said it without heat, which is how you know a man has finished arguing with himself. "It was him, and he'll be aboard their fleet right now, telling himself it's debt, it's habit, it's loyalty — telling himself some story where he's not what he is." He looked up, and his eyes in the lamplight were the gray-green of deep water over rock. "Same story the navy tells itself. Same story every man tells who trades other people's truth for his own comfort."

I cleared my throat. Thirty-one years of talking to strangers through a microphone gives you a certain authority on the subject of voices. "There's something else," I said. "Before they boarded. I swept the bands. The patrol was relaying orders on an old harbor frequency, and the callsign relaying them—" I made myself say it plainly, because plainness is all a radio man has. "It matched the survey team that went silent upstream. The one with the voice from your brother's crew. They were never lost, Mara. They were promoted."

Silence. Even the glacier seemed to lean in.

"So the navy didn't just bury the wreck," Kade said slowly, working it through, captain's mind clicking through the ledger. "It kept survivors. Kept them close. Kept them *signaling*." She laughed, short and ugly. "Of course it did. A dead witness is a risk. A bought witness is an asset."

"And a bought witness," I said, "knows every frequency, every patrol pattern, every channel the truth might travel on. Which is why we've been fighting this whole thing half-blind. They've been listening the entire time. Everything we've said on the radio since the gorge, they've heard."

Suri went pale. "Including—"

"Including the fifth-tide plan," I said. "Which is why they came tonight, and not tomorrow. They didn't need to catch us diving. They needed to catch us *before* we dove, and take the maps, and slow us past the closing of the route." I spread my hands. "Congratulations, everyone. We've been transparent as glass in a house full of thieves."

Mara stood. It took him a while — the deep dives had taken their toll, and I watched him favor his left knee — but he stood, and he tucked the logbook inside his suit, against his skin, where no boarding party would reach without stripping him.

"They missed it," he said. "They got the charts, the photos, the claims. They didn't get this. And the final pages are still down there, in the brother's chamber, past the collapsed corridor." He looked at Kade. "One tide left after this. Maybe less, the way that ice is talking."

"One tide," Kade agreed. "And they'll be watching the water now. Every approach, every channel."

"Then we don't use a channel," Mara said. "We use the wreck itself. The old salvage cuts on the outer hull — the ones somebody used years ago. There's a gap in the plating on the downstream side, under the fold of the current. Nobody watches a place the river punishes."

"That gap nearly killed the last man who tried it," Kade said.

"Yes," Mara said. "I know. I've been reading his notes." He patted the oilcloth, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen in twelve years of watching him grieve — not hope exactly. Aim. "Doss taught me that current. If I'm going to dive through it, I want the man who learned it from him watching my line."

"He betrayed you," Suri said.

"He's coming upriver right now with the people who own him," Mara said, "and somewhere inside all that debt and habit there's twenty years of a man who taught me to breathe underwater. I'm not done with him. Neither is the truth." He turned for the gear locker, then stopped, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "And if I'm wrong about him — if there's nothing left in him but the sale — then I'd rather find out at the waterline than wonder about it for another twelve years."

Outside, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, a sound like the world clearing its throat before it speaks.

I went back to the radio room and swept the bands one more time, out of habit, out of grief, out of the stubborn faith that somewhere in all that static a voice might still say *all well* and mean it.

What I found instead was a transmission, brief and unencrypted, on a fisherman's working channel — a frequency so humble no navy man would bother monitoring it. A position. A time. Slack water, sixth tide.

And then four words, in a voice I'd known for twenty years, rough as rope and steady as a mooring line:

*"Hold the line, boy."*

Doss Harrow. Signaling on the one band the navy never listens to. Either warning us away from a trap, or walking out ahead of us to meet it.

I put my hand on the transmitter and didn't key it. Some messages you receive alone, and carry.

The sixth tide was coming. So, it seemed, was Doss.
