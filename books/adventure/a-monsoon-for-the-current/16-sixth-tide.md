## 16. Sixth Tide

The tide table didn't lie. That was the one honest thing left in the gorge.

I sat in the wheelhouse of Kade's boat with the paper flattened under my palm like I could hold the water still by pressing on it. Sixth tide. After this one, the river would drop and the ice upstream would come down like a door slamming, and everything below the narrows—the wreck, my brother's last pages, whatever truth was still wedged in that drowned steel—would be sealed for a season. Or forever. The glacier wasn't sentimental. It had never once cared what any of us needed.

"You're doing the thing again," Suri said from the doorway.

"What thing."

"The staring-at-the-tide-table-like-it-owes-you-money thing." She came in carrying two mugs of something that claimed to be coffee and handed me the less chipped one. "It doesn't owe you money, Mara. It owes you six hours. Then it's done with all of us."

"Six hours is generous of it."

"It's the only generous thing in this river." She leaned against the chart table and looked out at the gray water. Downstream, around the first bend, the navy patrol was still working the wrong channel—we'd heard their sonar pinging all night, patient as a clock, searching a stretch of river where the deepest thing was an old logging snag and a rumor. Ilo's diversion had held. Two nights of false transponder echoes, a decoy light on a raft set adrift into the side channel, and the gray boat had taken the bait like it was starving.

"They'll figure it out," I said.

"Sure. In about seven hours."

"Then we'd better be quick."

Kade came down the companionway without knocking, because Vessna Kade had never knocked on anything in her life, including doors, decisions, and other people's patience. She looked like she hadn't slept, which was fair, since none of us had, but she wore it the way she wore everything—like a coat she'd chosen.

"Weather window holds till midday," she said. "After that the wind comes up the gorge and the surface chop makes the descent line a lottery. We go now, or we write letters to the glacier apologizing for wasting its time."

"You've been saving that one," Suri said.

"I save all my good ones for moments of maximum uselessness." Kade dropped into the chair across from me and put her hands flat on the table, mirroring mine over the tide table, so that between us we were pinning the whole day down like a specimen. "Mara. Before we run it. One question, and I want the ugly answer, not the polite one."

"Ask."

"If you go down there and the last pages aren't there—if the collapse took them, or the navy got them years ago, or your brother never wrote them at all—are you coming back up?"

The wheelhouse went quiet except for the engine idling below us, that deep patient thrum, the sound of a machine that did not care about anyone's brother.

"I don't know," I said.

Kade nodded slowly, like that was the correct answer, which maybe it was. "Good. Polite answers sink divers. Ugly ones float." She stood. "Rig the lines. We run the narrows on the turn."

* * *

Running the narrows on the turn is a phrase that sounds adventurous and means threading a boat through a channel that shrinks by the minute while the current changes its mind about which direction it hates you. Kade drove. I don't mean she steered—I mean she drove, the way you drive a animal you respect and don't trust. Suri called the depths off the sounder in a voice gone flat and fast, and I stood at the bow with a boathook I never once used, watching the black walls of the gorge slide past close enough to touch, streaked with the pale scars of old ice.

Twelve years ago my brother's expedition had come up this same channel. I tried to see it as he would have—young, loud, certain, the whole river an adventure with his name pre-written on it—and mostly what I saw was how little the river had changed and how much we had. The warning buoys still leaned where they'd always leaned, rusted to their moorings, listing notices no one had read in a decade. The navy never maintained them. The navy never maintained anything it wanted forgotten.

We came through the last pinch with a meter to spare on either side and slid into the wide dark pool where the wreck lay below, and the silence after the narrows was so complete I could hear my own pulse agreeing with the engine.

"There," Suri said, pointing.

The buoy we'd rigged on the first dive was still there—or half of it. It had been dragged, the line cut clean, the marker hanging sideways in the water like a hanged man. Someone had found our entry point.

Kade saw it the same moment I did. Her jaw tightened. "Navy?"

"Navy cuts sloppy," I said. "That's a knife work. Clean." I thought of the salvage cuts on the hull, years old, precise, professional. Whoever had been here before us had been back. Or had never entirely left.

"Could be Doss's people," Suri said quietly. Nobody said *the navy* anymore; we'd all agreed without agreeing that saying it aloud invited it, the way saying a drowning man's name over open water does.

"Doesn't matter," Kade said. "It matters in about nine hours. Right now it matters zero. Suit up."

* * *

The water took me the way it always did—not gently, not cruelly, just completely, the way a debt collector takes your collateral. Cold closed over my head and the world narrowed to the circle of my lamp and the rhythm of my own breathing, which I made myself slow down, breath by breath, because panic spends air faster than work does and today air was the only currency I had.

The descent line ran down along the hull into the dark. At forty feet the wreck emerged from the gloom the way large things do underwater—not appearing but assembling, piece by piece, out of pieces: a rail, a davit, the torn mouth of the cargo hatch. My brother had died inside that geometry. Or he hadn't. Both possibilities hung in the water around me like silt.

Inside, the corridors I'd mapped on earlier tides were worse. The fifth-tide surge had shifted things—a bulkhead that had been passable was now folded inward, and the route to the lower cabin where I'd found the logbook's chamber ran through a section that groaned when the current pushed through it. Not a creak. A groan. A structural opinion being voiced.

I checked my gauges. Plenty. I went in anyway. Desperate clarity, Kade had called it once, mocking me, and she'd been right—it feels exactly like this, like walking into a collapsing house because the truth is in the attic and the fire hasn't reached the stairs yet.

The flooded hold opened around me, black on black. My lamp swept across the collapsed corridor where Kade's diver had died on the second tide—Olek, his name had been, Olek who laughed too loudly on deck and went silent in the wrong place—and I gave him the three seconds of stillness I could afford, then kept moving, because the dead are patient and the tide is not.

The sealed cabin door hung where I'd left it, pried open on the fourth tide. Inside, the captain's safe stood gutted—I'd taken the documents on the third, the manifests, the sale orders, the paper trail proving the navy had sold the cargo out of a "lost" ship and let the crew drown to keep the arithmetic quiet. But the logbook itself had been incomplete. Pages torn out. Final entries missing. And Suri, going through the recovered papers with her archivist's patience, had found the reference: *remainder secured below, aft compartment, behind the plate.* Behind the plate. A false bulkhead panel, diver-installed, someone's private cache.

My brother's private cache, if the handwriting in the surviving pages meant anything. And it meant everything. *Dated after the official death date.* Twelve years of grief built on a lie, and the lie had a date on it.

The aft compartment was a crawl. The plate was where the notes said it would be—bolted, not welded, by hands in a hurry. I got my pry bar under it and worked it while the current breathed through the corridor behind me, pushing and releasing, pushing and releasing, like the wreck was trying to decide whether to inhale.

The panel came free with a shriek of metal that sounded enormous underwater and probably was nothing. Behind it: a waterproof case, military green, latched, furred with twelve years of silt.

I pulled it against my chest and felt my heart hammering against it, and for one stupid second I wanted to open it right there, forty feet down in a dying ship, read my brother's last words by lamplight in the cold. I didn't. Air discipline. Tide discipline. You do the job in the water and the feeling on the surface. That was the first rule Doss ever taught me, back when his lessons were the truest things in my life.

I turned for the exit.

And my lamp caught movement in the corridor. Not silt. Not fish. A shape, human-sized, at the far end of the passage, light glinting off a mask plate.

Then a second light, further back, near the hatch.

Two divers. Coming in.

* * *

On the surface, I broke water into wind that hadn't been there an hour ago, chop already whitecapping in the pool, the weather window closing early like everything else in this gorge. Kade hauled me over the gunwale by the tank harness, and Suri stripped my mask, and I got the case onto the deck between us, green and dripping and heavier than lead.

"That's it?" Kade said. "That's all of it?"

"That's the rest of it." I pulled my hood back. "Kade. There were divers on the wreck. Two. They saw me."

Her face did something complicated and settled on fury. "Through the narrows? In this building sea? Nobody runs the narrows in this—"

"Doss could."

The name landed on the deck between us like the case had. Suri looked away. Kade stared at me for a long three seconds, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I was doing: the cut buoy line, the clean knife work, the years of information flowing out of our trade like bilge water, all of it funneling down to one pair of hands. Hands that had taught me to tie my first bowline. Hands that had carried me, half-drowned and nine years old, out of my first bad surf.

"He wouldn't come himself unless they made him," Suri said. "Or unless he wanted to warn you."

"Or both," I said. "Doss has spent twelve years being both."

Kade turned to the helm. "Then we assume they know where we are and we assume the navy figures it out within the hour. Suri, get the case dry-bagged and strapped. Mara—" she stopped, and for a moment the fleet captain fell away and it was just a tired woman on a small boat looking at a man holding his dead brother's last words in a green box. "Open it," she said quietly. "Whatever's in there. Open it before someone takes the choice away from you."

I knelt on the pitching deck and popped the latches.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as the day it was hidden: a final bundle of pages, dense with my brother's handwriting, and beneath them a sealed envelope with a single word on it in block capitals, the ink faded but unmistakable.

*MARA.*

The wind gusted. Upstream, faint under the sound of the water, came a long, deep groan—the glacier clearing its throat, the ice beginning to move.

And downstream, around the bend, a gray shape slid into view at the mouth of the narrows, running lights doused, coming straight for us.
