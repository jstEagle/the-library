## 17. Doss at the Waterline

I've held a gun on men before. It's a thing the trade teaches you eventually, whether you want it taught or not—there's always somebody downstream who thinks your find is his, and there's always a moment when words stop working and steel has to do the talking. I've stood in the rain with a pistol leveled at strangers and felt nothing but the cold of it.

But I'd never pointed one at family before.

The skiff cut through the sixth tide's gray water with Mara at the tiller and Kade crouched low beside him, and Suri up front with her eyes on the wreck's black shoulder rising out of the current like a whale that had decided to die standing up. I knew they were coming before I saw them. I'd known for two days, since the moment I keyed the patrol frequency with a hand that shook worse than it had any right to, an old man's tremor or a guilty man's, hard to say which, probably both.

They were coming to the wreck because there was nowhere else left to go. And I was here because I had told the navy exactly where here was.

* * *

Let me tell you how a man arrives at a waterline with a gun and a debt and forty years of salt in his joints.

You start young. You start as a boy on a salvage barge with no name worth keeping, and an old diver named Harrow—you get the name from him, you wear it like a coat that never quite fits—teaches you knots and depths and the arithmetic of breath. You learn that loyalty is the only currency that doesn't rust. He says it so many times it stops sounding like wisdom and starts sounding like weather. You believe it the way you believe the tide comes in: not as a choice, just as the shape of the world.

Then the old man drowns, because the sea collects its debts too, and you keep the coat anyway.

Mara Reyes was eight years old when I pulled him off a dock in Corren's Reach, all elbows and fury, orphaned by a storm and by whatever slower storms had come before it. His brother Teo was already gone by then—gone upriver, gone into the navy's silence—and the boy had been left to rot among crate-stacks and crane-hooks. I fed him. I taught him to dive. I taught him the same arithmetic my own teacher taught me: a man is measured by what he keeps, and what he keeps is his people.

I never taught him the other lesson. The one I learned later, alone, in bad years: that a man can be kept, too. That debt doesn't care whose side you're on. That loyalty, once it's owed instead of given, is just another word for chain.

The navy owned me eleven years before Kade ever did. Small things at first. A blind eye here, a signature there. Then the year Teo Reyes went upriver with nine men and came back as nothing—not bodies, not gear, not even a rumor the navy would confirm—I signed something. I've never told anyone what it said. I'll tell you this much: it bought my silence about their silence, and the price was that I could never again look a Reyes in the eye without doing arithmetic I hated.

Twelve years of it. Twelve years of watching Mara grow into a diver better than me, quieter than me, carrying his brother like a stone sewn into his chest, and every year the debt got heavier and the habit got deeper, until telling myself *next year I'll come clean* sounded exactly like telling myself *next tide I'll quit the water.*

And then Vessna Kade found out what I was. Not the navy part—that she still doesn't know, God help me—but the useful part. That I knew these waters, these wrecks, this river, and that I knew Mara Reyes better than anyone alive. She hired me the way you hire a compass. And I let her, because a chained man tells himself the chain is a rope, and a rope is almost freedom.

* * *

The skiff came around the wreck's northern shoulder where the current slackened, and I stepped out from behind the ice-scarred piling where I'd been waiting, and I raised the gun, and I watched three faces do three different things.

Suri went still the way young divers go still—total, animal, every muscle listening. Kade's eyes narrowed and her hand drifted toward her belt, calculating odds, because that woman calculates everything, including the odds that an old man's hands are too cold to shoot straight. They weren't. I wish they had been.

And Mara—Mara looked at me the way you look at a house you grew up in when someone else is living in it now. Recognition first. Then the slow, terrible work of rearranging everything behind the recognition.

"Doss," he said. Just the name. He didn't waste breath asking why. He'd read the patrol boat's movements same as I had; he knew someone had sold them. He'd just spent five tides hoping the seller wasn't the man who taught him to tie his first bowline.

"Drop the tank straps," I said. "Slow."

"You're pointing that at Suri," he said. "Point it at me."

"I'm pointing it at whoever moves." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Nobody's dying today if nobody's stupid."

Kade laughed. Short, hard, no humor in it. "Harrow. You treacherous old barnacle. What did they pay you?"

"Nothing you'd understand," I said, which was true, because what they paid me was continuation—the right to keep being the man I'd already agreed to be, which is the only payment a coward ever really collects.

"The navy's boat is in the south channel," I went on. "Forty minutes, maybe less, once I signal. You turn this skiff around, you run downriver, you might make the narrows before they close on you. Kade, take your people and go. This isn't your grave."

"And whose is it?" Mara asked quietly.

I couldn't answer that. So I answered a different question, the one underneath it, the one he was really asking with those eyes that had learned to read water from me and were now reading me like bad water.

"The log," I said. "Whatever you pulled off that wreck. Give it to me. I'll get it to the coast, I'll see it printed, I swear it on Harrow's name—" and I heard how that sounded, swearing on my own dead teacher to prove my honesty, and Mara heard it too, and something in his face closed like a hatch.

"You had twelve years to print anything," he said. "You had twelve years to say one word."

"Yes."

"That's it? Yes?"

"What do you want, boy—a speech?" The gun was getting heavy. Guns get heavy fast when your arms know they're wrong. "You want me to tell you I'm sorry? Fine. I'm sorry. I'm sorry the way a drowning man is sorry, which is to say completely and uselessly. I made a bargain when you were fourteen years old and I have been paying on it ever since, and the interest, Mara—the interest is that I sat across a table from you last winter and ate your soup and listened to you talk about finding Teo's log someday, and I nodded, and I meant the nodding, and I still sent word to the navy the day you took Kade's contract."

Suri made a small sound. Not quite a sob. The sound of a young person discovering that adults are mostly wreckage too.

* * *

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, and I suppose a man holding a gun at a waterline ought to be entitled to one confession, since it may be his last:

I didn't signal the patrol because of the debt. Not today. Today I signaled because I thought I could control it. Forty years in the trade and I still believed, this morning, that I could stand between Mara and the navy the way I once stood between him and a collapsing crane, and walk everyone out alive. Tell them where the skiff would be, meet it myself, talk, stall, trade myself for the boy. An old man's vanity. The belief that you're still the load-bearing wall in a building that finished settling decades ago.

But then I got here early, and I dove the shallows along the hull while I waited, because old habits swim on their own, and I found the corridor. The collapsed one. The one that killed Kade's diver on the second tide. And wedged in the collapse, half-buried, I found a boot. Navy issue. Twelve years old, maybe more, preserved in the cold like everything else this river keeps.

Teo Reyes' size. Or close enough. Close enough that I surfaced and vomited bilge into the current and understood, finally, at sixty-one years of age, what I had helped bury. Not cargo. Not embarrassment. Nine men. Nine men who had found out what the navy was selling off that wreck and been left in the dark to drown with the knowledge. And I had signed the paper that kept it quiet, and taken the pension, and called it loyalty.

Loyalty. Harrow's word. The currency that doesn't rust. Standing there in the shallows with that boot under my heel, I finally understood that I had spent forty years polishing a coin that was counterfeit from the mint. Loyalty to whom? To the men who owned me? That's not loyalty, that's rent. Loyalty is the boy in the skiff in front of me, who came back to a river that ate his brother because some truths don't stay sunk no matter who signs what.

So no, I didn't raise the gun because I'm theirs. I raised it because I'm mine—because the last cowardly instinct of a cowardly life is to manage the disaster you made, to hold the door yourself rather than let it be kicked in.

And because some part of me, the part that isn't rotten yet, wanted one last hour of being the man Mara thought I was, before the navy arrived and made me officially the man I actually am.

* * *

"Mara," I said. "Listen to me. The patrol has your description, Kade's registry numbers, and orders to seize anything recovered from the wreck and detain the crew. Detain. That word has a history on this river. You know what happened to the survey team in spring. You know what happened to Teo's crew. Nobody who goes onto that gray boat comes back off it with their story intact."

"So help us," Kade said. "Put the gun down and help us, and maybe I forget the last ten minutes."

"I can't put it down. If I put it down they'll ask why I failed, and if I fail they stop needing me, and if they stop needing me—" I stopped. There was no elegant way to finish that sentence. "A chained man doesn't get to choose when the chain comes off. He only gets to choose which direction he pulls."

"That's a hell of a philosophy," Kade said. "Does it come with a refund?"

"Hush, woman, I'm confessing."

"You're stalling," she said. "You're stalling and we're burning tide."

She was right, damn her. The water was already talking. Downstream the current had picked up its morning voice, that deep glottal mutter the gorge makes when the glacier upstream starts shedding its winter weight, and every diver on this coast knows that voice the way farmers know thunder. The window was closing. Six tides, they'd said, and this was the sixth, and the river was counting louder than any of us.

Mara hadn't moved through any of it. He sat with the tiller in his fist and looked at me, and I watched him do the thing I taught him to do on a wreck site: read the structure before you trust it. Find the load paths. Decide what holds and what gives.

"You're going to let us pass," he said at last. "Or you're going to shoot me. Those are the options, Doss. There's no third one where you signal your friends and we all have tea."

"There might be," I said, and my voice cracked on it, an old man's crack, and I hated it. "There might be a third one. Get your log. Get in the water. When the patrol comes, they come to me. I'm the informant. I'm the asset. I'll tell them you doubled back downriver, I'll burn every minute I've got left in their ledger, and you take the current out through the narrows while they're looking at an old man standing in the shallows holding a gun on nobody."

Silence, except the river.

"They'll kill you," Suri said. Young voice, steady anyway. Braver than mine had ever been at that age.

"They might," I admitted. "Or they might decide a traitor who's served his use isn't worth the paperwork. Either way, boy, it's the first honest trade I've made in twelve years, and I'd as soon make it while I can still stand in moving water without falling down."

Kade was watching me with those merchant's eyes, running the numbers, and I could see her arrive at the sum: an old man's word against a patrol boat's schedule, with her ship and her skin as the stake. "Why?" she asked. Not cruel. Genuinely asking. "After all of it. Why now?"

I looked at Mara.

"Because he measures his life in miles," I said. "Has since he was a boy. Runs from port to port, wreck to wreck, never stays anywhere long enough to owe anybody anything. I taught him that too—God forgive me, I taught him that running was freedom. And I'm not going to die as the reason he never learns different." I lowered the gun. All the way down. Turned it and handed it grip-first toward the skiff, an offer and a surrender in one motion. "Take it. Take me as your hostage if you want insurance. Tie me in the bilge. But get your brother's log out of this river, Mara Reyes, and you get it to people who will read it out loud, in daylight, in front of witnesses. Promise me that and do whatever you like with the rest of me."

* * *

He didn't take the gun. He looked at it turning slowly in my hand, and then at me, and his face did something complicated that I'll carry down with me wherever I end up—grief and fury and, worst of all, something that might have been love, still alive in there after everything, the way a coal stays alive under ash.

"You absolute old fool," he said, and his voice broke too, and neither of us pretended otherwise. "Get in the skiff."

Upstream, the glacier spoke.

Not groaned—spoke. A sound like the world clearing its throat, deep enough to feel in the teeth, rolling down the gorge and slapping off the canyon walls and coming back doubled. The water shivered. The wreck's black shoulder seemed to lean. Suri grabbed the gunwale and Kade said a word in whatever language she swears in, and I stood in the shallows with an unloaded-feeling hand and understood that the river had stopped negotiating.

That was no calving warning. That was the overture. The whole valley was about to come down that channel, and the navy's boat was anchored in it, and the narrows would close like a fist.

"Skiff," Mara said again. "Now, Doss. We're all going to be in it, or none of this means anything."

I waded toward him through water that was already beginning to pull harder than it should, six hours ahead of any tide table, and behind us, up the gorge, the ice began to move.
