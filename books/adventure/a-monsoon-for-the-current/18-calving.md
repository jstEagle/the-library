## 18. Calving

The glacier spoke first.

Not a groan this time—those had been coming all night, long complaints of old ice shifting in its sleep—but a crack, sharp as a rifle shot, rolling down the gorge and bouncing off the walls like it was looking for someone to blame. I was forty feet down on the anchor line when I heard it through the water, and every diver knows what that sound means. The river doesn't warn you twice.

I came up fast, too fast, and broke surface with my regulator still hissing.

"Surge!" I shouted at Suri, who was already halfway up the mast with binoculars. "Get us off the wreck!"

"I see it!" Her voice cracked on the second word. She pointed upstream, past the gray shoulder of the patrol boat anchored in the narrows, to where the glacier's face hung over the channel like a rotten tooth in a blue jaw. A seam had opened along its base, weeping meltwater in ropes. "Mara—the whole face is moving."

Kade stood at the wheel of her own boat fifty yards downstream, and even across the water I could see her doing the arithmetic every salvage captain does at moments like this: what's left below against how many seconds we have to live. Six tides. We'd promised ourselves six tides, and the glacier had just torn up the contract and eaten it.

The final log pages were still down there. Forty feet down, inside a hull that had spent twelve years learning how to collapse, in a corridor I'd mapped by touch because my lamp kept dying. My brother's handwriting waited for me in the dark like a letter he'd been trying to mail since before they declared him dead.

And between me and the wreck, anchored square across the channel, sat the navy patrol with its boarding line already rigged and its loudspeaker warming up.

* * *

They'd found us an hour after dawn. Of course they had. Doss had signaled them from Kade's deck two nights ago—a single lamp flash, three short, the old coastguard courtesy code, so casual I'd watched him do it and thought nothing of it. That was Doss all over. He could betray you with such competence it looked like kindness.

Now the loudspeaker crackled across the water, that flat official voice that always sounds like it's reading your eulogy: *"Unregistered vessels in restricted waters. You are ordered to stand down and prepare for boarding. This is your final notice."*

Final notice. They'd been giving us final notices since the third tide. Somewhere in a navy office there was probably a form for it, FINAL NOTICE, boxes to tick, a line for how many witnesses needed erasing afterward.

Kade's answer was to put her helm hard over and run for the wreck anchorage, using the reef shelf as a shield. My boat followed. Behind us the patrol's launch dropped off its davits with a splash, outboard roaring, six men in gray with rifles slung across their chests the way men carry them when they're hoping not to use them and one man carrying his the other way, muzzle down and ready, because he'd been told what the mission actually was.

I knew that walk. I'd taught half of it to him myself, thirty years ago, on a dock in Saltmere, back when Doss Harrow could out-dive any man on the coast and thought rules were suggestions made by people who couldn't swim.

He was in the lead boat now. Standing beside the officer with the megaphone, pointing at our anchorage, playing his role right down to the last gesture.

Except his hand wasn't pointing at the divers in the water. It was pointing at the empty stretch of channel south of the reef, where the current ran hardest and no boat would sensibly go—and the launch turned that way, obediently, buying us minutes we hadn't earned.

"Doss," I said, to nobody. To the water. To twelve years of miles.

* * *

There wasn't time to think about it, which was probably the only mercy of the morning. Suri held our position behind the reef while I went down the line again, alone, because Kade's divers were still inside the wreck recovering the cargo manifests she wanted and somebody had to get the log.

The cold hit at ten feet and got honest at thirty. Below me the wreck lay on its shelf like a sleeping animal, and I dropped through the broken cargo hatch into the hold where I'd found the logbook two tides ago, where the waterproof pages had opened under my glove light and shown me my brother's handwriting dated four months after the navy said he drowned. Four months. They'd let them live down here, trapped, radioing for help that never came, while officials ashore signed papers about cargo tonnage and compensation and silence.

I stuffed the logbook against my chest under the strap of my harness. Sealed cabin documents were already in the bag at my hip. Everything else—the manifests, the pay ledgers, the names of the officers who'd sold the crew's lives by the ton—was evidence enough to burn a career or twenty.

That's when the second crack came, and this one I felt in my teeth.

The water moved. Not current—current has manners, current comes from somewhere and goes somewhere. This was the whole ocean flinching. The pressure wave rolled through the gorge ahead of the flood surge, and I heard it coming the way you hear a train through a wall: a deep rising note, getting louder, getting closer, the glacier finally letting go of whatever it had been holding for ten thousand years.

I kicked for the hatch. Above me, faint and far, I heard the loudspeaker voice cut off mid-sentence.

* * *

I surfaced into chaos.

The surge hadn't arrived yet—it was still a mile upstream, a brown wall of displaced water eating the channel—but everything downstream of it was already wrong. The current had reversed and doubled. Our boats were dragging anchors. And the navy launch was alongside Kade's vessel, lines being thrown, gray figures clambering aboard while Kade's crew stood in a knot near the wheel with their hands up and their faces saying exactly what they thought of the whole arrangement.

Suri hauled me over the gunwale by the collar, which hurt, and then hugged me, which hurt more.

"You've got it?" she said. "Tell me you've got it."

"I've got it."

"Then we need to leave. Now. That wall of water doesn't care whose side we're on."

She was right, and I was already moving to the winch when I saw the rest of it: the second launch, the one Doss had misdirected south, had come around the reef and was closing on my boat. Four men. Rifles unslung now. And at the bow, holding a boathook like he'd done it ten thousand times, because he had—

Doss.

He looked older than he had two days ago. Or maybe I'd just finally stopped lying to myself about how old he'd always been. His eyes found mine across thirty feet of violent water, and something passed over his face that I'll be untangling for the rest of my life. Not guilt exactly. Guilt would have been easier. It was more like a man reading his own ledger and finding the balance not at all what he'd hoped.

"Mara Reyes!" the officer shouted through his megaphone, though the wind was taking half of it. "Stand down! Surrender the recovered materials!"

Recovered materials. Twelve dead men, reduced to inventory. My brother, filed under materials.

"We're standing down!" I shouted back, and raised both hands, empty, the way you do when you want them to come close enough to hear the truth. "Come aboard and I'll give you everything!"

It worked, because greed works, and because Doss was already throwing the line.

They came aboard fast and professional, spreading out, weapons low but present. The officer—a lieutenant, young, with the particular stiffness of a man following orders he hasn't finished disliking—held out his hand for the logbook.

And the glacier calved.

* * *

You don't hear a calving so much as inherit it. The whole face of the ice came away at once—millions of tons of it, a cliff deciding to become a wave—and the sound arrived through the water and the rock and the air simultaneously, one enormous white noise that erased everything else. Then the wave itself, maybe fifteen feet of brown churning fury, rounding the bend of the gorge with the patience of something that has never once been late.

Every man on my deck turned to look at it. You can't not. It's the oldest reflex there is—see the big thing coming, forget everything else.

Doss didn't turn.

He was standing closest to me, close enough that I could see the salt in his beard and the scar on his knuckle from the day he taught me to tie a bowline, and while every other man on that deck watched the wave, Doss Harrow looked at me, and then at the officer's holstered sidearm, and then at the open water south of the reef where the current ran hardest and no sensible boat would go.

Then he did the thing I will carry until they bury me.

He grabbed the lieutenant's radio handset and shouted into it—coordinates, false ones, the deep channel north where the surge would sweep anything floating straight into the rocks—and shoved the officer backward into his own men, and screamed at the top of a voice worn down by thirty years of salt: "GO! Get off this boat, boy, GO—" and then he turned and put both hands on the rail and looked at me one last time.

"South channel," he said. "Hard. Don't stop for anything. Don't you dare stop for me."

"Doss—"

"The log, Mara." The wave was two hundred yards out and closing and his voice didn't shake at all, which broke me worse than shaking ever could. "Read it out loud someday. Where people can hear. That's all I ever—that's the only payment I—" He stopped himself. Old habit. Men like Doss die mid-sentence because they spend their whole lives editing.

The wave took the patrol launch first. I saw it go—flipped end over end like a toy in a bathtub, gray uniforms scattered across brown water—and then it took the gap between our boat and the reef, and the world became noise and water and Suri screaming my name and my hands on the wheel, hauling south, hard, exactly where he'd told me, because the last thing Doss Harrow ever asked of me was the one thing I couldn't refuse.

I looked back once. Just once.

He was in the water. He'd gone in deliberately—I'm sure of it, sure in the way you're only sure about things that ruin you—because if he'd stayed aboard he'd have been arrested with the rest of them, and the log would have gone into an evidence locker, and the truth would have drowned quietly in a filing cabinet the way his first crew had drowned quietly in this same channel twelve years ago. So he'd bought our escape with the only currency he had left, and the surge closed over his head like a door.

I dove for him. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone: I tied the wheel off, I stripped my harness, and I went over the rail into a flood surge to reach a man who had betrayed me, because he was also the man who taught me to swim, and love doesn't audit its accounts.

The water hit me like a building falling. It rolled me, tore at the logbook strapped to my chest, filled my ears with a roar that had no bottom. I swam until my arms stopped answering, swept south in the exact direction he'd sent us, searching brown water for a gray head that never surfaced. Once I thought I saw a hand—open, reaching, the way he used to reach for me off the dock at Saltmere when I jumped before I could swim—and I drove toward it and caught nothing but the current, which had already decided.

Suri's arm hooked around my throat and dragged me back toward the boat. I fought her. God forgive me, I fought her, and she held on anyway, because she was stronger than grief and I wasn't.

They pulled me aboard half-drowned. Kade's boat came alongside ours in the lee of the reef, both of us running south now with the surge at our backs like a hand shoving us toward the rapids, and through the spray I saw the wreck one last time.

The wave reached it before we did. Forty feet of water and twelve years of patience collapsed the old hull in a single exhalation—cargo hatch, sealed cabin, brother's chamber, all of it folding inward and downward into the silt, going wherever the river keeps the things it decides are finished. The evidence I'd risked six tides for was gone. The manifests, the ledgers, the proof of the officers who'd sold those men—they were silt now, and would be silt forever.

Everything except what was pressed against my chest.

I put my hand over the harness strap and felt the logbook's shape through the wet fabric, small and stubborn and alive, and I understood suddenly what Doss had known standing at that rail: the paper was never the point. The point was the reading. The point was a tavern full of strangers hearing a dead man's voice, and the truth leaving my hands and belonging to everyone, which is the only place truth survives.

"South channel," Suri shouted, hauling the wheel over. "Rapids ahead. Hold on to something."

Ahead, the gorge narrowed to a throat of white water, and behind us the patrol boat—if it still floated—wasn't following, and above it all the glacier kept calving, piece after piece after piece, like the world emptying its pockets.

I held the logbook tighter and steered into the roar.
