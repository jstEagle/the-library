## 19. What the River Takes

The water hit me like a door slamming.

I'd been at forty meters when the glacier let go—felt it first as a pressure change in my ears, a deep intestinal groan that had nothing to do with my body and everything to do with the mountain of ice two miles upstream deciding, after ten thousand years of patience, to stop being patient. Then the current came. Not water moving so much as the whole river picking itself up and relocating, all at once, with me inside it.

I grabbed the logbook tighter against my chest. That was the only thought left in me: *hold it*. Everything else—the regulator hose whipping across my face, the silt going from brown to black to something thicker than either, the deck beam that clipped my shoulder hard enough to make me see white sparks through the murk—all of that was weather. The book was the point.

*Hold it.*

The wreck groaned around me. I've heard ships die before; it's part of the trade, the way a doctor hears bones break. But this was different. This was a ship that had been dying for twelve years finally being allowed to finish, and the sound it made wasn't metal tearing so much as metal *sighing*—a long, structural exhalation as the corridor behind me folded shut like a hand closing over coins. Somewhere in that collapse went the sealed cabin. The manifests. The navy's neat little signatures on other people's lives. All of it crushed into a geometry no diver would ever open again.

Good, some cold practical corner of my brain noted. No one can dispute what we found if no one can go back to check it.

The same corner of my brain also noted that Doss Harrow was somewhere above me, or had been, holding a boarding line against men with rifles while a river came apart around him, and that thought didn't fit anywhere so I put it in the same pocket as the book and kept swimming.

* * *

Let me tell you what drowning almost feels like when you're good at not doing it.

It feels like arithmetic. You count your air—you don't look at the gauge, because looking takes light and light is gone, you put your fingers on the gauge and read it by shape, the way Doss taught me when I was fourteen and cocky and he said, *Boy, the river doesn't care how cocky you are, it cares whether your hands know things.* You count the kicks between known points: thirty strokes to the hull breach, fifteen up the guide line, then free ascent with your eyes on your bubbles because bubbles always know which way is heaven even when you don't.

Except there were no known points anymore. The breach I'd come through was gone—I knew it was gone because I swam into where it should have been and found steel, smooth and whole, like the wreck had healed over the wound just to spite me. The guide line was gone too, or rather it was everywhere, a loose snarl of rope drifting through the black like weed, and every piece of it felt exactly like every other piece and none of them went anywhere I could trust.

So I did the thing they tell you never to do, and I stopped thinking.

Not panic. Panic is thinking too fast. I mean I let my hands do the knowing. My left hand found the hull and followed it, reading rivets like braille, and my body remembered twelve years of miles—every wreck off every coast, every dark hold, every time the sea tried to keep something and I argued otherwise—and my hands walked me along that dead ship's spine until they found the gash we'd cut on the second tide, still open, still breathing, spitting debris past me like a crowd fleeing a burning building.

I went out with the crowd.

* * *

The surface, when I reached it, was not a surface. It was an argument. White water standing up in places water shouldn't stand, foam in slabs, and the rain—which had been coming down all day in the polite monsoon way, drumming on things—now falling sideways and hard enough to sting. I got my head above it and breathed, one huge ragged pull, and the first thing I registered besides air was weight against my chest, and my hands were still locked around the logbook, fingers so cramped around its cover that for a second I couldn't have let go if I'd wanted to.

I didn't want to.

"MAR!" Suri's voice, shredded by wind. She was on the rocks at the channel's edge, half in the water, a throw line coiled around her fist like she'd been born holding it. "Mar, left! LEFT!"

I looked left and saw why she was screaming. A tree—a whole tree, roots and all, torn loose somewhere upstream—was cartwheeling toward me through the flood, turning end over end with the slow terrible grace of something that used to be stationary. I dove. The trunk passed overhead close enough that I felt the displacement shove me down, a giant's hand pressing me deeper, and then I was up again and swimming for the rocks and Suri's arm was around my harness strap and Kade's hands were on my legs and together the three of us were hauling me out of the river like fishermen landing something enormous and uncooperative.

I came out of the water on my hands and knees, coughing up half the gorge, and the logbook was still pressed to my chest because my arms wouldn't do anything else. Suri peeled my mask off. Kade knelt in front of me, hair plastered flat, a cut over her eyebrow she hadn't mentioned yet, and she looked at my chest—at the book—and then at my face, and whatever she saw there made her not say anything. Which, from Vessna Kade, was practically a eulogy.

"Doss," I said. It came out as gravel.

Her jaw worked. "The line held," she said. "He held it. They couldn't board." A pause, and her voice did something I'd never heard it do—it dropped all its armor. "Then the surge took the bank. He was right there, Mar. Right there, and the bank just—" Her hands finished the sentence. Opened. Empty.

Ilo Venn was further up the rocks, wrapped in a tarp, radio dead in his lap. He'd seen it too, then. We all had our own private film of it now, and we'd be watching it for the rest of our lives.

* * *

Here's the thing about grief that nobody puts in the brochures: it doesn't wait for a convenient moment. It doesn't schedule itself for after the flood, after the escape, after you're safe and dry with a drink in your hand. It arrives when it arrives, and it arrived for me right there on the wet granite with the river roaring twenty feet below, and it arrived not as sadness but as a memory so specific it felt like an assault:

Doss Harrow, teaching me to tie a bowline, my hands failing it four times running, and him saying—not unkindly, because Doss was never unkind, he was just *occupied*, occupied the way a man is who owes money to dangerous people and has decided the interest is cheaper than the shame—"Your hands know things, boy. Your head's the one that needs convincing."

Twelve years of miles. Every one of them walked beside that man until the last three years, when he started walking beside someone else and lying to me about it with his whole honest face. And the worst part, the part I'll carry past the grave, is that when he drew that gun on us at the waterline yesterday, some traitor part of me had been *relieved*. Relieved to finally have him sorted. Traitor or mentor, pick one, and I can hate you cleanly either way.

But Doss Harrow had never once in his life done anything cleanly. So he'd stood there with the gun shaking, and then the navy boat had come around the bend, and he'd turned it—turned *himself*—into a wall instead, and walls don't get to explain themselves. Walls just hold until the water takes them.

I knelt on that rock and I shook. Not crying, exactly. Something more structural than that. The kind of shaking a building does when the earthquake has passed but the building hasn't gotten the memo yet.

Suri sat down next to me without asking permission and put her shoulder against mine. She's maybe twenty-five and she's lost more to this river than I have, and she didn't say *it'll be okay* or *he made his choice* or any of the things people say when they mean nothing. She just leaned, the way a shore leans against the sea, and let me shake against her until the shaking ran out of fuel.

Kade stood watch. That was her version of leaning.

* * *

When I could stand, I opened the book.

Not all the way. Just enough. Water had found the edges despite everything—the waxed canvas wrap, the sealant, my death grip—but the core pages were dry, and there on the third page was my brother's handwriting, tilted forward the way it always tilted, like each word was leaning into the next one eager to be said.

*Day 41. They came at night. Not pirates—navy launch, running dark. Captain Oyelaran told us to kill our lights and say nothing. Say nothing. As if we hadn't already spent six weeks saying nothing about what's in the hold.*

I closed it. My hands were trembling again and I couldn't read more, not here, not with the river still hungry below us and the sky still falling sideways. Some books you read. Some books you survive. This one I would survive first and read after.

But I held it against my chest again anyway, and I want to be honest about what that felt like, because honesty is the whole point of what comes next. It felt like holding my brother. Twelve years I'd measured my life in miles traveled—away from coasts, away from names, away from the question of what happened to Teo Reyes and the nineteen people who sailed up this gorge with him and never came back. Miles were easy. Miles didn't ask anything of you except more of themselves.

The book weighed maybe four hundred grams dry. It weighed more than every mile I'd ever run.

* * *

We climbed.

Upriver was suicide—the calving wasn't done, the ice shelf upstream was shedding like a dog in summer, and every hour another house-sized chunk came down with a report like artillery. Downriver meant the rapids, the narrows, the stretch of gorge the charts called the Throat with the kind of casual cruelty chartmakers reserve for places that eat boats. And somewhere downstream of that, a gray patrol vessel with our names on a list and Doss's last signal in its log.

But the expedition boat was gone. Taken by the surge, or pinned and stove-in somewhere downstream—we'd heard her scream against rock during the worst of it, a sound I won't describe because I can't do it justice. We had the dinghy, the one we'd stashed at the second-tide camp, and we had ourselves, and we had a waterproof bag containing a dead man's testimony against a living government.

"You understand," Kade said, while Suri and Ilo dragged the dinghy down to the water's edge, "that this stops being salvage the moment we push off. Salvage has courts. Salvage has claims and percentages and lawyers who bill by the syllable." She nodded at the bag strapped across my chest. "That's not salvage. That's a confession, and we're carrying it into a country that doesn't want to hear it."

"I know what it is."

"Do you? Because I've watched you since the first tide, Mar, and I genuinely cannot tell if you're the most calculating man I've ever employed or the most reckless. It's making my job very difficult."

"It's both," Suri called over her shoulder. "It's definitely both."

Kade almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked back up the gorge, at the wall of gray-white ice visible even through the rain, at the place where the wreck had been and now wasn't, where Doss had been and now wasn't, and the smile died before it finished being born.

"For what it's worth," she said quietly, "your brother's crew. The ones the navy let drown. My father ran supply boats on this river. He always said there were nineteen men who went up and twenty stories that came down, and none of them matched." She met my eyes. "I'm not in this for triple pay anymore, Reyes. In case that matters to you."

It mattered. I didn't have words for how much, so I did what Doss taught me: I let my hands know things. I gripped her forearm, diver to diver, and she gripped back, and that was the whole conversation.

* * *

We pushed off into the flood with the last of the light going out of the sky like a tide going out of a bay.

The river had changed its mind about everything. Channels that existed on the charts were hills now; hills that had been hills were channels. Suri took the bow paddle because she read water better than anyone I've ever known, better than Doss, though I'd have denied saying that a week ago. Ilo sat amidships with the tarp over the radio gear, useless and precious, like a priest carrying ashes. Kade and I took the stern, and I kept one hand on the tiller and one on the bag, and the boat went where the river told it, mostly, and where Suri screamed otherwise, occasionally, and the rain hammered down and the gorge walls rose up black on either side like the covers of a book closing around us.

And somewhere in the middle of the Throat—in the worst of it, when the boat was more airborne than afloat and Ilo was praying to a god he claimed he didn't believe in and Suri was laughing, actually *laughing*, wild and young and alive—in the middle of all that, I felt it happen. The release. Not the grief; the grief was staying, it had signed a lease, it was renovating. But underneath the grief, something else: the feeling of a breath held for twelve years finally, finally leaving the body.

They could take the wreck. They had taken it—the river had folded it shut forever, and no navy inquiry, no salvage claim, no bought silence would ever open it again. They could take the boat, the season, the money, the route. They could take Doss, God forgive them, they'd already taken Doss.

But the logbook was against my chest, and my brother's voice was in it, leaning word into word, eager to be said, and the river could rage all it liked.

It wasn't taking this.

The narrows roared ahead, white and total, and Suri pointed her paddle at the one thread of green water threading through the chaos and shouted something none of us would ever agree on the exact wording of later, and we dug in, and the boat leapt—

—and the last thing I saw before the spray took everything was a gray shape far downstream, anchored in the wide water below the gorge, lights blinking their patient official blink.

Waiting for us.
