## 20. Downriver

I have run a lot of water in my life. I've taken loaded barges through the Serrano Narrows with the tide against me and the owner's son drunk at the wheel. I've threaded the Mire Passage in fog so thick I steered by the sound of my own engine coming back off the cliffs. But I had never run rapids that were being born while I watched them.

That's what the calving gave us. Not a river anymore—a river arguing with itself, the old channel tearing loose from the new one, ice the size of chapel roofs grinding downstream like a congregation that had lost its faith in pews. And us in the middle of it, in a survey boat never built for anything braver than taking depth readings for men in offices.

"Starboard!" Suri screamed, and I threw the wheel over, and the world went white and green and sideways.

Let me back up. A captain should narrate her disasters in order; it's the only dignity left to us.

* * *

We'd come out of the gorge with the flood surge still chasing us, the whole upper river standing up on its hind legs behind us like something waking from a long sleep angry. Mara was half-drowned and shivering so hard his teeth sounded like dice in a cup. The logbook was wrapped in oilskin inside his jacket, inside his arms, inside whatever place a man keeps the things he has decided to die holding. Ilo Venn had the tiller because Ilo Venn, bless his terrified heart, was the only one of us whose hands stopped shaking when things got bad instead of starting.

The navy patrol was behind us. That was the arithmetic we were running: glacier astern, gray hull astern, six miles of white water between us and the delta, and a boat that drew too much for any of it.

"Kade." Mara's voice came out scraped raw from the cold water and the shouting. "They'll follow us down."

"They'll try." I checked the fuel gauge, which read like a eulogy. "But this channel narrows at the Splits, and their draft is twice ours. They can either lose the hull or lose us. Patrol captains are many things, but sentimental about their hulls isn't usually one of them."

"You're betting lives on a man's caution."

"I'm betting our lives on a man's pension," I said. "It's the safer wager. Nobody risks a pension for two salvage divers and a dispatcher."

Suri, wedged in the bow with a boat hook she was using mostly as a talisman, called back: "Rocks! Big ones! The kind with opinions!"

That girl had been with me three seasons and had never once described an obstacle without giving it a personality. It should have annoyed me. Instead it steadied me, the way a familiar voice steadies a man in a dark room. You know the shape of the fear when someone names it honestly.

* * *

The Splits came up on us faster than the chart promised, which charts do, being written by people who surveyed the river in summer and will not be running it in a flood.

Two channels. Left: wider, deeper-looking, but choked with the first of the calved ice, bergs jostling each other like commuters. Right: narrow, violent, a chute of standing waves between granite walls, but clear—clear because nothing sane had entered it.

"Left is death by slow crushing," Ilo said, reading the water the way he read everything, carefully and out loud. "Right is death by enthusiasm."

"Enthusiasm," I said, "we can survive. We've been surviving enthusiasm all week." And I took us right.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of the season: I took us right partly because it was the right call and partly because Mara Reyes was watching me to see what kind of captain I was when the ledger balanced against me. He'd spent days suspecting me of every sin short of arson. Maybe including arson. And I had spent those same days discovering that I minded what he thought, which was inconvenient, because I had built forty years of not minding what anyone thought, and it had kept me warm.

The right channel took us like a hand closing. The bow stood up, the stern went light, the engine screamed a note I'd only heard from engines about to file complaints. Suri whooped—not fear, actual joy, which told me something about how I'd been raising my crew—and then we were through, spat out into a broad brown reach below the gorge walls, rocking hard, whole, moving.

Behind us the gray patrol rounded into the Splits, hesitated at the mouth of the right channel—I saw the wake flatten as they throttled down—and turned away. Turned away. Their wake swung wide toward the left channel, then wide again, and then they simply stopped, anchored in the lee below the gorge, a gray dog that had chased the cart as far as its contract allowed.

"They're giving up?" Suri asked.

"They're doing paperwork," I said. "Whatever orders moved them this far upriver, none of them include wrecking a naval vessel in a flood chute to catch three nobodies in a rented survey boat. They'll wait for the water to drop. By then—" I looked upstream, where the ice was still coming down in continent-sized pieces, "—by then there won't be a route to follow us through."

Mara hadn't spoken. He sat with the logbook against his chest, watching the patrol shrink behind us, and there was something in his face I recognized from mirrors: the look of a man who has just learned that the thing hunting him was smaller than his fear of it. Fear makes monsters out of institutions. Institutions, it turns out, are mostly made of men who want to go home.

* * *

We ran the lower reaches through the afternoon, and this is the part I'll remember longest, though nothing dramatic happened in it. No rocks, no ice, no navy. Just four exhausted people in a battered boat, going fast down brown water under a sky the color of old tin, and slowly, without anyone deciding to, becoming something other than strangers thrown together by greed and grief.

It started with food. Suri produced biscuits and a tin of smoked fish from the bow locker, and we passed them around like communion, and eating together does something to people that treaties cannot. Then Ilo, of all people, started talking. He told us about the night the survey team went silent—how he'd sat with the radio for eleven hours calling into static, and how the last transmission had been a voice he half-knew saying *tell them we found it, tell them it wasn't supposed to* —and how he'd carried that sentence alone for weeks because no one above him wanted to hear it.

"We found it," Mara said quietly. "Now it's told."

Ilo nodded and looked out at the water and didn't cry, though his jaw worked like he was negotiating with it.

Then Suri asked Mara the question I'd been chewing on for two days and was too proud to ask. "What's in the book? I mean—we nearly died for it. Twice. Three times if you count the corridor. What's actually in it?"

Mara was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "My brother's handwriting. Dated nine days after the navy declared him dead. Names. Dates. A cargo manifest that doesn't match the official manifest. And a page where he wrote out what the captain of the patrol vessel offered the crew—passage home and silence—in exchange for leaving fourteen men on the wreck when the pumps failed." His voice stayed level the whole way through, which is how I knew it was costing him everything. "Fourteen men. The navy reported six lost. Somebody counted wrong on purpose."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The river talked enough for all of us.

And here is the thing I keep turning over, the thing I suppose I'm writing myself into admitting: twelve years ago, when the Reyes expedition went missing, I was twenty miles downriver with my own fleet, and I heard the rumors—the navy seal, the bought silence—and I did what everyone did. Nothing. Because the fleet that fed me then was owned by men who preferred the rumor dead, and I was young and hungry and good at preferring things too. I told myself the water was high, the season was wrong, there was nothing to be done. There is always nothing to be done. It is the most profitable sentence in the trade.

Mara Reyes dove into that silence for twelve years and came up holding it in his hands. And I helped him get there for triple pay and a grudge against a rival fleet, and somewhere in the black water under the ice I stopped being able to tell the difference between the grudge and the wanting-to-know, until there was only the knowing, and the knowing changed the shape of me.

A person doesn't surface from a thing like that unchanged. I've watched it happen to divers—the deep ones, the ones who go where the wrecks keep their secrets. They come up lighter or heavier, but never the same weight.

* * *

Dusk caught us at the last bend before the delta flats, and I put us into the reed shallows to wait out the dark rather than risk the sandbars blind. We grounded soft in the reeds, killed the engine, and let the river carry its ice past us in the gloom—white shapes sliding seaward, each one carrying a piece of the gorge, a piece of the wreck, a piece of everything that happened up there, dissolving into the salt.

Mara sat beside me at the rail. For a long time neither of us said anything, which felt, strangely, like the first honest conversation we'd had.

"Kade," he finally said. "Back at anchor. When you agreed to split the truth instead of selling it—you could have cut a deal with the navy. Better money than anything the find would pay. Safer, certainly."

"The navy doesn't pay better," I said. "The navy pays in staying quiet, and staying quiet is the one currency I've finally gone bankrupt in." I turned the wheel spoke under my hand, an old habit, steering nothing. "Besides. My father ran salvage his whole life and died owning one boat and a reputation for discretion. Discretion. You know what that word means in our trade? It means you know where the bodies are, and you agree to pretend the water's empty. Forty years I've pretended the water's empty."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen what's in it." I looked over at him. In the dark I could just make out the line of him, the oilskin bundle he hadn't let go of since the fifth tide. "Your brother waited twelve years in that hold for somebody to come down. I'm not proud it took a paid contract to get him fetched. But he's fetched."

Mara exhaled, a long breath, like a man setting down freight. "Doss," he said, and couldn't finish it.

"Doss," I agreed. I'd known Harrow thirty years. He'd taught half the coast to read current, and he'd sold information to whoever held his debts, and he'd stood at the waterline at the end and chosen—late, ruinously late, but chosen. "He was a rotten friend and a good teacher and both of those things were true at the same time, and I'm done trying to make them cancel out."

"He held the line," Mara said. "Long enough."

"Late-arriving choices cost the most," I said. "But they're still worth making. That's the only sermon I've got, and I stole it from the river."

From the bow, Suri's voice drifted back, drowsy: "Captain? When we get to the coast—what happens to us?"

"What happens to you," I said, "is taverns, dry socks, and the best meal your wages can buy. And then, if you're fool enough to stay with me, a season of honest work, because I am officially out of the dishonest kind."

Ilo laughed from the tiller—a small, cracked sound, the first laugh I'd heard from him since the radio went silent. It spread. Even Mara, hugging his brother's last words to his chest in the reeds of a river that had tried to kill us all day, made a noise that in better light might have been called a laugh.

We slept in shifts. The ice went by all night, patient as a funeral, carrying the mountain to the sea.

* * *

We came down onto the delta flats at first light, the water going slack and wide and silver, gulls working the mudflats, and the coast town's masts visible across the shallows like a row of needles waiting to sew us back into the world. Behind us, far upstream, the gorge had closed for the season—white wall to white wall, the route gone, the wreck sealed under new ice and a hundred feet of rearranged river. Whatever else was down there would stay down there. The river keeps what it takes. That used to frighten me. Standing in the bow with the morning coming up, it felt more like mercy.

Ilo raised the radio and called the harbor office, and the harbor office answered, ordinary as bread, asking our cargo and our business, and Ilo looked at me, and I looked at Mara, and Mara said:

"Tell them one survivor, recovered. Tell them we're bringing the rest of it home."

So that's what went out over the water, plain as daylight, on an open channel where anyone could hear it—including, I noted with some satisfaction, the frequency the navy monitored. Let them listen. Let the whole coast listen. The age of pretending the water was empty ended somewhere upriver, in the dark, at the price of a good man and fourteen old ones, and I intend to spend the rest of my career telling the truth at volume.

We crossed the bar at mid-morning, clean water under the keel at last, and the town came up to meet us—docks, smoke, the tavern with the crooked sign where the fleet men drink and lie. Mara stood at the rail with the logbook, looking at the shore like a man reading a difficult letter.

"There's a table in the back of that tavern," he said. "Big one. Room for anyone who wants to hear it."

I brought the boat alongside, and Suri threw the line, and we stepped ashore into the noise and smell of the living world, carrying the dead's testimony up the dock between us.

"Get the word out," Mara said to Ilo. "Tonight. Anyone who'll listen."

Word travels fast in a port town. Faster than ice, faster than tides, faster than regret. By sundown, I suspected, that back room would be full—and by morning, I suspected further, certain people in gray uniforms would wish very much that it weren't.

I tied off the lines myself. Old habit. New hands.
