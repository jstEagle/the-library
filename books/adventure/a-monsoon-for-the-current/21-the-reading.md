## 21. The Reading

The tavern was called the Drowned Gull, which I have always considered the kind of name a place earns rather than chooses. It sat on stilts over the harbor at Port Calloway, and the floorboards flexed underfoot like the deck of a ship that had given up pretending to go anywhere. The beer was bad in the honest way—cheap, warm, poured by a woman who looked like she'd personally fought every war the coast had ever lost.

I hadn't slept in two days. My hands still smelled of river silt no matter how many times I washed them, and there was a bruise on my ribs shaped exactly like the edge of a hatch cover, because of course it was. But my brother's logbook was dry, wrapped in oilcloth, sitting on the table in front of me like a small black heart that had finally stopped beating for someone else's benefit.

Kade sat across from me with her arms folded, which was her way of sitting anywhere. Suri had claimed the bench beside her and was nursing a cider she'd barely touched. Ilo Venn had come down from the relay station with his radio-calloused hands wrapped around a mug of something stronger than coffee, and around us, filling out the room, were maybe thirty people who had heard rumors and followed them like gulls follow a gutting table.

Divers. Dockhands. A retired navy clerk who kept glancing at the door. Two women who ran the chandlery. A boy of maybe fifteen who'd been on the dock when we came in and had simply attached himself to our wake and refused to be shaken off.

"You don't have to do this," Kade said quietly. "You could sell it to a paper. Let professionals handle it."

"I am a professional," I said. "I handle things underwater. Up here I'm just a man with a dead brother and a book."

"That's not nothing."

"No," I agreed. "It's everything. That's the problem."

I picked up the logbook. The leather was swollen from its years in the flooded hold, warped into a shape like a shell, but the pages inside—the ones that mattered—had been sealed in waxed sleeves by a man who knew water the way I did. My brother. Tomás Reyes, official date of death twelve years ago this spring, unofficial date considerably later, according to his own handwriting.

The tavern went quiet the way a harbor goes quiet before weather. Not silent—there was always the slap of water against pilings, the creak of rope—but hushed underneath, attentive.

"His name was Tomás," I said. "Some of you knew him. Some of you worked with him. Some of you were told he died when the *Meridian* went down in the gorge, and you believed it, because believing it was easier than asking why a survey vessel with a full crew and a navy escort sent no distress call."

Nobody moved. Even the barkeep had stopped wiping the counter.

"He didn't die then," I said. "He died eleven days later. And he wrote down why."

I opened to the first marked page and began to read.

* * *

*"Day one after the grounding. We're alive, all nine of us, though Reyes has a bad gash on his shin and Okafor won't stop shivering. The Meridian sits on a shelf at forty meters, hull intact enough. Current is manageable. We've signaled upriver twice. No answer."*

My voice held. I'd expected it not to. There's a strange thing that happens when you read a dead man's handwriting aloud—it stops being grief and becomes cargo, something you carry hand over hand, and the carrying keeps you steady.

*"Day three. A patrol boat came through the gorge today. We fired flares. They saw us—I know they saw us, Okafor watched them alter course toward our smoke before they turned away. Then a launch came down at dusk, alone. Officer named Brann. He told us the Meridian's cargo manifest is now classified and our survival is, quote, 'a complicating factor.' He offered extraction for officers only."*

A murmur went through the room. The retired navy clerk put his head in his hands.

*"Captain Voss refused. Told Brann he'd swim out himself before he left his crew. Brann smiled at that. I'll remember that smile as long as I live, which may not be long, so let the record show: he smiled."*

I paused and drank some of the bad beer. It helped. Bad beer is good for pausing; it gives your mouth something to do besides break.

Suri reached over and touched the edge of the book, not taking it, just resting her fingers there. "Keep going," she said softly.

*"Day five. Brann returned with an offer for everyone: stay quiet about what the Meridian carried, sign statements confirming we sank in open water far downstream, and extraction happens within the week. Voss asked him point-blank what the cargo was. Brann said armaments. Voss said armaments don't need a classified manifest and a drowned crew. Brann stopped smiling."*

*"Day seven. Two men took the offer last night. Went up the gorge with Brann's launch. I don't blame them. Cold does things to a man's principles. Eight of us left."*

*"Day nine. No launch. No patrol boat. Our flares are gone. We've been diving the wreck ourselves—Voss wants proof of what's in the hold, says evidence is the only leverage eight starving men have. He's right. He's usually right. It's infuriating."*

Here my brother's handwriting changed. It got smaller, tighter, the letters of a man writing fast in the dark with numb fingers.

*"Day ten. We opened hold two. It isn't armaments. It's payment. Bullion and bearer notes, sealed crates, shipping marks from three different governments. The navy isn't hiding a weapons shipment. They're hiding a purchase. Someone bought silence about something bigger than a wrecked survey ship, and the Meridian and her crew were the receipt they decided to shred."*

The tavern had gone past murmuring now. People weren't even breathing loud. Outside, the tide slapped against the pilings, patient as ever, keeping its own count.

*"Day eleven. If anyone finds this: the ledger is in the captain's safe, combination is Voss's daughter's birthday, 4-1-9. Copy everything. Names, dates, the transfer codes Brann used on his radio—we copied his frequencies. Don't trust any uniform. Trust the water. The water doesn't take bribes."*

Then, at the bottom of the page, in handwriting that had begun to wander:

*"Mara. If it's you reading this—and God help me, little brother, I hope it's you and nobody else—you always said I measured my life in miles. You were right. So measure yours in tides instead. A tide comes back. Every one, honestly kept, comes back. I love you. Tell Mamá the sea didn't beat me. Men did."*

I stopped.

The room stayed absolutely still, and I stood there holding a dead man's last words to me, words written eleven days after the world had officially ended him, and I understood that I had been carrying this book upriver not to read it but to hear it, and hearing it required other ears. Grief hoarded is just weight. Grief spoken is testimony.

"There's more," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it crack, because hiding it would have been another kind of lie. "There's a list of names. Officers who signed off on leaving the crew. Transfer records. And a final entry, day eleven, hour unknown, that just says: *Brann's launch is coming back down. It's not stopping.*"

I closed the book gently, the way you close a coffin.

"The navy called it a navigational accident," I said. "They paid salvage fleets for twelve years to stay away—not to protect a site, to protect a story. And the men who raised me, some of the best divers I ever knew, took that money and kept that silence, because the alternative was asking questions with bullets in the answers. One of those men was Doss Harrow. He died four days ago holding a boarding line open so the truth could surface instead of him. Whatever else he was, he chose at the end. I'm choosing earlier than that."

Kade unfolded her arms for the first time all evening. "The documents from the captain's safe," she said. "You got them?"

"Got them. Copies made this morning. Three sets, three directions, three ships leaving on tomorrow's tide." I looked around the room. "And the original goes wherever it can do the most damage. Which is why I'm not deciding alone anymore. This—" I put my hand flat on the logbook. "This stopped being mine the moment I read it out loud. It belongs to everyone who just heard it. That's the only safe place for a truth like this. Scattered. Everywhere. Like water."

The chandlery women exchanged a look. The boy at the back stood up on a bench like he was about to give a speech, thought better of it, and sat down again. The retired navy clerk lifted his head, wiped his face with both palms, and said, in a voice worn smooth by decades of filing, "Brann. Commander Aldous Brann. He retired a vice-admiral. He lives in the capital. He gives speeches about maritime honor."

The whole room absorbed that in silence.

"Well," said Kade, and there was something in her voice I'd never heard there before—not calculation, not command. Something almost like wonder. "Isn't that convenient. A man who gives speeches."

* * *

We talked until the lamps burned low. Plans, routes, names. Ilo Venn turned out to be the most valuable person in the room—he'd spent twenty years logging patrol frequencies, and when he pulled out his old notebooks, half the transfer codes in Tomás's ledger matched callsigns Ilo had written down years ago without knowing what they meant. "I keep everything," he said apologetically, as if it were a character flaw. "It's the dispatcher's disease. You never know which transmission will turn out to matter."

"This one," I told him. "This one mattered."

Around midnight the crowd thinned, and the ones who remained were the ones who'd already decided, in the wordless way coastal people decide things, that they were in it. Suri fell asleep sitting up against Kade's shoulder. The barkeep brought out food she claimed was leftover stew, and it was terrible, and we ate all of it.

I sat with the logbook open to the last page long after the others had gone quiet. My brother's final line wasn't about bullion or betrayal. It was a tide table. He'd drawn it himself, hand-ruled columns, six tides marked and counted down, and next to the sixth he'd written a single word: *soon.*

Twelve years ago, he'd been counting tides too. Waiting for one that would carry him home.

"Hey," Kade said. She'd come around the table and stood looking down at the page. "What does it say? The last line."

"A tide table," I said. "He was counting down."

She was quiet a moment. "To what?"

"To us finding it, probably. To someone coming." I ran my thumb along the faded ink. "Or maybe just to morning. Hard to say. He was a mile-measurer stuck counting tides at the end. Worst of both worlds."

Kade put her hand on my shoulder—brief, hard, the exact pressure of someone who means it and doesn't intend to make a ceremony of it. "Get some sleep, Reyes. Tomorrow we make sure every name in that book gets read somewhere louder than a tavern."

After she went up to the rented rooms, I sat alone with the lamp and the water slapping the pilings below, and I took out my knife, and on the inside back cover of the logbook, beneath my brother's tide table, I wrote a new column. Six more tides, counted forward this time instead of down. Next to the first one I wrote a single word too.

*Tomorrow.*

Outside, the tide turned. I felt it before I heard it—that faint change in the slap against the pilings, the whole harbor exhaling and drawing breath again. Somewhere out in the dark, the current reversed itself and began, patiently, honestly, keeping its promise to come back.

So would I. Every tide, honestly kept.

Starting with the one that carried a vice-admiral's name to the capital.
