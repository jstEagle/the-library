## 22. A Monsoon for the Current

The tide came in at 6:14, and I was in the water by 6:20, which by my old standards would have been a scandal. Twelve years of salvage work taught me to treat the sea like a creditor—get there first, take what you can, leave before it notices. Now I treat it like a landlord. I show up when I say I will, I pay my respects, and I don't take anything that isn't mine.

The water off Greyharbor Point was the color of old bottle glass, and cold in the way that water is always cold here, no matter what the season insists. I went down slow. No rush. That's the difference, I suppose. That's the whole difference, if I'm honest, and honesty is the trade I'm in now.

Below me the reef spread out like a drowned city that had never been above water in the first place. Coral the color of bruised plums. A school of silver fish moving as one nervous thought. And there, where the sand met the rock shelf, the marker I'd set two seasons ago: a length of brass rod, polished by salt, wrapped at the base in a coil of red line. Suri's idea, the red line. "So they can find their way home in the dark," she'd said, and I hadn't argued, because Suri has a way of saying things that end arguments before they start.

I knelt on the sand in front of it, the way you'd kneel at a grave, except the grave was everywhere. The whole sea is a grave if you think about it too long, which is why most people don't. I think about it exactly long enough.

* * *

People ask me sometimes—Ilo asks, usually, over cards, with the particular slyness of a woman who already knows the answer—how long I've been doing this. The diving for the drowned, I mean. Not the salvage. The other thing.

Three years now. Since the river. Since the monsoon season that came early and closed the gorge like a book slamming shut, with the wreck and everything in it still inside.

I tell them it started as a debt. That's the polite version. The true version is that after we ran the rapids, after the navy patrol gave up chasing us somewhere past the second bend because their boat drew too much water for their courage, after Kade docked us at Greyharbor and stood on the pier with her arms crossed like a woman deciding whether to burn her own fleet down—I didn't know what to do with my hands anymore.

That sounds small. It isn't. When you've spent your whole life measuring yourself in miles traveled, in wrecks stripped, in paydays collected and spent badly, and then you surface holding the only thing you ever actually wanted—your brother's handwriting, his last words, the proof that the navy let him and forty others drown to cover the sale of a cargo they'd already stolen—when you surface with that pressed to your chest and the flood roaring down the gorge behind you, erasing the scene of the crime and the crime itself, you find out that your hands don't know their job anymore.

Salvage is taking. I'd been a taker for twenty years. And I'd just watched the sea take the last thing back.

* * *

The reading happened the way Ilo planned it, which is to say it happened whether anyone approved or not.

The Saltglass Tavern, Greyharbor, a Tuesday in the wet season, rain hammering the roof like the sky had a grudge. Kade sat at the corner table with her back to the wall—she'd sit with her back to the wall at her own funeral—and Suri perched on a barrel, and Ilo stood by the door counting arrivals like a bouncer with a conscience. There were maybe forty people. Fishermen, mostly. A couple of retired navy men who came in scowling and stayed silent and left before the end, which I counted as a kind of testimony in itself. A woman from the coastal papers with ink-stained fingers. Two of the families. The Vess family, whose son had been on the original survey team, the one that went quiet on the radio. The mother sat in the front row with her hands folded, and when I read the part where my brother wrote *the crew is being told the wreck is unstable, that rescue is too dangerous, that they should sit tight and wait for a boat that is not coming*—when I read that, she didn't cry. She nodded. Slowly, like a door closing on a long argument.

I read the whole log. Took two hours. My voice gave out twice and Suri took over once, reading my brother's account of the last dive in a steady voice that didn't match the color of her face. Ilo had copies made. Forty copies, and then two hundred, and then Ilo stopped counting, because Ilo had understood something before the rest of us: that a truth kept in one pair of hands is a hostage, and a truth put into forty pairs of hands is a fire.

The navy didn't come for me that night. I half expected them to. Instead they did the modern thing, the bureaucratic thing, the thing that's somehow worse: they convened a commission. Hearings. Subpoenas. A rear admiral retired abruptly with full honors. A cargo manifest surfaced that had been "misfiled" for twelve years. Three officers were charged; two were convicted; one died before trial, which the papers called convenient and Ilo called overdue.

It wasn't justice. Justice would have been my brother walking out of that gorge twelve years ago, salt-crusted and furious and alive, telling me himself. What we got was accounting. But I've learned that accounting is what the living get, and you take it, and you keep the books honestly from then on.

* * *

Down on the reef, I unclipped the small brass plate from my belt and held it in the green light. This one was for Doss Harrow.

I still don't know how to write about Doss. I've tried. There are pages in my notebook with his name at the top and nothing under it, just the name, over and over, like a man knocking on a door that won't open. He raised me. That's the simplest true sentence about him and also the most complete. He taught me to read water, to read weather, to read men—which is the same skill, if you're wondering, all three are about knowing where the current is going before it takes you. He taught me that a diver's first loyalty is to the man on the surface holding his line.

And then, at the end, at the waterline, with the navy boarding party coming down the rocks and the glacier groaning like the world's largest animal in pain, Doss turned around. He stood between the boarding line and our little boat, an old man with a service pistol he'd kept oiled for thirty years and had never once wanted to use, and he held them. Four minutes. Ilo timed it. Four minutes against six armed men and a rising flood, and when the calving came—when the glacier let go that first shelf and the river stood up like a struck animal—Doss was still standing there, and the water took him the way it takes everything, without malice and without apology.

He chose late. I've made my peace with that, or something adjacent to peace, the way you make peace with a scar: not by forgetting it's there but by learning what it protects. He chose late. But he chose. And the man he chose to be in those four minutes is the man I put on the brass plate, not the man who spent years feeding Kade's fleet information for money he owed to men he feared. Both of them were Doss. But a man's last honest act is the one the sea should remember, because the sea forgets everything else anyway.

I fixed the plate to the reef with two pins and a prayer I don't believe in but say anyway, because Doss believed in it and someone should keep the habit going. The plate read:

*DOSS HARROW. HE HELD THE LINE.*

Suri wrote the epitaph. I just approved it, which took me a week, and I cried in the boatyard the day I did, and the boatyard cat watched me do it with the magnificent indifference of all cats, and that was fine. That was right, even. Grief should have a witness that doesn't flinch.

* * *

There are eleven markers on the reef now. My brother has one, though he isn't here—his body stayed in the gorge, in the dark, under a hundred meters of ice and stone, and I've stopped apologizing to him for that. I talk to the marker anyway. I've decided he doesn't mind. I've decided a lot of things about the dead that are really decisions about the living, and I've stopped pretending otherwise.

Kade comes out twice a season in her survey boat, the *Patient Answer*, a name she claims she chose ironically and which I have never once believed. She dives the markers with me sometimes. She's slower in the water than she used to be—she'll deny this—and she surfaces with that expression she gets, the one that looks like anger and is actually the opposite. Kade doesn't do grief the way other people do. She does it like a debt audit. She goes down, she checks the markers, she confirms everything is where it should be, and then she comes up and yells at somebody about mooring lines, and that's her version of a funeral. I've learned to let her have it.

Suri runs her own boat now. Small, fast, absurdly well-organized. She does the coastal runs and she does them clean, no gray cargo, no questions she won't answer. Last month she took on two apprentices, kids off the Greyharbor docks with salt in their blood and nothing in their pockets, and she teaches them the way Doss taught me, except she teaches them the part Doss left out: that the trade doesn't have to cost you your soul to pay your rent. She says it differently. She says, "We don't lift what isn't ours, and we don't lie about what is." Same lesson. Better packaging.

Ilo still runs the radio. She kept the transcripts of every transmission from the gorge, the survey team's last words, my brother's crew calling for a boat that never came, and she reads them aloud once a year on the anniversary, on an open channel, so that anyone listening has to sit with them for a few minutes. The navy asked her to stop. She asked them to make her. They didn't.

And me. I dive. Not salvage—I haven't taken a single object off a wreck in three years, and it turns out I don't miss it, which surprises me more than anyone. I dive to mark. I dive to check the brass and the red line. I dive to visit. And twice a year, in the calm season, I take charter work—not recovery, never recovery—guiding families down to see the markers, or running survey dives for the coastal commission that grew out of the hearings, mapping the wrecks the navy sealed and the navy forgot, so that the drowned are at least counted, even if they can't be raised.

I measure my life in tides now. That's not poetry; it's bookkeeping. Two tides a day, roughly seven hundred a year, and I try to end each one having told the truth in it at least once. Some days the truth is small—*no, that estimate's wrong, it'll take three days, not two*—and some days it's large, like the day I stood in front of the commission and read my brother's log into the record with the whole room watching, my voice steady until the last page and then not steady at all, and I let them see that. I let them see that a man can be precise for forty pages and come apart on the forty-first, and that the coming apart is not a failure of the testimony. It's the proof of it.

* * *

Today the tide turned while I was still down at the reef, and I felt it—the subtle change in the water's mood, that slow swing from giving to taking—and I started up. Slow. No rush. The landlord and I have an understanding.

Halfway up, hanging in the blue with the light going silver above me, I did the thing I do every dive now, the thing that took me three years to learn and that I'd have laughed at, back when I measured my life in miles: I looked around. Not for salvage. Not for threats. Just looked. The plum-colored coral. The nervous silver school, gone now, replaced by a lone grouper with the insolent stare of a tavern regular. The red line on the brass rod, bright as a vein. Eleven markers, eleven names, eleven debts I pay not in money but in presence, which is the only currency the dead accept.

My brother's log sits in a case in the Greyharbor library now, open to a page chosen by rotation, so that everyone who walks past has to see his handwriting, the ink blooming slightly where the water got in despite everything, the letters of a man writing in the dark with a dying lamp. Some nights I go and stand in front of the case. I don't read it anymore. I don't need to. I know what it says. What it says is: *they were here, they were afraid, they were not forgotten, and someone came back for them, even if it was too late, even if too late is the only time anyone ever comes.*

The surface broke over me like a held breath released. The boat—Suri's old training skiff, on loan, because I still can't be trusted with a vessel of my own, a long story involving a mooring line and a harbor master with no sense of humor—rocked gently, and Suri's hand came down to haul me over the gunwale, and Kade was at the tiller pretending not to have watched the whole dive, which is her way of watching the whole dive.

"Well?" she said.

"Held," I said. "All eleven."

She nodded, once, and turned the boat toward the harbor, and the rain had stopped and the light was doing that thing it does here in the wet season, gilding everything without promising to keep it.

I sat in the stern and let the water run off me and counted the day's tide as honestly kept. One down. The next one would come at 6:51, and I'd be there for it, because that's the job now. Not taking. Keeping.

And if that sounds like a small life, after everything—the gorges, the floods, the betrayals, the four minutes that cost a man everything—then you've never watched a tide come in and understood that it keeps its promise twice a day, every day, forever, and asks nothing back except that you notice.

I notice now.

Out past the harbor mouth, where the river met the sea, the water was turning the color of bottle glass again, and somewhere under all of it, the drowned were keeping their own time, and for the first time in my life, I was keeping mine.
